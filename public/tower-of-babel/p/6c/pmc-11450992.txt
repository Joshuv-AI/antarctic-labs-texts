Establishment of seven lung ultrasound phenotypes: a retrospective observational study of an LUS registry
Authors: Qian Wang, Tongjuan Zou, Xueying Zeng, Ting Bao, Wanhong Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung phenotypes have been extensively utilized to assess lung injury and guide precise treatment. However, current phenotypic evaluation methods rely on CT scans and other techniques. Although lung ultrasound (LUS) is widely employed in critically ill patients, there is a lack of comprehensive and systematic identification of LUS phenotypes based on clinical data and assessment of their clinical value.MethodsOur study was based on a retrospective database. A total of 821 patients were included from September 2019 to October 2020. 1902 LUS examinations were performed in this period. Using a dataset of 55 LUS examinations focused on lung injuries, a group of experts developed an algorithm for classifying LUS phenotypes based on clinical practice, expert experience, and lecture review. This algorithm underwent validation and refinement with an additional 140 LUS images, leading to five iterative revisions and the generation of 1902 distinct LUS phenotypes. Subsequently, a validated machine learning algorithm was applied to these phenotypes. To assess the algorithm’s effectiveness, experts manually verified 30% of the phenotypes, confirming its efficacy. Using K-means cluster analysis and expert image selection from the 1902 LUS examinations, we established seven distinct LUS phenotypes. To further explore the diagnostic value of these phenotypes for clinical diagnosis, we investigated their auxiliary diagnostic capabilities.ResultsA total of 1902 LUS phenotypes were tested by randomly selecting 30% to verify the phenotypic accuracy. With the 1902 LUS phenotypes, seven lung ultrasound phenotypes were established through statistical K-means cluster analysis and expert screening. The acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) exhibited gravity-dependent phenotypes, while the cardiogenic pulmonary edema exhibited nongravity phenotypes. The baseline characteristics of the 821 patients included age (66.14 ± 11.76), sex (560/321), heart rate (96.99 ± 23.75), mean arterial pressure (86.5 ± 13.57), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II)score (20.49 ± 8.60), and duration of ICU stay (24.50 ± 26.22); among the 821 patients, 78.8% were cured. In severe pneumonia patients, the gravity-dependent phenotype accounted for 42% of the cases, whereas the nongravity-dependent phenotype constituted 58%. These findings highlight the value of applying different LUS phenotypes in various diagnoses.ConclusionsSeven sets of LUS phenotypes were established through machine learning analysis of retrospective data; these phenotypes could represent the typical characteristics of patients with different types of critical illness.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03299-w.

Introduction

Lung phenotypes have been extensively utilized to assess lung injury and guide precise treatment [1–3]. Previous studies have reported that significant lung injury is associated with clinical disorders, physiological data, and radiographic images. Others studies have illustrated biological phenotypes, including plasma protein biomarkers, gene expression, and common causative microbiological pathogens [3–7]. These findings could lead to the identification of pathways for defining phenotypes and testing therapeutics that could lead to a more personalized approach for precise therapies for patients with lung injury [8, 9]. Gattinoni and colleagues demonstrated that COVID-19-related ARDS can be divided into type H (type high) and type L (type low) [5] ARDS according to chest CT images. The patients with these two types exhibited different therapeutic responses. Patients with type H ARDS benefited from low tidal volume and high PEEP, whereas those with type L, and the classified treatment has great medicinal value. lung ultrasound (LUS), a noninvasive imaging technique, is crucial for assessing various pulmonary conditions [10]. Vetrugno’s research has shown that the combining chest CT and lung ultrasound optimizes the diagnosis and management of patients during COVID-19 [11]. Although LUS is widely employed in critically ill patients [1, 12], several studies have reported that by monitoring different lung ultrasonographic signs [13–17], there are diagnostic differences between cardiogenic and noncardiogenic lung edema, particularly regarding subpleural consolidations [18–20]. Some related research has focused on diagnosing the primary disease [21, 22]; other studies have reported the clinical diagnostic value of lung ultrasonography for pneumonia and solitary obstructive atelectasis when combined with relevant clinical indicators to construct a diagnostic decision tree [23, 24].There is a lack of comprehensive and systematic studies of LUS phenotypes based on real clinical data and assessments of their clinical value. For these reasons, the objective of our study was to establish additional LUS phenotypes by combining patterns of different LUS presentations in various regions [10]. This goal was achieved through lecture review [1, 12, 25], expert discussions and experience [26, 27], and the fundamental principles of LUS [28]. We aimed to devise basic rules and create a foundational database of LUS phenotypes using machine learning [29]. It is essential to standardize lung ultrasound usage and establish uniform evaluation criteria. Additionally, to assess the diagnostic value of these phenotypes for clinical diagnosis, we investigated their auxiliary diagnostic capabilities.

Methods

Database extraction and sample establishment.

A retrospective study of 1902 LUS examinations of 821 patients in the LUS database was conducted from September 2019 to October 2020. Inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: individuals aged 18 years and older who were undergoing critical ultrasound examinations in the Intensive Care Unit (ICU). Exclusion criteria included patients with incomplete or normal pulmonary ultrasound images. Each LUS examination included twelve LUS images. We used a twelve-zone LUS examination protocol according to the technical specifications for the clinical application of critical ultrasonography [30]. The examination was conducted using a convex array probe, recommended by the operator. The probe was held perpendicular to the ribs in each area to ensure clear ultrasound images of the lungs were obtained. Ultrasound images and findings from each area were meticulously recorded, and a comprehensive analysis was conducted to ascertain the pathophysiological alterations in the lungs. The most representative lung ultrasound changes in each region were identified and designated as the lung ultrasound pattern for that specific region (refer to Table 1; Fig. 1). Furthermore, a Modified Lung Ultrasound Score (MLUSS) was applied to each region, with the scoring criteria detailed in Table 1.

In this study, the IntelliJ IDEA software platform was utilized for machine learning algorithm. Java was selected as the back-end development language, and Vue was selected as the front-end development framework. A team of five certified experts, each with a decade of experience in critical ultrasound and members of the Critical Care Ultrasound Study Group (CCUSG), developed an algorithm to classify Lung Ultrasound (LUS) phenotypes. This algorithm was informed by clinical practices, expert insights, and comprehensive lecture reviews. Initially, the algorithm was applied to a dataset comprising 55 LUS examinations focused on lung injuries. It was later validated and refined through analysis of over 140 LUS examinations, leading to five algorithmic revisions. For detailed procedures, please refer to the supplementary materials (S1, S2). Through these processes, utilizing K-means cluster analysis and expert evaluations, 1902 lung ultrasound phenotypes were identified, eventually categorizing them into seven distinct LUS phenotypes. Subsequently, the experts sampled 30% of these phenotypes and assessed the accuracy of the machine learning model by analyzing 571 LUS examinations.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. The values are expressed as the means ± standard deviations or medians and quartiles (first-third quartile) according to the distribution for continuous variables or as counts and percentages for categorical variables. The Wilson score and confidence interval of the single sample rate determined the accuracy of the LUS phenotypes. Finally, K-means cluster analysis and expert selection were used to establish distinct LUS phenotypes. The diagnostic value of the LUS phenotypes for clinical diagnosis was further explored.

Results

A total of 1902 LUS phenotypes were generated from the LUS database via machine learning (Fig. 2). We sampled and validated the accuracy of machine learning-based LUS phenotypes. A total of 30% of the 1902 LUS phenotypes were randomly selected for 517 LUSs examinations to verify the phenotypic accuracy. Since there were 1902 sets of LUS phenotypes, 7 LUS phenotypes were identified through statistical K-means cluster analysis and expert screening. The accuracy of gravity-dependent interstitial deaeration (G-I) was 100% (95% CI 0.92, 1), that of gravity-dependent consolidation deaeration (G-C) was 95.8% (95% CI 0.88 0.98), and that of gravity-dependent atelectasis deaeration (G-A) was 97% (95% CI 0.94, 0.98). The accuracy of diffuse non-gravity-dependent interstitial deaeration (D-NG-I) was 100% (95% CI 0.93, 1), that of bilateral/unilateral non-gravity-dependent interstitial deaeration (B/U-NG-I) was 93.4% (95% CI 0.84, 0.96), and that of non-gravity-dependent real deaeration (NG-C) was 88% (95% CI 0.76, 0.95). The accuracy of gravity-independent atelectasis deaeration (NG-A) was 96.3% (95% CI 0.94, 0.98) (Table 2).

Discussion

We established foundational rules for LUS ultrasound pathophysiological phenotype based on clinical practice and academic lectures [1, 10, 12, 18, 31–34]. Through statistical cluster analysis and expert review, we identified seven significant LUS phenotypes. These phenotypes facilitate the progressive stratification of lung pathophysiology diagnoses and support the implementation of targeted treatments [3, 5, 35]. Moreover, multiple iterations of machine learning [17, 36], we generated meaningful and valuable typical LUS phenotypes, consisting of three gravitational distribution phenotypes and four nongravitational distribution phenotypes [4]. Different LUS phenotypes can indicate distinct primary aetiologies in lung [33, 37]. Severe pneumonia is more frequently observed in individuals with gravity-dependent deaeration, is predominantly observed in individuals presenting with unilateral distribution [14, 21, 24, 38–41]. Moreover, ARDS is more commonly associated with gravity-dependent consolidation deaeration [7, 21, 42–44]. In contrast, the cardiogenic pulmonary edema is primarily manifests in patients with non-gravity-dependent interstitial deaeration [15, 45]. The diverse LUS phenotypes are invaluable for diagnosing and assessing disease conditions [31, 46, 47] and hold potential for guiding precise future treatments to guide precise treatment in the future [35].

Lichtenstein promoted LUS in 1995 [18, 32, 48–54]. From 1995 to 2005, Lichtenstein established the theoretical foundation and research significance of ten major LUS signs, proposing a systematic evaluation process for LUS encompassing the BLUE protocol and FALL protocol [18]. In 2012, guidelines for LUS were published [12], followed by the release of the second edition in 2023 [1]. The clinical application framework of LUS has been continuously enhanced. The understanding of this technique has significantly improved, leading to its widespread utilization in severe cases due to its remarkable value. Implementing twelve-zone assessment and LUSS aids clinical diagnosis and facilitates treatment decisions, particularly for patients requiring comprehensive postural and prognostic evaluations [38]. However, a comprehensive assessment of lung ultrasound is essential to accurately determine changes related to air and fluid within the lungs.

Numerous studies have employed regional ultrasonic scoring systems as a methodological approach to assess disease progression and diagnosis in preliminary research phases. Despite the practical value of LUSS, it exhibits limitations in accurately reflecting the pathophysiological changes within the lungs. To address this issue, we have developed lung ultrasound pathophysiological phenotypes based on three core elements: type of pathophysiological changes, lesion distribution and gravity dependence. These phenotypes are generated using a computer program that integrates these elements to establish a comprehensive lung ultrasound database. The data for this database are sourced from real-time ultrasound examinations of ICU patients both at admission and during subsequent changes in their condition. This ensures that the generated pathophysiological phenotypes are both highly representative and objective. These phenotypes not only facilitate a more precise evaluation of lung pathophysiology but also support clinicians in identifying the primary causes of respiratory failure, and in devising treatment plans and assessing prognoses. By association analysis with diagnosis, gravity-dependent classification can reflect the lung’s characteristics and potentially guide the treatment of patients in the prone position [44, 55].

However, our research has certain limitations. Our results are based on data from a single centre, which may have introduced certain biases. To further enhance the research, it is advisable to expand the sample size and actively conduct multicenter prospective studies to improve the reliability and generalizability of the results. Secondly, although the machine learning algorithm in this study has shown high accuracy in generating typical lung ultrasound phenotypes, its performance is limited by the quality and diversity of the training data. Future efforts should focus on optimizing the algorithm and increasing the diversity of the training data to enhance its applicability in different clinical settings. Additionally, our LUS phenotype data are derived from lung ultrasound examinations at different times during the patients’ hospital stays, which might better reflect the phenotypic characteristics of the disease at various stages. Compared to traditional imaging techniques, lung ultrasound is non-invasive, portable, and provides immediate feedback. However, interpreting lung ultrasound is complex and requires systematic training and stringent quality control. Vetrugno’s research demonstrates the widespread acceptance and implementation of LUS in Italy’s intensive care settings, underscoring the need for improved training and certification, as well as the development of standardized protocols [22, 56]. Our team comprises physicians with specialized training in critical care ultrasound and over ten years of experience. In this study, we aim to improve the accuracy of lung ultrasound assessments by employing a twelve-zone pathophysiological patterns. Should the imaging results be unclear, a seasoned critical care ultrasound physician will provide additional interpretation to reduce operator bias and decrease the likelihood of errors that could negatively impact the generation of lung ultrasound phenotypes.

Finally, future research should also focus on the potential applications of LUS phenotypes in specific diseases. Through continuous in-depth research and application, LUS phenotypes are expected to become an important tool in the management of pulmonary diseases, promoting the development of precision medicine.

Conclusions

In conclusion, based on our study, we established seven sets of LUS phenotypes through machine learning analysis of retrospective data; these phenotypes could represent the typical characteristics of patients with different types of critical illness.
