The association between childhood left-behind experience, help-seeking, and mental health status among university students in rural China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Lin Yin, Lawrence T. Lam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe experience of being left behind by parents in the care of other relatives during childhood is common in China. Students with such experiences may be vulnerable to adverse mental health outcomes, but seeking help may mitigate the impact. The relationships among childhood left-behind experience, help-seeking, and mental health status are yet to be explored, particularly in rural China.MethodsTwo cross-sectional health surveys were conducted among first-year university students in Guizhou, China, in 2018 and 2020, with a total sample of 4078. Single-item questions were used to gather information on whether the respondent had Childhood Left-Behind (CLB) experience and sought professional help. Mental health status was measured using the Chinese Symptom Checklist 90 (SCL-90). Data were analysed using multiple linear regression and logistic regression models.ResultsThere was a positive and significant relationship between CLB experience and the total SCL-90 scores in the sample population, with a regression coefficient of 4.8 (s.e.= 1.45, t4070 = 4.18, p < 0.001) after adjusting for potential confounding variables, including demographics, concern about one’s mental health, and help-seeking. A similar result was obtained using the dichotomized SCL classification, with an increased odds of positive psychiatric symptoms by nearly 25% for those who had a CLB experience compared to those who did not (OR = 1.24, 95%C.I.=1.05–1.46), after controlling for potential confounding variables. A significant modification effect was found between CLB and help-seeking in the association between CLB experience and mental health status. However, no significant modification effect was found between CLB and the year of data collection.ConclusionsThe results of this exploratory study suggest that the experience of being left behind by parents, even in the care of other relatives, can be detrimental to the mental health of these children as they grow up. Help-seeking for mental health problems can attenuate the impact of CLB experience and, in turn, reduce the risk of mental ill health in young people.

Background

Youth mental health has long been recognised by the World Health Organisation (WHO) as an important public health issue with negative consequences [1]. Studies have reported the growing trend of youth mental health problems in many countries around the world including China, Norway, Spain, and South Africa [2–5]. In China, it has been estimated that the prevalence of youth mental health problems, particularly depression among first-year university students, is nearly 25% [6]. A study by WHO suggested that stressful experiences were one of the major risk factors closely related to mental disorders globally [7].

In terms of lifetime stressful experiences, the lack of parental care and being left behind by parents during childhood have long been considered precursors to stressful life events and mental health issues, such as anxiety [8]. A Left-Behind child has been defined as a young child being left in the care of others, such as grandparents or other relatives, when both parents are away for a long period in a distant location for work or other reasons [9]. Childhood Left-Behind (CLB) experience is common for many young people in China, particularly in rural areas [10]. A recent survey study reported that the prevalence of CLB experience was about 29.2% in first-year university students [11]. The effects of CLB experience on youth mental health, have been recognised for sometimes. In 2018, Fellmeth et al. found that children and young people who had CLB experiences were at a higher risk of mental health problems, including depression, anxiety, substance use disorder, self-harm, and suicide compared to those without [9]. More recent studies, mainly conducted in China, also found that CLB experience is associated with adverse mental health conditions in young people [11–15]. Li et al., reported that the CLB experience is detrimental to the quality of life of first-year university students [12]. A systematic review and meta-analytical study by Wang et al. also found that CLB experience is significantly related to anxiety disorders among children [13]. For the individuals who had CLB experience, the social anxiety score was significantly higher by a Weight Mean Difference (WMD) unit of 0.35 (95%C.I.=0.23–0.48) than those who did not have the experience [13]. In the context of mental health research among young people, particularly studies on the relationship between CLB experience and mental health status in adolescents in more remote and rural regions in China, little has been found [16, 17]. As such, more concerted efforts in researching the topic are urgently needed.

Ample studies have demonstrated that help-seeking is one of the important protective factors against mental health problems, particularly among young people [18]. It works at the primary level of early prevention when the individual encounters mental health issues and is willing to receive help [18]. It also works at higher levels of early treatment and better ongoing management for preventing relapses of mental health problems [18]. As such, it is prudent to explore the role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health in the current study. Hence, this study aims to examine the association between CLB experience and the mental health status of young people who live in the rural and remote parts of China. It also aims to explore the role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health status, and whether help-seeking would moderate such a relationship.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey targeted first-year university students through a self-reported questionnaire, with data collection occurring in 2018 and 2020. The study was conducted at Anshun University, located in Guizhou, southwestern China. Guizhou is characterized by mountainous terrain and limited plains, with an urbanization rate of approximately 50%. Anshun University, a public institution governed by public education policy, primarily enrolls students from rural areas of Guizhou. Based on data from a previously unpublished study on the mental health status of young people in Guizhou, which reported a depression prevalence of 20% and a response rate of 45%, it was estimated that approximately 1559 students were required to achieve 80% power at a type I error rate of 5%. This study received permission to be conducted at Anshun University and was approved as a pilot study of a mental health research project by Macau University of Science and Technology. Ethical approval was granted by the Faculty Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Medicine, Macau University of Science and Technology (MUST-FED-20240902001).

Participants were recruited through a random process using the student list of all first-year students, aged 18 years old according to Chinese education policy, enrolled at Anshun University as the sample frame. A random list of students was generated from the full student list to form the potential pool of participants. These students were invited to respond to an online questionnaire through the university’s student centre. Information about the survey was posted on the front page of the online questionnaire, and participation was indicated by clicking on a consent icon. Informed consent was obtained from all participants by selecting the consent button to activate the online questionnaire. The same process was used for both surveys, with 2354 and 1724 respondents recruited in 2018 and 2020, respectively.

Measurements

The Childhood Left-Behind (CLB) experience was assessed using a single-item yes or no question. Students were asked: “When you were a child, did your parents leave you to stay with your grandparents or relatives when they went to another city/place for work?” A response of ‘yes’ was classified as having a CLB experience, and “no” otherwise.

The mental health status was assessed using the Chinese Symptom Checklist 90 (SCL-90), which has been commonly used in universities and colleges in China [19]. The scale consists of 90 items covering a range of psychiatric symptoms in emotional, cognitive, and behavioural aspects of mental health. It employs a five-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, with 0 indicating"never,"1"mild,"2"moderate,"3"slightly severe, and 4"severe."A higher score indicates a greater risk of exhibiting psychiatric symptomatology [20]. The psychometric properties of the scale have been widely studied with results indicating good validity and reliability [21]. The Chinese version of SCL-90 was translated by Wang in 1984 [22]. and was subsequently validated by Jin et al. in 1986 [23]. The optimal cutoff point of 160 or above, according to the instructions of the original author, was used to classify problematic psychiatric symptomatology [20]. In this study, the total SCL-90 scores and the dichotomised variables were both used for analysis.

Help-seeking from a mental health professional was assessed using a single-item, binary (yes or no) question. Respondents were asked to indicate whether they had recently sought assistance from a university counsellor for their mental health issues prior to the survey. An affirmative response indicated help-seeking behaviour, while a negative response indicated the absence of such behaviour.

Demographic data, including sex, single-child status, region of residence (rural or urban), and concern about mental health issues, were collected and incorporated into the data analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data, obtained from the online platform, were meticulously cleaned and analysed using SPSS 29.0 software. Descriptive statistics were generated to characterise respondents’ attributes, help-seeking behaviour, Childhood Left-Behind (CLB) experience, and SCL-90 psychiatric symptomatology, employing frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means and standard deviations (s.d.) for continuous variables. Bivariate analyses were conducted to examine the unadjusted associations between CLB experience, help-seeking behaviour, psychiatric symptomatology, and other variables with the mental health outcome variable. To investigate the adjusted associations between CLB experience and psychiatric symptomatology, multiple linear regression and logistic regression modelling approaches were applied to the different types of outcome variables, namely SCL-90 total scores and the dichotomised categorical variable. All other variables were considered potential confounding factors to be controlled. To explore the potential moderation role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health, an interaction term between CLB experience and help-seeking was created and tested in the multiple linear regression and logistic regression models. Given that one of the surveys was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, it was hypothesised that the year of data collection might also influence the relationship between CLB experience and mental health. Consequently, an interaction term between CLB experience and year was generated and tested for potential effects. A type I error rate of 5% was employed for all hypothesis testing.

Results

A total of 4078 complete and useful responses were obtained from the two surveys with 2354 (57.7%) in 2018 and 1724 (42.3%) in 2020. Among the respondents, 1229 (31.9%) were males, 374 (9.2%) were single children in the family, 395 (9.7%) were from urban areas, and 1700 (41.7%) had sought help for mental health issues prior to the survey. Regarding the CLB experience, in 1954 (47.9%) of respondents reported having such an experience. For mental health status, 725 (17.8%) scored 160 or higher on the SCL-90 indicating a high risk of psychiatric symptomatology, with a mean score of 130.9 (s.d. =36.3) (Table 1).

While exploring the moderating role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health status, significant differences were observed. Among those with CLB experience, there was a significant increase in the SCL-90 total scores of 6.5 units (s.e. = 1.50) for those who did not seek help compared to the referent group (non-CLB experience and did not seek help) (t4069 = 4.35, p < 0.001). However, there was no significant increase in the SCL-90 total score for those who had CLB experience but sought help for mental health issues compared to the referent group (Table 3). Similar results were also obtained from the logistic regression analyses, showing a significant increase in the odds of psychiatric symptomatology by 26% for those who with CLB experience but did not seek help compared with the referent group (OR = 1.26, 95%C.I.=1.03–1.55, z = 2.21, p = 0.027). No significant difference in the odds was found for those with CLB experience who sought help compared to the referent group (Table 3). The interaction terms of CLB experience and year of data collection were tested in both regression approaches, with results indicating no significant interaction between CLB experience and year of data collection on mental health status.

Discussion

This study investigated the associations among Childhood Left-Behind (CLB) experience, help-seeking behaviour, and the mental health status of first-year university students in rural China. Specifically, it explored the moderating role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health status. The results indicated that approximately 48% of the respondents had CLB experience, which is significantly higher than the prevalence reported in a recent study (29.2%) [11]. Regarding mental health status, the prevalence of psychiatric symptomatology, assessed by the Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90), was approximately 18%, consistent with a recent study reporting a symptomatology rate of about 20% [24]. However, compared to another similar study on CLB experience and mental health status, the prevalence of psychiatric symptomatology is much lower, with about 37% reported in the previous study [11]. The significant association between CLB experience and mental health problems has been long reported [11–15]. The results obtained from this study are consistent with similar studies conducted in different parts of China. The findings on the role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health indicate a significant moderation effect of help-seeking. A search in the literature of recent studies worldwide reveals no similar results have been reported. As such, the results on the moderating role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health status are unique.

Several observations can be made regarding the results mentioned above. First, the high prevalence of CLB experience is likely due to the study being conducted in rural and remote areas of China, whereas other reported studies were conducted in more urbanized locations, except for Qin et al.‘s study [11–15, 17]. Second, the discrepancy in mental health status between the results obtained from this study and other similar studies, such as the one by Liu et al., may be attributed to the timing of Liu et al.‘s study, which was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic [11]. The data for the current study were obtained in two different periods, before and during the pandemic, but no significant interaction effect of CLB experience and time of data collection was found. This suggests that the association between CLB experience and mental health status is not affected by the time of data collection. Third, the most significant finding of the current study is the moderating role of help-seeking in the relationship between CLB experience and mental health. These results highlight the importance of seeking help for mental health issues and reflect the function of help-seeking in minimizing the impact of adverse childhood experiences on mental health.

The results of this study have both theoretical and practical implications. Theoretically, the findings support the buffering hypothesis of social support theory [25, 26], which posits that social support acts as a buffer between adverse life experiences and psychological stress, thereby lessening the impact of traumatic experiences on the individual [25]. In the context of the current study, help-seeking for mental health issues serves a dual function. On one hand, in line with the buffering hypothesis, help-seeking functions as a buffer between CLB experience and psychiatric symptomatology, resulting in a reduced risk of mental ill health. On the other hand, the results of help-seeking produce a direct therapeutic effect on the individual, lessening the impact of CLB experience on mental health. Practically, the findings also inform prevention strategies for youth mental health. There is growing concern about the CLB experience and its effects on the mental health of young people in China. Consequently, various interventional trials have been conducted to address the mental health consequences of CLB experience [27, 28]. Help-seeking is identified as one of the important protective factors for mental health problems [18]. However, for any intervention program aiming to alleviate the burden of mental ill health and improve mental well-being to be successful, recipients’ willingness to seek help is of paramount importance. The results of this study demonstrate the detrimental effects on psychiatric symptomatology in young people with CLB experience who did not seek help. It is likely that these young people, for various reasons, were not willing to seek help. Therefore, when designing preventive intervention programs to address the CLB experience of young people, it is prudent to include components that enhance help-seeking willingness [29].

As with all studies, there are strengths and weaknesses. One of the strengths of this relatively large sample survey is that the sample was generated from the only university in Anshun, a prefecture-level city located in southwestern Guizhou province. The sample covered all first-year students enrolled over two years, providing a certain level of representativeness. Another strength is that the outcome variable was assessed using the SCL-90, a well-validated and widely used instrument in China and other parts of the world, which helps minimize systematic bias due to uncertain measurement error. Several limitations have been identified in this study. First, this was a cross-sectional study with limited capacity to draw conclusions on the causality between CLB experience and mental health status. Second, both study variables, namely CLB experience and help-seeking, were assessed using single-item questions rather than properly designed and validated instruments, which is a significant shortcoming that introduces a substantial risk of information biases. The redemption is that one variable is related to the individual’s factual experience and the other to actual behaviour, which may reduce possible information biases. Third, insufficient data collection on demographic and other potential confounding factors, such as recent stressful life events, health status, and sleep quality, resulted in inadequate adjustment of covariates in the data analyses, potentially causing inaccurate effect estimates in the direct and moderated relationships between CLB experience and mental health. Consequently, this may have led to inflated estimates. Additionally, future studies should employ a longitudinal cohort study design to overcome the limitations of the current study design. Finally, as discussed above, the data collection spanned across two distinct periods, one of which coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic. This temporal variation may introduce additional biases into the study and potentially affect the outcomes. However, the results of the data analysis indicate no significant interaction effect between the time periods and CLB experience. Future research should prioritise meticulous study design in health surveys to mitigate such biases.

Conclusions

The results of this exploratory study suggest that the experience of being left behind by parents, even in the care of other relatives, can be detrimental to the mental health of these children as they grow up. Help-seeking for mental health problems can attenuate the impact of CLB experience and, in turn, reduce the risk of mental ill health in young people.
