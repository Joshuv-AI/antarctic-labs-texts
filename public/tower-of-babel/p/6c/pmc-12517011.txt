Evaluating the efficacy of lower-level transversus abdominis plane block for postoperative analgesia following lower-uterine-segment caesarean delivery: study protocol for an observer-blinded, randomised controlled trial at a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Ning Zhang, Jiahe Tian, Gang Chen, Shien Cai, Xiaorong Zhu, Chenchen Wang, Kaiming Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionBoth transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) and transversalis fascia plane block (TFPB) have been shown to provide effective analgesia for caesarean section (CS) pain. However, the targeting of distinct spinal nerve branches by each technique suggests that neither approach offers complete analgesia. Moreover, the targeting of these branches by TAPB is influenced by puncture level, which has seldom been clearly defined in previous studies. This study aims to evaluate the efficacy of lower-level TAPB (L-TAPB), performed at the midpoint between the umbilicus and the anterior superior iliac spine along the anterior axillary line, by comparing it with those of classic umbilical-level TAPB (U-TAPB) and TFPB for postoperative analgesia following lower-uterine-segment (LUS) CS.Methods and analysisThis single-centre, observer-blinded, randomised controlled trial will include 99 full-term parturients scheduled for elective LUS CS with a transverse incision under morphine-free spinal anaesthesia administered via a ‘needle-through-needle’ technique. Participant recruitment commenced on 3 March 2025 at the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (WMU; Wenzhou, China). The anticipated trial completion date is October 2025. Participants will be randomised at a 1:1:1 ratio to receive L-TAPB, U-TAPB or TFPB postoperatively. Intrathecal morphine will not be used in this trial. The primary outcome of this trial will be hydromorphone consumption within the first 8 hours post-block. Secondary outcomes will include analgesic effectiveness and recovery indices such as pain scores over time, opioid consumption over time, the 15-item Quality of Recovery scale, overall satisfaction and other relevant measures. We will also assess adverse events.Ethics and disseminationAll study procedures will adhere to Good Clinical Practice guidelines, with ethical approval obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of WMU (approval no. 2024-K-322-02). The research findings will be published in peer-reviewed scientific journals.Trial registration numberChiCTR2500097026.

Introduction

Post-caesarean section (CS) pain presents a significant clinical challenge, impeding early ambulation and perinatal quality of life.1 It not only delays postoperative recovery but also substantially elevates the incidence of complications, including venous thromboembolism,2 postpartum depression3 and chronic pain syndrome.4 Furthermore, it can lead to delays in breastfeeding and the establishment of mother–infant bonding.5

Notably, an early study demonstrated that CS ranked ninth among 179 types of surgical procedures for severity of pain on day 1 after surgery,6 with a median worst pain score of 6.14 on a numeric rating scale of 0–10. Fortunately, the widespread use of fascia plane block (FPB) techniques appears to have led to a significant reduction in post-CS pain.79

The abdominal wall is innervated by intercostal nerves (T6–T12) and ilioinguinal–iliohypogastric nerves (branches of L1). These somatic nerves originate from the dorsal horns of their respective spinal cord segments.7 Their anatomical courses involve initial passage along the internal aspect of the erector spinae musculature, subsequent traversal superficial to the quadratus lumborum and final distribution between the transversus abdominis and internal oblique muscle layers.10 Accordingly, local anaesthetics injected into these fascial planes can effectively interrupt nociceptive transmission from the abdominal wall to the central nervous system. FPB techniques, including paravertebral block, erector spinae plane block, quadratus lumborum block, transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB), transversalis fascia plane block (TFPB) and rectus sheath block, are systematically classified based on their targeted fascial planes.10 11 TAPB is a preferred technique among anaesthesiologists due to its relatively straightforward execution and the clear visualisation of anatomical landmarks under ultrasound (US) guidance.

In lower-uterine-segment (LUS) transverse CS, iatrogenic trauma can be significant when the transverse incision is compounded by unrecognised vertical injury to both the muscle and peritoneum following blunt dissection of the rectus abdominis. Anatomically, this injury pattern mainly involves neural territories from T11 to L1, corresponding to the surgical site’s innervation. However, recent comparative evidence demonstrates that TFPB provides excellent recovery quality metrics and significantly reduces opioid requirements compared with TAPB in postoperative pain management.8 12 TFPB’s superior analgesic efficacy likely stems from its targeting of the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves within their anatomical plane between the transversus abdominis fascia and transversalis fascia, which might be heavily involved in post-CS pain transmission. Classic umbilical-level TAPB (U-TAPB), which is administered at a higher anatomical level, might not adequately cover these nerves. Moreover, most of the existing literature lacks standardised descriptions of injection levels for TAPB in CS. This anatomical variability can lead to inconsistent blockade of thoracoabdominal nerve branches, resulting in heterogeneous analgesic outcomes for LUS transverse CS.

Lower-level TAPB (L-TAPB), performed at the midpoint between the umbilical and anterior superior iliac spine (ASIS) levels along the anterior axillary line, is likely to provide more consistent blockade of the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves compared with classic U-TAPB. Based on this rationale, we hypothesise that the analgesic efficacy of L-TAPB may differ from that of classic U-TAPB and TFPB in women undergoing LUS CS. To test this hypothesis, we designed an observer-blinded randomised controlled trial (RCT).

Methods/design

Study design and trial status

This single-centre, observer-blinded RCT protocol adheres strictly to Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials 2013 Statement and Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials 2010 guidelines.13 Parturient recruitment began on 3 March 2025, in a class A tertiary general hospital, the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (WMU; Wenzhou, China). The trial is expected to end in October 2025. The current protocol is V.1.2, dated 7 February 2025.

Enrolment of participants and allocation

Consent to participate and enrolment of participants

Following eligibility screening by investigators who have been trained for study procedures, parturients will be fully informed of the potential advantages, disadvantages and risks of participating in the current study. Any parturient who declines to participate in the trial will not experience any form of discrimination or retaliation, and her standard medical care will not be affected in any way. We will not offer incentives, financial or otherwise, to participants. The informed consent form used in this study is provided in the online supplemental file 1.

Assignment of interventions

Randomisation and allocation

Using permuted block randomisation (PBR; block size=9) at a 1:1:1 allocation ratio, we will randomly assign each of the 99 participants to one of the following groups: (1) L-TAPB, (2) U-TAPB or (3) TFPB. Allocation will be concealed using sequentially numbered opaque envelopes, handled by a researcher not involved in parturient care or follow-up. After verifying a parturient’s eligibility, the attending anaesthesiologist will open the assigned randomisation envelope to confirm the allocation and perform the designated FPB. Blinding

Anaesthesiologists performing FPB cannot be blinded due to the technical requirements of the intervention. The anatomical variation in injection sites between intervention groups presents a potential risk of unblinding participants. To preserve the integrity of the blinding process, we will not inform participants of the specific FPB technique used. Outcome assessors, statisticians and other study investigators involved in data analysis will remain blinded to group assignments. In the event of a severe adverse event (AE) or complication that necessitates discontinuation of the study intervention, we will unblind outcome assessors as needed.

Interventions

Both TAPB and TFPB have demonstrated clinical efficacy in post-CS analgesia. The classic TAPB approach for lower-abdominal analgesia is typically performed at the anatomical intersection of the umbilical level and the anterior axillary line. In this trial, participants will receive one of three FPBs on both sides: L-TAPB, U-TAPB or TFPB.

Anaesthesia and perioperative management

Anaesthesia for CS

Following standard monitoring of blood pressure, ECG and pulse oximetry, all participants will receive spinal anaesthesia using the ‘needle-through-needle’ technique. Briefly, the anaesthesiologist will perform epidural puncture at the L2–3 or L3–4 interspace. The loss-of-resistance technique will be used to identify the epidural space as the Tuohy needle is advanced. The anaesthesiologist will then advance a spinal needle through the Tuohy needle to perform spinal anaesthesia after confirming free flow of cerebrospinal fluid. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of 0.75% isobaric ropivacaine, without morphine or any other adjuvants, will be injected via the Barbotage technique.14 After withdrawing the spinal needle, the anaesthesiologist will introduce an epidural catheter into the epidural space at the required depth and secure it.

Epidural local anaesthetics are not routinely administered before incision unless the level of sensory block fails to reach T6 within 15 min. Supplemental epidural local anaesthetics (5 mL of 2% lidocaine, with a minimum interval of 10 min between additional doses) will be provided as needed. Any parturient receiving such supplemental epidural medications will be excluded from this trial.

General anaesthesia will be allowed as a rescue method in cases of combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia failure or unintentional dural puncture by the Tuohy needle. We will follow standard intraoperative care for CS as outlined in the guideline.15

Intervention description

For L-TAPB, the puncture will be performed at the midpoint between the umbilical and ASIS levels along the anterior axillary line. We will perform U-TAPB following the classic TAPB technique at the umbilical level. TFPB will be administered above the iliac crest,8 as illustrated in figure 2. All punctures will be performed under US guidance using an HFL38/13-6 MHz high-frequency linear probe. After clearly identifying the external oblique muscle, internal oblique muscle and transverse abdominis muscle, the anaesthesiologist will perform the designated FPB using a 22G needle in a medial-to-lateral direction with an in-plane technique. Once the needle reaches the compartment, 20 mL of 0.375% ropivacaine will be injected at each site, and proper needle placement will be reconfirmed by distribution of the anaesthetic throughout the fascial layer. All FPBs will be performed postoperatively in the post-anaesthesia care unit by an experienced anaesthesiologist.

Pain management

We will manage postoperative pain via patient-controlled intravenous analgesia. A WiFi-based patient-controlled analgesia (PCA) pump (ZZB III; Jiangsu Aipeng Medical Technology Co, Ltd, Nantong, China), in combination with its software, will be employed to facilitate pain management. Real-time data, including the number and timing of PCA presses, can be remotely accessed and reviewed through the software. The PCA pump is programmed to deliver a bolus of 0.3 µg hydromorphone with a lockout time of 10 min without a background infusion. Celecoxib (0.2 g) will be administered orally once daily, starting from the time of transfer to the postoperative ward.

Management of AEs

A standardised institutional protocol and standard intraoperative care for CS as outlined in the guideline15 will be implemented for the management of potential AEs to ensure consistent and timely interventions, as follows.

Nausea and vomiting

Prophylactic ondansetron (8 mg) will be administered intravenously on completion of surgery. Breakthrough symptoms may be managed with repeat ondansetron dosing and/or the addition of metoclopramide 10 mg intravenous.

Pruritus

Mild pruritus will be managed conservatively with observation and non-pharmacological measures. If pharmacological intervention is required, diphenhydramine (20 mg) will be administered intravenously.

Intraoperative hypotension

Management will include rapid fluid administration, left-uterine displacement and intravenous vasopressors (norepinephrine, 8 µg or ephedrine, 6 mg boluses) as clinically indicated.

Criteria for discontinuing or modifying allocated interventions

Parturients may withdraw from the study at any time after enrolment for any reason. Investigators may discontinue a participant’s involvement if the fascial layer cannot be identified or if severe complications arise. In the event that a parturient is lost to follow-up after the block, their data will be collected and analysed accordingly.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome of this trial will be hydromorphone consumption within the first 8 hours post-block. Secondary outcomes will include (1) numbers of effective and total PCA presses within 6 and 12 hours post-block; (2) time to first PCA pump press post-block; (3) hydromorphone consumption within 12 hours post-block; (4) resting and movement pain visual analogue scale (VAS) scores at 30 min, 2 hours, 4 hours, 8 hours, 10 hours and 12 hours post-block, used to calculate the area under the curve for pain intensity over time; (5) overall satisfaction with analgesia at 24 hours postoperatively; (6) quality of recovery as assessed using the 15-item Quality of Recovery (QoR-15) scale16 at 48 hours postoperatively; and (7) incidence of postoperative AEs such as nausea, vomiting, hypotension, hypoxaemia and dizziness within 12 hours.

Sample size calculation

Sample size was calculated for the primary outcome of cumulative hydromorphone consumption within the first 8 hours post-block using the Power Analysis and Sample Size software (V.15, NCSS Statistical Software, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA) based on a one-way fixed-effects analysis of variance (ANOVA) with three groups. Based on the findings of a study by Pinarbaşi et al,8 which reported large effect sizes for morphine consumption (Cohen’s f=0.88 at 0–4 hours and 2.24 at 4–8 hours post-block), when comparing classic TAPB and TFPB groups. For the present study, which involves two primary comparisons (L-TAPB vs U-TAPB and L-TAPB vs TFPB), a between-group effect size of Cohen’s f=0.4 was assumed. We determined that a sample size of 33 subjects per group was needed to provide 90% power with a type I error rate of 2.5%, accounting for a 20% dropout rate.

Data and statistical analysis

We will perform both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol analyses. For ITT analysis, all randomised participants will be included according to their assigned groups, regardless of protocol adherence. Missing outcome data will be addressed primarily using complete case analysis, with sensitivity analyses employing multiple imputation under the missing-at-random assumption to assess the robustness of the findings. Numbers and proportions of missing values for all key variables will be reported in the manuscript.

We will analyse data using SPSS V.16.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA) for Windows. Normality of continuous variables will be assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms and Q–Q plots. Continuous data will be presented as mean±SD if normally distributed; otherwise, data will be reported as median (IQR). Parametric tests, including ANOVA and repeated-measures ANOVA, will be applied for normally distributed data, followed by Dunnett’s post hoc test as appropriate. Non-normally distributed data will be analysed using non-parametric alternatives such as the Kruskal–Wallis test. We will summarise categorical data as frequencies (percentages) and compare them between study groups using the χ2 or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Time-to-event outcomes will be evaluated via Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and log-rank tests, with Cox regression applied for multivariate adjustment if necessary. Exploratory multivariate analyses will adjust for potential confounders, including BMI and prior caesarean delivery. Ordinal data for parturient satisfaction will be analysed using the rank-sum test. p<0.05 will be considered significant.

Postoperative pain assessment

We will instruct all parturients to rate their pain intensity on a 0–10 VAS. Resting and movement pain VAS scores will be recorded at 30 min, 2 hours, 4 hours, 8 hours, 10 hours and 12 hours post-block. Time to first PCA press and numbers of effective and total PCA presses within 6 and 12 hours post-block will be reviewed using the WiFi-based PCA software. Quality of recovery

Postoperatively, we will record overall satisfaction with analgesia at 24 hours and evaluate quality of recovery using the QoR-15 scale at 48 hours.

Adverse events

We will record and appropriately manage any AEs that occur during the perioperative period. The incidence of postoperative AEs such as nausea, vomiting, hypotension, hypoxaemia and dizziness within 12 hours will also be recorded.

Monitoring

Given the small-scale nature of this trial and that it will be conducted at a single centre, in accordance with previous studies,17 we will not establish a data monitoring committee. Since this is a small, single-centre trial, no formal interim efficacy analyses are planned. However, safety will be monitored continuously, and the principal investigator will review any severe AEs as they occur. Unblinding will be permitted if necessary to ensure participant safety. Decisions to terminate the trial will be based solely on unexpected serious safety concerns or if continued enrolment would pose an unacceptable risk, rather than on efficacy outcomes. In such cases, an interim safety report will also be prepared for submission to the relevant institutional department. The study investigators will discuss and agree on any modifications to the protocol before submitting them to the Clinical Research Board for approval. Additionally, the protocol will be updated in the Chinese Registry of Clinical Trials.

Patients (parturients in our trial) and/or the public were not involved in the development of the protocol.

Discussion

TAPB involves the injection of local anaesthetics into the fascial plane between the internal oblique muscle and the transversus abdominis muscle, effectively blocking the afferent-nerve fibres of the anterior abdominal wall. This mechanism provides targeted analgesia to the sensory nerves associated with the surgical area in CS. Anatomically, the L1 spinal nerve and its branches, namely the iliohypogastric and ilioinguinal nerves, are located in the lower abdomen. However, classic U-TAPB, which is administered at a higher anatomical level, might not adequately cover these nerves. This notion is supported by a recent RCT18 demonstrating that the combination of TAPB with ilioinguinal–iliohypogastric nerve block offers superior postoperative analgesia with a significant delay of the first request for postoperative pain relief.

TFPB, performed between the transversus abdominis muscle and the transversalis fascia, specifically targets the branches of the L1 spinal nerve, including the ilioinguinal and iliohypogastric nerves, and might also cover the T12 spinal nerve branches.19 However, TFPB lacks adequate coverage of the upper spinal nerve branches. Existing evidence8 indicates that TFPB might offer better analgesia than TAPB, as discussed in the ‘Introduction’. In summary, L-TAPB might offer a balanced solution, providing targeted analgesia through a single anatomical-level injection that might more effectively cover the innervation associated with the surgical injury site of CS.

Notably, in full-term parturients, significant enlargement of the uterus leads to a marked increase in abdominal circumference, resulting in a tense abdominal wall. Post-delivery, as abdominal wall tension decreases and the fascial compartments loosen, the spread of local anaesthetics might become more limited after TAPB or TFPB.20 For optimal analgesic efficacy, the targeted nerve branches and the distribution of local anaesthetics must align precisely.

Although intrathecal morphine provides clinically equivalent analgesia to TAPB after caesarean delivery,21 it will be deliberately omitted from the present trial. This decision was based on both clinical practice patterns and pharmacological considerations. The use of intrathecal morphine is uncommon in routine obstetric anaesthesia in mainland China, largely due to a well-documented side effect profile that includes a high incidence of pruritus, as supported by the existing literature. Furthermore, the known synergistic analgesic effect between TAPB and intrathecal morphine, which substantially reduces opioid requirements, would confound the interpretation of our results on the independent efficacy of the FPBs under investigation.22

Since this is a small sample RCT study, sample size was estimated using a relatively large effect size (Cohen’s f=0.40), with the intention of detecting clinically meaningful differences between L-TAPB and comparator groups. To account for the two planned comparisons of the primary endpoint (L-TAPB vs classic TAPB or TFPB), the type I error rate was set at 2.5%. This approach allowed for an efficient preliminary evaluation of the intervention. Nevertheless, the use of a large effect size may limit this study’s power to detect more modest differences. Therefore, if negative findings are observed, the results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies with larger sample sizes or multicentre designs may be warranted, guided by observed effect estimates and clinical relevance.

Due to the limited sample size in this study, we used PBR to help balance potential confounding factors across groups; therefore, stratified randomisation was not employed. To further address potential confounding variables (eg, BMI, prior caesarean delivery), additional multivariate analyses will be conducted on an exploratory basis. Should significant imbalances in key confounders be detected, sample size may be enlarged for further study to enhance the robustness of the findings. Further limitations of this trial include its single-centre design, which might affect the external validity of our findings. Postoperative uterine contraction-induced pain (UCP) represents a significant clinical concern; although it is not anticipated to be directly influenced by the interventions under investigation, UCP remains a potential confounding variable. PBR was employed to mitigate the effect of UCP by promoting balanced distribution across treatment groups. Although UCP is not explicitly designated as a primary or secondary outcome, the QoR-15 scale will be used to comprehensively evaluate postoperative recovery, including UCP. Future larger-scale studies could help better balance this confounding factor and should consider this approach to ensure baseline equilibrium of major covariates across groups.

This ongoing trial, motivated by anatomical rationale and the inherent limitations of commonly used FPBs, seeks to refine a morphine-free spinal anaesthesia strategy to improve post-caesarean analgesia and could provide valuable guidance for future research.
