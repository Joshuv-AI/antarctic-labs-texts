Beyond Weight Loss: Skeletal Considerations in Obesity Treatment
Authors: Myung Jin Kim, Yun Kyung Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The rapid evolution of obesity therapeutics, led by glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) and metabolic surgery, has achieved unprecedented weight loss. However, the skeletal consequences of such substantial weight reduction are of increasing clinical concern. This review explores the relationship between obesity, weight-loss modalities, and bone health. While obesity was historically viewed as bone-protective due to mechanical loading, recent evidence highlights qualitative bone deterioration and site-specific fracture risks. Intentional weight loss through caloric restriction or bariatric surgery consistently accelerates bone turnover and reduces bone mineral density (BMD), with surgical interventions showing the most significant impact. Emerging data on GLP-1RAs suggest modest BMD declines, largely proportional to weight loss and likely driven by mechanical unloading. Conversely, preclinical studies indicate that GLP-1 signaling may exert direct osteoanabolic and anti-resorptive effects. To preserve skeletal integrity, obesity management must shift toward a holistic body composition framework. Integrating structured resistance exercise, optimizing nutrition (calcium, vitamin D, and protein), and implementing skeletal monitoring in high-risk individuals are essential. Future obesity care should prioritize the maintenance of bone quality and lean mass alongside fat reduction to ensure long-term skeletal resilience and prevent fragility fractures.

INTRODUCTION

Over the past decade, obesity therapeutics have advanced substantially. The introduction of highly effective anti-obesity medications, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), has shifted expectations from modest weight reduction to sustained double-digit weight loss [1]. Concurrently, metabolic and bariatric surgery (MBS) continues to produce durable weight loss, often exceeding 20% of baseline body weight [1,2].

Although weight reduction yields well-established cardiometabolic benefits, including improved glycemic control, blood pressure, hepatic steatosis, and cardiovascular outcomes, its skeletal consequences have become an important clinical concern. Obesity was historically considered protective against osteoporosis because higher body mass increases mechanical loading and bone mineral density (BMD) [3]. However, contemporary evidence indicates that individuals living with obesity exhibit site-specific fracture patterns and that rapid or substantial weight loss can accelerate bone turnover and reduce BMD [4–6].

Notably, pharmacotherapy now achieves weight loss magnitudes previously observed primarily after bariatric surgery. Weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg and dual incretin agonists such as tirzepatide produce mean weight reductions of 10%–20% in randomized controlled trials [7–10]. Consequently, skeletal health must be integrated into obesity management rather than regarded as a secondary consideration. Recent meta-analyses of anti-obesity interventions report significant BMD reductions after MBS, whereas lifestyle and pharmacologic strategies appear to have smaller or neutral overall effects [11]. However, emerging data from obesity trials in patients without diabetes suggest that high-dose GLP-1RA-induced weight loss may increase bone turnover and potentially elevate skeletal risk in susceptible subgroups [12].

Therefore, obesity management is evolving beyond weight reduction alone. A comprehensive therapeutic framework should incorporate preservation of skeletal integrity, maintenance of lean mass, and prevention of long-term fragility fractures. This review examines (1) the complex association between obesity and bone metabolism; (2) the skeletal effects of various weight-loss interventions; and (3) emerging evidence regarding GLP-1-based therapies and bone health.

BONE METABOLISM IN OBESITY

Obesity and bone density: mechanical loading and its limits

Individuals living with obesity generally demonstrate higher areal and volumetric BMD at weight-bearing skeletal sites [13,14]. Increased body mass augments mechanical strain, stimulates osteoblastic activity, and contributes to elevated BMD [15–17]. However, higher BMD does not consistently confer fracture protection. Prior studies indicate reduced hip and wrist fracture incidence but increased humeral, ankle, and distal lower limb fractures among individuals with obesity [6,18,19]. This ‘obesity paradox’ reflects altered fall biomechanics, greater impact forces attributable to higher body mass, and variability in fat distribution. Thus, BMD alone does not adequately characterize skeletal fragility in obesity.

Visceral adiposity, bone quality, and metabolic dysfunction

Adiposity influences bone remodeling through endocrine and inflammatory pathways in addition to mechanical loading. Visceral adiposity is associated with poorer bone quality and compromised trabecular microarchitecture [20,21]. Chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction, particularly type 2 diabetes, suppress bone formation despite preserved BMD [20,22]. Increased marrow adiposity is also linked to reduced BMD and greater fracture risk [23]. Collectively, these findings indicate that obesity-related skeletal vulnerability reflects qualitative deterioration in bone structure rather than reduced density alone.

Sarcopenic obesity: the convergent risk phenotype

Sarcopenic obesity, defined by excess adiposity combined with reduced muscle mass, represents a particularly high-risk phenotype. Individuals with sarcopenic obesity exhibit lower femoral neck BMD, greater fall risk, and higher fracture incidence compared with those with obesity alone [24–26]. These observations emphasize that body composition, rather than body weight alone, determines skeletal resilience. Accordingly, obesity treatment strategies should prioritize preservation of lean mass in parallel with fat reduction.

WEIGHT LOSS AND BONE HEALTH

Intentional weight loss is central to obesity management and confers substantial cardiometabolic benefit. However, across intervention modalities, weight reduction consistently increases bone remodeling and reduces BMD to varying degrees. The magnitude and clinical relevance of skeletal adaptation depend primarily on the intervention type, rate of weight reduction, and extent of preserved mechanical loading.

Caloric restriction versus exercise

Caloric restriction (CR) underpins most weight-loss strategies. The Comprehensive Assessment of Long-term Effects of Reducing Intake of Energy (CALERIE) trials demonstrated that moderate CR in non-obese and overweight adults produced approximately 2%–3% reductions in total hip and lumbar spine BMD within 12 months [27]. These reductions were accompanied by increased bone turnover markers and endocrine adaptations, including decreased insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) and increased parathyroid hormone (PTH) and cortisol, consistent with a catabolic state during sustained energy deficit.

In contrast, when weight loss is achieved through structured exercise, particularly resistance and impact training, remodeling imbalance is attenuated. In a randomized trial by Villareal et al. [28], older adults with obesity undergoing diet-induced weight loss experienced significant hip BMD reduction of approximately 2.6% over 1 year, whereas those assigned to exercise-induced weight loss maintained hip BMD despite similar body weight reductions. Exercise increased bone formation markers, including procollagen type 1 N-terminal propeptide (P1NP) and bone alkaline phosphatase (ALP), sufficiently to counterbalance elevations in resorption markers and preserve structural integrity.

These findings underscore a key principle: energy deficit alone predisposes to bone loss, whereas mechanical strain modulates remodeling and mitigates skeletal decline. Weight reduction without adequate mechanical loading carries a predictable skeletal cost.

Metabolic and bariatric surgery

MBS produces the greatest and most durable weight loss but also the most pronounced skeletal effects [11]. Following Rouxen-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), total hip BMD declines of 8%–10% during the first postoperative year are consistently reported, with lumbar spine reductions of 5%–7%. The mechanisms underlying this magnitude of bone loss extend beyond mechanical unloading and include malabsorption of calcium and vitamin D, secondary hyperparathyroidism, and persistent elevations in bone turnover markers [29–32]. Longitudinal studies indicate that BMD decline may persist for several years postoperatively, with sustained cortical thinning and microarchitectural deterioration [33,34].

A comprehensive meta-analysis confirmed that MBS is associated with significant lumbar spine BMD reduction (mean difference, −0.40 g/cm2; 95% confidence interval, −0.73 to −0.07), whereas lifestyle and pharmacologic interventions exert substantially smaller effects [11]. This marked quantitative difference positions surgical weight loss at the highest end of the skeletal impact spectrum.

Collectively, evidence from CR, exercise, and metabolic surgery supports a graded relationship between weight-loss modality and skeletal adaptation (Fig. 1) [11,27,35]. Greater and more rapid weight reduction, particularly when combined with nutrient malabsorption, results in larger BMD declines. These principles provide a framework for evaluating the skeletal effects of contemporary pharmacologic therapies.

GLP-1 RECEPTOR AGONISTS AND BONE

GLP-1RAs and dual GLP-1/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) agonists have redefined obesity management by producing sustained weight reductions of 10%–20% [7–10], approaching those historically achieved with metabolic surgery. As pharmacologically induced weight loss increases in magnitude, its skeletal consequences become clinically significant. Current evidence indicates that GLP-1RAs alter bone remodeling dynamics and produce modest declines in BMD, largely proportional to the magnitude and rate of weight reduction. These effects are quantitatively smaller than those observed after bariatric surgery and resemble adaptive remodeling seen with CR. Liraglutide: maintenance settings and exercise interaction

A pivotal 52-week randomized controlled trial evaluated skeletal outcomes in female participants without diabetes who underwent very-low-calorie diet-induced weight loss of approximately 12.1 kg (body mass index [BMI] 34.4 to 30.2 kg/m2) [36]. During the subsequent maintenance phase, liraglutide 1.2 mg/day preserved total body bone mineral content (BMC), whereas controls experienced continued decline. During rapid weight loss, the resorption marker C-terminal telopeptide of type I collagen (CTX) increased by approximately 0.06 ng/mL and osteocalcin decreased by −1.4 ng/L. In the maintenance phase, liraglutide attenuated further BMC loss and increased the bone formation marker P1NP by 16% relative to baseline, suggesting partial compensation for weight-loss–associated remodeling [36]. Although between-group differences in areal BMD were modest, these findings indicate that long-acting GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) agonism with liraglutide may mitigate ongoing bone loss following CR-induced weight reduction. Importantly, the data support a neutral-to-favorable skeletal safety profile in the context of medically induced weight loss [36].

More recently, a secondary analysis by Jensen et al. [37] evaluating liraglutide 3.0 mg combined with structured exercise provided additional insight. Liraglutide monotherapy produced significant weight loss but reduced hip and spine BMD compared with exercise alone. In contrast, the combination of liraglutide and exercise achieved the greatest weight reduction (mean 16.9 kg) while preserving BMD at the total hip, lumbar spine, and femoral neck. Forearm BMD remained unchanged across all groups, underscoring the site-specific nature of skeletal adaptations and suggesting that changes were primarily confined to weight-bearing regions sensitive to mechanical unloading. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that skeletal responses to GLP-1RA therapy are not fixed but are modifiable and strongly influenced by mechanical loading. From a clinical perspective, the combination of GLP-1RA therapy with structured resistance and weight-bearing exercise appears to represent the most effective strategy for maximizing weight loss while preserving skeletal integrity.

Semaglutide: active weight-loss phase and remodeling intensity

Semaglutide, administered once weekly at doses of 1.0 to 2.4 mg, induces greater and more sustained weight reduction than liraglutide, thereby providing an opportunity to examine skeletal responses during a phase of active and clinically meaningful weight loss. In a 52-week, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial involving adults without diabetes but with increased baseline fracture risk (mean age 63 years; mean BMI 27.7 kg/m2), semaglutide 1.0 mg weekly resulted in a mean weight reduction of 6.8 kg (−8.8%) compared with placebo [38]. In contrast to the maintenance-phase data observed with liraglutide, semaglutide did not increase the bone formation marker P1NP. The estimated treatment difference (ETD) for PINP at week 52 was not significant (ETD 3.8 μg/L; P=0.418), indicating the absence of a robust anabolic skeletal signal. By comparison, bone resorption increased significantly, with CTX rising by an ETD of 166.4 ng/L versus placebo (P=0.021), corresponding to a relative increase of approximately 55%.

Consistent with this uncoupled remodeling pattern, modest but statistically significant reductions in areal BMD were observed at the lumbar spine (ETD −0.018 g/cm2; P=0.007) and total hip (ETD −0.020 g/cm2; P=0.001) over 52 weeks. Notably, cortical parameters assessed by high-resolution peripheral quantitative computed tomography (HR-pQCT) demonstrated reductions in tibial volumetric BMD and cortical thickness, whereas changes at the non–weight-bearing distal radius were not significant. This site-specific pattern parallels observations in calorie restriction-induced weight loss and supports the concept that mechanical unloading, rather than direct drug-induced osteoanabolism, predominates during the active weight-loss phase. Taken together, these findings suggest that during rapid pharmacologically induced weight reduction, bone resorption may transiently exceed formation.

Importantly, the magnitude of BMD loss (approximately 1%–2% over 1 year) remains substantially smaller than that typically observed after RYGB, where first-year hip BMD reductions of 5%–10% are common [39–42]. Thus, although semaglutide-associated skeletal effects are measurable, they appear quantitatively moderate and largely proportional to the degree of weight loss, rather than indicative of a direct deleterious skeletal action. In the broader context of GLP-1RA therapy, these data reinforce a phase-dependent framework: whereas maintenance-phase liraglutide may permit partial recovery of bone formation, semaglutide during active weight reduction is characterized primarily by resorptive remodeling driven by mechanical unloading. This distinction has practical implications for the timing of bone monitoring and the integration of exercise interventions during intensive pharmacologic weight loss.

Dual agonists and emerging therapies

Dual incretin agonists, particularly tirzepatide (GLP-1/GIP), further expand pharmacologic weight-loss potential. In SURMOUNT-1, tirzepatide achieved weight reductions exceeding 20%, with approximately 25% of total weight loss attributable to lean mass reduction, consistent with the ‘quarter fat-free mass rule’ [10]. Although early trials did not prioritize BMD end-points, the magnitude of weight reduction warrants attention. Large real-world analyses suggest that patients initiating tirzepatide experienced a 44% higher hazard ratio (HR, 1.44) for composite new-onset osteoporosis or initiation of osteoporosis therapy compared with those starting other GLP-1RAs; initiation of osteoporosis therapy alone demonstrated HR 1.61 in some cohorts [43]. Although observational, these findings reinforce the principle that greater weight reduction necessitates enhanced skeletal surveillance.

Emerging triple agonists such as retatrutide, which have produced >24% weight loss in early trials, may intensify these considerations [44]. As pharmacologic efficacy approaches surgical magnitude, prospective evaluation of BMD trajectories, turnover markers, and body composition-adjusted skeletal outcomes is essential.

WEIGHT LOSS AND FRACTURE RISK

Although individuals living with obesity generally exhibit higher BMD at most skeletal sites, this apparent structural advantage does not consistently translate into fracture protection [5,45,46]. Increased fall risk, greater impact forces during falls, and altered body geometry, including coexisting sarcopenia and postural instability, may negate the benefit of higher bone mass [24,47–52]. Notably, approximately 60%–70% of fractures in postmenopausal females with obesity occur despite normal BMD [53]. Prospective cohort studies demonstrate that obesity is associated with site-specific fracture patterns and increased post-fracture mortality [5,54]. Altered fall mechanics, including a tendency toward sideways or backward rather than forward falls, may explain reduced wrist fracture risk but increased humeral and ankle fractures [5,51].

The relationship between weight change and fracture risk is heterogeneous and varies by skeletal site and clinical context [55,56]. In postmenopausal female individuals, weight loss ≥ 5% is associated with increased fracture incidence at the hip, central body, and upper limb, independent of intentionality [55,57]. Among older male individuals, weight loss ≥10% is associated with a 1.6-fold increase in hip, spine, or pelvic fractures [58]. In a Japanese cohort of patients with type 2 diabetes, greater weight loss from maximum body weight was dose-dependently associated with increased fragility fracture risk, with stronger associations in male individuals [59]. Similarly, a Korean cohort of patients with diabetes demonstrated that weight change exceeding 5%, whether gain or loss, was associated with higher hip fracture risk compared with stable weight, suggesting that deviation from long-term weight homeostasis may impair skeletal stability in diabetes [56]. The Look Action for Health in Diabetes (Look AHEAD) trial confirmed these findings in a randomized setting among overweight or obese adults with type 2 diabetes [60]. Participants assigned to intensive lifestyle intervention had a 39% higher risk of frailty fractures, particularly at the hip, pelvis, and humerus, compared with those receiving standard diabetes education [60]. Although exercise is recommended to mitigate weight-loss–associated bone loss, direct evidence demonstrating fracture risk reduction remains limited [61]. The skeletal consequences of MBS are more definitively established. Observational studies and meta-analyses consistently report increased fracture risk following bariatric procedures, especially RYGB, which combines rapid weight loss with nutrient malabsorption [62–64]. Fracture risk increases progressively over time and is most pronounced at non-weight-bearing cortical sites [65–67].

Fracture outcomes associated with GLP-1RAs have been evaluated as safety endpoints in large trials. Meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials in patients with type 2 diabetes show neutral or potentially protective associations between GLP-1RA therapy and fracture risk despite clinically meaningful weight loss [68,69]. A pooled analysis of 38 randomized trials involving nearly 40,000 participants demonstrated significantly lower fracture risk with liraglutide and lixisenatide, particularly with treatment durations >1 year [68]. A Bayesian network meta-analysis including >49,000 individuals found no overall increase in fracture risk with GLP-1RAs, although agent-specific differences were observed [69]. More recent data warrant cautious interpretation in obesity without diabetes [70]. Post-hoc analyses from the Semaglutide Effects on Heart Disease and Stroke in Patients with Overweight or Obesity (SELECT) trial evaluating once-weekly semaglutide 2.4 mg versus placebo in adults with overweight or obesity identified potential subgroup signals [70]. Although overall fracture incidence was similar between groups, numerically higher rates of hip and pelvic fractures were observed among female participants and individuals aged ≥75 years receiving semaglutide [70]. Because these groups already have elevated baseline fracture risk, careful skeletal assessment may be prudent when prescribing high-dose GLP-1RAs for weight reduction in vulnerable populations.

MECHANISTIC INSIGHTS FROM PRECLINICAL EVIDENCE

GLP-1 expression regarding bone homeostasis

Murine studies demonstrate that GLP-1R mRNA and protein are present in multiple skeletal cell populations, including whole bone, bone marrow, primary osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and periosteal cells, with variable expression among osteocytes depending on differentiation stage [71]. GLP-1R expression is absent in early osteocyte-like cells but detectable in mature osteocytes, indicating maturation-dependent regulation of GLP-1 signaling along the osteoblast–osteocyte continuum [71]. GLP-1R expressed in osteoblast-lineage cells is functionally active and regulates intracellular pathways controlling osteoblast proliferation and differentiation [72]. Under metabolic or oxidative stress, including diabetes or exposure to advanced glycation end products (AGEs), GLP-1R activation preserves osteogenic capacity and reduces bone loss [73], supporting a context-dependent role in skeletal homeostasis. GLP-1R expression is also present in thyroid parafollicular C-cells in rodents [74]. However, human and non-human primate thyroid C-cells show minimal or undetectable GLP-1R expression at mRNA and functional levels [74].

Effects of GLP-1RAs on bone in animal models

Animal studies consistently show neutral-to-protective skeletal effects of GLP-1RAs. In estrogen-deficient models, including ovariectomized mice and rats, exenatide, liraglutide, and semaglutide preserved or improved trabecular bone mass and microarchitecture, with limited cortical effects [71,75,76]. Double incretin receptor knockout (DIRKO) mice exhibited increased trabecular bone mass but impaired cortical structure and strength compared with wild-type mice [77]. In obese and type 2 diabetic rodent models, liraglutide and semaglutide improved bone structure and increased bone formation markers by mitigating AGE-related skeletal damage [73,76]. In diet-induced obese mice, semaglutide-induced weight loss did not replicate the trabecular or cortical bone loss observed after sleeve gastrectomy despite comparable weight reduction, indicating short-term bone-sparing effects [78].

Evidence for dual anti-resorptive and pro-anabolic role of GLP-1RA

GLP-1R knockout (KO) mouse models demonstrate that functional GLP-1R signaling is required for normal regulation of bone resorption [79]. GLP-1R KO mice showed increased osteoclast number and activity, while bone formation parameters remained largely unchanged [79]. The GLP-1R KO mice also exhibited elevated urinary deoxypyridinoline (DPD), a biomarker of bone collagen degradation [79]. One key mechanism involves indirect endocrine regulation through thyroid C-cells and calcitonin [79]. In mice and rodents, GLP-1R activation increased calcitonin gene expression, whereas GLP-1R deletion resulted in relative calcitonin deficiency [74,79]. Exogenous calcitonin administration normalized bone resorption markers, including urinary DPD, in GLP-1R–deficient mice [79]. This calcitonin-mediated mechanism appears robust in rodents but has limited translational relevance because human thyroid C-cells express minimal GLP-1R [74,80].

Direct effects of GLP-1RAs on mature bone cells are complex. In vitro, liraglutide and exenatide increased osteoclast number but reduced resorptive activity per osteoclast, resulting in decreased net bone resorption [71]. In type 1 diabetic mice, liraglutide suppressed osteoclastogenesis by downregulating osteoclast-specific genes, including triggering receptor expressed on myeloid cells 2 (Trem2), nuclear factor of activated T cells c1 (Nfatc1), cathepsin K (Ctsk), and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (Trap), independent of glycemic control [81]. In spontaneously diabetic Goto–Kakizaki rats, liraglutide restored trabecular and cortical microarchitecture without excessive osteoclast activation [82].

Preclinical and in vitro evidence indicates direct pro-osteogenic effects of GLP-1RAs on osteoblast-lineage cells. A consistent mechanism is inhibition of osteoblast apoptosis [72]. Liraglutide significantly reduced apoptosis in murine MC3T3-E1 osteoblastic cells [72], associated with increased expression of anti-apoptotic B-cell lymphoma 2 (Bcl-2) and decreased expression of pro-apoptotic bcl-2–associated X protein (Bax) and cleaved caspase-3 [72]. Beyond preserving mature osteoblasts, GLP-1RAs directly promote the generation of new bone-forming cells [75]. Liraglutide and exendin-4 enhance osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow stromal cells (BMSCs) while suppressing adipogenesis in rat models. This effect is evidenced by increased expression of osteoblast transcription factors and markers, including Runx2, ALP, and collagen type I (Col1), along with reduced peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor (PPAR) expression [75]. The phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (Akt)/glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) pathway also mediates bone-protective effects in in vivo semaglutide models [72]. Activation of the PI3K/Akt/GSK3β axis has also been identified as a key mediator of bone-protective effects in in vivo semaglutide models [76]. Exendin-4 is further hypothesized to stimulate preosteoblast proliferation and differentiation through mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathway activation [83]. In human bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells, GLP-1 reduces apoptosis, enhances proliferation and cytoprotection, and inhibits adipogenic differentiation via MAPK and PKC signaling pathways [84].

GLP-1RAs also regulate systemic hormonal pathways involved in bone turnover. GLP-1R activation suppresses osteocyte-derived sclerostin, a potent inhibitor of Wnt-mediated osteogenesis [71,85,86]. Additionally, GLP-1RAs modulate the osteoprotegerin/receptor activator of nuclear factor-κb ligand (OPG/RANKL) system, reducing osteoclastogenesis and bone resorption (Fig. 2) [87,88].

CONCLUSIONS

Weight reduction remains a primary objective in obesity management; however, substantial or rapid weight loss may adversely affect skeletal health. All weight-loss interventions can reduce bone mass, although the magnitude and skeletal distribution vary depending on treatment modality and patient characteristics. Current evidence indicates that GLP-1RAs, particularly when combined with structured exercise, provide a balanced therapeutic approach that supports weight reduction, metabolic improvement, and skeletal preservation. Newer incretin-based therapies, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, produce weight reduction comparable to metabolic surgery. However, their long-term effects on bone health and fracture risk remain insufficiently defined.

Exercise remains the most important intervention for preserving bone during weight loss, while adequate intake of protein, calcium, and vitamin D is essential. Individuals with obesity undergoing active weight reduction, particularly those at high fracture risk, require regular skeletal monitoring, including dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry and, when available, high-resolution peripheral computed tomography. Synthesizing these findings, we propose a comprehensive clinical framework for optimizing skeletal health during obesity management, as outlined in Fig. 3. Future research and clinical strategies should emphasize comprehensive obesity management that extends beyond weight reduction to include preservation of bone mass, bone quality, and skeletal muscle.
