Targeted Delivery of Schisandrin A to Cardiac Endothelium Alleviates Myocardial Ischemia-Reperfusion Injury via Suppression of Ferroptosis
Authors: Xue Guan, Xiaolei Li, Jing Yang, Mi Yang, Pengbo Sun, Ke Li, Yingqiu Ma, Ke Zeng, Ying Zhao, Jingquan Gao, Wei Duan, Hong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMyocardial microvascular injury plays a critical role in myocardial ischemia‑reperfusion injury (MI/RI), and ferroptosis has emerged as an important contributor to cardiac damage. Although Schizandrin A (Sch) possesses cardioprotective effects, its clinical application is limited by poor stability and low bioavailability. This study aimed to develop a CMEC‑targeted delivery system for Sch and evaluate its ability to attenuate ferroptosis in cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs), thereby alleviating MI/RI.MethodsA cardiac endothelium‑targeting peptide CRPPR was conjugated to DSPE‑PEG2000 to prepare a Sch‑loaded nanoparticle (CRPPR@Sch). MI/RI was induced in mice by 45‑minute ischemia followed by 6‑hour reperfusion. CRPPR@Sch, free Sch, or non‑targeted DSP@Sch was administered intravenously for seven consecutive days. Cardiac function, infarct size, microvascular integrity and ferroptosis‑related markers were evaluated in vivo. In vitro, CMECs were exposed to hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R), and ferroptosis was assessed.ResultsCRPPR@Sch efficiently accumulated in cardiac endothelial cells, improved cardiac function, reduced infarct size, restored microvascular perfusion, and alleviated CMEC injury. RNA‑seq indicated that CRPPR@Sch suppressed ferroptosis‑related pathways. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that CRPPR@Sch significantly up‑regulated GPX4 expression, reduced ROS, lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis markers. Moreover, TRIM25 expression was elevated in MI/RI mice and H/R‑injured CMECs. Co‑immunoprecipitation and molecular dynamics simulations revealed that Sch binds to GPX4 and interferes with TRIM25‑mediated GPX4 ubiquitination and degradation.ConclusionCRPPR@Sch effectively delivers Sch to cardiac endothelium, stabilizes GPX4 by disrupting the TRIM25‑GPX4 interaction, and thus inhibits ferroptosis in CMECs, ultimately protecting against myocardial microvascular injury in MI/RI.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a predominant global cause of mortality. Timely restoration of coronary blood flow (reperfusion) is the gold standard therapeutic intervention to salvage the ischemic myocardium. However, this critical process paradoxically incites additional myocardial damage, known as myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MI/RI), a complex pathological process that significantly blunts the clinical benefits of reperfusion therapies.1 The multifaceted pathogenesis of MI/RI involves deleterious events such as calcium overload, oxidative stress, inflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction, which collectively culminate in the death of cardiomyocytes and cardiac microvascular endothelial cells (CMECs).2 Among these, CMECs, which constitute the most abundant cell type in the heart and maintain microcirculatory integrity, are exceptionally susceptible to ischemia/hypoxia-reoxygenation injury. Their dysfunction is now recognized as a pivotal contributor to myocardial “no-reflow” and contractile failure post-MI.3 Therefore, developing strategies to specifically protect CMECs during MI/RI represents a promising yet underexplored therapeutic frontier.

Targeting specific cellular components within the heart has emerged as a key strategy in cardioprotective drug development. In recent years, considerable effort has been directed towards cardiac-targeted therapies, particularly those aiming to enhance drug delivery to ischemic myocardium or modulate key pathogenic pathways. For instance, antibody-drug conjugates and nanoparticle-based systems coupled with peptides (like CRPPR) or small molecules that recognize receptors enriched in cardiac tissues (eg, angiotensin-converting enzyme, ACE) have shown promise in preclinical models for delivering therapeutics specifically to the heart.4,5 Furthermore, numerous mechanisms have been targeted to mitigate MI/RI, including interventions that enhance endogenous antioxidant defenses (like Nrf2 activation), inhibit inflammation (via IL-1β blockade), or preserve mitochondrial function (through cyclosporine A analogues targeting the mitochondrial permeability transition pore, mPTP).6,7 Despite these advances, therapies explicitly designed to shield the vulnerable microvascular endothelium from ferroptosis remain limited.

The pathophysiology of MI/RI is intimately linked to various regulated cell death pathways. Recent evidence highlights the crucial role of ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of non-apoptotic regulated cell death initiated by excessive phospholipid peroxidation.8,9 The cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis is largely governed by the antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), which is the central regulator that reduces lipid hydroperoxides to harmless alcohols. Notably, mounting studies have demonstrated that GPX4 expression and activity are compromised in various cardiac pathologies including MI/RI, leading to cardiomyocyte and endothelial cell death via ferroptosis.10 Moreover, the tripartite motif-containing protein 25 (TRIM25), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, has been identified as a potent driver of ferroptosis by mediating the K48-linked ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of GPX4 in certain cancer cells.11,12 However, the potential involvement of the TRIM25-GPX4 axis in mediating endothelial ferroptosis in MI/RI and its amenability to therapeutic intervention remain largely unknown. Consequently, targeting this specific molecular cascade to inhibit CMECs ferroptosis presents a novel and potent strategy for alleviating microvascular damage in MI/RI.

Natural products, particularly bioactive compounds derived from Traditional Chinese Medicine, serve as a treasure trove for novel drug discovery. Fructus Schisandrae has been widely used for treating various ailments including palpitations and insomnia.13 Among its diverse lignans, Schisandrin A (Sch) possesses documented therapeutic potential in I/R injuries, offering protection to the liver and brain.14–16 Specifically for the heart, a study reported that Sch pretreatment can ameliorate MI/RI-induced cardiac damage, as evidenced by reduced infarct size and attenuated myocardial apoptosis, albeit its underlying mechanisms were not fully elucidated.17 Crucially, a major challenge limiting Sch’s clinical translation is its poor water solubility, chemical instability, and consequently, low systemic bioavailability.18 These intrinsic physicochemical flaws necessitate the design of a targeted delivery system capable of circumventing these limitations and shuttling the therapeutic agent specifically to the injured cardiac endothelium. Integrating such a targeted delivery platform for Sch with the evolving understanding of cardiac-targeting mechanisms could overcome its pharmacokinetic barriers and allow precise interrogation of its effects on the CMECs.

Advances in nanoparticle-based targeted delivery provide a viable solution. Specifically, peptide-directed targeting has shown immense promise in enhancing drug accumulation in desired tissues. For instance, the cell-penetrating peptide CRPPR exhibits selective binding affinity towards cardiac endothelial cells, offering a molecular key for CMEC-selective drug delivery. Building on this foundation, we hypothesized that conjugating this cardiac-homing peptide to a nanoparticle carrier could create a targeted vehicle (CRPPR@Sch) for the effective delivery of Sch to diseased CMECs, thereby overcoming its pharmacokinetic limitations and potentially revealing its detailed mechanism of action.

Taken together, the current understanding links microvascular injury in MI/RI to endothelial ferroptosis. Sch holds promise but suffers from poor delivery. The unresolved pivotal questions are: 1) Does the TRIM25-GPX4 axis drive CMEC ferroptosis in MI/RI? 2) Could Sch antagonize this process? 3) Can a CRPPR-conjugated nanoparticle effectively deliver Sch to cardiac endothelium in vivo? To address these interconnected questions, this study aimed to 1) engineer a CMEC-targeted nanoplatform (CRPPR@Sch), 2) evaluate its therapeutic efficacy in murine and cellular MI/RI models, and 3) systematically investigate whether its cardioprotective effect is mechanistically rooted in disrupting the TRIM25-GPX4 interaction, thus stabilizing GPX4 and ultimately inhibiting ferroptosis in CMECs. The successful validation of our hypothesis would elucidate a novel molecular pathway of endothelial damage while providing a rationale for a Sch-based targeted therapy against MI/RI.

Materials and Methods

Animal Experiment

Male C57BL/6 mice (16–20 g, 6–8 weeks old) were randomly divided into five groups: (1) sham group, subjected to sham surgery and treated with saline (tail vein injection); (2) MI/RI group, subjected to MI/RI and treated with saline; (3) MI/RI + Sch group; (4) MI/RI + DSP@Sch group; (5) MI/RI + CRPPR@Sch group. Sch was administered at a dose of 20 mg/kg via tail vein injection for groups 3–5. In our study, the 20 mg/kg dose specified for all treatment groups (free Sch, non-targeted DSP@Sch, and targeted CRPPR@Sch) is the equivalent dose calculated based on the pure mass of the active ingredient Sch. During surgery, an animal ventilator maintained respiratory function. MI/RI was induced by ligating the left anterior descending (LAD) coronary artery. A left thoracotomy was performed through a horizontal incision at the third or fourth intercostal space. The LAD was ligated with a 6–0 silk suture placed around a PE-10 tube. Successful occlusion was confirmed by ST-segment elevation on the electrocardiogram. After 45 minutes of ischemia, the PE-10 tube was removed to allow reperfusion for 6 hours. Groups receiving Sch, DSP@Sch, or CRPPR@Sch were treated with their respective interventions once daily for 7 consecutive days. Therapeutic efficacy was evaluated through blinded analysis of morphology, transthoracic echocardiography, and biomarkers. Animals were housed in standard plastic enclosures under controlled conditions (temperature: 20–26 °C; humidity: 50–60%) with alternating 12-hour light/dark cycles, and had ad libitum access to food and water. All experimental procedures were approved by the Ethics Committees of Harbin Medical University and complied with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals.

Isolation and Culture of CMECs

CMECs were isolated from the left ventricles of male C57BL/6 mice (9–12 g, 3–4 weeks old) as previously described. Briefly, the left ventricular tissue, after removal of epicardial coronary arteries and endocardial endothelium, was minced into approximately 1 mm3 fragments, digested with collagenase II, and cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1% vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), 1% endothelial cell growth supplement (ECGS), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and 100 mg/ml penicillin/streptomycin.

H/R-Induced CMEC Preparation

An in vitro MI/RI model was established by exposing CMECs to 12 hours of hypoxia (5% CO2, 95% N2 at 37 °C), followed by 24 hours of reoxygenation under normoxic conditions. The cells were then divided into five experimental groups: (1) Control group, cultured under normoxic conditions; (2) H/R group, subjected to hypoxia and reoxygenation without treatment; (3–5) H/R + CRPPR@Sch group, exposed to hypoxia and reoxygenation, followed by treatment with 5 µM, 10 µM, or 20 µM Sch for 48 hours.

Nanoparticle Preparation and Characterization

Soy lecithin, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG2K, DSPE-PEG2K-CRPPR (Xi’an Ruixi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China), and Sch (CAS No.: 61281–38-7, purity ≥ 98%, MedChemExpress, China) were co-dissolved in 6 mL of chloroform, then evaporated to form a thin lipid film. Nanoparticles were obtained by ultrasonication followed by extrusion through a polycarbonate membrane (pore size: 200 nm). Unloaded Sch was removed by filtration using the same membrane. The morphological characteristics and particle size were analyzed using electron microscopy (EM), while zeta potential and size distribution were determined using a dynamic light scattering (DLS) detector (Zetasizer-ZS90). The intracellular uptake of CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch by CMECs was observed via fluorescence microscopy.

Echocardiographic Assessment

Electrocardiography (ECG) was conducted as previously described.19 Following anesthesia with isoflurane and chest hair removal using depilatory cream, ultrasound gel was applied to the chest of mice, and short-axis M-mode images at the midventricular level were acquired to measure left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and fractional shortening (LVFS).

Triphenyltetrazolium Chloride (TTC) Staining

To assess myocardial infarct size, mice were euthanized after treatment with Sch, CRPPR@Sch, or DSP@Sch, and their hearts were rapidly excised. The hearts were frozen at −20 °C for 25 minutes, then sectioned into five transverse slices and stained with 2% TTC at 37 °C for 15 minutes in the dark.20

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) Staining

For histological analysis, heart tissues were paraffin-embedded and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin (Baso, BA-4041) for 5 minutes, followed by a 30-second rinse in tap water. Differentiation was performed with 1% hydrochloric acid in 70% alcohol for 20–30 seconds to remove unbound dye, after which the sections were rinsed in tap water for 10–15 minutes for nuclear bluing. Sections were then counterstained with eosin (Baso, BA-4024) for 1 minute, washed, dehydrated through a graded alcohol series, cleared in xylene (two changes, 10 minutes each), and mounted with neutral balsam.

Masson Staining

Masson’s trichrome staining was performed to evaluate collagen fiber deposition in cardiac tissue. Paraffin-embedded sections (4–5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded ethanol series (100%, 90%, 80%, and 70%), and then subjected to sequential staining for nuclei, cytoplasm, and collagen, followed by differentiation, acid washing, and dehydration. Stained sections were examined under light microscopy, where collagen fibers appeared blue, muscle fibers and cytoplasm stained red, and nuclei were stained dark blue/black. Collagen accumulation was quantified by image analysis.

Laser Speckle Contrast Analysis (LASCA) for Evaluating Blood Flow in Infarcted Tissues

LASCA was performed using a Moor FLPI-2 real-time blood flow zoom laser speckle imaging system (Moor Instruments, UK) to evaluate the spatial vascular profile and flow velocity within the infarcted region.

Gelatin-Ink Staining

To assess microvascular patency, a gelatin-ink mixture (3% gelatin in ink) was infused into the heart via the jugular vein at room temperature (30 °C). The hearts were then excised and stored at 4 °C for 1 hour. After fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, the samples were cryosectioned and examined under a light microscope.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase-Mediated Deoxyuridine Triphosphate Nick-End Labeling (TUNEL) Staining

Cardiac sections were subjected to TUNEL staining using the BrightGreen Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1088, Beyotime, China), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The sections were counterstained with DAPI (C1006, Beyotime, China) and examined under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) to assess apoptosis. The apoptotic rate was calculated as the percentage of TUNEL-positive nuclei relative to the total number of nuclei counterstained with DAPI.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Tissue specimens were collected, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned to 5 μm thickness. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0), preheated via microwave treatment for 5 minutes, followed by a 15-minute immersion. For cell cultures, PBS rinsing was performed three times, followed by fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for membrane permeabilization. The specimens were blocked with goat serum for 60 minutes at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD31 (A19014, Abclonal, China, 1:100 dilution) and 4-HNE (68538-1-Ig, Proteintech, China, 1:100 dilution). The following day, after removal of primary antibodies, corresponding secondary antibodies were applied for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark. The procedure concluded with DAPI nuclear staining, and images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated from CMECs and cardiac tissues using TRIzol reagent (Pufei Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) and reverse transcribed into cDNA. RNA purity was assessed by UV spectrophotometry, and reverse transcription was performed using the PrimeScript™ RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR was carried out using SYBR Green (Takara, Dalian, China), with GAPDH as the internal control. Primer sequences used were as follows: GPX4 Forward: CGCCAAAGTCCTAGGAAACG; Reverse: AACTCGGAGCTGTTGCAGTA; ptgs2 Forward: AAGCCTTCTCCAACCTCTCC; Reverse: GCTGGGCAAAGAATGCAAAC. TRIM21 Forward: TCAGACCACCCTCCACATTC; Reverse: TGAAGAAAGGTCGCAGAGGT. TRIM25 Forward: GGAACAGCTGAGACAGGAGT; Reverse: ATGAGCTCAACCTCTGCCTT. TRIM26 Forward: AAGCCAAGGGAGAAGCTGAT; Reverse: ATTCCGCCACAATGTACTGC. TRIM32 Forward: TCGGAAGTTCTTCACAGGCT; Reverse: CTCCAGGAGGGCTACATCAG. Data analysis was performed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.21

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Whole blood was collected from mice and maintained at room temperature in test tubes for 30 minutes. Plasma and supernatants were separated by centrifugation (6000 rpm, 15 minutes, 4 °C), rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for downstream analysis. Plasma concentrations of IL-1β (EM0029, HUABIO), IL-6 (EM0004, HUABIO), and IL-18 (EM0034, HUABIO) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell Viability Assay

CMECs were seeded into 96-well plates, and cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo Molecular Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western Blot Analysis

Protein was extracted from myocardial tissue specimens and cultured CMECs using RIPA lysis buffer for Western blot analysis. Protein samples (50 μg) were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against TRIM25 (A12938, Abclonal, China, 1:1000 dilution) and GPX4 (sc-166437, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA, 1:1000 dilution). Enhanced chemiluminescence reagents were used for band visualization, which was captured using an Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences).

Electron Microscopy

After fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (pH 7.4, Spi-Chem) for 2 hours, samples were washed and post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide at 4 °C for 2 hours. Following dehydration through a graded ethanol series, the specimens were embedded in Epon-Araldite resin, polymerized, and sectioned. Ultrathin sections were counterstained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate before being examined under a transmission electron microscope.

Co-Immunoprecipitation Assays (Co-IP)

Co-IP assays were conducted as previously described.22 Briefly, cell lysates were incubated overnight with either control IgG or target-specific antibodies at 4 °C. Immune complexes were then captured with Protein G Sepharose beads for 4 hours at 4 °C. After washing, the immunoprecipitated complexes were subjected to SDS-PAGE and analyzed by Western blotting using the appropriate primary and secondary antibodies.

Determination of JC-1 Monomer, ROS, Malondialdehyde (MDA), and Glutathione (GSH) Levels

JC-1 monomer (C2003S, Beyotime), ROS (S0033S, Beyotime), MDA, and GSH levels were measured according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Nitric Oxide (NO) Measurement

Nitric oxide (NO) concentrations were assessed by quantifying nitrite and nitrate levels, the stable end-products of NO degradation, using the Griess reaction-based NO assay kit (Calbiochem).

RNA-Seq Data Analysis

RNA was extracted from tissues using the TRIzol reagent kit (Ambion, Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A total of 1 μg of RNA per sample was used as input for RNA sample preparation. RNA-Seq reads were adaptor-trimmed, and data quality was assessed using the fastp (v 0.23.2) software before applying filtering criteria. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was conducted using clusterProfiler (v 4.2.0), with gene length bias correction. GO terms with a corrected P-value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was also performed using clusterProfiler. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) analysis of differentially expressed genes was based on the STRING database (v 11.5), which includes known and predicted protein-protein interactions.

siRNA Transfection

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) sequences were synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai Gene Pharma, China). The sequences for TRIM25 siRNA are as follows: Forward: CCUGGAGUACAAACUGAGGAATT, Reverse: UUCCUCAGUUUGUACUCCAGGTT. The sequences for GPX4 siRNA are: Forward: GGAGCCAGGAAGUAAUCAAGATT, Reverse: UCUUGAUUACUUCCUGGCUCCTT.

Molecular Docking

Molecular docking was performed using the HDOCK online server (http://hdock.phys.hust.edu.cn/) to predict the interaction between GPX4-5L71 and NF-TRIM25-4B8E. The docking process utilized a fast Fourier transform (FFT)-based global search algorithm to explore potential binding modes, followed by evaluation with a knowledge-based scoring function. The top 10 docking poses were visually inspected, and the top 100 results were made available for download. The complex with the most favorable docking score was selected for further interaction analysis using PyMOL.

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations were carried out with Amber 24 to assess binding stability. Systems were prepared with LEaP, using ff14SB and GAFF force fields for proteins and compounds, respectively, and solvated in a TIP3P water box with 10.0 Å padding and neutralizing ions. A two-stage energy minimization was performed, initially with restraints on protein backbones, followed by minimization without restraints. The systems were gradually heated to 300.0 K and equilibrated under constant pressure. Production runs of 100 ns were conducted under NPT conditions. Trajectories were analyzed using CPPTRAJ to compute RMSD, Rg, SASA, RMSF, and hydrogen bonds. Binding free energies were estimated via MM/GBSA using the last 1 ns of trajectories.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 9.2.0). Statistical significance between two groups was evaluated using a two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test. For multiple-group comparisons, normality (Shapiro–Wilk test) and homogeneity of variances (Levene’s test) were first confirmed. Then, either one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction was applied. Sample sizes (n) represent biological replicates, each derived from an independent animal or distinct cell preparation. Each group included a minimum of three replicates, and each assay was performed in at least three independent experiments. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05, with P-values reported as exact values or using standardized symbols (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001).

Results

Preparation and Characterization of CRPPR@Sch

In vitro screening studies have previously identified the short linear peptide CRPPR as specifically binding to cardiac endothelium.23 Building on this, the potential of CRPPR to selectively deliver Sch to cardiac endothelial cells was investigated. CRPPR was covalently conjugated to DSPE-PEG2000 to generate CRPPR@Sch for CMEC-targeted delivery. A schematic illustration of the CRPPR@Sch synthesis strategy is shown in Figure 1A. EM analysis revealed that CRPPR@Sch nanoparticles exhibited a uniform, spherical morphology with a mean diameter of approximately 121 nm (Figure 1B and C). Fluorescence spectral analysis indicated that both CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch (lacking CRPPR) displayed strong fluorescence intensity (Figure 1D). The zeta potentials of CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch were measured using dynamic laser scattering, yielding values of −25.32 ± 4.26 mV and −22.62 ± 4.82 mV, respectively (Figure 1E). Subsequently, the in vivo nanoparticle delivery efficiency was assessed. CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch nanoparticles were intravenously administered to mice, and 2 hours post-injection, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidneys) were harvested for fluorescence intensity evaluation using in vivo imaging system (Figure 1F). The data showed that elevated fluorescence signals were detected in the heart of mice treated with CRPPR@Sch compared to DSP@Sch (Figure 1G). We also performed a parallel analysis of the distribution of CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch in the liver, spleen, and kidneys. Our results revealed that the fluorescence intensity of DSP@Sch in the liver was higher than that of CRPPR@Sch, while no significant difference in fluorescence intensity was observed between the two nanoparticles in the spleen and kidneys (Figure 1H–J). Notably, CRPPR@Sch showed significantly greater accumulation in CMECs compared to DSP@Sch (Figure 1K and L). To assess the potential in vivo toxicity of CRPPR@Sch, histopathological changes in the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were evaluated using HE staining. The results indicated that CRPPR@Sch exhibited no observable toxic effects on any of the examined organs (Figure 1M). Additionally, serum levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-18) were measured in normal mice pretreated with CRPPR@Sch and DSP@Sch. CRPPR@Sch Protected the Myocardium Against I/R Injury

Cardiac function indicators revealed that treatment with CRPPR@Sch-20 (20 mg/kg Sch) significantly improved LVFS and LVEF compared to the model group (Figure 2A–C). Consequently, CRPPR@Sch-20 was selected for subsequent experiments. Furthermore, none of the nanoparticle preparations had any effect on the heart rate of the mice (Figure 2D). To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of Sch (20 mg/kg), DSP@Sch, and CRPPR@Sch, each formulation was administered to mice subjected to MI/RI. Histological analysis using TTC and HE staining confirmed a substantial reduction in infarct size in hearts treated with CRPPR@Sch (Figure 2E–G). Moreover, Masson’s trichrome staining indicated reduced myocardial damage following CRPPR@Sch treatment (Figure 2H). TUNEL immunofluorescence showed a significant increase in apoptotic cells following MI/RI, which was markedly reversed by CRPPR@Sch treatment (Figure 2I and J). CRPPR@Sch Improved Cardiac Microvascular Function

TEM analysis revealed that mitochondria in the MI/RI group exhibited atrophy and reduced cristae, indicative of mitochondrial dysfunction. In contrast, CRPPR@Sch treatment effectively ameliorated these morphological abnormalities (Figure 3A). Restoration of blood flow and revascularization are critical indicators of therapeutic efficacy in infarcted tissue. To assess these parameters, LASCA was employed to evaluate spatial vascular profiles and flow velocity within the infarcted regions. Consistent with the injury model, hearts subjected to MI/RI exhibited significant blood flow obstruction, as shown in Figure 3B and C. While Sch and DSP@Sch demonstrated limited effects, CRPPR@Sch treatment significantly enhanced blood flow recovery following MI/RI. To assess whether this benefit was due to improved microvascular integrity, gelatin-ink microvascular imaging was conducted. MI/RI-induced hearts showed pronounced linear and irregular microvascular filling defects, which were markedly reversed by CRPPR@Sch treatment (Figure 3D and E). This was further supported by CD31 immunofluorescence staining, which revealed substantial loss of CMECs in the MI/RI group. CRPPR@Sch treatment not only increased CMEC count but also restored their morphological integrity (Figure 3F and G). Additionally, NO content, an indicator of endothelial function, was significantly elevated in the CRPPR@Sch-treated groups compared to the MI/RI group (Figure 3H). These results demonstrate that CRPPR@Sch enhances cardiac microvascular function impaired by MI/RI. Corroborating the RNA-seq results, MI/RI injury caused a significant reduction in both GPX4 protein and mRNA expression, a decrease that was notably reversed following CRPPR@Sch treatment (Figure 5A–C). As shown in Figure 5D and E, only CRPPR@Sch significantly reduced ROS levels. To further explore the ferroptosis in endothelial tissue, CMECs and lipid peroxidation were assessed using CD31 and 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) staining, supplemented with quantitative measurements of MDA and GSH. This analysis demonstrated that CRPPR@Sch effectively suppressed MI/RI-induced lipid peroxide production (Figure 5F–I). Additionally, qRT-PCR analysis revealed that CRPPR@Sch significantly inhibited ptgs2 mRNA expression (Figure 5J). These results suggest that CRPPR@Sch reduces ROS levels and mitigates CMEC ferroptosis, thereby exerting cardioprotective effects in MI/RI. Therapeutic Effects of CRPPR@Sch on CMECs Exposed to H/R

Although CRPPR@Sch has been shown to possess antioxidant properties and inhibit ROS production, the underlying molecular mechanism remains to be clarified. To address this, the optimal concentration of CRPPR@Sch in CMECs exposed to H/R was first determined. CCK-8 assays and NO detection indicated that 20 µM CRPPR@Sch produced the most favorable effects (Figure 6A–C). Western blot analysis revealed that CRPPR@Sch significantly upregulated GPX4 expression (Figure 6D and E). Next, intracellular ROS and 4-HNE production were assessed in CMECs. Notably, CRPPR@Sch markedly reduced ROS and 4-HNE production in CMECs exposed to H/R (Figure 6F–I). To evaluate the impact of CRPPR@Sch on mitochondrial dysfunction, changes in mitochondrial membrane potential were analyzed using the JC-1 probe. In the H/R group, a marked increase in JC-1 monomer fluorescence was observed in CMECs, indicative of mitochondrial membrane depolarization. This dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential was significantly attenuated by CRPPR@Sch treatment, suggesting a protective effect on mitochondrial integrity (Figure 6J). Furthermore, in contrast to the H/R group, the CRPPR@Sch group exhibited a significant decrease in MDA and ptgs2 levels, along with a concurrent increase in GSH levels (Figure 6K–M). Overall, these results confirm that CRPPR@Sch protects CMECs against H/R injury by improving mitochondrial function and inhibiting ferroptosis. TRIM25 Knockdown Inhibited the Ferroptosis of H/R-Induced CMECs

Inhibition of GPX4 is considered the primary mechanism driving ferroptosis, distinct from other contributing pathways such as ROS elevation or increased intracellular iron levels.26,27 Moreover, lysosomal activation has been implicated in mobilizing iron and subsequently inducing ferroptosis.28 Recent studies have shown that TRIM family proteins play critical roles in mediating ubiquitination, leading to lysosomal degradation of protein including GPX4.29–31 Therefore, we performed quantitative real-time PCR to detect changes in mRNA expression of TRIM21, TRIM25, TRIM26, and TRIM32 across both in vivo and in vitro. Our results showed that only TRIM21 and TRIM25 exhibited significantly upregulated expression in the model groups, with TRIM25 showing the most prominent change. This suggests that TRIM25 may participate in the degradation of GPX4 protein during MI/RI. Accordingly, we selected TRIM25 as the focus of our subsequent investigation (Figure 7A and B). To explore this mechanism in the context of MI/RI, this study assessed TRIM25 protein expression in myocardial tissues and CMECs and found that CRPPR@Sch did not affect TRIM25 levels (Figure 7C–F). To further investigate the relationship between TRIM25 and GPX4 in MI/RI, additional experiments were conducted. Immunoprecipitation (IP) assays revealed a clear interaction between TRIM25 and GPX4 (Figure 7G). Having established this interaction, this study further examined whether CRPPR@Sch could improve mitochondrial function by disrupting the TRIM25-GPX4 binding. Molecular docking studies of the TRIM25-GPX4 complex yielded a docking score of −253.01 (Figure 7H), confirming the binding relationship between TRIM25 and GPX4. Additionally, TRIM25 knockdown significantly increased GPX4 levels compared to H/R-induced CMECs (Figure 7I–N). To confirm that CRPPR@Sch’s protective effects were mediated through TRIM25 and GPX4, simultaneous knockdown of both proteins was performed. TRIM25 knockdown enhanced cell viability and NO levels, but these effects were abolished by further knockdown of GPX4 (Figure 7O and P). Furthermore, simultaneous knockdown of TRIM25 and GPX4 reversed the effect of TRIM25 silencing on oxidative stress indicators (Figure 7Q and R). Fluorescence analysis of ROS and 4-HNE revealed that TRIM25 knockdown reduced their expression, an effect that was negated by concurrent GPX4 knockdown (Figure 7S–V). Consistent with these findings, silencing TRIM25 decreased JC-1 monomers and ptgs2 levels, whereas simultaneous silencing of GPX4 counteracted these reductions (Figure 7W and X). These data demonstrate that TRIM25 induces mitochondrial dysfunction and ferroptosis by promoting the degradation of GPX4. Molecular Dynamics Analysis of Sch and GPX4

Molecular docking revealed a potential interaction between Sch and GPX4, yielding a docking score of −6.019 kcal/mol, with the compound forming a hydrogen bond with SER18 of the protein (Figure 8A and B). The free energy decomposition diagram of the GPX4-Sch complex is shown in Figure 8C. To assess the hydrogen bonding properties at the binding sites, the number of key hydrogen bonds stabilizing the interaction between the ligand and protein was calculated. The results indicated that the number of hydrogen bonds fluctuated slightly, remaining stable at 1–2 throughout the process, suggesting a relatively stable hydrophilic interaction between the two (Figure 8D). Root mean square deviation (RMSD) is a key parameter for evaluating the stability of the GPX4-Sch complex. The RMSD curve of the complex showed minimal fluctuation, remaining largely within 2 Å, indicating high stability (Figure 8E–G). Solvent accessible surface area (SASA) measures the contact area between the protein surface and the solvent, which is an important parameter for understanding protein stability, interactions, and folding. The results revealed a significant increase in SASA values around 60 ns and 90 ns, indicating enhanced interaction between the molecular surface and solvent, potentially influencing solubility, reactivity, and biological activity (Figure 8H). The radius of gyration (Rg) is a physical parameter that reflects the compactness of a protein structure; smaller Rg values indicate denser and more stable protein structures. The Rg curve calculated from molecular dynamics simulations shows that the Rg of the GPX4-Sch complex remains stable with minor fluctuations throughout the process, consistent with the RMSD curve. Slight fluctuations were observed towards the end of the simulation (approximately 90 ns), which aligned with the SASA analysis, suggesting that the overall stability of the complex was maintained (Figure 8I). This study identified stable conformations of the complex within the specific ranges of RMSD and Rg by analyzing the free energy landscape, providing a thermodynamic foundation for understanding its binding mechanism and structural dynamics (Figure 8J and K). Additionally, Co-IP results revealed minimal expression of GPX4 after CRPPR@Sch treatment (Figure 8L). In summary, these results suggest that CRPPR@Sch exerts its anti-ferroptotic effects by binding to GPX4, thereby preventing its degradation by TRIM25. Discussion

MI/RI most commonly occurs in the context of ischemic heart disease, with atherosclerosis as the primary underlying cause.32 While early reperfusion achieved through coronary angioplasty, coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), thrombolytic therapy, or primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PPCI) effectively limits acute MI morbidity, patient outcomes remain suboptimal.33 Despite extensive research focused on cardiomyocyte protection, significant gaps remain in the development of protective strategies for microvascular endothelial cells, which are essential for maintaining microcirculation and tissue perfusion during MI/RI.

This study, for the first time, reveals that Sch, a natural lignan compound derived from the traditional Chinese medicine Schisandra chinensis, significantly inhibits ferroptosis by targeting the TRIM25-GPX4 signaling axis. This protective effect on the microvascular endothelium in MI/RI not only provides new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying microvascular injury in MI/RI but also opens a potential avenue for developing natural product-based cardioprotective therapies. Our finding that ferroptosis plays a key pathogenic role in CMECs aligns with the established involvement of ferroptosis in cardiomyocyte death during MI/RI, but crucially extends the scope by defining this cell death mechanism in the microvascular compartment.

Members of the tripartite motif-containing (TRIM) protein family are characterized by a conserved N-terminal RING domain, with most members exhibiting E3 ubiquitin ligase activity. Growing evidence suggests that TRIM family proteins play critical roles in various physiological and pathological processes, including innate immunity, tumorigenesis, and neurodegenerative diseases, primarily through the regulation of protein quality control.34–37 For example, TRIM19 facilitates the clearance of nuclear misfolded proteins via sequential SUMOylation and ubiquitination, while TRIM11 efficiently eliminates both nuclear and cytoplasmic misfolded proteins and has been implicated in tumorigenesis.37–39 Distinct members of TRIM exert opposing effects on ferroptosis through differential ubiquitination of GPX4. Specifically, TRIM26 promotes K63-linked ubiquitin chain formation, stabilizing GPX4 and suppressing ferroptosis,40 whereas TRIM25 facilitates K48-linked ubiquitination, leading to GPX4 proteasomal degradation and subsequent ferroptosis induction.41 While the oncogenic role of TRIM25 in degrading GPX4 to induce ferroptosis has been documented in pancreatic cancer, our study is the first to report this specific ubiquitination-degradation axis within the cardiovascular system, linking a known mechanistic pathway from cancer biology to the pathophysiology of MI/RI in endothelial cells. Recent studies have established a connection between ferroptosis and various pathological conditions. Dysregulated ferroptosis, particularly its suppression, has been implicated in tumor development and progression.42,43 While ferroptosis serves essential physiological functions, its excessive activation has been linked to numerous pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases, I/R-induced organ injury, and infectious diseases.44,45 Importantly, accumulating evidence highlights the critical role of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of several cardiovascular diseases.46,47 Furthermore, mitochondrial overexpression of GPX4 has been shown to attenuate I/R injury and reduce lipid peroxidation,48 suggesting a mechanistic link to ferroptosis. Consistent with this, recent studies have revealed that cardiac I/R injury disrupts the GPX4 system at both expression and functional levels, promoting the accumulation of lipid peroxidation products—such as oxidized phosphatidylcholines—and ultimately inducing cardiomyocyte ferroptosis.49,50 The present study demonstrates that TRIM25 initiates the process of ferroptosis in MI/RI-induced CMECs by binding to GPX4 and triggering its degradation.

Sch, the primary bioactive lignan isolated from FSC, exhibits diverse pharmacological activities.51 This compound demonstrates high solubility in benzene, chloroform, and acetone, moderate solubility in methanol and ethanol, and is insoluble in petroleum ether and water. Notably, intraperitoneal administration of Sch has been shown to limit infarct size in mice with acute myocardial infarction,52 indicating a direct cardioprotective effect on cardiomyocytes. This is in line with the reported anti-apoptotic and anti-inflammatory effects of Sch against I/R injury in various organs, including the brain and liver. However, the specific molecular targets and mechanisms through which Sch protects the cardiac microvascular endothelium remained incompletely defined. To address this gap, the present study investigated whether Sch exerts protective effects against MI/RI. Our results suggest that targeted delivery of Sch to damaged CMECs reduces oxidative stress, inhibits ferroptosis, and effectively mitigates MI/RI.

Recent advancements in bioimaging and targeted delivery systems have become a major focus in disease treatment research.53,54 Given that Sch is insoluble in water and has low bioavailability, CRPPR@Sch was designed as a non-invasive, targeted treatment for MI/RI. This targeted strategy builds on prior research identifying the CRPPR peptide as a ligand with affinity for cardiac endothelium, confirming its utility in vivo and illustrating the potential of peptide-guided nanotherapeutics to address the delivery challenges of natural products. CRPPR@Sch selectively accumulated in the injured myocardium and was internalized by CMECs, thereby exerting therapeutic effects against MI/RI. Under normal physiological conditions, a dynamic equilibrium exists between endogenous antioxidants—such as superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), glutathione (GSH), glutathione peroxidase, glutathione transferase, and the heme oxygenase/bilirubin system—and ROS, including oxygen radicals, hypochlorite, and ozone. Disruption of this balance leads to oxidative stress and subsequent cellular damage.55 Our findings indicate that CRPPR@Sch treatment reduces MDA levels in CMECs, increases GSH activity, and significantly decreases ROS and 4-HNE expression. These results suggest that CRPPR@Sch alleviates MI/RI by inhibiting oxidative stress. Importantly, our data directly correlate this alleviation of oxidative stress and inhibition of ferroptosis with the observed improvements in microvascular perfusion, thus robustly linking the biochemical findings to a functional therapeutic outcome relevant to the study’s objective of alleviating microvascular injury. One of the key mechanisms identified in this study is the binding of TRIM25 to GPX4, which promotes the degradation of GPX4. Inhibition of GPX4 is the primary mechanism driving ferroptosis.25 Molecular docking and Co-IP assays demonstrated that Sch can stably bind to GPX4, preventing its degradation and thereby alleviating oxidative stress and inhibiting ferroptosis in CMECs. These findings demonstrate that CRPPR@Sch exerts cardioprotective effects by competitively binding to GPX4, suppressing TRIM25-induced GPX4 degradation, and ultimately inhibiting ferroptosis. This strategy offers a promising therapeutic framework for the application of Sch in the management of MI/RI.

While this study establishes a clear mechanistic link, several limitations and future directions require consideration. First, the pharmacokinetic profile, optimal therapeutic window, and long-term safety of Sch in preclinical models need systematic evaluation. Additionally, allergic reactions and other complications arising during anesthesia, such as surgical traction, could induce myocardial ischemia. Preventive administration of Sch may be designed to alleviate MI/RI, but due to the inherent uncertainty of such preventive strategies, further exploration is necessary before clinical application. Lastly, the potential interactions between this pathway and other cardioprotective signals (eg, RISK, SAFE pathways) should be explored, along with the possibility of combining Sch with existing therapies, such as reperfusion agents or antiplatelet drugs, to achieve synergistic effects.

In conclusion, our study reveals the TRIM25-GPX4 regulatory axis as a critical driver of microvascular endothelial ferroptosis in MI/RI. Furthermore, Sch functions as a novel targeted therapeutic that disrupts this axis by competitively binding to GPX4. This interaction stabilizes GPX4 and offers significant protection against I/R-induced microvascular damage. The core mechanistic insight from this work is the elucidation of Sch’s unique mode of action. Unlike conventional antioxidants that merely scavenge ROS, this study employed molecular docking and Co-IP to uncover Sch’s role as a competitive molecular interceptor. It directly binds to a specific site on the GPX4 protein, overlapping or allosterically linked to the TRIM25-binding interface. This binding physically prevents TRIM25 from accessing and ubiquitinating GPX4, thus shielding GPX4 from TRIM25-mediated degradation. By maintaining GPX4 protein stability and enzymatic activity during I/R insult, Sch preserves redox balance, prevents the accumulation of toxic lipid peroxides (eg, 4-HNE, MDA), and blocks ferroptosis in CMECs. Collectively, these findings successfully address the two primary aims of this research: (1) to develop and validate a CMEC-targeted nanoparticle (CRPPR@Sch) capable of effectively delivering Sch, and (2) to uncover the underlying molecular mechanism of protection, which we have identified as the competitive disruption of the TRIM25-GPX4 interaction leading to ferroptosis inhibition. In the broader context of cardiovascular research and ferroptosis biology, this study contributes a novel mechanistic dimension by linking a classical E3 ligase-substrate interaction (TRIM25-GPX4) to endothelial damage in MI/RI and translating a natural product into a mechanistically defined, targeted therapeutic (Figure 9). These findings not only deepen our understanding of the molecular pathophysiology of MI/RI but also translate traditional medicine into a modern, mechanism-based therapeutic strategy. Sch holds promise as a natural compound for the development of novel cardioprotective agents targeting protein-protein interactions in the ferroptosis pathway, with the ultimate goal of improving clinical outcomes for patients with ischemic heart disease.
