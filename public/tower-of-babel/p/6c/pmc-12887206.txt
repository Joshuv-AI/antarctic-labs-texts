Adhering to Life’s Essential 8 Is Associated With Greater Bone Mineral Accrual During Childhood and Adolescence
Authors: Hongbo Dong, Hong Cheng, Jingfan Xiong, Pei Xiao, Yan Li, Xingyin Shan, Junting Liu, Fangfang Chen, Jie Mi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiovascular disease and osteoporosis both originate from childhood and share several risk factors. However, the relationship between cardiovascular health (CVH) and bone mass remains unclear in children.MethodsData of 11 468 (50.7% male) Chinese children aged 6 to 18 were obtained from a nationwide cross‐sectional survey during 2013 to 2019. CVH was assessed based on 8 metrics (4 behavioral metrics: diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, sleep health; and 4 factor metrics: body mass index, blood lipids, blood glucose, blood pressure) according to Life’s Essential 8 construct. Scores for CVH behavior and CVH factor were categorized as low (0–49), moderate (50–79), or high (80–100). Bone mineral content and bone mineral density for the total body less head and lumbar spine were measured using dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry.ResultsIn unadjusted models, subjects with high CVH behavior but low CVH factor scores showed higher level and greater growth rate of bone mass than their counterparts. After adjustment for covariates, subjects with high scores of CVH behavior or CVH factor demonstrated 0.165 to 0.427 higher Z‐score of total body less head bone mineral content than those with low scores. Consistent relationships were also found for other bone measures among CVH behavior categories. Moreover, low bone mass risks were 29% to 34% lower in subjects with moderate or high scores of CVH behavior than those with low scores.ConclusionsHigh CVH, especially high CVH behaviors, is significantly associated with greater and faster accrual of childhood bone minerals. These results suggest that behavioral guidance for promoting optimal CVH may optimize bone mass accretion in early life.

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and osteoporosis are highly prevalent globally, resulting in significant health and economic losses. 1 , 2 In China, the annual deaths caused by CVD and osteoporosis almost doubled from 1990 to 2019 and were projected to increase over the next decade. 3 , 4 Rather than 2 independent age‐related diseases, growing evidence indicates that CVD and osteoporosis are likely to coexist with shared risk factors, such as physical inactivity, unhealthy diet, and abnormal cardiometabolic profiles. 5 Moreover, both diseases and their risks originate from childhood. 6 Faced with alarming trends of the early onset of CVD and osteoporosis, 7 , 8 the focus of prevention has been shifted from targeting single disease in adulthood to multirisk reduction starting in childhood. 9

The concept of cardiovascular health (CVH) was proposed by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2010, 10 composed of 7 lifestyle factors and biological metrics, that is, healthy diet, physically active, nonsmoking, normal body mass index, normal blood pressure, normal total cholesterol, and fasting glucose. In 2022, sleep health was added to expand the recommendations to Life’s Essential 8 (LE8). 11 Not merely a measure of CVH, the LE8 score encompasses important metrics that are crucial for primordial prevention for other major health problems. 12 , 13 Several studies have reported that greater LE8 scores or high adherence of AHA recommended lifestyles were associated with higher bone minerals and lower osteoporosis risks in adults. 14 , 15 , 16 However, data regarding the relationship between CVH and pediatric skeletal development are lacking.

Based on a nationwide cross‐sectional survey, this study aimed to investigate the association between CVH status, defined by the LE8 score, and bone mineral development patterns, levels, and low bone mass risks among children and adolescents from a large Chinese population.

METHODS

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This study is based on the CCACH (China Child and Adolescent Cardiovascular Health) study. The CCACH study was a nationwide, population‐based cross‐sectional study involving 8 regions in China. The study, which was conducted from 2013 to 2019, was designed to understand cardiovascular health, bone mineral density, and body composition in urban children and adolescents aged 3 to 18 years. Sampling procedures of the CCACH study were described in detail elsewhere. 17 , 18 Briefly, a total of 13 454 subjects underwent a series of examinations, including a questionnaire survey, anthropometric measurements, and dual‐energy X‐ray absorptiometry (DXA) scans. After excluding individuals with chronic metabolic diseases (diabetes, kidney disease, heart disease, or thyroid disease) or those with complete missing data for LE8 metrics, 11 468 children and adolescents aged 6 to 18 years were eligible for analysis. All the individuals had whole body DXA scans, but only 1961 individuals underwent lumbar spine (LS) scans. Individuals with LS data had similar distribution of sex, but were younger with lower fat mass index, higher appendicular muscle mass index, and a smaller proportion of subjects entering puberty, from families of low income and parental education of high school and above than their counterparts (Table S1).

This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Capital Institute of Pediatrics (approval number: 2012062), with written informed consent obtained from the participants or their parents.

General Examination

All the measurements were conducted by trained staff following standardized examination protocols. Weight and height were measured twice with a calibrated digital scale (Jianmin II; China Institute of Sport Science). Participants in lightweight clothing without shoes were instructed to stand up straight with head positioned in the Frankfort horizontal plane. Readings for weight and height were, respectively, taken to the nearest 0.1 kg and 0.1 cm, and mean values were used to calculate BMI.

Blood pressure (BP) was measured on the right arm in the sitting position according to standard protocols with an oscillometric device (Omron HEM‐7012, Omron, Kyoto, Japan). This oscillometric device was validated in children and adolescents against a mercury sphygmomanometer according to the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrument standards. 19 BP was measured 3 times with at least a 1‐ to 2‐minute interval between the measurements, and the average of the last 2 readings for systolic BP and diastolic BP were used for data analysis.

Whole‐Body and Lumbar Spine DXA Scans

DXA scans (Hologic Discovery‐A&W, Bedford, MA) of whole body and LS from L1 to L4 were carried out by a trained technician according to the standard operating method of International Society for Clinical Densitometry. 20 A high level of quality control was maintained throughout the DXA measurements, including the same DXA device and trained technicians at each center, and phantom calibrating every morning before scanning the participants. All DXA scans were analyzed by the software (APEX version 4.0) following the manufacturer’s guidelines, which provided values for whole and site‐specific body fat mass, lean mass, bone mineral content (BMC) and bone mineral density (BMD). The total muscle mass was calculated as total‐body lean mass minus BMC. The appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was calculated as the sum of the upper and lower limb muscle mass (kg)/height (m2), and the fat mass index (FMI) was calculated as total fat mass (kg)/height (m2). The visceral‐to‐subcutaneous fat ratio (VSR) was calculated as visceral fat area/subcutaneous fat area. FMI and ASMI Z‐scores were calculated by referencing to the body composition standards for Chinese children and adolescents. 18 , 21 VSR Z‐scores were calculated by sex‐specific regression residual analyses with adjustment for age. Bone mass status was classified into low and normal groups, defined as below and above the sex‐, age‐, and height‐specific lowest quartile of BMC/BMD at either total body less head (TBLH) or LS derived from the present study.

CVH Assessment With LE8 Score

CVH status was assessed by the childhood LE8 scoring algorithm suggested by the AHA Presidential Advisory in 2022, 11 which involved 4 behavior metrics (diet, physical activity, nicotine exposure, and sleep health) and 4 factor metrics (BMI, blood lipids, blood glucose, and blood pressure).

CVH Behaviors

Dietary behavior was assessed using a modified 16‐item Mediterranean Dietary Quality Index in children and adolescents. 22 Given the missing data for the item of “commercially baked goods or pastries for breakfast” in the original scoring system, we replaced it with “daily intake of salty snacks” in this study. Physical activity was assessed by weekly duration of moderate‐vigorous‐intensity leisure time activity using a self‐administered questionnaire. Nicotine exposure was assessed through active smoking and secondhand smoke exposure within the past 30 days. As the information on e‐cigarettes was not available for current study, participants were categorized as current smokers, noncurrent smokers with previous smoking attempts, and nonsmokers, to determine their active smoking status. Secondhand smoke exposure was defined as living with people who smoke. Sleep health was evaluated by the average hours of sleep per night. The optimal sleep duration was defined based on the Recommendation from Healthy China Action (2019–2030), which suggests primary school students, middle school students, and high school students sleep no less than 10, 9, and 8 hours per day.

CVH Factors

BMI values were converted to age‐ and sex‐specific percentiles based on the Child Growth Standards. 23 BP values were categorized as normal, elevated, stage 1 hypertension, and stage 2 hypertension calculated according to the 2017 American Academy of Pediatrics’ Clinical Practice Guideline for screening high BP in children and adolescents. 24 Venous blood samples were collected to measure fasting plasma glucose and lipids; these measurements were performed at the central clinical laboratory of Capital Institute of Pediatrics in Beijing. Fasting blood glucose was measured by using an enzyme hexokinase method, and cholesterol was measured with the enzymatic method. Non‐high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C) was calculated as total cholesterol minus high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol‐C.

Each metric was scored on a scale from 0 to 100, and the average of all 8 metrics was used to determine the LE8 CVH score. According to the AHA’s recommendations, scores for LE8, CVH behavior, CVH factor, and individual metrics were categorized as low (0–49), moderate (50–79), or high (80–100). The detailed definition and scoring algorithm for each behavioral metric is shown in Table S2.

Other Covariates

Data on demographics (age, sex), puberty (menarche/spermarche), and socioeconomic information (parental education and annual household income) were obtained via standardized questionnaires, with self‐reporting for participants >12 years old and parental reporting for those <12 years old. Parental education level was categorized as below college versus college or above. Household income was classified into 3 categories based on a poverty‐income ratio, an index derived by dividing family annual income by the established poverty level, accounting for household size and year of assessment. We categorized the family income as low‐income level (poverty‐income ratio <1.3), middle‐income level (poverty‐income ratio =1 ~ 3.5), and high‐income level (poverty‐income ratio >3.5).

Statistical Analysis

Characteristics were presented as mean±SD for continuous variables and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Differences in characteristics among LE8 CVH score levels were compared using chi‐square test for categorical variables and analysis of variance for continuous variables.

To illustrate the differences of bone development patterns across CVH categories, sex‐ and age‐specific growth curves were generated within each CVH score category (low, moderate, and high score) by using the Generalized Additive Model for Location Scale and Shape. 25 This approach allows fitting the response variable via an ample range of distributions, such as Box‐Cox Cole and Green, Box‐Cox Power Exponential, and Box‐Cox t with the additive functions to model the influence of age on parameters of the considered distribution. Final models were selected based on the Bayesian information criterion. The 50th centile of each bone mass measure was used to illustrate age‐related variation, and its corresponding age‐specific velocities were estimated by using the first derivatives of the median trajectory. Using the same approach, we generated the relative timing and magnitude of peak height velocity and peak TBLH BMC acquisition velocity of total population to help place skeletal accrual within the pubertal spectrum.

Differences in bone mass levels among CVH score categories were examined by the analysis of covariance. Before analyses, Z‐score of each bone mass measure was calculated by sex‐specific regression residual analyses with adjustment for age and height. Covariates included in all models for adjustment were age, parental education, household income, and Z‐score of ASMI, FMI, and VSR. We further conducted the dose–response analyses for the continuous score of LE8, CVH behavior, and CVH factor using restricted cubic spline functions with 3 knots. The F‐test was used to test for linear and nonlinear relationships. We also compared risks of low bone mass among CVH score categories using logistic regressions.

To detect potential interactions, stratified analyses were performed by age groups (6–11 or 12–18 years), puberty (prepuberty or puberty), household income (low to middle or high), and parental education (less than high school or high school and above). Multiplicative interaction between CVH score and these factors was evaluated by adding a cross‐product term in the regression model. However, given the relatively small sample size of participants with LS scanning, stratified analyses were performed only for TBLH bone measures.

To test the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses. Considering the strong association between BMI and bone measures, we excluded it from LE8 CVH and CVH factor score calculation. Associations of all 4 bone measures with LE8 CVH and CVH factor were reassessed afterwards.

All the statistical analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.4.1 (R Core Team 2021, Vienna, Austria). All tests were 2 sided, and P values <0.05 were denoted as statistically significance.

RESULTS

Characteristics of the Participants

This study included 5812 boys and 5656 girls, with the mean age of 12.7 (3.5) years and 13.0 (3.6) years, respectively (Table 1). Among the 8 CVH metrics, mean scores were lowest for diet and highest for BP. The overall adherence to the AHA’s CVH recommendations was low, with only 23.2% of children and adolescents achieving high LE8 CVH scores. Compared with participants with low LE8 CVH scores, participants with moderate or high scores were younger, more likely to be female and to have entered puberty, having lower levels of FMI and ASMI Z‐scores but similar VSR Z‐score, and coming from families with higher income and parental education. Unsurprisingly, scores for each metric increased with the categories of LE8 CVH.

Unadjusted Growth Patterns of Bone Minerals by CVH Score Levels

Figure 1A through 1F and Figures S1 through S3 illustrate age‐related changes in bone mass and annual growth rates across childhood and adolescence, stratified by CVH levels. In both sexes, individuals with moderate and high LE8 CVH scores showed similar or even lower BMC and BMD at each site. However, in late adolescence, such differences became smaller at TBLH BMC/BMD as the growth rate of the high score group surpassed that of the low score ones after 14 to 15 years old. Distinct relationships were noticed between CVH behaviors and CVH factors with bone development patterns. Subjects with greater bone mineral attainment across childhood and adolescence were mostly found in those with high CVH behavior scores but low CVH factor scores. Nevertheless, after 15 years old, individuals with high CVH behavior or factor scores exhibited greater growth rates than those with lower scores.

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

In subgroup analyses, significant interactions (P<0.05) were observed between age groups, puberty, family income, and parental education (Tables S4 and S5 and Figures S7 through S10). Although the associations were mostly unchanged in stratified analyses, the positive relationship between CVH scores and bone measures was more pronounced among individuals of older age (12–18 years old), entering puberty, from families with higher household income and parental education of high school and above.

In sensitivity analyses, the associations between CVH score and bone measures were all reassessed after multiple imputation for missing values (Tables S6 and S7 and Figures S11 through S14). The results remained consistent with previous findings, supporting the conclusions.

DISCUSSION

In this large cross‐sectional study of Chinese children and adolescents, we first found the positive link between CVH and pediatric bone mineral development. These findings were especially predominant for behavioral scale across various bone mass measures, underscoring the importance of healthy CVH behaviors in bone mineral optimization in early stages of life.

Our study observed contradicting relationships between LE8 CVH score and bone mass before and after adjustment for covariates. Such opposite associations were also noted for CVH factors but not CVH behaviors. Thus, these discrepancies may stem from the interplay among CVH factors, bone mass, and covariates such as ASMI, FMI, and VSR. It has been proved that muscles exert beneficial influence on bones for providing mechanical force and bone‐active factors (such as irisin), whereas the impact of fat is more complex, which can be beneficial, dual, or deleterious depending on the quantity and distribution. 26 , 27 Specifically, excessive visceral fat can harbor greater immune cells than subcutaneous fat, leading to a higher release of proinflammatory cytokines, which may induce metabolic dysfunction and stimulate bone resorption. 28 , 29 In current study, subjects having high LE8 CVH scores presented lower FMI and ASMI, but similar VSR, compared with those having lower CVH scores. It is plausible to observe negative associations of LE8 CVH score and CVH factors with bone mass measures in unadjusted models.

It is well established that CVD and osteoporosis are closely related with shared behavioral risk factors. 30 Unsurprisingly, we found similar positive connections for CVH behavior scores and each metric with bone mass as consistent with previous adulthood studies. 14 , 15 , 16 We also demonstrated that the peak growth rate of TBLH BMC occurred nearly 1 year later in boys with high LE8 scores than those with moderate/low score (14 years old versus 13 years old). In the current population, the age of peak TBLH BMC velocity was roughly ±2 years within the age of peak height velocity (Figure S15), indicating that midpuberty might be a sensitive period for a beneficial association between cardiovascular health and bone mass acquisition. Considering the substantial accumulation of peak bone mass during adolescence, 31 , 32 fostering optimal CVH behaviors in this critical period could markedly enhance lifelong bone health and help prevent osteoporosis later in life. Nonetheless, due to the cross‐sectional nature of current study, further confirmation through longitudinal studies is necessary.

Unlike the well‐known favorable impact of CVH behavioral factors on bone health, evidence is still controversial for several CVH biological metrics, such as blood glucose, blood lipids, and BP. Take the association between fasting blood glucose and BMD for example, although a positive relationship was reported by a Mendelian randomization study reported, null relationship was found in the a meta‐analysis of 23 340 patients from 14 population‐based studies indicated no statistically significant relationship between type 2 diabetes and low bone mineral density. 33 , 34 The associations of blood pressure and lipid profile with BMD were also inclusive and the underlying mechanisms are still unclear. 35 , 36 , 37 , 38 Besides, there might be a lag‐time or accumulative burden of cardiometabolic risks on bone health, and possibly resulting in a null relationship between subcomponents of CVH factors and BMC/BMD in young ages. The long‐term impacts of cumulative burden of CVH factors on bone mineral accrual still need to be investigated in more cohort studies.

The major strengths of this study include the application of LE8 construct for CVH evaluation in a large sample of Chinese children. This enabled us to generate stable bone growth curves stratified by CVH score levels and determine the sensitive period for the beneficial association. In addition, BMC/BMD was determined by DXA, which is considered as the gold standard for assessing and monitoring of pediatric bone health. This study also has several limitations. First, given the cross‐sectional design of present study, the causality cannot be inferred. Prospective cohorts are needed to investigate whether a high CVH score could promote bone mass gains and reduce osteoporotic risks in the future. Second, we used modified definitions of dietary and nicotine exposure metrics, which could have led to a slight overestimation or underestimation of the LE8 CVH score in this study. Third, study subjects were drawn from urban areas, and thus, our findings might not be generalizable to rural Chinese children and adolescents. Fourth, although we considered several potential confounders, other covariates, such as history of fracture, time of sunlight exposure, and supplementation of vitamin D and calcium, were not collected in the present study.

CONCLUSIONS

This study provides the first evidence of beneficial associations between CVH scores and bone health among pediatric populations. Our findings highlight the potential of synchronizing primordial prevention strategies for 2 major public health problems in the early stage of life.
