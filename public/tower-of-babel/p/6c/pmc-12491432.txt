Population viability analyses provide key insights into how alternative conservation efforts can prevent the extinction of a marsh passerine
Authors: Iván Alambiaga, Pablo Vera, Juan S. Monrós
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Habitat degradation and loss worldwide has led to dramatic population declines in several bird species. Population Viability Analysis (PVA) is a commonly used method to assess the extinction risk of a given species that can also assist in the design of recovery plans by simulating alternative management scenarios. This study conducts a PVA for the Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting (Emberiza schoeniclus witherbyi) population, with 85% of the estimated 250 breeding pairs confined to three wetlands. Viability analyses were then applied to simulate previously proposed conservation measures with the aim of predicting their potential to improve the subspecies’ critical status. Results predicted that the population will halve in the next 20 years and will be reduced to two wetlands, becoming completely extinct in the 2070s. Simulated habitat restorations and predator control succeeded in delaying the estimated extinction time but did not prevent the disappearance of many small localities. Population reinforcements and reintroductions from captive breeding programmes, combined with in situ actions, were the measures predicted to be most effective for the study species’ conservation. Urgent measures are required to avoid the short-term extinction predicted, and the findings provided practical guidance for the developing national conservation strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17289-3.

Introduction

Today, half of the world’s bird species are in decline, with one in seven expected to become extinct by the end of the century1,2. Landscape fragmentation, driven by human-induced habitat loss and degradation, has resulted in unfavourable conservation statuses for many species, increasing their risk of extinction3,4. This situation is especially critical for species that evolved in naturally fragmented habitats embedded within a matrix of adverse ecological conditions, as the loss of suitable areas connecting habitat patches has led to the isolation of remnant populations5. This is particularly true for bird species inhabiting wetlands, probably the most degraded ecosystem globally over the past centuries6 and supporting a large number of highly specialised taxa dependent on their conservation status7. Conservation efforts targeting wetland-associated birds in recent decades have primarily focused on protecting nodes of wetland networks8. However, these efforts have often been ineffective due to, among other causes, major gaps in national and international policies9,10. As a result, many species, especially wetland specialists with limited movement, are severely threatened7,11, highlighting the need for precise conservation assessments and targeted strategies to reverse negative trends.

The Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting (Emberiza schoeniclus witherbyi) is a paradigmatic case in this context. This subspecies, strictly linked to Mediterranean wetlands, has experienced a sharp decline in its metapopulation over recent decades due to habitat fragmentation and degradation12,13. Recent surveys estimate the Spanish breeding population at just 250 pairs confined to 14 wetlands, with 85% of the population concentrated in only sites14. According to available data, the Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting population meets the IUCN’s criteria for classification as Critically Endangered15. To reverse this scenario, and as part of a national conservation strategy, several studies have aimed to build a solid multidisciplinary scientific background to guide future measures12,16–18. However, a population viability analysis is lacking, which would accurately assess the species’ extinction risk and the effectiveness of proposed conservation strategies.

Population Viability Analysis (PVA) is a widely used tool in conservation biology to assess the extinction risk of a given species based on life history parameters19,20. These approaches have been recognised as a valid method for evaluating national species conservation statuses when reliable data are available21, and are even preferable to IUCN criteria22. PVAs also allow simulation of a wide range of alternative scenarios and have been used to explore how different conservation strategies might affect the viability of threatened populations23,24, assisting in the design of species recovery plans22. This is particularly valuable in species with fragmented metapopulations such as Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting, as PVAs can project how conservation measures targeted at specific localities could be translated into enhanced landscape viability due to increased connectivity25. PVAs thus have the potential to provide concise management guidelines, optimise available resources, and represent a promising solution to the widely documented science-practice gap26,27. However, their utility in decision making depends on whether the simulated scenarios are realistic and relevant to the context of the target species management28,29.

In this study, we conducted a population viability analysis for the threatened Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting population and evaluated the potential of alternative conservation measures previously proposed. Our main objectives were: (1) to assess the extinction risk of the current national population and that of the three management units previously described in Kvist et al. (2011); (2) identify the populations at critical risk of extinction; (3) evaluate the potential effectiveness of proposed conservation measures on the viability of the study species, including habitat restoration and predator control, population reinforcements, and reintroductions in localities without current populations; and (4) test how variation in specific life history parameter values that could be adjusted through targeted conservation measures could affect the national viability of the species.

Results

Eastern Iberian Reed bunting’s Spanish population viability analysis

According to the base model, probability of extinction for the Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting population is 0% over the next 20 years, 54.2% (CI95% ± 2.0%) over 50 years and 100% over 100 years (Table 3). Simulations predicted a population size decline of 50% within the first 20 years, reaching 205 individuals (CI95% ± 5.5) restricted to two populations (Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera). The probability of extinction was low until year 40 (7.0%), but sharply increased to 91.2% by year 60. Mean time to extinction (MeanEXT) across 500 iterations was 51.6 years (CI95% ± 0.7), with an annual mean growth rate (r) of -0.073 (CI95% ± 0.002). Maximum time to extinction observed for the population, representing the most optimistic scenario, was 75 years.

Castilla la Mancha cluster had an extinction probability of 100% over the next 20 years, with a MeanEXT of 10.6 years (CI95% ± 0.2) and an r of -0.278 (CI95% ± 0.009). Out of the nine populations, six were at imminent risk of extinction (MeanEXT < 2 years). Tablas de Daimiel was the most persistent locality, with a MeanEXT of 8.5 years (CI95% ± 0.3). For Ebro Valley populations, the probability of extinction was 0% over 20 years, 61.4% (CI95% ± 0.9%) over 50 years and 100% over 100 years. MeanEXT was 48.9 years (CI95% ± 0.8), with a mean growth rate of -0.075 (CI95% ± 0.003). Delta de l’Ebre was the most viable population analysed in the Ebro Valley, with a MeanEXT of 47.7 years (CI95% ± 0.7). Utxesa had the greatest difference between MeanEXT and MaxEXT, at 13.8 (CI95% ± 1.0) and 67 years respectively. Both Navarrese populations were at imminent risk of extinction. Finally, S’Albufera had an extinction probability of 0% over 20 years, 84.6% over 50 years (CI95% ± 3.1%) and 100% over 100 years. Mean and maximum time to extinction were 39.9 years (CI95% ± 0.9) and 69 years respectively, and r was − 0,075 (CI95% ± 0.002) (Fig. 1).

Conservation measures potential to reduce extinction risk

Habitat restoration and predator control for current breeding wetlands

Restoration efforts focused on the three main wetlands led to increases in MeanEXT for these localities and the national metapopulation (Fig. 2a; Appendix 8). When restorations were implemented in secondary populations, the Spanish population MeanEXT did not differ from that estimated with the base model (Appendix 8). These actions also failed to increase the viability of the target wetlands except for Utxesa (MeanEXT = 34.6 ± 1.4), and only slight MaxEXT increases were detected. Significant differences in Spanish MeanEXT were found when comparing first and second scenarios (Kruskal-Wallis test P = 0.01), indicating that measures focused on the main populations were more relevant to national viability. No differences were found when the first scenario was compared with a third scenario combining restoration efforts in both main and secondary wetlands (P = 0.83). In the latter, only Utxesa improved over the two previous simulations due to frequent recolonisations from Delta de l’Ebre.

Population reinforcements through translocations

Simulated individual extraction for translocations revealed differing effects on the viability of the two proposed donor populations. To avoid severely reducing donor wetlands’ MeanEXT, the considered optimal number of pairs (1♂+1♀) to be translocated was 7 each year for 3 years (A scenario, 5 + 2 from Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera respectively), 5 for 4 years (B scenario, 4 + 1) or 4 for 5 years (C scenario, 3 + 1) (Fig. 3a). Once translocations were simulated, national MeanEXT and that of donor populations slightly declined in all scenarios (Fig. 2b, Appendix 9). No significant differences were found in national MeanEXT when comparing results between the three translocation scenarios (Kruskal-Wallis test P = 0.40), the receiving wetland type (P = 0.26), or whether translocation occurred before or after restoration of the receiving wetland (P = 0.11). Only Tablas de Daimiel, Almoguera and Taray enhanced their population viability, especially when translocations were made after habitat restorations (Fig. 3b, Appendix 9). Negligible increases in MeanEXT were detected in critical populations when translocations followed habitat restorations (Fig. 3c).

Population reinforcements through breeding programme’s releases

When simulating supplementations from captive breeding programmes, the receiving wetland type (Kruskal-Wallis test P < 0.001) and whether or not there was a previous habitat restoration (P = 0.04) had a significant effect on the predicted national MeanEXT. The species’ national viability significantly increased when reinforcements were focused on wetlands with ‘good’ and ‘intermediate’ viabilities and after in situ measures (Fig. 2c). In this scenario, MeanEXT increased with the delay in the start of supplementations, with maximum national viability improvements when reinforcements occurred between years 20 and 50. Increases in MeanEXT were also detected when reinforcements occurred in critical populations in the year 50. However, this appears to be an artefact resulting from reintroduction of individuals into already extinct populations.

At local scale, substantial increases in critical population’s MeanEXT were achieved when reinforcements occurred in the third year following habitat restorations (Fig. 4a). MeanEXT declined sharply with each year that the initial supplementation was delayed due to mass extinctions in the first few years (Appendix 10). Viability of ‘intermediate’ populations also improved with reinforcements following habitat restorations, though maximum viability values varied among populations according to the first year of releases (Fig. 4b; Appendix 11). In Almoguera and Taray, MeanEXT was higher when reinforcements occurred in the third year, in Tablas de Daimiel when supplementations began in year 10, and in Utxesa when started after year 20. Viability also increased without prior restoration, except for Utxesa, but at lower rates and only when reinforcements took place during the third year. The viability of S’Albufera and Delta de l’Ebre peaked when reinforcements occurred at year 50 after habitat restoration (Fig. 4c; Appendix 12). In the absence of prior restorations, year 20 was the optimal timing to increase the MeanEXT for both populations.

Population restorations

Population restorations increased national viability, as well as that of the Castilla la Mancha and Ebro management units and their principal wetlands (Fig. 2d; Appendix 13). All Castilla la Mancha wetlands’ MaxEXT at least doubled under the best-case scenario (10 populations restored). Increases in MeanEXT and MaxEXT were non-linear, with notable gains occurring between the 2nd and 4th re-established populations and again between the 8th and 10th ; however, between 4th and 8th restorations, there were no differences in viability. When population reestablishments followed habitat restoration, national MeanEXT (178.9 ± 0.7; 10 populations reestablished) significantly exceeded that of the same scenario without habitat restoration (MeanEXT = 67.1 ± 0.4; Kruskal-Wallis test P = 0.009) and also surpassed viability values from habitat restoration alone (MeanEXT = 165.0 ± 0.7). A similar pattern was observed in the Ebro Valley cluster and individual wetlands such as Tablas de Daimiel and Delta de l’Ebre.

Reintroduced populations in the Ebro Valley cluster exhibited significantly higher MeanEXT values than those in Castilla la Mancha, both with restoration (MeanEXT ± CI95% = 114.1 ± 47.0 vs. 20.4 ± 4.0; Kruskal-Wallis test P = 0.009) and without restoration of base model wetlands (43.3 ± 13.3 vs. 19.8 ± 3.9; P = 0.016). Furthermore, reintroductions increased the number of wetlands with MeanEXT > 20 years from 2 (Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera) in the base model to 9 wetlands without prior habitat restorations, and 12 wetlands with habitat restoration.

Sensitivity analysis

A simulated 3% increase in juvenile and adult female mortality resulted in a dramatic rise in the probability of extinction over 50 years, from 50% to > 95% (Fig. 5). Population viability was less sensitive to variations in male mortality, although values exceeding 50% led to an almost certain probability of extinction. Productivity emerged as the most critical parameter for extinction risk, with probability of extinction going from 100 to 0% as productivity increased by just 0.4 offspring per brood (from 2.0 to 2.4). A 3% increase in carrying capacity (K) reduced extinction probability by 10%, however increases beyond this threshold did not improve viability. Decreases in K, though, had a severe impact on population persistence. Finally, simulated changes in disperser survival had no detectable effect on population viability predictions in the base model.

Regarding scenarios of conservation measures, simulations of different survival rates during translocation processes showed that this parameter had no significant impact on the estimated national extinction probability within 50 years (Appendix 14). Extinction probability in all cases, including prior restoration scenarios, was higher than in the base model (54.2% ± 2.0% CI95%). For supplementations from captive breeding programmes without prior habitat restoration, the 100-year extinction probability remained above 60% when releases began in year 3, even with 30 pairs reintroduced every two years (Appendix 15). In contrast, when supplementation began in years 10 and 20, with of 15 and 10 pairs released biennially, respectively, the probability fell below 50%.

Discussion

Population Viability Analyses conducted predict that the Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting population will be extinct by the 2070s. Results suggest that the population will halve in 20 years and will be reduced to two populations: Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera. Both coastal localities currently hold the largest and most stable populations14, however they face an uncertain future due to incipient salinisation and habitat degradation resulting from climate change and aquifer overexploitation30,31. In addition, evidence suggests that both populations are highly isolated13 and are therefore vulnerable to several factors, such as genetic constraints, diseases or stochastic factors32–34. Massive extinction of Ebro Valley populations has led to the isolation of the two Navarre wetlands, which are now at imminent risk due to their small population sizes and the unlikely arrival of dispersers. Utxesa appears to be the last remnant wetland connected to Delta de l’Ebre, which would be a source of individuals improving the viability of Utxesa through frequent recolonisations predicted (increased MaxEXT). Effective management of this wetland should be ensured to prevent it from acting as a sink and to safeguard the persistence of at least one population buffering potentially demographic fluctuations in Delta de l’Ebre35,36.

Short-term extinction is predicted for the Castilla la Mancha cluster. With between 160 and 205 breeding pairs recorded across 36 localities, Castilla la Mancha was the most important metapopulation cluster in 200537. Nearly 20 years later, following massive local extinctions38, only 36 breeding pairs have been detected in 9 wetlands. Degradation and loss of breeding sites due to water disturbances caused by agricultural intensification have resulted in the fragmentation of what was once a highly connected wetland network characterised by recurrent extinction-colonisation dynamics that buffered local events15. Consequently, most of the remaining Castilla la Mancha wetlands are now at imminent risk of extinction due to low carrying capacity, small initial population sizes, and poor projected recolonisation probabilities. This is exacerbated by the critical condition of historical source populations such as Tablas de Daimiel, which has suffered severe drought problems in recent years, leading to a reduction in suitable habitat extent and pronounced population fluctuations12,39.

Wetland degradation and the disappearance of stepping stones populations connecting the metapopulation appear to be the main drivers of the sharp decline in the Spanish population and its predicted high risk of extinction. Conservation measures focused on addressing these problems, as well as small population sizes, are therefore urgently required.

Restoration of optimal habitats by promoting mixed patch vegetation areas is the most frequently proposed conservation strategy for the study species18,40. Management of large monospecific reedbeds through cutting, grazing or controlled fires, and replanting of saw sedge, are among the specific actions previously recommended for this purpose17,41. Long-term preservation of these restored habitats, however, depends on effective water management ensuring permanently flooded areas39, so appropriate regional and national water policies are urgently required42. Besides habitat actions, predator control has also been recommended15, as this factor has been found to substantially affect productivity, with up to 60% Reed Bunting clutches predated in some areas43. This measure has been widely used in managing threatened bird species, leading to successful conservation outcomes44,45. Other ecological interactions, such as interspecific competition, could influence breeding population parameters. However, the Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting shows limited niche overlap with other marsh passerines in south-eastern Europe due to its habitat and diet specialisation46–48, and interspecific competition is therefore not expected to have a significant impact on the scenarios modelled.

Simulations of these in situ measures led to significant predicted enhancements in national metapopulation viability and in that of the three main historical breeding sites. They also resulted in a significant improvement in the viability of Utxesa, with frequent reinforcements from Delta de l’Ebre dispersers, and an increase in recolonization rates in some Castilla la Mancha wetlands. However, higher recolonization rates were insufficient to improve the long-term viability of critical populations, so according to the results, additional ex situ measures appear to be required to prevent the further reduction of the already fragmented wetland network49.

Translocations have proven to be an effective conservation measure for recovering critically endangered bird populations50,51. However, in this case no significant viability improvements were predicted for populations at imminent risk of extinction, even when translocations were conducted after habitat restorations. Furthermore, such actions slightly reduced metapopulation viability by affecting donor populations, consistent with previous studies on endangered species with similar population structures23. Local positive effects though were observed when reinforcements were targeted at specific localities, indicating that translocations could be an effective short-term rescue method when applied to these populations. Small initial population sizes and limited donor populations seem to be a major reason for the predicted low effectiveness of translocations52,53. Benefits are also expected if translocations are used to manage genetic variability and prevent isolation-related issues such as inbreeding depression, as they can emulate historical gene flow and help address genetic drivers of extinction when natural dispersal is negligible54. Inbreeding, genetic drift, and loss of heterozygosity have been proved to significantly increase extinction risk in endangered species by reducing fitness and evolutionary potential55,56, especially in fragmented metapopulations57,58. Therefore, although we modelled default values for inbreeding depression due to the lack of recent data, obtaining accurate, specific information on these parameters should be a priority for conservation planning.

Larger population reinforcements through hypothetical captive breeding programmes combined with the described in situ actions were the only conservation measures likely to improve the MeanEXT of all analysed populations, an approach that has been reported as successful for other threatened species59,60. Scenario simulations enabled the identification the optimal time to start supplementations in each wetland and which target wetlands contributed most to improving the national viability of the species, thus helping to prioritise resources and define guidelines for future management decisions61. For instance, immediate reinforcements were required to increase the persistence of wetlands with lower projected viability, which had no effect on national viability but may be critical for maintaining current network connectivity62. In contrast, supplementations at the main localities were more successful for their viability and that of the national population when implemented at later stages, probably because this allowed the management of genetic diversity and other problems arising from isolation63,64.

Given these predictions, and assuming unlimited resources, it would be advisable to implement in situ measures in all breeding wetlands and then reinforce populations, prioritising small, critical populations before subsequently acting on more viable populations. However, in a more realistic scenario with limited resources, the optimal way to ensure the medium- to long-term persistence of the study species would require focusing efforts on the main populations, even if this means that the possibility of local extinctions cannot be avoided. In both scenarios, although with increased average extinction times, most populations would still be highly susceptible due to the lack of immigrant individuals to buffer adverse local phenomena62. In this context, ex situ programmes could support reintroductions aimed at reconnecting the highly fragmented metapopulation once viability in the main populations has been enhanced65,66.

Simulated reintroductions led to a significant viability enhancement for the national metapopulation and the Ebro Valley and Castilla la Mancha clusters. In addition to increasing the number of breeding sites connecting the network, reintroductions were also predicted to improve the viability of source populations which is crucial in fragmented metapopulations67. Conversely, simulated population restorations had no effect on the two isolated Navarre localities, so if these sites are to be conserved, restoration of other intermediate wetlands such as Sariñena, Badina Escudera or El Juncal lagoons should be considered. No improvement in the viability of S’Albufera was detected either due to the apparent isolation of this island population13,68. Finally, even though these actions improved the viability of the Castilla la Mancha and Ebro Valley populations, the two clusters remained unconnected. Future actions might thus focus on the restoration of recently extinct localities potentially connecting inland and coastal Mediterranean wetlands, such as Lagunas de Ruidera or Laguna Ojos de Villaverde37.

Simulations conducted revealed how some proposed measures could significantly reduce the study species’ imminent extinction risk, but the pace of the ongoing critical situation and the effectiveness of the projected scenarios depend on other aspects that should be considered.

Today, most analysed wetlands are still undergoing severe degradation processes, potentially affecting critical parameters according to the sensitivity analysis such as carrying capacity, productivity or survival39,69. Some of these problems could be reversed with targeted measures, such as those suggested here. Nevertheless, other ongoing degradation processes are beyond the control of managers, as they depend on regional, national and international policies, such as water-related threats12 or climate change, which is predicted to cause salinisation of coastal populations30. Climate change is also expected to increase the frequency of catastrophes jeopardising the study species, such as droughts, wildfires during the breeding season, and extreme weather events such as hailstorms70,71. In this context, previous studies have highlighted the potential of moving populations to less sensitive areas72, which could be considered given the high vulnerability of Mediterranean wetlands to climate change9.

Given the current critical situation of the study species, findings highlight the high potential of captive breeding programmes to provide a stock of individuals for future supplementations and reintroductions. Captive breeding projects are increasingly being used to assist the conservation of threatened species73, but this approach is resource-consuming and can be technically challenging74. The success of these measures depends on numerous factors73,75, including the target species’ characteristics, adequate maintenance, genetic management, the release method, and, according to the results of sensitivity analyses, the number of individuals released, This, in turn, may require multidisciplinary teams and the maintenance of resources throughout time76, but could be the only viable alternative when in situ actions are expected to be insufficient, as in this case59.

Urgent conservation measures are required to avoid the short-term extinction of the Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting population predicted here. This study provides a basis for a national conservation strategy for the target species. Further efforts should be made to obtain additional data on demographic parameters that are currently lacking, such as a new population genetics study, in order to update the models and improve their reliability. Future research should also evaluate the conservation outcomes of implemented measures and adjust future priority actions in light of new information, enhancing results through adaptive management strategies77. Finally, it is important that future studies examine how potential abrupt changes in environmental conditions, which are expected to increase in the near future, could affect the viability of this species and others inhabiting sensitive habitats to anthropogenic pressures and climate change.

Methods

Target species and study area

The Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting (Emberiza schoeniclus witherbyi) is a sedentary Reed Bunting ‘thick billed’ subspecies specialised in Mediterranean wetlands and patchily distributed across a few wetlands in south-eastern France, north-western Morocco, eastern Iberia and the Balearic Islands. In these ecosystems, it preferentially inhabits mixed patches of helophytic vegetation in the flooded area of wetlands, mainly composed of reed (Phragmites australis) and saw sedge (Cladium mariscus)12.

The study area comprises the entire Spanish distribution of this marsh passerine (Fig. 1, Appendix 1), currently consisting of 14 wetlands belonging to three distinct genetic clusters or management units with asymmetric population dynamics: Castilla la Mancha, Ebro Valley and Balearic Islands13,14. Castilla la Mancha cluster includes nine wetlands, seven of them part of the ‘La Mancha Húmeda’ Biosphere Reserve. These are inland wetlands of variable size and hydrology, which were once highly connected before the extensive loss and degradation of wetlands in the region. The Ebro Valley cluster currently contains four breeding populations located along the Ebro river: two in Navarra (Laguna de Pitillas and Embalse de Las Cañas) and two in Catalonia (Utxesa, and Delta de l’Ebre), with the two groups separated by more than 200 km. Finally, Balearic Islands cluster consists of a single coastal wetland, s’Albufera de Mallorca.

Eastern Iberian Reed bunting’s Spanish population viability analysis

To assess the extinction probability of the current Spanish Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting breeding population and that of its three management units, a population viability analysis (PVA) was performed using VORTEX v.10.6.0.078. A base model was developed based on Reed Bunting’s life history parameters, combining data collected by the authors with that available in the literature (Table 1). Data on Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting were prioritised over those from other subspecies whenever available. For parameters lacking specific Reed Bunting evidence, VORTEX default settings were applied. Models were run with 500 iterations to assess the extinction probability at 20, 50 and 100 years, defining extinction as the point at which only one sex remained. For each model, the following parameters were estimated: mean time to extinction (MeanEXT; for the 500 iterations), maximum time to extinction (MaxEXT; year in which extinction occurred in all iterations) and mean annual growth rate (r).

Number of populations and population sizes were based on the results of the 2021 national census14. Population sizes for Tablas de Daimiel and S’Albufera were updated to 2023 due to recent monitoring surveys conducted using the same methodology. To identify critical populations and prioritise conservation efforts, the national PVA was employed to determine which populations were at imminent risk of extinction (MeanEXT < 2 years). When assessing the probability of extinction for the three described genetic clusters, Castilla la Mancha, Ebro Valley and Balearic Islands, were analysed separately as there is considered to be no genetic flow between them13. Inbreeding depression was modelled using VORTEX default values, including 6.29 lethal equivalents, derived from the combined effects of inbreeding on fecundity and first-year survival79. Default parameters were applied due to the absence of recent population genetic data for this subspecies; the sole available study, analyzing samples collected between 1995 and 2008, reported no substantial decline in genetic diversity during that period13.

Dispersal

Dispersal in Reed Buntings, including the study subspecies, occurs mainly during the first year through post-juvenile dispersal events involving both sexes80,81. Although seasonal dispersal processes outside the breeding season have been recorded80,82, adults exhibit high territory fidelity and generally remain in the same breeding areas81,83. Therefore, only first-year individuals were modelled as dispersers. Survival of dispersers was assumed to be 50%, VORTEX’s default value, since there are no previous studies evaluating this parameter.

The proportion of dispersers for each cluster was estimated using the ratio of individuals recaptured in a different population from the one they were ringed relative to all recaptures. For Castilla la Mancha, we used available ringing data for the study subspecies collected since 1982 and provided by the Spanish scientific ringing scheme (CMA, http://www.anillamientoseo.org). The estimated proportion of dispersers was 10.8% (n = 65 recaptures). Based on this proportion, probability of dispersal from one wetland to all the others in Castilla la Mancha was estimated while controlling for the distance effect with the exponential distribution dispersion formula (F(d) = 1 − e −λd), where (d) represents the distance between wetlands and λ was set to 0.02 (assumed after checking that it best fitted ringing data; Appendix 2). For the Navarra populations, proportion of dispersers was estimated at 8.6% by Gutiérrez et al. (2018)72. This value was applied to estimate the proportion of dispersers between Las Cañas and Pitillas, and between the other two Ebro valley populations: Utxesa and Delta de l’Ebre. Dispersal between the two Ebro population groups was modelled as 0, following the results of a recent study analysing the Spanish population connectivity84. The Balearic Islands cluster comprises only one population (S’Albufera), and dispersion among the three Spanish population clusters was modelled as 0% due to the observed genetic structuring13.

Reproductive system and rates

Reed Bunting has a monogamous breeding system with a high incidence of extra-pair paternity85. Spanish ringing data indicate that the oldest recorded individual in the study area was 7 years old; therefore, both longevity and maximum age of reproduction were set as 7 years. Females can lay up to two clutches during the breeding season, each containing a maximum of five eggs82. Based on monitoring data from Switzerland, 73% of females produce one clutch per season, whereas 27% produce two86. Productivity rates varied among populations, so results of two studies that precisely quantified this parameter87,88 were averaged, obtaining a value of 2.2 offspring per brood, with a 0.5 standard deviation also added to the model. Sex ratio at hatching was assumed 50%. All males were assumed to be part of the breeding pool based on observations from a Dutch breeding population where no floating males were detected89. Similarly, and in line with similar studies with passerines23, all females in a population were assumed to breed, and a 10% standard deviation was applied to simulate environmental variation.

Mortality rates and catastrophes

Mortality was modelled using the mean values reported in several studies of breeding populations: 59% for first-year individuals, and 51% and 42% for adult females and males respectively90,91. Other studies analysed these metrics in Spain for wintering populations92, but data from breeding populations such the one under study were considered more appropriate.

Two distinct catastrophes were modelled, as they have been recorded as potential negative impacts on Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting populations. Eight years of severe drought, with less than 10% of the floodable area inundated during breeding season, have been recorded in Tablas de Daimiel since 2000. Between 2021 and 2024, the population declined from 48 to 16 breeding pairs due this situation (authors’ own data). Assuming that the decline was constant, i.e. 40% per year, and primarily attributable to reduced survival, this catastrophe was modelled for this locality with a frequency of 33% of years and a severity of 40% in survival. Conversely, although no long-term detrimental effects have been detected17, wildfires can negatively affect populations when they occur during breeding. In S’Albufera, seven wildfires burning more than one hectare of potential breeding areas have been recorded since 1986 during the breeding season (data provided by S’Albufera Natural Park’s technicians). Wildfires affected a mean of 43.2 ha. Given the current breeding density of 0.04 breeding pairs per hectare and assuming uniform distribution17,93, a fire of this size could affect two breeding pairs, representing a 4% reduction in the number of breeding females with a frequency of 18%. These parameters, along with the variations in carrying capacity simulated in the key life history parameters sensitivity analysis (final section of Methods), were the ones used to model changes in environmental conditions in the study area.

Carrying capacity

Carrying capacity (K) in each wetland was estimated based on availability of suitable habitat for the Eastern Iberian Reed Bunting and its territory size. National availability of suitable habitat for the target species was recently assessed84. For each wetland, the total area with a > 80% probability of containing a breeding territory was considered suitable habitat for the species. The carrying capacity of a wetland was calculated by dividing the suitable area by the mean territory core area size of the study species (KDE50 = 2.6 ha)17, and subsequently doubled assuming that each territory has a male and a female (Appendix 1).

Conservation measures potential to reduce extinction risk

Habitat restoration and predator control for current breeding wetlands

Habitat restoration and predator control are among the most mentioned conservation measures for this subspecies17,41. Three scenarios simulating restorations in distinct target wetlands were conducted to evaluate the potential of these measures (Table 2). In the first scenario, habitat restorations were focused on the three historically main breeding populations (Tablas de Daimiel, Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera; K > 100 individuals) simulating an annual increase in K of 10% for 4 years. This time period was chosen based on the plant growth period required to reach suitable structures for nesting94. As productivity in Reed Buntings is strongly influenced by availability of suitable habitats and predation pressure43, five different increases in productivity of 1, 5, 10, 15 and 20% were simulated, representing varying intensities in the provision of suitable habitats and predator control actions. Improved water availability in Tablas de Daimiel National Park was also simulated by reducing the frequency and severity of extreme droughts by 50%.

Population reinforcements through translocations

Translocations of individuals from the largest and most viable populations (population size > 80 and MeanEXT > 35 years, Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera) to various types of receiving wetlands were simulated (Table 2). The effect of harvesting individuals was first tested to determine which conditions reduced the risk for donor populations (reduction in MeanEXT < 5 years). Fifteen scenarios were built, comprising periods of 3, 4 or 5 years, during which 1, 2, 3, 4 or 5 breeding pairs were extracted annually. For each period length (3, 4 or 5 years), the scenario allowing the maximum number of individuals to be translocated without significantly reducing the MeanEXT of donor populations (reduction in MeanEXT < 5 years) was selected. This criterion was established to maximise the number of translocated individuals while avoiding severe impacts on donor populations and to assess the effect of translocation duration. Translocations from the two donor wetlands to two receiving sites were simulated for the three selected scenarios.

Translocations were simulated into two different types of receiving wetlands, classified according to their population viability: “critical” (MeanEXT < 2 years) and “intermediate” (2 years < MeanEXT < 35 years; Almoguera, Tablas de Daimiel, Taray and Utxesa) (Appendix 3). Navaseca, Alarcos, Pitillas and Las Cañas were selected as representatives of “critical” wetlands for Ebro valley and Castilla la Mancha to assess the effect of translocations on this wetland type. Translocations were simulated on (1) the base model and (2) considering previously described habitat restoration interventions (+ 20% scenario). In both cases implementation started in the third year, reflecting realistic timelines, and when part of the simulated restorations would already be completed. It was assumed that all translocated individuals (100%) survived and individuals were randomized to increase allele diversity23. MeanEXT and MaxEXT were calculated for the Spanish population, as well as for both donor and receiving populations. Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to test whether there were differences in national MeanEXT according to receiving wetland type, the three translocation scenarios and whether translocations were preceded by habitat restoration or not.

Population reinforcements through breeding programmes

A captive breeding programme starting in 2024 coordinated by the Spanish government and regional administrations consists of an initial founding population of 5♂+5♀ from Delta de l’Ebre and S’Albufera. Assuming an optimistic scenario in which an average of four chicks per pair are produced in each breeding season, less than half of the potential productivity, up to 60 chicks could potentially be produced annually after the second generation. Considering variability in productivity and individual renewals to maintain the captive breeding programme, annual population reinforcements of 40 individuals, with a sex ratio of 1:1, were modelled starting from the second generation offspring (3rd year) (Table 2).

Receiving wetlands were divided into three types to test the effect of alternative approaches to national viability: “critical” (MeanEXT < 2 years), “intermediate” (2 < MeanEXT < 35 years; Almoguera, Tablas de Daimiel, Taray and Utxesa), and “good” (MeanEXT > 35 years; Delta de l’Ebre, S’Albufera). 10 reinforcements were modelled for each wetland, with the number of individuals supplemented varying among receiving wetland types depending on the number of available individuals and the number of target wetlands (Appendix 4). With the aim of planning reinforcements over time for each wetland type, four different scenarios were simulated to assess how variations in the initial year (3, 10, 20 and 50) would affect national and individual wetland viability (Table 2, Appendix 4). The same process was simulated but considering previous in situ measures described above. MeanEXT and MaxEXT for the national population, for receiving populations, and for donor population were estimated. Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to evaluate whether there were differences in national MeanEXT according to receiving wetland type, the four scenarios, and whether supplementations were preceded by habitat restoration or not.

Population restorations

Reintroductions in wetlands without current populations were modelled to test the effects of increasing the number of breeding localities and improved connectivity on viability (Table 2). Top 10 previously identified wetlands with the highest potential to improve national connectivity were considered (Appendix 5 and 6). Five scenarios were simulated in which a variable number of best connecting wetlands (2, 4, 6, 8 and 10) were incorporated into the base model. For each wetland, prior habitat restoration was simulated to increase carrying capacity, allowing them to support 10 breeding pairs and a 20% increase in productivity over the base model. Two population supplementations of 10 breeding pairs each, separated by 5 years, were simulated for each wetland, with initial year of supplementation varying slightly (year 3 to 7) due to the limited availability of individuals for reintroductions assumed (40 annually; Appendix 7).

Dispersal between wetlands was recalculated using the method described in the ‘dispersal’ section of the base model, and a 25% increase in disperser survival was simulated due to increased populations. Dispersal between population clusters was kept at 0 because of the lack of evidence of this phenomenon and because none of the wetlands were assumed to be intermediate populations. Population restorations were also simulated considering previous habitat restorations of the initial model wetlands (+ 20% productivity scenario). MeanEXT and MaxEXT were estimated for the Spanish population, the three described management units, and all wetlands, including those restored,. Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to test whether national MeanEXT differed depending on whether reestablishments were preceded by habitat restoration of the base model wetlands or not. It was also tested whether there were differences in the MeanEXT of reestablished wetlands if they were part of the Ebro Valley or Castilla la Mancha clusters.

Sensitivity analysis

Parameters expected to be critical for the population viability and whose values could be adjusted through targeted conservation measures, or that could change due to abrupt environmental changes, were used for an elasticity analysis. This involved modifying the target parameter values while maintaining those of the rest of base model, with the aim of assessing the effect of their variation on the national extinction probability in 50 years. Parameters selected for this analysis were: juvenile survival (variation range: 55–63%), adult female and male survival (47–56%, and 26–54% respectively), productivity (2.0 to 2.4 offspring/brood), carrying capacity variations in 10 years (-10–10%) and disperser survival (0–100%). Parameter ranges were set to cover 50-year extinction risk from 0 to 100% to identify critical and optimal thresholds.

Sensitivity analyses were also applied to some of the simulated conservation scenarios to assess how variation in certain parameters with assumed values could affect their effectiveness. In the case of translocations, given that the processes of capture, transport, and release can affect individual survival96, variations in survival rates following translocation (50–100%) were simulated within an intermediate scenario in terms of both duration and intensity (Scenario B targeting “intermediate” wetlands, Appendix 3). National extinction probability within 50 years was calculated for these scenarios. For supplementations from captive breeding programmes, we simulated variations in the number of breeding pairs reintroduced (1 to 30) into “good” wetlands (MeanEXT > 35 years) under Scenarios A, B, and C (starting in year 3, 10, or 20) (Appendix 4). Extinction probability was calculated over 100 years. Habitat restoration was not simulated in this case, as this alone reduced the probability of extinction within 100 years to zero.
