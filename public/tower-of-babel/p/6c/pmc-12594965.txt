Enhancement in hazardous gas detection capabilities of MoS2 monolayer-based devices through defect engineering and photonic activation
Authors: Pukhraj Prajapat, Anis Ahmad Chaudhary, Aditya Yadav, Vaibhav Kandwal, Pargam Vashishtha, Mohamed A M Ali, Sumeet Walia, Govind Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gas-sensing potential of transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs) drew attention owing to their high surface sensitivity and tunable optoelectronic features. Among the TMDs, monolayer MoS2 stands out as a promising material for advanced gas sensors. However, TMDs-based gas sensors still require considerable improvement in room temperature sensitivity, response times, and stability, which may be achievable through alterations in kinetics. Herein, we report a highly sensitive NH3 gas sensor based on monolayer MoS2, whose sensing performance is greatly enhanced by defect engineering and photonic activation. Intestinally induced sulfur vacancies create chemically active adsorption sites, increasing adsorption energy and enhancing charge transfer between NH3 molecules and MoS2. On the other hand, visible-light illumination stimulates photoresponsivity by generating electron-hole pairs to speed up desorption and recovery time. With these combined stimuli, very large modulations to the electronic band structure occur, thus enhancing the gas-surface interaction dynamics and hence sensing performance. Thus, this study highlights the potential of defect-engineered and photonic-activated monolayer MoS2 as a strong candidate for advanced gas detection and presents a scalable pathway for next-generation sensor development, meeting the demands of environmental and industrial monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22978-0.Keywords: Monolayer MoS2, NH3 gas sensor, Defect engineering, Sulfur vacancies, Photonic activation, Room-temperature sensing, 2D materialsSubject terms: Materials for devices, Sensors and biosensors

Introduction

The rising demand for advanced gas sensing technologies reflects a proactive approach toward monitoring air quality, safety within the industry, and real-time detection of harmful gases1,2. This compact, phenomenally efficient, and ultra-sensitive technology allows huge opportunities to address the rising threats of toxic gases and environmental pollution3. Metal oxide semiconductors have been regarded as pioneer chemical sensors for several decades because of their efficiency and lower cost4,5. However, they have some important drawbacks, such as lower sensitivity and selectivity at room temperature. By improving these qualities, we can develop solutions that not only mitigate these dangers but also significantly enhance the overall well-being and safety of the public, thus leading toward better alternatives like carbon nanotubes, graphene, and transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs)6,7. Among the different materials investigated for gas sensing, two-dimensional (2D) materials such as molybdenum disulfide (MoS2) have gained attention for their unique properties in gas sensing applications. Single-layer MoS2 (SL-MoS2) can detect trace amounts of gases because of its high surface-to-volume ratio, a direct bandgap of ~ 1.8 eV, and high carrier mobility6.

Several recent studies have demonstrated that MoS2 is a promising material for detecting hazardous gases at room temperature due to its high surface reactivity and customizable electrical characteristics8,9. Gas sensing using MoS2 mainly relies on charge transfer interactions between gas molecules and the surface of the material10. However, some challenges are posed by such MoS2-based gas sensors, like limited recovery times, selectivity issues, and the need to improve the selectivity and sensitivity threshold level. So far, these issues have not been resolved for real-world application developments. To solve these problems, it is necessary to adopt new strategies for optimizing the interaction of the material with target gases while maintaining fast response and recovery cycles11. To address these limitations, a novel dual-stimulus gas sensor based on SL-MoS2 could be developed by incorporating two synergistic mechanisms, defect engineering and photonic activation12. Defect engineering or controlled sulfur vacancies are introduced into the material, enhancing the affinity for gas molecules and creating localized adsorption sites for improved sensitivity and selectivity. The photonic activation enhances the performance regarding gas desorption acceleration and drastically shortens recovery times13. The dual-stimulus design makes the sensor multifunctional, allowing it to furnish photodetection capabilities and its primary gas sensing function. It opens additional opportunities for applications of the device in integrated sensing systems, which are emerging as compact and flexible. The synergistic effects of bias modulation, photonic activation, and defect engineering present a unique approach to gas sensing by overcoming the shortfalls in present technologies and creating new performance standards.

This study demonstrated a remarkable enhancement in ammonia (NH3) gas sensing performance, achieved through defect engineering and photonic activation. Introducing defects in SL-MoS2 creates highly active sites that significantly improve the adsorption and interaction of gas molecules, addressing the sensitivity and selectivity limitations of conventional sensors. Photonic activation further amplifies the device performance by modulating charge carrier dynamics, resulting in faster response (12.8s) and recovery times (14.7s). The synergistic effect of these techniques enables detection limits that rival or surpass state-of-the-art gas sensors, even under challenging environmental conditions. This study highlights the potential of SL-MoS₂ as a versatile platform for hazardous gas detection and establishes a pathway for developing next-generation sensors with unparalleled performance and practical applicability in real-world scenarios.

Experimental

We have grown SL-MoS2 using the chemical vapor deposition (CVD) technique shown in Fig. 1(a). The MoO3 and S (Sigma-Aldrich 99.9%) precursors were employed for reaction at a temperature of 780 °C. Raman spectroscopy (Horiba Scientific LabRAM HR evolution) and atomic force microscopy (AFM), Bruker Dimension Icon in ScanAsyst-air mode, were used to analyze the vibrational modes & high-resolution topographic images to confirm its growth. Furthermore, the surface composition of the thin film was examined using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (Scienta Omicron with an Al Kα source) by calibrating the C 1s position at 284.6 eV. The optical properties of the film are analysed using the CRAIC 20/30 UV-visible spectrometer and the Edinburgh photoluminescence (PL) (FLS-980- customised) D2D2, respectively. The device was fabricated on a single MoS2 flake using the lithography technique. The size of the triangular flake is 33 μm, and the spacing between the electrodes is 2 μm.

(a) Schematic of the CVD growth chamber, (b) Schematic of the customised gas sensing setup.

The gas sensing measurements were conducted at room temperature (23 °C) using a two-probe station equipped with two mass flow controllers (MFCs), a programmable temperature controller, and a Keithley 2450 source meter, as displayed in Fig. 1(b). Specifically, the gas sensing setup included a temperature controller capable of operating from room temperature up to approximately 850 °C. The system featured two MFCs with a flow control range from 0.01 to 100 SCCM (0.01–100% of full scale), a pressure range of 11.5 to 160 PSIA, and a flow measurement repeatability of ± (0.1% of the reading + 0.02% of full scale). The integrated sensing chamber and source meter supported voltage ranges from 20 mV to 200 V, current ranges up to 1 A, basic accuracy of 0.012%, and typical wideband noise of 2 mVrms.

The gas used in the experiments, such as NH3, was commercially procured, calibrated, and certified to a concentration of 50 ppm using a weigh/pressure gauge calibrated to National Metrology Institute (NMI) standards. All measurements were carried out under a constant relative humidity (RH) of 36% and maintained in a sealed environment to prevent RH fluctuations. Additionally, laser sources emitting wavelengths of 640 nm were employed to examine gas sensing under photoactivation.

Result and discussion

The optical microscopic image of synthesized SL-MoS2, exhibiting unique features of the material, is shown in Fig. 2(a). This image reveals well-defined, isolated triangular MoS2 flakes with fine edges, indicating a high grade of crystallinity. Each triangular flake manifests a perfect geometrical shape arising out of 3-fold symmetry inherent in the structure of MoS214. This symmetry implies that the flakes are possible single crystals or have a continuous crystal lattice without grain boundaries15. Upon thermal treatment, the optical image of the SL-MoS2 shows a shift in color contrast and the appearance of patchy or dark regions, indicating defect formation such as sulfur vacancies. At elevated temperatures, wrinkle formation, edge irregularities, or microcracks developed due to thermal stress and lattice reconfiguration, which can be observed in Figure S1(a) (Supporting Information, S.I.). These changes in optical contrast reflect modifications in surface morphology and are often correlated with enhanced defect density. Additionally, the thickness of the flakes was measured with the help of AFM measurements, as shown in Figure S1(b) (S.I.). The topography image supports this observation made through optical images, stating that the sample is uniform, and its thickness was thus determined to be ~ 0.68 nm, which confirms that the grown flakes are SL-MoS216. Moreover, Raman spectroscopy was carried out to identify whether our grown flakes are monolayers and the vibrational modes present in them. The results of Raman measurements are shown in Figure S1(c). In Raman spectra, two intense peaks are very clear at 385.22 cm− 1 and 403.26 cm− 1. These are indicators of distinct vibrational modes within the material. Precisely, the peak at 385.22 cm− 1 is related to the E12g vibrational mode, which consists of in-plane atomic vibrations, whereas the peak at 403.26 cm− 1 corresponds to the A1g mode and hence, is related to out-of-plane vibrations. The Raman shift between these two peaks is 18.04 cm− 1, which characterizes the structural properties of the monolayer17. After the thermal treatment, an additional satellite peak was observed near 379 cm− 1, which is attributed to a localized vibrational mode of LO(M) shown in Fig. 2(b). The satellite peak’s presence points to further intricate vibrational interactions within the material, which could be affected by numerous considerations, including defects or local strain variations. The optical properties were studied via the absorption spectra in Fig. 2(c)18,19. The spectra distinctly show excitonic peaks at about 720 nm and 640 nm. These peaks correspond to exciton A and B, confirming the semiconducting nature of SL-MoS2 and that it has a direct bandgap.

(a) Optical microscopic image of the grown MoS2. (b) Raman spectra of defect-engineered MoS2 (c), absorption spectra of the grown MoS2 (d), XPS- Mo core level (e), XPS- S core level (f), Schematic of SL-MoS2 with defects.

XPS was used to analyze the elemental and chemical states of the materials being investigated. The core-level spectra analysis for Mo-3d is given in Fig. 2(d). The spectra revealed the presence of doublets arising from the spin-orbit splitting, having 229.22 eV and 232.52 eV as binding energies for the Mo 3d5/2 and the Mo 3d3/2, respectively. A spin-orbit split energy of 3.3 eV corresponds to Mo in the + 4 oxidation state (Mo+ 4), which specifically signals Mo-S bonding exhibited by MoS2. A distinct peak observed at 226.39 eV as evidence for the Mo-S bond points to the chemical interaction between molybdenum and sulfur in the material. On the other hand, Fig. 2(e) depicts the S 2p core level spectra that verify the oxidation states of sulfur. The doublets for the S 2p orbitals S 2p1/2 and S 2p3/2 were located at binding energies of 163.59 eV and 162.380 eV, respectively. The peak splitting energy of 1.21 eV reveals distinct sulfur species and confirms the identification of their respective oxidation states. The stoichiometric ratio of Mo and S was calculated based on the XPS Mo 3d core level spectra. The results revealed an estimated ratio of molybdenum to sulfur of 1:1.83. This finding indicates a sulfur deficiency in the synthesized MoS2 flakes and could impact their electronic and catalytic properties. The discussion analysis confirmed that the grown MoS2 had been engineered to introduce defects, as represented in the schematic (Fig. 2(f)) of SL-MoS2 20.

Moreover, PL measurement was conducted to analyse the defect engineering in grown MoS2. As shown in Fig. 3(a), the as-grown MoS2 shows a prominent peak of emission centred at around 1.83 eV, characteristic of the A-exciton transition observed in pristine SL-MoS2. Such emission strength is an indicator of low defect density and optical quality21.The as-grown MoS2 underwent thermal treatment to engineer the vacancies present. After the thermal treatment, PL measurement was conducted, which is shown in Fig. 3(b). The decrease in the PL intensity of the prominent peak has been observed, and a new broad emission peak appears at a lower energy, which can be attributed to recombination via defect-bound excitons. These defect peaks confirm the mid-gap states introduced by sulfur vacancies acting as trapping centres for excitons, thereby decreasing radiative recombination at the neutral exciton level. The observed redshifted and broadened defect-related emission therefore provides direct optical evidence of the successful creation of sulfur vacancies, validating our defect-engineering strategy. After that, we have conducted PL measurement in the NH3 gas environment, significant change in PL spectra can be observed in Fig. 3(c). The emission peak intensity was increased, and the defect-related peak was suppressed. The strong modulation arises from charge transfer interactions between NH3 molecules and sulfur vacancy sites. NH3 acts as an electron donor, injecting carriers into the MoS2 lattice. These additional electrons increase the occupation probability of defect states, which enhances defect-related recombination pathways while simultaneously reducing neutral exciton recombination. In effect, NH3 adsorption strengthens the emission signature of defect-bound excitons, providing a spectroscopic fingerprint of gas-defect interactions21.The Fig. 3 not only confirms the formation of engineered defects but also demonstrates their active role in enhancing NH3 adsorption and modulating electronic structure.

(a) PL spectra of the grown MoS2. (b) PL spectra of defect-engineered MoS2 (c), PL spectra of defect-engineered MoS2 under NH3 gas environment.

To thoroughly examine the gas sensing properties of the grown SL-MoS2, an MSM device with a top electrode was fabricated as shown in Fig. 4(a). The MSM structure allows for the effective measurement of the response of the material under different gases, thus giving an insight into its sensing capabilities. The I-V characteristics of the device based on SL-MoS2 were measured with dry air, NH3, and light (626 nm), respectively, as displayed in Fig. 4(b). Under dry air, the I-V curve demonstrated symmetry and linearity, as the device with ohmic contacts under baseline conductivity exhibited little gas adsorption. The current amplitude increased significantly when exposed to NH3 (a reducing gas), because NH3 donated electrons into the MoS2, whose carrier density is enhanced. Under 626 nm light illumination of the device, current increases across due to photogeneration of electron-hole pairs. Under NH3 with light, the current reached a maximum under the two mechanisms of photogeneration and electron donation, which indicated the greatest sensitivity enhanced by the device toward reducing gases22.

To explore the gas-sensing performance of the SL-MoS2-based device, we exposed it to varying concentrations of NH3 ranging from 10 ppm to 90 ppm. As shown in Fig. 4c, the sensor exhibits a clear and progressive increase in response with rising NH3 concentration, confirming its ability to detect trace levels of the gas. The response signal displays a consistent and reproducible modulation in conductance, attributed to charge transfer interactions between NH3 molecules and sulfur vacancy sites in MoS2. To further validate the stability of our sensor, repeated exposure-recovery cycles were conducted at different concentrations; the resistance values stayed consistent along the eight successive cycles shown in Figure S3 (SI). NH3, being an electron-donating gas, introduces additional electrons to the MoS2 channel, effectively shifting the Fermi level and decreasing the Schottky barrier height at the metal-semiconductor junction, enhancing the carrier transport and sensor response. The steps for sensing mechanisms are elaborated in the S.I. We illuminated the device with light to further boost sensitivity while repeating the NH3 sensing measurements. Optical excitation significantly increased the response amplitude across all concentrations, as seen from the pronounced current modulation under illumination. This enhancement can be attributed to the combined effect of photo-generated carriers and gas-induced charge transfer, improving conductivity and reducing the recombination rate. The response and recovery time also improved due to dual stimuli, which is explained in the S.I.The photogenerated electron–hole pairs, when coupled with NH3 adsorption, facilitate more efficient separation and transport of carriers, demonstrating a photo-assisted gas sensing mechanism in MoS223.

The schematic and energy band diagrams in Fig. 5 represent charge transport and band alignment modulations at the Au/MoS2 interface under various conditions: pristine, gas adsorbed, and gas adsorbed with illumination. Starting with the prototype, the Au/MoS2 contact forms a Schottky barrier, which is apparent from the band offset between the Fermi level of Au and the conduction band of MoS2. Figure 5(a) presents the position of the energy level relative to the vacuum level. In the pristine state, the Schottky barrier height (SBH) impeded electron flow across the interface, resulting in a low dark current. In Fig. 5(b), the band diagram of the pristine Au/MoS2 device is shown under equilibrium conditions. At the interface between the metal and semiconductor, due to the mismatch of work functions between Au and MoS2, the electrons are transferred from MoS2 to Au until the Fermi levels are aligned. This process causes upward band bending in MoS2 near the contact and creates a Schottky barrier to impede electron injection from the metal into the semiconductor channel. This barrier limits the device baseline conductivity under dark conditions. The relatively high SBH ensures that few thermally excited carriers are able to cross the junction, configuring a low dark current, as can be seen in the I-V characteristics. This equilibrium band alignment thus sets the transport characteristics of the Au/MoS2 device prior to external stimulus. A considerable alteration in band structure occurs upon exposure to NH3, as shown in Fig. 5(c). Being a reducing gas, NH3 provides electrons when adsorbed on the MoS2 surface, especially at the defect sites introduced by sulfur vacancies. The Fig. 5(c) provides a direct visualisation of the charge transfer process underlying NH3 detection. The electrons donated by NH3 molecules to MoS2 act to modulate the electronic structure, and the extent of this modulation is directly correlated with the number of adsorption sites and the efficiency of gas-material interaction. In defect-engineered MoS2, the abundance of sulfur vacancies amplifies this effect by providing localised states that strongly interact with NH3 molecules, leading to a more pronounced Fermi level shift and a greater reduction in barrier height. Amid this charge transfer, the upward shifting of the Fermi level of MoS2 occurs, lowering the Schottky barrier height (ΔSB1) and the built-in potential (Vbi1) at the Au/MoS2 interface. Accordingly, balanced carrier injection from Au to MoS2 occurs, enhancing the device’s conductivity in the ambient NH3 gas.

Upon light illumination, up to Fig. 5(d), the device will feature further improvement for the charge transport process. When photons impinge on MoS2, they form electron-hole pairs, and the adsorbed NH3 molecules help keep the photogenerated holes stable, while lengthening the carrier lifetime and the carrier separation. This dual effect by gas doping and photocarrier generation further reduces the effective barrier height (ΔSB2) and shifts the band edges more favourable for carrier injection, which ends with enhancing the performance24.

Conclusion

In this study, we demonstrated a novel NH3 gas sensor based on SL-MoS2 of high performance, reported with a dual-stimulus strategy that merged defect engineering with photonic activation. Controlled sulfur vacancy introduction via thermal treatment post-growth significantly increased the adsorption energy of NH3 gas molecules and provided highly active sites for their adsorption. The vacancies are considered to provide high transfer charge interactions with NH3 molecules that can modulate the Schottky barrier, resulting in enhanced carrier transport across the MoS2 channel. To further enhance sensor performance, photonic activation by visible light (λ = 640 nm) was implemented, facilitating enhanced photoresponse and recovery by creating electron-hole pairs. Due to this collaboration, a pronounced enhancement in sensitivity, selectivity, and response/recovery time was achieved at room temperature. The sensor responses are repeatable and remain stable during multiple operation cycles, demonstrating sensor stability. Also, selectivity studies prove the sensor’s strong selectivity towards NH3 over common interfering gases such as CO, H2S, SO2, and NO2. These results demonstrated the crucial roles of defect engineering and photonic activation in high-performance NH3 sensing.
