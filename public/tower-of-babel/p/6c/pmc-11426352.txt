Amoebic colitis in a 39-year-old man with HIV
Authors: Yu Miyazaki, Takuya Adachi
Published: 2024

A 39-year-old man with HIV was admitted to our hospital for treatment of pneumocystis pneumonia. He had discontinued antiretroviral treatment (ART) 2 years earlier, and his CD4 cell count was 47 (reference range 500–1000) cells/μL with an HIV-RNA level of 1.7 × 105 copies/mL. We prescribed oral sulfamethoxazole–trimethorprim (1200 mg and 240 mg) 3 times daily and oral prednisolone 80 mg/d for 5 days, followed by 40 mg/d for 5 days. He had engaged in sexual intercourse without using a condom with multiple male partners and had not travelled abroad. On day 7 of his hospital stay, he developed abdominal pain and bloody diarrhea. His vital signs were normal, and he had right lower quadrant tenderness. An intravenous contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan showed wall thickening of the ascending colon (Figure 1A). Stool microscopy identified Entamoeba trophozoites, and the stool culture was negative for bacteria. A colonoscopy showed multiple ulcers with yellow exudates (Figure 1B). Biopsy specimens taken from the colon confirmed amoebic trophozoites (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.231816/tab-related-content), and further testing did not show cytomegalovirus, tuberculosis, malignant lymphoma, or Kaposi sarcoma. Amoebic colitis was diagnosed, and we treated the patient with oral metronidazole 500 mg 3 times per day for 10 days, followed by oral paromomycin 500 mg 3 times per day for 10 days, to treat intraluminal cysts. His symptoms improved gradually, and he was discharged from hospital. At a 2-week follow-up visit, he was asymptomatic and ART was restarted.

(A) A contrast-enhanced computed tomography scan showing marked wall thickening of the ascending colon (arrow) in a 39-year-old man with amoebic colitis. (B) A colonoscopy shows multiple large ulcers with yellow exudates in the ascending colon.

Travellers returning from regions where amoebiasis is endemic and men who have sex with men have an increased risk of infection in regions where the disease is relatively rare.1,2 Corticosteroids predispose patients with no symptoms of amoebiasis to the development of amoebic colitis.3 In this case, although we observed substantial immunodeficiency, the patient had reported no previous symptoms of amoebiasis. However, symptoms rapidly occurred shortly after corticosteroid therapy. Physicians treating patients with a higher risk of Entamoeba histolytica infection using corticosteroid therapy should be aware of the risk of amoebiasis.
