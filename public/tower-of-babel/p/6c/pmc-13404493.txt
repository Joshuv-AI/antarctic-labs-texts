Nationwide trend analysis of burnout among resident physicians in Japan before and after the national workstyle reform: a repeated cross-sectional survey of 25 368 participants
Authors: Ryohei Ono, Kiyoshi Shikino, Yuji Nishizaki, Miwa Sekine, Kazuya Nagasaki, Taro Shimizu, Hiroyuki Kobayashi, Yasuharu Tokuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study examined temporal changes in burnout and working environments among Japanese resident physicians across the COVID-19 period and the preparation and implementation phases of the national workstyle reform. Furthermore, it assesses associated changes in their working environments.DesignThis study was conducted as a multicentre repeated cross-sectional study.SettingData were used from the General Medicine In-Training Examination surveys conducted in 2020, 2022, 2023 and 2024.ParticipantsParticipants included postgraduate year 1 (PGY-1) and PGY-2 residents across Japan.Primary and secondary outcome measuresData on working hours, emergency duties, inpatient load, self-study time and burnout were collected through standardised questionnaires. Multivariable logistic regression was performed to assess associations between workstyle reform phases and burnout, adjusting for demographic and institutional characteristics.ResultsAltogether, 25 368 residents participated (6816 in 2020; 6063 in 2022; 6584 in 2023; 5905 in 2024). Burnout prevalence was the highest during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020 (21.7%), which decreased to 13.3% in 2024. Weekly working hours of ≥80 hours declined from 19.9% to 2.3%, and those working <45 hours increased from 2.5% to 24.7%. Compared with the pre-reform period (2020–2022), odds of burnout were significantly lower during the preparation (2023; adjusted OR (aOR) 0.60, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.66) and implementation (2024; aOR: 0.67, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.74) phases.ConclusionsResidents’ burnout was the highest in 2020 and subsequently declined in parallel with improvements in job satisfaction, working hours and clinical workload. These findings suggest that system-level efforts to improve workload balance may be associated with better resident well-being, while causal attribution to the reform should be interpreted cautiously.

Introduction

Physician burnout is a critical global issue, especially among early-career physicians.1 2 The literature indicates that most physicians experience burnout, which displays emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation and a loss of professional fulfilment.3 Resident physicians are particularly vulnerable and are considered a high-risk group for burnout owing to long working hours, sleep deprivation, frequent evaluations and limited autonomy during training.4 Additionally, residents’ burnout is associated with reduced quality of care, medical errors and early career attrition.2 3 As residency is a formative stage in physicians’ professional development, burnout may have long-term effects on mental well-being. Additionally, job satisfaction is an important indicator of physician well-being and has been associated with workforce retention, professional fulfilment and quality of patient care.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, physician burnout increased substantially worldwide, particularly among residents. Previous studies reported that postgraduate clinical residents’ burnout was notably high in 2020, when they were heavily engaged in COVID-19 care.5 6 In the USA, longitudinal surveillance data showed that burnout among healthcare workers increased from 30.9% in 2020 to 39.8% in 2022, which declined to 35.4% in 2023. Stress specifically related to COVID-19 decreased steadily from 32.0% in 2020 to 21.4% in 2023, indicating that pandemic-related psychological burdens gradually subsided.7 In contrast, some studies reported that physician burnout remained persistently high from 2020 to 2022.8 9 However, to our knowledge, longitudinal evidence on residents’ burnout trends remains limited, although they may have distinct stressors and recovery trajectories compared with other healthcare professionals.

Many countries have introduced restrictions on physicians’ working hours to ensure patient safety and promote physician well-being.10–12 The European Working Time Directive has limited physicians’ working hours to 48 hours weekly since 2009. In the USA, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education limits residents to about 80 working hours weekly, averaged over 4 weeks. Therefore, residents may work up to 24 consecutive hours, with an additional 4–6 hours permitted for patient handoffs and educational activities. They are required to take 1 day off every 7 days.10–12

Following these international trends and with preparatory measures initiated in 2023, the Japanese government implemented a nationwide physician workstyle reform in April 2024 to reduce physician overwork and improve their well-being.13 Before this reform, Japanese physicians were exempted from statutory overtime limits. The reform introduced legally enforceable limits on overtime and mandatory health protection measures. For most physicians, annual overtime was capped at 960 hours, with restrictions on continuous duty periods (up to 28 hours), mandatory rest intervals between shifts (at least 9 hours) and occupational health interviews for physicians working excessive overtime.14 15 Additionally, healthcare institutions were required to monitor physician working hours, develop plans to reduce excessive workloads and promote organisational changes, such as task shifting and workflow optimisation. Recognising the educational requirements of postgraduate training, postgraduate year 1 (PGY-1) and PGY-2 residents were placed under a separate training-oriented framework that permitted up to 1860 hours of annual overtime while maintaining the health protection measures.13–15 Therefore, the reform targeted working hours and broader aspects of the working environment that influenced resident burnout and job satisfaction. It is important to evaluate the extent to which this large-scale policy initiative contributed to alleviating residents’ burnout and improving job satisfaction. Hence, this study primarily evaluated changes in residents’ burnout before and after the national workstyle reform.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a multicentre, repeated cross-sectional study using data from the General Medicine In-Training Examination (GM-ITE) for academic years 2020, 2022, 2023 and 2024. Data from 2021 were not included because the relevant questionnaire items (including burnout-related items) were not collected that year. The GM-ITE 2020 survey corresponded to the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, which was expected to affect the mental health of resident physicians.5 The GM-ITE 2022 represented the subsequent and more stable period. These 2 years were collectively defined as ‘the pre-workstyle reform cohort’. The GM-ITE 2023 was considered the ‘preparation phase’16 and the GM-ITE 2024 represented the ‘post-workstyle reform cohort’.

Setting

The GM-ITE, developed in 2011 by a non-profit organisation, the Japan Institute for Advancement of Medical Education Programme (JAMEP), is a nationwide standardised examination. It is modelled after the US Internal Medicine In-Training Examination.17 18 As of 2024, 690 clinical training hospitals and 9663 participants, accounting for approximately 67% of all such institutions and almost half of residents in Japan, respectively,19 had participated in the GM-ITE.

Participants

The participants included PGY-1 and PGY-2 residents in Japan. Participants completed the GM-ITE in 2020, 2022, 2023 or 2024 and voluntarily participated in the study. After completing the clinical knowledge section of the examination, they completed a standardised survey on mental health, workload and training environment-related factors. Residents who did not provide informed consent were excluded from the final analysis. In the GM-ITE 2020, 2022, 2023 and 2024, 7669, 8438, 9106 and 9179 residents participated, respectively, of which 6816 (88.9%), 6063 (71.9%), 6584 (72.3%) and 5905 (64.3%) were included in the final analyses (online supplemental figure 1).

Measurements

Resident characteristics

Participant characteristics included self-reported variables obtained from the questionnaire and objectively obtained data from the GM-ITE database and hospital records. Self-reported data from a standardised questionnaire included PGY level, sex, age, monthly emergency shifts, inpatient load, average daily self-study time, average weekly working hours, average sleep duration over the past month, burnout and job satisfaction. The last two items were collected using Mini-Z 2.0, a simple and non-proprietary tool for assessing physician well-being and burnout. Previous literature validated the Japanese version of the Mini-Z 2.0 for use with residents.20–22 Additionally, institutional characteristics, including hospital type (community or university hospital) and location (urban or rural), were obtained from the GM-ITE administrative database and linked to individual survey responses through the participating institution identifier.

Working and studying conditions

Residents reported their weekly average working hours and daily average self-study time throughout the training period. Working hours included weekdays, weekends and night emergency department duties. Working hours were reported using a 10-category scale: 1 (<45 hours), 2 (≥45 hours and <50 hours), 3 (≥50 hours and <55 hours), 4 (≥55 hours and <60 hours), 5 (≥60 hours and <65 hours), 6 (≥65 hours and <70 hours), 7 (≥70 hours and <80 hours), 8 (≥80 hours and <90 hours), 9 (≥90 hours and <100 hours) and 10 (≥100 hours). The self-study time was classified into five categories: 1 (None), 2 (>0 and ≤30 min), 3 (>30 min and ≤60 min), 4 (>60 min and ≤90 min) and 5 (>90 min).

Burnout and job satisfaction

The Mini-Z 2.0 survey was originally developed and validated in the USA as a brief instrument to assess physician burnout and work environment.23 We employed the Japanese version, which has proven reliable among Japanese physicians, to assess burnout and job satisfaction.21 Burnout was defined as a score of ≤3 to the item: ‘Using your own definition of burnout, please select one of the following statements’. The five response options ranged from (1) ‘I feel completely burned out and may need help’ to (5) ‘I enjoy my work; I have no symptoms of burnout’, where lower scores indicated severe burnout. Job satisfaction was defined as a score of ≥4 to the item: ‘Overall, I am satisfied with my current job’, with responses ranging from (1) strongly disagree to (5) strongly agree.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise baseline characteristics, working hours, burnout and job satisfaction. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared across the four groups using χ2 tests. Missing responses were coded as ‘unknown’. Descriptive statistics were calculated using available data for each variable after excluding missing responses. Furthermore, p value for trend was calculated using the Cochran-Armitage test for binary categorical variables and the Cochran-Mantel-Haenszel test for variables with three or more categories. In this analysis, the p value for trend was calculated only for items excluding responses marked as ‘unsure’ or ‘unknown’. Annual trends in the proportion of residents reporting burnout, job satisfaction and working environment indicators were analysed using line graphs showing each variable’s share within the corresponding year.

Effect sizes for burnout and job satisfaction were quantified using Cramer’s V, Trend r and Cohen’s h (comparing the first and last survey years). Univariable logistic regression analyses were conducted with burnout (yes/no) as the dependent variable. Multivariable logistic regression analyses were additionally adjusted for PGY, sex, hospital location, hospital type, age category, average monthly accident and emergency (A&E) duties, average number of inpatients under charge, daily self-study hours and weekly working hours. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p value <0.05. All statistical analyses were performed in R (V.4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and STATA V.18 (StataCorp; College Station, Texas, USA).

Regarding the working environment, monthly A&E duties decreased, with the proportion of residents performing more than six duties declining from 10.2% in 2020 to 7.7% in 2024 (figure 1A). The inpatient charge decreased as well. The proportion of residents caring for 10 or more patients decreased from 14.1% to 10.4%, and those caring for 5–9 patients decreased from 59.9% to 46.1% between 2020 and 2024. However, the proportion of residents caring for 0–4 patients increased slightly from 2020 to 2022 (figure 1B). Conversely, self-study time exceeding 60 min increased significantly, particularly from 2023 to 2024. The proportion of residents studying for more than 90 min per day increased from 4.3% in 2020 to 9.0% in 2024 (figure 1C). Similarly, weekly working hours substantially declined, with those working ≥80 hours decreasing from 19.9% in 2020 to 2.3% in 2024. Notably, those working <45 hours increased significantly (from 2.5% in 2020 to 24.7% in 2024) (figure 1D). Hospital location (urban vs rural) remained stable across years; however, the proportion of residents at university hospitals increased from 16.1% to 19.2%.

Association of burnout with workstyle reform phases and subgroups

Online supplemental table 1 and figure 2 show the association between burnout and each subgroup. When burnout was used as the outcome, both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses showed significant associations with the workstyle reform phase. Among other covariates, PGY2 was associated with a slightly lower risk of burnout than PGY1 (aOR 0.89, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.96, p=0.003). Female residents had lower odds of burnout compared with males (aOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.91, p<0.001). Urban hospital location was modestly associated with lower burnout risk than rural settings (aOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.84 to 0.98, p=0.015), whereas hospital type was insignificantly associated with burnout. Compared with residents without A&E duties, duty frequency was associated with lower adjusted odds of burnout. Residents aged ≥28 years had higher odds of burnout compared with those aged 24–25 years (aOR 1.29, 95% CI 1.16 to 1.43, p<0.001). A higher number of inpatient charges was related to burnout as well. Those caring for ≥15 inpatients were at significantly higher risk than those caring for 0–4 patients (aOR 1.74, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.11, p<0.001). In contrast, longer daily self-study hours were associated with lower odds of burnout (aOR 0.64, 0.52, 0.53 and 0.51 for 0–30 min/day, 31–60 min/day, 61–90 min/day and ≥91 min/day, respectively; all p<0.001). Weekly working hours showed a clear gradient. Compared with <45 hours/week, the risk of burnout increased progressively with longer hours (aOR 1.39 and 1.61 for 90–100 hours/week and ≥100 hours/week, respectively; p=0.008 and p<0.001, respectively).

Discussion

This study comprehensively examined temporal changes in burnout, working environments and well-being across the COVID-19 and workstyle reform periods in Japan. It is among the largest nationwide analyses among resident physicians in Japan. The findings demonstrated two notable transitions. First, residents’ burnout was the highest during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in 2020. Second, following the introduction and preparation for the national workstyle reform, working conditions and mental health indicators improved substantially.

Changes from the COVID-19 period to the post-pandemic era

During the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, Japanese residents faced unprecedented challenges, including increased clinical workload, staffing shortages and emotional distress from patient care in high-risk environments, which likely contributed to increased burnout in 2020 (21.7%), as previous nationwide findings reported.5 As the pandemic subsided and clinical systems stabilised, burnout decreased to 17.8% in 2022, while job satisfaction increased from 62.3% to 66.3%. Notably, the effect size for the change in job satisfaction was greater than that of burnout. This improvement paralleled international observations showing partial physician well-being recovery during the post-pandemic era.7

Changes in burnout before and after the national workstyle reform

Beyond the pandemic’s recovery effect, the temporal decline in burnout appeared closely associated with the implementation of Japan’s national workstyle reform. This policy was aimed at capping excessive working hours and promoting physician well-being, with preparatory measures underway in 2023 and full implementation in 2024.13–15 Our multivariable analyses revealed that the odds of burnout were significantly lower in the preparation (aOR 0.60) and implementation (aOR: 0.67) years compared with the pre-reform period. The corresponding improvements in workload indicators, including reduced A&E duties, fewer inpatients per resident and shorter weekly working hours, suggested that structural changes in the working environment directly contributed to the reduction in burnout.24

Interestingly, when using no A&E duties and no self-study hours as reference categories, increased duty frequency and study time were associated with lower odds of burnout. This suggested the possibility of reverse causation, whereby residents who were already experiencing burnout may have been unable to take on duties or engage in self-study or may have been assigned fewer duties. Conversely, being able to work moderately and allocate time for study appeared to be associated with a lower risk of burnout. The increase in self-study time and job satisfaction further supported the possibility of a more sustainable work-life balance as a result of these reforms. Additionally, burnout prevalence in our cohort remained relatively low throughout the study period (13–22%), compared with some international reports. Linzer et al reported a burnout prevalence of 42% among the US residents during and shortly after COVID-19. Therefore, the relatively low baseline burnout prevalence in our cohort may have resulted in a floor effect, potentially limiting the magnitude of observable reductions over time.25

Globally, efforts to reduce physician burnout emphasise the value of organisation-directed, rather than physician-directed, approaches.26 27 Previous studies showed that strategies, such as improving staffing, optimising workflow and enhancing communication, were more effective in mitigating burnout than interventions focused solely on individuals.27 28 Notably, our findings were consistent with previous evidence showing that organisation-directed interventions effectively reduced burnout. Another systematic review reported that shorter resident duty hours were associated with improvements in resident-related outcomes, particularly emotional exhaustion, overall well-being, sleep duration and sleepiness.29 Therefore, Japan’s policy-driven approach represents a real-world, nationwide implementation of such system-level strategies, providing an important model for workforce sustainability in postgraduate medical education.

Factors contributing to burnout reduction

The study findings indicated that a reduction in working hours and clinical workload was central to the observed decline in residents’ burnout, with shorter hours and fewer patient responsibilities alleviating chronic fatigue and emotional exhaustion, which are the core components of burnout. The concurrent increase in self-study time and job satisfaction reflected enhanced intrinsic motivation and autonomy, both acting as protective factors in physician well-being.30 From an international perspective, Japan’s workstyle reform resembles global efforts, such as the European Working Time Directive and the duty hour restrictions by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education in the USA.10 11 However, the Japanese model is distinctive owing to its centralised policy scope that includes all physicians rather than only trainees. While previous reviews showed mixed effects of duty hour reforms on resident wellness,31 32 the consistent decline in burnout in this study suggested that Japan’s system-level approach was more effective for sustainable workforce well-being.

Although these analyses were exploratory, subgroup analyses revealed that PGY2 residents, women and those working in urban hospitals had slightly lower burnout risk compared with their counterparts. The lower prevalence among PGY2 residents aligned with previous studies reporting that first-year trainees experienced higher emotional exhaustion owing to steeper learning curves and limited autonomy early in residency.33 34 However, interestingly, this finding differed from reports from the USA, where burnout prevalence has been reported to be higher among PGY2 than PGY1 residents.25 One possible explanation is that efforts to improve working conditions for PGY1 residents received considerable attention in the USA, potentially attenuating burnout risk in this group.

Regarding sex differences, the lower burnout among women in this study differed from several previous reports, where female residents demonstrated higher rates of emotional exhaustion or overall burnout.25 30 35 A recent study suggested that this difference reflected Japan’s unique sex and work culture, where women were often expected to balance clinical duties with family responsibilities, while men faced stronger pressure to work long hours and assume greater professional responsibilities. Female residents experienced relatively lower work-related stress and emotional exhaustion, possibly contributing to their lower burnout levels. Additionally, using a single-item burnout measure may have underestimated depersonalisation, a component more common among men.36 Nonetheless, the mechanisms underlying these sex differences remain incompletely understood. Future studies exploring organisational, cultural and social factors associated with lower burnout among female residents in Japan could provide insights to inform efforts to improve physician well-being and promote gender equity internationally. Furthermore, residents in urban hospitals had a lower burnout risk, consistent with previous reports showing higher burnout levels among physicians working in rural areas.37

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, burnout was defined using a single self-reported item rather than the full Mini-Z 2.0 instrument or the Maslach Burnout Inventory.38 Although single-item measures of emotional exhaustion have correlated reasonably with validated instruments, they may not fully capture the multifaceted nature of burnout, including depersonalisation and reduced personal accomplishment. Furthermore, since the full Mini-Z was not administered, we were unable to evaluate other workplace factors known to be associated with burnout, such as time pressure, work control, workplace culture and chaotic work environments. Future studies using more comprehensive instruments could clarify the relative contribution of these factors to resident well-being. Second, each fiscal year’s survey involved different cohorts of residents rather than a longitudinally followed population. Third, since the data were based on self-reported questionnaires, potential biases, such as recall bias and social desirability bias, cannot be dismissed. Fourth, there is a potential selection/participation bias, as residents experiencing burnout may have chosen not to take the examination.

Fifth, while the study included residents from several hospitals nationwide, the participating institutions may not fully represent all clinical training environments in Japan. Sixth, since the implementation of the national workstyle reform occurred during a period when the COVID-19 pandemic was receding, the individual contributions of these factors to the observed changes in burnout and working conditions could not be determined. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether the effects of the workstyle reform were attenuated or amplified by concurrent changes related to the pandemic. Therefore, causal attribution of the observed trends should be interpreted with caution. Finally, although several workload-related measures changed significantly over time, the corresponding Cohen’s h effect sizes were generally small. Additionally, the absolute reduction in burnout prevalence was modest. Therefore, the practical significance of these observed changes may have been limited despite their statistical significance.

Conclusions

In this large nationwide study of Japanese residents, burnout peaked during the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequently declined, along with improvements in working conditions following the implementation of the national workstyle reform. The marked reductions in working hours, emergency duties and inpatient loads after the reform, along with increases in self-study time and job satisfaction, suggested that improvements in the working environment were associated with reduced burnout. Hence, Japan’s nationwide reform provides a real-world example of how coordinated system-level initiatives enhance sustainability in postgraduate medical training and support a healthier, more balanced medical workforce.
