Cerebrofacial arteriovenous metameric syndrome: Case report and brief literature review
Authors: Prapthi Jayesh Sirrkay, Rashid Skeik, Yasha Kayan, Jesse Manunga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCerebrofacial arteriovenous metameric syndrome (CAMS) is a rare, noninherited vascular malformation characterized by an arteriovenous malformation of the cerebral, orbital, and facial structures. Patients with CAMS often present with visual loss, progressive neurological deficits, seizures, and or bleeding. They may also present with cosmetic concerns such as skin discoloration, proptosis and facial asymmetry. Diagnosis of CAMS is based on history, clinical presentation, and findings on cerebral computed tomography and/or magnetic resonance imaging. We share a case of a young man who presented with mildly symptomatic right cerebrofacial arteriovenous malformation reported since childhood and imaging findings consistent with CAMS. We also provide a brief literature review and comparison of reported cases of CAMS. This paper underscores the significance of recognizing the vascular manifestations in patients with CAMS. Further research on the presentation and diagnosis of CAMS is critical to provide evidence-based treatment options to improve outcomes.Keywords: Arteriorvenous malformation, Carotid artery, Cerebrofacial arteriovenous metameric syndrome, Vascular surgery

This study highlights the importance of early recognition and diagnosis of cerebrofacial arteriovenous metameric syndrome (CAMS), a rare and complex vascular malformation.1,2 This case report provides a brief literature review and detailed case analysis to underscore the significance of individualized management strategies. The finding suggests the value of regular imaging surveillance to mitigate complications and allow for timely interventions. Additionally, this case report expands the limited information available on CAMS to foster the development of standardized diagnostic criteria and therapeutic strategies to improve overall clinical management. The patient provided written informed consent for the case report and imaging details. The patient has provided consent to have his case and related images published.

Case report

A 27-year-old Caucasian man with a history of vacuum suction birth presented with right upper lip swelling and cheek enlargement since the age of 7 years. His symptoms have progressed over the years and started to cause mild discomfort and cosmetic concerns without affecting his daily activities. At presentation, his blood pressure was 130/80 mmHg, and his heart rate was 75 beats per minute. The physical examination revealed right conjunctival erythema and pulsatile right facial mass involving the right upper lip up to the infra-orbital area, causing obvious asymmetry (Fig 1).

The patient had an adenoidectomy at the age of 4 years but had no other significant medical history. There is no family history of vascular disease. The patient’s blood workup including metabolic profiles and complete blood count were unremarkable.

A head and orbital magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) without and with intravenous contrast revealed evidence of an extensive right facial arteriovenous malformation (AVM) with arterial feeders arising from the external carotid artery. The AVM involved the superficial right nasal, lip, premaxillary, and mandibular soft tissues (Fig 2, A). The MRI also showed intranidal aneurysms in the right anterior premaxillary soft tissues and along the anterior wall of the right maxillary sinus, each about 1 cm in size. The intracranial involvement was centered in the suprasellar region and involved the right cavernous sinus and right orbit (Fig 2, B). The overall extent of this vascular malformation measured up to approximately 3 × 2 cm, similar to the prior MRI obtained 1 year earlier when remeasured in a similar fashion.

A digital subtraction angiogram confirmed the presence of a 3 × 1.5 × 1.5 cm AVM at the medial aspect of the supraclinoid right internal carotid artery (Fig 3, A) and a diffuse AVM behind the right globe involving the retina with drainage to the cavernous sinus. The AVM is supplied by the ophthalmic artery, which arises from the internal carotid artery, and by the internal maxillary and facial arteries, both of which arise from the external carotid artery. The right ophthalmic artery was enlarged, and a 5-mm feeding artery aneurysm in the nose was visualized (Fig 3, B). In addition, the images confirmed the right maxillary AVM is supplied by an enlarged internal maxillary artery that harbors 7- and 10-mm feeding artery aneurysms (Fig 3, C). The right facial artery is enlarged and harbors a 12-mm feeding artery aneurysm and ultimately leads to an upper lip nidus, again with facial vein drainage (Fig 3, D).

The case was discussed within a multidisciplinary team that included vascular medicine, neurointerventional radiology, and vascular surgery. The first line of management would favor minimally invasive endovascular approaches, primarily neurointerventional techniques. In the event of treatment, open surgical repair would involve a coordinated approach between vascular surgery and otolaryngology, with plastic surgery participation to optimize reconstructive and cosmetic outcomes. Based on multidisciplinary discussions that also involved the patient, a decision was made for conservative management and surveillance given the complexity of the AVM and mild symptoms. Conservative management included blood pressure monitoring with goal of less than 120/80 mmHg, and avoidance of straining exercise. The patient is scheduled for a follow-up assessment in 2 years or sooner if symptoms worsen with a repeat MRI study.

Discussion

Cerebral vascular malformations can manifest in various forms and may lead to serious complications. These malformations encompass a range of vascular pathologies that differ significantly in their morphology, clinical presentation, and prognosis. History and clinical and imaging findings are important tools to help make the correct diagnosis.3 CAMS refers to an extremely rare group of nonhereditary unilateral AVMs involving the metameric distribution including the craniofacial region.4 This rare disorder comprises only about 0.5% of all cerebrovascular malformations.5 Few cases of CAMS have been documented in the literature (Table).

CAMS typically manifests in childhood, although it can affect individuals of all ages. The clinical symptoms are varied and may include muscle weakness, headache, red eye, epistaxis, facial discoloration, gait ataxia, seizures, and other neurological deficits.6 Our patient presented with right conjunctival erythema and facial asymmetry caused by a pulsatile mass extending from the right upper lip to the infra-orbital area. The patient also reported a complicated birth using vacuum suction, which could have been associated with the development of the vascular malformation.

CAMS can be classified into three groups based on the area of involvement. CAMS I refers to the involvement of the medial prosencephalic structures including the hypothalamus, pituitary, and nose. CAMS II is characterized by the involvement of the lateral prosencephalic structures, including the occipital lobe, thalamus, optic tract, retina, and maxilla. CAMS III is considered when the lateral rhombencephalon structures are involved, which include the cerebellum, nasal bridge, and mandible.7

Cerebral AVMs are usually diagnosed using MRI and can be confirmed via catheter based angiography.1 Based on presentation, clinical and imaging findings using MRI and catheter-based cerebral angiogram, our patient meets the criteria for the diagnosis of CAMS type II.

Management is based on presenting symptoms and complications such as intracranial hemorrhage, or arterial and nervous involvement. The general approach for asymptomatic patients or for those with mild symptoms is conservative management with ongoing monitoring and blood pressure control. Invasive interventions such as endovascular embolization, stereotactic radiosurgery, and microsurgical resection are usually reserved for patients with severe symptoms or significant complications.6 Indication for intervention includes rapid aneurysmal growth, development of new facial aneurysms, and symptoms. An intracranial aneurysm may be treated using a variety of techniques, including intrasaccular or intravascular flow diversion, coil embolization, or a combination thereof. An extracranial aneurysm may be treated using coil embolization or a liquid embolic agent, depending on the anatomy and its relationship to the AVM. Our patient was managed conservatively based on mild symptoms and complex AVM.

All the aneurysms are located on the face rather than within the intracranial space. The natural history of facial aneurysms differs significantly from that of intracranial aneurysms. Intracranial arteries lack the normal three-layer vessel wall structure found elsewhere in the body, making intracranial aneurysms a distinct disease process with a unique risk profile that does not apply to extracranial facial aneurysms. Even if hypothetically a facial aneurysm were to rupture, the bleeding, although possibly severe, could likely be controlled until definitive endovascular or surgical management could be achieved with a low risk of long-term life-threatening consequences. Therefore, conservative management is reasonable in this case.

The literature was systematically reviewed to identify and compare known cases of CAMS by searching PubMed databases for full-text articles published during any time. The keyword used was ‘cerebrofacial arteriovenous metameric syndrome.’ The search yielded 12 published relevant cases of CAMS. Among these cases, CAMS presented dominantly in childhood, with ages ranging from 3 months to 49 years. Among the 12 published cases, gender distribution was nearly balanced, with five males and seven females. Similar to our case, diagnosis was primarily achieved through MRI, with some cases requiring additional imaging such as carotid catheter-based angiography. Although our patient is being managed conservatively, most published cases opted for embolization, with varying degrees of success. Two cases reported successful embolization outcomes, whereas one case was explicitly noted as unsuccessful. Unlike other cases, our patient presented with a rare pulsatile facial mass and has not had a complication up to the date of this publication. Our approach aligns with other cases where conservative treatment was chosen, although long-term outcomes in such cases remain underreported. Our case further highlights the importance of surveillance to monitor disease progression.

Conclusions

We present a rare case of type II CAMS, which was treated conservatively with blood pressure monitoring and surveillance. We also provide a brief literature summary of currently reported cases with CAMS. More research is needed to help standardize the diagnosis and management for this rare disorder.
