Racial/ethnic disparities in neighborhood physical activity environments: Comparing Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander and Asian Americans in the United States
Authors: Ning Xiong, Neng Wan, Ming Wen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNative Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (NHPI) populations have often been overlooked in health equity research due to their aggregation with Asian Americans. This study compares associations of NHPI and Asian population proportions with physical activity environments (PAEs) at the census tract level across the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska, using 2020 Census and 2018 neighborhood data. We analyze four PAE domains—built environment, physical facilities, natural environment, and social environment—using regression models controlling for urbanicity and state. Findings reveal diverging patterns. NHPI proportion is negatively associated with favorable built environment (e.g., physical walkability indicators), physical facilities, and social environment in the contiguous U.S., but shows strong positive associations with favorable natural environment in Hawaii. In contrast, Asian proportion is positively associated with favorable built environment, physical facilities, and social environment in the contiguous U.S., but negatively associated with favorable natural environment, with positive social environment associations in Hawaii. Non-Hispanic White co-residence is associated with better natural environment and physical facilities in Asian tracts but worse built environment in the contiguous U.S. Non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic proportions are associated with better built environments but worse in the other three PAE domains, especially in the contiguous U.S. Alaska's results are limited by small sample sizes. Controlling for poverty strengthens NHPI's natural environment associations, suggesting that these patterns are not explained solely by socioeconomic disadvantages. This study shows that aggregated data mask group-specific PAEs, highlighting NHPI's unique PAE profiles and underscoring the value of disaggregated evidence for informing context-sensitive public health research and intervention design.Keywords: Physical activity environments, Native Hawaiian and other pacific islander (NHPI), Asian, Data disaggregation, Neighborhood

Introduction

Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (NHPI) populations, totaling 1.59 million and comprising 0.5% of the United States (U.S.) population in 2020 (Bureau, 2020), are among the fastest-growing racial groups, with projections indicating a remarkable growth rate of 100.8% by 2060 (Colby & Ortman, 2015). Yet, their health conditions and neighborhood environments remain understudied compared to those of other racial and ethnic groups. NHPI communities face a disproportionately high and rising burden of health challenges, including higher rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease (Bitton et al., 2010; Hawley & McGarvey, 2015; Madan et al., 2012; Mau et al., 2009; Moy et al., 2010). Engagement in physical activity is critical for reducing the risk of these conditions (Aune et al., 2015), but NHPIs are less physically active than the general U.S. population. For example, a California study shows that about 80% of NHPI adults failed to meet physical activity recommendations (Moy et al., 2010). National data aggregating Asian and NHPI populations indicate that only around 39.0% of adults in this population met lifestyle physical activity guidelines, lower than the 45.4% U.S. average (Kruger et al., 2004).

Neighborhood physical activity environments (PAEs) (Ding et al., 2011; Franzini et al., 2010; Giles et al., 2021; Kegler et al., 2022; Sallis, 2009), including the built environment (Cleland et al., 2019; Ewing & Cervero, 2010; Sallis et al., 2012), physical facilities (Roux et al., 2007), natural environment (Dalton et al., 2016; Shanahan et al., 2016), and social environment (Florez et al., 2018; McNeill et al., 2006; Wang et al., 2022) domains, have been linked with individuals’ physical activity engagement. However, the distribution of these environments has been influenced by historically rooted racial and ethnic hierarchies and political–economic processes that govern land use, infrastructure investment, and neighborhood development. For NHPI populations, these structural forces operate differently across regional contexts. In Hawaii, NHPI communities have been influenced by a complex historical trajectory that includes nineteenth-century missionary influence and expanding foreign commercial interests, contributing to the illegal overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893 and subsequent U.S. annexation, followed by the consolidation of foreign political authority, the restructuring of land tenure and governance systems, and the expansion of plantation and tourism economies that reoriented land ownership, infrastructure investment, and economic priorities (Goodyear-Kaopua et al., 2014; Kameʻ;eleihiwa, 1992; Silva, 2004; Trask, 1999). These developments unfolded over time and collectively reshaped political authority, public policy, and land access in ways that privileged foreign commercial elites while constraining opportunities available to Native Hawaiians and other racialized communities. Policies such as the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act and the establishment of Native Hawaiian Homelands, while intended to address historical dispossession, have also influenced residential patterns and provisions of infrastructure relevant to physical activity and continue to influence where NHPI can reside.

In the contiguous U.S., NHPI settlement patterns reflect a range of historical and structural processes, including domestic migration from Hawaii and within the mainland U.S., as well as, for certain Pacific Islander populations (particularly from Micronesian nations), migration influenced by post–World War II geopolitical arrangements and U.S. military governance in the Pacific (Hess et al., 2001; Hezel, 2001). Among these, mechanisms such as the Compacts of Free Association facilitated migration for certain Pacific Islander groups and have been associated with settlement in under-resourced neighborhoods influenced by broader histories of urban disinvestment and unequal governance (Hess et al., 2001; McElfish et al., 2015). These regionally distinct historical and geopolitical processes provide important context for understanding observed variation in neighborhood PAEs across the U.S.

In contrast, Asian populations in the U.S. have been racialized through distinct logics mainly tied to immigration regimes, labor stratification, selective inclusion, and the “model minority” narrative, resulting in different patterns of urban settlement, socioeconomic positioning, and municipal investment (Lee, 2015; Lee & Zhou, 2015). Asian migration to the U.S., including to Hawaii, Alaska, and the contiguous U.S., has largely occurred through labor recruitment and immigration policies rather than Indigenous dispossession or treaty-based relocation, producing different relationships to land, housing, and neighborhood infrastructure. These divergent racialized histories suggest that NHPI and Asian populations may be embedded in structurally distinct neighborhood PAEs, even within the same regions.

Research on PAE differences often overlooks NHPI populations, largely due to limited data available and data aggregation with Asian American populations (Ghosh, 2010; Kanaya et al., 2022). Such aggregation obscures meaningful heterogeneity in PAEs and risks producing interventions that are misaligned with NHPI-specific structural, place-based, and historically contingent contexts by failing to account for differences in neighborhood PAEs (Taparra, 2021; Taparra & Pellegrin, 2022). Disaggregating NHPI and Asian subgroups is critical for identifying PAE differences and informing interventions tailored to each group's structural and place-based context, thereby improving their effectiveness (Kaholokula et al., 2018; Mau et al., 2010; Okamoto et al., 2014; Palmer-Wackerly et al., 2014). While efforts to disaggregate traditionally aggregated or underrepresented populations have been undertaken in other contexts, including studies of immigrant food environments (Chuquitarco-Morales et al., 2024) and marginalized populations in Europe (La Parra-Casado et al., 2023), very little U.S. national-scale evidence exists on differences in neighborhood PAEs between NHPI and Asian populations, particularly across diverse regions like the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska.

This study addresses this crucial gap by analyzing the associations between the proportion of NHPI and Asian populations and PAE indicators in U.S. census tracts with over 5% NHPI or Asian populations, using 2020 Census and 2018 neighborhood data. To better understand the racialized dynamics of place, we also explore how the presence of non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic populations relates to these PAEs in NHPI and Asian neighborhoods. This sheds light on whether racial integration or co-residence with historically advantaged or disadvantaged groups is associated with PAE differences, potentially reflecting structural racial hierarchies in multiracial neighborhood conditions. The analysis covers four PAE domains—built environment, physical facilities, natural environment, and social environment—and accounts for regional variations across the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska. By situating empirical patterns within these historically rooted structural and spatial logics, this study provides one of the first U.S. national, multi-domain assessments within the PAE literature of neighborhood PAEs among NHPI and Asian populations. These historical and structural racial logics provide an interpretive framework for the observed differences in PAEs discussed below.

Methods

Study area and sample

Our study area includes census tracts in the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska, each typically encompassing about 4000 residents and commonly used as proxies for neighborhoods. We initially identified 580 census tracts with >5% NHPI (alone or in combination with other races) and 24,595 census tracts with >5% Asian (alone or in combination with other races) populations. These are referred to as NHPI tracts or Asian tracts. We include multiracial individuals to ensure accurate representation of diverse populations in data and policy and maintain consistency with federal reporting standards, while enabling broader geographic analysis. The 5% threshold ensures meaningful representation of each group within a tract, thereby improving the reliability of subgroup comparisons. Although this threshold may exclude tracts with smaller but potentially meaningful NHPI or Asian populations, including such tracts would increase measurement noise and reduce comparability, particularly for smaller populations; this trade-off is therefore methodologically justified.

Population data were obtained from the 2020 Decennial Census, which offers full-count data rather than estimates, providing greater precision, particularly for smaller racial/ethnic groups such as NHPI and Asian populations. These data are used to characterize neighborhood population composition in the analysis. To match 2018 neighborhood PAE data using 2010 census tract boundaries, we crosswalked 2020 population data to 2010 boundaries using the U.S. Census Bureau's relationship files with land area weighting for geographic consistency. This approach is widely used in spatial and neighborhood health research to harmonize data across changing census boundaries (Logan et al., 2014) and represents a standard method for conducting consistent small-area analyses across the 2010 and 2020 censuses at the national level. Although our PAE variables are based on 2018 sources, we selected the 2020 Decennial Census over the 2018 American Community Survey (ACS) to improve accuracy and avoid sampling error. While crosswalking introduces some spatial imprecision, the gain in population count accuracy justifies the approach. As a result, the analysis involves a temporal mismatch between population (2020) and PAE (2018) data. To minimize potential confusion for readers, findings should be interpreted as reflecting cross-sectional associations between neighborhood population composition and PAEs measured within a narrow two-year window, rather than causal relationships. Given the relative stability of neighborhood PAEs over short periods, this mismatch is unlikely to meaningfully affect interpretation while improving demographic accuracy.

After excluding 65 NHPI tracts and 220 Asian tracts due to missing data and removing 7 NHPI tracts that were the only such tracts in their states (thereby lacking within-state variation), the final analytic sample includes 508 NHPI tracts and 24,375 Asian tracts, covering 87.59% and 99.11% of qualifying tracts, and 98.95% and 99.76% of the respective 2020 NHPI and Asian populations in those qualifying tracts. The number of qualifying tracts by state is reported in online Supplementary Appendix Table A.1.

Data and measures

We used tract-level data to examine associations between racial/ethnic composition—specifically, proportions of NHPI and Asian populations—and PAEs across four domains: built environment, physical facilities, natural environment, and social environment. All data were drawn from 2018 or the nearest available year to ensure temporal alignment across datasets, as 2018 is the most recent year for which nationally comprehensive built environment measures are available from the Smart Location Database (SLD) (Version 3.0) (Chapman et al., 2021). All variables were harmonized to the 2010 census tract boundaries for geographic consistency.

Because racial and ethnic classifications are institutionally defined, we explicitly followed the operationalization used by the U.S. Census Bureau. We derived measures of racial/ethnic composition from the 2020 Decennial Census, which uses a self-identification framework in which respondents may select one or more racial categories, while Hispanic or Latino origin is collected separately as an ethnicity. Accordingly, we constructed measures for NHPI and Asian populations using two definitions: individuals identifying as NHPI or Asian alone, and individuals identifying as NHPI or Asian alone or in combination with other races. For contextual comparison, we also included measures of non-Hispanic White (alone), non-Hispanic Black (alone), and Hispanic (of any race) populations. Both proportions and total counts of these populations were included and aligned to 2010 tract boundaries using the crosswalk approach described in Section 2.1.

Urbanicity data was obtained from the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, which uses National Center for Education Statistics (NCES) classifications based on 2010 Census data. The five categories include city, suburb, town, rural, and mixed areas. Poverty rate was calculated as the proportion of individuals with incomes below the federal poverty threshold, based on the 2014–2018 ACS 5-Year Estimates. These variables accounted for regional and socioeconomic contextual differences that may confound the relationship between racial/ethnic composition and neighborhood PAEs.

We captured neighborhood built environment characteristics derived using data from SLD Version 3.0. Key indicators included land use mix, road network density, street intersection density (excluding auto-oriented intersections), and transit access.

Physical facilities data came from the 2018 National Neighborhood Data Archive (NaNDA) (Melendez, Finlay, Clarke, Noppert, Gypin, et al., 2024). These measures included the densities of recreational and physical fitness centers, membership sports and recreation clubs, specialized recreational establishments, and public golf courses and driving ranges. Natural environment characteristics included summertime maximum Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) values from the Open Science Framework, indicating peak vegetation greenness within each census tract by offering a seasonally consistent measure of natural environment quality for assessing nature exposure (Browning et al., 2022). Additional measures included tree canopy cover rate derived from the 2018 National Land Cover Dataset USFS dataset, open park counts (top-coded at 10), and the proportion of park area within each tract from the NaNDA (Melendez et al., 2022).

Neighborhood social capital was captured using multiple indicators. Following previous research by Chetty et al. (2018), we included the Census self-response rate from the 2020 Decennial Census as a proxy for social capital and civic engagement. Like the population data, the self-response rate, originally available in 2020 tract boundaries, was crosswalked to 2010 boundaries using land area weighting to maintain consistency. Additional indicators from the NaNDA included the densities of civic and social associations, youth organizations, veterans’ organizations, and religious organizations (Melendez et al., 2024).

Analytical approach

We examined the relationship between racial/ethnic composition—specifically, the proportion of NHPI and Asian populations—and PAEs across four domains. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression analyses were conducted separately for census tracts meeting the 5% population threshold for each racial/ethnic group.

We estimated two sets of models for each outcome: one controlling for state fixed effects and urbanicity, and another additionally controlling for tract-level poverty rate. The first model captures the overall association between racial/ethnic composition and PAEs, reflecting the joint patterning of structural and socioeconomic differences. The second model adjusts for tract-level poverty to assess whether associations persist beyond socioeconomic disadvantage. For each set, we conducted four OLS regressions separately for qualifying NHPI and Asian tracts: (1) nationwide, including the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska; (2) the contiguous U.S. only; (3) Hawaii only; and (4) Alaska only. The Hawaii- and Alaska-specific models excluded state fixed effects due to their single-state scope. This tiered approach allows us to differentiate national trends from region-specific dynamics, particularly relevant given the geographic concentration of NHPI populations in Hawaii, and to evaluate whether patterns observed in the contiguous U.S. are consistent in non-contiguous states.

We conducted sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings to alternative model specifications and key analytic decisions. First, we re-estimated all OLS regressions weighted by the NHPI or Asian population size within each tract, depending on which racial/ethnic group was used as the independent variable. This approach accounts for variation in subgroup size across tracts, giving more influence on areas where the focal population is more numerically present. Weighting improves the stability of estimates, reduces the influence of tracts with very small subgroup populations (which may introduce noise), and ensures that the results more accurately reflect the distribution of PAEs across the population rather than individual-level exposure or experience. Second, we re-estimated all regressions using the proportion of NHPI or Asian individuals identifying as alone, rather than alone or in combination with other races, as the independent variable. These models were run using census tracts where the NHPI or Asian alone population exceeded 5%, to evaluate whether our findings were sensitive to how racial/ethnic identification was defined.

To provide additional context, we conducted supplementary analyses examining how the proportion of other racial/ethnic groups—specifically, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic populations—relates to neighborhood PAEs in NHPI and Asian tracts. These analyses assess whether co-residence with historically advantaged (White) or disadvantaged (Black and Hispanic) populations is associated with PAEs, to describe contextual associations rather than infer causal mechanisms or lived experiences. Importantly, these analyses are intended to contextualize NHPI and Asian tracts within broader multiracial compositions rather than to treat non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, or Hispanic tracts as reference groups. White and Black populations were defined as non-Hispanic and identifying with a single race to avoid overlap with the Hispanic category. This coding prevents double-counting, reduces multicollinearity, and facilitates clearer interpretation of racial/ethnic associations in regression models, aligning with federal standards and conventions in public health research. These models were estimated using the same specifications and controls as the main analyses and are interpreted as descriptive of neighborhood context within NHPI and Asian tracts.

All continuous variables were standardized prior to analysis (mean = 0, SD = 1) to enable comparison of coefficient sizes across models.

Fig. 1 highlights the geographic concentration of NHPI tracts (n = 508), which are predominantly located in Hawaii, where the NHPI population is a significant demographic component; specific states like California, Washington, Utah, and Nevada also show clusters, particularly in urban areas such as Los Angeles, Seattle, and Salt Lake City, reflecting migration patterns of NHPI communities. Notably, only part of Hawaii is shown in the figure, as the state's smaller islands lack census tracts meeting the >5% NHPI threshold, highlighting the geographic specificity of NHPI populations. Built environment characteristics reveal both similarities and notable contrasts between NHPI and Asian tracts. Both exhibit comparable levels of mixed land use, with Asian tracts (mean: 0.531) showing slightly higher values than NHPI tracts (mean: 0.494) nationwide. However, Asian tracts have higher road network density (mean: 19.657 vs. 16.056) and street intersection density (mean: 98.905 vs. 80.157) than NHPI tracts, suggesting a more developed street network. The most pronounced difference is transit access, which is markedly lower in NHPI tracts than in Asian tracts (mean: 0.033 vs. 0.134), suggesting considerably more limited transit availability in NHPI tracts.

In the physical facilities domain, both NHPI and Asian tracts exhibit low densities of amenities, though key differences emerge. Asian tracts have a notably higher density of recreational and physical fitness centers (mean: 2.691) compared to NHPI tracts (mean: 1.225). This contrast represents one of the largest relative differences observed across the physical facility measures. The densities of membership sports and recreation clubs and specialized recreational establishments remain sparse across both groups, but are slightly more common in Asian tracts. Public golf course and driving ranges density is uniformly low in both NHPI (mean: 0.089) and Asian tracts (mean: 0.055).

Natural environment characteristics are broadly similar between NHPI and Asian tracts. NDVI values, which indicate vegetation greenness levels, are comparable across both groups. Tree canopy cover rate is slightly lower in NHPI tracts (mean: 0.150) than in Asian tracts (mean: 0.172). Open park counts are nearly identical (mean: 2.437 for NHPI; 2.393 for Asian), though NHPI tracts have a marginally higher proportion of park land (mean: 0.064 vs. 0.054).

In the social environment, Asian tracts consistently exhibit higher values than NHPI tracts across multiple indicators. Asian tracts show a higher census self-response rate (mean: 0.663) than NHPI tracts (mean: 0.583), suggesting greater social capital and civic engagement. They also have higher densities of civic and social associations (mean: 4.385 vs. 2.573), youth organizations (0.356 vs. 0.196), religious organizations (8.805 vs. 5.270), and veterans’ organizations (0.210 vs. 0.186). Among these indicators, the gaps in religious organization density and civic and social association density represent some of the largest absolute differences observed between the two groups.

Regional variations across the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska reveal distinct patterns for NHPI and Asian tracts. In the built environment, the contiguous U.S. shows higher transit access (NHPI: 0.094; Asian: 0.136) and street intersection density (NHPI: 101.587; Asian: 99.429) compared to Hawaii (transit: 0.000; intersection: 67.194 for NHPI; 67.502 for Asian) and Alaska (transit: 0.000; intersection: 82.194 for NHPI; 56.820 for Asian), with the absence of transit access in Hawaii and Alaska standing out as a particularly stark regional difference. Physical facility density is generally higher in the contiguous U.S., particularly for recreational and physical fitness centers in Asian tracts (2.711), compared to Hawaii (1.600) and Alaska (0.790), while NHPI tracts in Hawaii have higher membership sports and recreation club density (1.789) than the contiguous U.S. (0.459). Alaska stands out for stronger natural environment indicators, with higher NDVI values (NHPI: 0.572; Asian: 0.631) and park land proportion (NHPI: 0.115; Asian: 0.166) compared to the contiguous U.S. (NDVI: 0.474 for NHPI; 0.512 for Asian) and Hawaii (park proportion: 0.060 for NHPI; 0.059 for Asian). Social environment indicators are generally stronger in the contiguous U.S., with higher civic and social association density in Asian tracts (4.407) than in Hawaii (3.286) or Alaska (1.815), while NHPI tracts in Hawaii show higher religious organization density (5.527) compared to Alaska (6.144).

In summary, Asian tracts generally exhibit more developed infrastructure and greater social capital than NHPI tracts, with higher road network density and street intersection density, better transit access, greater fitness center and recreational facility density, and stronger civic engagement. Natural environment characteristics are largely similar, with Asian tracts having slightly more tree canopy cover rates and NHPI tracts having greater park land proportions. Regional differences show the contiguous U.S.’s urban advantages in transit access and facility provision, Hawaii's unique NHPI neighborhood characteristics, and Alaska's favorable natural environment.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess robustness to subgroup population size, we compared Table 3 (unweighted regressions) with online Supplementary Appendix Table A.3 (weighted by NHPI or Asian population size, alone or in combination with other races). Most associations between the proportion of NHPI or Asian populations and PAE variables across the U.S., contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska generally remain directionally consistent, but several effect sizes and levels of statistical significance change when weighting is applied. For the proportion of NHPI, weighting reveals stronger negative associations with favorable built environment indicators and weaker negative associations with favorable physical facilities. It also strengthens positive associations with favorable natural environment measures and negative associations with favorable social environment indicators. For the proportion of Asian, weighting generally weakens associations across the favorable built environment, natural environment, physical facilities, and social environment domains. Both tables consistently show that the proportion of Asian is positively associated with urban infrastructure, while the proportion of NHPI is positively associated with favorable natural environment features in the U.S.

To evaluate the sensitivity of results to definitions of racial/ethnic identification, we conducted additional analyses using NHPI and Asian populations identified as “alone” rather than “alone or in combination.” online Supplementary Appendix Table A.4 presents descriptive statistics by region for NHPI and Asian populations alone, while online Supplementary Appendix Figures A.1 and A.2 map census tracts across the U.S. where over 5% of the population identifies as NHPI or Asian alone. Comparing Table 3 and online Supplementary Appendix Table A.3 (unweighted and weighted regressions for NHPI and Asian populations alone or in combination; n = 508 and n = 24,375, respectively) with online Supplementary Appendix Tables A.5 and A.6 (unweighted and weighted regressions for NHPI and Asian populations alone; n = 319 and n = 19,460, respectively), we find that associations between NHPI and Asian proportions and PAE variables across the U.S., contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska generally remain directionally consistent, but vary in magnitude and statistical significance. The “alone” definition generally produces weaker associations than the broader “alone or in combination” approach.

Supplementary analyses

We conducted supplementary analyses to examine how the presence of other racial/ethnic groups—non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic populations—relates to PAEs within NHPI and Asian tracts. This analysis explores how co-residence with historically advantaged or disadvantaged groups is associated with variations in PAEs.

The regression associations between the proportions of non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic populations and PAEs in NHPI and Asian tracts are presented in online Supplementary Appendix Tables A.7 and A.8. In Asian tracts, the proportion of non-Hispanic White is positively associated with the favorable natural environment and physical facilities but generally negatively associated with the favorable built and social environments; the proportion of non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic populations is generally negatively associated with the favorable natural environment, physical facilities, and social environment but positively associated with the favorable built environment, with similar patterns in the contiguous U.S. and generally weaker or less significant associations in Hawaii and Alaska. In NHPI tracts, the proportion of non-Hispanic White is positively associated with favorable physical facilities and shows mixed or limited associations across other domains, while the proportions of non-Hispanic Blacks and Hispanic populations are generally negatively associated with the favorable social, natural, and built environments, with overall weaker and less consistent associations observed in the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska. To test the robustness of the above results, we also ran regressions in NHPI and Asian tracts defined using the “alone” classification, as shown in online Supplementary Appendix Tables A.9–A.10. In addition, we conducted regressions weighted by the corresponding population, presented in online Supplementary Appendix Tables A.11–A.14. All models yield similar results, confirming the stability of observed associations.

Discussion and conclusion

This study was motivated by the underrepresentation of NHPI populations in national environmental and health research and the limited availability of disaggregated data, which have constrained understanding of their health status and the corresponding neighborhood contexts. It also responds to the need for comparative analysis with Asian populations, who are often aggregated with NHPI in national data (Ghosh, 2010; Kanaya et al., 2022). Efforts to disaggregate traditionally aggregated or underrepresented populations have been undertaken in other contexts, such as studies of immigrant food environments (Chuquitarco-Morales et al., 2024) and marginalized populations in Europe (La Parra-Casado et al., 2023). Building on this broader work, this study examines disaggregated data across four PAE domains—built environment, physical facilities, natural environment, and social environment—and provides one of the first U.S. national assessments within the PAE literature of neighborhood PAEs among census tracts with at least 5% NHPI or Asian populations across the contiguous U.S., Hawaii, and Alaska.

Findings reveal clear and contrasting PAE patterns for NHPI and Asian populations that would be obscured in aggregated analyses, after controlling for state and urbanicity. The proportion of NHPI is negatively associated with favorable built environment, physical facilities, and social environment domains in the U.S. and contiguous U.S., but positively associated with favorable natural environment, particularly in Hawaii. In contrast, the proportion of Asian is positively associated with favorable built environment, physical facilities, and social environment domains but negatively associated with favorable natural environment in the U.S. and contiguous U.S. Importantly, these results document differences in the spatial distribution of PAE indicators across neighborhood contexts, rather than directly measuring lived experiences, access, or use of these PAEs.

The distinct association patterns observed for NHPI and Asian proportions suggest that these groups tend to be embedded in different neighborhood PAEs, based on parallel associations rather than direct between-group comparisons. NHPI proportion's positive associations with favorable natural environment, especially in Hawaii, highlight a regional pattern not observed elsewhere in the U.S. This pattern is most plausibly interpreted as being consistent with historically specific land governance and settlement processes, although the present analysis cannot adjudicate among competing explanations. These processes include historically specific governance transformations following nineteenth-century missionary influence, the illegal overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893, subsequent U.S. annexation, and the restructuring of land tenure and political authority that continue to structure land allocation and resource provision (Goodyear-Kaopua et al., 2014; Kameʻ;eleihiwa, 1992; Silva, 2004; Trask, 1999). Within this context, policies such as the Hawaiian Homes Commission Act and the establishment of Native Hawaiian Homelands—though intended to address historical dispossession—have influenced residential selection and infrastructure placement through state-structured land allocation, often situating NHPI households in areas with preserved natural landscapes but limited built facilities and institutional investment (Trask, 1999). Consistent with this interpretation, NHPI's negative associations with favorable built environment, physical facility, and social environment domains may reflect enduring patterns of infrastructural disinvestment, potentially influenced by racialized housing markets, land-use regulation, and unequal public investment (Trask, 1999).

In contrast, Asian proportion shows positive associations with favorable built environment, physical facilities, and social environment, patterns consistent with prior studies documenting greater infrastructure density and service availability in Asian-concentrated neighborhoods (Guan et al., 2023), and with broader scholarship on racialization through immigration regimes, labor stratification, selective inclusion, and differential incorporation into urban political economies (Lee, 2015; Lee & Zhou, 2015). Asian migration to the U.S., including to Hawaii, Alaska, and the contiguous U.S., has largely occurred through labor recruitment and immigration policies rather than Indigenous dispossession or treaty-based relocation, producing different relationships to land, housing, and neighborhood infrastructure. This pattern aligns with literature showing that immigration-based incorporation often occurs in dense contexts with relatively strong infrastructure provision (Lee, 2015; Lee & Zhou, 2015). The Asian proportion's negative association with the favorable natural environment is consistent with prior research documenting lower levels of green space and vegetation in Asian-concentrated neighborhoods (Klompmaker et al., 2023). These results are consistent with a trade-off where higher infrastructure and development intensity are associated with reduced green space availability, even across diverse state contexts (McDonald et al., 2023). These interpretations do not imply direct experiential advantage or disadvantage or evidence of causal disparities but instead highlight how distinct structural and racialized histories are associated with observed PAEs.

Regional differences add nuance. In Hawaii, NHPI proportion is uniquely positively associated with favorable natural environment indicators, while showing limited or negative associations with built, physical facility, and social environment, reflecting the continuing relevance of historically rooted land tenure and governance regimes, Native Hawaiian Homelands policy, and tourism-oriented development. By contrast, associations for Asian populations in Hawaii are fewer and largely confined to individual indicators, with positive associations observed for park land proportion and census self-response rate, which limits broader inference. Alaska's limited associations are likely due to smaller sample sizes, reducing statistical power.

In comparison, in the contiguous U.S., higher NHPI proportion is associated with less favorable built, physical facility, and social environment indicators, with no statistically significant associations observed for the natural environment, a pattern consistent with, but not definitive evidence of, incorporation into under-resourced contexts influenced by post–World War II geopolitical arrangements and U.S. military governance in the Pacific affecting certain Pacific Islander populations (Hess et al., 2001; Hezel, 2001; McElfish et al., 2015). Among these processes, post-war treaty arrangements, such as the Compacts of Free Association, facilitated migration for some NHPI populations and have been associated with settlement in under-resourced neighborhoods shaped by longstanding urban disinvestment and unequal governance (Hess et al., 2001; McElfish et al., 2015). These findings point to plausible structural contributors to PAE distributions while acknowledging that alternative explanations remain plausible and require further empirical study. In contrast, Asian proportion shows generally positive associations across built, physical facility, and social domains, alongside negative associations with the favorable natural environment, reinforcing how distinct racialization pathways are associated with different PAEs even within the same regional settings.

The effect of controlling for poverty also explains differences in results. For NHPI tracts, models without poverty control show positive associations with favorable natural environment indicators at the national level but limited significance in Hawaii; after poverty adjustment, these associations strengthen, and additional natural environment indicators become statistically significant, particularly in Hawaii. This pattern indicates that poverty partially suppresses the observed association between NHPI proportion and favorable natural environments, particularly in Hawaii, without implying that poverty fully explains these associations. Poverty adjustment also reveals clearer differences in physical facility environments for NHPI in Hawaii: negative associations with recreational and fitness centers strengthen, and additional significant negative associations emerge for membership sports and recreation clubs and specialized recreational establishments, suggesting that poverty masks part of the association between NHPI proportion and facility availability. These findings suggest that facility availability in NHPI neighborhoods is not fully explained by socioeconomic disadvantage alone and may reflect structural or spatial factors beyond socioeconomic disadvantage, such as land-use priorities. In contrast, negative associations between NHPI proportion and indicators in the built environment and social environment domains are slightly stronger after controlling for poverty. These results indicate that neighborhoods with higher NHPI proportions tend to be characterized by less favorable environments across these domains, net of poverty.

For Asian tracts, controlling for poverty clarifies associations between Asian proportion and natural environment indicators in the contiguous U.S., as initially negative associations with NDVI and tree canopy cover become statistically non-significant after adjustment. This pattern suggests that the observed negative associations largely reflect socioeconomic stratification rather than associations independent of poverty. In contrast, associations involving Asian proportion remain largely stable across models with and without poverty control for most built environment, physical facility, and social environment indicators. This stability suggests that these associations are less sensitive to socioeconomic adjustment than those observed for NHPI, consistent with documented dominant patterns of immigration-based incorporation into resource-rich contexts.

Supplementary analyses examine how the presence of non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and Hispanic populations is associated with PAEs of NHPIs and Asian Americans in NHPI and Asian tracts. In Asian tracts, non-Hispanic White co-residence is positively associated with favorable natural environment, physical facilities, and social environment, but is negatively associated with favorable built environment, suggesting that co-residence with more socioeconomically advantaged groups is linked to lower physical walkability alongside resource-rich contexts. In contrast, non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic proportions are positively associated with favorable built environment but negatively associated with other domains, reflecting higher physical walkable neighborhoods with fewer resources across certain PAE dimensions. In NHPI tracts, non-Hispanic White co-residence is positively associated with built and social resources, while non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic proportions exhibit mixed or negative associations, highlighting heterogeneity in how neighborhood racial composition relates to PAEs across groups. These patterns indicate that neighborhood racial composition is differentially associated with multiple dimensions of PAEs in NHPI and Asian tracts, suggesting that structural racial hierarchies may continue to influence PAEs within multiracial neighborhoods (Williams & Collins, 2001).

Non-significant results, particularly in Alaska, are not merely null findings but may reflect low statistical power due to small sample sizes or limited environmental variability across some PAE domains. For example, Alaska's sparse infrastructure and dispersed settlement patterns may limit the ability of census-tract level measures to capture meaningful environmental variability. Sensitivity analyses further support the robustness of the findings. Weighting by population strengthens observed associations between NHPI proportion and natural environment indicators. Using single-race definitions yields consistent but weaker associations, affirming the value of inclusive racial categorizations. These analyses underscore the importance of methodological transparency and cautious interpretation when examining structural inequities using cross-sectional data.

From a public health perspective, these findings have important theoretical and policy implications. Theoretically, they suggest that racial/ethnic-specific environmental contexts may be incorporated in models of physical activity behavior, motivating frameworks that reflect differential provisions of natural environments, infrastructure, and urban resources across NHPI and Asian populations (Kaholokula et al., 2018). This challenges one-size-fits-all interventions and underscores the value of disaggregated data to guide health equity efforts (Ghosh, 2010; Kanaya et al., 2022). Policy-wise, interventions for NHPI neighborhoods may prioritize addressing structurally shaped constraints, such as deficits in infrastructure and social resources, while leveraging existing favorable natural environments, particularly in Hawaii, through context-sensitive programs (Mau et al., 2010; Okamoto et al., 2014; Palmer-Wackerly et al., 2014). For Asian neighborhoods, the findings indicate limited natural environmental features, highlighting green space provision as a potentially important consideration within structurally constrained settings. The racial composition analyses also may prioritize the importance of considering structural racial hierarchies in integrated neighborhoods through more equitable resource allocation, particularly where non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic populations remain underserved. Urban planners and public health officials may consider acknowledging and responding to the legacy of uneven investment to ensure that resources are equitably distributed across racially mixed communities, not just affluent or majority-White ones. Regional variations further underscore the importance of place-based interventions to promote health equity, while cautioning against overgeneralization of findings.

Limitations of this study include small sample sizes in Alaska (n = 24 NHPI tracts; n = 66 Asian tracts), which likely reduced statistical power and may account for the limited associations observed in this region and constrain the generalizability of the findings. The temporal mismatch between 2020 population data and 2018 PAE data may introduce minor inaccuracies, though partially mitigated by the reliability of 2020 decennial census data. The study's focus on census tracts with greater than 5% NHPI or Asian populations may exclude less concentrated areas, potentially underrepresenting diverse neighborhood contexts. However, this threshold was selected to ensure analytic stability and reliable subgroup comparisons for relatively small populations. Most importantly, given the cross-sectional design and limited subgroup sample sizes, particularly in Alaska, the findings should be interpreted as associational and do not support causal inference regarding relationships between racial composition and environmental characteristics. In addition, the absence of nationally consistent, fine-scale data on ocean and freshwater access prevented the inclusion of comparable blue-space measures. As a result, the natural environment domain examined here may incompletely represent environmentally relevant contexts in water-dominated settings. Finally, the cross-sectional design and use of tract-level indicators limit our ability to assess how neighborhood PAEs translate into lived experiences, access, or health outcomes.

Future studies may employ longitudinal designs incorporating individual-level data to clarify how these PAEs translate into lived experiences, physical activity behaviors, and health outcomes among NHPI and Asian populations. Future work would benefit from incorporating blue-space indicators, such as shoreline proximity, coastal access, or freshwater resources, using regionally tailored or longitudinal data. Future research could also extend this work by comparing NHPI and Asian neighborhood PAE patterns with those observed among other Indigenous populations (e.g., American Indians and Alaska Natives living on ancestral lands) or other immigrant groups in the U.S., to assess whether the observed associations reflect group-specific dynamics or broader structural settlement processes. Investigating sub-groups within Asian populations could further disaggregate findings, providing additional nuance.
