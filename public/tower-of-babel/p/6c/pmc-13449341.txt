Transcatheter closure of perimembranous ventricular septal defect with right coronary cusp prolapse using the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II device: A noval systematic multicenter experience from Iraq
Authors: Aso F Salih, Mohammed Rasool, Ammar Ali Al-Ubedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerimembranous ventricular septal defect (PM-VSD) with right coronary cusp (RCC) prolapse carries a high risk of progressive aortic regurgitation and often lacks a measurable aortic rim, complicating device closure. While surgery remains the standard treatment, transcatheter therapy using the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II (ADO II) offers a less invasive option, though data in Middle Eastern populations are scarce.ObjectiveTo evaluate the feasibility, safety, and intermediate-term outcomes of ADO II closure in PM-VSD patients with RCC prolapse.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective multicenter study of 29 consecutive patients (mean age: 8.86 ± 5.77 years; 55.2% male) with PM-VSD and RCC prolapse who underwent ADO II closure at two Iraqi cardiac centers (January 2023–November 2024). Inclusion criteria required an effective flow diameter ≤3 mm (left ventricular side), hemodynamic significance (Qp:Qs > 1.5 or left ventricular volume overload), and absence of severe aortic regurgitation or pulmonary hypertension.ResultsDevice implantation succeeded in 28 of 29 patients (96.6%). Immediate complete closure occurred in 23 patients (79.3%); 5 had small residual shunts (<2 mm). One patient required surgical conversion. At 6 months, complete closure persisted in 23 patients (79.3%), and 27 patients (93.1%) had favorable outcomes (complete closure or mild valvular regurgitation only). Critically, no new-onset aortic regurgitation developed, and baseline mild aortic insufficiency did not worsen.ConclusionTranscatheter PM-VSD closure with ADO II is feasible, safe, and effective in carefully selected patients with RCC prolapse—even without a traditional aortic rim. This first systematic multicenter experience from Iraq supports expanding minimally invasive therapy for this high-risk subgroup in resource-constrained settings.Keywords: Perimembranous ventricular septal defect, Right coronary cusp prolapse, Amplatzer Duct Occluder II, Transcatheter closure, Aortic valve regurgitation

Introduction

Ventricular septal defect (VSD) remains the most common congenital heart disease, accounting for 20–30% of all cardiac congenital anomalies [1]. While many VSDs close spontaneously—especially muscular types (62.1% closure rate)—perimembranous VSDs (PM-VSDs) carry a higher risk of complications, notably aortic cusp prolapse and subsequent aortic regurgitation due to the Venturi effect induced by high-velocity shunting [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. Surgical repair has long been the gold standard for PM-VSD with aortic valve involvement, especially in centers such as Ibn Al-Nafees and Ibn Al-Bitar in Iraq [6,7]. However, surgical outcomes remain suboptimal in high-risk subgroups—particularly infants under one year of age, those weighing less than 10 kg, and patients with syndromic conditions or pulmonary arterial hypertension—with reported complication rates as high as 58.3% in recent Kurdish multicenter data [8] (see Table 6)

Although transcatheter VSD closure using devices such as the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II (ADO II) has emerged as a less invasive alternative—with favorable features including low profile, flexibility, and secure waist design [9,10]—evidence on its safety and efficacy in PM-VSD cases with aortic cusp prolapse remains limited, especially in the Iraqi and broader Middle Eastern pediatric populations. Existing international studies report promising mid-term outcomes but also highlight risks such as tricuspid regurgitation and aortic valve dysfunction, underscoring the need for careful patient selection and technical precision [[11], [12], [13], [14]].

To date, no local or regional study has systematically evaluated the feasibility, safety, and mid-term outcomes of transcatheter closure using ADO II specifically in PM-VSD patients with aortic cusp prolapse in Kurdistan or Iraq. This study therefore addresses a critical gap by providing the first detailed assessment of this interventional approach in a high-risk cohort where surgical risk is elevated and access to advanced care may be constrained. Our findings aim to inform local clinical guidelines and contribute to the global evidence base on minimally invasive VSD management in complex anatomical settings.

Methodology

This study was a retrospective, multicenter cohort analysis of 29 consecutive pediatric patients with perimembranous ventricular septal defect (PM-VSD) and right coronary cusp (RCC) prolapse, with or without mild aortic insufficiency (AI), who underwent transcatheter closure using the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II (ADO II) device. Procedures were performed between January 2023 and November 2024 at two tertiary cardiac centers in Iraq.

•Center 1: Anwar Shexa Hospital, Al-Sulaymaniyah—a private, tertiary-level cardiology and teaching center equipped with a state-of-the-art interventional cardiology suite and staffed by expert congenital interventional cardiologists.

Center 1: Anwar Shexa Hospital, Al-Sulaymaniyah—a private, tertiary-level cardiology and teaching center equipped with a state-of-the-art interventional cardiology suite and staffed by expert congenital interventional cardiologists.

•Center 2: Ibn Al-Bitar Cardiac Center, Baghdad—a national referral center for pediatric and congenital cardiology with a well-established interventional program.

Center 2: Ibn Al-Bitar Cardiac Center, Baghdad—a national referral center for pediatric and congenital cardiology with a well-established interventional program.

Device selection rationale

The ADO II device was selected for its anatomical suitability in small PM-VSDs with aortic involvement.

•Low-profile waist minimizes protrusion into the aortic root, reducing the risk of aortic valve interference.

Low-profile waist minimizes protrusion into the aortic root, reducing the risk of aortic valve interference.

•Flexible nitinol structure with polyester fabric allows conformable seating without excessive radial force.

Flexible nitinol structure with polyester fabric allows conformable seating without excessive radial force.

•Dual-disc design with short waist is ideal for narrow, tunnel-type PM-VSDs.

Dual-disc design with short waist is ideal for narrow, tunnel-type PM-VSDs.

•Published evidence supports its off-label use in PM-VSD with cusp prolapse, with lower risk of aortic regurgitation progression compared to asymmetric devices [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Published evidence supports its off-label use in PM-VSD with cusp prolapse, with lower risk of aortic regurgitation progression compared to asymmetric devices [[9], [10], [11], [12]].

Device waist size was chosen to be 1–2 mm larger than the maximal effective VSD diameter (typically the LV-side measurement, as it represents the high-pressure origin of the shunt).

Results

Patient demographics and baseline characteristics

Twenty-nine patients underwent ADO II device closure for PM-VSD with RCC prolapse across the two Iraqi cardiac centers. The cohort comprised 16 males (55.2%) and 13 females (44.8%), with a mean age of 8.86 ± 5.77 years (range: 1–23 years). Age distribution revealed: 12 patients (41.4%) in the 0–5-year group, 5 patients (17.2%) aged 6–10 years, 7 patients (24.1%) aged 11–15 years, and 5 patients (17.2%) aged 16–25 years. Mean body weight was 22.4 ± 15.3 kg (range: 8–65 kg).

Anatomical and defect characteristics

Associated RCC prolapse was present in all 29 patients, representing the first documented systematic institutional series exclusively targeting this specific anatomical presentation. Twenty-two patients (75.9%) demonstrated isolated RCC prolapse, and 7 patients (24.1%) had RCC prolapse with mild aortic insufficiency. Defect sizes were small (<4 mm) in 16 patients (55.2%) and medium (4–6 mm) in 13 patients (44.8%) (Table 1).

Residual shunt evolution

Among the 5 patients with small residual shunt immediately post-procedure, 4 showed improvement at 6 months: 3 achieved complete closure (representing 60% resolution), and 1 demonstrated reduction in jet size from 3 mm to 1 mm. One patient maintained a small 3 mm jet at 6 months without hemodynamic consequence.

Valve-related outcomes

Tricuspid Regurgitation: New or progressive tricuspid regurgitation occurred in 5 patients in total (17.2%). Four patients (13.8%) developed mild tricuspid regurgitation during the study period (3 by 6 months post-procedure, 1 detected only at 6-month imaging). One patient (3.4%) developed moderate tricuspid regurgitation requiring surgical evaluation, though surgical intervention was ultimately deferred as the patient remained hemodynamically compensated.

Aortic Regurgitation: No new-onset aortic regurgitation developed in patients without baseline aortic insufficiency. Among the 7 patients with preexisting mild aortic insufficiency, no worsening occurred through 6-month follow-up. One patient showed slight improvement in aortic insufficiency degree (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Outcome summary

Discussion

This study reports the first multicenter Iraqi experience with transcatheter closure of perimembranous ventricular septal defect (PM-VSD) associated with right coronary cusp (RCC) prolapse using the Amplatzer Duct Occluder II (ADO II) device. While transcatheter VSD closure is increasingly adopted worldwide, PM-VSD with aortic cusp prolapse remains a technically demanding subset due to the absence or extreme thinning of the aortic rim, dynamic valve-leaflet interaction, and risk of progressive aortic regurgitation (AR) [3,4,12].

Our cohort of 29 patients with documented RCC prolapse confirmed by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and angiography demonstrated a 96.6% procedural success rate, 79.3% complete closure at 24 h, and 93.1% favorable outcomes at 6 months, defined as successful occlusion without new or worsening valvular dysfunction. Notably, no patient developed new-onset AR, and none of the 7 patients with baseline mild AR experienced progression over follow-up—a critical finding given that AR is the primary driver for early intervention in this anatomy [3,4,15].

These results are comparable to international series using ADO II or similar devices in complex VSD anatomies. Tang et al. [12] reported durable closure with no late AR over 45 months in subarterial VSD with cusp prolapse using ADO II. Zhou et al. [16] described successful off-label use of modified Amplatzer devices in PM-VSD with aortic prolapse, emphasizing soft, low-profile devices to avoid leaflet trauma. Ramakrishnan and Kothari [17] and Vijayalakshmi et al. [18] further validated ADO II for PM-VSD, citing its flexible waist and minimal aortic disc projection as key advantages.

Our findings also address a key anatomical misconception: in true RCC prolapse, the aortic valve leaflet herniates into the defect, often leaving no measurable aortic rim—contradicting a strict “≥1–2 mm rim” criterion. We want to clarify that inclusion was based on effective flow diameter ≤3 mm (measured from the left ventricular [LV] side, confirmed by right ventricular [RV]-side TTE and angiography), not on a rigid aortic rim measurement. In most cases, the aortic annulus was contiguous with the superior edge of the defect, consistent with the pathophysiology described by Tweddell et al. [3,4] This nuance is critical: the presence of cusp prolapse itself—not a measurable rim—was the defining anatomical feature, and the ADO II's short waist and soft discs accommodated this “rimless” configuration without valve interference.

A second major concern involved apparent contradictions in defect size: while the inclusion criterion specified an effective flow diameter ≤3 mm, some reported defects reached 4–6 mm. This discrepancy arises from confusing the anatomical tunnel width with the effective shunt orifice. In prolapsing PM-VSDs, the tunnel may appear wide on two-dimensional echocardiography (e.g., 6 mm), but the actual high-velocity jet origin (LV-side effective diameter) was ≤3 mm, as confirmed by color Doppler flow convergence and angiographic jet width—the true determinants for device sizing. We have amended the Methods section to clarify this distinction and specify that all devices were selected based on the LV-side effective flow diameter, not the maximal anatomical size.

The 13.8% incidence of new mild tricuspid regurgitation (TR) aligns with prior reports (10–20%) and likely reflects device interaction with the tricuspid apparatus during delivery, especially in defects near the septal leaflet [19,20]. Importantly, no patient developed complete atrioventricular block, a known risk in PM-VSD closure, underscoring the safety of real-time TTE guidance and careful deployment technique.

Our collaboration between Anwar Shexa Hospital (Sulaymaniyah) and Ibn Al-Bitar Center (Baghdad) demonstrates that standardized protocols for complex congenital interventions can be implemented successfully in resource-constrained settings. This model—emphasizing uniform imaging criteria, shared device-sizing algorithms, and joint outcome review—offers a replicable framework for the broader Middle East.

Limitations include the retrospective design, modest sample size (n = 29), and intermediate (6-month) follow-up. Long-term surveillance (>5 years) is essential to assess late AR or device-related complications. Additionally, while our outcomes appear favorable compared to a 58.3% surgical complication rate in local Iraqi series [8], direct comparison is precluded by the lack of a concurrent control group.

Conclusion

This multicenter Iraqi study demonstrates that transcatheter closure of PM-VSD with RCC prolapse using the ADO II device is feasible, safe, and effective in carefully selected patients. Despite the absence of a traditional aortic rim, the flexible, low-profile design of ADO II allows secure deployment without worsening aortic valve function. With a 96.6% implantation success rate, 79.3% complete closure at 24 h, and 93.1% favorable outcomes at 6 months, this approach offers a minimally invasive alternative to surgery in a high-risk anatomical subgroup.

While not the first global report of ADO II use in VSD with cusp prolapse, this is the first systematic, multicenter experience from Iraq and the Middle East focused exclusively on this indication. Our findings support expanding transcatheter therapy to select PM-VSD cases with RCC prolapse, provided that effective flow diameter (LV side) is ≤ 3 mm, baseline AR is mild or absent, and procedures are performed in centers with expertise in congenital interventions.

Future work should include prospective registries, long-term echocardiographic follow-up, and multinational collaborations to validate these results and refine patient selection criteria. The inclusion of representative imaging—such as pre-procedure TTE showing RCC prolapsing into the defect, angiographic jet sizing, and post-closure echocardiograms—will further enhance reproducibility and clinical adoption.
