Integrated proteomic analysis identifies TYMS-dependent AMPK-mTOR signaling in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: Vinod Vijayakurup, Benjamin Meyer, Syeda Rida Rizvi, Kara H. Kievit, Iram Hyder, Jin Koh, Michael Feely, Maria V. Guijarro, Frederic J. Kaye, Maria Zajac-Kaye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWhile regulated TYMS expression is essential for DNA synthesis, sustained TYMS overexpression is a key biomarker for drug resistance and poor prognosis in patients with advanced solid tumors, including pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs). Using proteomic analysis, we observed that TYMS regulates proteins involved in energy-dependent metabolism. Further, we demonstrate that TYMS directly impacts the AMPK-mTOR signaling pathway, a critical axis governing the metabolic adaptation of cancer. TYMS inhibition activates AMPK signaling in PanNET cells, while TYMS overexpression suppresses AMPK activation and enhances mTOR signaling in PanNETs arising in TYMS-overexpressing Men1−/− null transgenic mouse model. In addition, TYMS expression positively correlates with mTOR-associated genes in patients with PanNET. TYMS levels also impact the efficacy of everolimus, an FDA-approved mTOR inhibitor for patients with PanNET, underscoring the clinical significance of our findings. In summary, our study uncovers a new role of TYMS linking nucleotide metabolism to growth signaling pathways via the regulation of the AMPK-mTOR axis.

Introduction

Pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PanNETs) are neoplasms arising from specialized endocrine cells within the pancreas.1 These malignancies present a clinical challenge due to their variable biology, ranging from indolent, slow-growing tumors to aggressive metastatic tumor progression.2,3 The heterogeneous spectrum of PanNETs has stimulated research to define somatic molecular biomarkers that can both predict tumor behavior and suggest therapeutic strategies.4 Recent studies investigating the genomic landscape of PanNETs have identified mutations within the genes governing mTOR, histone acetylation, telomerase, and DNA damage response pathways as candidate mutational targets that impact PanNET biology.5 Notably, somatic MEN1 inactivation, a tumor suppressor gene functioning as a nuclear scaffold to modulate histone chromatin structure, emerged as a primary event in PanNET etiology.6,7

We recently reported that the overexpression of thymidylate synthase (TYMS or TS), a metabolic enzyme required for the synthesis of thymidylate, accelerates tumor development in a Men1-null mouse tumor model.8 TYMS normally functions to maintain thymidylate levels for DNA synthesis, whereas aberrantly elevated TYMS expression is consistently linked with tumor progression, resistance to drug therapy, and poor overall survival across a wide range of common human adult tumors.9,10,11 Our laboratory demonstrated that ectopic TYMS can transform rodent cells using in vitro assays as well as the in vivo formation of tumors in nude mice.12 We also showed that transgenic mice overexpressing TYMS (designated CMV-hTS) in the pancreas resulted in endocrine islet cell tumor development.13 In addition, we generated a novel mouse model (hTS/Men1−/−) by breeding CMV-hTS mice with a conditional Men1−/− genotype and observed that hTS/Men1−/− mice developed PanNETs earlier as compared to Men1−/− mice. We also demonstrated that elevated TYMS levels in a Men1-inactivated genetic background accelerated PanNET progression and were associated with DNA damage and reduced survival.8 These experiments support the hypothesis that high TYMS levels are not a passive biomarker for tumor growth but rather represent an active participant driving tumor progression. Most importantly, these experiments are consistent with human clinical data, where the subset of patients with PanNET with elevated TYMS levels correlates with worse clinical outcomes.8,14 Though TYMS increases tumor incidence and accelerates tumor progression in mice with a Men1-deleted genetic background, the molecular mechanism driving TYMS-induced tumor progression remains unknown. In addition to its role in thymidylate synthesis, TYMS also regulates the metabolic pathways for the folate and methionine cycles and other downstream amino acids that are crucial for tumor cell growth and proliferation.9,15,16 Therefore, TYMS activity has a broad influence on nucleotide metabolism, the folate cycle, and amino acid synthesis. In addition, elevated levels of TYMS in mouse embryo fibroblast (MEF) cells can reshape the metabolic landscape, including changes in nucleotide and amino acid metabolism.8 Since these observations suggest that TYMS plays a critical role in growth-related metabolism, we hypothesized that aberrant TYMS overexpression may impact the AMPK master metabolic regulator and its function as a rheostat sensor for nucleotides and amino acid pools in mammalian cells.17,18

AMPK serves as a regulator of metabolic homeostasis to conserve energy in tumor cells by inhibiting ATP-anabolic processes and promoting ATP-generating pathways.19 When activated, AMPK reprograms cellular metabolism and imposes growth checkpoints, particularly through the inhibition of the mTOR signaling pathway.20,21,22 Since mTOR is activated in a subset of patients with PanNET,5 the AMPK-mTOR signaling axis is a crucial target to evaluate the risk for chemotherapy drug resistance and overall prognosis in these patients. For example, pharmacological agents such as everolimus that target the mTOR signaling pathway and metformin, an AMPK activator, both show promise in PanNET clinical studies.23,24

We now report that elevated TYMS levels downregulate AMPK signaling and activate mTOR signaling in PanNET tumor cells. These data suggest a new role for TYMS inhibition to modulate the AMPK-mTOR signaling axis and impact the efficacy of current mTOR inhibitory agents in the management of patients with advanced PanNETs.

Results

Thymidylate synthase downregulation induces energy-related metabolic pathways and activates AMPK in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors cells

To study the molecular pathways regulated by TYMS in PanNET cells, we first investigated the global proteomic alterations in PanNET BON1 cells following TYMS knockdown. TYMS expression was silenced in BON1 cells by stable transfection with TYMS-specific short hairpin RNA (hTSshRNA) or control nonspecific shRNA (NSshRNA), resulting in the creation of BON1-hTSshRNA and BON1-NSshRNA cells, respectively (Figure 1A). To assess the global proteomic changes induced by TYMS knockdown, we compared protein expression levels between BON1-hTSshRNA (n = 5) and BON1-NSshRNA (n = 5) cells using a Tandem Mass Tag (TMT)-labeled proteomic approach (described in STAR Methods), as depicted in Figure 1B and Table S1. We found 583 proteins that displayed differential expression in BON1-hTSshRNA cells as compared to BON1-NSshRNA cells (with fold changes ranging from 0.8 to 1.2 and a cutoff p-value of 0.05) (Figure 1C). Among these, 388 proteins showed upregulation and 195 exhibited downregulation in BON1-hTSshRNA cells when compared to BON1-NSshRNA (Figure 1C, Table S1). To identify the activated biological pathways resulting from TYMS downregulation, we used Gene Ontology to conduct GO-enrichment analysis on the upregulated and downregulated proteins within BON1-hTSshRNA cells as compared to BON1-NSshRNA using ShinyGO, a graphical gene-set enrichment tool.25 Subsequently, the hierarchical clustering of the identified biological pathways was performed based on the shared genes within these processes. The results revealed proteins related to nineteen biological pathways increased in BON1-hTSshRNA when compared with BON1-NSshRNA cells, which were then clustered into three distinct groups (labeled as x, y, and z) (Figure 1D, Tables S1 and S2). Cluster x represented 32% of the pathways associated with DNA damage, including pathways involved in chromatin assembly or disassembly, nucleosome assembly or organization, and DNA packaging (Figures 1D and 1E). Cluster y represents 10% of the pathways identified, related to nucleic acid transport (Figures 1D and 1E). Finally, cluster z, which represents 58% of the activated pathways (11 out of 19), was related to energy metabolism, encompassing oxidative phosphorylation, ATP biosynthetic processes, aerobic respiration, and ATP metabolic processes (Figures 1D and 1E). Given the enrichment of energy metabolism pathways in BON1 cells with downregulated TYMS, we examined whether TYMS knockdown affects cellular ATP levels. We observed that ATP levels were significantly reduced in BON1-hTSshRNA cells compared to BON1-NSshRNA controls (Figure S1). Energy metabolism in tumor cells is primarily governed by AMPK, a molecule that senses nutrient status based on metabolic changes involving nucleotides and amino acids.17,18 Previously, we demonstrated that TYMS induces metabolic changes, including alterations in amino acid and nucleotide metabolism, in mouse embryo fibroblast (MEF) cells.8 Depending on growth-related metabolic changes and nutrient status, AMPK is activated or inactivated through phosphorylation or dephosphorylation at threonine-172 (Thr-172) within its catalytic subunit.26 Activated AMPK blocks energy-consuming processes, such as mTOR signaling and fatty acid synthesis, while promoting oxidative phosphorylation. Conversely, inactivated AMPK allows these anabolic energy-consuming processes to proceed.20,26 Given the association of TYMS with growth and energy-related metabolic changes, we hypothesized that TYMS levels might regulate AMPK. To investigate the relationship between AMPK and TYMS, we examined Thr-172 phosphorylation status of the catalytic AMPK subunit and Ser-79 of its downstream substrate Acetyl-coA carboxylase (ACC)26 in BON1-hTSshRNA compared to BON1-NSshRNA and parental BON1 cells (Figure 1F). We also obtained an independent PanNET tumor cell line CM,27 and tested AMPK and ACC-phosphorylation status in CM-hTSshRNA compared to CM-NSshRNA cells and their parental CM cells (Figure 1F). Our findings showed elevated Thr-172 pAMPK and Ser-79 pACC in both BON1-hTSshRNA and CM-hTSshRNA cells compared to corresponding controls and the parental cells, suggesting that TYMS downregulation activates AMPK signaling (Figure 1F). To establish whether TYMS overexpression leads to AMPK downregulation, we compared the expression of pAMPK in MEF cells stably transfected with human TYMS (MEF-TS, clone TS1-1) to vector transfected control (MEF-V, clone V1-1) generated as described previously.8 Immunoblot analysis demonstrated reduced pAMPK expression in MEF cells overexpressing ectopic human TYMS (MEF-TS) as compared to vector control cells (MEF-V) (Figure 1G), suggesting that elevated TYMS downregulates AMPK activity. Furthermore, to assess the effect of the pharmacological inhibition of TYMS on AMPK signaling in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor cells, we treated BON1 and CM cells with 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU), a TYMS inhibitor, and analyzed its impact on AMPK activity via immunoblotting. We confirmed that the treatment of BON1 and CM cells with 5-FU (1 μM or 5 μM) induced the expected enzymatically inactive ternary complex due to a covalent binding of 5-FU metabolite to TYMS catalytic domain resulting in mobility shift on SDS gel (Figures 1H and 1I). We also observed that the inhibition of TYMS activity in BON1 and CM cells induced pAMPK expression, suggesting that the inhibition of TYMS by 5-FU is associated with enhanced AMPK signaling in PanNETs (Figures 1H and 1I).

Thymidylate synthase downregulation induces AMPK activity in the pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor xenograft mouse model

We previously showed using the MTT assay that the downregulation of TYMS in BON1 cells (BON1-hTSshRNA) reduced cell proliferation as compared to control BON1-NSshRNA cells.8 We now show that the downregulation of TYMS also reduces proliferation in CM-hTSshRNA cells compared to CM-NSshRNA using the MTT assay (Figures S2A and S2B). To investigate the in vivo role of TYMS in regulating AMPK signaling, tumor xenografts were established by the injection of BON1-hTSshRNA or BON1-NSshRNA cells into 6–8-month-old NSG mice, following the experimental timeline outlined in Figure 2A. After 7 weeks, mice were sacrificed, and tumors were harvested. The experiment was repeated twice to generate BON1-NSshRNA xenografts (n = 6) and BON1-hTSshRNA xenografts (n = 8) (Figure 2A). We observed that tumors derived from BON1-hTSshRNA cells, as compared to control tumors derived from BON1-NSshRNA cells, showed a significant decrease in volume (1421 mm3 vs. 608 mm3; p = 0.0015) and weight (2.6 g vs. 1.3 g; p = 0.036) (Figure 2B), suggesting that the downregulation of TYMS expression inhibits the growth of pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor cell lines. To investigate whether TYMS modulates AMPK in tumor tissues, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis to examine the expression of pAMPK and TYMS in BON1-hTSshRNA (n = 3) and BON1-NSshRNA (n = 3) xenografts. Staining intensities were scored using ImageJ software. Results demonstrated the lower expression of TYMS in tissues derived from BON1-hTSshRNA tumors compared to BON1-NSshRNA xenografts (h-score 120 vs. 150; p < 0.0001), while steady-state levels of pAMPK were higher in tissues derived from BON1-hTSshRNA compared to xenografts of BON1-NSshRNA (h-score 200 vs. 140; p < 0.0001) (Figure 2C). To confirm these results, we performed immunoblot analysis to determine the expression of TYMS and pAMPK in lysates isolated from BON1- hTSshRNA (n = 3) and BON1-NSshRNA (n = 3) tumors. We observed lower TYMS expression in tumor lysate isolated from BON1-hTSshRNA compared to lysate isolated from BON1-NSshRNA tumors, whereas pAMPK expression was higher in BON1-hTSshRNA compared to lysate isolated from BON1-NSshRNA xenografts (pAMPK/GAPDH ratio 0.24 vs. 0.62; p = 0.02) (Figure 2D). Elevated thymidylate synthase downregulates AMPK signaling in the pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor hTS/Men1−/− mouse model

We next tested whether TYMS regulates AMPK signaling using spontaneous PanNET tumors arising in the genetically engineered hTS/Men1−/− PanNET mouse model (Figure 3A).8 We performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis to measure the expression of TYMS and pAMPK in PanNET tumor tissue sections isolated from 9-month-old tumor-bearing hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice. These mice show distinct tumor nodules originating independently from discrete islet cells that underwent Men1 deletion.8 Therefore, for TYMS staining, we analyzed 9 tumor nodules each from hTS/Men1−/− mice (n = 3) and Men1−/− mice (n = 3). For pAMPK staining, we examined 6 nodules from Men1−/− mice (n = 3) and 9 nodules from hTS/Men1−/− mice (n = 3). Details regarding the number of nodules analyzed per mouse used for IHC scoring are demonstrated in Figure S3. The results showed increased TYMS staining in tumors from hTS/Men1−/− compared to Men1−/− mice (h-score 198 vs. 278; p < 0.0001) (Figure 3B). In contrast, the level of pAMPK was decreased in tumor tissues with high TYMS levels in hTS/Men1−/− compared to Men1−/− mice (h-score 165 vs. 72; p = 0.0012) (Figure 3B). These data further support an association between TYMS expression and AMPK signaling by showing that the ectopic overexpression of TYMS reduces AMPK signaling in PanNETs arising within a Men1−/− background. To validate these IHC results, we analyzed the levels of TYMS and pAMPK in lysates isolated from PanNET tissues generated from hTS/Men1−/− (n = 3) and Men1−/− mice (n = 3) using immunoblotting analysis. Elevated thymidylate synthase activates mTOR signaling in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors

Since TYMS downregulates AMPK signaling, we investigated whether TYMS expression activates mTOR, a growth signal negatively regulated by AMPK. mTOR activity can be monitored through the phosphorylation status of two downstream substrate proteins, 4EBP1 and S6.28,29 Schematic illustration depicting a possible relationship between TYMS, AMPK, and mTOR signaling is shown in Figure 4A. To examine the role of TYMS in mTOR signaling, PanNET tissues from Men1−/− and hTS/Men1−/− mice were immunostained for mTOR substrates phospho-4EBP1 (p4EBP1) and phospho-pS6 (pS6). For p4EBP1 staining, we analyzed 7 tumor nodules from control Men1−/− mice (n = 3) and 8 tumor nodules from hTS/Men1−/− mice (n = 3). For pS6 staining, we examined 11 tumor nodules from Men1−/− mice (n = 3) and 7 tumor nodules from hTS/Men1−/− mice (n = 3 mice). The number of nodules analyzed for each mouse is illustrated in Figure S4. We calculated the percentage of cells positively stained for pS6 and p4EBP1, with at least 100 cells selected from a random area of each tumor nodule shown in Figure S4. The results show the elevated expression of pS6 in hTS/Men1−/− compared to Men1−/− tissues (37% vs. 1.7%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4B). Similarly, the expression of p4EBP1 in hTS/Men1−/− tissues show elevated expression compared to Men1−/− samples (73% vs. 2.4%, p < 0.0001) (Figure 4C). We observed reduced p4EBP1 expression in BON1-hTSshRNA and CM-hTSshRNA cells compared to BON1-NSshRNA and CM-NSshRNA controls, as well as to parental BON1 and CM cells (Figure 4D). These findings indicate that reduced TYMS expression downregulates mTOR pathway activity in PanNET cells. To confirm the role of reduced TYMS activity in mTOR signaling, we studied the expression of p4EBP1 in BON1 and CM cells treated with TYMS inhibitor 5-FU. We observed that the treatment of BON1 and CM cells with 5-FU led to the inhibition of TYMS activity and reduction of the expression of p4EBP1 (Figure 4E), further supporting the hypothesis that TYMS activates mTOR signaling.

Thymidylate synthase expression in pancreatic neuroendocrine tumor patient tumor samples correlates with the expression of mTOR-related genes

Since we found that elevated TYMS activates mTOR signaling, we next sought to investigate the relationship between TYMS and mTOR-related genes in human samples of PanNETs from a clinical trial database.30 While the modulation of mTOR activity is primarily regulated at the post-translational level, there is also evidence suggesting that mTOR signaling can also influence gene expression at the transcriptional level under certain conditions.31,32 To study the relationship between TYMS and mTOR signaling in PanNET clinical samples, we analyzed RNA-seq data of 47 patients with PanNET from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE117853, subseries GSE117851) using the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) program33 to test the association between TYMS and mTOR expression and genes related to the downstream mTOR pathway. Our results show a significant positive enrichment between TYMS expression levels and genes implicated in the mTOR signaling pathway (normalized enrichment score = 1.97; FDR q-value = 0.007) (Figure 5A). Our analysis revealed that TYMS negatively correlates with TSC1 and TSC2 but positively correlates with RHEB, a key activator of mTOR (Figure 5B). To determine whether the TYMS-RHEB correlation is conserved across tumor types, we analyzed their relationship in multiple tumor datasets using the TNM plotter.35 The results show that this correlation was detected in 12/31 tumor types (38.7%). The 12 tumor types positively correlated with TYMS are shown in Figure 5C. To verify the TYMS-dependent regulation of RHEB, we analyzed RHEB and TYMS gene expressions using RT-PCR in BON1-hTSshRNA and CM-hTSshRNA cells compared to their respective controls (BON1-NSshRNA and CM-NSshRNA). We found that RHEB expression was significantly reduced in both BON1 and CM cell lines upon TYMS knockdown: a 0.28-fold reduction in BON1-hTSshRNA cells (p = 0.0007) and a 0.77-fold reduction in CM-hTSshRNA cells (p = 0.008) (Figure 5D). These findings demonstrate that reduced TYMS expression downregulates the mTOR-related gene RHEB in PanNET cells. Moreover, elevated TYMS is strongly associated with increased mTOR gene expression in clinical samples (Figure 5A).

Elevated thymidylate synthase enhances the efficacy of the mTOR inhibitor everolimus

Having established that TYMS levels impact mTOR signaling in PanNETs, we investigated whether the mTOR inhibitor everolimus exhibits different efficacy in PanNET tissues with elevated TYMS. To assess the comparative efficacy of everolimus in extending survival of mice with PanNETs, we administered everolimus and vehicle control to hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice. The experimental timeline (Figure 6A) illustrates the treatment cycles of everolimus and vehicle control delivered via oral gavage to both hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice. Both mouse models received everolimus or vehicle control three times per week starting at 6.5 months of age until mice reached the humane endpoint due to tumor burden, following IACUC guidelines. The average lifespan of hTS/Men1−/− mice treated with everolimus (n = 21) was 376 days, significantly higher compared to hTS/Men1−/− mice treated with corn oil (n = 35) with an average lifespan of 333 days (p = 0.001) (Figure 6B). In contrast, the average lifespan of Men1−/− mice treated with everolimus (n = 16) was 443 days, showing no statistically significant change compared to Men1−/− mice treated with corn oil (n = 18) with an average lifespan of 441 days (p = 0.65) (Figure 6B). To further investigate the role of TYMS expression in modulating everolimus efficacy, we performed a colony formation assay following the treatment of either BON1-NSshRNA or BON1-hTSshRNA cells with everolimus and compared them to untreated controls. We observed a significantly higher inhibition of colony formation in the everolimus-treated BON1-NSshRNA compared to everolimus treated BON1-hTSshRNA cells relative to corresponding untreated controls (40.28% vs. 67.4%, p = 0.006) (Figure S5). To further explore the influence of TYMS expression on the efficacy of everolimus, NSG mice bearing tumors developed from BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells were subjected to everolimus treatment. The experimental timeline delineates the treatment protocol (Figure 6C), with everolimus treatment starting 25 days following subcutaneous injection (6 x 106 cells) of either BON1-NSshRNA or BON1-hTSshRNA cells. Subsequently, mice injected with BON1-NSshRNA (n = 8) or BON1-hTSshRNA cells (n = 7) were randomly assigned into two cohorts and treated with everolimus five times weekly by oral gavage for 15 days or with corn oil as a control. BON1-NSshRNA xenografts received everolimus (n = 5) and corn oil (n = 3), and BON1-hTSshRNA received everolimus (n = 4) and corn oil (n = 3). Tumor volume and weight were measured in all groups after mice were euthanized early (40 days following cell inoculation), since it became apparent that treatment with everolimus significantly inhibited tumor growth. Two independent experiments were performed as shown in Figure 6D. The results were quantified using data from both experiments, and we observed that the BON1-NSshRNA xenograft that received everolimus treatment had reduced tumor volume and tumor weight compared to the control BON1-NSshRNA xenograft that received corn oil (618 mm3 vs. 222 mm3; p = 0.02 and 0.38g vs. 0.94; p = 0.03, respectively) (Figure 6E). However, the BON1-hTSshRNA xenograft treated with everolimus did not show statistically significant changes in tumor volume or tumor weight as compared to the control BON1-hTSshRNA that received corn oil (536 mm3 vs. 817 mm3 p = 0.36 and 0.69 g vs. 0.85 g; p = 0.6, respectively) (Figure 6E). To compare the effects of everolimus treatment in tumors with different expressions of TYMS, we selected xenografts derived from BON1-NSshRNA cells as a reference and compared everolimus treated BON1-NSshRNA xenograft tumor (high TYMS) and BON1-hTSshRNA tumors (low TYMS) to corn oil treated BON1-NSshRNA control. We observed that everolimus treatment reduced tumor growth in BON1-NSshRNA (high TYMS) but not in BON1-hTSshRNA (low TYMS) as compared to corn oil treated control (Figure S6). We also confirmed that xenografts derived from parental BON1 cells that express high levels of TYMS exhibited a significant reduction in both volume and weight following everolimus treatment (Figure S7). We did not observe a significant difference in tumor growth between BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA xenografts after only a 40-day incubation period. We previously showed that detecting an effect of hTSshRNA in vivo required at least 49 days of tumor growth (Figure 2) since the exponential growth of the BON1-NSshRNA tumor results in a significantly enlarged tumor size compared to the tumor size of the BON1-hTSshRNA xenograft. Taken together, these results suggest that everolimus has higher anti-tumor efficacy preferentially in hTS/Men1−/− than in Men1−/− mice and in xenograft mouse models with overexpressed TYMS.

Since we found evidence that TYMS expression may augment mTOR pathway signaling, we investigated whether everolimus influences TYMS expression in PanNET cells. To study the effect of mTOR inhibition on TYMS expression, we treated BON1 cells for 24 h at increasing concentrations of everolimus. Our results show that TYMS levels, along with the phosphorylation of ribosomal protein S6 (RPS6 or S6), are inhibited in BON1 cells treated with increasing concentrations of everolimus (0.1 or 1 μM) (Figure 6F) without reducing cell viability as analyzed by MTT assay (Figure S8). This suggests that everolimus may block TYMS expression along with mTOR signaling in PanNET cells (Figure 6F). To further confirm the role of everolimus in blocking TYMS, we tested TYMS expression in tumor xenografts developed in NSG mice treated with everolimus (n = 3) or its vehicle control (n = 3) according to the treatment schedule shown in Figure 6G. These results confirmed that mice treated with everolimus had reduced TYMS levels compared to vehicle treated controls (TYMS/GAPDH ratio: 0.42 vs. 0.16; p = 0.003) (Figures 6H and 6I). Taken together, everolimus works more efficiently in PanNET tumors with high TYMS levels, suggesting that TYMS downregulation by everolimus treatment results in the inhibition of tumor growth in xenografts and prolongation of survival in GEMMs.

Discussion

The overexpression of TYMS is a consistent biomarker for drug resistance and poor survival that is observed across a wide range of tumors.9,10,11 Our laboratory demonstrated that ectopic TYMS expression can transform rodent cells that are capable of inducing tumor growth in nude mice.12 In addition, we demonstrated that ectopically elevated TYMS expression within the mouse pancreas induces a PanNET-like phenotype in CMV-hTS mice,13 while its overexpression in Men1-deleted islet cells of hTS/Men1−/− mice accelerates PanNET progression.8 Additionally, we showed that knocking down TYMS reduces proliferation in BON1 cells, a PanNET cell line, while its overexpression in MEF cells induces proliferation.8 Our previous work also showed that elevated TYMS expression predicts worse clinical outcomes in patients with PanNET.8 In addition, we demonstrated that TYMS promotes genomic instability and tumor progression in an Ink4a/Arf-null background,36 and that the pharmacological inhibition of TYMS in this model increases the survival of the mice.37 These data were among the first to show that elevated TYMS is not simply a passive biomarker for tumor cell proliferation but reflects an important driving event in tumor progression.

To further define insights into cell signaling events resulting from TYMS alteration in PanNETs, we investigated the proteomic changes in BON1 cells following TYMS downregulation. Our findings demonstrated that the most common pathways activated following TYMS downregulation are associated with energy-related metabolism. We also found reduced ATP levels in TYMS downregulated BON1 cells, suggesting a potential stress response. Recently, we demonstrated that TYMS overexpression in MEF cells alters growth-related metabolism, including nucleotide and amino acid metabolism.8 Notably, TYMS is an integral part of the folic acid cycle, which transfers methyl groups from amino acids to generate nucleotides.9,15,16 Given the association of TYMS with cell growth and energy-related metabolism, we hypothesized that TYMS expression might be linked to AMPK signaling, the master regulator of growth and energy-related metabolism in mammalian cells. For example, AMPK signaling has been previously reported to downregulate the folic acid cycle through the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) coactivator 1α/estrogen-related receptor α (PGC-1α/ERRα), a signaling axis involved in mitochondrial biogenesis.38 Our results now demonstrate that shRNA-mediated TYMS downregulation in BON1 and CM cells induces AMPK phosphorylation both in vivo and in vitro. Similarly, the pharmacological inactivation of TYMS also led to increased pAMPK expression in PanNET cell lines, suggesting that TYMS downregulates AMPK signaling in PanNET cells.

We also demonstrated that elevated TYMS downregulates AMPK signaling in spontaneously developed PanNETs in hTS/Men1−/− mice by observing the reduced expression of pAMPK as compared to control PanNET Men1−/− mice tissues. While AMPK signaling in normal mouse islet tissues is required for normal maturation, mitochondrial biogenesis, and oxidative metabolism,29 its role in islet tissues post-tumor progression remains unclear. Since AMPK is a potential therapeutic target as a negative regulator of mTOR,39 and since mTOR is a promoter of PanNET progression,40 this data suggests further investigations into the clinical application of dual TYMS and mTOR inhibition. For example, metformin, an AMPK activator that enhances progression-free survival in patients with PanNET, is believed to exert its effects by inhibiting mTOR through AMPK activation.24 Therefore, elevated TYMS may oppose the growth-inhibitory effects of metformin by acting as a negative regulator of AMPK to activate mTOR.

Since mTOR is activated by AMPK downregulation, we tested whether TYMS-induced AMPK downregulation is associated with mTOR signaling in PanNETs. We found that PanNET tissues generated from hTS/Men1−/− mice exhibit the increased expression of mTOR substrates p4EBP1 and pS6 compared to tissues isolated from Men1−/− mice. mTORC1 and AMPK form a reciprocally coupled switch central to the maturation of pancreatic β cells in mouse tissues. High mTOR and low AMPK predominate in highly proliferative islet cells of young mice before weaning; however, post-weaning, the signal switches to high AMPK and low mTOR status, resulting in reduced proliferation.29 Low mTOR status is observed in Men1−/− induced tumors and is associated with the low expression of mTOR substrates pS6 and p4EBP1 in islet tumor tissues as compared to hTS/Men1−/−mice. This data indicates that the inactivation of Men1 alone might not be sufficient to induce the aberrant mTOR activation that is frequently observed in patients with PanNET.5 However, concurrently elevated TYMS in a Men1-null genetic context may be required to induce mTOR activity associated with the tumor progression of TYMS-mediated PanNET acceleration.

We also observed that TYMS gene expression exhibits a strong positive correlation with genes involved in the mTOR pathway in PanNET patient samples. Key mTOR-related genes, such as RHEB, a critical regulator that is activated when AMPK signaling is low and serves to enhance mTOR signaling34 were also positively correlated in PanNET clinical samples. In this study, we found that genes encoding the catalytic component of the MTORC1 complex and its downstream target RHEB were significantly reduced in BON1 and CM cells with reduced TYMS levels. While mTOR signaling is largely regulated through post-translational phosphorylation as measured by downstream effectors such as 4EBP1 and S6, emerging evidence suggests that the inhibition of mTOR signaling can also lead to the transcriptional regulation of mTOR pathway components under certain conditions.31,32 Our findings demonstrate that the downregulation of TYMS suppresses RHEB expression, an mTOR signaling regulator, further supporting gene involvement in TYMS-dependent mTOR regulation. Interestingly, RHEB positively correlated with TYMS expression in many different tumor subtypes examined from RNA-seq data of patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) using TNM plotter. This pattern suggests a broader influence of TYMS on modulating mTOR signaling beyond PanNET tumors. The elevated expression of mTOR-related genes in PanNET clinical samples with high TYMS expression underscores a potential mechanistic link that could guide therapeutic interventions. Given that mTOR inhibition remains one of the most effective treatment strategies for PanNETs, these findings highlight the need to explore the TYMS-AMPK-mTOR axis as a potential target for future therapies.

Since everolimus is an FDA-approved treatment option for blocking mTOR in PanNETs,23 we investigated whether the increased mTOR status in PanNET tissues due to elevated TYMS is correlated with an enhanced response to everolimus treatment. We demonstrated that everolimus enhanced overall survival in the TYMS-overexpressing PanNET mouse model, hTS/Men1−/−, but not in the Men1−/− mouse model lacking ectopic TYMS. Similarly, everolimus showed greater efficacy in downregulating the growth of TYMS-expressing xenografts derived from BON1 cells compared to xenografts from BON1 cells with TYMS knockdown. These results indicate that TYMS not only acts as a tumor-driving factor but also influences the antitumor efficacy of everolimus. Previous PanNET clinical data show wide variation in TYMS expression, with 4% of patients with PanNET exhibiting high TYMS expression, 17% showing low to moderate expression, and the large remaining subset of 79.2% does not exhibiting detectable TYMS expression.8 Since TYMS expression levels might impact everolimus efficacy, this data further supports a role of TYMS expression as a guiding biomarker for therapeutic decisions. Given that TYMS expression influences the efficacy of everolimus, the varying levels of TYMS expression observed in PanNETs could potentially impact the strategy to use everolimus treatment.

In summary, we demonstrated a non-canonical role of TYMS beyond nucleotide synthesis, linking nucleotide metabolism to metabolomic and growth signaling pathways via the regulation of the AMPK-mTOR axis. We also show that TYMS expression is associated with enhanced sensitivity to everolimus in PanNET tumor models. Given that elevated levels of TYMS are found in a wide range of tumors, the significance of everolimus efficacy based on TYMS levels may suggest new therapeutic strategies that can be tested in future pre-clinical and clinical studies.

Limitations of the study

The observed differences in everolimus anti-tumor efficacy associated with TYMS expression levels required further confirmation through clinical studies. This limitation highlights the need for additional research.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cell culture

BON-1 cells, derived from a serotonin-secreting PanNET,42 were a gift from Kirk Ives (University of Texas, Galveston, Texas, USA) and were grown in DMEM-F12 (50/50) medium (Corning). CM cells derived from an insulin-secreting PanNET43 were a gift from Aldo Scarpa (University of Verona, Verona, Italy) and were grown in RPMI-1640 medium (Millipore Sigma). All the media was supplemented with 5 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin and fetal bovine serum (FBS) (10% FBS for BON1 cells and 5% FBS for CM cells). Stable BON1 and CM cell lines expressing non-specific shRNA (NSshRNA) or TYMS-targeting shRNA (hTSshRNA) were maintained in the corresponding media containing puromycin (5 μg/mL) along with the supplements mentioned above. MEF cells and Men1-deleted MEF cells stably transfected with human TYMS (MEF-TS, clone TS1-1; MEF-TS/Men1−/−, clone TS2-1) and their corresponding vector-transfected control cells (MEF-V, clone V1-1; MEF-V/Men1−/−, clone V2-1)8 were maintained in DMEM (Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS, 5 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin, and G418 (300 μg/mL). All cells were grown at a constant temperature of 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% carbon dioxide. Mycoplasma contamination testing was performed on all cell lines used in this study, and all tested negative. Cell line authentication was not performed.

Animal experiments

All mice used in this study were maintained within the University of Florida Cancer Genetics Research Center, and all animal experiments were done in accordance with approved protocols from the University of Florida Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice generated by crossing RIP2-Cre/floxed Men1 mice (Men1−/− mice C57BL/6J x FVB; 129S)44 with CMV-human TYMS transgenic mice (FVB; 129S)8 were used for the survival studies. Genotyping was conducted using primers provided in Table S3: 8F/8R for floxed Men1, H2/NCI2 for human TYMS, and IMR567/IMR568 for Cre detection.8 The PCR amplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 60°C for 30 s and 72°C for 1 min, and a final extension at 72°C for 10 min. For survival studies, everolimus or vehicle control (corn oil) was administered to hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice by oral gavage, three times per week, starting at 6.5 months of age. Treatments continued until mice reached the humane endpoint due to tumor burden in accordance with the IACUC-approved protocol. The experimental timeline of the treatment cycle is shown in Figure 6A.

For the development of xenografts, 6 x 106 BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA or BON1 parental cells were resuspended in 200 μL of PBS and injected subcutaneously into 6- to 8-week-old NSG mice. To compare the growth of tumors developed from BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells (Figure 2), the mice were sacrificed 7 weeks post-injection, tumors were isolated, and weight and volume of the tumors were measured. The volume of the tumor was calculated using the ellipsoid volume equation, =43π×L×W×H2 , where L, W and H represents length, width and height of the tumor, respectively. The tissues were further processed for immunoblotting or IHC analysis. To study the influence of TYMS expression on the efficacy of everolimus treatment (Figure 5C), NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with BON1-NSshRNA or BON1-hTSshRNA cells. 25 days after injection when tumors became palpable, mice were randomly assigned to two cohorts which were treated five times weekly by oral gavage. Cohort-1, from both the BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA xenografts, received corn oil as a control, while cohort-2 received everolimus (2.5 mg/kg body weight). After 15 days of treatment, mice were euthanized, and tumor volume and weight were measured as described above. To determine whether everolimus may play a role in the regulation of TYMS expression (Figure 5G), BON1 cells were subcutaneously injected to NSG mice, and the experiment was performed as described above for BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells. Male and female mice were included in all experiments and were assigned randomly to different groups.

Method details

MTT assay

BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells (3,000 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to attach overnight. Cell viability was assessed at 24 and 48 h using the MTT assay (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Cat# M6494). The reduction of MTT was measured by recording the absorbance at 570 nm using a SpectraMax M3 plate reader (Molecular Devices). Data was analyzed using GraphPad Prism to determine the viability.

Colony formation assay

For colony formation assays, BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells (500 cells per well) were seeded into 6-well plates. After 72 h, cells were treated with everolimus (25 μM) for an additional 48 h. Following treatment, the medium was removed and replaced with fresh medium which was renewed weekly. After 21 days, colonies were fixed in methanol for 20 min and stained with 1% crystal violet (in PBS) for 20 min. Excess stain was removed by extensive washing with water, and plates were air-dried. Colonies were counted using ImageJ software, and the data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9. The percentage of colony formation was calculated as: percentage of colony formation = (number of colonies formed after treatment/number of colonies formed in control group) × 100%.

Lentivirus production and lentivirus-mediated TYMS inhibition in BON cells

To produce lentiviral TYMS shRNA for transduction, HEK293T cells (2.5 x 106) were seeded in a 100 cm plate and incubated overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 10–20 h. Cells were transfected with 10 μg of either hTSshRNA encoding plasmid (#133 Dharmacon, RHS4430-200162272) or with Non-Specific (NS) shRNA (#128 Dharmacon, RHS4346). The shRNA plasmids were co-transfected with 5 μg psPAX (lentiviral packaging plasmid) and 5 μg pMD2.G (VSV-G envelope expressing plasmid) in media with no serum using Xtreme Gene HP DNA Transfection Reagent (Roche #06366546001) in a 3:1 ratio. Viral supernatant was collected 48 h post-transfection and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter.

For stable transduction, BON1 cells (5 x 106 cells) were seeded in a 6-well plate and left to attach overnight. 2 mL of viral supernatant previously collected from HEK293T cells that were packaged with either hTSshRNA #133 or NSshRNA #128, along with 8 μg/mL polybrene (Millipore, TR-1003-G), was added to BON1 cells. The plate was then centrifuged at 500 rpm for 10 min to improve transduction efficiency and then incubated for 6 h at 37°C. Following incubation, media was replaced with 2 mL growth medium, and cells were incubated overnight at 37°C. This transduction procedure was repeated 3x times using the same BON1 cells. After the final transduction, cells were incubated for 48 h in antibiotic-free media. At 80% confluence, cells were split 1:2 into 60 mm dishes and maintained in selection media containing 4 μg/mL puromycin up to 10 days. Stable transduction of hTSshRNA in BON1 cells was verified by detection of GFP florescence positivity and decreased TS expression by immunoblotting.

For stable transfection of CM cells, we used viral supernatant generated in our previous work,36 carrying either hTSshRNA #64 (TRCN0000045667; Sigma) or NSshRNA #89 (Sigma SHC002) plasmids. To generate the viral supernatants used for CM cell transduction, 12 × 106 HEK293T were seeded per T175 flask and incubated overnight at 37°C and 5% CO2. After 10–20 h, HEK293T were co-transfected with lentiviral construct, TS shRNA#64 (TRCN0000045667; Sigma) or non-specific (NS) shRNA (32 μg) (NSshRNA #89, Sigma SHC002) viral packaging plasmid (psPAX, 16 μg) and viral envelope plasmid (pMD2.G, 16 μg) using a mixture of 1 mg/mL polyethyleneimine (Polysciences) and 1.5 M NaCl. Viral supernatant was collected 40 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm filter and pooled. The pooled supernatants were concentrated using an Amicon Ultra-15 100 K cutoff filter device (EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) according to manufacturer’s instructions. The final concentrated virus was aliquoted and stored at −80°C until used. For infection, 1x106 CM cells were seeded in a 6-well plate, allowed to attach overnight, and transduced with viral supernatant following the same procedure described for BON1 cells.

Immunoblot analysis

Tissues were homogenized using the Omni general laboratory homogenizer (GLH) following the manufacturer’s protocol (OMNI international, Marietta, GA). Cell lines were collected by scraping and washed twice with cold PBS. The cells and tissue homogenates were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Santa-Cruz, sc-24948) and centrifuged (12000 rpm, 20 min) to obtain whole-cell lysates. Protein concentration was measured using the Bradford assay (BioRad). Aliquots of the lysates (10–20 μg of protein) were separated on 10% or 4–20% Tris-glycine gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and probed with specific primary and secondary antibodies described in the Table S4. Blots were visualized by Super Signal Chemiluminescent Dura substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For densitometric analysis of proteins, ImageJ software was used for each immunoblot to quantify protein intensity, and each band intensity value was normalized to the loading control. Images of uncropped western blots are provided in Data S1.

Histopathology and immunohistochemistry

For routine histological analysis, specimens were fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin for 48 h, transferred to 70% ethanol, then processed using Leica TP 1020 instrument. Processed tissues were then molded into paraffin blocks in a Leica EG1150C heated dispenser using Fisher Scientific TissuePrep paraffin #T565. Solidified tissue blocks were serially sectioned into 5 μm thick slices on a Leica RM2125 RTS microtome and stained with conventional hematoxylin and eosin (H&E).45 H&E-stained slides were interpreted by masked pathologist, Michael Feely, Dept of Pathology, Immunology and Laboratory Medicine, Univ of Florida.

For immunohistochemistry, samples were processed as described above. Antigen retrieval was performed using pressure heated citrate buffer (1.8 mM citric acid, Sigma-Aldrich, S1804-500G and 8.2 mM sodium citrate Sigma-Aldrich, C1857-100G) prepared as follows: 20.5 mL of 0.1 M sodium citrate stock solution mixed with 4.5 mL 0.1 M citric acid stock solution into 225 mL DI water and set in a pressurized decloaker (Biocare Medical #DC2002) for 15 min at 95°C. Cooled slides then underwent peroxidase blocking in 3% hydrogen peroxide and methanol solution, then were washed in 1X TBS (Boston Bio #BM-301) containing 0.01% Triton X-100 (Sigma #T8787-50 mL) three times. Sections were blocked in 2% BSA/TBST solution for 1-h, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight (detailed in Table S4). Primary antibodies were discarded, and slides were washed three times in TBST solution before secondary antibody incubation using either the Vector Labs avidin-biotin ABC kit or Cell Signaling SignalStain (CST #8125) detection kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Secondary antibody or detection solution was discarded, and slides washed in TBS three times before application of Cell Signaling DAB (CST #8059) substrate diluted according to kit instructions. DAB reaction was allowed to progress for up to 10-min before quenching in DI water. Slides underwent hematoxylin counterstaining before clarifying and bluing, were mounted and cover slipped with xylene based mounting media and dried overnight.

For quantification of pAMPK and TYMS expression, tumors from Men1−/− and hTS/Men1−/− mice as described in Figure S3 were scored using ImageJ software. For quantification of percentage of cells positively stained for pS6 and p4EBP1 expression, a random portion of each Men1−/− and hTS/Men1−/− tumor with at least 100 cells were scored.

Proteomics

Protein extraction, digestion, and tandem mass tag labeling

Proteins from BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells were extracted using the EasyPep MS Sample Prep kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, tissues were lysed with 100 μL of the lysis solution with the addition of 1 μL of the Halt protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Pierce, Waltham, MA, USA) and universal nuclease, and sonicated at 4°C for three cycles of 1 min on and 30 s off. Samples were clarified by centrifugation at 16,000g for 15 min, at 4°C, and the supernatant was transferred into the new 1.5 tube. Purified proteins were assayed by the EZQ Protein Quantification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) with the SoftMax Pro Software v5.3 under the SpectraMax M5 (Molecular Devices, LLC) following manufacturer’s instruction. The control group was labeled with TMT tags 126, 127N, 127C, 128N, and 128C, and experimental treated group was labeled with TMT tags 129N, 129C, 130N, 130C, 131N, and 131C respectively. Therefore, all experiments were performed with biological pentaplicates for the control group and sextuplicate for the treated group.

Basic pH reversed-phase fractionation and LC-MS/MS analysis

Labeled peptides were desalted with C18-solid phase extraction and dissolved in basic pH reversed-phase (bRP) buffer A (10 mM NH4HCO2, pH10, 5% ACN). The peptides were fractionated using an Agilent HPLC 1260 with a Zorbax Extended C18 bRP column (2.1 × 150 mm, 5 μm, Cat. No. 773700-902, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA) using a gradient of 10–40% bRP buffer B (10 mM NH4HCO2, pH10, 90% ACN) over 50 min by ramping up to 100% solvent B in 5 min at a flow rate of 200 μL min−1. The absorbance at 280 nm was monitored and a total of 19 fractions were collected. The fractions were lyophilized, desalted using zip tip (Millipore C18 Zip Tips, Cat. No. ZTC 18M096, Billerica, MA, USA) and re-suspended in LC solvent A (0.1% formic acid in 99.9% water (v/v)).

A hybrid quadrupole Orbitrap Fusion Tribrid MS system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, CA, USA) was interfaced with an automated Easy-nLC 1200 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Bremen, Germany). Each sample fraction was loaded onto an Acclaim Pepmap 100 pre-column (20 mm × 75 μm; 3 μm-C18) and separated on a PepMap RSLC analytical column (500 mm × 75 μm; 2 μm-C18 100 Å) at a flow rate at 300 nL/min during a linear gradient from 5% solvent A (0.1% formic acid (v/v)) to 25% solvent B (0.1% formic acid (v/v) and 80.0% acetonitrile (v/v)) for 110 min, to 40% solvent B for 10 min, to 95% solvent B for 10 min and hold 95% solvent B for additional 10 min.

A data-dependent method with multi-notch synchronous precursor selection MS3 scanning was employed. Full MS scans were acquired in the Orbitrap mass analyzer between 400 and 1400 m/z range at a set 120,000 resolution. MS2 scans were enabled automatically to isolate fragment precursor ions with charge state filtering from 2 to 6, using collisional-induced dissociation (CID) with a normalized collision energy (NCE) of 35%, an AGC target of 4x105, and a maximum accumulation time of 50 ms. Fragment ions were selected for MS3 scans based on a precursor selection range of 400–1200 m/z. The top 10 precursors were selected for MS3 scans that were acquired in the Orbitrap after HCD fragmentation (NCE 55%) with a maximum fill time of 120 ms at a set 50,000 resolution, 110–500 m/z scan range, ion injection for all parallelizable time turned OFF, and an AGC target value of 1.2x105. MS1 and MS3 scans were acquired in profile mode and MS2 in centroid format under a 3-s duty cycle.

Proteomics data analysis

The raw MS/MS data files were processed by a thorough database searching approach considering biological modification and amino acid substitution against the UniProt Human database (download October 17, 2023; 182,643 entries) using the Proteome Discoverer v2.5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with the SEQUEST algorithm.46 The following parameters were used for all the searching: peptide tolerance at 10 ppm, tandem MS tolerance at ± 0.02 Da, peptide charges of 2+ to 5+, trypsin as the enzyme, allowing one missed cleavage, TMT label and carbamidomethyl (C) as fixed modifications, acetylation (n-terminus), 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (K), loss of methionine (n-terminus), oxidation (M) and phosphorylation (S, T, Y) as variable modifications. For peptide confidence, we adopted the following cutoff values of Xcorr that are commonly used for the SEQUEST algorithm41: 2.31 for 2+, 2.41 for 3+, and 2.6 for 4+ and 5+ peptides. The false discovery rate (FDR) was calculated using the Percolator algorithm in the Proteome Discoverer workflow based on the search results and set at 1%. For protein quantification, only MS/MS spectra that were unique to a particular protein and where the sum of the signal-to-noise ratios for all the peak pairs >9 were used for quantification. The data was normalized with the total peptide amount and scaled with the average of the control group, and the significance of differential expression was assessed by a t-test of p ≤ 0.05 with a fold change cutoff >1.2 or <0.8.

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA isolated from BON1-NSshRNA or BON1-hTSshRNA cells after 24 h of incubation using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). cDNA was synthesized using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 μg of total RNA was converted to cDNA, and further used for PCR analysis. The real-time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green Universal Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) on the ABI 7900HT Fast Real-time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Information regarding the primers used for gene amplification is provided in Table S3. PCR protocol included 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 s, 60°C for 60 s and 95°C for 15 s. The expression levels of the genes were normalized against the expression levels of the housekeeping gene, GAPDH and relative expression was calculated using 2 –ΔΔ CT method.

ATP assay

ATP levels were measured using the Glycolysis/OXPHOS Assay Kit (Dojindo, Cat# G270-10) following the ATP detection protocol provided by the manufacturer. BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells (3,000 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. ATP quantification was performed at 24 h post-seeding. The experiment was repeated three times independently, and in each experiment, two or three technical replicates (individual wells from the 96-well plate) were included for both BON1-NSshRNA and BON1-hTSshRNA cells. All technical replicates were plotted as individual data points for statistical analysis.

Human PanNET data analysis

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from 47 PanNET patients were obtained from the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE117853, subseries GSE117851) for investigating the relationship between TYMS and mTOR signaling in PanNET clinical samples. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the GSEA software33 to assess the association between TYMS and genes related to mTOR signaling pathway. Correlation of TYMS with RHEB, TSC1 and TSC2 were analyzed from the above dataset using GraphPad Software. Gene expression data were obtained from different tumor samples available in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database of the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI-GEO). Similarly, TNM Plotter was used to analyze the correlation between TYMS and RHEB. For this study, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data available in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were utilized.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, USA). Quantification of IHC staining was performed using ImageJ with the IHC Profiler plugin, and proteomics data were analyzed using SHINYGO version 0.77. Data was analyzed using a two-tailed Student’s t test for comparison between two groups. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were analyzed using the log rank Mantel-Cox test to determine the significance of survival studies. The number of replicates and independent experiments is specified in the figure legends. Details of samples used for IHC scoring from hTS/Men1−/− and Men1−/− mice are provided in Figures S3 and S4. The details of the statistical tests used for each experiment are provided in the figure legends, and the corresponding p-values are indicated in the figures. Data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
