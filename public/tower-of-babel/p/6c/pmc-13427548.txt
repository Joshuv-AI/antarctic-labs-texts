Risk, Management, and Outcomes in PCI-Treated Acute Coronary Syndrome With Cardiogenic Shock
Authors: Takanori Ohata, Shun Kohsaka, Ryo Nakamaru, Yasuyuki Shiraishi, Ikuko Ueda, Toshiki Kuno, Masaki Kodaira, Tomohiko Ono, Yohei Numasawa, Masaki Ieda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute coronary syndrome complicated by cardiogenic shock (ACS-CS) remains associated with high mortality despite advances in percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and increasing attention to mechanical circulatory support (MCS).ObjectivesThis study aimed to evaluate temporal trends in patient risk profiles, treatment strategies, and in-hospital outcomes among patients undergoing PCI for ACS-CS.MethodsFrom 2009 to 2019, 24,527 consecutive PCI procedures were recorded in the Keio Interhospital Cardiovascular Studies-PCI registry, including 870 cases (3.5%) with ACS-CS. Patients were categorized into 4 time periods (T1: 2009-2011; T2: 2012-2013; T3: 2014-2016; T4: 2017-2019). Temporal changes in risk profiles were evaluated using National Cardiovascular Data Registry Catheterization PCI mortality and bleeding risk scores. Trends in MCS use, in-hospital mortality, and post-PCI complications were analyzed.ResultsPredicted in-hospital mortality and bleeding risk remained stable over time, at approximately 20% (P for trend = 0.053) and 40% (P for trend = 0.39), respectively. Overall MCS use declined from 66.5% in T1 to 56.7% in T4, primarily due to reduced intra-aortic balloon pump use (46.5% to 31.2%). Despite these changes in management, crude and National Cardiovascular Data Registry risk–adjusted in-hospital mortality remained persistently high at approximately 30% throughout the study period.ConclusionsAmong patients with ACS-CS selected for PCI in a multicenter Japanese registry, management strategies changed substantially over the past decade, particularly regarding MCS utilization. However, no significant temporal improvement in in-hospital mortality was observed, underscoring the persistent clinical challenges of this high-risk PCI-treated population.

Acute coronary syndrome complicated by cardiogenic shock (ACS-CS) remains a critical and life-threatening condition, with reported 30-day mortality rates approaching 40%.1,2 Early revascularization with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is a well-established treatment and has been shown to improve clinical outcomes.3, 4, 5 Over the past decade, several aspects of PCI and acute cardiovascular care have evolved, including the broader use of radial access and bleeding avoidance strategies, second-generation drug-eluting stents, intravascular imaging, and guideline-supported culprit-lesion-only PCI in selected patients with multivessel disease (MVD).2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 During the same period, the role of mechanical circulatory support (MCS) in ACS-CS was reassessed. In particular, RCTs and updated guideline recommendations discouraged routine intra-aortic balloon pump (IABP) use,1,3,4 whereas venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was increasingly considered for selected patients with refractory shock.11,12 Nevertheless, RCTs validating newer PCI-related and MCS strategies for ACS-CS remained limited during much of the study period, highlighting the need to reassess temporal trends in patient management and outcomes among patients selected for PCI.11, 12, 13, 14

Prior temporal trend analyses in ACS-CS have often failed to adequately account for changes in patient risk profiles over time and have reported inconsistent trends in in-hospital mortality, with outcomes variably improving, remaining stable, or worsening.15, 16, 17 These discrepancies underscore the value of reassessing temporal trends with careful consideration of patient risk profiles, including the use of established risk scores, in addition to crude mortality rates.

Accordingly, this study aimed to comprehensively assess the temporal trends in patient risk profiles, PCI-related strategies and device use, and MCS utilization, as well as in-hospital mortality and complications, among patients undergoing PCI for ACS-CS. We analyzed data from the Keio interhospital Cardiovascular Studies (KiCS) PCI registry, developed in collaboration with the National Cardiovascular Data Registry Catheterization PCI (NCDR CathPCI) registry.18,19

Methods

Data source

The KiCS-PCI registry is a multicenter prospective cohort study that consecutively registered patients who underwent PCI between 2009 and 2019 at 15 hospitals in the Tokyo metropolitan area. Of these, 4 were classified as high-volume hospitals (over 300 PCI procedures per year between 2008 and 2018) and 11 institutions were classified as moderate-volume hospitals (performing 150-300 PCI procedures per year between 2008 and 2013). The present analysis was intentionally restricted to the pre-COVID-19 era. This restriction was chosen to evaluate secular trends in PCI practice, MCS utilization, and in-hospital outcomes using complete and consistently collected registry data before pandemic-related disruptions to cardiovascular care, including changes in patient presentation, emergency care systems, procedural volume, case selection, and in-hospital outcomes.20,21

Details of the KiCS-PCI registry have been previously described.18 Briefly, the clinical variables were defined in accordance with those of the American College of Cardiology-sponsored NCDR CathPCI registry to enable direct comparison.18, 19, 20 Patient data were collected from the medical records by dedicated clinical research coordinators and entered into an electronic data capture system. For appropriate registration to maintain data quality, the senior study coordinator (U.I.) and principal investigator (S.K.) checked each institution’s data consistency and queried local clinical research coordinators.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Keio University School of Medicine and each participating hospital, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before registration. Information about the objective and social significance of this registry was submitted for clinical trial registration to the University Hospital Medical Information Network of Japan (UMIN000004736) before the launch of this registry.

Patient selection

During the pre-COVID-19 study period between 2009 and 2019, 24,527 consecutive patients who underwent PCI were analyzed. Patients with stable ischemic heart disease and those with missing data on age, sex, PCI indications, CS, or PCI date were excluded. Definition of clinical variables

The clinical variables and outcomes were defined according to the NCDR CathPCI Registry v4.1 data elements.18,22 CS was defined as refractory shock requiring the use of catecholamines or MCS to maintain systolic blood pressure >80 mm Hg or cardiac index >1.8 L/min/m2. Cardiopulmonary arrest (CPA) was defined as cardiopulmonary resuscitation or defibrillation within 24 hours of admission. Obstructive coronary artery disease was defined as coronary angiography findings of ≥75% stenosis for left anterior descending artery, left circumflex artery, and right coronary artery lesions, or ≥50% stenosis for left main trunk. MVD was defined as 2- or 3-vessel obstructive coronary artery disease. Heart failure (HF) was diagnosed based on documented signs or symptoms, including dyspnea, orthopnea, fluid retention, pulmonary congestion on examination or imaging, or a history of hospitalization for acute HF. The use of hemostatic devices was defined as the application of a radial compression device after the transradial approach or a vascular closure device after the transfemoral approach.

Definition of clinical outcomes

The primary clinical outcome of the present study was in-hospital all-cause mortality. Major bleeding was defined as any bleeding event within 72 hours after PCI, including access-site bleeding, intracranial hemorrhage, cardiac tamponade, retroperitoneal, gastrointestinal, or genitourinary bleeding, a hemoglobin decrease ≥3 g/dL, or blood transfusion.

Statistical analysis

The temporal trend of PCI indications and the proportion of ACS-CS were analyzed across the overall KiCS-PCI cohort. To evaluate changes in baseline patient characteristics and procedural strategies, including PCI-related techniques and treatment approaches that reflect evolving practice patterns, patients were categorized into 4 time periods according to the year of PCI: T1 (2009-2011), T2 (2012-2013), T3 (2014-2016), and T4 (2017-2019). These time periods were used to improve interpretability of the descriptive tables and to reduce the influence of year-to-year random variation in variables with relatively small annual numbers. Temporal trends in MCS utilization and in-hospital outcomes, which were expected to demonstrate more dynamic year-to-year variation and to be closely associated with disease severity and prognosis, were primarily assessed on an annual basis. Annual trend analyses were additionally performed for selected variables and are presented in the Supplemental Appendix. Continuous variables were expressed as medians (IQRs) and compared using the Jonckheere-Terpstra test for trend analysis. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies with percentage and compared using the Cochrane-Armitage test for trend analysis.

The NCDR CathPCI Risk Score and Bleeding Risk Score were calculated to predict the risk of in-hospital mortality and major bleeding events after PCI.23,24 The NCDR CathPCI mortality and bleeding scores were applied as pragmatic tools for case-mix adjustment in this study, rather than as definitive risk estimates for Japanese patients with ACS-CS. Then, temporal trends in crude, predicted, and risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality and bleeding complication rates were evaluated annually using the Cochran-Armitage trend test. Predicted event rates were calculated by summing individual predicted risks and dividing by the number of patients for each year. Risk-adjusted event rates were derived by multiplying the ratio of predicted to observed events for each year by the overall event rate of the ACS-CS cohort, as previously described.25

To further evaluate temporal changes in the risk of in-hospital mortality, a modified Poisson regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the risk of in-hospital mortality in 2010 to 2019, using 2009 as the reference year. In this analysis, covariates were selected based on both the number of observed events and their clinical relevance as reported in previous literature.2,23,26, 27, 28, 29 The following variables were included in the adjusted model: sex; medical history variables (ie, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary artery bypass grafting, and stroke); PCI strategy (1-vessel disease vs culprit-lesion-only PCI in MVD vs multivessel PCI in MVD); achievement of TIMI flow grade 3 in the culprit lesion after PCI; culprit lesion location (left main trunk vs other vessels); hospital procedural volume (high-volume centers [>300 PCI cases/year] vs moderate-volume centers [150-300 PCI cases/year]); and the predicted in-hospital mortality (%) calculated using the NCDR CathPCI Risk Score. In addition, the use and timing of MCS (IABP and/or VA-ECMO) were incorporated as surrogate markers of CS severity,30,31 given the absence of direct hemodynamic parameters (eg, catecholamine use, serum lactate levels) in the registry.

To address potential multicollinearity and overfitting, elastic net regularization was applied.32 A sensitivity analysis was subsequently conducted by refitting the modified Poisson model after excluding covariates not retained by the elastic net procedure to confirm the robustness of the findings. In addition, as part of the subgroup analyses, we explored temporal trends in crude in-hospital mortality stratified by key clinical characteristics known to influence prognosis.33,34 These subgroups included: ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) vs non-STEMI; presence vs absence of CPA; use vs non-use of MCS; and age group (<75 vs ≥75 years).

Missing values required for calculating the NCDR CathPCI Risk Score and the Bleeding Risk Score were imputed according to the methods described in the original model development papers23,24 as follows: 1) previous medical history variables (ie, HF, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, prior PCI, and diabetes mellitus) were imputed as absent; 2) body mass index was imputed using sex-specific median values; 3) estimated glomerular filtration rate was imputed using medians stratified by sex, prior dialysis status, and STEMI presentation (for mortality risk), and by sex and prior dialysis status (for bleeding risk); 4) hemoglobin was imputed using the overall median; and 5) left ventricular ejection fraction was imputed using medians stratified by history of chronic HF, prior myocardial infarction, and STEMI status. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using the R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Among 24,527 consecutive patients who underwent PCI between 2009 and 2019 and were registered in the KiCS-PCI registry, 22,983 were included in the indication-based trend analysis after excluding patients with stable ischemic heart disease and those with missing key data (Figure 1). Within this eligible cohort, the proportions of ACS and ACS-CS among all PCI procedures gradually declined over time (P for trend <0.001 for both) (Supplemental Figure 1 and Supplemental Table 1). Of the 10,053 patients with ACS undergoing PCI, 870 (8.7%) had ACS-CS, with a median age of 69 years (IQR: 61-78), and 20.8% were females (Figure 1). In contrast, the proportion of ACS-CS among patients undergoing PCI for ACS remained relatively stable throughout the study period, accounting for approximately 7% to 10% of ACS cases each year (P for trend = 0.50) (Supplemental Table 1). Procedure characteristics

From T1 to T4, the use of radial artery puncture, hemostatic devices, second-generation drug-eluting stents, and intravascular ultrasonography increased significantly (all P < 0.001). Mechanical circulatory support

The use of MCS decreased significantly over time, from 66.5% in T1 to 56.7% in T4 (P for trend = 0.033) (Supplemental Figure 2), largely driven by a reduction in IABP use (46.5% to 31.2%; P for trend = 0.006). Consistent findings were observed in the annual analysis, with overall MCS use declining from 78.3% in 2009 to 56.8% in 2019 (P for trend = 0.026) (Figure 2). Although the concomitant use of VA-ECMO and IABP remained stable throughout the study period, the overall use of VA-ECMO (with or without IABP) increased significantly in the annual analysis (15.9% to 27.0%; P for trend = 0.039), although this trend did not reach statistical significance when analyzed by time periods. Adjusted Relative Risk of In-Hospital Mortality by Year

Adjusted relative risks of in-hospital mortality for each year were estimated using modified Poisson regression, with 2009 as the reference year. Among 870 patients with ACS-CS, missing data were identified for several covariates, and 44 patients were excluded from this analysis, leaving 826 patients for the final model.

Results of elastic net regularization demonstrated that most covariates were retained in the adjusted model, except for sex, hypertension, prior coronary artery bypass grafting, and 1-vessel disease (vs multivessel PCI in MVD). A sensitivity analysis excluding these variables yielded consistent results, with no significant changes in the adjusted relative risks of in-hospital mortality across calendar years (Supplemental Figure 4). In this revised model, culprit-lesion-only PCI in patients with MVD was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital mortality (relative risk: 0.81; 95% CI: 0.67-0.99; P = 0.04) (Supplemental Table 4).

Subgroup analyses demonstrated higher crude in-hospital mortality among patients with non–ST-elevation myocardial infarction, CPA, MCS use, and age ≥75 years. However, no significant temporal trends in crude mortality were observed within any subgroup (Supplemental Table 5), indicating that the lack of improvement in in-hospital mortality was consistent across key clinical strata.

Discussion

In this multicenter Japanese PCI (J-PCI) registry, we evaluated temporal trends in patient risk profiles, PCI-related management including MCS, and in-hospital outcomes among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI. The notable findings are as follows.1.Over the past decade, several procedural performance measures improved, including the increased use of radial access, hemostatic devices, second-generation drug-eluting stents, and intravascular ultrasound. However, the proportion of culprit-lesion-only PCI in MVD has remained largely unchanged, signifying the need for further advancements in this area.2.Reflecting insights from major randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and updated clinical practice guidelines, overall MCS utilization declined, primarily driven by a reduction in the use of IABP alone. Although crude bleeding complications increased over time, this trend was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for the NCDR CathPCI Bleeding Risk Score, suggesting that changes in patient risk profiles partially explained the observed increase.3.Despite these numerous changes in clinical practice and management strategies, both crude and risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality rates remained consistent throughout the study period among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI. This consistency across key clinical subgroups such as STEMI, CPA, MCS use, and older age, underscores the persistent clinical challenge of ACS-CS management.

Time trends of outcomes in patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI

Although several previous observational studies reported a decreasing trend in in-hospital mortality among patients with ACS-CS,15,16 we observed persistently high crude and risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI over the past decade. In earlier eras, improved access to PCI was considered a major contributor to better outcomes in ACS-CS.35,36 However, in Japan, access to emergent PCI is already widely established nationwide, with minimal regional variation.37 Consequently, further reductions in in-hospital mortality through expanded procedural access alone may be limited. In addition, although PCI-related techniques evolved and VA-ECMO use increased during the study period, in-hospital mortality among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI remained high. This observation should not be interpreted as indicating that procedural advances were ineffective; rather, it underscores the complexity of interpreting mortality trends in a PCI-treated ACS-CS cohort. System-level factors, including prehospital delays, delayed recognition or triage of shock, variability in MCS initiation, and the absence of standardized shock protocols or multidisciplinary shock teams, may also have contributed to the observed lack of improvement.38,39 These observations highlight the need for system-level strategies such as early triage algorithms, regionalized shock care, and coordinated multidisciplinary management.38,39

This finding should nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. The stable mortality rates observed in this study should not be interpreted as evidence of a true therapeutic plateau, because several factors may have influenced the observed trends. First, patient risk profiles remained consistently high throughout the study period. Second, granular hemodynamic markers of shock severity, such as serum lactate level, vasopressor dose, and standardized shock stage, were unavailable, limiting our ability to determine whether case severity changed over time.30 Third, because this was a PCI-based registry, temporal changes in referral patterns and selection for PCI may also have influenced the observed trends. Although access to emergent PCI is relatively well established in Japan, patients considered unlikely to benefit from catheterization or PCI, such as those with extreme advanced age, sustained cardiac arrest, profound shock severity, severe comorbidities, or presumed clinical futility, may not have been captured in this registry. This concern is also supported by prior Japanese nationwide data showing that broader AMI-CS cohorts have reported high short-term mortality, underscoring the importance of case selection when interpreting observed trends across registries.40 Taken together, the lack of improvement in in-hospital mortality may reflect persistent high-risk case mix, residual confounding, changes in selection among patients brought to the catheterization laboratory, and the possibility that gains from evolving treatment strategies were offset by temporal changes in case mix.

Use of MCS

Our study showed notable changes in the use of MCS. Consistent with the IABP-SHOCK II trial and subsequent guideline updates, the role of routine IABP use in ACS-CS has been downgraded, and routine IABP use is no longer recommended in contemporary guidelines.1,3,4 Accordingly, IABP-alone use declined substantially during the study period. In contrast, VA-ECMO use increased, although prior RCTs have not demonstrated a survival benefit with routine VA-ECMO use in ACS-CS.11,12 The reduction in IABP use was not fully offset by greater use of VA-ECMO, likely because these devices serve different clinical roles and are applied to patients with different hemodynamic severity. IABP had historically been used across a broader spectrum of ACS-CS, whereas VA-ECMO tends to be reserved for more refractory cases. In addition, VA-ECMO use may be constrained by institutional capability, bleeding risk, and confounding by indication. Taken together, in the present study, the observed decline in IABP use and increase in VA-ECMO use should be interpreted as changes in support patterns among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI, rather than as reflecting overall temporal changes in MCS use across all patients with ACS-CS.

The timing of MCS initiation in routine practice also differed from that reported in recent RCTs. In the present study, VA-ECMO was initiated before or during PCI in approximately 90% of patients who received VA-ECMO, whereas nearly half of patients assigned to VA-ECMO in the ECLS-SHOCK trial received support after PCI.12 This contrast suggests that patients selected for VA-ECMO in routine PCI practice often receive hemodynamic support at or before the time of PCI. However, because standardized shock-severity metrics were unavailable, these data should not be interpreted as indicating superiority of real-world patient selection or as evidence that patients enrolled in prior RCTs were less appropriate candidates for VA-ECMO. Rather, they underscore the importance of considering timing of support, clinical selection, and shock severity when interpreting observational MCS data and designing future trials.

Interpretation of temporal trends in MCS use should also be made cautiously. Although Impella use was collected in the registry, no Impella use was observed during the study period; therefore, Impella did not contribute to the temporal changes in MCS utilization in this cohort, and its clinical impact could not be evaluated. Because this was a PCI-based registry, the observed temporal trends in MCS utilization should also be interpreted cautiously, as broader Japanese nationwide shock cohorts that included patients who did not undergo PCI had substantially higher short-term mortality than PCI-based cohorts, highlighting the importance of case selection and the limited ability of this registry to reflect overall system-level changes in ACS-CS management.40

Bleeding complications

Bleeding complications increased over the observation period in our study, despite a similar bleeding risk profile. Notably, patients on VA-ECMO exhibited a higher incidence of bleeding complications than those using IABP or not using MCS, which is consistent with the results of previous RCTs.11,12 In patients with ACS-CS, bleeding complications were significantly associated with higher in-hospital mortality, and MCS use was statistically significant predictor of bleeding.41 Therefore, proper patient selection for MCS could be important for preventing avoidable bleeding complications, especially in East Asian populations with a relatively higher bleeding risk than in Western populations.18,42

Other challenges in ACS-CS

A lower proportion of TIMI flow grade 3 in culprit lesions after PCI for patients with ACS-CS than in those without CS was observed in a previous study.5 In our study, TIMI flow grade 3 in the culprit lesion after PCI was consistently achieved in approximately 90% of patients after T2. In addition, it was independently associated with improved in-hospital mortality, as reported by previous studies.43 Achieving TIMI flow grade 3 after PCI can be facilitated by shortening both the door-to-balloon and onset-to-balloon times, as suggested in previous studies.44 These times could potentially be modified through enhanced public education regarding the symptoms of ACS and the improvement of system delays, including emergency medical services and in-hospital care.4

A notable advancement in PCI strategy for ACS-CS has been the establishment of culprit-lesion-only PCI in patients with MVD.2, 3, 4 The CULPRIT-SHOCK trial showed that this strategy reduced the 30-day risk of all-cause mortality or renal replacement therapy compared with immediate multivessel PCI.2 In the present study, the proportion of patients with MVD treated with culprit-lesion-only PCI remained approximately 70% across the observation period. In the revised multivariable model, culprit-lesion-only PCI was associated with a lower risk of in-hospital mortality. However, this observational finding should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating because PCI strategy may be influenced by coronary anatomy, lesion complexity, hemodynamic instability, operator judgment, and residual confounding. Further research is warranted to determine how best to implement evidence-based PCI strategies in routine care for patients with ACS-CS selected for PCI.

Study Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, detailed hemodynamic variables and standardized shock-severity measures, including serum lactate level, vasopressor dose, serial hemodynamic parameters, markers of hypoperfusion, and Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) shock stage,28 were not consistently available. Because much of the study period preceded the widespread adoption of the SCAI shock classification and the registry was not designed for retrospective SCAI staging, we did not assign SCAI stages to avoid potential misclassification. Second, because the registry included only patients who underwent PCI, we could not evaluate temporal changes in referral patterns, treatment deferral, futility decisions, selection for catheterization or PCI, or outcomes among patients with ACS-CS who were not taken to the catheterization laboratory or were managed without PCI. As a result, selection bias is inherent, and patients deemed unsuitable for catheterization or PCI, such as those with extreme advanced age, sustained cardiac arrest, profound shock severity, severe comorbidities, or presumed clinical futility, may have been excluded. Third, MCS use and timing were included as clinically relevant treatment variables that may partially reflect shock severity, but they are also strongly influenced by institutional practice patterns, physician preference, device availability, and treatment-selection bias. Therefore, these variables should not be interpreted as direct or standardized measures of shock severity. Fourth, left ventricular ejection fraction was missing in a substantial proportion of patients, likely reflecting survival bias. Fifth, the NCDR CathPCI mortality and bleeding scores were used as pragmatic tools for case-mix adjustment; although the KiCS-PCI registry was developed using harmonized definitions with the NCDR and NCDR-based models have previously been evaluated in J-PCI cohorts, these models were not specifically developed for Japanese patients with ACS-CS, and the bleeding model in particular has not been fully validated in this setting. Sixth, the impact of Impella could not be evaluated because no Impella use was observed during the study period. This limits the applicability of our findings to contemporary practice in the Impella era, particularly after recent evidence from the DanGer Shock trial suggesting a potential survival benefit of microaxial flow pump support in selected patients with infarct-related CS.45 Seventh, because the present study focused on early in-hospital outcomes, specifically major bleeding within 72 hours after PCI and all-cause in-hospital mortality, our findings may not be directly comparable with studies evaluating longer-term bleeding or mortality outcomes. Finally, institutional variability in treatment strategies and residual unmeasured confounders may have influenced the observed outcomes. In addition, this study was based on a metropolitan J-PCI registry in the pre-COVID-19 and pre-Impella era. Although harmonized definitions aligned with the NCDR CathPCI registry and consistency of patient characteristics and in-hospital outcomes with those reported in the nationwide J-PCI registry support comparability with other J-PCI registries, the generalizability of the findings to all patients with ACS-CS, non-PCI-treated patients, other health care systems, and contemporary MCS practice remains limited.28

Conclusions

In this Japanese multicenter PCI registry, management strategies for patients with ACS-CS selected for PCI changed substantially between 2009 and 2019, including the increased use of contemporary PCI-related practices, decreased IABP use, and increased VA-ECMO use. However, no significant temporal improvement in crude or risk-adjusted in-hospital mortality was observed. Given the PCI-based design, the absence of granular shock-severity measures, and potential changes in patient selection over time, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be considered evidence of a definitive therapeutic plateau. COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Among patients with ACS-CS undergoing PCI, patient risk profiles remained stable over the past decade, whereas procedural strategies and MCS utilization changed substantially. Despite these changes in management, in-hospital mortality remained persistently high, highlighting the ongoing challenges in improving outcomes in this high-risk population.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: Future studies should incorporate standardized shock-severity assessment, contemporary MCS strategies including microaxial flow pumps, and patients not selected for PCI to clarify how evolving treatment strategies and patient selection influence outcomes in ACS-CS.
