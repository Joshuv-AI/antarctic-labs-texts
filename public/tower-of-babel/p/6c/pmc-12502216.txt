First-trimester cervical length screening and the risk of preterm birth: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Sedigheh Hantoushzadeh, Nafiseh Saedi, Kyana Jafarabady, Mohadese Dashtkoohi, Mohammad Haddadi, Nasim Eshraghi, Nikan Zargarzadeh, Hiba Mustafa, Alireza Shamshirsaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe clinical utility of first-trimester cervical length (CL) measurement remains uncertain. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the association between first-trimester CL measurement and preterm birth (PTB) risk.MethodsThis study was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42023462610). A comprehensive search was conducted across four electronic databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library) until January 29, 2024, using MeSH terms and keywords. We included randomized controlled trials, comparative observational studies, and retrospective studies that examined first-trimester CL measurement and reported PTB as a primary or secondary outcome. Two independent reviewers conducted study selection and data extraction, resolving discrepancies through discussion. Risk of bias was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. A meta-analysis using a random-effects model was conducted, with heterogeneity evaluated via I² statistics.ResultsFive studies comprising 16,053 pregnancies were included. CL was significantly shorter in individuals delivering before 34 weeks compared to those delivering at or after 34 weeks, with a pooled mean difference of -0.59 mm (95% confidence interval [CI]: -1.14 to -0.04; p = 0.04; I²=72.56%). Similarly, those delivering before 37 weeks had a significantly shorter CL than those delivering at or after 37 weeks, with a pooled mean difference of -0.23 mm (95% CI: -0.43 to -0.03; p = 0.02; I²=76.26%).ConclusionFirst-trimester CL measurement is significantly associated with PTB risk. However, due to study heterogeneity and physiological variability in early pregnancy, its clinical utility as a predictive tool remains uncertain. Further research with standardized protocols and larger cohorts is needed to assess its role in screening and early intervention strategies for both low- and high-risk pregnancies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08210-x.

Introduction

Although preterm birth (PTB) is a recognized condition, its definition varies depending on the patient’s condition and settings. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), PTB is defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation [1]. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) report, in 2022, PTB affected approximately one out of every 10 infants born in the United States. The PTB rate decreased by 1% in 2022 to 10.4%, following a 4% increase from 2020 to 2021 [2]. PTB accounted for approximately 14.8% of infant deaths. Infants born prematurely may experience various complications, including vision and hearing problems, respiratory-related issues, feeding difficulties and neurological disorders [3].

The proposed mechanisms for PTB initiation include mechanical factors, inflammation, infection, circulatory disturbances, or a combination of several causes [4, 5]. Moreover, many socio-economic and medical risk factors are increasing the chance of PTB, such as previous PTB, infection, low and high weight, smoking, extremes of maternal age, short cervical length (CL), and others [5]. CL measurement is considered a valuable screening tool for predicting PTB and guiding preventive interventions for pregnancies at risk [6]. Previous studies have reported a correlation between CL measurement during the second trimester and the risk of PTB [6–8]. Some publications advocate the beneficial effect of universal transvaginal ultrasound CL screening, as it is linked to a decrease in spontaneous PTB rates compared to not conducting screening [9, 10]. Timely intervention to decrease the risk of spontaneous PTB yielded significant benefits [9]. There are many offered management to prevent PTB, such as vaginal progesterone, cerclage, or pessary implantation due to the patient’s situation [11]. The majority of interventional studies have used a CL cutoff of 25 mm as the best threshold for predicting PTB [12]. Cervical measurement is often performed during the second trimester while measuring CL through the first trimester is controversial [13]. While some studies showed that measuring CL before the second trimester of gestation could not predict PTB [13–15]other evidence supports the role of CL in the first trimester in predicting PTB [16, 17]. Assessing CL in earlier trimesters can provide sufficient time to implement appropriate management strategies, thereby helping to prevent emergency procedures associated with high fetal and maternal complications.

In the current study, our goal is to assess the current evidence regarding the significance of measuring CL during the first trimester and its association with the risk of PTB.

Materials and methods

This systematic review was carried out in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [18, 19] (Supplementary Table 1). Our study protocol is registered at PROSPERO under the number CRD42023462610.

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed through the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL, via the Cochrane Library), MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and Web of Science until January 29, 2024. The following keywords were utilized as search strategies: “Cervical length” and “preterm birth”. No language restrictions were obtained. The detailed search strategy with results is summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Study selection and data extraction

Two reviewers independently performed screening of the retrieved articles to include studies that met the following inclusion criteria: (1) patient population: preterm pregnancies; (2) Intervention: CL measurement in the first trimester; (3) Comparison: term pregnancies; (4) Outcome: preterm birth; (5) Setting/Time: until January 29, 2024 and (6) study design: randomized controlled trials (RCTs), comparative observational studies, and retrospective studies.

Quality assessment

Two reviewers carried out the risk of bias assessment by using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) which assesses studies in three sections including selection, comparability, and outcome [20]. Studies with 7–9 scores are rated as “Good”, 4–6 scores as “Fair”, and lower than three are rated as “Poor”. The third reviewer solved disagreements.

Statistical analysis

A meta-analysis was conducted utilizing RevMan 5.4 and STATA to compute pooled effect estimates, represented as standardized mean differences (SMD), along with their respective 95% confidence intervals. RevMan 5.4 was used to calculate mean, and standard deviation if they were not mentioned in studies, STATA was utilized for conducting pooled proportion analysis and performing forest plots, funnel plots, and sensitivity analysis. Heterogeneity among studies was evaluated utilizing I2 statistic. To address the high heterogeneity (I² >70%), sensitivity analyses were conducted by systematically excluding one study at a time to assess its influence on the overall effect size. Potential sources of heterogeneity were explored, and the impact of study characteristics was considered in the interpretation of results.

Results

Study selection

A comprehensive investigation of electronic databases identified a total of 233 articles. Following the removal of duplicates and screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a final set of five articles, a total of 16,053 patients, were included in this systematic review and meta-analysis [13, 14, 21–23]. Quality assessment

The assessment of study quality was conducted using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies, as detailed in Supplementary Table 3. The studies exhibited a good overall quality level, with all studies achieving a score of eight on the NOS.

Meta-analysis results

Comparison of first-trimester CL between patients who delivered before 34 weeks gestation versus after 34

The meta-analysis of the three studies revealed that first-trimester CL was significantly lower in patients who delivered before 34 weeks versus those who delivered after 34 weeks. The pooled mean of first-trimester CL measurements indicated a significantly lower value among individuals who delivered before 34 weeks, with a mean difference of −0.59. (95% confidence interval [CI] −1.14 to −0.04, p = 0.04; I² =72.56%). Figure 2 presents the forest plot, which shows the results of individual studies as well as the overall pooled effect.

The meta-analysis of the five studies indicated a significant decrease in first-trimester CL among patients delivering before 37 gestational weeks compared to those delivered after 37 weeks gestation. The pooled mean of first-trimester CL exhibited a notable difference, with a pooled mean of −0.23 (95% CI −0.43 to −0.03, p = 0.02; I2 = 76.26%). Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted by systematically excluding one study at a time to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall effect size. A leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the results.

The exclusion of the study conducted by G. Conoscenti would lead to an increased overall effect size of −0.81 when comparing patients who delivered before 34 weeks gestation to those who delivered after 34 weeks. Additionally, the results of sensitivity analyses concerning patients who delivered before 37 weeks gestation versus after 37 weeks indicated that the removal of Greco’s 2012 study would reduce the effect size to −0.14. (Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

CL cut-off for predicting PTB

Three studies have undertaken the accuracy of CL in predicting PTB by defining a cut-off for CL in first trimester [24–26].

Ozdemir et al. revealed that CLs measured at 10–14 weeks did not show a statistically significant difference between patients who had delivery after 35 weeks (40.9 ± 4.8 mm) and those who delivered before 35 weeks (38.6 ± 6.3 mm) (P> 0.05); they used a CL of 39 mm as a cut-off[24].. Another study done by Berghella et al. showed that CL < 25 mm between 10 and 14 weeks for predicting PTB at < 35 weeks had 14% sensitivity, 97% specificity, 50% positive, and 85% negative predictivity [25]. Additionally, Carvalho et al. found that CL measured at 11 to 14 weeks did not show a statistically significant difference between the group that delivered after 35 weeks (42.7 mm) and the preterm delivery group (40.6 mm, P = 0.2459). They used 20 mm as the cut-off for defining a short cervix [26]. (Table 1)

Discussion

The meta-analysis comprised five articles, which examined the relationship between CL measurement during the first trimester of pregnancy and PTB at two cut-offs (< 34 weeks and < 37 weeks). The analysis revealed that pregnant individuals who underwent PTB before 37- and 34-weeks’ gestation exhibited significantly shorter CL during the first trimester compared to those who had full-term pregnancies.

The utilization of CL measurement during the first trimester for predicting spontaneous PTB continues to be a topic of debate. Although the observed differences in CL are statistically significant, their relatively small effect sizes prompt concerns regarding their clinical applicability as an isolated predictor. This is particularly due to the considerable overlap in CL measurements, coupled with the inherent physiological variability of the cervix during early gestation. Nevertheless, these findings indicate that first-trimester CL may still play a valuable role in risk stratification when integrated with additional factors such as maternal obstetric history or relevant biomarkers.

A retrospective cohort study assessing the factors associated with predicting PTB in the short cervix population, revealed a significant association between the gestational age at which cervical measurements were first taken and the risk of PTB [27]. Kaplan et al. suggest that factors such as maternal characteristics and a history of previous preterm delivery may be more valuable than CL measurement in assessing the risk of spontaneous PTB during the first trimester [28]. Additionally, Greco et al. showed that utilizing CL measurement during the 11–13 gestational weeks, along with other maternal characteristics, resulted in approximately 55% sensitivity in predicting PTB [22]. Another prospective study indicated that CL under 27 mm during 11–14 weeks was notably related to higher risk of early preterm labor [29]. On the Other hand, a prospective study indicated that the measurement of CL during the first trimester did not show a significant difference between women with PTB (less than 37 weeks of gestation) and those who had term delivery. Similarly, there was no notable difference in CL measurement during the first trimester between women who had a previous preterm delivery and those who did not [23]. Furthermore, a study conducted by Cordero et al. revealed that CL measurement between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation might not reliably identify women at risk of spontaneous PTB [30].

The discrepancies observed in the results of studies regarding the accuracy of CL measurement during the first trimester for spontaneous PTB may be attributed to the anatomical characteristics of the cervix, particularly its curvature between 11 and 14 weeks of gestation [31]. This natural curvature can result in shorter measurements when using the conventional method. To address this issue, Feng et al. proposed an alternative method known as the “two lines” method (adding the distance from the internal os to the highest point of cervical curvature to the distance from the most curved part of the cervix to the external os). In following, the study showed that utilizing this method for measuring CL during the first trimester resulted in significantly shorter measurements in women who experienced PTB compared to those who had a full-term delivery [21].

The meta-analysis comprised five studies, including a total of 16,053 pregnancies, which provides a substantial dataset. However, we recognize that the inclusion of only five studies may limit the generalizability and robustness of our findings. The comparatively small number of included studies restricts the diversity of patient populations, clinical settings, and methodologies used for CL measurement, potentially introducing selection bias.

A primary limitation of our meta-analysis is the high heterogeneity observed (I² >70%), indicating substantial variability across the included studies. This heterogeneity likely arises from multiple sources, including differences in gestational age at screening (ranging from 11 + 0 to 15 + 6 weeks), study populations (e.g., variations in maternal risk factors or demographic characteristics), and methodological approaches (e.g., the use of the two-line method in Feng et al. versus the conventional straight-line method in others). Additionally, one study (Greco et al.) did not explicitly confirm restriction to singleton pregnancies, introducing potential uncertainty about population consistency, though sensitivity analyses suggest this had minimal impact on the pooled estimates. The small number of included studies further limits the generalizability of our findings, as it restricts the diversity of clinical settings, measurement protocols, and patient cohorts represented. Publication bias could also not be fully assessed due to the limited sample size, though the quality of included studies, as evaluated by the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale, was consistently high. To enhance the clinical applicability of first-trimester CL measurement, future research should validate these findings in larger, more diverse populations with standardized screening protocols and clearly defined pregnancy types. Despite these limitations, our study synthesizes the available evidence on first-trimester CL and PTB risk, offering a foundation for ongoing investigations and underscoring the need for refined approaches to early pregnancy screening.

In conclusion, while a shorter first-trimester CL is associated with an increased risk of PTB, it does not serve as a definitive standalone predictor, partly due to the underdeveloped lower uterine segment before 14 weeks of gestation and physiological variability. Nevertheless, first-trimester CL measurement may serve as a supplementary tool, but its widespread clinical adoption requires validation through standardized measurement protocols and larger, diverse cohorts. The use of specific CL cut-offs in early pregnancy remains uncertain, underscoring the need for further research to refine its predictive accuracy and establish thresholds that could guide early risk stratification or preventive strategies.
