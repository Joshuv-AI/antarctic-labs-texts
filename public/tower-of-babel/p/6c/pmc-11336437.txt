Response of field pea (Pisum sativum L.) genotypes for grain yield in a multi-environment trial in Southeastern Ethiopia
Authors: Gebeyaw Achenef Haile, Deressa Tesfaye
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractField pea (Pisum sativum L.) is a key cool-season food legume grown in Ethiopia, particularly in the Southeastern Arsi Zone. Although there is potential for field pea production in the area, adopting new and improved varieties is challenging because local farmers frequently prioritize cereal crops over field peas. To tackle this issue, a study was conducted to identify and promote high-yielding, improved field pea varieties suitable for the Southeastern farming community and similar agro-ecologies. The study focused on assessing the relationship between genotype and environment as well as the stability pattern of 14 advanced field pea genotypes. The genotypes were evaluated in eight environments in two consecutive cropping seasons (2014–2015) in southeast Ethiopia. The study utilized a randomized complete block design consisting of four replications. Various parametric stability analyses were used, including joint regression, additive main effect and multiplicative interaction (AMMI), and additive main effect and multiplicative interaction stability value (ASV). The grain yield was significantly influenced by genotype, location, and their interactions. The average grain yield ranged from 2809.5 kg ha−1 to 3509.1 kg ha−1. Eberhart's stability analysis identified stable genotypes: G3, G4, and G8, while G14 was unstable. According to additive main effect and multiplicative interaction stability value (ASV) and yield stability index (YSI), genotypes G12 and G13 had very low ASV values, whereas genotype G4 had very low ASV and YSI values. The AMMI and GGE biplot graphs showed that the principal component axes (PC1) and (PC2) accounted for 64.1 % and 61.36 % of the total variation, respectively. The results indicated that genotypes responded differently to environmental conditions and that the environment also influenced genotype performance. Genotypes G4 and G3 consistently performed well and exhibited remarkable stability, making them excellent cultivars for improving field pea productivity.Keywords: AMMI, Environment, Field pea, Genotype, GGE biplot, Interaction, Stability

Introduction

Field pea is considered a significant cool season pulse crop, contributing to a global production of 12.5 million metric tons across 7,881,943 ha [1]. Field pea is grown mainly for human consumption, animal feed, and soil improvement. In Ethiopia, the cultivated land area and yield of field pea are recorded at 0.23 million hectares and 0.34 million metric tons, respectively. In addition to providing sustenance, animal feed, income, and the restoration of soil fertility, the cultivation of this crop serves various economic and ecological purposes. This particular crop thrives in a cool and relatively humid climate, making it suitable for cultivation in high altitude regions of tropical areas.

The favorable combination of humidity and cool temperatures promotes the vegetative growth of field peas. While field peas can adapt to a wide range of soil types, they flourish in fertile, light-textured, and well-drained soils. However, they are susceptible to salinity and extreme acidity. Therefore, it is advisable to maintain a soil pH level between 5.5 and 7.0 for optimal field pea production [2]. Analysis of genotype-by-environment interaction can provide information about the possible adaptation areas of a cultivar [3]. In breeding programs, it is also useful for determining the most effective use of time and resources. Genotype-environment interaction causes variation in yield and phenotypic traits across seasons [4]. A genotype's sustainability characteristics are determined by its genotype-environment interaction (GEI).

Genotype-environment interaction (GEI) occurs when genotypes demonstrate diverse responses under varying conditions. The genetic manifestation of genotypes varies based on the surrounding milieu, thereby complicating the process of selecting genotypes for traits in accordance with environmental circumstances. In situations where genotype performance is contingent upon environmental factors, stability analysis emerges as the most effective approach. Numerous quantitative tools, both parametric and nonparametric, are accessible for the examination of GEI by employing multi-environment trials (MET) data to reveal patterns of genotype-by-environment interaction (GEI).

Furthermore, through the utilization of stability measurements, prominent producers will have the capability to discern cultivars with extensive or precise adaptability as a substantial interaction between the genotype and environment (G x E). Various approaches have been employed to examine genotype-by-environment interaction. The GGE-biplot is a valuable graphical display that helps in visually assessing the relationship between the test environments, genotypes, and genotype-environment interactions in cultivar evaluation [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Additionally, the Additive Main Effect and Multiplicative Interaction (AMMI) model, which considers both main effects and interactions, is the preferred graphical analysis to visualize the pattern of genotypes in multi-environment trials [3].

Despite the breeding program's efforts to improve yield, disease resistance, and quality traits, the performance of the improved field pea varieties developed over the last three decades has been inconsistent across different environments and years. Thus, the breeding program seeks to develop new and improved field pea varieties that can endure varying climatic conditions and reduce climate impact on production. To this end, this study aims to evaluate the yield performance of several field pea genotypes, measure the interaction between genotype and environment, and identify genotypes that exhibit high yield and stability. The results of this study will provide critical insights into the performance of various field pea genotypes and their interaction with different environments, which will be useful in breeding programs that aim to develop field pea varieties with higher yields and stability.

These locations are known for growing cool-season pulse crops and are situated in highland regions. Each combination of location and year was treated as a distinct environment for the experiments. The test sites differed in terms of agro-climatic factors such as altitude, rainfall, soil type, and temperature. More information about each test environment can be found in Table 1.

Genotypes and experimental design

Fourteen (14) field pea genotypes advanced from preliminary variety trials, along with two standard checks (Table 2), were employed during the main cropping season for two years (2014–2015). A randomized complete block design was used at all testing sites, with four replications. The plot's proportions were constant throughout all trial sites, measuring 4 m long and 0.8 m wide. The replication, plot, row, and plant distances were carefully kept at 1.5 m, 1 m, 20 cm, and 5 cm, respectively. The suggested ammonium phosphate (DAP) fertilizer rate was 100 kg ha−1, and hand weeding was done three to four weeks following emergence.

Data collection and analysis

The grain yield (gram) of each plot was determined using clean, dried seed, adjusted to 10 % standard moisture content, and then translated to kg/ha for data analysis. The acquired data were subjected to variance analysis, and raw data from individual and combined environments were critically examined using R version 4.01. A randomized complete block design (RCBD) was used to examine the significance of the main effect and interactions related to the parameters measured in each of the eight conditions.

For each test scenario, individual and combined ANOVA models were used to account for fixed genotypes, random locations, and interactions. The combined ANOVA models include genotype (G), location (L), and G × E interactions. However, a year is considered an environment.

The model: Rijkr = m + Gi + Lj + r (L) + GLij + eijkr. Where m = grand mean; G = genotype; L = location; and GLij = genotype by location interaction; r (L) = replication within location; and eijkr = random error. The initial statistical analyses were examined, including the normality test using the Shapiro-Wilk normality test, the normal QQ plot, and residual vs. fitted value, constant leverage, and scale location. For the purpose of combined analysis, Bartlett's test was performed to assess any differences in error variance among the groups, and no significant variations were observed. Posthoc analysis was performed using Duncan's Multiple Range Test (DMRT) to determine the significant differences between the means of various genotypes and environments.

Stability analysis

To identify the best genotype, the multi-environment yield trial is one of the most common experiments in agricultural research to extract maximum information [9]. Therefore, the AMMI (additive main effect and multiplication interaction) analysis, as suggested by Ref. [10] was done with the help of R-software version 4.01 using the Metan package [11], using the adjusted mean output of single-environment (location-year) analysis. Joint regression analysis was used to determine whether genotype effects and interaction effects within specific environments are connected to environmental impacts. The sum square of interaction is divided into two parts: heterogeneity of linear regression coefficients (bi) and pooled differences from unique regression lines (S2di). The other univariate stability statistics, i.e., Shukula's stability variance (σi2), Kang's yield-stability statistics (YSI), and one of the multivariate stability statistics, AMMI and AMMI stability value (ASV), for genotypes, were computed using the Metan package of R-software version 4.01 [11].

The performance of the stability index was estimated by applying the sum of the ranking based on performance and the ranking based on the AMMI stability value to compute the yield stability index (YSI), according to Refs. [12,13], YSI = rASV + rGY, where YAI = yield stability index, rASV = rank of genotypes based on AMMI stability value, and rGY = rank of genotype yield across environments. The AMMI analysis of variance was run on 14 field pea grain yield observations collected from environments with two distinct growing seasons. AMMI partitions the residual matrices through singular value decompositions after fitting the main effect using the following model:Yij=μ+Gi+Ej+∑n=1nλnαinyjn+eijk.

Where, Yij is the yield of the ith genotype in the jth environment, μ is the grand mean, Gi is the mean of the ith genotype minus the grand mean, Ej is the mean of the jth environment minus the grand mean, λn is the square root of the Eigenvalue of the principal component (PCA) axis αin and yjn are the principal component scores for PCA axis n of the ith genotype and jth environment and eijk is the error term.

Stability indices and regression coefficient

The regression coefficient (bi) categorized genotypes into three groups. G3, G4, and G8 were identified as stable genotypes across all environments, with bi values close to unity (bi = 1). G5, G6, G9, and G12 were found to be adaptable to low-yielding environments (bi < 1), whereas the remaining genotypes (G1, G2, G7, G10, G11, G13, and G14) were adapted to high-yielding environments (bi > 1).

Based on the ASV and YSI, G4 was determined to be the most stable genotype. Among the fourteen genotypes, G12, G4, and G7 were identified as the most stable genotypes, exhibiting the lowest AMMI stability value. When considering this stability method, genotypes G5, G6, G1, and G12 displayed high ASV and a high rank of yield stability index (YSI), indicating that they are the most unstable genotypes (Table 6). These results are consistent with those found in the yield stability analysis of maize hybrids as reported in Ref. [17].

Additive main effect and multiplicative interaction (AMMI)

The mean squares obtained from the AMMI analysis indicated that, out of the various G × E interactions, two PCAs were found to be significant. The first two PCAs accounted for approximately 64.1 % of the total GEI, with 38 corresponding degrees of freedom, indicating that the majority of the variation was captured by these components for the grain yield of field pea. Given that the first two PCAs were significant (P < 0.001); the AMMI2 model was deemed to be the best-fit for the two-year field pea yield trial data (Table 7).

The AMMI procedure involved the partitioning of genotype by environment (G x E) interactions through principal component axis (PCAs), followed by the assessment of interaction significance using the F-test [18]. F-statistics were utilized to determine the appropriate number of PCAs to be considered for each axis of the testing mean square, along with an estimated error. In the analysis of multi-location experiments, the AMMI model serves as a significant statistical tool for comprehending the intricate interactions between genotype and environment (G x E) [19].

Based on this graph, the genotypes located on the right side of the mean axis were found to be suitable for environments E2, E3, E4, and E6. On the other hand, the genotypes and environments on the left side of the mean axis yielded the lowest results for their respective environments (Fig. 2). Discussion

The analysis of variance assesses the primary impact of location, genotype and their interaction. In order to comprehend and describe the interaction between genotype and environment, as well as the consistent performance of genotypes, various statistical procedures are necessary to characterize cultivar performance. The notable interactions of the primary factors of genotype, environment, and their interactions suggest that the average values of the 14 field pea genotypes differed across various locations. These variations can be attributed to the existence of substantial genetic diversity among the fourteen field pea genotypes. The result of the present study suggest that significant genotype by environment (G x E) interactions were observed, indicating that distinct genotypes exhibited different responses to various environmental conditions. These results were also evident across different geographic locations. The findings are supported by previous research; including those by Refs. [14,15].

The cultivar mean across locations are an important indicator of the potential of the cultivar to be high yielder in the entire production area. Therefore, precise evaluation of yield performance of new genotypes across sites is critical for plant breeding programs [23]. The average grain yield of field pea genotypes exhibited a range of variability, with genotype G5 yielding 2809.5 kg ha−1 and genotype G6 yielding 3509.1 kg ha−1 (Table 4). The test site Bekoji had the largest mean yield (5137.9 kg ha−1); while Assasa had the lowest mean yield (1123.2 kg ha−1). The genotypes G4 and G6 exhibited commendable performance in the Bekoji, Assasa, and Kofele environments; however, their response in the remaining test locations was dissimilar. In essence, the comprehensive analysis of variance confirmed the presence of genotype-environment interaction in field pea genotypes.

In this study, the joint regression and AMMI-based stability statistics were employed to assess the interactions between genotypes and environments, as well as yield stability among field pea genotypes. According to Ref. [15], a regression coefficient (bi) that approximates unity, along with a deviation from regression (S2di) close to zero, indicates stability. In terms of field pea grain yield, the bi value for genotypes G3, G4, and G8 was found to be close to unity (bi = 1), while genotypes G5, G6, G9, and G12 exhibited values below unity (bi < 1) for the regression coefficient. These genotypes demonstrate a high level of adaptation to areas with less favorable conditions, whereas the remaining genotypes, those with values greater than unity (bi > 1), are well-suited to favorable environments (Table 6). This finding aligns with the results reported in Ref. [24], where it was observed that genotypes with a slope of the regression coefficient bi < 1 were specifically adapted to poor environments, while those with bi > 1 were adaptable to high-yielding environments in terms of genotype by environment interaction and stability of corn hybrids. A comparison of genotypes G2, G10, G8, and G13 revealed a greater degree of stability. On the other hand, genotypes G6 and G1 were identified as the most unstable yet high-yielding genotypes, while G11, G9, and G12 were the most stable but low-yielding genotypes. G5, however, was both the most unstable and low-yielding genotype.

In the GGE biplot, PC1 represented the average performance of the genotypes, and it was found to be a reliable measure of the overall performance of the genotypes. On the other hand, PC2 was an indicator of the genotype-by-environment interaction associated with each genotype. The principal component axis (PCA) revealed two major components, namely PC1 and PC2. The first component, PC1, accounted for about 41.68 % of the total variation, while the second component, PC2, explained 19.68 % of the variation. Together, these two components accounted for 61.36 % of the variation, indicating that they were the most important factors in the analysis. [25], found that the biplot graph helped identify top-performing genotypes for specific locations or stable genotypes across multiple locations, as well as representative locations. This interaction provided insights into the performance of the genotypes across different environments. During this study, genotypes G5, G8, G9, G11, and G12 were grouped together because their PC1 values were less than 0, indicating lower yields. This information was presented in Fig. 3. The interaction between the genotypes and the environment was measured by PC2, which provided an estimate of the variability and stability of the genotypes. Overall, the results of the analysis provided valuable insights into the performance of the genotypes and their response to different environments.

In GGE biplot graph the genotype-focused scaling method evaluates the performance of different genotypes by measuring their dissimilarity in discriminating the genotypes. Among all the genotypes, G12 was found to be stable as it had PC2 values close to zero. The other researchers conducted by Refs. [26,27], revealed that the analysis of polygon graphs revealed three distinct superior mega-environments. Additionally, they identified the appropriate genotypes for each mega-environment to improve the yield of faba bean seeds. However, the yield potential of this genotype was found to be poor. On the other hand, G4 and G2 were identified as high-yielding and relatively stable genotypes. This suggests that these genotypes can produce high yields consistently across different environmental conditions. Overall, these findings provide valuable insights for the selection and breeding of superior genotypes for improved crop yields.

The polygon view of the GGE biplot was divided into five sectors using perpendicular lines known as rays, which originate from the polygon sides (as shown in Fig. 4). The polygon is created by connecting the most extreme genotypes present in the biplot. This method is referred to as the Which-won-where view of the GGE biplot. When considering the impact of different environments on genetic traits, it is important to note that certain genotypes may be more successful in certain sectors than in others. This means that a crossover G x E pattern exists, where different genotypes will be more successful in different sectors due to variations in environmental factors. The vertex genotype refers to the genotypes (G6, G3, G13, and G8) that have shown the highest success in a specific environment or set of environments within a particular sector. This result is explained by Ref. [28], have provided support for this result by explaining that if diverse test environments exist in these sectors, there are several high-yielded hybrids available. However, in sectors where G5, G9, and G12 do not have suitable environments, the vertex genotype is considered to be underperforming in all environments. This result aligns with the findings of [28], who reported that genotypes found in sectors lacking suitable environments are the least performing genotypes. The genotypes that were positioned at the vertexes, which were the farthest from the biplot origin, were the most responsive to the environments present in the sector of each vertex.

Conclusions

Crop researchers aim to develop stable, high-yielding crop varieties, but face challenges due to genotype by environment interactions. Factors such as environmental conditions and genetics must be considered to create varieties that perform well across different environments. Varieties that excel in one environment may not perform well in others. The investigation revealed unique trends in grain yield variation among fourteen field pea genotypes in different environments. Genotypes exhibit different rankings in different environments due to their genetic composition and environmental or climatic conditions. Hence, it is essential to assess the performance of cultivars in a range of conditions to identify high-yielding and adaptable varieties. Although genotypes with high yields and lacking stability can be suitable for specific environments where they perform well, it is important to validate the study's findings by evaluating different genotypes in various environments over multiple cropping seasons.
