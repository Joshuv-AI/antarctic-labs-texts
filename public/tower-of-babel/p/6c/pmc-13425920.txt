Pain, sleep complaints, and depressive symptom clusters and their association with cognitive decline in older Mexican Americans
Authors: Utpol Das, Matthew Phan, Tyler R. Bell, Mukaila Raji, Sadaf Arefi Milani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionOlder Mexican Americans, a fast-growing segment of the U.S. population, have high rates of pain, depression, and sleep complaints. Data are lacking on how these symptoms overlap and affect cognition in this population. We examined patterns of pain-sleep-depressive symptom clusters and their association with cognitive decline in older Mexican Americans.MethodsWe used data from the Hispanic Established Populations for the Epidemiologic Study of the Elderly (2010-2016), a study of older Mexican Americans in Southwestern U.S. We used latent class analysis to identify clusters based on pain on weight-bearing, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints. We used linear mixed-effects models to examine association between different patterns of symptom clusters and cognitive decline, measured by the Mini-Mental State Examination.ResultsWe identified three latent classes (n=935): pain only (66.5%), pain with depression (21.0%), and pain with depression and sleep complaints (12.5%). The pain with depression class (β=-4.13, p<0.001) and the pain with depression and sleep complaints class (β=-2.17, p<0.001) had significantly lower baseline cognition compared to the pain only class. There were no significant differences in rate of decline across classes. Education was a significant effect modifier: In stratified analyses, among those with zero and one to six years of education, both the pain with depression and pain with depression and sleep complaints classes had significantly lower baseline cognition than the pain-only class. Among those with seven or more years of education, only the pain with depression class showed significantly lower baseline cognition compared to the pain-only class (β=−2.16, p=0.036). There were no significant differences in rate of decline across classes by education.ConclusionsCo-occurrence of pain with depression or/and sleep complaints is a common and potentially modifiable contribution to poor cognition. Our findings highlight the need for targeted interventions addressing co-occurring conditions to mitigate cognitive decline among older Mexican Americans. Educational attainment modified these associations, with higher schooling attenuating baseline cognitive differences across symptom classes, consistent with a passive cognitive reserve framework, and should be considered when designing targeted cognitive monitoring and intervention strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07633-1.

Introduction

Pain is common, affecting over 50% community-dwelling older adults [1]. Pain also rarely occurs alone and often co-occurs with depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances [2]. For instance, among older adults with chronic pain, rates of comorbid major depression can be as high as 50%, and significant sleep problems are reported by 50–80% of this population [2, 3]. These three conditions are among the most prevalent and disabling conditions in later life, each associated with impaired function, and lower quality of life [4]. All three also have independent links to worse cognitive performance, faster cognitive decline, and increased dementia risk [5–7].

Chronic pain in older adults is associated with deficits in attention, memory, and executive function, and with greater risk for cognitive impairment [8–10]. Late-life depressive symptoms consistently predict worse cognitive outcomes, including incident dementia and accelerated decline [5, 9, 11]. Sleep disturbances, including insomnia symptoms, sleep fragmentation, and short and long sleep duration are prospectively associated with cognitive decline and dementia [6, 7]. Bidirectional links between depression, pain, and insomnia have been documented and can produce symptom clusters that are particularly detrimental to brain health, in part by exacerbating symptom severity and functional impairment [12, 13]. Together, the pain-depression-sleep triad are potentially modifiable targets for cognitive health, that interact through shared biological and behavioral pathways and may magnify risk beyond any single symptom considered in isolation [12, 14].

These issues are especially salient for Mexican American older adults, the largest group of Hispanic adults in the U.S [15]. Older Mexican Americans have been under-represented in symptom burden research [16–18] despite experiencing disproportionately high rates of multiple symptom burden, chronic disease, disability, and late-life cognitive impairment [19, 20]. Prior work using data from the Hispanic Established Population for the Epidemiological Study of the Elderly (HEPESE), a longitudinal study of older Mexican American adults, has documented links between depressive symptoms and subsequent cognitive decline [20, 21]. Prior work among older Hispanic populations has also noted that higher pain is associated with cognition, cognitive decline, and incident cognitive impairment, with depressive symptoms implicated in those pathways [22–24]. However, the role of sleep disturbances in cognitive health remains less explored in this population, representing a critical gap given the high prevalence of insomnia symptoms among older Hispanic adults [25]. Examining these associations within the Mexican American population, rather than through between-group comparisons, offers a methodologically distinct and complementary perspective. Within-group analyses are critical for identifying heterogeneity and behavioral processes in underserved populations, enabling the tailoring of interventions in ways that between-group comparisons alone cannot support [26]. In older Mexican Americans, heterogeneity in immigration history, educational attainment, language of acculturation, and cultural health practices may shape the relationships among pain, depression, sleep complaints, and cognition in ways that are not captured by population-level or cross-group estimates. In Mexican American populations, limited schooling due to historical and structural inequities is common, raising the question of whether education modifies the cognitive impact of co-occurring pain, depressive symptoms, and insomnia [19]. Education, a proxy for cognitive reserve, is a strong determinant of cognition in late life, though its relation to cognitive decline rate is nuanced and may vary when symptom burden accumulates [20, 21, 27, 28].

How these symptoms cluster together, the effects of these co-occurring symptoms on cognition, and whether education modifies those associations remain underexplored in Mexican American older adults. The present study addresses this gap by characterizing symptom profiles of pain, depression, and sleep complaints in the HEPESE and evaluating their associations with baseline cognition and subsequent cognitive decline, with attention to modification by educational attainment. We hypothesize that older Mexican Americans with co-occurring pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints will have lower baseline cognitive function and experience faster cognitive decline compared to those with pain alone. We further expect that educational attainment will modify these associations, with weaker adverse effects observed among participants with higher levels of education.

Methods

Study design and population

We used data from the HEPESE, a longitudinal cohort study of older Mexican Americans. The HEPESE study was established in 1993–1994 via a multistage area-probability sample of community-dwelling Mexican Americans aged 65 and older (3050 participants originally) from five Southwestern states (Texas, California, New Mexico, Colorado, and Arizona). The detailed sampling procedures have been described previously [29]. Since its inception, follow-up interviews have been conducted every two to three years. At Wave 5 (2004–2005), the sample was supplemented with a probability sample of Mexican Americans aged 75 years and older (n = 902). All participants provided oral informed consent; procedures were approved by the University of Texas Medical Branch Institutional Review Boards (IRB #92 − 85).

For this analysis, we used data from Wave 7 (n = 1,078; 2010–2011) as the analytical baseline, with follow-up through Wave 8 (2012–2013), and Wave 9 (2016). We conducted the latent class analysis (LCA) on the full sample at Wave 7, as LCA handles missing data using full information maximum likelihood estimations. Supplemental Fig. 1 displays analytical sample selection. For our longitudinal analysis, we required that participants have full information on baseline Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score, pain, depressive symptoms, sleep complaints, and covariates of interest. Our final sample size was 935.

Measures

Exposure: clusters of pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints

To identify distinct symptom profiles, we utilized six dichotomous variables measured at Wave 7, encompassing pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints. Pain was defined as a self-reported ‘yes’ response to experiencing pain on weight-bearing, assessed over a one-month recall period (“In the past month, did you notice any pain or discomfort when you stood or walked?”). While weight-bearing pain in this cohort is most likely musculoskeletal in origin given the high arthritis prevalence, vascular or neuropathic etiologies (e.g., diabetic peripheral neuropathy) may also contribute in some participants, and the single-item measure cannot distinguish between these origins.

Depressive symptoms were measured with the 20-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D; range 0–60, past week). Depression was indicated with a score of ≥ 16 indicating clinically significant symptomatology [30]. Sleep complaints were captured using four self-reported items reflecting sleep complaints domains, difficulty falling asleep, waking up during the night, trouble staying asleep, and waking up feeling tired in the morning. Each sleep complaint was dichotomized as present if the problem occurred on 15 or more (i.e., response options “15 + days”) versus absent (i.e., response options ‘Not at all,’ ‘1–3 days,’ ‘4–7 days’, or “8–14 days”) [31]. This categorization is consistent with the International Classification of Sleep Disorders [32] chronic insomnia definition where symptoms occur at least 3 times per week.

Primary outcome: cognition

The primary outcome was cognitive function, assessed longitudinally at Waves 7, 8, and 9 using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) [33]. The MMSE is a 30-point scale assessing multiple cognitive domains. Scores were treated as a continuous variable in our models, with lower scores indicating poorer cognitive function.

Covariates

Sociodemographic covariates included baseline age (continuous), sex (male/female), years of education, marital status (single, married, widowed) country of birth (US, Mexico), language of interview (English, Spanish), and comorbid conditions (self-reported physician diagnosis of diabetes, hypertension, stroke, arthritis). Education was treated as a continuous variable in the main analysis and categorized for stratified analysis consistent with prior HEPESE work (0, 1–6, 7 + years). These cutpoints were used because older Mexican Americans in the HEPESE cohort experienced markedly limited educational opportunities, with average schooling around 5–6 years and many having no formal education; therefore, the conventional 12-year U.S. threshold would not meaningfully differentiate cognitive reserve in this population [19, 21, 34]. These categories align with established HEPESE methodology and capture meaningful variation in primary schooling attainment. Age, sex, years of education, and country of birth were analyzed as time-stable covariates while marital status, language of interview, and comorbid conditions were analyzed as time-varying covariates, with the potential to change at follow-up waves.

Statistical analysis

LCA was performed on the six baseline indicators to identify symptom profiles. Competing models with two to five classes were compared using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC), entropy, log-likelihood, and clinical interpretability. Based on model fit indices, parsimony, and clinical interpretability, the three-class solution was selected (Supplemental Table 1). Baseline characteristics were compared across classes using chi-square tests or analysis of variance tests, as appropriate.

To examine longitudinal associations, we fit linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts and slopes for time (years since baseline). Models estimated the effect of symptom class on baseline cognition and cognitive decline, with class × time interaction terms assessing differential decline rates. All models adjusted for covariates. Effect modification by education and sex was tested via interaction terms and stratified analyses. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using Stata 19.

Results

Symptom profiles identified by latent class analysis

Latent class analysis identified three distinct symptom profiles. Frequency of symptoms by latent class are shown in Fig. 1. Class 1: Pain Only (n = 622; 66.5%) was the largest group, defined by a moderate probability of pain on weight-bearing (43.9%) but very low probabilities of depressive symptoms (0%) and sleep complaints indicators (ranging from 0.0% for waking up tired to 26.7% for waking up in the night). Class 2: Pain with Depression (n = 196; 21.0%) showed a high probability of pain (60.2%) combined with a very high probability of elevated depressive symptoms (94.9%), while sleep complaints probabilities remained low (trouble falling asleep 4.1%, waking at night 12.2%, waking tired 13.3%). Class 3: Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints (n = 117; 12.5%) represented the most symptomatic group, with the highest probability of pain (82.2%), a moderate probability of depressive symptoms (49.6%), and very high probabilities of multiple sleep complaints symptoms, trouble falling asleep (76.9%), waking at night (87.2%), staying asleep (69.2%) , and waking tired (60.7%).

Sample characteristics by symptom class

At baseline, the mean age was 85.7 years (SD = 3.8), and 64.8% were female. Educational attainment was low, with 15.3% reporting no formal education, 55.3% reporting 1–6 years, and 29.4% reporting ≥ 7 years. Table 1 presents sociodemographic and clinical characteristics stratified by latent symptom class. Compared to Pain Only group, participants in the Pain with Depression group and the Pain, Depression and Sleep Complaints group were more likely to be female and had fewer years of education on average. Those in the Pain with Depression group were more likely to be widowed, born in Mexico, and to complete the interview in Spanish compared to those in the other two groups. They also had lower average years of education and lower baseline MMSE scores. Arthritis prevalence was highest in the Pain, Depression, and Sleep complaints group.

Symptom class and cognitive outcomes

Across Waves 7 to 9, participants contributed 1,798 person-wave observations, an average of 1.9 waves per participant over a six-year follow-up period. In fully adjusted mixed-effects models, baseline cognitive function differed significantly by symptom profile (Table 2; Fig. 2). The Pain with Depression group and the Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints group had lower baseline MMSE scores than the Pain-Only group (β = −4.13, 95% CI= − 5.14, − 3.11 and β = −2.17, 95% CI =− 3.40, − 0.95, respectively; both p < 0.001). These findings indicate that symptom classes exhibited significant baseline differences in cognition, but no significant differences in the overall rate of global cognitive decline were observed across classes (Pain with Depression x Time: β = −0.15, 95% CI: -0.48, 0.17, p = 0.343; Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints x Time: β = 0.05, 95% CI= -0.31, 0.40) p = 0.801). However, we observed a significant interaction between class and educational attainment (p = 0.033) but not between symptom class and sex (p = 0.0481).

Stratified analyses by education

Stratified models revealed that the association between symptom class and cognition varied by educational attainment (Fig. 3; Supplemental Table. 3). Among participants with no formal education (n = 229), both the Pain with Depression group (β = −3.97, 95% CI= − 6.53, − 1.41, p = 0.002) and the Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints group (β = −5.97, 95% CI= − 9.36, − 2.57, p = 0.001) had significantly lower baseline cognition than the pain-only group, with no significant class × time interactions observed (Pain with Depression × Time: β = −0.64, 95% CI =− 1.45, 0.17; Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints × Time: β = 0.07, 95% CI =− 0.86, 1.01). For those with 1–6 years of education (n = 512), both the Pain with Depression group (β = −4.81, 95% CI= − 6.13, − 3.48, p < 0.001) and the Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints group (β = −2.05, 95% CI= − 3.71, − 0.40, p = 0.015) also had significantly lower baseline MMSE compared with the Pain Only group, with no significant class × time interactions in this stratum (Pain with Depression × Time: β = 0.12, 95% CI= − 0.30, 0.54; Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints × Time: β = −0.02, 95% CI= − 0.49, 0.45). In contrast, among participants with ≥ 7 years of education (n = 194), only the Pain with Depression group showed significantly lower baseline cognition than Pain Only group (β = −2.16, 95% CI − 4.17, − 0.14, p = 0.036), whereas the Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints group was not significantly different (β = −0.99, 95% CI= − 3.09, 1.12, p = 0.358). As in the other educational strata, no significant class x time interactions were observed (Pain with Depression x Time: β = −0.58, 95% CI= − 1.20, 0.04; Pain, Depression, and Sleep Complaints x Time: β = 0.16, 95% CI= − 0.49, 0.82), indicating that the rate of cognitive decline did not differ by symptom class even among participants with higher education.

Discussion

This study identified clusters of pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints among older Mexican American adults and tested their association with cognitive decline over six years. This analysis yielded three clinically meaningful symptom profiles: [1] pain only [2], pain with depressive symptoms, and [3] pain with depressive symptoms and sleep complaints. These profiles, which align with established literature on the co-occurrence of depression and pain [2] as well as sleep and pain [12], represent a graded spectrum of symptom burden. This finding is in alignment with the concept of syndemic clustering [35], a framework that has been applied to understand the complex, interacting nature of multimorbidity in aging populations [36, 37]. Baseline cognition was significantly lower for those with pain and depression and those with pain, depression, and sleep complaints compared to the pain-only class, but the groups did not differ in their rates of cognitive decline. Notably, the pain with depression class exhibited a lower mean baseline cognition than the pain, depression, and sleep complaints class. This may reflect the particularly high probability of clinically elevated depressive symptoms in the pain with depression class compared to the pain, depression, and sleep complaints class, as depression has consistently shown strong cross-sectional associations with lower cognitive performance. Our findings on lower baseline cognition are in agreement with prior reports linking late-life depression [5] and sleep complaints [6, 7] to poorer cognitive function, and extend earlier H-EPESE work demonstrating associations between depressive symptoms and cognitive outcomes in this population [20, 21].

Findings for pain are in alignment with community studies where pain predicted worse cognition [8] and with recent Hispanic cohort work showing pain interference as a risk for incident impairment [23]. The absence of slope differences by symptom profile is consistent with a growing body of literature demonstrating baseline associations between pain and lower cognitive performance but inconsistent or null effects on subsequent decline rates [24, 38]. This pattern extends to depression and sleep disturbances: elevated depressive symptoms predict lower baseline cognition without accelerating decline after adjustment [28], while insomnia and fragmented sleep show stronger cross-sectional than prospective links to cognitive impairment when baseline function is controlled [7]. While some studies report that pain predicts faster cognitive decline [10], findings are inconsistent, with several longitudinal studies reporting that pain is associated with lower baseline cognitive performance but not the rate of subsequent decline [24, 38]. Discrepancies may be explained by differences in how pain is measured and conceptualized over time. For instance, high-impact chronic pain has been associated with lower baseline cognition but not with a faster rate of global cognitive decline over six years [24]. In contrast, a study of older Puerto Ricans found that it was the increase in pain interference over four years, rather than baseline pain status, that was associated with concurrent cognitive decline [22]. Our study’s focus on musculoskeletal pain on weight-bearing at baseline, rather than longitudinal changes in high-impact or interfering pain, may explain the lack of an association with the rate of decline. Additionally, the MMSE may have floor effects in the oldest-old, limiting sensitivity to further decline [20]. In older Hispanic cohorts, including HEPESE, pain and related psychosocial stressors may have already exerted much of their cognitive impact earlier in life, resulting primarily in baseline cognitive differences rather than detectable differences in rates of decline in very late life. Moreover, evidence from a recent meta-analysis indicates that follow-up periods shorter than approximately 4.5 years rarely detect pain-related cognitive decline, and associations seen in shorter intervals likely reflect reverse causality rather than true longitudinal change [39].

Educational attainment significantly modified these associations. Consistent with our initial hypothesis, we observed that higher educational attainment was associated with attenuated baseline cognitive differences across symptom classes. Among those with little or no schooling, the symptomatic groups showed lower baseline cognition but no differences in decline over time. Among participants with ≥ 7 years of education, baseline differences were partially attenuated: the pain with depression and sleep complaints class was no longer significantly different from the pain-only class at baseline, while the pain with depression class retained a significant, though smaller, baseline deficit. This pattern is fits within the cognitive reserve framework [27]. Specifically, it aligns with the concept of passive cognitive reserve, which posits that while individuals with higher education have better cognitive performance at baseline, they do not necessarily show a different rate of age-related decline [40]. A recent large-scale meta-analysis strongly supports this view, concluding that the link between education and cognition in aging primarily reflects advantages established early in life rather than protection against decline [41]. This is particularly relevant for the H-EPESE population. Prior work using this cohort has demonstrated that older Mexican Americans cluster into distinct long-term cognitive trajectory groups, and that higher educational attainment is a key predictor of membership in more trajectories characterized by maintained high function or only slight decline [20]. Our finding that higher education attenuates the baseline cognitive gap associated with symptom burden, without accelerating or decelerating subsequent decline, is consistent with this broader pattern: education shapes where individuals begin cognitively, rather than how quickly they change. Prior work has also demonstrated that education buffers the risk of cognitive impairment associated with other life-course factors such as migration history [34], further underscoring its protective role across multiple dimensions of aging.

The clustering of pain, depression, and sleep complaints may reflect shared biological mechanisms, including systemic inflammation, HPA-axis dysregulation, reduced synaptic plasticity, hippocampal atrophy, and impaired sleep-dependent clearance of neurotoxic metabolites [12, 42–44]. The prominence of sleep complaints in the most symptomatic class is noteworthy and may be mechanistically relevant, suggesting this subgroup may be on a distinct biological pathway toward neurodegeneration. This aligns with evidence that short or fragmented sleep is linked to dementia risk, hypothetically by impairing glymphatic clearance of neurotoxic proteins [43] and promoting neuroinflammation [6]. These overlapping biological pathways may account for the disproportionate cognitive vulnerability observed in the most symptomatic class, though the current observational data do not permit mechanistic conclusions. From a clinical perspective, symptom clustering underscores the importance of integrated assessment and treatment. When pain is present in older adults, clinicians should routinely screen for depressive symptoms and sleep disturbance. While causal conclusions cannot be drawn from this observational study, these findings are consistent with the hypothesis that multicomponent interventions targeting pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep complaints jointly, such as combined behavioral activation, cognitive behavioral therapy for sleep disorders, and pain self-management may warrant evaluation in future experimental or clinical research [6, 12]. Another clinical implication is for clinicians to consider use of medication that target two or more of the pain-insomnia-depression symptom clusters. Duloxetine—a serotonin noradrenergic reuptake inhibitor-is an effective treatment for depression, musculoskeletal pain syndrome, and neuropathic pain [45]. Mirtazapine—a noradrenergic antagonists and selective serotonin antagonists—improves depression and insomnia. Such approach also has the added benefit of reducing polypharmacy and overall medication burden and potential adverse drug-drug interaction events [44, 46].

At the population level, culturally tailored strategies are critical. As our findings underscore the importance of screening for and managing clustered symptoms, it is crucial to recognize that Mexican American older adults face unique barriers. These include structural obstacles like language access and insurance gaps, which can amplify the health impact of these symptom clusters [19, 23]. Furthermore, cultural factors significantly influence pain coping and treatment-seeking behaviors. For instance, Mexican Americans may be less likely to report chronic pain or use analgesic medications compared to other groups [47]. This can be partly attributed to cultural beliefs such as stoicism (an emphasis on bearing pain with courage), a fear of addiction to pain medication, and a preference for alternatives like home remedies or religious coping [23, 48]. Given the low awareness of but high interest in non-pharmacological options like physical therapy, culturally sensitive interventions that integrate pain self-management with behavioral health support are essential for reducing disparities in cognitive aging outcomes [23]. A key strength of this study is its within-group analytic design. Rather than positioning Mexican American older adults as a comparison group relative to a majority population, we examined variation in symptom clustering and cognitive outcomes within this community [6, 12]. This approach demonstrates that the pain-depression-sleep cluster does not affect all older Mexican Americans uniformly: educational attainment meaningfully modifies the expression of cognitive vulnerability, a finding that would likely be obscured in cross-group analyses where between-group variance dominates. These within-group insights have direct clinical utility — screening protocols, cognitive monitoring strategies, and treatment prioritization can be tailored to the specific symptom-education risk profiles identified here, rather than applied uniformly across a heterogeneous population.

Limitations

Our findings should be interpreted within the context of limitations. First, the study is subject to selection bias inherent in longitudinal research with very old cohorts. The exclusion of participants with missing information on pain, depressive symptoms, and sleep, likely also excluded those with severe cognitive impairment. We did not assess pain severity, chronicity, or location, which is a common limitation in epidemiological studies. We did not examine how pain-sleep-depression clusters affect engagement in physical and social activities,—factors known to be associated with risk of cognitive impairment [49]; this is an area for future qualitative studies. Additionally, given the advanced age of our sample, we likely have selection bias where those who survived to age 80 were in better health compared to those who did not. This may have led to an underestimation of the association between symptom clusters and cognition. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference and leaves potential for residual unmeasured confounding. Despite these limitations, this study has notable strengths, including the use of a large, longitudinal, community-based sample of oldest-old Mexican Americans. The person-centered analytic approach employed in the current study — specifically, latent class analysis, which classifies individuals based on their patterns of symptom co-occurrence rather than treating each symptom as an independent predictor variable (i.e., a variable-centered approach) — allowed for the identification of clinically meaningful symptom profiles that reflect how multiple symptoms manifest together in real patients.The stratified analysis by educational attainment further provided novel insight into the heterogeneity of these associations and the role of cognitive reserve in this population.

Conclusion

In this cohort of very old Mexican Americans, co-occurring pain and depressive symptoms, with or without sleep complaints were associated with significantly lower cognitive function at baseline, though rates of cognitive did not differ by symptom profiles. Education modified the magnitude of these baseline associations: higher educational attainment was associated with attenuated baseline cognitive differences across symptom classes, consistent with a passive cognitive reserve framework, while those with little or no formal schooling showed the largest baseline deficits. These findings suggest that clustered late life symptoms mark cognitive vulnerability and that reserve dynamics influence its expression. Future work should test integrated interventions for pain, mood, and sleep, with particular attention to education sensitive monitoring strategies, to mitigate cognitive disparities in this rapidly growing and underserved population.
