An Example of Cascade Stomach
Authors: Jesse Fletcher, Justin Howard
Published: 2025

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old woman with a history of appendectomy, hysterectomy, and bilateral inguinal hernia repairs presented with 1.5 years of intermittent dyspepsia and vomiting without change in bowel pattern. Laboratory testing, magnetic resonance elastography, and colonoscopy were largely unremarkable. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy revealed mild reflux esophagitis and a J-shaped stomach with a submucosal band demarcation between the body and fundus (Figure 1). Subsequent X-ray upper gastrointestinal series confirmed the diagnosis of cascade stomach by showing contrast pooling within a posteriorly oriented proximal stomach that had minimal flow while standing supine unless the patient underwent torso flexion (Figure 2). Cascade, or “Cup-and-spill,” stomach is a unique anatomical variant in which the fundus of the stomach is folded dorsally over the body resulting in a pooling of ingested material in the fundus before eventually “cascading” into the body.1,2 Associated upper gastrointestinal symptoms are common, and while there is no current treatment guideline, conservative management is often tried before surgical correction.2–5 This patient was prescribed a proton pump inhibitor and advised to engage in postprandial forward fold stretching and to avoid lying supine for several hours after meals. On follow-up 4 months later, she reported an improvement in her symptoms.
