An extension module for a two-photon microscope enables flexible in vivo imaging and all-optical physiology
Authors: Ryosuke F Takeuchi, Risa Ishida, Riki Kamaguchi, Masatoshi Nishimura, Keigo Tsutsumi, Kei N Ito, Shota Adachi, Keisuke Isobe, Fumitaka Osakada
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryTwo-photon imaging is essential for revealing the structure, function, and interaction of various cell types. However, conventional two-photon microscopes have limitations in sample accessibility and optogenetic manipulation during imaging. Here, we present Ex2p/Ex2pO, an open-source extension module that adds an objective lens rotation axis and one-photon optical stimulation synchronized with the non-imaging intervals of resonant scanning to a two-photon microscope. The Ex2p/Ex2pO can be seamlessly integrated into existing microscopes by simply replacing the standard objective lens assembly without any modifications. It enables imaging of curved structures while maintaining the subject’s natural posture and simultaneous imaging and one-photon stimulation with low crosstalk between stimulation and fluorescent light. We demonstrate one-photon stimulation during two-photon imaging of mice behaving in a virtual reality environment and pluripotent stem cell-derived cortical organoids. Thus, Ex2p/Ex2pO expands the versatility to investigate neural circuit motifs in behaving animals and organoids, enabling all-optical physiology in existing two-photon imaging systems.Subject areas: biological sciences, optical imaging, systems neuroscience

Introduction

The central nervous system comprises distinct regions, each uniquely contributing to cognitive processes and motor outputs. Different cell types in each area organize long-range and local neural circuits and perform computations to produce physiological functions.1,2 Understanding the mechanisms underlying these functions requires the ability to selectively monitor and manipulate specific cell populations, thereby dissecting their roles in neural circuits.3,4,5

Recent advances in genetic and optical methodologies have enabled precise in vivo monitoring and manipulation of specific cell types.6,7,8,9,10 The combination of transgenic animals and viral vectors with advanced imaging and optogenetic techniques provides mechanistic insights into dynamic neural circuit interactions in living organisms.4,11

Two-photon imaging is a powerful method for monitoring the structure and activity of large neuronal populations in behaving animals, offering cell-type-specific neural activity with single-cell and even single-spine resolution.12,13,14,15 However, early commercial two-photon microscopes were limited by their inability to tilt the objective lens, requiring the subject to be tilted instead. This limitation posed challenges, particularly for imaging curved tissues, such as the brain, or for accommodating large animals requiring fluorescence microscopes with sufficient space under the objective lens.16 Even in smaller animals, such as mice and flies, additional space is often required to integrate behavioral setups, including virtual reality (VR) environments.17,18,19 Recently developed multiphoton microscopes are specifically designed to solve these problems, but replacement of existing microscopes is costly. To overcome this limitation at a low cost, we developed Ex2p, a low-cost modular objective assembly that enables curved tissue imaging without tilting the subject while expanding the available space beneath the objective lens.

Another challenge of commonly used microscopes is simultaneous neural imaging and optogenetic manipulation. Optogenetic manipulation of behaving animals during two-photon Ca2+ imaging is a crucial method for investigating circuit interactions among various cell types in the brain. Irreversible modifications to microscopes are required to combine imaging with optogenetic photo-stimulation by adding stimulation light to fluorescence. Some studies have overcome the crosstalk problem by incorporating a customized stimulation pathway prior to the detector.20 However, many commercial two-photon microscopes lack these customization capabilities. Another approach involves using a fiber ferrule at the imaging site, which would lead to spatially off-target stimulation and misalignment across multiple imaging sessions.21 To address this limitation, we developed another version of Ex2p, named Ex2pO, which incorporates a coaxial light input pathway, enabling simultaneous optogenetic neural manipulation and Ca2+ imaging with minimal crosstalk through precise laser modulation timing. These functions are achieved without any permanent modifications to the microscope itself.

In this study, we present a single extension module that addresses these limitations. Our Ex2p module can be integrated into an existing two-photon microscope, enabling flexible imaging of curved samples. In addition, Ex2pO provides coaxial one-photon excitation with simultaneous Ca2+ imaging at high signal-to-noise ratio and minimal crosstalk. The module primarily consists of off-the-shelf components, making assembly straightforward and available for under US $3,000, excluding the light source. This module provides notable advances for neuroscientists investigating neural circuit mechanisms, as well as for biologists studying complex cellular interactions in living systems with precise optical control.

Results

Ex2p expands the optical accessibility of existing microscopes

First, we introduce the mechanical features of Ex2p. The Ex2p mechanical design for imaging neural activity of behaving animals was based on two constraints: expanded space for setting the behavioral rig and tilting the objective lens. The expanded space was achieved by combining two silver mirrors (Figure 1A). It increases the working distance between the objective lens and the main body of the microscope, providing 90.4 mm of space for a behavioral rig or large animals, such as non-human primates. It also houses the optical components required for optogenetic manipulation, which will be discussed later. The objective lens was tilted using hollow rotary stage components positioned between the mirrors (Figures 1A and 1B). The angle of the tilting objective is maintained at ± 0.1° for 2 h (Figures 1B and S2B). This feature enables 180° flexible adjustment of the imaging angle for curved specimens without modifying the subject’s posture.

To demonstrate Ex2p module functionality, we imaged hippocampal neurons in Thy1-GCaMP6f mouse performing a spatial navigation task in a custom VR system coupled to a two-photon microscope with objective lenses tilted at approximately 15° (Figures 1B–1D). In this task, the mouse was trained to lick a water port when reaching a specific landmark on the linear track. We simultaneously recorded activity from 369 active neurons in the right dorsal CA1 region (Figure 1D). We then analyzed the activity of 85 neurons that have a response preference at the position in the treadmill (Figure 1E, correlation coefficient between neural activity on even and odd laps >0.5). The response properties of these neurons are consistent with typical patterns of local activity in dorsal CA1.18 These findings demonstrate that Ex2p coupled with an existing two-photon microscope enables neuronal imaging in head-fixed animals during VR behavior without tilting the subject.

Development of an additional one-photon excitation pathway

To overcome limitations in simultaneous two-photon Ca2+ imaging and photo-stimulation, such as optical crosstalk between imaging signal and stimulation, and difficulties in integrating stimulation light paths into existing microscope setups, we developed an extension module equipped with a fiber input port for delivering stimulation light (Ex2pO, Figures 2A and S1). The Ex2pO design was based on two constraints: (1) incorporating the excitation line to an existing microscope without modifications and (2) minimizing crosstalk between optical stimulation light and fluorescence imaging signals. The excitation line was added by inserting a unit consisting of a narrow-band laser and a filter combined with a notch dichroic mirror placed between the paired silver mirrors. To protect the detectors from potential damage caused by the excitation light, we placed a long-pass blocking filter in front of the line of the detector (Figures 2A–2C). As same as Ex2p, Ex2pO has the capability of stable and flexible imaging to access the tilted sample plane (Figure S1B).

We then tested whether increasing the detector-to-sample distance caused serious degradation in optical performance. Given the optical components’ specifications, fluorescence excitation and fluorescent signal detection quality would not be significantly compromised by the installation of Ex2pO. To validate the optical performance of Ex2pO for two-photon imaging, we first measured the field of view (FOV) of an Ex2pO assembly. In the standard assembly, the maximum scanning area was 800 × 800 μm, while in Ex2pO, it was 765 × 765 μm (Figure S2A). This ∼4% reduction in FOV is due to the altered geometry of the extended light path. This reduction ratio was consistent across all zoom levels, indicating it is a fixed geometric effect rather than scan-angle-dependent vignetting. Subsequently, we measured the transmission rate of a two-photon excitation laser (920 nm) through the Ex2pO. Compared to the standard objective lens assembly, transmission was reduced by approximately 13% both at the center and periphery of the scanning area, indicating that laser power attenuation did not critically impair imaging performance (Figures S2C and S2D). In addition, laser transmittance in the Ex2pO was consistent even as the objective lens tilting angle varied from 0° to 90° (Figure S2B). The image resolution was estimated by measuring the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of 0.2-μm fluorescent beads imaged beneath the cover glass. At the center of the scanning area, the lateral FWHM was approximately 0.8 μm for both standard and Ex2pO assemblies (Figure S2E). In the peripheral region, the lateral FWHM of the Ex2pO assembly increased by approximately 0.1 μm compared to the standard assembly. This performance was maintained at different tilting angles of the objective lens when optical alignment was optimal (Figure S2F). These results demonstrate that the elongation of the optical path caused by Ex2pO preserves resolution with negligible degradation of optical performance compared to a standard objective mount.

To minimize crosstalk between stimulation and imaging, we implemented laser power modulation with precise temporal control.20,22,23,24 The gallium arsenide phosphide (GaAsP) detector used in multiphoton microscopy exhibits high sensitivity for capturing weak fluorescence signals from samples. Therefore, crosstalk between excitation light and fluorescence occurs even when using a band stop filter for the excitation light. To address this issue, we synchronized the peak power timing of the stimulation laser with resonant scanning using a low-cost commercial field programmable gate array-based multifunction instrument with simple circuit modifications (Figures 2D and S3). This programmable circuit enables rapid adjustments of duty cycle, peak power, and stimulation laser timing. The pattern generator settings can be configured through a manufacturer-provided graphical user interface and are designed to generate a low-latency and low-jitter logical sequence for temporally precise optogenetic stimulation. This circuit and the fast-modulation laser enable microsecond-level latency modulation without jitter (Figure 2E). To assess crosstalk levels in actual imaging, we measured the crosstalk ratio through optical stimulation. We performed laser stimulation (wavelength: 638 nm, average laser power: 10.0 mW) and calculated ΔF/F for each pixel under two conditions (synchronized and non-synchronized). In synchronized conditions, the laser was turned on during the resonant scanner’s turnaround period; in the unsynchronized and delayed condition, the laser was turned on during image acquisition (Figure 2F). In the synchronized condition trial, laser stimulation did not affect pixel intensity in the laser-off region (<0.01%). In contrast, under the non-synchronized condition, stimulation increased the ΔF/F of arbitrary X-position by 3.0% ± 1.6% (mean ± SD). These results demonstrate that Ex2pO effectively minimizes crosstalk between imaging and wavelength/time-domain excitation, making it suitable for Ca2+ imaging combined with simultaneous optogenetic manipulation.

Spatial profile of excitation with Ex2pO

Furthermore, we propose that the Ex2pO illumination region can be adjusted to achieve the desired operation according to user requirements. While illumination through a high numerical aperture (NA) objective lens results in more localized and efficient stimulation in the focal plane, it illuminates a larger area in the out-of-focus region. Conversely, illumination through a low-NA objective lens provides uniform illumination along the optical axis. To investigate whether this effect could be achieved in Ex2pO, we imaged the laser spatial profiles along the axial distance using two different back aperture-filling configurations (Figures 2G–2K). Under the overfilled condition, the entire back aperture field was filled with a stimulation laser. The FWHM of the light diameter at the objective lens back aperture was set to 595.9 μm under the underfilled condition. First, we obtained a lateral view of the excited area using fluorescence imaging (Figures 2G and 2I). As expected, in the overfilled configuration, the lateral FWHM of the excitation light was at its minimum at the focal plane and clearly broadened with increasing axial distance from this plane (Figure 2I). In the underfilled configuration (lower NA), a more uniform axial profile was observed. This effect was also observed in agarose gels, with a more “cone-like” excitation pattern under the overfilled conditions (higher NA). In contrast, the excitation band exhibited a more needle-like pattern under the underfilled conditions (lower NA). These results suggest that Ex2pO achieves efficient and targeted illumination to the population of interest to the user through collimation design.

Neural manipulation with Ex2pO

To assess Ex2pO’s capability for precise neural manipulation, we injected the adeno-associated virus vector (AAV) AAV1-Ef1a-ChrimsonR-tdTomato into the anteromedial cortical visual area (AM) of Thy1-GCaMP6f mice, in which excitatory neurons express GCaMP6f throughout the cerebral cortex (Figure 3A). Target sites were identified through wide-field Ca2+ imaging using retinotopic mapping procedures.25 Opsin expression was restricted near the injection site (Figures 3B and 3C). Two-photon imaging of the mice in the VR revealed GCaMP6f- and tdTomato-expressing neurons in the injection site periphery. Brief, repeated laser stimulation (0.1 s duration, 2.5 mW) through Ex2pO elicited reproducible neural responses in AM neurons (Figures 3D–3F). These findings demonstrate that Ex2pO effectively delivers stimulation laser pulses and acquires Ca2+ signals in the living mouse brain.

Furthermore, we tested its utility in human cortical organoids. Neural organoids derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells have emerged as a crucial model system for understanding pathogenesis and drug screening in neurological and psychiatric disease research. However, providing the necessary external inputs for normal development and assessing their effects on organoids are still challenging. Here, to monitor and manipulate neural activity in human cortical organoids, we introduced jGCaMP7f and ChrimsonR into organoids using AAV-DJ-hSyn-jGCaMP7f and AAV-DJ-CAG-ChrimsonR-tdTomato viral vectors (Figure 3G). Neurons were exposed to repeated laser stimulation (0.5 s duration, 638 nm, 10.0 mW). We observed laser-evoked neuronal activity in a subset of neurons within human cortical organoids (Figures 3H–3J). These results demonstrate Ex2pO’s versatility for precise neural manipulation across different biological systems, including animal models and human-derived neural systems, such as neural organoids.

Functional mapping of long-range input effects in the posterior cortex

We tested whether Ex2pO enables mapping the influence of long-range inputs on individual neuronal responses in the cerebral cortex. We developed an AAV construct with a CAG promoter to maximize opsin expression in axonal terminals (AAV9-CAG-ChrimsonR-tdTomato). The virus was stereotaxically injected into the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) of Thy1-GCaMP6f mice. An imaging window was implanted over the posterior cortical region, including the primary visual cortex (V1), posteromedial cortical visual area (PM), and retrosplenial cortex (RSC) (Figure 4A). Imaging regions were identified using wide-field Ca2+ imaging following cranial window implantation. The expression of fluorescent reporter proteins was successfully detected in the regions where ACC neurons send axonal projections (Figures 4B and 4C).

We then performed simultaneous two-photon Ca2+ imaging and one-photon excitation (0.5 s duration, 8.0 mW) of V1, PM, and RSC (Figure 4B). We recorded stimulation responses from 950 regions of interest (ROIs) (from seven sites and from two mice). We observed both excitatory and inhibitory responses in the imaging sites during and after stimulation periods (Figures 4D and 4E). Statistically significant responses were observed in 15.9% of the recorded cells (Figures 4F and 4G). In all cortical regions, the number of excited ROIs exceeded that of inhibited cells. To validate that opsins mediated the observed effects, we performed negative control experiments under identical optical conditions using the same mouse strains but without AAV injection. The effect of optical stimulation on neurons in negative control mice was significantly weaker than in AAV-injected mice (p = 0.025, Levene test). These results indicate that the observed changes in activity were due to ChrimsonR activation rather than nonspecific effects of light stimulation. Thus, Ex2pO enables functional mapping of long-range inputs through simultaneous Ca2+ imaging and optical excitation.

Discussion

In this study, we present Ex2p/Ex2pO, an open-source module enabling flexible imaging and optical manipulation in existing multiphoton microscopes. The optical performance, excitation efficiency, and temporal precision of Ex2pO are suitable for neurophysiological studies in living animals and organoids. It is important to note that most components required to build the module are commercially available, allowing researchers to assemble and implement Ex2pO into their experimental setups without modifying the microscope’s core structure.

Enhancing imaging flexibility by Ex2p

Recent advances in neurophysiology have placed a growing emphasis on investigating the causal relationships between inputs from distinct brain regions during animal behavior. In rodents, for example, motor signals are essential for explaining neural activity, even in early sensory areas.26,27 The presence of prediction error neurons in V1 and A1 implies top-down inputs.28,29 To investigate the circuit motifs underlying predictive coding, sensorimotor integration, and related computations, it is essential to allow animals to move as naturally as possible while maintaining imaging capabilities.18 For this purpose, several commercial microscopes with rotatable objective lenses have been developed. In many of these microscopes, only the optical system near the objective lens rotates (e.g., models from Sutter Instrument and Bruker). However, some microscopes rotate the main body around the sample. While the latter type provides more precise alignment of the objective lens to the sample plane, it requires a large, motorized stage to move the microscope’s main body for control, making it more expensive (e.g., a model from Thorlabs). On the other hand, the Ex2p/Ex2pO adopts the former strategy, which requires some user skill to adjust the angle accurately but offers a more affordable and compact solution.

Furthermore, optogenetic manipulation of specific cell types or brain regions enables causal understanding of neural computations.20,23,24 Ex2pO addresses this need by providing a platform for simultaneous two-photon imaging and one-photon excitation with high spatiotemporal resolution.

Manipulating neurons with a coaxial light source

A major challenge in combining functional imaging with optogenetic manipulation is optical crosstalk. In general, photodetectors used in two-photon imaging, such as photomultiplier tubes (PMTs), have high detection sensitivity, and crosstalk between fluorescence and stimulated light persists even when band-pass filters are employed. The time-domain multiplexing approach using resonant scanning turnaround time has proven to be an effective solution to overcome the crosstalk.20

The wavelength and power of the excitation light are critical parameters for optogenetic manipulation of target neurons. In a typical two-photon microscope equipped with a resonant scanning unit, the turnaround time constitutes approximately 10% of the scan cycle. This time window limits the energy per time for light-sensitive channel protein conduction. To maximize stimulation effects, the excitation light source needs to be modulated with low latency and rapid rise and decay times. In this study, we employed a semiconductor laser diode synchronized with the resonant scanner to achieve high temporal precision. A current limitation of semiconductor laser products is the lack of wavelength range from 530 to 600 nm, commonly used in optogenetics.30,31,32 Acousto-optic modulators with diode-pumped solid-state lasers or high-power LEDs for excitation will address this limitation.

High-NA objective lenses commonly used in two-photon microscopy are excellent for improving axial spatial resolution by achieving high photon density in the focal plane. However, these characteristics are not always optimal for one-photon optogenetics. For example, when there is an axial distance between the cell being imaged and the target cell to be stimulated, the stimulating light profile becomes a crucial consideration. In the cortex, functional columnar architecture, such as orientation columns, barrel columns, and movement-related clusters, is fundamental for understanding cortical function and neural computation.33,34,35,36,37 To investigate these structures and their underlying circuit mechanisms, a high-intensity, laterally confined excitation profile is required. As shown in Figure 2, the underfilling of the stimulation laser generates a needle-like excitation profile, suitable for optogenetic stimulation at different depths. The laser divergence is also a noteworthy parameter,38 and Ex2pO offers extensive divergence configurations due to the short distance between the objective and collimator lenses.

However, the physical geometry of the stimulation light does not fully determine the in vivo effect of stimulation. The final population of neurons affected in vivo depends on a combination of this light profile with other critical factors, including wavelength-dependent light scattering in the tissue and the specific expression pattern of opsins. Therefore, the versatility of Ex2pO in controlling the illumination geometry offers a robust option in investigating circuit motifs underlying various behaviors.

Potential applications with Ex2pO

We have successfully implemented Ex2pO for optogenetic neural manipulation using a red laser (633–638 nm) in conjunction with two-photon microscopy. Notably, the platform can be readily adapted to any microscope requiring imaging and photo-stimulation, or to other optical applications, such as experiments using caged compounds,39 photoconvertible proteins,40,41,42 and tissue ablation.43 Customization is straightforward; users simply replace or design the dichroic mirror, blocking filter, and illumination to suit their specific needs. The versatility of Ex2pO enables a wide range of neurophysiological and biological experiments with precise spatiotemporal optical control.

Conclusion

Both observation and manipulation of cellular activity are essential for understanding biological mechanisms in living animals, organs, and organoid systems. The Ex2p/Ex2pO module provides a cost-effective solution (∼$3,000, excluding light source) for upgrading existing two-photon microscopes. The Ex2p is a simple, cost-effective option for users seeking to incorporate a behavioral setup such as VR. The Ex2pO has the same mechanical features as the Ex2p and is ideal for conducting all-optical physiology experiments. The key advantages include cost-effectiveness, adaptability to curved samples, and low crosstalk. As open-source tools, the Ex2p/Ex2pO will also benefit advanced users seeking to incorporate external light paths in front of the objective lens or expand the space under the microscope to accommodate larger animal species, such as non-human primates. Ex2p/Ex2pO will serve as a versatile tool for a broad range of applications in neuroscience and biological research.

Limitations of the study

Ex2pO does not provide a single cellular-level manipulation method, unlike patterned holographic illumination with a multiphoton laser.44,45,46,47,48 However, when combined with cell-type-specific expression of genetically encoded sensors and actuators via genetic and viral dissection techniques,10 Ex2pO enables simultaneous photoactivation of a broader population of cells extending both laterally and axially. This population-level approach has advantages over highly focused multiphoton point-by-point stimulation49 for manipulating genetically distinct cell populations or assessing the effects of input from specific brain regions through axonal stimulation. Therefore, single- and multiphoton stimulation approaches are complementary, each offering unique advantages depending on the experimental goals. Their appropriate use will vary depending on the phenomena and circuits under investigation.

Ex2p and Ex2pO consists of two silver mirrors and, in the case of Ex2pO, an additional dichroic filter in the standard optical path. These optical elements caused slight signal attenuation, particularly at the peripheral field of view or under scattering condition, due to mirror reflection, fluorescence loss, and the extended optical path distance (approximately 100 mm) from sample to detector (Figures 1, S1, and S2). This trade-off is justified by the added functional advantages, such as flexibility and optical stimulation. The observed attenuation can be readily compensated by minor adjustments in excitation laser power. Importantly, Ex2pO’s optical performance remains robust even in the scattering sample (Figure S2H). Practically, given the high performance of modern genetically encoded fluorescent probes and pulsed lasers, this attenuation does not significantly impact imaging quality. Indeed, using Thy1-GCaMP6f transgenic mice, we successfully imaged neurons distributed in cortical layers 2/3 through 5 at practical resolution with Ex2pO. For deeper-layer imaging, combining red-shifted Ca2+ indicators with blue light-sensitive opsins provides a suitable strategy to obtain sufficient signals while minimizing spectral overlap.50,51,52

Experimental model and study participant details

Animals

All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Nagoya University. All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of mice used in the experiments. Thy1-GCaMP6f (GP5.17) mice were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (JAX025393). Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room (24°C ± 1°C) under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Both male and female mice were used in this study (adult mice aged 20–40 weeks at the time of the imaging session).

Human cortical organoids

Human cortical organoids were generated from the hiPSC lines (clone: 1383D6, derived from an Asian male aged 36 years). Undifferentiated hiPSCs were maintained on iMatrix 511-coated culture plates in StemFit AK02N medium at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Single-cell suspensions of undifferentiated hiPSCs were plated into a low-adherent 96-well V-bottom plate (10,000 cells/well) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM)/Ham’s F12 + KnockOut Serum Replacement (KSR) medium (DMEM/F12, 15% KSR, 1% Minimum Essential Medium Non-Essential Amino Acid [MEM-NEAA], 1% L-glutamine, and 100 μM β-mercaptoethanol) supplemented with 100 nM LDN-193189, 500 nM A-83-01, 2.0 μM IWR-1-endo, and 50 μM Y27632. Half of the medium was replaced with fresh medium every other day. Y27632 was removed on day 4. After 10 days, the organoids were transferred to a spinning culture (70 rpm) in a low-adherent 6-cm dish and maintained in DMEM/F12+Neurobasal medium (1:1 mixture of DMEM/F12 and Neurobasal medium, 0.5% N2 supplement, 1.0% B27 supplement minus vitamin A, 0.5% MEM-NEAA, 1.0% L-glutamine, 0.025% insulin, 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin) supplemented with 20 ng/mL basic fibroblast growth factor (bFGF). The medium was replaced every other day until day 18. From day 18, organoids were maintained in DMEM/F12+Neurobasal medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and 200 μM ascorbic acid, with medium changes every other day thereafter. This protocol was adapted from a previously established method.53

AAVs were used to express jGCaMP7f and ChrimsonR. Cortical organoids (day 101) were transferred to a low-binding 6-well plate containing 3 mL of DMEM/F12+Neurobasal medium and incubated with AAV mixture (10 μL of AAVDJ-hSyn-jGCaMP7f and 10 μL of AAVDJ-CAG-ChrimsonR-tdTomato) overnight at 37°C with 5% CO2. After 10 days of AAV infection (day 111), the cortical organoids were used for two-photon imaging and one-photon optogenetic stimulation.

Method details

AAV production

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was performed with PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase (TaKaRa) on a PCR thermal cycler (Dice Touch, TaKaRa). Plasmids were digested with appropriate restriction enzymes (New England BioLabs) at optimal conditions. Plasmid and PCR fragments were assembled with NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (New England BioLabs). Chemically competent Stbl3 E.coli (Thermo) was transformed with plasmids.54 The respective pAAV genomic plasmid, pHelper, and either pAAV2/9 or pAAV2/DJ were cotransfected into HEK293T cells using PEI-Max for AAV production. AAVs were harvested by a freeze-thaw cycle and purified by centrifugation with a gradient (15%, 25%, 40%, 58%) of iodixanol OptiPrep. AAV genome titers were quantified by qPCR targeting the WPRE sequence. The titers of AAV1-Ef1a-ChrimsonR-tdTomato and AAV9-CAG-ChrimsonR-tdTomato for mouse experiments were 1.97 × 1012 and 5.68 × 1013 vg/mL, respectively. The titers of AAVDJ-hSyn-jGCaMP7f and AAVDJ-CAG-ChrimsonR-tdTomato for human organoid experiments were 1.52 × 1013 and 3.76 × 1011 vg/mL, respectively. Virus aliquots were stored at −80°C until use.

Surgery

A custom-made metallic head plate was implanted onto the skull to ensure stable head fixation (Figure 1). During surgery, the eyes were protected with ofloxacin ointment (0.3%, Toa) to prevent dry eye and inadvertent injury, and body temperature was maintained using a heating pad. After surgery, mice were injected with atipamezole hydrochloride solution (0.75 mg/kg, Meiji Seika) for rapid recovery from the effects of medetomidine hydrochloride. The skull surfaces were covered with transparent dental cement for functional mapping of visual areas. Virus injections into the cortex were performed by pulsed-air pressure to glass pipette. The injection target site (anteromedial cortex) was identified using wide-field transcranial imaging and retinotopic mapping, followed by craniotomy in the posterior medial regions.55,56 For optogenetic stimulation preparation, skull surfaces coated with transparent dental cement were covered with black dental cement to prevent direct light-stimulation to eyes.

Cortical aspiration for hippocampal imaging is performed using a 3.1 mm diameter metallic cannula with glued cover glass. A metal cannula was stereotaxically implanted following careful aspiration of the cortex. A bovine collagen sponge prevented peripheral bleeding.

VR training

Mice aged 11–20 weeks were used in virtual reality (VR) experiments due to their body size compatibility with head-mounted VR. All mice were maintained under standard light-dark cycle. The implanted imaging window is covered with a black aluminum tip and silicone adhesive. Prior to VR habituation, mice were placed in the setup without visual feedback for ∼30 min of acclimation on the first day. Subsequently, animals were habituated to the head-fixed VR through 1–2 h sessions over 5–9 days until they showed consistent locomotion in the VR setup. Each closed-loop VR imaging session began with a 10-min acclimation period.

For the spatial navigation task, water restriction is performed on the subject. During experiments, subjects were water-restricted to 85% of their free-feeding body weight, with food available ad libitum. During the first training phase, water was automatically delivered at a reward landmark position (5 sessions). After mice learned to obtain the reward, it was modified to be triggered by licking at the landmark location. Rewards were granted only once per lap.

Two-photon imaging and one-photon optogenetic stimulation

Two-photon Ca2+ imaging experiments were performed using a Nikon A1R-MP + microscope (Nikon, Japan). Water-immersion objective lenses (CFI75 LWD 16×W, NA0.8 or CFI75 Apo 25×, NA1.1, Nikon) and a femtosecond-pulsed laser (Insight DeepSee+, Spectra-Physics) were used for two-photon imaging.25,55 A 25× objective lens was used for imaging of the human cortical organoid. The fluorescent proteins GCaMP and tdTomato were visualized using 920-nm and 1040-nm wavelength lasers, respectively. During time-lapse imaging, 1040-nm illumination was omitted to prevent ChrimsonR activation.

The objective lens was mounted on a custom-built extension adapter (Ex2pO) to enable one-photon stimulation laser with simultaneous two-photon imaging. The angle of the objective lens was adjusted by the set screw equipped in the rotation mount (CRM1T/M, Thorlabs). The rotation angle is maintained at less than 0.1° during the imaging session by appropriate screw locking (Figures 1B and S1B). The Ex2pO consists of a pair of mirrors to provide excitation laser and fluorescence. A notch dichroic mirror (NFD01-633-25x36, Semrock) was positioned between this pair of mirrors to direct red excitation laser (633 nm, 400-μm multimode fiber coupled laser, Civil laser with underfilled condition for Figures 2E–2G and 3A–3F; 638 nm, single mode fiber coupled laser, LuxX, Omicron with underfilled condition for Figures 3G–3I and 4) to the objective lens. The laser illumination inputs were equipped with bandpass filters (FLH633-5, Thorlabs for 633-nm laser; LD01-640/8–12.5, Semrock for 638-nm laser). A short pass blocking filter (FESH0600) was fitted to a microscope filter cube to protect the microscope’s fluorescence detector. Direct light stimulation to the mice’s eyes is blocked using a 3D-printed, black-coated light cone and dental cement covering the skulls.

Imaging data processing

All raw image data were processed using Fiji and Suite2p software. Fiji is used to visualize anatomical data from epifluorescence and confocal images. Whole-brain images were mirrored for ease of visibility. Suite2p is used to process time-lapse recordings.57 Non-rigid registration is applied to imaging data and used for pixel-based and ROI-based analyses. The cell body and putative apical dendrite were included in the ROI-based analysis. Small and inactive ROIs were excluded from analysis based on manual screening using the Suite2p GUI.

Scanner and stimulation synchronization

The LED and laser modulation signals were generated by gating the duty cycle and timing of TTL pulses using an FPGA-based multifunction board (Analog Discovery 2, Digilent), as shown in Figure S3. The horizontal line signal synchronized to the scanner of the Nikon A1 microscope was used as a trigger to generate modulation signals for photo-stimulation. The microscope’s typical scanning frequency is 7.92 kHz. The LED backlights for visual stimulus presentation in the VR system were synchronized using the same protocol (see Figure S3 for details). External TTL pulses for stimulation events (0.1–1.5 s) were generated by a multifunction data acquisition board (USB-6343, National Instruments) controlled through custom Python code.

The typical rise and fall characteristics of the stimulation lasers were measured using a Si Amplified Photodetector (PDA10A2, Thorlabs). The TTL control for the laser and the detector output signal were recorded using an FPGA-based multifunction digital oscilloscope (Analog Discovery 2, Digilent).

Optical evaluation

A photodiode sensor coupled to a power meter (S120C and PM100A, Thorlabs) measured the laser intensity under the objective. During measurement, the two-photon laser position is fixed using a galvanometer mirror mounted on a microscope.

Point spread functions (PSFs) were measured using 0.2-μm fluorescent yellow-green (505/515) beads (F8811, Thermofisher). The beads were embedded in a melted 1% agarose gel solution and placed in a glass-bottom dish (D11140H, Matsunami). The agarose gel surface was flattened using a cover glass (0.17 μm, Matsunami). The plate was filled with distilled water and carefully removed using tweezers. z stack imaging was performed using a 16× water-immersion objective lens (LWD ×16, NA 0.8, Nikon) and scanned with Galvano mirrors to quantify the PSF. Gaussian fitting was performed on the x-, y-, and z axis profiles for each bead, and the FWHM was estimated. The lateral PSF is defined by the average FWHM of the x and y axes.

To evaluate optical performance in scattering conditions, we prepared tissue-like phantoms, which contain both red (580/605) fluorescent polystyrene beads with a diameter of 2-μm (F8826, Invitrogen) and yellow-green (505/515) fluorescent polystyrene beads with a diameter of 0.2 μm (F8774, Invitrogen). A total of 12 μL of the 2-μm fluorescent bead solution (4.55 × 109 beads/mL) and 5 μL of the 0.2-μm fluorescent bead solution (4.55 × 1010 beads/mL) were mixed with 183-μL low-melting-point agarose gel. The mixture was pipetted onto a glass slide and covered with a cover glass. The phantom contained the 2-μm fluorescent polystyrene beads at a final concentration of 2.7 × 108 beads/mL as scatterers and 0.2-μm fluorescent polystyrene beads at a final concentration of 1.1 × 109 beads/mL as tracers. The scattering coefficient of the tissue like phantom was calculated to be 2.9 mm−1 by using the Mie scattering theory.58 The emission spectrum of the 2-μm red fluorescent beads is well-separated from that of the 0.2-μm yellow-green beads, and our detection pathway includes a 550/88-nm bandpass filter that completely blocks the scatterer’s fluorescence, ensuring no crosstalk contaminates the PSF measurements.

To evaluate the spatial profiles of different filling conditions, we employed two distinct experimental setups. An overfilled light source was provided by combining a 400-μm multimode fiber, a 633-nm laser (Civil laser), and an aspheric lens (f = 20.1 mm, NA = 0.60, ACL2520U, Thorlabs). A fiber-coupled single-mode laser (∼3.5 μm, 638 nm, Omicron LuxX) combined with an achromatic lens (f = 7.5 mm, NA = 0.30, PAF2-A7A, Thorlabs) served as an underfilled light source. Side-view images were captured using a CMOS camera (DMK33UX249, The Imaging Source) equipped with a camera lens (50 mm, F1.4, SAMYANG), a close-up adapter, and a triacetate NIR filter (IR-76, Fujifilm). The side-view imaging was performed using a fluorescent solution comprising 1.0% agarose gel dissolved in water, infused with 0.05-mM Styryl 9M (CAS: 82988-08-7, Sigma-Aldrich) and contained in a cuvette with an 8-mm optical path length. The laser profiles were measured using the same CMOS camera sensor (DMK33UX249, The Imaging Source). Beam profiles at each focal depth were quantified using Gaussian function fitting FWHM measurements.

Measurement of mechanical stability

The mechanical stability of objective lens tilting was measured with acceleration sensor equipped in the microcontroller (Circuit Playground Express, Adafruit). The data is averaged in 1-min time window to reduce the effect of measurement noise.

Laser stimulation profiles

We used light sources and collimation settings to deliver the excitation light with an appropriate spatial profile. For Figures 3G–3I and 4, a narrow excitation area configuration with an illumination diameter of ∼1 mm (FWHM = ∼596 μm at the objective lens back aperture) was used for excitation (f = 7.5 mm lens and single-mode fiber laser, Omicron) to excite a restricted neuronal population. For Figure 3D, a larger stimulation area was employed using an illumination diameter of approximately 16 mm (f = 11.0 mm, CFC11A-A, Thorlabs; 633 nm, 400-μm multimode fiber laser, Civil Laser).

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data processing and statistics

Custom Python scripts were used to process the experimental data. Two-photon calcium imaging data were processed using the Suite2p library. Statistical tests were performed using the SciPy library.59 A one-sided sign rank test detected excited or inhibited populations through excitation.
