Reading about sensations recruits the posterior insula: An intracranial EEG study
Authors: William Dupont, Vincent Dornier, Richard Palluel-Germain, Alexis Robin, Jean-Philippe Lachaux, Philippe Kahane, Lorella Minotti, Juan R Vidal, Marcela Perrone-Bertolotti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHow do we assess the meaning conveyed by words and sentences such as “it burns me” or “I feel cold”? As an alternative to symbolic and abstract semantic representation, embodied cognition suggests mental simulations of bodily experiences through sensorimotor activation. We recorded intracranial neural activity in human patients while they read short sentences related to abstract, action, and sensation semantic categories. Our results revealed an early functional dissociation between anterior and posterior insula: anterior insula showed a non-selective response across all semantic categories, whereas posterior insula presented an early selective response (around 170 ms) for sensation-related only. Furthermore, the intensity of this selective neural response in the posterior insula varied according to the intensity of subjective perceived pain evoked by the sentence. These results provide direct intracranial evidence for body sensation-related mechanisms in language comprehension.Subject areas: Natural sciences, Biological sciences, Neuroscience, Clinical neuroscience

Introduction

Embodied approaches propose that when a concept is evoked (e.g., by reading or hearing a word), the brain reactivates related sensory, motor and contextual experiences. These re-enactments of experiences could be involved, when imagining a sensation, observing an action, or processing a word referring to such action or sensation1,2 (e.g., simulating the act of running when we read the word “run” or feeling pain when reading the word “burn”). If this view of cognition underpins conceptual processing, then sensory and motor cortices should play an early role in language comprehension. In this line of research, a substantial body of evidence—albeit mostly indirect—suggests that the semantic processing of action-related words and sentences engages sensorimotor cortical regions. Much of this evidence stems from studies demonstrating that action word comprehension modulates the motor pathway excitability in transcranial magnetic stimulation studies3,4,5,6,7,8 and the involvement of motor cortex in neuro-imaging studies.9,10,11,12,13 To a lesser extent, using fMRI, few studies have demonstrated that processing pain-related words is associated with neural activity changes within sensory cortices.14,15,16 Nevertheless, this body of evidence remains indirect and is constrained by limited temporal resolution, making it challenging to capture the temporal dynamics of how specific brain regions contribute to meaning construction. To overcome these limitations, intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) in human offers a unique methodological opportunity, providing direct recordings of neural activity with exceptional temporal (1–2 ms) and spatial resolutions (∼3 mm). In addition, iEEG yields a signal-to-noise ratio that is approximately 20–100 times higher than that of non-invasive EEG.17 Importantly, unlike non-invasive EEG—which predominantly reflects the summation of postsynaptic potentials—iEEG enables the direct measurement of high-frequency activity (HFA; 50–150 Hz18,19,20,21,22,23,24,25,26), a proxy of population-level spiking activity and a well-established marker of cognitive processing.19 Finally, iEEG provides the ability to record brain activity from deep brain regions, such as the posterior insula, thereby surpassing the spatial limitations of scalp EEG.

Thus, we recorded direct intracranial HFA in epileptic patients while they read short sentences from different semantic categories: abstract-, action-, and sensation-related. Specifically, we analyzed insular cortices activity—a brain region involved in processing bodily and painful sensations27,28,29,30,31—while patients read sensory-related sentences. We hypothesized that if language comprehension engages mental simulations grounded in bodily experiences, then reading about sensory experiences (e.g., “It burns me”) should involve brain regions responsible of actual bodily sensation, such as the insular cortex. Based on prior research into the integration of bodily and pain-related signals, we further predicted a functional differentiation between the anterior and posterior subdivisions of the insula when reading such sentences. More specifically, the posterior insula has been consistently associated with the early processing of nociceptive and interoceptive signals, while the anterior insula is thought to contribute to higher-order integration, such as the elaboration of emotional and cognitive components of pain and bodily experiences.28,32 This functional segregation provides a framework to distinguish so-called early embodied simulation effects—presumably occurring in the first step of word processing—from later integrative functions involved in reading comprehension. Accordingly, we predicted that reading sensation-related sentences would elicit a selective early increase in HFA within the posterior insula compared to both the baseline and other semantic categories. In contrast, the anterior insula would exhibit more delayed and general HFA responses across all semantic categories, reflecting its domain-independent role in integrative, higher-order processing.

Results

Sixteen patients (see Table 1 in STAR Methods for demographic details) with drug-resistant epilepsy took part in a semantic categorization task. During the task, participants read minimal sentences including a pronoun-verb segment while brain activity was recorded from a total of 3,329 electrode contacts (iEEG) widely distributed across the cortex. Sentences pertained to three semantic categories: abstract (e.g., “I hope”), action (e.g., “I grasp”), and sensation (e.g., “I suffer”). We analyzed HFA (50–150 Hz)—as a proxy of population-level spiking activity and a marker of cognitive processing19—to investigate semantic category-specific brain responses. We focused our analyses on the anterior and posterior insula, which are key regions in interoception and pain processing.27,28 We also evaluated HFA selectivity in functional control regions including: the inferior frontal gyrus in the pars triangularis for language processing,33,34 the ventral occipito-temporal cortex for visual word recognition,35,36,37 and the precentral gyrus (motor cortex) for action-related processing.38,39 Experimental procedure and electrode contact distribution within the insula are illustrated in Figure 1 (the total electrode distribution is available in the supplemental information, see Figure S1). Behavioral performances were assessed by sentence categorization accuracy (±SEM), which was 83.53% ± 4.23% for abstract, 84.02% ± 3.72% for action, and 75.73% ± 3.50% for sensation-related sentences, indicating successful task execution and ensuring participants’ engagement throughout the experiment (see Table S1 for details).

HFA response selectivity and graduation to sensation sentences in the posterior insula

The semantic categorization task revealed singular HFA responses to sensation sentences in the posterior insular cortex in 5 of 6 patients, clearly functionally distinct from its anterior counterpart (Figure 1C). Sensation sentences triggered an early and pronounced increase of HFA in the posterior insula (150–400 ms from word onset, p < 0.05), while no significant responses were detected for action or abstract sentences (p > 0.05). Among these five responsive patients, two exhibited a significant difference of HFA responses between sentence categories, specifically distinguishing sensation from abstract or action sentences (P8 and P16). In contrast, the anterior insula did not elicit specific responses, showing instead a delayed (400–750 ms) and equal HFA increase for all sentence categories, in 13 of 14 patients (p < 0.05). This finding aligns with prior intracranial studies40,41 reporting anterior insula involvement during abstract, symbolic language processing, such as minimal phrase composition. This inter-patient replicability is further corroborated by a group-level analysis, which shows a significant HFA increase in the posterior insula exclusively for sensation sentences (p < 0.05), differing significantly from the two other sentence categories (abstract and action, Figure 1C). Compelling evidence for the stability of the response pattern in the posterior insula emerged from a patient (P15) whose dual recording sessions, separated by 3 months involving no brain tissue resection and a different electrode implantation site within the posterior insula, showed identical HFA selectivity for sensation sentences across both sessions (Figure 1D). This rare within-subject replication—despite different electrode trajectories and a 3-month interval between sessions—supports the robustness of the posterior insula activation during the processing of bodily sensation language, independently of precise electrode placement or prior task exposure.

To confirm the observed effects in the posterior insula, we focused on the 6 responsive recordings from 5 patients and assessed a more sensible measure of this phenomenon through single-trial response concentration across conditions and sequential time intervals. Specifically, we examined the distribution of trials with the lowest and highest HFA amplitudes within each semantic condition, by calculating the difference in proportion of trials falling into the highest (Q4) versus lowest (Q1) response quartiles (ΔQ4-Q1; see STAR Methods). This measure captures the degree to which responses are skewed toward high- or low-amplitude activity, providing a complementary index of semantic selectivity beyond average signal amplitude. A repeated measures ANOVA (timing × condition) revealed that trial concentration was also selectively modulated during sensation sentence reading. More specifically, we found no main effect of timing (F(5, 25) = 1.939, p = 0.123, ηp2 = 0.279) but a main effect of condition (F(2, 10) = 5.489, p = 0.025, ηp2 = 0.523) and a timing by condition interaction (F(10, 50) = 2.997, p = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.375). This interaction was characterized by a concentration difference between the lowest and highest quartiles, observed exclusively in the 156–297 ms interval for sensation sentences (ΔQ4-Q1 = 17.63 ± 3.69), with significant difference when compared to both abstract (ΔQ4-Q1 = −8.31 ± 4.45) and action sentences (ΔQ4-Q1 = −7.32 ± 3.89, Bonferroni corrected p < 0.01, Figure 1C). Coherently, the only significant difference occurred in a time window aligned with the selective HFA response time window (to see values for all timing, see Table S2; Figure S2). Interestingly, no such effect was observed in the anterior insula across all types of semantic categories (false discovery rate corrected p = 0.42). Taken together, these findings suggest that HFA responses in the posterior insula exhibit response selectivity to sensation-related sentences, characterized by a transient increase in both HFA responses and single-trial concentration. Moreover, the striking consistency enhances the reliability and validity of the posterior insula selectivity for sensation-related sentences.

This functional dissociation between the posterior and the anterior insula aligns with existing literature identifying functionally distinct regions in the human insula.27,28,29,30 While the posterior insula has been consistently associated with pain and interoceptive sensation processing,28,29,30,42 the anterior insula has been proposed as a central hub for the multimodal integration.27 Interestingly, our results are in strong agreement with previous research reporting posterior insula implication during actual pain perception.27,28,30 What is particularly compelling is the near-perfect spatial overlap of the posterior insula activations in our study with those reported in the literature for pain perception (mean: ±X = 36, Y = −20, Z = 11; ±X = 34, Y = −20, Z = 1427). This remarkable spatial concordance bolsters the strength and consistency of our findings, demonstrating that pain information, whether caused physically or elicited by a word, could involve the activity of the same neuronal assemblies. It is to be noted that HFA response selectivity was detected in 20% (n = 7 of 35) and 32% (n = 35 of 109) of the posterior and anterior insula sites, respectively, indicating that HFA responses are restricted to a subset of recording sites rather than being distributed across all sites. Although the effects of touch-related words on tactile perception43 and pain-related words on neural activity using fMRI14,15,16 have been previously investigated, our study provides a direct intracranial evidence of posterior insula implication during the processing of sensation-related sentences.

Finally, we explored whether the posterior insula’s HFA selectivity could be influenced by the quality of perceived pain intensity evoked by the different sensation sentences. Therefore, patients rated each sensation-related sentence on a scale from 1 to 5 (1 = no pain, 5 = very intense pain). In 4 of 5 patients, sentences describing moderate to very intense pain elicited significantly stronger HFA modulations in the posterior insula compared to the baseline, while sentences associated with mild or no pain did not (p < 0.05). However, at the group-level, we found a significant HFA increase in posterior insula in response to both types of sensation sentences. Importantly, this HFA increase was significantly greater for sentences perceived as describing moderate to very intense pain compared to those associated with mild or no pain (p < 0.05). This group-level difference aligns with the individual-level findings, which consistently showed an absence of significant modulation for perceived mild or non-painful sentences and a greater increase for sentences perceived as describing moderate to very intense pain. These signals thus also convey information about the subjectively perceived intensity of the sensation-related sentences. Again, the anterior insula did not show such response feature, demonstrating more generalized activity for both very intense and mild pain sentences, emphasizing the functional dissociation between the two subregions (Figure 1E). Interestingly, these results align with prior findings reporting an intriguing correlation between the amount of posterior insula activation and the pain intensity subjectively rated by individuals.42

HFA response selectivity to action sentences inside motor cortices

Figure 2A shows evidence that HFA in the primary motor cortex (M1), located within the precentral gyrus, frequently increases around 150–400 ms when patients read sentences describing hand movements—i.e., action-related sentences (in 5 of 6 patients, 37% responsive electrode contacts, n = 7 of 19). This inter-patient replicability is corroborated by a group-level analysis, which demonstrates a significant HFA increase in M1 exclusively for action sentences, differing significantly from the two other sentence categories (abstract and sensation, p < 0.05). Four of these patients also underwent motor localizer task, which involved actual hand, foot, and face movements. This provided an opportunity to directly compare the HFA responses during real hand movements and during hand action-related sentences. In three of these four patients, we observed that HFA was significantly different from baseline activity, during the real action performance and through action-related sentence reading. These overlapping activations within M1 suggest that reading action sentences can trigger neural activity in the motor cortex similar to those activated during actual physical movement, providing converging evidence consistent with embodied cognition frameworks, where action language comprehension may be closely tied to sensorimotor systems.3,6,11,12 Interestingly, while the main HFA increase in the motor cortex was expectedly associated with action sentences, we also observed that abstract sentences could trigger an HFA increase in some cases (in 2 out of 6 patients). For example, one patient displayed a delayed HFA increase when reading abstract sentences, with activity peaking between 500 and 600 ms, compared to the 300–400 ms window for action sentences. This delayed response might reflect that motor areas may be recruited in different time intervals when processing abstract concepts, hinting at underlying complex mechanisms even for seemingly non-motor-related words. This pattern of results is in line with the view that the motor cortex is not only engaged during direct physical actions but could also be activated during cognitive representations of those actions,38,39 even through language. Moreover, the occasional engagement of M1 during abstract sentence processing re-opens exciting questions about the broader role of motor systems in the comprehension of abstract concepts.44,45,46,47

Generalized HFA responses to all words in functional language regions

Finally, Figures 2B and 2C illustrate generalized HFA in language functional regions: the inferior frontal gyrus (IFG) at the pars triangularis (IFG Tri), associated with language and more specifically related to word semantic processing,33,34 and the ventral occipito-temporal cortex (VOTC), involved in visual word form recognition.35,36,37 HFA increased significantly for all sentence categories within the two language regions. These HFA patterns are consistently observed, with 8 of 9 in IFG Tri (47% responsive electrode contacts, n = 16 of 34) and 6 of 6 patients in VOTC (64% responsive electrode contacts, n = 23 of 36). This inter-patient replicability is further corroborated by a group-level analysis, showing significantly increased HFA for all sentence categories within these two regions (p < 0.05). These findings align with existing literature, which identifies these regions as core components of reading network, as evidenced by neuroimaging studies,35,36,48,49,50 as well as direct electrical stimulation and intracranial recordings.51

Directed interactions between the posterior insula and IFG

We analyzed directed interactions (Granger causality [GC]) between the posterior insula and the IFG in one patient (P11) with electrodes in both regions of interest. The IFG was selected due to its central role in language processing. Within the time window of interest (50–400 ms), we observed a significant directed influence from the posterior insula to IFG for sensation sentences (GC = 7.8 ∗10−4; p < 0.05). No significant effects were found in the two other conditions—abstract and action (p > 0.05).

Discussion

Taken together, these results provide evidence that the human posterior insula is not only involved in the perception of actual physically induced sensation or pain but may also be selectively engaged when processing language describing bodily sensations, such as pain (as supported by fMRI evidence14,15,16). This selective engagement of the posterior insula was consistently supported through various analyses demonstrating selective positive modulation of high frequency activity for sensation-related sentences. Furthermore, the inter- and intra-patient replicability provides compelling insights into the functional dissociation between the anterior and posterior insula. This selective neural engagement occurring during an early time window suggests that the posterior insula may contribute to lexico-semantic processing of bodily sensation-related sentences. In addition, the timing of this neural selectivity—emerging around 150 ms after word onset—closely mirrors those observed during the perception of actual pain in prior intracranial EEG studies,28 in which the posterior insula was identified as the first cortical regions to respond to actual nociceptive input. By receiving substantial projections from the spinothalamic tract, the posterior insula is thought to contribute to the initial stages of subjective experience of pain,52 preceding the emotional and cognitive response mediated by the anterior cingulate cortex, the anterior insula, and the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.53,54 This raises the possibility that during the processing of sensation-related sentences, the brain may simulate sensory and pain experiences, thereby partially reactivating regions typically involved in the processing of actual sensations. Interestingly, this suggests a top-down, cognitively driven activation of a region traditionally associated with bottom-up, sensory input—a reversal of the usual flow of nociceptive information. The engagement of the posterior insula in response to such sentences, along with the functional dissociation between the anterior and posterior parts, provides converging evidence for the embodied nature of language comprehension, whereby accessing the meaning of sensation-related words involves a partial re-experiencing of the corresponding sensory state.

Importantly, the generalized responses observed in linguistic and perceptual regions, alongside specific responses in motor areas for action sentences, underscore the distinct and specialized role of the posterior insula in sensation word processing.

Limitations of the study

The current work presents some limitations, many of which stem from the invasive methodology used in a clinical population. One major limitation is the relatively small sample size (N = 16) combined with the heterogeneity of electrode implantation across patients. Stereoelectroencephalography anatomical implantation varies according to individual clinical needs, making it uncommon to find two patients with comparable implantations. Consequently, a larger cohort would be required to achieve sufficient anatomical coverage of the entire functional network of interest. Another limitation of the present study concerns the possible impact of epilepsy itself or of antiepileptic medication on the results, although several methodological steps were taken to minimize this potential influence (such as discarding electrode contacts exhibiting pathological waveforms). Furthermore, it is important to keep in mind that epilepsy may induce cortical and functional reorganization across widespread networks. Finally, although our results were observed during a reading task involving short written sentences, it remains necessary to replicate the study with more complex or contextually rich sentences, as well as with other modalities such as listening. This would help to confirm the reliability and broader applicability of the observed effects.

Conclusion

The current study offers a direct intracranial evidence converging toward embodiment in language processing, particularly during silent reading of words associated with sensation or painful experiences. Our results open new avenues for understanding how the brain integrates sensory and linguistic information, offering important insights into the neural mechanisms underlying language embodiment. Nonetheless, although these findings are groundbreaking, the limited number of intracranial recordings in the posterior insula warrants further replication in future studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Intracranial recordings were obtained from 16 neurosurgical patients with drug-resistant epilepsy (8 women, mean age: 31, range 22-48) at the Epilepsy Department of the Grenoble Neurological Hospital (CHUGA, Grenoble, France). All patients were stereotactically implanted with multilead EEG depth electrodes to localize seizure sites for subsequent clinical treatment. All patients were French native speakers, and provided an oral informed consent before their inclusion in the present study and signed a non-opposition file in the context of the MAPCOG sEEG study (IdRCB: 2017-A03248-45) as approved by the National French Science Ethical Committee and in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Edinburgh inventory55 was used to assess the laterality of participants (see Table 1 for details). All electrode contact data exhibiting pathological waveforms were discarded from the present study. This was achieved in collaboration with the medical staff, using visual inspection of the recordings and systematically excluding data from any electrode site that was a posteriori found to be located within the seizure onset zone.

Method details

Stimuli and behavioral task

Participants were instructed to perform a Semantic Categorization Task in an oddball paradigm with written sentences presentations. The written minimal sentences used in the study were divided into three different semantic categories, determined by the verb used: abstract, action, and sensation-related verbs. More specifically, we constructed a 90 French sentences list (Table S3): 30 items for the abstract condition (e.g., “I admire”), 30 items for the action condition -that were specifically related to hand actions- (e.g., “I squeeze”), and 30 items for the sensation condition -including verbs describing different bodily and interoceptive feeling (e.g., “I suffer” or “he pinches me”). Psycholinguistic variables (written frequency, number of letters, number of syllables, and spelling neighbors) were controlled using the Lexique.org database56 and statistical analyses showed no difference between the three semantic categories (Table S4).

During the semantic task, participants were presented with written sentences in block sessions. They were instructed to press a keyboard key when the written sentences presented on the screen were from a different semantic category than the one initially indicated at the beginning of each block session. Indeed, sentences from the same semantic category were presented in each block, thus constituting three different types of blocks: four Abstract blocks, four Action blocks and four Sensation blocks. A total of 12 blocks, including 22 trials were presented with a total of 264 trials. Each item was presented two times during the experiment. In each block, volunteers could encounter 15 valid sentences (e.g., action sentences inside action blocks) and 7 distractor trials (e.g., an abstract sentences appearing in the action condition, and inversely). Participants were instructed to detect “distractors” (i.e., sentence that did not correspond to the semantic category of the announced block) by press a key on the keyboard (“c”). Sentences were presented in black on a white background, and in the first-person present tense. However, some sensation-related sentences were presented in third-person to evoke sensations generated by others, and prevent the description of actions performed by patients (e.g., “he pinches me”). All stimuli order was randomized and counterbalanced. Each trial started with a fixation cross (1500ms), followed by a pronoun (500ms), and then the target verb (1500ms, Figure 1A).

Motor localizer task

During the motor localizer task, participants were instructed to continuously perform dynamic finger flexion and extension movements from the onset to the offset of a visual cue presented at the center of the screen. The task comprised two blocks of 15 trials each, with 5 second movement periods alternating with 4.5 second rest periods, allowing the functional localization of sensorimotor regions involved in voluntary hand movements.

The experimental session took place in patients’ hospital rooms. Stimuli were presented to the participant on a 15-inch computer screen at 60 cm viewing distance. Reading task was delivered via the E-prime 3.0 software (Psychology Software Tools, Pittsburgh, PA), which managed the generation of triggers on SEEG signal.

Electrode implantation

Fourteen to nineteen semi-rigid multi-lead electrodes were stereotactically implanted in each patient (SEEG57). The SEEG electrodes had a diameter of .8 mm and, depending on the target structure, consisted of 8-18 contact leads (2 mm wide and 1.5 mm apart,58 DIXI Medical Instruments). The electrode locations selection was entirely based on clinical considerations, without any reference to the current experimental protocol. This approach introduced a significant inter-individual variability in cortical sampling across participants. Each electrode contact was identified using coregistration of pre-MRI and post-CT scan images. MNI coordinates were computed using the SPM toolbox (SPM12, Wellcome Department of Imaging Neuroscience, University College London) and the position were visually displayed using a custom script (Figure S1).

Intracranial recordings

Intracranial EEG recordings were performed using a video-SEEG monitoring system (Micromed), which allowed the simultaneous data recording from 256 depth EEG recording sites. For all patients, data were sampled at 512 Hz with an in-line bandpass filtered from 0.1 to 200 Hz. At the time of acquisition, data are recorded using a reference electrode located in white matter, and each electrode trace is subsequently re-referenced with respect to its direct neighbor (bipolar derivations). This bipolar montage has a number of advantages over common referencing. It helps eliminate signal artifacts common to adjacent electrode contacts (such as the 50 Hz mains artifact or distant physiological artifacts) and achieves a high local specificity by canceling out effects of distant sources that spread equally to both adjacent sites through volume conduction. The spatial resolution achieved by the bipolar SEEG is on the order of 3 mm.57,58,59

Quantification and statistical analysis

Preprocessing

SEEG data preprocessing was conducted via Matlab (The MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) and the Fieldtrip toolbox.60 Data preprocessing consisted of a series of steps previously used by our team.23,24,26 As previously mentioned, we first applied a bipolar set-up by re-referencing each recording site to its direct neighbor. Then, we converted raw data into HFA time-series. Indeed, as proposed by Lachaux et al. (2012), HFA is a reliable physiological marker of neuronal population activity and an index of cognitive processing.19 To do this, we performed the following procedure21,23,24,25: (1) Continuous SEEG signals were first bandpass-filtered into consecutive series of 10 Hz frequency bands using a zero-phase forward and reverse filter (e.g., 10 frequency bands beginning with 50–60 Hz and ending with 140–150 Hz). (2) Then, we computed the envelope using standard Hilbert transform for each bandpass-filtered signal.61 (3) Next, the resulting envelope was downsampled to 64 Hz (i.e., one sample every 15.625 ms). (4) Afterward, for each band this envelope signal (i.e., time-varying amplitude) was divided by its means across the entire recording session and multiplied by 100. This procedure yields instantaneous envelope values expressed in percentage (%) of the mean. (5) The envelope signals (expressed in %) for each successive frequency bands were averaged together to provide one single time series (HFA) across the entire experimentation. (6) Finally, the HFA time-series was epoched into data segments centered on each stimulus (0 to 750 ms after the pronoun-verb pair presentation onset) and baseline corrected (200 ms preceding the beginning of the pronoun-verb pair presentation).

Electrode contacts selection

Electrode contacts located in the posterior and anterior insula, the precentral gyrus (primary motor cortex), the inferior frontal gyrus (pars triangularis), and the fusiform gyrus were selected for analysis. The anatomical and functional locations of these electrodes were confirmed using the Julich62 and the AICHA63 brain atlases, respectively.

Statistical analysis

To ensure that patients performed correctly the semantic decision task, we analyzed the percentage of correct responses. We excluded filler trials from the analysis, and we have selected only the target trials for which participants answered accurately to the semantic decision, to prevent movement and motor contamination during words reading. For instance, within an action condition block, participants were instructed to press a key on the keyboard only when the presented sentence did not correspond to an action condition. However, if a participant made an error and responded to an action sentence, the resulting HFA signal from that otherwise valid trial could be confounded by the motor response. To address this issue, we systematically excluded both filler trials and target trials associated with incorrect responses. Each patient performed correctly the task, with a percentage of correct responses that exceeded the chance level (50%) for each of the experimental conditions (abstract: mean = 83.53%, SEM = 4.23%, action: mean = 84.02%, SEM = 3.72%; sensation: mean = 75.73%, SEM = 3.50%). These percentage of correct responses indicate that patients successfully performed the semantic task (Table S3 for details).

Statistical analyses were conducted with Matlab (The MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA) for the HFA time series (50-150Hz) as previously described. Comparisons were performed separately for each recording site. First, we used nonparametric cluster-based randomization tests.64 This analysis allowed the investigation of the precise onset time and duration of the HFA increases or decreases for each experimental condition (i.e., during abstract, action and sensation sentences, as well as motor localizer task) compared to baseline activity. Second, to compare the activity between the experimental conditions, we used the same nonparametric cluster-based randomization tests. Statistical analyses were first performed at the individual level for each participant, and subsequently at the group level. For the group-level analysis, only electrodes identified as responsive—i.e., showing significant HFA modulation during sentences processing—in the anterior and posterior insula were included for each participant. This approach allowed us to account for both within-subject and between-subject variability. Moreover, to examine whether posterior insula HFA responses were influenced by pain intensity evoked by sensation sentences, Patients rated the pain intensity elicited by each sensation sentence on a scale from 1 to 5 (1 = no pain, 5 = very intense pain). Based on these ratings, the items were divided into two groups: PainMax (sentences describing moderate to very intense pain) and PainMin (sentences evoking no to mild pain). As previously described, nonparametric cluster-based randomization tests were used to compare each condition against baseline activity, and to examine differences between experimental conditions. We used nonparametric cluster-based randomization tests to control for multiple comparisons across time points. This method addresses the problem of inflated Type I error rates by identifying clusters of contiguous time samples where condition differences are consistently significant (p < 0.05), and by computing a cluster-level statistic based on the sum of t-values within each cluster. The significance of the observed clusters was then assessed by comparing a null distribution generated from 1000 random permutations using the Monte-Carlo procedure. The p-value was estimated according to the proportion of the randomization null distribution exceeding the observed cluster-level test statistic.64

Trial concentration

We further analyzed the distribution of trials exhibiting the lowest and highest HFA amplitudes within each semantic condition to gain insights into response variability. The procedure consisted of the following steps: (1) All trials were ranked in ascending order based on their HFA amplitudes and divided into quartiles (Q1 to Q4), with Q1 and Q4 corresponding to the lowest and highest HFA responses, respectively. (2) The percentage of trials in each quartile was computed for each condition (abstract, action and sensation sentences) relative to the total number of trials in that condition (NumberoftrialsinQuartileTotaltrialsincondition X100). (3) To quantify the disparity in trial concentration between the lowest and highest HFA amplitudes, the difference between Q4 and Q1 (ΔQ4-Q1) was calculated for each condition. (4) This analysis was performed across multiple time windows: baseline (-156 ms to -15.6 ms), as well as post-stimulus intervals of 0–141 ms, 156–297 ms, 313–453 ms, and 469–609 ms, and 625–750 ms. (5) Since normality and sphericity were not violated (Shapiro-Wilk and Mauchly tests, respectively), a parametric repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted with Timing (six time windows) and Condition (abstract, action, and sensation sentences) as factors. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni corrections were applied to identify differences between conditions. The data are presented as mean values (± SEM).

Directionality interactions

Granger Causality -GC- was estimated to assess mutual influences between brain regions65,66—specifically between posterior insula and inferior frontal gyrus—across the three verb semantic categories. The analysis was conducted using the Multi-Variate Granger Causality (MVCG) MATLAB Toolbox67 and following the same processing routine as in a previous study.24 Granger Causality was computed separately for each condition across trials within a single time window of interest (50-400 ms). The following steps were applied to the data: detrending, subtraction of the ensemble mean, and first-order differencing. No violations of covariance stationarity were detected. According to the procedure by Barnett and Seth, Granger Causality terms were calculated and statistical significance of differences in directional influence was determined using permutation testing (500 permutations).
