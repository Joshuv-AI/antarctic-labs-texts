Isolated aseptic loosening in total knee arthroplasty: a comprehensive 10-year review of partial vs. total component revisions
Authors: Yu-Chih Lin, Sheng-Hsun Lee, Chih-Hsiang Chang, Yu-Kai Lin, Chih-Chien Hu, Sheng-Hsuan Lin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn total knee arthroplasty (TKA), isolated aseptic loosening (IAL) requires the replacement of prosthetic components, with ongoing debate regarding the effectiveness of partial component revision (PCR) compared to total component revision (TCR). This study aims to compare implant survival and surgical outcomes between PCR and TCR in the context of IAL.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 285 patients who underwent revision TKA for IAL between January 2000 and December 2013. After applying exclusion criteria, 112 patients were included in the analysis—60 undergoing TCR and 52 undergoing PCR.ResultsPCR was associated with shorter operative times and hospital stays compared to TCR, alongside significant differences in the choice of revision prostheses. Although the prosthesis failure rates were comparable between the groups (13.6% for TCR and 18.33% for PCR), significant risk factors for failure were identified, including a canal filling ratio (CFR) below 0.8 and a discrepancy over 0.2 between CFR views. However, no significant differences in overall survivorship were observed between the groups.ConclusionsBoth PCR and TCR provide similar survival rates and clinical outcomes for managing IAL in TKA. PCR provides advantages in terms of surgical efficiency and patient recovery, while reducing the need for more constrained prosthetic solutions. The study identifies CFR as a critical predictor of prosthesis failure, highlighting the importance of detailed preoperative planning and implant selection. These findings contribute valuable insights for improving revision strategies in IAL, enhancing surgical outcomes in TKA.Level of evidenceIII.

Background

Aseptic loosening (AL) is a primary factor for driving the revision of total knee arthroplasty (TKA) [1]. Specific risk factors, such as high body mass index (BMI) [2], malrotation or malalignment of the components, and inconsistent cementing techniques [3], contribute to its occurrence. Knee revision surgeries are inherently challenging and have been associated with increased mortality rates in certain patient subgroups when compared to the general population [4]. In cases of IAL, where one of the components remains securely fixed, PCR could be a viable alternative to TCR. However, the use of PCR versus TCR remains a subject of ongoing debate [5–10]. While some reports highlighting mediocre midterm outcomes with PCR or single-component revision TKA [6], more recent literature presents a positive trend, with studies reporting promising clinical outcome for PCR in specific conditions [7].

Isolated revisions, especially in cases of tibial AL, have shown significant clinical and radiographic improvements [8]. These advances have led to enhanced functional outcomes and increased implant longevity. Partial tibial component revisions have demonstrated superior mid-term outcomes and implant survivorship when compared to TCR [9]. The advantages of isolated revisions are notable, including shorter operative times, greater cost-effectiveness [5], and reduced blood loss [6].

However, performing PCR presents numerous challenges. These challenges range from limited surgical access [11] and the need for more soft tissue releases [12] to potential difficulties in achieving fixation [13] and the risk of joint instability [14]. Current treatment guidelines for isolated component loosening are not uniformly clear, and they are often influenced by factors such as patient demographics, specific diagnoses, surgeon preferences [15], in-depth knowledge of the implant, and the immediate availability of necessary components [16].

A recent investigation demonstrated that isolated component revisions and complete prosthesis overhauls exhibit similar levels of performance concerning implant failure rates, complication incidences, and clinical outcomes after five years of follow-up [14]. The study reported that 9.5% of patients undergoing TCR and 13.5% of those receiving isolated revisions experienced prosthesis failure. Notably, certain ethnic groups were associated with a higher likelihood of undergoing isolated component revisions, with these patients often presenting with poor American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) status, more comorbid conditions, increased instability, and significant bone deficiencies. These factors were correlated with reduced functional improvements compared to TCR. Furthermore, while the study highlighted the potential benefits of PCR for patients with isolated component loosening, it is essential to note that the inclusion criteria also encompassed individuals with both component loosening and significant bone defects. This broader inclusion criterion may have influenced clinical decisions towards performing TCR more frequently.

As a result, the objective of this investigation is to elucidate the efficacy of PCR versus TCR in cases of IAL. In light of the varying perspectives and gaps in the current literature, our study aims to address these complexities by conducting a detailed examination of the effects of PCR and TCR on isolated component loosening. This includes an analysis of complications, implant failure rates, and patient outcomes, with an extensive ten-year follow-up period. Our approach involves a thorough assessment of failure rates over time, the demographic characteristics of the patient population, the surgical techniques utilized, and strategies for managing the patella.

Materials and methods

Patient demographic

This retrospective study assessed joint registry data maintained at a premier tertiary referral center, where over 2,500 primary total joint surgeries are performed annually. The study was approved by the institutional ethics review board, which waived the need for informed consent. Data were extracted from electronic medical records and cross-verified with implant stickers to obtain information on revision TKA (isolated or full components), prosthesis details, constraint types, bone defect presence and management strategies (graft/augment/stem/cone), and postoperative complications. Each patient was preoperatively evaluated for infection (two sets of positive culture before 2011, Musculoskeletal Infection Society criteria after 2011), and postoperative negative tissue culture reports were confirmed.

Surgical technique

This study focused on individuals who underwent revision total knee arthroplasty, using two primary types of knee implants: the NexGen® Legacy Constrained Condylar Knee (LCCK; Zimmer®, Warsaw, Poland) and the United® U2 PSA (Posterior Stabilized Augmentable) Revision Knee system. These implants were chosen for revisions performed between 2000 and 2013, representing the major revision devices utilized during this period, selected for their appropriateness in meeting the varied needs of revision surgery. Patients who experienced IAL following primary TKA were specifically included. Consistent with prior research, significant bone loss was recognized as a risk factor for unsuccessful outcomes in PCR; therefore, individuals with severe bone loss (AORI classification type IIb or III) were excluded.

At the institute, a hybrid fixation approach was employed for revision TKA, utilizing cement for securing the metaphyseal part of the prosthesis and a press-fit technique for the stem. Cases where the all-cement technique was used (cement fixation for all implant parts) were excluded. Additionally, patients with a preoperative or intraoperative diagnosis of loosening in both components were excluded. All surgeries were performed by three senior joint reconstruction surgeons from our institution, each with substantial expertise in performing revision TKAs. This ensured consistency in surgical technique and adherence to standardized protocols throughout the study. Patients were followed up for > 10 years or until failure occurred within the 10-year postoperative period. Of the 154 patients who received revision TKA for IAL, 16 patients with periprosthetic fractures, 10 with knees exhibiting less than 70 degrees of flexion or more than 20 degrees extension deficit, 8 with component malposition, and 8 with patellar instability were excluded from the study. The final cohort included 112 patients, monitored for an average of 81.2 months, with follow-up periods ranging from 62 to 123 months. Thanks to the national healthcare system, no patients were lost to follow-up. The patient selection process is depicted in Fig. 1.

Outcome measures

The rationale for choosing PCR or TCR TKA was documented based on intraoperative findings, including component loosening, compatibility of new and retained components, need for a prosthesis with higher constraint, implant size mismatch, and prosthesis availability from the manufacturer. Stem augmentation decisions were influenced by the severity of bone loss and the stability of the revised component fixation. In cases involving minor bone defects, bone grafts or metal augments were used without stems. Regarding the surgical approach, the medial parapatellar approach was predominantly employed; however, a quadriceps snip or even a V-Y quadricepsplasty was utilized when limited exposure was encountered. The reasons for revision TKA failure were also documented and categorized by group.

Confounding variables

The recorded covariates included age, sex, BMI, operative side, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), ASA physical status classification, surgical approach, preoperative and postoperative range of motion, loose components, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, length of hospital stay, and follow-up duration (Table 1). Contributing factors to isolated aseptic loosening were also recorded (Table 2), and characteristics of the prosthetic device and bone defect management strategies were analyzed (Table 3). The canal filling ratio (CFR) was calculated as the ratio of the extension stem diameter to the medullary canal width on anteroposterior (AP) and lateral radiographs. These measurements were performed independently by three senior joint reconstruction surgeons, each with extensive experience in radiographic analysis. To illustrate the measurement process, an example radiograph with CFR measurement has been provided (see Fig. 2). The higher CFR value, whether on the AP or lateral radiograph, was recorded for each knee.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages. For numerical variables, both parametric (means, 95% confidence intervals [CIs], and standard deviations) and nonparametric measures (medians, interquartile ranges [IQRs], and ranges) were reported. The competing risk for outcome analysis included baseline characteristics, surgical considerations, implant details, and changes in patient-reported outcomes. Univariate analyses were performed to assess the influence of patient demographics, surgical factors, and prosthesis details on complications, using two-sample t-tests, chi-square tests, Fisher’s exact tests, and logistic regression models. The cumulative occurrence of TKA failure was evaluated using proportional subdistribution hazard (PSH) regression models, with mortality as a competing risk. Subsequent multivariable PSH competing-risk regression models and logistic regression models incorporated all variables with p values < 0.10 in the univariate analyses.

Results

Among the 52 patients who underwent TCR (both femoral and tibial components), 13.46% (7 patients) experienced subsequent prosthesis failure, whereas 18.33% (11 patients) of the 60 patients who underwent PCR reported the same issue (Table 4). Failure rates due to AL were 11.67% (7/60) and 5.77% (3/52) in the PCR and TCR groups, respectively, whereas those for other causes were 6.67% (4/60) and 7.69% (4/52), respectively. The two groups demonstrated no significant differences in the reasons for the revision.

The PCR group consisted entirely (100%, 60/60) of revisions with additional stem extensions, with some patients requiring bone grafts or metal augments. Nearly all of the TCR group (88.46%) converted to a semi-constrained prosthesis, while the remaining 11.54% retained their original posterior-stabilized (constrained) prosthesis. In these remaining cases, no additional constraint was required, as the posterior-stabilized prosthesis was sufficient to maintain joint stability. The PCR group predominantly retained the original posterior-stabilized constraint (91.67%), with a significant difference between the groups (p < 0.001) (Table 3).

The failure rates of isolated femoral (8.33%, 2/24) and tibial (13.89%, 5/36) revisions in the PCR group were not statistically significant (p = 0.221). Additionally, no notable differences were found between isolated femoral revision (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.65 (0.66–4.21), p = 0.421) and isolated tibial revisions (HR: 1.32 (0.56–3.11), p = 0.213) regarding prosthesis failure rates.

Key competing risks, following univariate analysis, included quadriceps snip approach, AORI type IIa defect, maximum CFR of < 0.8 for the stem of a loosening component < 2 cm, a > 0.2 difference between the CFR in AP and lateral views, RHK constraint, isolated tibial revision, and isolated femoral revision. Table 5 shows the multivariable risk analyses for time-to-prosthesis-failure. Patients with concomitant “maximum CFR for the stem of a loosening component < 0.8 for < 2 cm” and “the difference between the CFR in the AP view and the lateral view > 0.2” were at a higher risk of prosthesis failure (HR: 5.32 [2.12–9.12], p < 0.001; and HR: 8.21 [3.54–11.32], p < 0.001, respectively).

Discussion

Our results, enhanced by robust sample sizes and advanced statistical methodologies, revealed no significant discrepancy between the isolated and full-component revision groups in terms of complications and prosthesis failure rates. Early time-to-failure was linked to specific metrics: a maximum CFR for the stem of a loosening component < 0.8 for engagement less than 2 cm and a CFR discrepancy > 0.2 between the AP and lateral views. This underscores the need for precision in preoperative measurements and predictions.

Interestingly, our survival curve comparisons mirrored the ambiguity in the current literature, with no marked variance discerned between groups (p = 0.492). For instance, Tracey et al. [17] and Martin et al. [18] reported similar findings in studies focusing on isolated and full-component revision TKAs, although their work concentrated on isolated tibial loosening, limiting the scope and duration of their observations. These results reflect the complex interplay between component fixation and long-term survival in revision TKA.

The practical benefits of isolated revision include reduced operation time (p = 0.003), shorter hospital stay (p = 0.002), and fewer complications. However, while we observed no significant disparity in blood loss across the groups (p = 0.100), Lee et al. [19] reported the advantages of isolated revisions. Our decision to prioritize the operation time over the tourniquet time stems from the diverse surgical practices observed.

The main story here highlights a complex issue: TCR presents a more thorough approach to knee surgery, but it’s not without its problems. These include the risk of losing more bone when taking out an implant that’s firmly in place, longer times spent in surgery, and increased expenses. On the other hand, PCR particularly when we decide to keep the femoral part while replacing the tibial part [20], comes with its own set of challenges. It’s only a good option if the remaining part matches well with the new piece in terms of type and brand, and if it ensures the knee remains stable.

The choice of metaphyseal fixation remains a critical factor in achieving successful outcomes in revision TKA. According to the work of Rossi et al. [21], the use of metaphyseal sleeves improves implant stability, particularly in cases with extensive bone loss, which parallels our findings on the importance of achieving stable fixation. Additionally, Jannelli et al. [22] underscored the risks associated with inadequate metaphyseal engagement, especially applying hybrid fixation, further corroborating our data that early loosening may be predicted by specific radiographic metrics such as the CFR. Meanwhile, the choice of the extension stem in revision TKA remains controversial. Parsley et al. [23] and Gililland et al. [24] proposed different alignment and stability metrics, highlighting the complexity of the issue. Our previous studies indicated that femoral and tibial components may possess different stability thresholds [25]. By examining a cohort of patients with hybrid fixation and limited bony defects, we identified specific risk factors for early loosening.

Our findings highlight a significant tactical shift in the type of constraint used during surgery. We observed a notable move towards using semi-constrained implants in TCR cases (88.46%), compared to the preference for posterior-stabilized types in PCR (91.67%), which were initially constrained type. This difference is statistically significant (p < 0.001). Our findings are consistent with those of Rosso et al. [26] who advocated for mid-level constraints as a balance between stability and bone preservation in revision cases. The ability to adapt the level of constraint in total revisions appears to directly influence the overall success of the revision strategy. This suggests that the decisions made regarding the level of constraint during surgery are crucial and directly affect the revision strategy chosen. In PCR, we were able to keep more of the original constraint because the existing implant limited our options for changing the constraint type. For example, as only part of the prosthesis was replaced, some patients who initially had cruciate-retaining (CR) constraints required conversion to posterior-stabilized (PS) constraints due to the instability caused by IAL. Retaining the original CR constraint was often not feasible, and in cases where collateral ligament stability was compromised, further upgrading to a semi-constrained design was necessary to maintain joint stability. These intraoperative decisions were based on evaluations of ligament integrity and joint balance during surgery. However, in TCR, where both components were removed, the need for greater stability typically necessitated the use of semi-constrained designs. This is a key distinction between the two procedures, as the complete removal of components in TCR often leaves insufficient stability to support a posterior-stabilized constraint. Our data suggest that PCR can yield outcomes comparable to those of TCR under specific conditions and preserved the original constrained as well.

Despite the valuable insights from this study, limitations exist. One key limitation is the follow-up period, which averaged 81.2 months, with a range of 62 to 123 months. This timeframe may be considered relatively short compared to the expected survival of prosthetic components, particularly for assessing long-term outcomes such as prosthesis longevity. While our data offer important insights into early and mid-term failures, longer follow-up studies are necessary to evaluate the true durability of the revision procedures. Second, as a retrospective analysis, patient selection biases may influence the interpretation of our results. Additionally, surgical challenges related to retained components may not have been fully captured, leading to potential underreporting of complications. In some cases, the need to switch to a TCR was because we had to increase the level of constraint from an original implant that was not adequately compensating, especially in a PCR situations.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that isolated revision TKA provides comparable long-term outcomes to full-component revisions, with notable benefits in terms of reduced operative time and hospital stay. The decision to use semi-constrained implants in total revisions requires careful consideration, particularly in cases where higher levels of constraint are necessary to ensure implant stability. A maximum CFR for the stem < 0.8 for engagement less than 2 cm and a variance > 0.2 between the AP and lateral views emerged as predictive risk factors for premature prosthetic failure.
