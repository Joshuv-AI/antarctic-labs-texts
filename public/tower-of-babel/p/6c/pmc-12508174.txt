The effects of the human-like features of generative AI on usage intention and the moderating role of information overload
Authors: Xiyang Li, Tingfa Zhou, Chao Hu, Haibin Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWith the rapid adoption of generative artificial intelligence (GenAI) chatbots on e-commerce platforms, users’ expectations for anthropomorphic service experiences have risen significantly. Despite the growing presence of GenAI, little is known about how different types of anthropomorphic users’ self-efficacy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid through distinct cognitive pathways. Addressing this research gap, this study draws on the elaboration likelihood model (ELM) to develop a comprehensive framework that integrates central and peripheral cues. Using large-scale survey data from e-commerce users and structural equation modeling, the research empirically examines the mediating role of self-efficacy and the moderating effect of information overload. Results indicate that human-like empathy and perceived warmth (peripheral cues) and perceived competence (central cue) all significantly enhance self-efficacy, which in turn positively influences the intention to adopt as a decision aid. Moreover, information overload intensifies the effect of peripheral cues on self-efficacy but has a limited impact on central cues. These findings advance the theoretical understanding of GenAI–human interaction by clarifying the mechanisms through which anthropomorphic features operate, and provide actionable insights for designing user-centric GenAI recommendation services to optimize user experience and encourage the intention to adopt as a decision aid in e-commerce.Keywords: Generative artificial intelligence chatbots, Anthropomorphism, Self-efficacy, Information overload, The intention to adopt as a decision aidSubject terms: Psychology, Human behaviour

Introduction

GenAI, as intelligent question-answering tools, can efficiently identify user inquiries and assist in addressing complex tasks1. Notably, intelligent Q&A systems are often equipped with recommendation functions, enabling personalized content delivery by analyzing users’ past behaviors and preferences, thereby enhancing user experience2. For instance, Amazon utilizes GenAI-powered chatbots to help users filter products, place orders, and resolve queries, significantly streamlining the shopping process and improving user satisfaction3. However, despite the increasing quality and availability of AI services, issues such as information overload, technological anxiety, and concerns about system transparency remain critical barriers to user adoption and continued use4,5.

Emotion infusion is widely regarded as a key to achieving human-like AI, while anthropomorphism is considered an effective strategy to enhance human-computer interaction6. Anthropomorphic features can serve as heuristic cues to support quick user judgments or act via a systematic route to encourage deliberate evaluation of a system’s usefulness and ease of use2. Nevertheless, studies have found that users’ mental models of algorithmic cognition remain underexplored7. Therefore, McKee et al.8 emphasize the need to further examine how the perceived warmth and competence, which are anthropomorphic traits of AI, affect user behavior and decision-making. Cognitive perception and algorithmic information processing are essential in explaining how and why people perceive and react to algorithmic platforms, and how they collaborate with algorithms in content co-development9. Therefore, understanding how both heuristic and systematic anthropomorphic features influence information processing is theoretically important for advancing algorithmic development.

With the widespread adoption of intelligent systems in scenarios such as online shopping, users’ ability to operate these tools has become essential for managing complex and personalized tasks10. In fact, algorithm adoption is a dynamic and proactive psychological process in which users analyze the procedural value and performance of algorithms through information processing. Heuristic and systematic processing are the two primary routes for algorithmic information processing, with learning ability playing a crucial role11. Regarding technological anxiety, Gupta et al.5 suggest it is closely tied to users’ confidence in their abilities. In this context, self-efficacy not only reflects an individual’s belief in their capacity to use intelligent systems, but also plays a critical role in user learning, as well as in shaping their future intention to adopt such systems and their actual behavioral performance12. Therefore, an in-depth investigation of self-efficacy can help reveal the underlying psychological mechanisms behind algorithmic information adoption and support user-centered design in GenAI.

When faced with complex retail product and service search and evaluation tasks, users often experience information overload during online shopping4. In GenAI-driven environments, increased cognitive friction and saturated attention resources make information overload a significant factor affecting user decision-making. Studies have shown that when users are required to process large volumes of data or predict content popularity, they tend to rely on algorithmic evaluation, and “algorithm appreciation” effect becomes more prominent as task difficulty increases2. Therefore, this study argues that information overload not only directly impacts user cognition and decisions but may also moderate the relationship between anthropomorphic features of GenAI and self-efficacy. Accordingly, information overload is introduced as a moderating variable in the research model to explore its regulatory role between GenAI anthropomorphism and users’ self-efficacy, aiming to better understand users’ cognitive mechanisms under complex informational conditions.

Although existing research has explored GenAI services, user experience, and certain anthropomorphic traits, there remains a lack of empirical studies on how these features influence self-efficacy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid through different cognitive processing routes, especially under the moderating influence of information overload. In highly complex and dynamic information environments, the cognitive decision-making mechanisms and psychological processes involved in users’ responses to algorithmic recommendations remain under-theorized and insufficiently examined. Therefore, it is necessary to integrate theoretical and empirical analyses to systematically elucidate these relationships and provide theoretical and practical insights into the human-centered design and user experience optimization of GenAI.

The ELM, as a dual-process theory, provides a valuable theoretical lens for this study by enabling a deeper analysis of how users process various anthropomorphic features and how these processing routes influence their cognitive responses. ELM categorizes heuristic processing as the peripheral route and systematic processing as the central route. This study systematically investigates how anthropomorphic features affect users’ intention to adopt GenAI through both the peripheral and central routes. Furthermore, it explores the mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between anthropomorphic features and adoption intention, as well as the moderating effect of information overload.

This study aims to explore the following research questions:

RQ1:To investigate the impact of anthropomorphic features on self-efficacy.

RQ2:To examine the mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between anthropomorphic features and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

RQ3:To explore the moderating effect of information overload on the relationship between anthropomorphic features and self-efficacy.

RQ4:To assess the impact of self-efficacy on the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

By addressing these questions, this study enriches the theoretical understanding of anthropomorphic design in GenAI and the cognitive mechanisms underlying adoption intention. It provides valuable theoretical and practical insights for human-centered GenAI in understanding human cognition, emotion, and behavior, thereby informing future applications.

Literature review

The elaboration likelihood model

The ELM was first proposed by Petty and Cacioppo13 as an example of a dual-process model. Specifically, when individuals are exposed to persuasive information, they are influenced by two distinct pathways: the central route and the peripheral route14. The central route of persuasion requires recipients to exert cognitive effort in carefully examining the information presented. In contrast, the peripheral route involves minimal cognitive effort, with recipients evaluating information based on simple cues rather than detailed scrutiny14.

If recipients have a high elaboration likelihood (EL), they are likely to carefully scrutinize the information and make judgments about whether the arguments or information support the viewpoint through in-depth analysis and evaluation. Such arguments or information that enable recipients to process information carefully are referred to as central cues, which can facilitate attitude change via the central route. Conversely, if recipients have low EL, or lack sufficient cognitive resources to process information deeply, they may rely more on the emotional content, appearance, or other simpler, more direct cues—known as peripheral cues—which typically influence attitude change through the peripheral route.

Applying the ELM in GenAI recommendation contexts

When users receive algorithmic information, they either actively evaluate it using cognitive resources or simply browse it based on their limited knowledge and understanding2. Therefore, in the recommendation and persuasion process of GenAI chatbots, consumers may engage in deep processing of central cues via the central route, or rely on affective, appearance-related, or other intuitive cues via the peripheral route, thereby shaping their attitudes and decisions. In the context of AI chatbot applications for online shopping, central cues include recommendation methods, credibility, and accuracy15,16, as well as information, intelligence17 and perceived interaction quality6. On the other hand, when users lack motivation or cognitive capacity, persuasion occurs via the peripheral route, requiring less cognitive investment. Previous studies indicate that peripheral cues include perceived inertia, perceived threat, and anticipated regret avoidance6, as well as recommendation choice15 and appeal and design17. These cues influence decision-making by leveraging users’ psychological shortcuts. These findings suggest that anthropomorphic features can enhance users’ emotional resonance and confidence, thereby increasing their intention to adopt GenAI chatbot recommendations as a decision aid.

Therefore, to effectively persuade users to adopt recommendations, it is crucial to investigate specific anthropomorphic features in depth. In the literature on social cognition and psychology, perceived warmth and perceived competence are considered the two fundamental dimensions shaping perceptions of personality18. Perceived warmth encompasses traits such as friendliness, trustworthiness, caring for others, and helpfulness, whereas perceived competence reflects intelligence, strength, skill, and efficiency18. Perceived competence, as a central cue, refers to users’ perception of the professionalism and efficiency exhibited by GenAI when performing explicit tasks19, which requires substantial cognitive effort and deep information processing. Similarly, perceptual intelligence is also a central cue, focusing on GenAI’s ability to process complex information, understand user needs, and provide effective solutions20. In contrast, human-like empathy, as a peripheral cue, reflects the chatbot’s attentiveness to and genuine concern for user needs15, thus shaping users’ intuitive impressions of the recommendations.

In addition, self-efficacy is defined as “an individual’s belief in their ability to successfully use technology systems and make functional decisions when interacting with such systems”21. ChatGPT can enhance individuals’ digital entrepreneurial self-efficacy, thereby strengthening their entrepreneurial intentions. Notably, self-efficacy serves as a key mediator between technology adoption and entrepreneurial intention22. This finding suggests that self-efficacy, as a psychological mechanism, has a significant impact on users’ decisions to adopt emerging intelligent technologies. This study further posits that users with high self-efficacy are more likely to proactively try and adopt new technologies, while those with low self-efficacy may be more conservative, refraining from adopting GenAI chatbot recommendations due to fear or distrust of technology. Moreover, although the anthropomorphic features of GenAI chatbots can enhance consumers’ information filtering and decision-making efficiency, users still frequently experience information overload in online shopping, often feeling confused and stressed by the abundance of product recommendations. This complexity complicates the decision-making process and drives consumers to rely more on psychological shortcuts (i.e., heuristic processing) for quick judgments, rather than making decisions through in-depth logical analysis16. Exploring how information overload moderates the relationship between the anthropomorphic features of GAI chatbots and users’ self-efficacy can help reveal users’ preferences for chatbot anthropomorphism, particularly in terms of tone, emotional responses, and interaction styles under conditions of information overload.

This study aims to enrich the existing ELM by systematically evaluating the effects of GenAI chatbots’ anthropomorphic features (human-like empathy and perceived warmth as peripheral cues; perceived competence and perceptual intelligence as central cues) on users’ self-efficacy and their intention to adopt as a decision aid. Furthermore, information overload is incorporated into the model as a moderating variable in the relationship between anthropomorphic features and self-efficacy. Additionally, self-efficacy serves as a mediating variable between anthropomorphic features and the intention to adopt the system as a decision aid. Research model and hypothesis development

Central cues, peripheral cues, and Self-Efficacy

Previous research on human–computer interaction indicates that enhancing perceived human-like empathy in chatbots during service delivery can improve consumer experience23. Self-efficacy is not a static or uniform concept, but rather a dynamic and context-dependent one24. In educational settings, chatbots play roles similar to teachers, and studies have shown that by providing empathetic feedback, chatbots can positively influence certain dimensions of students’ self-efficacy25. These findings suggest that chatbots with strong human-like empathy can help users feel that their needs are genuinely acknowledged in information-rich persuasive environments, thereby increasing their confidence in effectively using GenAI chatbots for decision support.

H1: There is a positive relationship between human-like empathy and self-efficacy.

Studies have found that when parents create a warm, supportive environment allowing children to act autonomously, make choices, and express their own opinions, it has a positive impact on children’s self-efficacy26. Regarding environmental and emotional benefits, scholars argue that attributes such as perceived warmth can influence individuals’ beliefs about self-efficacy27. Research suggests that teachers should enhance emotional support, which can effectively improve students’ self-efficacy, promote interactive participation, and thereby improve their subjective well-being and academic performance28. These findings indicate that perceived warmth and support play a critical role in enhancing individual self-efficacy. However, no studies have yet proposed similar hypotheses in the context of GenAI chatbot recommendation and persuasion. Therefore, we hypothesize:

H2: There is a positive relationship between perceived warmth and self-efficacy.

Meeting autonomy and competence needs is a predictor of self-directed learning, and the process of self-directed learning is primarily cognitive rather than emotional, as social information can be processed regardless of whether it comes from AI or other people29. AI knowledge is considered a form of computer efficacy, and perceived competence is one of the most important factors in online learning self-efficacy, mainly referring to the learner’s perception of competence tendency and goal achievement, as well as their confidence in achieving online learning goals30. Therefore, this study posits that highly competent GenAI chatbots can meet consumers’ competence needs in challenging service environments and significantly enhance users’ confidence in making effective decisions in complex online shopping environments. Thus, we hypothesize:

H3: There is a positive relationship between perceived competence and self-efficacy.

In the field of education, the AI capability of higher education institutions significantly affects students’ self-efficacy and creativity31. Service-oriented chatbots equipped with a multi-intelligence framework can conduct efficient and precise conversations with users and can learn autonomously based on defined contexts to make appropriate responses or interactions. Such a structure not only motivates users to continue using the services provided by the chatbot but also indicates that perceptual intelligence is positively correlated with attitudes toward chatbot service interaction20. These findings suggest that chatbots with high perceptual intelligence can significantly enhance users’ confidence in making effective decisions in complex online shopping environments by providing effective solutions.

H4: There is a positive relationship between perceptual intelligence and self-efficacy.

Self-Efficacy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid

Empirical research shows that information and communication technology (ICT) self-efficacy is a key predictor of digital competence and subsequent use of digital systems32. This finding indicates that computer self-efficacy not only influences individuals’ acceptance of emerging ICTs but may also play an important role in facilitating or hindering the development of effective skills for interacting with digital systems. Individuals with higher technological self-efficacy have greater confidence in making effective decisions when interacting with automated technologies and are better at controlling the outcomes and processes of technological interactions. This confidence leads to more positive attitudes toward AI systems and a greater tendency to trust these systems’ capabilities and reliability33. Such positive attitudes suggest that the higher the self-efficacy, the more likely individuals are to adopt and rely on these technologies as decision aids. Therefore, it can be inferred:

H5: There is a positive relationship between self-efficacy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

Central and peripheral paths: the mediating effect of Self-Efficacy

Research shows that AI with high emotional mimicry and empathetic care can generate higher arousal and pleasure, thereby enhancing users’ continuous usage intention34. Practitioners in the hospitality and tourism industries should recognize the significant impact of service robots’ enthusiasm and competence on customers’ or tourists’ willingness to use them35. Additionally, studies indicate that incorporating anthropomorphic features into information technology and establishing an integrated model, perceived AI intelligence plays a crucial role in enhancing users’ intention to continue using the technology36.

For anthropomorphic chatbots, self-efficacy helps improve users’ level of cognitive absorption32. Self-efficacy is particularly important in online shopping and purchase decision-making. If consumers believe they cannot effectively use GenAI chatbots to assist in decision-making, developing GenAI chatbots to support consumer decisions becomes meaningless. Individuals with higher technological self-efficacy are generally more confident in their ability to use these “humanized” AI systems effectively for decision support, while those with lower self-efficacy may be skeptical or even refuse to use them. In the context of cross-border e-commerce platforms offering abundant shopping choices, self-efficacy acts as a key mediator between various antecedent variables and consumers’ actual purchase decisions37.

H6: Self-efficacy positively mediates the relationship between human-like empathy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

H7: Self-efficacy positively mediates the relationship between perceived warmth and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

H8: Self-efficacy positively mediates the relationship between perceived competence and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

H9: Self-efficacy positively mediates the relationship between perceptual intelligence and the intention to adopt as a decision aid.

The moderating effect of information overload

When individuals are confronted with a large amount of information, information overload can lead to anxiety, resulting in negative emotions such as fatigue, anger, irritability, and even distrust of the Internet, which may trigger avoidance behaviors38. In customer service scenarios, human-like empathy in AI chatbots can help alleviate consumers’ anxiety39. When users face a large volume of information, if GenAI chatbots exhibit human-like empathy, users are more likely to adopt their recommendations, as such empathy helps reduce consumer anxiety and enhances users’ confidence in making effective decisions in complex online shopping environments.

During service processes, customers experience pleasure resulting from perceived warmth (in relaxed activities) or perceived competence (in challenging activities), which can serve as positive emotional cues40. Such warmth and competence help alleviate consumer anger, thereby increasing users’ confidence in making effective decisions in complex online shopping settings and making them more likely to adopt recommendations. Perceptual intelligence in AI also positively influences customers’ trust in AI, thereby facilitating purchase behaviors through AI41. Users are more inclined to adopt recommendations because this trust helps alleviate online skepticism and enhances users’ confidence in making effective decisions in complex online shopping environments. Therefore, we propose:

H10: The positive relationship between human-like empathy and the intention to adopt as a decision aid will be stronger when information overload is higher.

H11: The positive relationship between perceived warmth and the intention to adopt as a decision aid will be stronger when information overload is higher.

H12: The positive relationship between perceived competence and the intention to adopt as a decision aid will be stronger when information overload is higher.

H13: The positive relationship between perceptual intelligence and the intention to adopt as a decision aid will be stronger when information overload is higher.

Method

Data collection and variable measurement

We collected data through an online survey, recruiting respondents via the Taobao e-commerce platform (Taobao.com). Prior studies have adopted Taobao-based samples to explore how users interact with AI chatbots26. To ensure participant relevance, we included a screening question: “Have you ever used the generative AI shopping assistant Ali Xiaomi on Taobao?” Ali Xiaomi is a GenAI-powered shopping assistant launched by Alibaba Group. After applying this filter, we obtained a total of 306 valid responses. Among them, 52.3% were male and 47.7% were female, with an average age of 33.3 years. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Guizhou University. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their involvement in the study. Participants were informed of the purpose of the study, their rights, and the confidentiality of their responses.

We adapted measurement items from established literature to assess human-like empathy15, perceived warmth and perceived competence42, perceived intelligence20, self-efficacy37, and information overload43. Items measuring the intention to adopt AI chatbot-generated decisions were adapted from15. All items were slightly revised to better fit the context of a generative GenAI chatbot used in online shopping scenarios. All items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree).

Data analysis

Common method bias

To address the threat of common method bias, we conducted thorough pretesting of the questionnaire, involving both expert feedback and pilot administration with potential respondents to ensure clarity and understandability of all items44. We emphasized the importance of honest feedback and explicitly stated on the cover page and throughout the questionnaire that there are no correct answers, encouraging truthful responses. Anonymity of responses was guaranteed to further encourage honesty. Additionally, we performed Harman ’ s single factor test, which revealed that the maximum variance explained by a single factor (24.43%) was well below the 50% threshold, suggesting that common method bias was not a significant issue45.

Structure model test

To ensure the reliability and robustness of the results, we employed the bootstrapping algorithm in Smart-PLS 4.049, running 10,000 iterations to estimate the path coefficients and their significance levels. In line with Cohen’s50 guideline, an R² value above 0.26 is considered Substantial, 0.13 moderate, and 0.02 weak. In our model, the R² value for self-efficacy is 0.567 (adjusted R² = 0.554), indicating a substantial level of explanatory power. For the intention to adopt the AI chatbot as a decision aid, the R² is 0.386 (adjusted R² = 0.384), also suggesting a strong explanatory capacity.

As summarized in Table 2, most hypotheses were supported. Specifically, human-like empathy (β = 0.274, p < 0.001), perceived warmth (β = 0.322, p < 0.001), and perceived competence (β = 0.269, p < 0.001) had significant positive effects on self-efficacy, thus supporting H1, H2, and H3. In contrast, perceptual intelligence had no significant effect on self-efficacy (β = 0.040, p = 0.153), so H4 was not supported.

Furthermore, self-efficacy significantly predicted the intention to adopt the AI chatbot as a decision aid (β = 0.622, p < 0.001), supporting H5. Mediation analyses showed that self-efficacy significantly mediated the effects of human-like empathy (β = 0.171, p < 0.001), perceived warmth (β = 0.167, p < 0.001), and perceived competence (β = 0.200, p < 0.001) on adoption intention, supporting H6, H7, and H8. However, the indirect effect of perceptual intelligence was not significant (β = 0.025, p = 0.154), hence H9 was not supported.

In terms of moderation, the interaction term between information overload and human-like empathy had a significant positive effect on self-efficacy (β = 0.132, p < 0.001), supporting H10. Similarly, information overload significantly moderated the relationship between perceived warmth and self-efficacy (β = 0.129, p < 0.001), supporting H11. However, the moderation effects for perceived competence (β = 0.004, p = 0.432) and perceptual intelligence (β = −0.033, p = 0.072) were not significant, thus H12 and H13 were not supported.

Discussion

Discussion of the results

This study, drawing on the ELM, examines the effects of specific anthropomorphic features as central and peripheral cues on users’ adoption of online recommended advertisements. Among the thirteen hypotheses proposed, H4, H9, H12, and H13 were not supported, while the rest were consistent with the analysis results. Empirical results clearly indicate that human-like empathy, perceived warmth, and perceived competence directly enhance users’ self-efficacy regarding chatbots, supporting H1, H2, and H3. Building on the findings of Chen et al.15, which demonstrate that both central and peripheral cues in the ELM affect customers’ intention to adopt AI recommendations, this study further finds that human-like empathy and perceived warmth, as peripheral cues, as well as perceived competence, as a central cue, all positively enhance self-efficacy. While Sarraf et al.32 highlighted the predictive power of ICT self-efficacy in users’ continued use of digital systems, the present study extends this understanding by demonstrating that self-efficacy also plays a pivotal role in users’ decision-making and adoption of GenAI chatbots, positively influencing adoption and confirming H5.

Shin et al.9 found that when users interact with AI, heuristic processing not only draws on existing knowledge and reduces cognitive load, but also enhances users’ confidence and their ability to analyze information presented by the system. The results of this study are partly consistent with these findings and further indicate that information overload not only facilitates heuristic processing but also enhances the positive effects of peripheral cues (human-like empathy and perceived warmth) on self-efficacy, thus supporting H10 and H11. In addition, Zhi et al.51 found that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between personality traits such as emotional intelligence and technology adoption. Consistent with their findings, this study further demonstrates that self-efficacy also mediates the relationships between personality traits (human-like empathy, perceived warmth, and perceived competence) and the intention to adopt as a decision aid, thus supporting H6, H7, and H8.

This study found that information overload did not strengthen the relationships between perceived competence and perceptual intelligence (as central cues) and the intention to adopt as a decision aid. Moreover, perceptual intelligence does not have a significant positive effect on self-efficacy, and self-efficacy does not mediate the relationships between perceived competence, perceptual intelligence, and the intention to adopt as a decision aid. Therefore, H4, H9, H12, and H13 are not supported. On the surface, it seems that more functionalities in technical systems may have a positive impact, increasing users’ perceptions of usefulness and facilitating adoption. However, in reality, the effect of competence on technology adoption may be both positive and negative. For example, Gieselmann and Sassenberg19 point out that when an AI system is perceived as being too “competent” or exhibiting human-like thinking, users may not increase their willingness to disclose information, but may instead experience the “uncanny valley of mind” effect, leading to discomfort and alienation. Furthermore, research has shown that overly complex or intelligent systems lacking sufficient explainability and transparency can make it difficult for users to understand the AI’s internal decision logic, further affecting their trust and adoption52. Therefore, simply increasing GenAI intelligence and competence may not directly enhance users’ self-efficacy and intention to adopt; improving algorithm explainability and transparency is also an important prerequisite for promoting user acceptance of GenAI.

Theoretical implications

This study makes several theoretical contributions to the field of GenAI-driven recommendation systems and user decision-making behavior. These findings provide a solid theoretical foundation for future academic research and intelligent system design optimization.

First, based on the ELM, this study systematically distinguishes between central cues (perceived competence and perceptual intelligence) and peripheral cues (perceived warmth and human-like empathy) among anthropomorphic features, and empirically examines their differentiated effects in the process of user adoption of recommended advertisements. Building on the findings of Chi et al.53, which examined the effects of consistency between service robots’ competence and warmth on user attachment and recommendation intention, this study further distinguishes the independent effects of competence and warmth. By mapping distinct anthropomorphic features onto the dual-process framework of the ELM, it demonstrates that both central and peripheral cues can enhance users’ self-efficacy, thereby shaping their intention to adopt GenAI chatbots. McKee et al.8 emphasized that perceived enthusiasm and competence are central to users’ overall impressions of AI systems. This study builds on their work by clarifying how these impressions translate into psychological mechanisms. Specifically, it explains that these impressions influence adoption intention through self-efficacy, which reflects users’ confidence in understanding and using GenAI. Furthermore, based on Zhang et al.16, this study extends the applicability and importance of peripheral cues and heuristic processing models in human–GenAI interaction by exploring various anthropomorphic features.

Second, this study deeply reveals the mediating role of self-efficacy. Empirical results indicate that self-efficacy significantly mediates the effects of perceived warmth, perceived competence, and human-like empathy on users’ intention to adopt as a decision aid, but the mediating effect for perceptual intelligence is not significant. This finding not only echoes Bui and Duong22 regarding the mediating role of self-efficacy in technology adoption, but also further demonstrates that not all anthropomorphic features can effectively enhance user confidence and positive behavior in complex AI environments. Building on Zhang et al.16, who explored the intention to adopt AI chatbots through the ELM, this study shifts the focus from technological trust to self-efficacy and systematically examines the role of self-efficacy in user decision persuasion mechanisms, deepening our understanding of the underlying mechanisms affecting adoption intentions in technological mediation contexts and highlighting the theoretical significance of self-efficacy as a key psychological variable. In addition, this study enriches Shin’s2 theoretical framework of user learning ability in algorithm adoption by introducing self-efficacy and provides a new theoretical perspective and supplement for understanding the psychological mechanisms of user adoption of AI systems.

Third, this study further enriches the theoretical understanding of information overload as a moderating variable. The results show that information overload significantly enhances the positive effects of certain peripheral cues, such as perceived warmth and human-like empathy, on self-efficacy. However, its moderating effects on perceived competence and perceived intelligence are not significant. Although previous literature has primarily emphasized the negative consequences of information overload, including cognitive dissonance, attention distraction, and usage interruption54,55, this study builds on Shin’s2 work on user–algorithm interaction, cognitive load, and user-centered GenAI experiences to highlight its potential benefits. It reveals that under certain conditions, information overload can activate specific anthropomorphic cues as heuristic shortcuts, thereby enhancing users’ self-efficacy and offering theoretical support for the design of anthropomorphic GenAI systems in complex information environments.

Practical implications

This study provides important practical guidance for the design and development of GenAI chatbots in the e-commerce sector. First, it emphasizes that developing effective user-centric algorithmic services depends on understanding users’ cognitive processes. Different cognitive processes correspond to different user needs for anthropomorphic features. Developers should flexibly balance and integrate two types of features: those focused on cognitive processing (such as demonstrating competence and wisdom through accurate responses to technical questions and data-driven professional advice), and those emphasizing intuitive perception (such as conveying human-like empathy through friendly language, warm expressions, and supportive interaction). Only by fully understanding and responding to users’ actual needs and precisely adjusting these elements can the human–GenAI dialogue experience be optimized, user confidence strengthened, and system engagement improved. In the e-commerce context, such anthropomorphic design not only enhances users’ confidence in decision-making but also significantly promotes their intention to adopt GenAI recommendations as a decision aid.

Second, the study finds that enhancing users’ confidence in their own ability to use chatbots, or self-efficacy, is key to encouraging adoption. It is worth noting that increasing system intelligence and complexity does not necessarily lead to greater user acceptance. Users are more likely to actively adopt a system only when they perceive it as easy to understand, operate, and master. Therefore, in practical development, developers should avoid blindly pursuing technological complexity and instead prioritize emotional design. By incorporating anthropomorphic features such as warmth and human-like empathy through friendly language, positive feedback, and empathetic interaction, user confidence and comfort in using GenAI chatbots can be significantly enhanced. This, in turn, strengthens their intention to adopt chatbots as a decision aid.

Furthermore, in the face of information overload, developers need to understand users’ cognitive load and psychological needs. Intuitive interface design, clear information structures, and emotionally supportive interactions such as warm prompts and timely affirmations can effectively reduce user anxiety and enhance self-efficacy, particularly for non-expert users. Understanding human cognition is a central objective of human-centered GenAI because it enables systems to align with the ways users perceive, reason, and make decisions. However, this focus on cognition alone is not sufficient. As AI systems increasingly simulate aspects of human intelligence and behavior, it is equally important to address the ethical implications of these capabilities. Transparency, explainability, and accountability, together with the ability to ensure that outputs are perceived as fair and trustworthy, must be integrated into the design process. In the absence of such safeguards, particularly under conditions of information overload, users may feel overwhelmed or uncertain, which can ultimately hinder their intention to adopt GenAI systems as reliable decision aids.

Limitations and future directions

Compared with previous extended ELM, this research framework provides a holistic understanding based on the specific characteristics of online shopping scenarios and chatbot anthropomorphism. However, there are some limitations to the current study that need to be recognized. First, there are various GAI tools (some paid and some free) on the Internet, which are used for different purposes4. It is recommended that future research can distinguish between paid and free GAI tools. In addition, the relationship between antecedent factors and behavioral intention to use smart services is also moderated by gender, age, and usage experience56. Future research can further explore the impact of these moderating variables, especially analyzing the differences between different gender, age, and usage experience groups. Finally, the two main information types, visual information and text information, determine how consumers obtain relevant information from most online product displays, so future research needs to further distinguish between these two information types.
