Optimization of ultrasonic-assisted extraction of total saponins from Semen Ziziphi Spinosae and evaluation of their antioxidant activity and pH stability
Authors: Xuxu Zhang, Yuxian Shi, Jue Wang, Shuying Huo, Yuqing Yang, Xuejing Wang, Yurong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to optimize the ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE) process for Semen Ziziphi Spinosae total saponin (SZS-TS) from defatted Semen Ziziphi Spinosae powder and to assess the antioxidant activity and pH stability of the SZS-TS obtained under these conditions. Single-factor experiments, along with a four-factor, three-level Box-Behnken response surface methodology, were utilized to optimize the UAE process parameters. The optimal extraction conditions included an ultrasonic temperature of 50℃, an ethanol concentration of 70%, a solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), an ultrasonic power of 360 W, and an ultrasonic time of 50 min, with an average SZS-TS extraction yield of 2.719%. Compared with the traditional water decoction method (WDM), the optimized UAE significantly increased (P < 0.001) the yields of SZS-TS and jujuboside A by 137.88% and 161.15%, respectively, while no significant difference was observed in the yield of jujuboside B (P > 0.05). In vitro antioxidant assays showed that SZS-TS exhibited concentration-dependent scavenging effects against ABTS+·, DPPH·, ·OH, and O2⁻· free radicals, with the scavenging rates of 83.30%, 75.18%, 57.20%, and 13.00% at the maximum tested concentration of 2.5 mg/mL, respectively, but its antioxidant activity was lower than that of vitamin C at the same mass concentration. In vivo experiments utilizing a diquat-induced oxidative stress model in broilers confirmed the antioxidant effect of SZS-TS, which dose-dependently increased serum superoxide dismutase (SOD) and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) activities, and enhanced total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC). Furthermore, pH stability tests indicated that SZS-TS exhibited the highest stability under weak acidic conditions (pH 5.77), with a total retention rate of 85.51% after 12 d of storage at 4°C. This study provides a scalable, green extraction strategy for SZS-TS, laying a theoretical foundation for its industrial applications in functional foods and pharmaceuticals.

Introduction

Semen Ziziphi Spinosae, the dried mature seeds of Ziziphus jujuba var. spinosa (Rhamnaceae), is a classic Chinese medicinal and edible herb [1]. Its major bioactive constituents include saponins, flavonoids, alkaloids, and proteins [2], [3], which collectively contribute to multiple pharmacological effects, including sedation, anxiolysis, antidepressant activity, and cardiovascular protection [4]. In turn, these versatile biological properties make Semen Ziziphi Spinosae highly valuable in the fields of functional foods, health products, and pharmaceuticals [5], [6], [7], [8]. Among these, Semen Ziziphi Spinosae total saponins (SZS-TS) are the key active components responsible for its therapeutic efficacy. However, improving the extraction yield of SZS-TS while retaining its bioactivity remains a critical challenge in current research and application [7], [8], [9]. Various extraction methods have been reported for Semen Ziziphi Spinosae. The traditional decoction method, owing to the high extraction temperature, tends to degrade saponin components, resulting in a low yield. The heat reflux extraction method, although widely used, suffers from drawbacks such as high solvent consumption and prolonged processing time [10]. Novel techniques such as microwave-assisted and enzyme-assisted extraction offer advantages, including time and energy savings; however, they face challenges, including high equipment costs and difficulties in precise control of extraction conditions [11], [12].

In contrast, ultrasonic-assisted extraction (UAE) has emerged as a promising alternative technology for extracting natural products. Leveraging the cavitation effect of ultrasound, this technique generates microjets and shock waves that can specifically disrupt the cellulose and hemicellulose networks in plant cell walls, reduce mass transfer resistance, and promote the penetration of extraction solvents into plant matrices and the dissolution of target compounds [13], [14]. Notably, the UAE experiences relatively mild temperatures, which help avoid structural damage to heat-sensitive components from thermal stress, thereby retaining their biological activity [6]. Owing to these core advantages of high efficiency and mild operating conditions, UAE has been widely applied in the extraction of various plant-derived bioactive compounds [14], [15]. It therefore remains necessary to verify its feasibility for SZS-TS extraction and to improve the retention of the biological activity of SZS-TS through the optimization of the extraction process parameters [7].

Accumulating evidence shows that the pharmacological effects of SZS-TS are partly due to its antioxidant activity [16]. Imbalanced antioxidant systems produce excess free radicals, damaging biomacromolecules and causing cell injury and disease [6]. Moreover, SZS-TS is widely used in functional foods, health products, and pharmaceuticals. Product matrix pH varies greatly and is frequently manually adjusted. Thus, determining the acid-base stability of SZS-TS is essential for excipient screening, process optimization, preventing saponin degradation, and ensuring product quality.

To fill the aforementioned research gaps, this study aimed to optimize the UAE process of SZS-TS with defatted Semen Ziziphi Spinosae powder (d-SZS powder) as the raw material. The extraction yield of SZS-TS and the contents of key active components (jujuboside A and jujuboside B) were compared with those obtained by the traditional WDM. Furthermore, the antioxidant activity of SZS-TS extracted under optimal conditions was comprehensively evaluated, including in vitro assessments of its free radical scavenging capacity against ABTS+·, DPPH·, hydroxyl radical (·OH), and superoxide anion radical (O2⁻·), as well as in vivo assessments of antioxidant activity in a diquat-induced oxidative stress broiler model. Additionally, the acid-base stability of SZS-TS was systematically investigated. Thus, this study seeks to provide valuable theoretical and experimental support for the industrial-scale extraction, exploration of its biological activity, and large-scale practical application of SZS-TS.

Materials and methods

Reagents and instruments

Reference standards: oleanolic acid (used as the reference standard for the determination of total saponins, purity 99.15%) was purchased from North Weiye Metrology Group Co., Ltd. (Xinyang, China). Jujuboside A and jujuboside B (purity 99.15%) were supplied by Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Reagents for the determination of in vitro antioxidant activity: vanillin (purity 99.00%), 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), and 2,2′-azinobis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS, purity 98%) were all sourced from Shandong Keyuan Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Heze, China).

Kits for the determination of in vivo antioxidant capacity: superoxide dismutase (SOD) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) assay kits were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). The glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) assay kit was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemical reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified.

An ultrasonic processor (QSonica Q500, QSonica LLC, Newtown, CT, USA) was employed. An Agilent 1100 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an EcoPak™ C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) was used to analyze the samples.

Pretreatment: preparation of d-SZS powder

Spine date seeds (Semen Ziziphi Spinosae collected from Xingtai, Hebei Province) were authenticated as the dried ripe seeds of Ziziphus jujuba Mill. var. spinosa (Bunge) Hu ex H.F. Chou (Rhamnaceae). The cleaned material was crushed and sieved with an 80-mesh sieve, then degreased by reflux extraction with petroleum ether twice, for 2 h each time. After filtration, the residue was vacuum-dried at 60°C to a constant weight, reground, and re-sieved with the same sieve. The resulting d-SZS powder was stored at 4°C in a sealed container and used as the raw material for the extraction of SZS-TS.

Optimization of ultrasonic extraction process for SZS-TS

Extraction process of SZS-TS

The experiment was performed according to the method of reference [14] with minor modifications. The detailed procedures were as follows: 5.0 g of d-SZS powder was soaked in an ethanol solution for 10 h, then subjected to UAE. This extraction step was repeated three times. For each experimental condition, triplicate extractions were performed, and the filtrates were combined for subsequent concentration and purification to ensure the accuracy and repeatability of the extraction yield determination. The mixture was filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane, and the filtrate was collected and concentrated to 5 mL at low temperature. For long-term storage, the concentrated filtrate was further freeze-dried to obtain the extract paste. The crude extract was redissolved in an appropriate volume of distilled water and extracted with water-saturated n-butanol at a 1:1 vol ratio. After vigorous shaking, the mixture was allowed to stand for 30 min to achieve phase separation. The aqueous layer was re-extracted twice, and all n-butanol fractions were pooled. The combined n-butanol phase was evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure to obtain Semen Ziziphi Spinosae total saponins.

Fixed ultrasonic parameters included: 20 kHz ultrasonic frequency, a 12 mm-diameter titanium alloy probe, pulsed ultrasound mode (50% duty cycle, 5 s on/5 s off), 5 cm liquid depth in the extraction system, and 3 cm probe immersion depth.

Single-factor experimental design

The effects of five factors on the extraction yield of SZS-TS were investigated, namely ethanol concentration (50%, 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%), solid–liquid ratio (1:10, 1:15, 1:20, 1:25, 1:30 w/v), ultrasonic power (120, 200, 280, 360, 440 W), ultrasonic time (30, 40, 50, 60, 70 min), and ultrasonic temperature (30, 40, 50, 60, 70°C). For each single-factor experiment, only one factor was varied while the others were kept at a constant level: ethanol concentration 70%, solid–liquid ratio 1:20 (w/v), ultrasonic temperature 40°C, ultrasonic time 50 min, and ultrasonic power 280 W. All single-factor experiments were performed in triplicate.

Experimental design for optimal conditions

Based on the results of the single-factor experiment, response surface methodology (RSM) with Box-Behnken design (BBD) was employed to optimize the extraction conditions using Design-Expert 8.0.6 software. Since ANOVA revealed no significant difference in the extraction yield of SZS-TS between 50 and 60 min, 50 min was set as the extraction time for economic considerations. Independent verification experiment for ultrasonic extraction

Verification experiments were performed in triplicate under the optimal extraction parameters obtained from the RSM optimization. The experimental extraction yield was compared with the model's predicted maximum value, and the relative error was calculated. A relative error of less than 2% was used as the criterion to verify the reliability of the established model.

Extraction of SZS-TS by the traditional water decoction method

The extraction was performed according to Zhou et al. [17], and the extraction time and solid–liquid ratio were optimized. Exactly 5 g of d-SZS powder (prepared in Section 2.2) was weighed and extracted with ultrapure water at the optimized solid–liquid ratio of 1:15 (g/mL). The mixture was soaked at room temperature for 10 h and then extracted twice under slight boiling reflux (1 h each). The two filtrates were combined and concentrated to a semisolid extract by rotary evaporation; the semisolid extract was then further extracted following the procedure described in Section 2.3.1 to obtain the total saponin extract.

Determination of SZS-TS content

The assay followed the method in reference [18]. Briefly, 16.67 mg of oleanolic acid standard was accurately weighed, dissolved in methanol, and diluted to 25 mL to prepare the stock standard solution. Aliquots of 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 μL of this standard solution were precisely pipetted into test tubes, adjusted to 1 mL with methanol to obtain gradient-concentration working solutions, and a reagent blank (1 mL pure methanol) was set up separately. Subsequently, 0.2 mL freshly prepared 5% methyl orange-glacial acetic acid solution and 0.8 mL perchloric acid were added sequentially to each tube. After mixing, the mixtures were incubated at 55°C in a water bath for 20 min, rapidly cooled in an ice bath for 5 min, then 5 mL of glacial acetic acid was added, shaken vigorously, and allowed to stand for 10 min before the absorbance value was measured at 589 nm. The standard curve (Fig. S1) had the regression equation y = 1.196x + 0.0102 (R2 = 0.9990), where x = mass concentration (mg/mL) and y = corrected absorbance (blank-subtracted).

The SZS-TS extraction yield (%) was calculated as: extraction yield of SZS-TS (%) = C·V·n/M (C: the mass concentration of SZS-TS (mg/mL), V = total volume of the sample solution (mL), n: the dilution factor, M = mass of d-SZS powder (mg)).

Determination of jujuboside A, and jujuboside B content

Determination was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). Liquid samples were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The resulting filtrates were used for HPLC analysis. The chromatographic conditions were as follows: an Agilent 1100 HPLC system equipped with an EcoPak C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase was acetonitrile–water (40:60, v/v) under isocratic elution for 20 min. The column temperature was 35°C, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, the injection volume was 5 μL, and the detection wavelength was 203 nm. For standard curve preparation, 10 mg of jujuboside A standard was accurately weighed, dissolved in methanol, and diluted to a final volume of 20 mL. A series of standard solutions (1, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, and 500 μg/mL) was prepared by serial dilution with methanol. All solutions were analyzed under the above conditions. Linear regression was performed with peak area as the ordinate and concentration as the abscissa. For jujuboside A: y = 1.4752x − 0.5991 (R2 = 0.9999, Fig. S2), retention time = 6.412 ± 0.021 min.

Jujuboside B was determined using the same method: y = 1.7377x − 0.6511 (R2 = 0.9999, Fig. S3), retention time = 10.195 ± 0.034 min.

Sample filtrates were injected into the HPLC system and analyzed under the same chromatographic conditions.

Determination of the in vitro antioxidant activity of SZS-TS

The SZS-TS extract was evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure and re-dissolved in an appropriate volume of ultrapure water. Based on the results from Section 2.5, a series of SZS-TS aqueous solutions with gradient concentrations (0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, and 2.5 mg/mL) was prepared. Vitamin C (Vc) solutions at the same five concentrations served as the positive control, and ultrapure water served as the negative control.

DPPH· Scavenging Assay: The assay was performed as described in reference [19]. Briefly, 1 mL of sample solution at each concentration was mixed with 3 mL of 0.25 mmol/L DPPH ethanol solution. After incubation in the dark for 30 min, the absorbance value was measured at 517 nm.

Hydroxyl radical (·OH) scavenging activity: The assay was performed according to reference [20]. Briefly, 1 mL of sample solution at each concentration was mixed successively with 1 mL of 3 mmol/L FeSO4, 1 mL of 0.6 mmol/L salicylic acid–ethanol solution, and 1 mL of 6 mmol/L H2O2 solution. After mixing thoroughly, the mixture was incubated in a 37°C water bath for 1 h. Following cooling to room temperature, the absorbance value was measured at 510 nm.

Superoxide anion (O2⁻·) scavenging assay: The assay was determined according to reference [21]. Briefly, 1 mL of sample at each concentration was added to a mixture of 2.5 mL 0.05 mol/L Tris‑HCl buffer (pH 8.2, containing 1 mmol/L EDTA) and 2.2 mL ultrapure water. After incubation at 37°C for 10 min, 0.5 mL of 0.793 mmol/L pyrogallol was added to initiate the reaction for 4 min, followed by 50 μL of 0.1 mol/L HCl to stop the reaction. The absorbance value was measured at 325 nm.

ABTS+·free radical scavenging assay: according to reference [22]. Briefly, the preparation of free radical working solution: The 7 mmol/L ABTS stock solution was mixed equally with 2.45 mmol/L K2S2O8 solution, incubated in the dark for 12 h, and diluted with ultrapure water to an absorbance of 0.7 ± 0.02 at 734 nm. Reaction and detection: 0.3 mL of the sample solution at each concentration was added to 0.3 mL of the above-prepared working solution. After thorough mixing, the mixture was incubated in the dark for 6 min, and its absorbance value was determined at 734 nm.

All samples were determined in triplicate, and the average values were adopted for calculation. The absorbance of the negative control was measured as A0, and the absorbance of the sample solutions was measured as A1. The scavenging rates of DPPH·, ·OH, O2⁻·, and ABTS+· were calculated using the following formula: scavenging rate (%) = [(A0 – A1) / A0] × 100%.

Determination of the in vivo antioxidant activity of SZS-TS

Diquat-induced oxidative stress assay: Forty 14-day-old white feather broilers were randomly assigned to five groups, 8 broilers per group (5 groups), for in vivo antioxidant activity evaluation. The groups were designated as follows: normal control group, diquat-induced oxidative stress model group (10 mg/kg diquat, intraperitoneally injected twice at 24 h intervals), and three diquat-treated groups orally administered SZS-TS extract at doses of 30, 60, and 120 mg/(kg·d⁻1), respectively. The control and model groups were fed only the basal diet. SZS-TS supplementation was initiated immediately after the second diquat injection, and blood samples were collected after 12 consecutive days of feeding. The levels of serum superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) were measured using corresponding ELISA kits. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Hebei Agricultural University (Baoding, China), with the official approval number 2023097.

pH stability test of SZS-TS

According to reference [23], the SZS-TS sample solution was mixed with buffer solutions at pH 2.0, 4.0, 5.0, 8.0, 10.0, and 12.0. The actual pH values were measured using a pH meter after mixing, due to the pH buffering capacity of SZS-TS itself. The retention rate of SZS-TS was determined by measuring the total saponin content at 3-day intervals during storage. The actual pH values of the resulting dilutions were 2.87, 4.88, 5.77, 7.70, 9.32, and 11.90, with three replicates per group. After storage at 4°C for 12 days, samples were taken every 3 days to determine the saponin content. The retention rate was calculated as follows: retention rate of SZS-TS (%) = (determined content/initial content) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by SPSS 26.0. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan's multiple range test. All experimental data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), where P < 0.05 and P < 0.01 indicated significant and highly significant differences, respectively. Response surface data were analyzed using Design-Expert 12 for process optimization, and plotting was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 and Origin 2021.

Optimization of ultrasonic-assisted extraction of SZS-TS

Single-factor experiment results

As shown in Fig. 1(a–e), all five tested single factors significantly influenced the SZS-TS extraction yield and exhibited a trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Similarly, the SZS-TS extraction yield reached a maximum (2.27% ± 0.03%) when the extraction solvent concentration (ethanol concentration) reached 70%, with a significant decrease observed at both sides of the peak (Fig. 1b).

When the solid–liquid ratio ranged from 1:10 to 1:20 (w/v), the SZS-TS extraction yield increased from 1.83% ± 0.02% to 2.24% ± 0.02%, and then it declined, with statistically significant differences between the peak value and the values on either side (Fig. 1c).

The extension of extraction time from 30 min to 50 min led to a gradual increase in the SZS-TS extraction yield, which rose from 1.88% ± 0.02% to a maximum of 2.24% ± 0.01%. Notably, when the extraction time exceeded 50 min, the SZS-TS extraction yield began to decline gradually. However, no statistically significant difference was observed in the extraction yield between 50 min and 60 min, indicating that the SZS-TS extraction yield maintained relative stability within the range of 50–60 min. When the extraction time was extended to 70 min, the extraction yield decreased significantly compared with that of 50 min (Fig. 1d).

Finally, the SZS-TS extraction yield reached a maximum (2.16% ± 0.03%) at an ultrasonic temperature of 50°C, with significant decreases observed on both sides of the peak (Fig. 1e).

Therefore, the optimal levels of the four significant factors identified in the single-factor experiments, namely ultrasonic power (360 W), ethanol concentration (70%), solid–liquid ratio (1:20, w/v), and ultrasonic temperature (50℃), were selected as the optimal levels for the subsequent RSM optimization experiments.

Specifically, the one-order term coefficients of factors B and C were 0.209 and 0.297, respectively, with P-values of 0.0078 and 0.0008 (both < 0.05), indicating that ethanol concentration (B) and solid–liquid ratio (C) had significant effects on the SZS-TS extraction yield (Table 3). In contrast, factors A (ultrasonic temperature) and D (ultrasonic power) had P-values of 0.2197 and 0.0902 (both > 0.05), showing no significant effect on the SZS-TS extraction yield (Table 3). The quadratic terms (A2, B2, C2, D2) all had P-values < 0.0001 (Table 3), exerting extremely significant statistical effects on the SZS-TS extraction yield. The contour plots of AB (ultrasonic temperature and ethanol concentration) and BD (ethanol concentration and ultrasonic power) were approximately elliptical (Fig. 2a, e), and the P-values of both pairs (AB: P = 0.0016, BD: P = 0.0302) were less than 0.05 (Table 3), suggesting that significant interaction effects existed between A and B, and between B and D. Among them, the contour plots of AB had the highest density and the most significant deformation, indicating the strongest interaction effect between these two factors (Fig. 2a). No significant interaction effects were observed between other factor pairs (AC, AD, BC, CD) under the experimental conditions, which were consistent with the P-value analysis of interaction terms in Table 3.

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for the regression model revealed that ethanol concentration (B) and solid–liquid ratio (C) had a highly significant effect on the SZS-TS extraction yield (P < 0.01), while ultrasonic temperature (A) and ultrasonic power (D) showed no significant effect (P > 0.05). Although ultrasonic temperature and ultrasonic power had no statistically significant effect on the extraction yield (P > 0.05), they showed a clear trend of first increasing and then decreasing. Their optimal levels (50°C, 360 W) were critical for the balance between cell wall breakage and structural stability of SZS-TS, which was consistent with the results of single-factor experiments.

Discussions

Feasibility analysis of defatting pretreatment before ultrasound and ethanol as the extraction solvent for SZS-TS

It has been reported that UAE extraction can be directly performed for SZS-TS from Jujube seed powder without defatting [6]. The theoretical basis is that saponins are readily soluble in ethanol, while lipids have low solubility in ethanol. However, this study still adopted defatting pretreatment in accordance with most related reports [24], [25]. In practical operation, our research group found that without defatting, the ultrasonic extract was prone to filter clogging, which required a significantly increased solid–liquid ratio, thereby increasing experimental costs and operational difficulty. In addition, some lipids are soluble in ethanol, which not only decreases the leaching efficiency of saponins but also introduces a large number of impurities, thereby impairing the reliability of subsequent purification processes and content determination results. Therefore, defatting is a critical pretreatment step before the ultrasonic extraction of SZS-TS, ensuring the scientificity and accuracy of the quality control method.

Wang et al. reported that methanol had better extraction efficiency than ethanol for the ultrasonic extraction of SZS-TS [6]. However, ethanol was selected for its biosafety in food and health product applications in line with the National Food Safety Standard of China (GB 10343–2024).

Optimization of ultrasonic-assisted extraction process

This study optimized the UAE parameters for SZS-TS using single-factor experiments combined with RSM. The results indicated that all key parameters (solid–liquid ratio, ultrasonic power, ultrasonic temperature, ultrasonic time, and ethanol concentration) exerted a significant trend of increase followed by decrease on the SZS-TS yield within the experimental parameters (Fig. 1). The optimal extraction conditions were determined as follows: ultrasonic temperature of 50°C, ethanol concentration of 70%, solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), ultrasonic power of 360 W, and ultrasonic time of 50 min. Under these conditions, the average extraction yield of SZS-TS was 2.719%. The extraction temperature, ethanol concentration, and solid–liquid ratio of this optimal process were comparable to those reported by Guo et al. [26], whereas the ultrasonic power employed in this study was higher. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the ultrasonic equipment used, sample origin, and pretreatment methods.

In the single-factor experiments, the solid–liquid ratio was identified as a critical factor affecting the extraction efficiency of SZS-TS, as it directly determines the mass transfer driving force between the solvent and the raw material. Insufficient solvent volume (1:10) can lead to rapid saturation during saponin dissolution [27], forming a mass-transfer barrier that limits the release of target components. Conversely, an excessive amount of solvent (1:25 or 1:30) not only increases the cost of subsequent concentration and purification [28] but also promotes the co-extraction of impurities such as polysaccharides, thereby interfering with the separation and purification of target components and complicating downstream processing. Moderately extending the ultrasonic treatment time and increasing the temperature can enhance molecular diffusion [13] and improve the mass transfer between the solvent and active components, thereby promoting the release of saponins. In this study, the extraction yield of SZS-TS remained relatively stable when the ultrasonic time was 50–60 min, and the temperature was 50℃, indicating that saponin dissolution had reached equilibrium. Further extending the time or raising the temperature did not improve mass transfer efficiency; instead, it resulted in a lower extraction yield. This decline is likely attributable to localized overheating caused by excessive ultrasonic treatment, which promotes the degradation of thermolabile saponins, particularly jujubosides A and B [29]. Therefore, a trade-off between extraction yield and product stability is necessary when determining optimal extraction parameters. Ethanol exhibits both suitable polarity and biosafety, promoting saponin dissolution through hydrogen bonding [4]. Appropriately increasing the ethanol concentration can enhance saponin solubility and extraction efficiency. The optimal ethanol concentration in this study was 70%. This value can be attributed to its compatibility with the polarity of SZS‑TS and its ability to support optimal ultrasonic cavitation effects within the ethanol‑water system. At lower ethanol concentrations (50–60%), the higher water content may increase the co‑extraction of polar impurities such as polysaccharides and proteins, which can interfere with saponin purification. However, excessively high ethanol concentrations (80–90%) may induce saponin degradation and precipitation, while simultaneously enhancing the co-dissolution of lipophilic impurities [26].

Ultrasonic power is the primary driving force in the UAE, influencing extraction efficiency through the regulation of cavitation and thermal effects. At low power levels, the number of cavitation bubbles is limited, and their collapse intensity is weak, making it difficult to disrupt the cellulose-hemicellulose network structure of the Semen Ziziphi Spinosae cell wall [9], [13]. This impedes solvent penetration, resulting in low extraction yields. Conversely, excessive ultrasonic power induces over-cavitation and intense turbulence, which reduces the effective contact area between the solvent and the raw material. Additionally, it generates localized instantaneous high temperatures [30], which may degrade the glycosidic bonds and aglycone structures of saponins, leading to the decomposition of thermolabile components such as saponins A and B. This consequently reduces both extraction yield and bioactivity. Therefore, the selection of ultrasonic power must balance the efficiency of cell wall disruption with the stability of active components.

RSM analysis revealed significant interactive effects between ultrasonic temperature and ethanol concentration, as well as between ethanol concentration and ultrasonic power, both of which jointly regulate the dissolution behavior and stability of SZS-TS. These findings are consistent with those reported by Wang et al. and Guo et al. [26], [31]. As a polar extraction solvent, ethanol exhibits enhanced solubility with increasing temperature; moderate heating reduces solution viscosity and accelerates solvent penetration, thereby promoting saponin release from disrupted plant cells [29]. However, at high ethanol concentrations, temperature elevation exacerbates solvent volatilization, which in turn suppresses the dissolution of target components. Ultrasonic power facilitates cell wall disruption and enhances mass transfer through cavitation effects [32]. At low ethanol concentrations, the solvent polarity is relatively high, and saponin solubility is limited; thus, even increased ultrasonic power and enhanced cavitation fail to significantly improve the extraction yield [28]. Conversely, excessively high ethanol concentrations promote the co-extraction of lipophilic impurities, while the localized high temperatures generated by high-power ultrasound may degrade saponin structures. In summary, the combination of 50℃, 70% ethanol, and 360 W ultrasonic power enables efficient cell wall disruption while minimizing impurity co-extraction and component degradation, achieving efficient extraction of SZS-TS through synergistic parameter interactions.

The results of this study demonstrate that the extraction yield of SZS-TS obtained by UAE was 2.719%, which is significantly higher than that achieved by WDM at 1.143%, confirming that UAE effectively improves the extraction efficiency of SZS-TS. This finding is consistent with previous reports [10]. Ultrasonic extraction is well known for its rapid, efficient, simple, and safe characteristics [33]. Its mechanical action promotes solvent penetration into otherwise inaccessible plant cells, enhances solute transfer efficiency, and intensifies cavitation effects, thereby facilitating the release of intracellular components [34]. Compared with high-temperature treatments, the mild temperature conditions employed effectively prevent thermal degradation of heat-labile saponin monomers, thereby preserving their structural and functional stability.

Selection of reference substances for the determination of SZS-TS and its monomer contents

SZS-TS is a general term for saponin active ingredients, belonging to triterpenoid saponins, which mainly include tetracyclic triterpenoids (the main active components) and pentacyclic triterpenoids. Its core components are jujuboside A, B, A1, and B1, among which jujuboside A serves as the key marker for quality control and activity evaluation of SZS-TS [35], [36], [37]. Notably, traditional processing technologies, such as stir-frying and steaming, can alter the contents of jujuboside A and B [38], which constitutes a critical consideration for the selection of reference substances in saponin quantification. For the determination of total saponins by ultraviolet spectrophotometry, oleanolic acid [18] or jujuboside A [6] is commonly used as the reference substance, while jujuboside A and B are preferred reference substances for the quantitative analysis of monomeric saponins [39], [40]. In the present study, oleanolic acid was selected as the reference standard for total saponin determination because it is the common aglycone of all triterpenoid saponins in Semen Ziziphi Spinosae. This selection not only avoids the interference of processing-induced variations in jujubosides A and B contents on the accurate quantification of total saponins but also ensures the reliability of monomeric saponin determination, which is consistent with the aforementioned structural and compositional characteristics of SZS-TS.

Antioxidant activity of SZS-TS

In vitro antioxidant assays showed that SZS-TS exhibited scavenging capacity against ABTS+·, DPPH·, ·OH, and O2⁻· free radicals within the concentration range of 0.5–2.5 mg/mL, in a concentration‑dependent manner. Its overall antioxidant activity was lower than that of Vc at the same mass concentration, an observation consistent with the findings of Zhang et al. [8] on oat saponins. Among these free radicals, the scavenging capacity of SZS-TS against O2⁻· was relatively weak, with a maximum scavenging rate of only 13.00% at the highest tested concentration (2.5 mg/mL). As a triterpenoid saponin, SZS-TS contains active groups such as phenolic hydroxyl groups and enol structures in its molecular structure; these groups can donate hydrogen protons, neutralize unpaired electrons of free radicals, and terminate the oxidative chain reaction [41], which constitute the core mechanisms underlying its antioxidant activity. The weak O2⁻· scavenging capacity of SZS-TS observed in this study may be attributed to the fact that O2⁻· is mainly eliminated via enzymatic reactions catalyzed by enzymes such as superoxide dismutase, whereas SZS-TS lacks the corresponding catalytic structure and specific binding sites for O2⁻· [42].

T-AOC reflects the overall antioxidant capacity of the biological system, while SOD and GSH-Px, as key enzymes in defending against oxidative stress, are responsible for scavenging superoxide anions and peroxides, respectively, directly reflecting the organism's antioxidant capacity. Diquat can induce oxidative stress, reduce total antioxidant capacity, and inhibit antioxidant enzyme activities [43], [44]. The results of the present in vivo experiment demonstrate that SZS-TS treatment significantly increases the activities of serum SOD and GSH-Px and enhances T-AOC levels in broilers under diquat-induced stress, thereby effectively alleviating oxidative damage. These findings are consistent with those reported by Tian et al. and Pan et al. [45], [46].

Effect of pH on the stability of SZS-TS and its optimal storage conditions

This study demonstrated for the first time that pH significantly affects the stability of SZS-TS. SZS-TS remains stable under weakly acidic conditions but becomes unstable under strongly acidic or alkaline conditions. Specifically, strongly acidic conditions cause hydrolysis of the glycosidic bonds, whereas strongly alkaline conditions cause saponification of glycosidic bonds and oxidative ring-opening of aglycones, both of which are irreversible [47]. A weakly acidic environment with a pH of approximately 5.77 was identified as the optimal storage condition in this study. Under this condition, SZS-TS exhibits a high retention rate and a slow degradation rate, providing a valuable reference for subsequent applications. The high stability of SZS-TS at pH 5.77 can be attributed to the inhibition of glycosidic bond hydrolysis, which represents the primary degradation pathway of triterpenoid saponins under extreme acidic or alkaline conditions.

Conclusions

The optimal UAE process for total SZS-TS was established, with an ultrasonic temperature of 50℃, an ethanol concentration of 70%, a solid–liquid ratio of 1:20 (g/mL), an ultrasonic power of 360 W, and an ultrasonic time of 50 min. This process achieved an extraction yield of 2.719%. Compared with the traditional WDM, the optimized UAE significantly increased the yields of SZS-TS and jujuboside A by 137.88% and 161.15%, respectively. SZS-TS exhibited concentration-dependent antioxidant activity in both in vitro (free radical scavenging) and in vivo (diquat‑induced oxidative stress broiler model) experiments. SZS-TS exhibited the highest stability at pH 5.77, providing a reference for its storage pH conditions. This study provides a theoretical basis for the industrial extraction of SZS-TS and its development as a functional food ingredient.
