FMRP protects breast cancer cells from ferroptosis by promoting SLC7A11 alternative splicing through interacting with hnRNPM
Authors: Nan Wang, Bin Shi, Lu Ding, Xu Zhang, Xiaolan Ma, Songlin Guo, Xia Qiao, Libin Wang, Duan Ma, Jia Cao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFerroptosis is a unique modality of regulated cell death that is driven by iron-dependent phospholipid peroxidation. N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modification participates in varieties of cellular processes. However, it remains elusive whether m6A reader Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein (FMRP) are involved in the modulation of ferroptosis in breast cancer (BC). In this study, we found that FMRP expression was elevated and associated with poor prognosis and pathological stage in BC patients. Overexpression of FMRP induced ferroptosis resistance and exerted oncogenic roles by positively regulating a critical ferroptosis defense gene SLC7A11. Mechanistically, upregulated FMRP catalyzes m6A modification of SLC7A11 mRNA and further influences the SLC7A11 translation through METTL3-dependent manner. Further studies revealed that FMRP interacts with splicing factor hnRNPM to recognize the splice site and then modulated the exon skip splicing event of SLC7A11 transcript. Interestingly, SLC7A11-S splicing variant can effectively promote FMRP overexpression-induced ferroptosis resistance in BC cells. Moreover, our clinical data suggested that FMRP/hnRNPM/SLC7A11 expression were significantly increased in the tumor tissues, and this signal axis was important evaluation factors closely related to the worse survival and prognosis of BC patients. Overall, our results uncovered a novel regulatory mechanism by which high FMRP expression protects BC cells from undergoing ferroptosis. Targeting the FMRP–SLC7A11 axis has a dual effect of inhibiting ferroptosis resistance and tumor growth, which could be a promising therapeutic target for treating BC.Keywords: FMRP, Ferroptosis, M6A modification, SLC7A11, Alternative splicing

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is the most common malignant tumor among women worldwide and has increasing morbidity and mortality rates. Currently, surgical resection, chemoradiotherapy, endocrine therapy and targeted therapy have been applied in clinical practice. However, these treatment measures still cannot ideally reduce the recurrence and metastasis of BC [1]. It is easy to develop apoptosis resistance of cancer cells during BC treatment [2]. Therefore, identification of therapeutic targets with high sensitivity and specificity to break through the limitations of traditional anti-apoptotic mechanisms is of great significance for improving prognosis of BC patients.

Ferroptosis is a unique modality of regulated cell death that distincts from apoptosis, necrosis and autophagy. The important hallmark of ferroptosis is the imbalance of redox states with increased levels of intracellular toxic lipid peroxides [3]. Recent study has indicated that member 11 of the solute carrier family 7 (SLC7A11), a main functional executive protein of the Xc-system, is responsible for the reverse transport of cystine/glutamic acid, which then promoting cystine uptake of glutathione [4]. The glutathione peroxidase-4 (GPX4) specifically catalyzes the loss of oxidative activity of lipid peroxides in a GSH-dependent manner, thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress and ferroptosis [5]. Besides, cancer therapies underlying ferroptosis is considered a novel approach to reverse drug resistance [6]. The natural anti-ferroptosis metabolite 7-Dehydrocholesterol plays an important role in cancer pathology by regulating the distal cholesterol synthesis pathway [7]. Previous research have also shown that SLC7A11 is highly expressed in malignant cancers and is associated with tumor progression [8,9]. These pieces of evidence suggest a crucial role for inducing tumor cells ferroptosis in cancer therapy. However, the precise mechanisms of SLC7A11-regulated ferroptosis in BC still require further elucidation. Hence, identification of specific molecules and pathways responsible for SLC7A11-regulated ferroptosis is pertinent for the development of therapeutic targets to prevent BC.

Fragile X Mental Retardation Protein (FMRP), encoded by FMR1 gene, is a highly conserved RNA binding protein (RBP) that regulates mRNA splicing and protein translation [10,11]. The mutation or deficiency of FMRP cause Fragile X Syndrome (FXS), and its activity is crucial for regulating brain function [12]. FMRP contains nuclear localization sequence (NLS) and nuclear output signal (NES) that allow it to shuttle between the cytoplasm and nucleus, and thus participates in the regulation of multiple biological functions, including RNA alternative splicing, mRNA stability and protein translation modification [13,14]. Emerging evidence suggest that FMRP is involved in different molecular mechanisms associated with cancer progression and metastasis. The carcinogenic role of FMRP has been shown in multiple human and mouse cancer models, and it has an immunomodulatory effect in tumor microenvironment to promote evasion of T-cell immune responses [15]. Interestingly, abnormal activation of FMRP regulates necrosis factor RIPK3 to activate the necroptotic pathway in colorectal cancer [16]. Moreover, in the context of BC, FMRP is involved in regulate the epithelial mesenchymal transformation (EMT) related mRNA to promote BC cells proliferation and metastasis [17,18] and it is also a key prognostic factor for liver and lung specific metastasis of BC [19]. These data consistently support the important regulatory role of FMRP in different stages of tumor progression, but the mechanism through which FMRP triggers the ferroptosis resistance in BC is still not fully elucidated.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is a widely present base modification in mRNA, and it is the most abundant methylation modification on eukaryotic mRNA [20]. m6A modification is a reversible event that requiring dynamic regulation between m6A writers (METTL3 and METTL14) and m6A erasers (ALKBH5 and FTO) [21]. Recent studies have provided clear evidence that m6A modulator dysregulation has been closely related to tumor aggressiveness and clinical prognosis in diverse malignancies [22,23]. Moreover, dynamic m6A modifications exert the biological functions such as pre-mRNA splicing and mRNA stability by recruiting specific m6A readers (IGF2BP and IMP) [24]. Reports have showed that FMRP, as a novel m6A reader, preferentially binds to m6A-modified RNA and promotes nuclear output of m6A-labeled transcripts [25,26]. Notably, a recent study in colorectal cancer deliberated that FMRP served as a reader for m6A-modified and stabilized EGFR [27]. However, the specific mechanism and regulatory network of FMRP-mediated m6A modification in BC remains elusive.

In this study, we provided evidence to investigate the mechanism by which FMRP protects BC cells from ferroptosis. A series of in vivo and in vitro experiments found that FMRP knockdown significantly promoted lipid peroxidation levels by down-regulating SLC7A11 expression. The results of double luciferase reporting assay combined with MeRIP experiment showed that FMRP-mediated m6A modification is enriched in SLC7A11 mRNA and further influences the SLC7A11 translation through METTL3-dependent manner. Further research revealed that FMRP interacts with splicing factor hnRNPM to synergically promote the occurrence of abnormal alternative splicing events in the downstream target SLC7A11. Our findings suggest that FMRP upregulation might be a key driver of BC progression through regulating the SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis resistance, which indicate that targeting FMRP could be an effective strategy against BC.

Results

High expression of FMR1 is associated with clinical characteristics and poor prognosis of BC patients

We initially analyzed the FMR1 mRNA expression through TCGA pan-cancer analysis. Higher expression of FMR1 was discovered in several other cancers, such as breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Moreover, based on TCGA and GEO public BC datasets, and the results indicated that FMR1 expression was upregulated in BC clinical tissues than normal tissue (Fig. 1A and B, Supplementary Fig. S1B,C), and FMR1 was also highly expressed in various BC subtypes including Luminal, Triple Negative and HER2 (Fig. 1C). We also investigated the clinical significance of FMR1, and noted that elevated FMR1 was closely related to clinical stage and survival prognosis of patients with Luminal subtype (Fig. 1D and E). Further analysis showed that BC patients with high FMR1 expression had poorer overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) by analyzing GEO20685 and GEO42568 datasets (Supplementary Fig. S1D,E).

To further detect the expression of FMR1 in BC tissues, RT-PCR was performed on 60 paired BC tissues and adjacent normal tissues, showing that FMR1 was dramatically overexpressed in BC tissues (Fig. 1F), which was consistent with the findings in the database. Subsequent correlation analysis of FMR1 expression with clinical characteristics of patients revealed that the positive relationship between FMR1 expression and tumor size, tumor grade and malignant metastasis (Supplementary Table 1). According to the results of Kaplan–Meier survival curves and ROC curve, high expression of FMR1 predicted worse prognosis in BC patients, further supporting the tumor-promoting role of FMR1 in BC (Fig. 1G and H). Consistently, western blotting and IHC staining also confirmed that the protein level of FMRP was upregulated in paired BC tissue samples (Fig. 1I and J). Additionally, an elevated FMR1 mRNA and protein levels were further confirmed in BC cell lines, including MCF-7, BT474, T47D, MDA-MB-231, HCC1937, compared with MCF-10 A control cells, and the highest expression of FMRP was observed in Luminal type cells (MCF-7 and BT474) (Fig. 1K and L). Together, these results indicated that FMRP expression was increased in BC tissues and regarded as an independent prognostic indicator of BC patients.

Knocking down FMRP inhibits BC cells proliferation and promotes ferroptosis in vitro and in vivo

Considering the potential clinical value of FMRP for BC prognosis, we then investigated whether FMRP can alter the tumor biological characteristics of BC cells.

A series of in vitro experiments were performed in MCF-7 and BT474 cells. We successfully constructed FMRP overexpressing lentiviral vector (LV-FMRP) and knockdown vector with FMRP-specific siRNA (siFMRP) (Fig. 2A and B and Supplementary Fig. S2A,B). The CCK8 and colony formation assays indicated that FMRP overexpression promoted BC cells proliferation (Supplementary Fig. S2C,D), while knocking down FMRP exerts inhibitory effects on cell viability and proliferation (Fig. 2C and D). RNA-seq analysis of FMRP knockdown cells showed that FMRP deficiency resulted in differential expression of 323 up-regulated genes and 170 down-regulated genes (fold change = 1.5, P < 0.05) (Fig. 2E). KEGG pathway analysis showed significant enrichment of differential expression genes (DEGs) in Glycolysis, HIF-1, Amino acids biosynthesis, Ferroptosis pathways (Fig. 2F). Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent oxidative cell death that plays an important role in the progression of malignant tumors. To confirm whether knocking down FMRP could induce ferroptosis in BC, we constructed a BALB/c nude mouse subcutaneous xenograft tumor model. We collected control group and siFMRP group cells and subcutaneously injected them into the back of nude mice at a dose of 5 × 106 cells. The specific steps are shown in Fig. 2G. The evaluation of tumor photography revealed that the tumor size of xenograft mice had a significantly reduction after transplantation of siFMRP vectors (Fig. 2H). And knockdown of FMRP significantly suppressed tumor growth by measuring tumor volume and weight (Fig. 2I and J). Subsequently, we detected ferroptosis-related protein expression and found that FMRP knockdown inhibited SLC7A11 and GPX4 expression (Fig. 2K). Consistently, IHC assays also presented a similar alteration of these indicators in the xenograft tumors (Fig. 2L), which further proved that FMRP knockdown promotes ferroptosis during BC progression. In conclusion, FMRP functions as an oncogene that causes ferroptosis resistance and promotes BC development in vitro and in vivo.

FMRP deficiency induced BC cells ferroptosis in vitro

It is well known that lipid peroxidation, ROS accumulation, and GSH depletion are crucial events in ferroptosis. To further confirm our hypothesis, we first measured changes in some important ferroptosis-related proteins. As shown in Fig. 3A, the protein levels of SLC711 and GPX4 decreased, while HO-1, TFR, and FHC expression increased when FMRP knockdown. Treatment of MCF-7 and BT474 cells with the ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 (2 μM) to perform the recovery experiment, and the results showed that the expression of the above proteins was partially reversed (Supplementary Fig. S3A). Intracellular iron accumulation is also one of the typical signs of ferroptosis, and iron ions play a crucial role in ferroptosis, including the formation of lipid peroxidation enzymes and catalyzing redox reactions. So we examined the effect of FMRP on intracellular iron levels using a Fe2+ fluorescence probe. The results showed that knockdown of FMRP exhibited significantly stronger red fluorescence compared to the control group (Fig. 3B), which were almost reversed by treatment with Fer-1 (Supplementary Fig. S3B). Then, we conducted intracellular ROS detection in MCF-7 and BT474 cells by flow cytometry analysis. Indeed, compared to the control cells, the increased ROS level induced by FMRP deficiency treatment was also reversed by pre-treatment with Fer-1 (Fig. 3C and Supplementary Fig. S3C). Accordingly, we also used the BODIPY (581/591) C11 probe to detect the accumulation of lipid ROS, and found that a significant increase in green fluorescence after being treated with FMRP knockdown (Fig. 3D). As expected, Fer-1 intervention effectively alleviated FMRP induced lipid ROS (Supplementary Fig. S3D). Moreover, we detected the content of MDA and GSH, which are the end product of lipid peroxidation and important substrates of GPX4. The results indicated an increase in MDA levels and a decrease in GSH levels following the down-regulation of FMRP (Fig. 3E and F), while this difference was alleviated after Fer-1 treatment in MCF-7 and BT474 cells (Supplementary Fig. S3E,F), confirming changes consistent with ferroptosis. Taken together, these findings strongly suggest that FMRP deficiency induces ferroptosis in BC.

FMRP facilitates ferroptosis resistance through the upregulation of SLC7A11

Knowing that ferroptosis is a complex process involving the regulation of multiple genes. In order to excavate the downstream targets of FMRP that affect the BC cellular phenotype and ferroptosis resistance, we first analyzed the changes in ferroptosis--associated genes through ferroptosis PCR array. The results suggested that compared with the control group, blocking FMRP enriched 22 differentially expressed genes as shown in heatmaps (Fig. 4A), with significantly different genes presented in bar chart (Fig. 4B). To further screen downstream targets, we validated the relative expression of the aforementioned genes through RT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 4C, compared with the control group, FMRP knockdown resulted in decreased expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4, while FHC, ACSL4, VDAC2, CP and SAT1 expression increased. On the contrary, the expression of these signatures were opposite when FMRP overexpressed. Consistently, the expression of the above markers was further confirmed via Western blot verification, and found that the expression difference of SLC7A11 was most significant in the intervention of FMRP (Fig. 4D). SLC7A11 acted as a cystine/glutamate antiporter to synthesize GSH, and several articles reported that it was a critical ferroptosis defender and tumor promoter. To verify whether FMRP affects the occurrence of ferroptosis through SLC7A11, the localization and expression of SLC7A11 were evaluated after interference with FMRP by immunofluorescence staining. The results showed that FMRP and SLC7A11 were not only consistent mainly distributed in the nucleus, but also a corresponding decrease in SLC7A11 fluorescence expression when FMRP knockdown (Fig. 4E). Furthermore, transcriptome profiling was performed using the TCGA database to visualize the positive linear correlation between FMRP and SLC7A11 expression (R = 0.304, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4F). Overall, these data emphasize the importance of FMRP targeting SLC7A11 to regulate ferroptosis resistance in the progression of BC.

FMRP regulates SLC7A11 mRNA m6A modification and enhances its translation in a METTL3 dependent manner

It has been reported that FMRP as a new m6A reader to identify the m6A site containing the sequences “GGAC” or “ACU” [25]. Existing literature also has indicated that FMRP recognized the m6A-modification site in EGFR mRNA to promote the tumorigenesis and metastasis of colorectal cancer [27]. m6A readers regulates the eukaryotic transcriptome to affect multiple aspects of RNA biology, including translation, splicing and stability [21]. Based on this, we wondered whether altered expression of SLC7A11 was attributed to m6A modification of FMRP. According to the SRAMP website's (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp/) bioinformatics prediction results, SLC7A11 has multiple m6A methylation modification sites throughout its full-length mRNA sequence (Fig. 5A). Among these sites, we screened three potential high confidence binding sites containing “GGACU” (1795, 4385, 5396) in the SLC7A11 3′UTR for subsequent verification (Fig. 5B). To further validate the binding of FMRP to SLC7A11 mRNA, RNA fragments of SLC7A11 were constructed with either wild-type (WT) or mutant-type (MT) m6A site (Fig. 5C). The results of the dual luciferase assay showed that FMRP overexpression promoted the WT1 and WT2 activity of SLC7A11 3′UTR, but has no effect on the activity of MT1 and MT2 (Fig. 5D). The RIP-PCR assay demonstrated that the FMPR-IP group showed a significant enrichment trend in SLC7A11 mRNA compared to the IgG group (Fig. 5E). These findings indicated that FMRP promotes SLC7A11 expression by directly binding to the m6A site of SLC7A11 in BC cells.

It is clearly recognized that m6A modification exerts various functional consequences on mRNAs depend on the regulation of m6A writer. To identify the m6A writer METTL3 responsible for recognition of FMRP-mediated m6A on SLC7A11 mRNA, the MeRIP-PCR assay was performed and results indicated that the m6A abundance of SLC7A11 mRNA was obvious abolished when METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 5F). Consistently, m6A dot blot assay using an m6A-specific antibody showed the overall m6A levels significantly decreased when treated with METTL3 knockdown (Fig. 5G). In addition, we found that regulatory effect of FMRP on SLC7A11 could be weakened through the m6A methylation inhibitor cycloleucine (Fig. 5H and I). FMRP also influenced the SLC7A11 protein levels depending on METTL3 expression, and METTL3 knockdown blocked the function of FMRP (Fig. 5J and K). Taken together, these data suggest that FMRP directly binds to the m6A site on SLC7A11 and enhances the SLC7A11 translation by METTL3-dependent manner.

FMRP interacts with hnRNPM to regulate SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis

It has been reported that FMRP plays critical roles in RNA binding, mRNA translation and transport as RBP. However, whether FMRP regulates the SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis by other mechanisms remain unclear. Therefore, we completed the mass spectrometry analysis to identify the proteins that FMRP may bind to (Fig. 6A). According to coverage and number of peptides, the top 50 proteins were selected to complete KEGG enrichment analysis to determine the functional relevance of these proteins. The results showed that the top-ranked pathway were estrogen signaling and spliceosome signaling pathway (Fig. 6B). There are 6 potential interacting proteins involved in the spliceosome pathway (Fig. 6C), among which hnRNPM, hnRNPK and DDX5 were selected to confirm their binding ability to FMRP through independent CO-IP experiments (Fig. 6D). Afterwards, the RT-PCR assay were conducted in BC cells to analyze the effects of three candidate proteins on the key regulatory genes of ferroptosis. The results revealed that only the hnRNPM protein had a positive feedback effect on the expression of ferroptosis-related genes, which is similar to the regulatory pattern of FMRP (Fig. 6E). To this end, we initially performed the endogenous CO-IP assays with anti-FMRP or anti-hnRNPM antibody to confirm the interaction between FMRP and hnRNPM (Fig. 6F). To determine whether FMRP has a direct binding with hnRNPM and to examine the critical binding site, we truncated and segmented both proteins according to their domains. Among these, the region (10–731 in human hnRNPM, 123–633 in human FMRP) was identified to be necessary for their interaction (Fig. 6G and H). Furthermore, the colocalization of FMRP and hnRNPM in the nucleus of MCF-7 cells was achieved through confocal assay (Fig. 6I), and TCGA database showed that there is a positive correlation between FMRP and hnRNPM (Fig. 6J). Collectively, these findings convinced us that hnRNPM acts as an interacting partner of FMRP in BC cells.

To further elucidate the critical functions of FMRP interaction hnRNPM involved in SLC7A11 expression and ferroptosis phenotype during BC progression, a series of related functional investigations were performed in BC cells. First, we utilized Western blot results to find that knockdown of FMRP resulted in a decrease in the proteins levels of both hnRNPM and SLC7A11 (Supplementary Fig. S4A), and the combination of LV-FMRP and hnRNPM knockdown could reverse the promoting effect on SLC7A11 protein expression (Supplementary Fig. S4B). Next, we further observed the effect of hnRNPM on ferroptosis phenotype, and the results showed that knocking down hnRNPM promoted the increase of intracellular ROS and MDA levels and the decrease of GSH levels, while this effect could effectively blocked by treating with Fer-1 (Supplementary Figs. S5A–C). Moreover, the expression levels of intracellular ROS and MDA were restored after FMRP overexpression with hnRNPM silencing in MCF-7 cell (Fig. 6K and L) and BT474 cell (Supplementary Figs. S6A and B). Consistent with these findings, hnRNPM knockdown also significantly reduced the GSH levels induced by FMRP overexpression (Fig. 6M and Supplementary Fig. S6C). Thus, inhibition of hnRNPM resulted in an impact similar to that of FMRP knockdown on ferroptosis. In conclusion, these results supported that FMRP interacts with hnRNPM to regulate SLC7A11-mediated ferroptosis in BC cells.

FMRP promoted SLC7A11 alternative splicing by binding to splicing factor hnRNPM

It was found that RNA m6A modification can also act on the alternative splicing (AS) process, and increasing studies have shown that multiple m6A readers interact with splicing factor to affect the regulation of splicing [28,29]. Given that FMRP was reported to widely participate in regulating the splicing events of most transcripts involved in cell proliferation and differentiation [30]. hnRNPM, a well-known splicing factor, participates in pre-RNA splicing events to mediate several cellular processes. Therefore, we hypothesized that FMRP might regulate SLC7A11 splicing events in BC cells by binding to splicing factor hnRNPM. Single-molecule nanopore isoform sequencing was performed to detect the AS events in FMRP knockdown and control cells, finding that the five types of AS events, especially exon skipping, was significantly higher in FMRP knockdown cells than in control cells (Fig. 7A). Moreover, KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that AS-event-related genes were mostly enriched spliceosome and ferroptosis pathway, suggesting that FMRP knockdown might affect the variable exon skipping events of ferroptosis-related genes (Fig. 7B). Based on sequencing results and preliminary experimental data, we selected SLC7A11, FTH1 and VDAC2 as candidate differential transcripts of ferroptosis, and completed DNA gel electrophoresis by designing specific primers (Supplementary Table 2) to detect the expression changes of these transcripts. We found that the fourth exon skipping in SLC7A11 was most obviously change when FMRP or hnRNPM deficiency, which was consistent with the sequencing results (Fig. 7C). Next, to explore the effects of the fourth exon skipping of SLC7A11 on BC cells, we visualized and constructed the exon-skipped transcript (SLC7A11-S) and normal SLC7A11 transcript (SLC7A11-L) (Fig. 7D and E). Consistently, FMRP overexpression decreased lipid ROS and MDA levels, as well as increased GSH content, which were all significantly exacerbated by SLC7A11-S overexpression, whereas overexpression of SLC7A11-L only partially alleviated these effects (Fig. 7F–H). These data suggested that BC cells ferroptosis was inhibited by SLC7A11-S production, which possessed a specific mechanism that was partially independent of the reduced SLC7A11-L.

SLC7A11 and hnRNPM were elevated and correlated with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis of BC patients

To further clarify the role of SLC7A11 and hnRNPM in BC patients, we evaluated their expression in BC patients’ tissue and found that both SLC7A11 and hnRNPM were highly expressed (Fig. 8A and B). Further examination of the clinical significance of SLC7A11 and hnRNPM showed that BC patients with higher expression of SLC7A11 and hnRNPM had poorer overall survival according to Kaplan–Meier and ROC curves analysis, but the difference in hnRNPM expression was not statistically significant (Fig. 8C and D). Interestingly, correlation analysis of clinical data showed a positive correlation between FMRP and hnRNPM (r = 0.1046, P = 0.0117), but there is no correlation between FMRP and SLC7A11 (r = 0.0056, P = 0.5701), possibly due to the small sample size (Fig. 8E and F). Consistently, Western blot results also revealed that the protein expression of SLC7A11 and hnRNPM was significantly increased in BC cancer tissues than in paired normal tissues (Fig. 8G). Finally, we confirmed the tumor-promoting effects of FMRP and SLC7A11 in BC progression by IHC staining (Fig. 8H). These data demonstrated that SLC7A11 and hnRNPM also participate in the malignant progression of BC, the series detection of FMRP/SLC7A11/hnRNPM provides a strong diagnostic basis for the prognosis of BC patients.

Discussion

In the present study, we identified FMRP functions as a tumor-promoting effector for BC proliferation and progression. FMRP knockdown leads to the decreased levels of SLC7A11 and GPX4. Then, lower expression of SLC7A11 and GPX4 enhances the increased levels of lipid peroxidation, thereby promoting the occurrence of ferroptosis, and consequently restricting malignant proliferation and tumor progression. Mechanistically, on the one hand, FMRP-mediated m6A epigenetic modification of SLC7A11 enhances the SLC7A11-dependent ferroptosis suppression that partially attributes to the METTL3-dependent manner. On the other hand, we confirmed that FMRP interacts with the splicing factor hnRNPM to regulate the exon skipping splicing event in SLC7A11 transcript, leading to increased splicing of SLC7A11-S and subsequent blocked the occurrence of ferroptosis. Our findings reveal a new cancer-promoting mechanism, that is the critical modulation network underlying m6A reader FMRP-mediated the splicing switch of SLC7A11, which suggests that the FMRP-SLC7A11 axis jointly promote ferroptosis resistance in the development of BC (Fig. 9).

The working model explaining how FMRP targets SLC7A11 to protect breast cancer cells from ferroptosis.

FMRP, a member of the Fragile X-associated (FXR) family, is a RBP that plays a critical role in neuronal plasticity, transcriptional control and RNA metabolism. Recent studies have shown that identification of novel RNA and protein targets revealed this family has an equally important regulatory roles in neurological disorders and cancer biology [31]. Increasing evidence suggests the critical role of FMRP in the malignant progression of different cancers. Particularly, it has been recently proposed that FMRP recruits M2 macrophages to form an immunosuppressive microenvironment to promote tumorigenesis [32]. Pedini et al. discovered FMRP activates Wnt signaling pathway to regulate the proliferation of glioblastoma stem cells [33]. Grazia et al. have reported that FMRP regulates RIPK1 to resist the colorectal cancer cell necroptosis [34]. Although some studies have shown that FMRP promotes the occurrence and development of tumors, the specific mechanism of FMRP affecting the ferroptosis of BC remain poorly understood.

In this study, using the database and clinical samples, we found that BC patients with high FMRP expression had worse prognosis, and overexpression of FMRP promoted BC cells proliferation and growth, while knocking down FMRP had the opposite inhibitory effect. Subsequently, we performed the transcriptome sequencing and bioinformatic analysis and found that FMRP deletion was significantly correlated with ferroptosis signaling pathway, and a series of in vivo and in vitro experiments indicated that knockdown of FMRP accelerated the levels of ROS and MDA, and reduced the content of GSH. Furthermore, the anti-ferroptosis role of FMRP was further confirmed by observation ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1 to reverse the promoting effect of FMRP deficiency on ferroptosis. Interestingly, through integration analysis of ferroptosis PCR array and subsequent functional validation, we found that FMRP targets SLC7A11 and elevated FMRP promotes ferroptosis resistance by upregulation of SLC7A11. This discovery confirmed the previous findings that SLC7A11 is frequently overexpressed in human malignancies and plays critical role in protecting cancer cells from ferroptosis [35,36]. Thus, sensitizing BC cells to ferroptosis inducers by targeting this newly discovered FMRP/SLC7A11 pathway may be an important strategy for treating BC.

Increasing number of studies have revealed a wide range of different types of RNA chemical modifications, among which m6A is the most common and conserved RNA modification in eukaryotes. The effects of m6A were determined by m6A writers, erasers, and readers, who jointly participate in regulating RNA splicing, translation, and stability [37,38]. Research has shown that m6A participates in the pathogenesis and progression of tumors by regulating tumor associated gene expression [39]. Dynamic m6A modification is mainly recognized by recruiting specific m6A reader, and its expression imbalance is related to the malignant invasion and clinical prognosis of various cancers [22,40]. In recent years, more and more m6A readers have been discovered. FMRP, as a newly m6A reader, has been reported to recognize m6A modification sites containing “GGAC” and “ACU”, thereby stabilizing its targeted mRNA and promoting its nuclear output [26,41]. Based on this, we proposed whether FMRP promotes SLC7A11 expression in an m6A dependent manner. To our knowledge, there is evidence to support that SLC7A11 expression is regulated at the transcriptional level by transcription factors and at the post-transcriptional level by RBPs [[42], [43], [44]]. In consistent with these results, we identified that FMRP is able to regulate the SLC7A11 mRNA expression by binding SLC7A11 m6A modification sites. And the enrichment level of SLC7A11 m6A decreased significantly due to m6A writer METTL3 knockdown. Moreover, we used m6A blockers cycloleucine or METTL3 knockdown to verify that interference with the overall level of m6A would affect the regulatory effect of FMRP on SLC7A11 expression. METTL3 induces HIF-1α upregulation via m6A-IGF2BP2-dependent mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming to exacerbate ferroptosis in sepsis-induced acute lung injury [45]. Our results also demonstrate FMRP directly binds to the m6A-containing site of SLC7A11 in advance and then enhances SLC7A11 protein expression through METTL3-mediated m6A modification. However, the detailed pathway by which FMRP regulates SLC7A11 expression still elusive, and the specific mechanism needs to be further explored.

Notably, FMRP-regulated alternative splicing (AS) has gradually drawn researchers’ attention in recent years. Shah et al. [13] demonstrated that FMRP regulates ribosome translocation and promotes chromatin modification and alternative splicing of neuronal genes. They also found the antisense oligonucleotides can promote the change of mis-splicing of FMR1-217 transcription isoform, and promote FMRP level to treat FXS disease [46]. AS is an important RNA processing event that contributes to RNA complexity and protein diversity in cancer. A growing amount of evidence suggests the different splicing patterns of pre-RNA induced by AS are involved in the sustained proliferation, escape of apoptosis and immune tolerance of tumor cells [47,48]. Interestingly, the relevant mechanisms of AS regulating mRNA ferroptosis are also reflected. RNA-binding protein NKAP recruits splicing factor SFPQ in an m6A-dependent manner and affects the AS of SLC7A11 pre-mRNA to protect glioblastoma cells from ferroptosis [44]. Similarly, a recent investigation revealed that CircRAPGEF5 interacts with RBFOX2 to regulate the AS of TFRC and confer ferroptosis resistance in endometrial cancer [49].

In this study, we showed that FMRP indeed affected the AS pattern of BC cells, leading to the formation of SLC7A11 with exon-4 skipping (SLC7A11-S) and thus coordinating the inhibitory effect of FMRP overexpression on lipid ROS and MDA levels. It has been reported that FMRP can bind to hnRNP family members to influence the translation level of genes [[50], [51], [52]]. Our results provide important evidence for the interaction of FMRP with hnRNPM protein by using mass spectrometry and CO-IP data. hnRNPM, as a well-known splicing factor, has been proven to regulate the RNA splicing process in several cancer types [[53], [54], [55]]. In the splicing patterns regulated by FMRP/hnRNPM complex, our results demonstrate that SLC7A11 is a critical factor affecting the ferroptosis fate of BC. The collaborative partners FMRP and hnRNPM bind to SLC7A11 mRNA and mediate a splicing switch from the SLC7A11-L to the SLC7A11-S isoform. The inability of SLC7A11-L to exert its normal amino acid transport function, but SLC7A11-S could reduce lipid peroxidation to resist ferroptosis in BC cells. Based on these results, we conclude that FMRP could desensitize BC cells to ferroptosis by recruiting splicing factor hnRNPM to promote SLC7A11 splicing change. Therefore, we believe that this may be a novel mechanism underlying resistance to ferroptosis in BC cells and suggest that FMRP could serve as a potential therapeutic target for BC.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study first identified the subtle relationship between FMRP, m6A modification and ferroptosis in BC cells. Our results demonstrated that abnormal activation of the m6A-mediated METTL3-FMRP complex contributes to ferroptosis resistance by regulating of SLC7A11 translation. Meanwhile, we also discovered that FMRP is essential for maintaining ferroptosis resistance through recruitment of hnRNPM to affect SLC7A11 splicing to reduce lipid ROS production. These findings will help in the elucidation of mechanisms underlying the critical role of FMRP protects BC cells from ferroptosis. Our study suggests targeting ferroptosis via FMRP presents a potential therapeutic strategy to improve prognosis of BC patients.

Materials and methods

Clinical information and ethical statements for BC patients

We enrolled 60 BC patients undergoing surgical resection and received clinical diagnoses of BC at General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University from 2020 to 2022. These samples were assessed by two highly experienced pathologists who also conducted postoperative follow-up evaluations. A total of 60 pairs of cancerous and paracancer tissues samples were preserved in liquid nitrogen for follow-up studies. Written informed consents were obtained from all participants involved in the study. All experiments were approved by Ethical Committees of the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (No. KYLL-2024-0532).

Cell culture, transfection and in vitro treatment

The human breast cancer cell lines (MDA-MB-231, HCC1937, T47D) and normal breast epithelial cell (MCF-10 A) were sourced from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. MCF-7 and BT474 cells were purchased from iCell Bioscience Inc, Shanghai. MCF-7, MDA-MB-231 and HCC1937 cells were cultured in high glucose Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's medium (DMEM, Gibco) with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). BT474 and T47D cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10 % FBS. The MCF-10 A cells were cultured in a complete MCF-10 A special medium (Gibco). Each cell culture medium was supplemented with 100U/mL of streptomycin and 100 mg/mL of penicillin.

FMRP overexpression plasmid vector (pGMLV-hFMR1-3∗FLAG-EF1A-GFP) were constructed from Starfish Biotechnology (Suzhou, China). MCF-7 and BT474 cells were transfected with overexpressing plasmid according to the manufacturer's instructions and selected using puromycin (Biosharp, China). The siRNAs targeting FMRP, hnRNPM and METTL3 were constructed from the Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China) and transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions respectively. For detailed siRNA sequences, see Supplementary Table 2. To evaluate the involvement of ferroptosis, the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, 2 μM, Cat No. HY-100579, MCE) were supplemented to MCF-7 and BT474 cells for further experimentation.

Cell proliferation assays

In the CCK-8 assay, cells were seeded into 96-well dish at a density of 5 × 103 per well with incubating treatment for 24 h, 48 h and 72 h. Then the 10 μL CCK-8 working solution (Cat No. C0039, Beyotime) was added and placed the microplates in the dark for 2 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, we measured the absorbance at a wavelength of 450 nm with a microplate reader (PerkinElmer, Finland). In the colony formation assay, cells were initially counted and seeded at a density of 1 × 103 in a 6-well culture plate. After 10–15 days, the media from the wells was removed and cells were fixed using 4 % paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and subsequently stained with 1 % crystal violet (Sigma) for 10 min. Colonies that contained more than 50 cells were analyzed and quantified using Image-J program.

MDA, GSH and intracellular iron measurement

Cells were initially seeded into 6-well plates at 50 % density and collected after 48 h following the previously described treatment method. The contents of malondialdehyde (MDA) of cellular lysate under different conditions were detected using lipid peroxidation MDA assay kit purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Beyotime, S0131S). The ratio of GSH/GSSG was determined using GSH assay kit accordance with the manufacturer's instructions (Beyotime, S0053). Additionally, intracellular iron levels were evaluated by using FerroOrange assay kit (Dojindo, F374). Briefly, cells were seeded in 6-well plates and exposed to the specified treatment. After 24 h, FerroOrange (1 mM) dispersed in serum-free medium was applied to the cells, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Subsequently, the fluorescence was observed using confocal microscopy (Leica, Germany).

Assessment of intracellular ROS and lipid ROS levels

The levels of intracellular ROS was detected with Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (Beyotime, S0033S). In brief, following transfection treatment, DCFH-DA probe was incubated in cells with medium at 37 °C for 30 min. The cells were trypsinized and washed with PBS three times, and then the cell suspension was filtered through a BD 40 mm cell strainer. The results were analyzed using the flow cytometry. The levels of lipid ROS was detected with Lipid Peroxidation Test Kit (Beyotime, S0043S). The cells were stained with BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe for 30 min. The lipid ROS was observed under a fluorescence microscope to measure the intensity of fluorescence.

Real time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from BC tissues or cells using TRizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). 1 μg RNA was utilized as the template to generate cDNA by PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, RR036A). The acquired cDNAs were used as templates for quantitative real-time PCR analysis using TB Green Real-time PCR kit (TaKaRa, RR820A). The 2−ΔΔCt method was calculated to represent the relative RNA expression level, with levels being normalized to GAPDH. All primers were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). The primers used for RT-PCR amplification were listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Western blotting

Treated cells and tissues protein were extracted by Total Protein Extraction Assay Kit (KeyGen BioTECH, KGA1339) and quantified by BCA Protein Assay Reagent Kit (Thermo, A55861). After concentration detection, the extracted protein was separated by 8–12 % SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5 % nonfat milk for 2 h and incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. Then, transferred to secondary antibody for 1 h, and the protein bands were detected and scanned by Amersham ImageQuant 800 system (Cytiva, Japan). The antibodies are listed in Supplementary Table 3.

Nanopore RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

The cells of the control group and the siFMRP transfected group were collected to obtain total RNA, followed by the purification of poly(A) RNA, which was subsequently utilized for cDNA library construction. RNA Sequencing analysis of the full-length transcriptional library was completed based on next-generation Oxford nanopore technology by Biomarker Technologies (www.biomarker.com.cn). Astalavista software was used to evaluate the quality and types of reads after alternative splicing, and the occurrence of five kinds of alternative splicing events in transcripts were statistically analyzed. Differential expression genes analysis was conducted in both groups by setting Fold Change ≥1.5 and p value < 0.05. The results were then visually represented using heatmaps and subjected to GO and KEGG for enrichment analysis. All sample sequencing data was analyzed on the BMKCloud platform (www.biocloud.net).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were plated and treated on coverslips, then fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde and subsequently permeabilized with 0.5 % Triton X-100. The pretreated samples were blocked with 10 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were exposed to anti-hnRNPM antibody (1:100, NB200–315SS, Novus), anti-SLC7A11 antibody (1:200, MU601071, Abmart), anti-FMRP antibody (1:100, ab17722, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. After that, the coverslips were washed and incubated with corresponding secondary antibody (1:200, Chengdu Zen-bioscience, 550,040) protected from light for 1 h. Finally, the cells were staining using DAPI (Sigma, China) solution and visualized through an Olympus fluorescence microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

The tissue specimens were paraffin-embedded and cut into 5 μm thick slices for IHC. The tissue sections were fixed by 10 % neutral formaldehyde solution, dehydrated by gradient alcohol, and sliced by conventional paraffin embedding. Endogenous peroxidase antigen was added for repair, goat serum was sealed and incubated overnight at 4 °C with monoclonal antibody. After cleaning with PBS, enhanced enzyme labeled secondary antibody was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After cleaning, DAB chromogenic solution was dyed, hematoxylin was re-dyed, differentiated and sealed. The prepared sections were placed under a microscope to observe the captured images, and the staining results were semi-quantitatively analyzed using the immune response score system.

Mouse xenograft model

All animal experiments were approved by the Ningxia Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. 6-8 week-old BABL/c female nude mice were purchased from the Center of Laboratory Animal Science of Beijing (Beijing, China). Twenty-four female BALB/C nude mice were randomly divided into three groups as Control group, siFMRP-1 group and siFMRP-2 group. The various groups MCF-7 cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and subcutaneously injected (5 × 106 cells). The tumor growth was measured with slide caliper and recorded every two days. Tumor volume = 1/2 × (length × width2). After 21 days, the mice were sacrificed and the tumors were collected, weighed, and performed for immunohistochemistry and western blotting analysis.

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and LC-MS

The indicated cells were harvested and cell lysates were obtained using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China) supplemented with protease/phosphates inhibitors. After centrifugation for 15min at 14,000×g, supernatants were collected and immunoprecipitated with the anti-FMRP antibody for 4 h at 4 °C, and then Protein A/G agarose beads (Abcam, USA) were added for incubation overnight at 4 °C. Next, the beads were washed four times with lysis buffer and immunoprecipitated proteins were eluted by boiling with 4 × SDS loading buffer. The proteins were subsequently analyzed by western blotting. FLAG-FMRP Co-IP product were loaded on a 10 % SDS-PAGE gel and stained with Coomassie brilliant blue. After gel digestion, the separated peptides were analyzed by RIGOL L-3000 mass spectrometer.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

To explore the interaction between the FMRP protein and SLC7A11 mRNA, we conducted RIP following the manufacturer's guidelines of the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein kit (Millipore, USA). In short, MCF-7 cells were lysed using the RIP lysis buffer containing proteinase inhibitors and RNase inhibitors. The cell lysate was partitioned into three groups (input, IP and IgG control). Magnetic beads conjugated with specific antibodies against IgG or FMRP for 4 h at room temperature. After cleaning the magnetic beads, 1 mg/mL proteinase K was added to co-incubate with the RNA-protein complex to separate immunoprecipitated RNA. The relative abundance of protein-bound RNA was evaluated using RT-PCR analysis.

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)-qPCR assay

Total RNA was extracted from MCF-7 cell using FreeZol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA was fragmented into fragments in lysis buffer, and Protein A/G agarose beads (Thermo Fisher) were prepared and incubated with 4 μg anti-m6A antibody (Abcam, USA) or IgG at 4 °C for 4 h. Following three times of PBS cleaning, the methylated mRNAs were extracted with a mixture of phenol-chloroform-isoamyl alcohol after digestion with proteinase K buffer. Next, SRAMP (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp) was used for the prediction of the m6A site of SLC7A11, and the enrichment of SLC7A11 in m6A-modified RNA was assessed by RT-PCR.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The full-length cDNA sequence of SLC7A11 was obtained from Ensembl online website (http://www.ensembl.org/index.html?redirect=no), and the m6A modification site of SLC7A11 cDNA was predicted through SRAMP online website (http://www.cuilab.cn/sramp/), from which three methylation sites containing “GGACU” were screened. According to the 3′UTR sequence of SLC7A11 mRNA, wild type (WT) or mutant type (MUT) sequences were synthesized and inserted into the pmirGLO plasmid vectors. 293 T cells were inoculated into 24-well plates and co-transfected with SLC7A11-WT or SLC7A11-MUT and FMRP overexpressing lentivirus using Lipofectamin 3000 (Invitrogen). Luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Gene Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) after transfection 48 h. The Renilla luciferase activity values were normalized against the firefly luciferase activity values that reflect expression efficiency.

Ferroptosis PCR array

Ferroptosis PCR Array Kit was purchased from ShangHai WcGene Biotech CO. LTD. The array includes iron ion, mitochondrial function regulation, GSH homeostasis regulation, redox reactions. The kit uses a 96 well plate as a carrier to add upstream and downstream primers of specific biological function related genes to the wells. Total RNA of cells in the control group and siFMRP group was extracted and reverse-transcribed into cDNA, which was mixed with qPCR Mix and added into 96-well plates for reaction. Three biological repeats were performed in each group, qPCR results were analyzed to identify differentially expressed genes in the samples, and draw heat maps, volcano maps and bar charts to display the results.

m6A dot blot assay

To conduct the m6A dot blot assay, total RNA was denatured in the RNA incubation buffer at 65 °C for 5 min, followed by chilling on ice. RNA samples were applied and spotted onto an Amersham Hybond-N+ membrane (Beyotime, China). After UV crosslinking three times, the membrane was washed with TBST and blocked with 5 % non-fat milk for 1 h. Then, the membrane was washed with PBS and incubated with anti-m6A antibody (ab284130, 1:3000, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with HRP conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Invitrogen), and the membrane was visualized by ECL substrate. After taking photos, the membrane was placed in 10 mL methylene blue dyeing buffer and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. Clean the background with ultrapure water and take white light photos.

Statistical analysis

To assess the significance of differences, two-tailed Student's t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were used to compare between two or more groups. Spearman's correlation coefficient was used to calculate the correlation between the groups. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and differences in survival probabilities were determined via the log-rank test. All data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) values, and statistical analyses were performed by SPSS version 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. P values < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
