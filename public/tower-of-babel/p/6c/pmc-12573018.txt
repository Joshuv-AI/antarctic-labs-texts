Thorough Chemical Characterization of Boerhavia elegans: GC-MS and LC-MS Analysis of Leaf and Root Extracts
Authors: Lateefa A. AL-Khateeb, Nahed O. Bawakid, Saleh O. Bahaffi, Tahreer M. AL-Raddadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The phytochemical composition of Boerhavia elegans remains poorly understood, particularly in its leaves and roots, despite the plant’s traditional medicinal use. This study addresses this gap by conducting a comprehensive chemical characterization using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC-MS) and liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Sequential Soxhlet extraction with hexane, ethyl acetate, and methanol yielded extracts ranging from 0.86 to 12.07%, with methanol providing the highest recoveries (12.07% in leaves, 10.01% in roots). GC-MS analysis of leaf hexane extracts identified 12 compounds (86% of total content), dominated by fatty acids (palmitic and oleic acids, 28.9%) and steroids (38.5%), particularly stigmast-5-en-3-ol (27.0%). Ethyl acetate fractions were enriched in sterols (42.5%), including stigmasterol and campesterin. LC-MS profiling of methanol extracts revealed bioactive molecules, including lutein, echinulin, pheophorbide a, and pectolinarigenin. The prevalence of sterols, fatty acids, flavonoids, and chlorophyll derivatives with antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities underscores the pharmaceutical potential of Boerhavia elegans. These findings provide the first quantitative insight into the chemical diversity of Boerhavia elegans leaves and roots, establishing a foundation for its future use in medicinal and pharmaceutical applications.

Introduction

Natural products (NPs) are biologically active compounds derived from diverse natural sources, including plants, animals, and microorganisms, and are known for their inherent biological properties. They are generally classified as secondary metabolitessmall molecules (molecular weight <2000 amu) synthesized by organisms but not essential for their survival. Natural products can be obtained from various sources, including plant leaves and flowers, specific animal organs, extracts or exudates of organisms, or as isolated pure compounds. They encompass a wide range of chemical classes, including alkaloids, coumarins, flavonoids, glycosides, iridoids, lignans, steroids, and terpenoids, originating from plants, animals, and microorganisms.

The family Nyctaginaceae comprises 300–400 species of trees, shrubs, and herbs classified into approximately 30 genera. This family is commonly known as the Four O’Clock family, as many species have flowers that open from late afternoon to early evening. Within this family, the genus Boerhavia includes about 40 species, widely distributed in the tropics and subtropics, and characterized by a high degree of polymorphism. , Members of this genus contain distinctive chromoalkaloids and quinolizidine alkaloids, such as punarnavine, along with flavonoids, phenolic glycosides, phenolic acids, sterols, and organic acids.

Boerhavia elegans (B.elegans) is classified within the Nyctaginaceae family and placed under the genus Boerhavia, which comprises more than 100 species. This flowering plant is most commonly found in tropical and subtropical regions, and it is also distributed in the West Indies, Iran, Pakistan, Oman, and Saudi Arabia. ,

Leaves and roots play crucial roles in the phytochemical composition of plants, each contributing unique compounds that serve various functions. Leaves often contain higher concentrations of certain phytochemicals. For instance, the leaves of Ageratum conyzoides are rich in alkaloids, flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and terpenoids, all of which contribute to their antioxidant activity. These compounds not only protect cells from oxidative stress but also demonstrate therapeutic potential in the treatment of various cancers. Additionally, leaves can be a significant source of essential oils used in aromatherapy and as natural preservatives due to their antimicrobial properties.

Roots provide unique benefits due to their distinct nutrient composition, which often sets them apart from other plant parts. They generally contain higher levels of minerals that are essential for plant growth and development and frequently serve as storage organs for these nutrients. In human nutrition, many roots are also recognized as important dietary sources of vitamins such as vitamin C, B vitamins, and provitamin A carotenoids, in addition to being rich in dietary fiber that supports digestion and helps regulate blood sugar levels. ,

Overall, leaves and roots contribute significantly to a plant’s phytochemical profile, with each part playing distinct roles that enhance the plant’s medicinal properties and ecological interactions.

This study extends our previous work on the seeds and stems of B. elegans by investigating the bioactive compounds in its leaves and roots. − Information on the phytochemical composition of these plant parts is lacking in the literature. Therefore, the present study aims to characterize the compounds in hexane, ethyl acetate, and methanol extracts of B. elegans leaves and roots using GC-MS and LC-MS analyses.

Materials and Methods

Solvents

The solvents used in the various extraction processes included ethyl acetate (HPLC grade, 99.9%, Fisher Scientific UK Ltd.), methanol (HPLC grade, >99.9%, Fisher Scientific UK Ltd.), and n-hexane (HPLC grade, >99%, Chem-Lab, Belgium).

Plant Collection and Preparation

The Boerhavia elegans leaves and roots were collected from Alborgat, Hadramout, South Yemen. Plant materials were washed with deionized water, freeze-dried to preserve thermolabile metabolites, ground to a fine powder, and sieved (100-mesh) to obtain a uniform particle size.

Extraction Procedure

The extraction strategy followed a polarity gradient: hexane was first used to extract nonpolar metabolites such as fatty acids, hydrocarbons, and sterols; ethyl acetate was then applied to isolate moderately polar metabolites, including phenolics, sterols, and esters; and methanol was finally employed to recover polar and thermolabile metabolites such as flavonoids, alkaloids, and glycosides. This sequential approach ensured comprehensive recovery and a wide diversity of phytochemicals. Soxhlet extraction was performed using 30 g of powdered plant material with 300 mL of solvent in a 500 mL round-bottom flask for 72 h per solvent cycle. The extracts were filtered, concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator, and dried in a desiccator. The weights of the dried extracts and their percentage yields were calculated using the following equation

yield%=(weight of dry extractweight of plant material used for extraction)×100%w/w

Hexane and ethyl acetate extracts were analyzed using GC–MS to identify volatile and semivolatile metabolites, whereas the methanol extract was analyzed using LC–MS to characterize polar and thermolabile compounds.

The same steps were repeated for the roots of B. elegans.

Gas Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS)

The hexane and ethyl acetate extracts were analyzed using a Thermo Scientific Trace 1300 GC coupled to an ISQ 7000 single quadrupole mass spectrometer. An Rtx-5MS column (30 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness) was used with helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. The oven program was set at 80 °C for 2 min, then increased to 300 °C at 6 °C/min with a final hold of 10 min. Injections were made in splitless mode with a volume of 1 μL, and the ion source was maintained at 250 °C with electron impact ionization at 70 eV. Mass spectra were acquired over a range of 40–450 m/z. Compounds were identified by comparing retention times and mass spectra with the NIST 2020 and Wiley databases, considering only those with similarity indices above 90%.

Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS)

The methanol extract was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific LTQ-XL linear ion trap mass spectrometer, coupled with an Accela autosampler and Accela pump (San Jose, CA), which operates with the electrospray ionization (ESI) compartment as the ion source. Xcalibur Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., version 2.07 SP1, controls the system. The spray voltage reaches 5.0 kV, with a sheath gas flow rate of 42 mL/min, an auxiliary gas flow rate of 10 mL/min, a capillary voltage of 60 V, and a capillary temperature of 325 °C. The scan range spans 100–1100 m/z, and the collision energy reaches 35 V. The system uses an Eclipse Plus C18 column, 3.5 μm, 4.6 × 100 mm (Agilent, Palo Alto), maintained at 40 °C in the column oven, with a tray temperature of 25 °C. The mobile phase consists of (A) acetonitrile and (B) water containing 0.1% glacial acetic acid, with a flow rate of 400 μL/min. The gradient elution program begins at 15% A at 0 min and increases to 98% A at 60 min. The system applies to a positive ESI-MS scan-dependent mode and confirms the retrieved product ion spectra MSn using the NIST 2020 database.

Results

Herbal extracts present significant analytical challenges due to their complex composition, which contains diverse phytochemicals, coextracted endogenous substances, and other matrix compounds. These components can introduce noise and elevated baselines in chromatographic analysis, making it difficult to distinguish true analyte peaks from background signals. Matrix constituents may also coelute with target analytes or cause signal suppression/enhancement, thereby compromising accurate detection and quantification. In addition, factors such as solvent choice, mobile phase additives, instrument condition, and environmental fluctuations can further exacerbate baseline disturbances. Consequently, robust sample cleanup, optimized chromatographic conditions, and the use of matrix-matched calibration or internal standards are essential to minimize interferences and ensure reliable analytical results.

Matrix-matched calibration, standard addition, and sample dilution are widely adopted approaches for mitigating matrix effects in quantitative analytical chemistry; each poses significant methodological and practical challenges. Matrix-matched calibration relies on the availability of a true blank matrix that exactly replicates the sample’s composition but lacks the analyte of interest; sourcing such a matrix is often impractical or impossible in complex biological or environmental systems, where endogenous analyte levels are ubiquitous. Standard addition circumvents the need for a blank matrix. Still, it increases analytical complexity by requiring numerous spiked sample preparations, thereby elevating reagent consumption and overall workload, particularly in high-throughput or multianalyte assays. Sample dilution, on the other hand, can alleviate matrix effects by reducing the concentration of interfering substances; however, this process inevitably lowers analyte concentration and may compromise sensitivity, potentially leading to subthreshold quantitation. Collectively, these limitations restrict the routine applicability of such methods and necessitate careful consideration of resources, instrument sensitivity, and available reference materials when selecting a matrix-effect compensation strategy for real-world analytical workflows.

Baseline noise and elevation in herbal extract chromatographic analysis commonly arise from both chemical and instrumental factors inherent to these complex systems. The coextraction of diverse phytochemicals and endogenous matrix components increases background signals and elevates baselines, as these substances may absorb at detection wavelengths or ionize in mass spectrometry, thereby mimicking or masking target analytes. Instrumental factorsincluding lamp aging, outgassing, improper mixing, environmental fluctuations, and electronic noisecan further exacerbate baseline irregularities. Consequently, some degree of baseline fluctuation is expected in herbal analyses; however, stability can be improved through the use of fresh solvents, rigorous sample cleanup, matched mobile phases, and regular instrument maintenance.

The smallest detectable signal is generally estimated as three times the average baseline noise, corresponding to a signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio of 3:1, which defines the “limit of detection” (LOD). If the analyte concentration is below this threshold, the signal cannot be distinguished from noise. For quantitative analysis, a minimum S/N ratio of 10:1 is recommended to establish the “limit of quantitation” (LOQ).

Discussion

Figure provides a comparative overview of compound classes in Boerhavia elegans. Leaf hexane extracts were rich in fatty acids (28.9%) and steroids (38.5%), with stigmast-5-en-3-ol (27.0%) as the major sterol. Root hexane extracts showed high levels of alkenes (42.2%) and esters (37.0%). Ethyl acetate extracts from both leaves and roots contained sterols (42.5%), including stigmasterol and campesterin, which are associated with antioxidant and anticancer properties. Methanol fractions, which yielded the highest extractable content (12.07% in leaves and 10.01% in roots), displayed the greatest chemical diversity. LC-MS profiling revealed pharmacologically relevant compounds such as lutein, echinulin, pheophorbide a, and pectolinarigenin. These compounds contribute to antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer activities, with pheophorbide a linked to photodynamic therapy and echinulin showing potent antiproliferative effects. The chart emphasizes that B. elegans is a promising source of bioactive compounds for therapeutic applications, especially in cancer prevention and treatment.

Pharmacological Activity and Applications

Lipids and Membrane Function

Among the lipids, 1-palmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine and phytosphingosine play critical roles in cell membrane structure and function. 1-palmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine, also known as lysophosphatidylcholine (Lyso-PC), is a biologically active lipid with several essential functions in cellular processes and signaling. It is generated through the hydrolysis of phosphatidylcholine by phospholipase A2 and plays a significant role in various physiological and pathological processes.

Surfactant Amines and Agricultural Applications

Both tetradecylamine and N,N-Dimethyldodecylamine N-oxide (DDAO) are long-chain amines important in plant care due to their surfactant properties and potential antimicrobial effects. Tetradecylamine enhances the effectiveness of agricultural products like pesticides and herbicides by improving their spread and adhesion on plant surfaces, which ensures more uniform coverage and better penetration into plant tissues. Similarly, DDAO functions as a zwitterionic surfactant, known for its strong amphiphilicity, which effectively reduces surface tension and stabilizes emulsions. This property also aids in applying agricultural products, enhancing their efficacy. Moreover, both compounds exhibit notable antimicrobial properties that can protect plants from bacterial infections. Tetradecylamine may help shield plants from pathogens, promoting overall health, while DDAO has demonstrated effectiveness against various bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. Thus, incorporating tetradecylamine or DDAO into agricultural formulations could improve the performance of pesticides and herbicides and contribute to healthier plants by mitigating microbial threats. This synergy between their surfactant capabilities and antimicrobial action underscores the potential of these compounds in advancing sustainable agricultural practices.

Antibiotic Potential

Minocycline, a tetracycline antibiotic, extends the antimicrobial potential of B. elegans to human applications, with broad-spectrum activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria. These compounds collectively highlight the potential of B. elegans in agricultural and pharmaceutical formulations for controlling microbial growth.

Antioxidants and Photoprotection

The plant’s potent antioxidant properties are evident from compounds like Echinulin, an indole alkaloid, which is notable for its powerful antioxidant properties. This compound not only suppresses oxidative stress by reducing intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) but also shows significant anticancer activity, making it a valuable candidate for therapeutic applications related to oxidative stress-related conditions and various cancers. , Similarly, Lutein and xanthophyll are essential plant pigments that play crucial roles in photosynthesis and overall plant health. Xanthophylls are a class of carotenoid pigments that contain oxygen atoms, making them more polar than carotenes, and they are found in high concentrations in the leaves of most green plants. These pigments modulate light energy during photosynthesis and act as nonphotochemical quenching agents to manage excess light energy, thereby protecting plants from photoinhibition. Lutein, a specific type of xanthophyll, is an accessory light-harvesting pigment in chloroplasts, absorbing light in the 425–475 nm wavelength range and providing antioxidant protection against excessive light damage. Both lutein and other xanthophylls are produced through the carotenoid biosynthetic pathway and are vital for plant adaptation to varying light conditions. They also exhibit antioxidant properties that help protect plant cells from damage and aging. During autumn, when chlorophyll breaks down, lutein and xanthophylls become more visible, contributing to the vibrant colors of falling leaves. These pigments are essential for efficient photosynthesis and play a significant role in safeguarding plants against oxidative stress.

Anti-Inflammatory and Multifunctional Compounds

Moupinamide, also known as N-trans-feruloyl tyramine, is a naturally occurring phenylpropanoid amide compound found in various plants such as cannabis, black and white peppers, eggplant, and Chinese pepper tree leaves. In plants, its presence reflects an important ecological role, as it contributes to defense mechanisms and can serve as a biomarker for the consumption of specific foods. In human health, moupinamide exhibits significant antioxidant properties and demonstrates anti-inflammatory potential by inhibiting Cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) and Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) enzymes. By targeting these pathways, it reduces prostaglandin synthesis and alleviates inflammation, a mechanism particularly relevant in conditions driven by COX overactivation. Phytosphingosine, on the other hand, contributes to anti-inflammatory effects by modulating T-cell activity, which is crucial in inflammatory diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease. Its role as a precursor to ceramides not only supports skin health by maintaining the skin’s barrier function but also highlights its broader therapeutic relevance in managing inflammation and protecting cellular membranes. Phytosphingosine’s dual role in cellular structure and immune regulation complements the direct enzymatic inhibition observed with Moupinamide.

Additionally, 14,17,20 -Trihydroxy-1-oxowitha-2,5,24-trienolide, a withanolide compound, extends the anti-inflammatory theme by combining these properties with its known antioxidant and anticancer activities. Its steroidal lactone structure allows it to modulate inflammation at a systemic level, providing relief in conditions characterized by oxidative stress and chronic inflammation, such as cancer. Together, moupinamide, phytosphingosine, and 14,17,20- trihydroxy-1-oxowitha- 2,5,24-trienolide exemplify the therapeutic potential of plant-derived compounds in addressing inflammation and supporting health in both plant biology and human medicine. This interconnectedness emphasizes the significance of exploring natural products for developing effective treatments for inflammatory diseases.

Anticancer Agents

Several compounds showed significant potential as anticancer agents. Echinulin, besides its antioxidant effect, exhibits an antiproliferative impact on colorectal cancer cells, significantly outperforming standard chemotherapeutic agents. Also, Ferocaulidin, a coumarin derivative, exerts its anticancer effects through cytotoxic activity against various cancer cell lines and inhibition of cell proliferation, making it particularly relevant for targeting aggressive tumor growth. Its additional anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties provide a multitargeted approach, which could enhance its therapeutic applicability in oncology by addressing inflammation and infections that often accompany cancer. Similarly, pectolinarigenin, a flavonoid found in Cirsium species, has garnered attention for its diverse biological activities, including anticancer and anti-inflammatory effects. In vitro, studies have shown that it can inhibit the growth of liver cancer cells while exhibiting lower toxicity toward normal cells, suggesting its potential for targeted cancer therapies. Its antioxidant properties also reduce inflammation, paralleling the effects observed with ferocaulidin. Pectolinarigenin, a flavonoid, aligns with ferocaulidin’s ability to inhibit cancer cell growth, specifically focusing on liver cancer cells. Its selective cytotoxicitytargeting cancer cells while sparing normal oneshighlights its promise as a safer alternative to conventional chemotherapeutics. Beyond its anticancer effects, pectolinarigenin offers antioxidant and anti-inflammatory benefits, which could protect noncancerous tissues during treatment and lessen side effects such as oxidative stress or fibrosis.

Moreover, pheophorbide a, a chlorophyll derivative, introduces an innovative approach to cancer therapy through photodynamic therapy (PDT). Upon light activation, it generates reactive oxygen species that selectively induce cell death in cancer cells while leaving healthy cells unharmed. This mechanism complements the direct cytotoxicity observed in ferocaulidin and pectolinarigenin, expanding the therapeutic strategies available. Additionally, pheophorbide’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties further reinforce its role as a compound that treats cancer and supports overall health during treatment. ,

Metabolic and Nutrient Regulation

Compounds like 1,4,7,10,13,16-Hexaoxacyclo octadecane (18-crown-6) and Sucrose illustrate the plant’s role in supporting metabolic regulation and nutrient transport. 18-crown-6, primarily a chemical compound, enhances nutrient uptake in plants by solubilizing metal salts and facilitating their transport. Its role in optimizing potassium and other essential minerals parallels sucrose’s function in energy and nutrient allocation, both working to improve growth and productivity. In agricultural applications, 18-crown-6′s ability to enhance fertilizer efficiency aligns with sucrose’s role in promoting robust plant development and defense, providing complementary strategies to boost crop performance.

Moreover, sucrose, 9-oxo-10­(E),12­(E)-octadecadienoic acid (9-oxo-ODA), and 1,4,7,10,13,16-hexaoxacyclooctadecane (18-crown-6) are interconnected through their contributions to energy management, nutrient optimization, and metabolic regulation, both in plants and in broader biological systems. Sucrose, as the primary product of photosynthesis, is central to plant energy production and distribution, acting as both a nutritional and signaling molecule. By transporting energy from source tissues (leaves) to sink tissues (roots, fruits), sucrose plays a crucial role in supporting growth, flowering, and immune responses, highlighting its multifaceted importance in plant physiology. 9-oxo-ODA, a fatty acid derivative, complements sucrose’s role in metabolic regulation by addressing lipid metabolism in human systems. As a natural agonist of Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor α (PPARα), it modulates triglyceride levels and lipid-related disorders such as obesity and dyslipidemia. This metabolic balance reflects sucrose’s function in plants, where it sustains source-sink dynamics, highlighting comparable mechanisms in energy and nutrient regulation across biological kingdoms.

Pharmacological Potential of Flavonoid Constituents

The diverse flavonoid and phenylpropanoid content of B. elegans contributes to its biological activities. Glaucin B, Sissotrin, and N-trans-feruloyl octopamine collectively highlight the diverse therapeutic potential of natural compounds, particularly in cancer research and plant-based defense mechanisms. Glaucin B, a triterpenoid compound, stands out for its potent and selective cytotoxicity against solid tumor cell lines, making it a key candidate in cancer therapy. Its pharmacological relevance is further emphasized by its solubility in organic solvents, facilitating in-depth studies on its biological activity and application in oncology. Sissotrin, an isoflavonoid, complements Glaucin B’s role in plant-derived bioactive compounds by emphasizing such molecules’ ecological and medicinal importance. Isoflavonoids like Sissotrin are integral to plant defense mechanisms and signaling pathways, suggesting a dual role in maintaining plant health and offering potential therapeutic benefits. Although its specific biological activities are less explored, its presence alongside other bioactive isoflavonoids in medicinal plants highlights its significance in ecological and pharmaceutical contexts.

Similarly, 7,4′-Dimethoxy-5-hydroxyisoflavone, another isoflavonoid, adds to this ecological and therapeutic narrative by contributing to plant defense while potentially functioning as a phytoestrogen with health benefits. N-trans-feruloyl octopamine, a phenylpropanoid amide, bridges the cytotoxic effects of Glaucin B and the plant-based defense roles of Sissotrin with its anticancer properties, particularly in liver cancer. Its ability to inhibit hepatocellular carcinoma cell invasion underscores its therapeutic potential. At the same time, its high antioxidant activity adds a protective dimension that complements its role in managing oxidative stress during cancer progression. 3-Hydroxy-7,8,3′-trimethoxyflavone extends this link by highlighting the antioxidant potential inherent in flavonoids. Its ability to neutralize free radicals and potentially exhibit anti-inflammatory effects aligns with the health benefits of N-trans-feruloyl octopamine and isoflavonoids like Sissotrin. Additionally, its possible cardiovascular benefits suggest a broader scope of therapeutic applications, complementing Glaucin B’s and N-trans-feruloyl octopamine’s cancer-focused activity.

3-Hydroxy-7,8,3′-trimethoxyflavone enhances understanding of flavonoids by showcasing their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. Its capacity to neutralize harmful free radicals and potential cardiovascular benefits align with the functions of phenylpropanoids and isoflavonoids, underscoring its importance in disease prevention and promoting overall health. Grossamide, a 2-arylbenzofuran flavonoid, complements these properties with its potential as a dietary biomarker and its solubility in various organic solvents, which supports its pharmacological exploration. Although its biological activities are not yet fully understood, grossamide bridges the connection between dietary habits and health benefits, contributing a nutritional dimension to the therapeutic potential of plant-derived compounds.

While these findings are promising, particularly for potential applications in cancer treatment, antioxidant therapies, and metabolic disorders, further extensive research, including clinical trials, is needed.

Overall, the diverse compounds detected in B. elegans, from lipids and peptides to antibiotics, antioxidants, and bioactive alkaloids, suggest its potential for pharmaceutical and therapeutic applications.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive chemical characterization of Boerhavia elegans leaves and roots, highlighting the importance of solvent-specific extractions in accessing different classes of bioactive metabolites. The hexane extracts of both leaves and roots were dominated by fatty acids and sterols, compounds that not only contribute to structural and metabolic functions in plants but also exhibit strong anticancer and anti-inflammatory potential in humans. The ethyl acetate extracts were particularly enriched in sterols such as stigmasterol and campesterin, known for their antioxidant and anticancer properties, thereby underscoring the value of medium-polarity solvents in isolating pharmacologically active sterols. The methanol extracts yielded the highest recovery and contained a wide range of polar compounds, including flavonoids, alkaloids, phenolics, and chlorophyll derivatives. Key molecules such as echinulin, lutein, and pheophorbide a are of particular significance: echinulin has documented antioxidant and antiproliferative activity, lutein is a carotenoid essential in managing oxidative stress, and pheophorbide a has unique relevance in photodynamic cancer therapy. Together, these findings demonstrate that B. elegans is not only chemically diverse but also a valuable source of compounds with high biomedical potential. The significance of these results lies in how they expand the current phytochemical knowledge of B. elegans and open avenues for future pharmacological, nutraceutical, and clinical research aimed at exploiting its diverse bioactive repertoire.
