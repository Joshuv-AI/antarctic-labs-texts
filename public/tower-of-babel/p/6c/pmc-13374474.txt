Relationship between perceived social support and domestic violence with maternal postpartum stress in Iranian women: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Fatemeh Shabani, Maryam Montazeri, Fatemeh Yousefi Tabaei, Mojgan Mirghafourvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the relationship between perceived social support and domestic violence with maternal postpartum stress in an Iranian context.DesignThis cross-sectional study was conducted in 2023 in Tabriz, Iran.Setting and participantsA total of 349 mothers, 2–6 months post partum, who were living with a spouse and had no history of mental health disorders, were selected from municipal health centres using a cluster sampling method.Primary and secondary outcome measuresData were collected using the Maternal Postpartum Stress Scale, the Perceived Social Support Questionnaire (Personal Resource Questionnaire-85 (PRQ-85)), and the Hurt, Insult, Threaten, Scream tool for domestic violence. Data were analysed using descriptive statistics, bivariate tests (independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance and Pearson’s correlation coefficient) and generalised linear model.ResultsMaternal postpartum stress showed a significant negative correlation with perceived social support (r=−0.16, p=0.002) and a significant positive correlation with domestic violence (r=0.14, p=0.006) in the initial analysis. In the final adjusted general linear model with robust standard errors, perceived social support was significantly associated with lower stress (B=−0.1, p<0.001) and domestic violence was significantly associated with higher stress levels (B=0.4, p=0.041). Other factors associated with lower stress were having a birth attended by a midwife compared with a gynaecologist, increasing infant age, high husband support and moderate income sufficiency.ConclusionsThis study reveals a complex web of associations where perceived social support, domestic violence, economic stability and the model of birth care are linked to maternal postpartum stress. These findings highlight the urgent need for systemic support structures that empower the family unit and prioritise the emotional health in the postpartum period.

Introduction

The postpartum period, while traditionally defined as the 6 weeks following childbirth, is increasingly recognised by health researchers as a transformative phase extending through the first year of life.1 This extended period is critical for maternal and infant well-being, as the physiological and psychological transitions initiated at birth continue to evolve over many months.2 The initial weeks following birth are foundational for the long-term health and welfare of the mother and child, establishing a trajectory for future development and bonding.3

Childbirth is a profound event that extends beyond physical recovery, significantly impacting a woman’s mental health.4 Hormonal fluctuations during this time can trigger a wide spectrum of emotional responses.5 While up to 80% of new mothers experience transient mood changes known as the ‘baby blues’, a substantial 15%–20% face more severe mental health challenges.6 The early postpartum period is often characterised by intense emotional upheaval, with feelings of joy, fatigue, confusion, loneliness and anxiety being commonplace.7 This period can also see the onset or exacerbation of mental health disorders such as depression, anxiety or obsessive-compulsive disorder. Furthermore, traumatic birth experiences can lead to post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and in rare cases, postpartum psychosis.8

Maternal postpartum stress is a prevalent condition that varies widely in its manifestation and intensity.9 Its impact is not confined to the mother; it also significantly affects the newborn. Exposure to maternal stress during this sensitive developmental window increases the infant’s risk for a range of adverse outcomes, including metabolic and psychological disorders later in life.10 Chronic or recurrent maternal stress is linked to negative impacts on infant growth, feeding, attachment, mood and overall psychological development. The mechanisms for this transmission of stress are complex and are thought to involve alterations in breastfeeding composition, disruptions in mother–infant bonding and changes in maternal caregiving behaviours.11

A key protective factor against maternal stress is perceived social support. Perceived social support refers to an individual’s belief that they are cared for, loved, esteemed and part of a social network that can provide assistance in times of need.12 This support can be emotional (empathy, care), instrumental (tangible aid), informational (advice, guidance) and appraisal (affirmation, feedback).13 A high level of perceived social support from partners, family and friends can act as a significant buffer, mitigating the risk of postpartum depression (PPD) and enhancing maternal well-being.14 Conversely, a lack of adequate social support is a well-established risk factor for the development of postpartum mental health problems.15

In stark contrast, domestic violence, often termed intimate partner violence (IPV), is a potent risk factor for poor postpartum mental health.16 IPV encompasses physical, sexual, emotional and psychological abuse by a partner.17 Women who experience IPV during pregnancy and the postpartum period are at a markedly higher risk for depression, anxiety and PTSD.18 While data on the prevalence of different forms of violence are limited, studies indicate that violence against women is a significant global health issue.19 Risk factors for IPV include a history of violence, young maternal age and unintended pregnancy. The consequences are severe, ranging from maternal injury and death to adverse birth outcomes like low birth weight and subsequent child abuse.20

Research indicates a complex relationship between social support, domestic violence and postpartum stress.21 Given the profound and lasting impact of postpartum stress on both mother and child, there is a need to clarify its correlation with social support and domestic violence within the Iranian context. This study aims to examine the relationship between perceived social support, domestic violence and maternal postpartum stress in a sample of Iranian women. By employing robust and culturally relevant assessment tools, this research seeks to provide valuable insights that can inform targeted interventions and support services to improve maternal and infant health outcomes in Iran.

Methods

This study employed a cross-sectional design. The target population consisted of mothers who had given birth within the preceding 2–6 months and were receiving services at municipal health centres in Tabriz, Iran. The study was conducted in 2023.

Sample size

The sample size was calculated based on a previous study by Nakić Radoš et al,22 which reported an SD of 15.76 for maternal stress scores. Using a 95% confidence level (α=0.05) and a precision of 0.05 around the mean (40.31), the initial sample size was calculated to be 235 participants. To account for the cluster sampling method, a design effect of 1.5 was applied, resulting in a final target sample size of 349 participants.

Sampling

A cluster sampling method was used. The city of Tabriz has 29 urban health centres and 59 affiliated health posts. The main centres were selected using a random quota method. From the selected centres, the investigator obtained a list of all women who were 2–6 months post partum. A simple random sampling method was then used to select the required number of participants from each centre, proportional to the total number of postpartum women served by that centre.

Data collection procedure

The researcher initially contacted the randomly selected women by telephone to explain the study’s objectives and procedures and to screen them for eligibility. Women who were eligible and expressed a willingness to participate were invited to visit their local health centre on a designated day. During the in-person meeting, the study’s aims were explained again in detail, and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were then provided with the questionnaire packet to complete.

Data collection tools

A set of questionnaires was used to gather the necessary data.

Sociodemographic and obstetric information sheet

A researcher-developed questionnaire collected data on maternal age, educational attainment, employment status, family income, body mass index (BMI), parity, number of previous abortions, whether the pregnancy was planned or unplanned, etc.

Specific questionnaire for the assessment of maternal postpartum stress

The Maternal Postpartum Stress Scale (MPSS) is a scale developed by Nakić Radoš et al to assess the intensity of stress arising from various stressors during the first year postpartum. The initial MPSS consisted of 46 items, which has been revised to 22 items in the current version. Respondents are tasked with rating the intensity of stress they experienced in the preceding month on a 5-point scale, ranging from 0 (very little or no stress at all) to 4 (very high stress). This questionnaire assesses three domains: (1) Personal Needs and Fatigue, (2) Infant Care and (3) Body Changes and Sexuality. An internal consistency analysis indicated that the Cronbach’s α was 0.85 for the Personal Needs and Fatigue subscale, 0.83 for the Infant Care subscale and 0.79 for the Body Changes and Sexuality subscale. The Cronbach’s α for the full MPSS scale was 0.88.22 The psychometric properties of this scale are explored by the research team in another project (Cronbach’s α=0.90).

Perceived Social Support Questionnaire

The Personal Resource Questionnaire-85 (PRQ-85) was designed by Weinert and Brandt23 to measure social support. This questionnaire consists of two parts. The second part measures perceived social support and comprises five dimensions: Friendship, Assistance, Social Integration, Worth and Provision of Care (Nurturance). This questionnaire contains 25 items and is scored on a 7-point Likert scale: 1=strongly disagree, 2=disagree, 3=somewhat disagree, 4=don’t know, 5=somewhat agree, 6=agree, 7=strongly agree. In the present study, the second part of this questionnaire was used. It is noteworthy that the validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been previously established in a study on haemodialysis patients in Iran. Its validity was determined through content validity, and its reliability was assessed using the test-retest method, which reported a correlation coefficient of 0.80.24

Domestic Violence Questionnaire (Hurt, Insult, Threaten, Scream tool)

In this study, the 4-item Hurt, Insult, Threaten, Scream (HITS) tool was used for violence screening. This is a four-item instrument that asks respondents how frequently their partner has physically Hurt, Insulted, Threatened them with harm and Screamed at them. These four items form the HITS acronym. Each question is answered on a 5-point scale: 1=never, 2=rarely, 3=sometimes, 4=fairly often, 5=frequently.25 In a study by Shirzadi et al, Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for testing the consistency of answers for all questions of HITS showed acceptable reliability.26

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, V. 25.0. The normality of the quantitative variables was assessed via skewness and kurtosis, all of which showed a normal distribution. Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, frequency, percentage) were used to summarise participant characteristics and study variables. Bivariate analyses were conducted using independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance and Pearson’s correlation coefficient to explore relationships. Finally, a generalised linear model was performed to identify predictors of maternal postpartum stress while controlling for potential confounders. Collinearity diagnostics were performed prior to model entry; Variance inflation factor values were <2 for all variables, confirming the absence of multicollinearity. To account for the cluster sampling design, the model used robust standard errors to adjust for the nesting of participants within the 10 municipal health centres selected for this study. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

The findings are presented in three parts: the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants, the descriptive statistics and bivariate correlations of the main study variables, and the final adjusted model identifying predictors of maternal postpartum stress.

A statistically significant, weak negative correlation was found between perceived social support and maternal postpartum stress (r=−0.16, p=0.002). Several other factors emerged as significant associated factors. Having a birth attended by a gynaecologist (compared with a midwife) was associated with significantly higher stress scores (B=8.9, 95% CI 5.0 to 12.9, p<0.001). Conversely, increasing infant age was associated with a significant reduction in maternal stress (B=−2.0, 95% CI −3.2 to −0.9, p<0.001). Regarding husband support, ‘high’ support was associated with lower levels of stress (B=−15.1, 95% CI −23.5 to −6.6, p<0.001). Finally, income sufficiency was a significant factor; specifically, women reporting moderate income sufficiency had significantly lower stress scores than those with low income sufficiency (B=−8.5, 95% CI −11.1 to −5.8, p<0.001). Maternal age, history of violence and the number of prenatal classes attended were not significantly associated with postpartum stress levels in the final model.

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between perceived social support and domestic violence as central factors associated with maternal postpartum stress among Iranian women. Our findings reveal a multifactorial model of postpartum stress, where both relational and socioeconomic factors are closely linked to maternal well-being.

A key finding of this study is the significant protective role that perceived social support plays in reducing maternal postpartum stress, which aligns with extensive international research.27 28 Social support serves as a crucial buffer, alleviating the psychological challenges faced during the postpartum period by enhancing coping mechanisms, decreasing feelings of isolation and boosting maternal self-esteem.29 Our study further refines this understanding by emphasising the critical importance of support from husbands. The observation that ‘high’ support from husbands was strongly associated with lower stress levels highlights the unique and powerful influence that partners have on the maternal postpartum experience, particularly within the Iranian cultural context.30 This suggests that interventions aimed at improving maternal mental health may be most effective when they actively involve and educate partners on how to provide both practical and emotional support.

Domestic violence was significantly associated with increased postpartum stress, remaining an independent factor in the final adjusted model. This significance suggests that violence is a primary stressor that maintains a direct relationship with maternal psychological distress, even after accounting for the buffering effects of social support and economic stability. While the prevalence and reporting of such violence can be affected by social desirability bias31 and cultural stigmas in the Iranian context,32 its persistence in our statistical model underscores it as a critical factor that healthcare providers must prioritise. These results suggest that the relationship between violence and stress is not merely overshadowed by other variables but is a distinct and potent associate of poor maternal mental health.

The prevalence of caesarean sections in this study was remarkably high at 86.8%, far exceeding the WHO’s recommended rate of 10%–15%.33 This mirrors a significant trend in the Iranian healthcare system towards the medicalisation of childbirth. Our findings suggest that despite its prevalence, such high rates of surgical intervention may contribute to the 'web of stress’ experienced by mothers. Conversely, the protective effect of midwife-led care found in this study is noteworthy. Unlike the often highly medicalised approach of gynaecologist-led care in hospitals, midwives are more likely to offer continuous emotional presence and support for physiological birth.34 This human-centred approach likely enhances maternal self-efficacy and serves as a buffer against the stressors of the early postpartum months.

The significance of socioeconomic status, particularly income sufficiency, serves as an important protective factor for new mothers.35 This research reaffirms that financial strain is a significant stressor during the early stages of motherhood. When mothers have economic stability, they can access essential resources and experience fewer worries, which could otherwise exacerbate the challenges of caring for a newborn.36 Additionally, it is understandable that stress tends to decrease as the infant grows older. This reduction in stress likely reflects the mother’s increasing confidence, the establishment of routines and her recovery from the immediate physiological and psychological demands of childbirth.37

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including a robust sample size determined by a power analysis, the use of validated and standardised scales for all key variables and the application of a multivariate linear model to account for potential confounders. However, there are important limitations to acknowledge. First, the cross-sectional design prevents us from establishing causality; we can only report associations. A longitudinal study that follows women from pregnancy through the first postpartum year would be necessary to better understand the dynamic and causal relationships between these variables. Second, the data were collected through interviews, which introduces the possibility of social desirability bias, particularly when reporting sensitive information such as domestic violence and income. Finally, the study was conducted in a single city in Iran (Tabriz), which means the findings may not be generalisable to all Iranian women or to other cultural contexts.

Implications

The findings of this study offer several actionable pathways for clinical practice. First, healthcare providers should move beyond clinical screenings and implement community-based interventions, such as peer-led breastfeeding support sessions, which have been shown to reduce maternal anxiety and foster social connection. Second, given the significant role of husband support, maternal health programmes should transition to a 'family-centred’ model, actively inviting partners to participate in postnatal counselling. Finally, the protective association of midwife-led care suggests that integrating midwives more deeply into the postpartum follow-up period—rather than just the delivery room—could provide the continuous emotional support necessary to mitigate stress.

Conclusions

This study reveals a complex web of associations where perceived social support, domestic violence, economic stability and the model of birth care are linked to maternal postpartum stress. Although the cross-sectional nature of this study precludes the determination of causality, these findings highlight the urgent need for systemic support structures that empower the family unit and prioritise the emotional health of the mother during the first 6 months of the postpartum period.
