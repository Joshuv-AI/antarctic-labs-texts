Case reports of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil
Authors: Matheus Oliveira Bastos, Fernando Almeida da Silva, Mayara Secco Torres da Silva, Pedro Henrique Nascimento Theodoro, Huila Luiza Santos da Fonseca, Marcela de Faria Ferreira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractZoonotic sporotrichosis is hyperendemic in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. Primary pulmonary sporotrichosis is a rare manifestation that can lead to high morbidity and mortality. Diagnosis can be challenging, and optimal treatment is not defined by clinical studies. We report two cases of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis treated in an infectious disease's hospital in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. One of our cases progressed to death despite antifungal treatment, and the other needed long-term antifungal suppression for symptom control. Primary pulmonary sporotrichosis is a life-threatening condition with few cases reported in the literature. For this reason, there are currently no randomized studies to define treatment time and case management to define the best strategies.Keywords: Sporotrichosis, Sporothrix, Fungal lung disease, Zoonoses

Introduction

Sporotrichosis is a mycosis with diverse clinical presentations caused by the dimorphic fungus Sporothrix schenckii sensu lato. The fungus is ubiquitous in nature, found in soil, wood, and plants, and is endemic to tropical and subtropical areas worldwide. With advances in molecular biology techniques, it has been possible to identify different species, and virulence patterns with their geographical distribution [1], [2]. Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, has been considered a hyperendemic area, with a significant increase in cases in the last two decades. The most prevalent species in the region is Sporothrix brasiliensis, which has been associated with a more virulent pathogenesis [3], [4].

The main clinical presentation of sporotrichosis is lymphocutaneous. Primary pulmonary sporotrichosis is a rare clinical form that adds a great burden to the disease due to significant morbidity and mortality. It has been previously reported as a manifestation of inhalation of conidia from soil and plants, but evidence-based risk factors and transmission dynamics are still under investigation [5], [6].

Suspicion of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis is difficult because radiological and clinical manifestations mimic other infectious diseases, especially tuberculosis. Diagnosis is made only by fungal culture of sputum or bronchoalveolar fluid with Sporothrix spp isolate, histopathology, or by molecular biology. If sporotrichosis is not suspected and treated in time, the patient deteriorates with poor outcomes. Furthermore, due to the low incidence of this clinical form, the current therapeutic strategies are based on few case reports and expert opinion, which poses challenges for management [7].

In this article, we report two cases of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis in apparently immunocompetent hosts with different outcomes. Furthermore, we performed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for molecular identification. Both cases are summarized in Table 1. The data of both patients was retrospectively collected from the medical records. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of Instituto Nacional de Infectologia Evandro Chagas (CAAE: 82018824.0.0000.5262). Participants or their next of kin provided written informed consent.

Case reports

Case 1

A 60-year-old man presented to the primary care with a two-month history of productive cough, fever and weight loss. He was a retired bricklayer living in a low-income neighborhood in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, and reported a history of treated pulmonary tuberculosis (TB) three years before, current tobacco smoking (50 pack-years) and alcohol consumption (50 units/week). He had a healthy cat. During a one-year investigation, he had multiple sputum samples negative for tuberculosis, chest x-ray revealed a right apical opacity and chest computed tomography (CT) showed a right apical consolidation associated with a tree-in-bud sign. HIV serology was negative. He was empirically treated for community acquired pneumonia (CAP) two times with poor response. After 15 months of symptoms, he had worsening of health status and was diagnosed with a large left pleural effusion, receiving empiric TB treatment.

Due to no improvement after two months of treatment, he was submitted to a bronchoscopy. In the bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), there was absence of mycobacteria and fungal culture yielded Sporothrix spp. isolation, confirming the diagnosis of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis due to the lack of cutaneous or lymphatic manifestations. He was referred to an Infectious Diseases Hospital and started on itraconazole 200 mg twice daily but had poor adherence to treatment. On this occasion, chest CT revealed a 4 × 2 cm right apical lung cavitation with thick walls and multiple nodules (Fig. 1) and spontaneous sputum yielded Sporothrix spp growth, subsequently identified as S. brasiliensis using DNA-based method. Besides, anti-Sporothrix antibodies were detected by ELISA (index ratio = 8523). Six months later, he presented to the hospital with worsening dyspnea, hypoxemia, hypotension, tachycardia, fever, productive cough and severe malnutrition, associated with increased alcohol consumption. Chest CT (Fig. 2) showed a new consolidation and enlarged cavitation (6 ×4,5 cm), with new smaller cavitations. He was treated with amphotericin B lipid complex, piperacillin-tazobactam and azithromycin due to the hypothesis of associated bacterial pneumonia. Despite seven days of antimicrobials and intensive life support, the patient died.

Case 2

A 66-year-old man presented with a history of cough, fever and exertional dyspnea for the previous eight months. He was born in a rural area in Rio de Janeiro/Brazil but had lived in the metropolitan region for many years in a house with a garden that was frequently visited by neighbor's dogs and cats. There was also previous exposure to soil manipulation. He had a medical history type 2 diabetes mellitus and heavy tobacco smoking with Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease. His brother was treated for cutaneous sporotrichosis twelve years before. At physical examination, there were no cutaneous lesions or lymphadenopathy. Chest CT showed pulmonary apical cavitations with irregular and thick walls, multiple soft tissue density nodules, middle lobe calcifications and emphysema. He was submitted to a bronchoscopy and the BAL had negative culture for mycobacteria and fungi, and a positive galactomannan (GM) of 0.97 (reference < 0.5). The serum immunodiffusion test for Aspergillus sp. was negative. Even so, the hypothesis of chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis persisted and he started on itraconazole 200 mg daily for a total of six months, presenting clinical improvement.

Two months after treatment was stopped, his chest CT showed a reduction in the previous cavities and nodules, but another bronchoscopy was performed and culture of bronchoalveolar specimens yielded Sporothrix spp. growth in fungal culture, identified as S. brasiliensis using DNA-based method. Anti-Sporothrix antibodies were detected by ELISA (index ratio = 5846). Subsequent chest CT showed new multiple nodules, ground glass opacities, tree-in-bud sign and diffuse consolidations. Following the diagnosis of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis, itraconazole was re-started with a higher dose of 400 mg daily. After 12 months on this regimen, he had full remission of symptoms, radiological improvement, but still had persistent BAL cultures with S. braziliensis, which motivated the change to posaconazole 800 mg daily. After clinical improvement with 12 months of posaconazole, treatment was interrupted, but due to persistent symptoms and positive sputum, he was prescribed itraconazole 200 mg daily for chronic maintenance and has been stable on this regimen since September 2022.

Molecular identification

The molecular identification was performed using species-specific primers, previously described [8]. The DNA from the studied samples were tested against the specific primers targeting S. brasiliensis (Sbra-F) 5’ – CCC CCG TTT GAC GCT TGG – 3’ and (Sbra-R) 5’ – CCC GGA TAA CCG TGT GTC ATA AT – 3’, S. schenckii (Ssch-F) 5’ – TTT CGA ATG CGT TCG GCT GG and (Ssch-F) 5’ – TTT CGA ATG CGT TCG GCT GG – 3’) and S. globosa (Sglo-F) 5’ – CGC CTA GGC CAG ATC ACC ACT AAG and (Sglo-R) 5’ – CCA ATG TCT ACC CGT GCT – 3’), which generates an amplified fragments of 469 bp, 331 bp and 243 bp, respectively. These reactions included 100 ng of a target DNA, 1.5 mM MgCl2 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), 200 mM of each dNTPs (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), and 50 U/mL of Platinum Taq polymerase (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 10 mM of forward and reverse primers in a final volume of 25 µl. The touchdown temperature cycles were performed with a initial denaturation of 5 min at 95 ºC; 35 cycles of 1 min at 95 ºC, 1 min at 70 ºC (with the reduction of 1 ºC/2 cycles until twentieth cycle, with the temperature stabilized at 60 ºC for the last 15 cycles – touchdown PCR), 1 min at 72 ºC and a final extension of 10 min at 72 ºC. The reactions were performed in a SimpliAmp Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc) and the amplified fragments were visualized in a 1 % agarose gel stained with 0.5 μg/mL of ethidium bromide.

Discussion

We presented two cases of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis. Both cases presented were HIV-negative patients with no other clear immunosuppressive comorbidities. The patient in 2.2 had type 2 diabetes mellitus, but it was well controlled. Both patients had structural alterations in the lung parenchyma caused either by smoking or by sequelae of tuberculosis. However, adherence to treatment was essential for the outcome. In 2.1, irregular use of itraconazole led to worsening of the disease. In 2.2, despite good adherence, the fungus remained active, isolated in culture, despite treatment, indicating that, if interrupted, it could progress with the lung disease and worsen the condition, which is why the patient was kept on suppressive therapy for life.

Pulmonary sporotrichosis is a clinical form that is difficult to suspect and diagnose. In the literature, there are case series and retrospective studies with few cases observed when compared to cases of other clinical forms, thus highlighting the rarity of this presentation (Table 2). The primary pulmonary form is even rarer than pulmonary involvement secondary to infection.

In the 50 years between1960–2010, only 64 cases of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis were reported in the English-language literature [9].

In Rio de Janeiro, from 1998 to 2019, a single-center retrospective study was carried out at a referral center for the treatment of infectious diseases. Of 5264 patients diagnosed with sporotrichosis, only 14 cases (0.27 %) had pulmonary manifestations, and of these 14, only 1 case was primary pulmonary sporotrichosis and the remaining 13 were disseminated, all caused by S. brasiliensis [10].

Another literature review searched for publications of atypical cases of sporotrichosis caused by S. brasiliensis between 1998 and 2023, including mucosal, osteoarticular, hypersensitivity reactions, pulmonary, and meningeal forms. They found 791 publications and among these, only 14 2.2%) were pulmonary forms [11].

Classical risk factors differ from primary to disseminated sporotrichosis. While the first is mostly related to pulmonary chronic conditions and alcohol and tobacco consumption, the second is more prevalent in patients with immunosuppressive conditions, such as HIV/AIDS [6], [9]. In the present article, the two cases reported were HIV-negative individuals with pre-existing structural lung disease, and both were smokers.

S. brasiliensis is considered the most virulent species within the Sporothrix genus, however, the influence of pathogen characteristics in pulmonary manifestations is not defined4. Most primary pulmonary sporotrichosis reported cases are from the United States of America, where S. schenckii in people exposed to soil and plants predominates, but publication bias could also explain the limited number of cases reported in Brazil7, where S. brasiliensis is endemic. The state of Rio de Janeiro, especially the metropolitan area, has experienced significant population growth in recent years. New areas of high population density associated with sanitary conditions and urban disorder may have contributed to the increase in cases of transmission of Sporotrix brasiliensis from animals, especially cats, to humans [12]

Due to the lack of case reports on pulmonary sporotrichosis, we did not find in the literature a cross-reaction of GM aspergillus with pulmonary sporotrichosis. In 2.2, the patient was initially diagnosed with aspergillosis with a GM BAL of 0.97 [13]. The hypothesis is a cross-reaction of GM aspergillus with Sporothrix spp, which produces an important antigen called rhamnomannan, which is not shared with other common mycosis agents [13].

Our cases show how challenging the diagnosis of primary pulmonary sporotrichosis can be, due to the low level of suspicion by most physicians in a patient without skin lesions and who simulates tuberculosis or other fungal infections. This can lead to a delay in diagnosis and the initiation of ineffective empiric therapy. Therefore, primary pulmonary sporotrichosis should be in the differential diagnosis of chronic lung infections, especially in cavitary and nodular diseases when tuberculosis and aspergillosis are excluded [9], [15].
