A Rapid Mapping Review of Medication Burden in Adults with an Intellectual Disability: What We Know and What We Do Not Know
Authors: Sandra Marquis, Renée O’Leary, N Esmé Marquis, Jennifer Baumbusch
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study asked the questions: what is known and what is not known about medication burden in adults with an intellectual disability. We used a rapid mapping review methodology to collate, describe and catalog the wide variety of evidence in this area. Ninety-one studies were included. Study characteristics, indicators of medication burden and sub-populations at increased risk were catalogued in a table and tabulated as tree maps. The authors concluded that there is evidence of a high medication burden in this population, and that the burden is highest in older adults with an ID, those living in supervised housing and those with diagnosed comorbidities and multi-morbidities. Areas that need further exploration are the total burden of medications used, including the age at which medications are first prescribed and the duration of medication use; the long-term effects of a high medication burden; and the long-term effects of anticholinergic burden. In addition, there is little information on the effects of demographic factors such as race and income.Keywords: intellectual disability, medication, psychotropic medication, polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden

Introduction

The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5) defines intellectual disabilities (ID) as neurodevelopmental disorders that begin in childhood and are characterized by intellectual difficulties as well as difficulties in conceptual, social, and practical areas of living.1 The high prevalence of use of medications by people with intellectual disabilities has been of concern for decades.2–5 Some of this medication use is due to co-morbidities such as epilepsy and mental health issues.6 However, there is also evidence of the misuse and over use of medications in this population.3 Recent efforts in the United Kingdom7,8 are a response to years of evidence of medication overuse. Adults with an ID take a wide range of medications including anti-seizure medications9 and a large variety of psychotropic medications.10,11 Medications to treat constipation are also used frequently by people with an ID.12,13 Many of these medications have serious side effects. Side effects can include weight gain,14,15 metabolic syndrome,16 behavioral problems,9,10,17 sedation17 and decreased self-reported quality of life.18 Many of these medications also contribute to a high anticholinergic burden in this population19 which can result in a large number of serious adverse effects.20 Evidence shows increasing use of these medications as people with an ID age.9

There is no agreed upon definition or measurement of medication burden.21–23 Medication burden has been variously described as prevalence of medication use in a population, number and type of medications used, polypharmacy, anticholinergic burden and physical and cognitive side effects of medications.21,23 In all instances, medication burden is understood to be associated with adverse events, reduced functional status and other negative clinical outcomes.24

Many studies of adults with an ID have focused on the use of psychotropic medications and polypharmacy of psychotropic medications16,25,26 and anti-seizure medications.9,27,28 There are fewer studies and reviews of other types of medications in adults with an ID. To account for the various definitions and descriptions of medication burden and to include the wide range of medications used by people with an ID, this review included studies that reported on a number of indicators of medication burden. In addition, the review included any information that identified demographic characteristics of the population that resulted in a higher risk of negative outcomes. Our goal was to identify what we know and what we do not know about medication burden in adults with an ID. This evidence base can facilitate moving forward in improving the health and well-being of adults with an ID.

Materials and Methods

Mapping reviews “collate, describe, and catalog the available evidence relating to the question of interest”.29 Mapping reviews retrieve what is known about a research question and where the evidence comes from.30 A primary function of mapping reviews is to identify gaps in the research29 – what we do not know. Mapping reviews have been categorized as a “big picture review”.31 Unlike a systematic review that synthesizes data, a mapping review provides a catalogue of studies; often comprising 80 or more studies classified by topic or variable.31 For this review, we chose a rapid mapping review as the method best suited for the wide range of indicators of medication burden and to accommodate the inclusion of the 80 or more studies that we anticipated.29

The rapid methodology was conducted in line with recently published recommendations.30,31 For the search processes database searches were limited to two databases. A search of narrative and systematic reviews also took advantage of their systematic searches to identify potential studies.32 Our high reviewer agreement on study inclusion/exclusion (88% on 107 studies) allowed for single reviewer screening of the remaining studies.31 To expedite the review, no grey literature search was conducted. Finally no risk of bias assessments were made for the individual studies, as they are frequently omitted in mapping reviews.31,33

Search

The search was conducted sequentially, with a secondary search of citation chasing made concurrently as studies were included. See Table 1. The first search was in the library of one of the authors. This library was compiled as a reference library of publications on medication use by persons with an ID and consisted of 107 papers from 1998 to 2025. This search was completed on June 6, 2025 and reviewed for updates on July 7, 2025. The second search was run in PubMed. Search syntax: ((((polypharmacy[Title/Abstract]) OR (anticholinergic*[Title/Abstract])) OR (side effects[Title/Abstract])) OR (drug adverse[Title/Abstract])) AND (intellectual disabilit*[Title/Abstract]) Filters: from 2005–2025. The search was conducted on June 9, 2025. As studies were included, they were citation chased in Google Scholar.

Search Processes and Study Inclusion

The library and database searches identified 24 narrative or systematic reviews (Author’s Library 15, PubMed 10, Medline 1). These reviews were used for the third search - a reference list check of the 24 reviews. A list of these literature reviews is in Supplementary Material 1. The fourth and concluding database search was conducted in Medline (Ovid) on July 2, 2025. Search syntax: Search field: abstract; Multiple search: polypharmacy OR anticholinergic OR dose OR interactions OR side effects OR adverse events; Population field: AND intellectual disability* AND adults; Filters: 2005 – current. The variation in retrievals between PubMed and Medline (Ovid) was the result of omitting “adult” from the PubMed search.

Publication dates of 2005 to 2025 were selected because some medications (eg lithium) prescribed 20 years ago are still in use, but are included in very few recent studies. Languages were limited to English or French. No French studies were retrieved.

Study Inclusion

The retrievals were included or excluded by title and abstract based on the following criteria: the study was on adults with an intellectual disability or intellectual/developmental disability (as defined by the study authors) and the research included at least one measurement of medication use. Studies that referenced adults with autism but without an ID were excluded. A study was excluded if it was an individual case study. After the title/abstract inclusion, full papers were reviewed and excluded if they did not contain data on at least one of our medication burden indicators (see Coding).

The author library was 100% dual screened with 88% agreement between reviewers; discrepancies were resolved by discussion. Having obtained a satisfactory level of inter-rater agreement,31 subsequent inclusion/exclusion processes were conducted by one reviewer. All included studies were verified by a second reviewer.

Coding

A study protocol was developed, but not published. An initial coding schema was developed for the protocol, then pilot tested on 15 included studies and revised. Data extracted were: research design and dates of data collection; number and age of participants; participant comorbidities; participant sub-group analyses; the medications reported; and indicators of medication burden. Medication burden indicators were: prevalence of use; polypharmacy; excessive polypharmacy; psychotropic polypharmacy; off label use of psychotropic medications for behavior management; anticholinergic burden; deviations from dosing guidelines including extended use of medications and medication dosages over recommended limits; side effects of medications; and adverse events linked to medication use. Adverse events and side effects were not categorized consistently in the literature, so studies were coded as adverse events – other (AOTH) and adverse events – side effects (ASIDE) based upon the studies’ terminology. For this review, polypharmacy was defined as five or more medications,34,35 excessive polypharmacy was defined as ten or more medications.35–37 Psychotropic polypharmacy was defined as two or more psychotropic medications prescribed concurrently.38,39 Coding was proofread by the coder and 100% checked by the first author.

An overview of the studies was tabulated from the coding; it is reported in the Results section. A study table (Table 2) catalogued demographics, categories of medication burden, sub-groups at increased risk of medication burden and medications referenced in the studies. Results

The search retrieved 91 articles reporting data on medication burden for adults with intellectual disability. The search is presented in Table 1. Table 2 lists the details of the studie. Over half of the studies were published in the past 5 years, 18 were published in 2024–2025. The studies were conducted in 18 countries, including multi-site studies. The majority of studies were conducted in North America and Europe. Fifteen studies had over 1000 participants, extracted from ongoing national surveys and medical record databases; 18 studies had 500–1,000 participants, data often from medical records. Small studies, with 100 or fewer participants, comprised 25% of the studies. Participants with specific comorbidities (seizure disorders, dementia) were examined in 21 studies.

Measures of Medication Burden

Prevalence of Medication Use

Many studies stated that adults with an ID are frequently prescribed medications, however, only ten studies compared the prevalence of medication use by adults with ID to a control group or to the general population. Among these ten studies, Erickson et al64 reported that people with an ID were prescribed significantly more medications and more complex medication regimens compared to the non-ID population. Ward et al119 found that adults with ID were prescribed more central nervous system, gynecological/urinary tract, musculoskeletal, and respiratory medications compared to matched controls. Psychotropic medications in particular were prescribed frequently to adults with an ID. Ward et al119 found that compared to adults without an ID, adults with an ID had greater odds of being prescribed antipsychotics, anti-seizure medications and anxiolytics.

Other categories of medications were also more prevalent in adults with an ID compared to adults without an ID. These medications included gastro-intestinal medications; particularly proton pump inhibitors to treat gastroesophageal reflux disease.100 Erickson et al64 found frequent use of thyroid therapy medications among adults with an ID. Non-prescription medication use was also found to be high among people with an ID.100 Numerous authors noted frequent laxative use although these were not compared to a control group.69,84,97,104,105

Polypharmacy

Of the 91 studies included in this review, 58 (64%) reported that polypharmacy was common among adults with an ID (Figure 1). Polypharmacy encompassed an extensive variety of medications, both prescription and non-prescription.84 The largest study reporting polypharmacy (N=7,936), calculated prevalence of polypharmacy at 64.2% among 55-year-old Swedish adults with an ID.40 In a study in the Netherlands, polypharmacy was found to be a strong predictor of mortality in people with intellectual disabilities.107

Several studies also reported that excessive polypharmacy was common. Erickson et al64 reported that 30% of their participants experienced excessive polypharmacy, using 12 or more medications. O’Connell et al35 found that excessive polypharmacy was significantly associated with frailty.

Psychotropic Polypharmacy

Of the 58 studies that reported polypharmacy, 34 reported specifically on psychotropic polypharmacy (Figure 1). Psychotropic medications included antipsychotics (first and second generation), antidepressants, mood stabilizers, anxiolytics, and medications for the treatment of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). A chart audit of 517 subjects in Ontario Canada reported a 22% prevalence defined as 3 or more psychotropic medications.87

Off-Label Use of Psychotropic Medications

Twenty-four studies reported that a wide variety of psychotropic medications were used “off-label” to treat “challenging behavior”. Challenging behavior was variously described in the literature. Bowring et al47 defined challenging behavior using an inventory that measured stereotyped behavior that occurred hourly or self-injurious and aggressive behavior. Deb et al58 defined challenging behavior using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD10) code of F70; significant impairment of behavior requiring attention or treatment. Sheehan et al108 described challenging behavior as aggression, self-injury, agitation, disruptive or destructive acts, withdrawn behavior, arson and sexual misconduct.

Antipsychotics were the most commonly prescribed medication for challenging behavior.47,52,57,58,108 Antipsychotic medications were also prescribed for behaviors such as restlessness,53 irritability57 and anxiety.57

A wide variety of other medications were used to treat behaviors defined as challenging. Carfi et al48 and Deb et al57 reported that antidepressants were used to treat behavior problems with no diagnosis of depression. Mood stabilizers were also prescribed for challenging behaviors58 and evidence suggests that use of these drugs for this purpose is increasing.58 Anti-seizure medications were prescribed both as a treatment for epilepsy and a treatment for challenging behavior.57 Carfi et al48 found that 10% of a sample of adults with an ID were prescribed anti-seizure medications with no diagnosis of epilepsy. In fact, many researchers found that psychotropics were prescribed for behavior issues more often than for mental illness.52,57,58,77,82,108

Anticholinergic Burden

Ten studies found that medications prescribed to people with an ID often have anticholinergic effects and contribute to a high anticholinergic burden. Ward et al118 reported that compared to the general population, adults with an ID had a significantly higher risk of being prescribed anticholinergic medications and experiencing a high anticholinergic burden. Some of these medications are prescribed specifically for their anticholinergic effects (eg anti-nausea medications and medications used to treat excessive drooling), while other medications have anticholinergic side effects. De Vreese et al55 calculated that psychotropic medications, particularly antipsychotics and anti-seizure medications, were the highest contributor to anticholinergic burden in a group of 276 adults with an ID. De Vreese et al55 also reported that a high anticholinergic burden was significantly more frequent in adults with an ID who used laxatives. Anticholinergic burden was associated with a large variety of side effects, including: dizziness, blurred vision, increased risk of falls, urinary retention, constipation, nausea, dry mouth (leading to increased risk of inflammation and bacterial colonization, respiratory tract infections and periodontal disease), dry eyes, increased heart rate, sedation, cognitive impairment, dementia and delirium.41,97,125

Inappropriate Prescribing

A number of prescribing issues were identified in the studies. In a sample of adults with an ID, 47.5% had experienced prescription errors (dosage errors, unnecessary drug therapy, drug-drug interactions etc).;124 the risk of errors was associated with age, polypharmacy, less severe ID, use of psychotropic medications, Body Mass Index (BMI) and frailty. In a medication review of 55 patients with an ID, 34% had a drug related problem – most often “no indication/unclear indication” for the prescription.106 AlMutairi et al43 found in a study of 677 adults with an ID that they were frequently prescribed proton pump inhibitors without any indication for their use.

The most common prescribing error reported was dosages above the Defined Daily Dosage (DDD) (the assumed average maintenance dose per day for a drug used for its main indication in adults126). Twenty-two papers found errors in providing medications at appropriate dosages, dosages over DDD levels were the most common error. In a population of adults with an ID who were prescribed psychotropic medications, Bowring et al47 found that 30% of people had prescriptions at a level above the DDD. McMahon et al90 reported that in a group of 265 adults prescribed antipsychotics, 23% were prescribed amounts over the DDD.

Unlike other medications reviewed in this paper, evidence indicates that adults with an ID are prescribed fewer pain medications than adults without an ID.74 Kim et al81 reported that during the COVID-19 pandemic people with an ID received approximately 40% fewer opioid prescriptions and lower doses of prescriptions compared to adults without an ID.

Duration

There is evidence that people with an ID remain on some medications for long periods of time. Fourteen studies reported on the duration of medication use within specific time periods, often within the study period. Unfortunately, only one study examined the age at which a medication (lithium) was initially prescribed.79

The most common medications to be given for a long duration were psychotropic medications. Five studies found that adults with an ID had been prescribed psychotropic medications for 10 years or more. In a study of antipsychotic deprescribing among people with an ID in the Netherlands, 54% of the population studied had been prescribed antipsychotics for 10 years or more and 21% had been prescribed antipsychotics for 5–10 years.53 Fodstad et al70 reported that adults with an ID who had taken antipsychotics for longer time periods had an increased risk of tardive dyskinesia compared to those with a shorter duration of use. Contrary to medication recommendations, AlMutairi et al43 found that older adults with an ID were often taking proton pump inhibitors for long periods of time.

Drug-Drug Interactions

Four papers reported frequent drug-drug interactions among adults with an ID.66,68,85,91 In a survey of 598 adults with an ID, Erickson et al66 found that over 80% had experienced at least one drug interaction. Polypharmacy increased the risk of drug interactions. In a population of adults with an ID, McMahon et al91 reported a 0.87 increase in potential drug interactions of clinical significance with every prescribed medication.

Adverse Medication Events

Adverse medication events are significant issues associated with medication use that negatively impact health or well-being. Sixteen studies found adverse effects of medications prescribed to adults with an ID. Schoufour et al107 found that among older adults with an ID, polypharmacy was an independent predictor of mortality. Two studies reported on hospitalizations due to adverse medication events. Erickson et al65 found that adults with an ID had greater odds of hospitalization associated with adverse medication events compared to the adult non-ID population.

Side effects of medications are also an indication of medication burden. Fifteen studies reported on specific medication side effects in participants. A variety of medication side effects were reported. Two studies reported on renal insufficiency, a side effect of chronic lithium administration.79,80 Several studies reported serious side effects of psychotropic medications. These included high sedative load,35 tardive dyskinesia,26,70,89 problems with gait and balance,88 metabolic syndrome,16 weight gain and type 2 diabetes,78 Parkinson-like symptoms,82 and severe constipation.82,104 Ballester et al46 reported hypercholesterolemia and rhabdomyolysis frequently in a group of adults with an ID receiving anti-seizure medications. Other side effects of these drugs included sedation, language impairment60 and challenging behavior.60,94 Winterhalder et al122 reported that use of anti-seizure medications reduced serum vitamin D levels, thereby contributing to the risk of osteoporosis.

An additional contributor to medication burden was the prescription of drugs to counteract side effects of other medications. Koch et al82 found that 11% of adults with an ID taking psychotropic medications also received prescriptions for side effects (Parkinson-like symptoms and obstipation) of the psychotropics. Deb et al56 reported that 23% of adults with an ID taking psychotropic medications took medications for the side effects. Use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were a risk factor for prescriptions of proton pump inhibitors in adults with an ID.43

Evidence also indicated that adults with an ID may be more prone to serious side effects of prescription medications compared to adults without an ID. For example, Sheehan et al109 found that adults with an ID who were taking antipsychotics had greater odds of movement side effects such as acute dystonia, akathisia, parkinsonism and tardive dyskinesia, compared to a matched control group of adults without an ID who were also taking antipsychotic medications.

Participant Sub-Group Analyses – Populations at Increased Risk

Fifty-nine of the studies conducted one or more sub-group analyses on their findings based on participant demographics. A statistically significant effect of increased prevalence or risk of the medication burden under study was reported for age, severity of disability, residence in a supervised facility, mental health diagnoses, gender, diagnoses of challenging behavior, multimorbidity, income or socio-economic status and race. These variables are mapped in Figure 2.

Age

A statistically significant effect of increased prevalence or risk of medication burden was reported for age in thirty-two studies. Ten studies reported an increase in the use of psychotropic medications with age.47,48,55,58,67,70,72,84,108,123 Polypharmacy was also associated with increasing age.40,76 In a group of adults with an ID who were aged 45 to 88 years, Garcia-Dominguez et al72 found an increase in psychotropic polypharmacy with age. In addition, with increasing age, there was evidence of an increase in the risk of serious side effects of psychotropic medications127 and an increase in medication errors.124 AlMutairi43 found an increase in the prevalence of prescriptions for proton pump inhibitors by age in adults with an ID.

Severity of the ID

A statistically significant effect of increased prevalence of medications or risk of a medication burden due to severity of disability was reported in 22 studies. The majority of studies reported that severe or profound intellectual disability was related to higher risk of being prescribed a psychotropic medication47,58 and of experiencing polypharmacy.40,76 Use of proton pump inhibitors was higher in adults with severe intellectual disability.43 Matson et al127 found an increased risk of tardive dyskinesia in people with profound ID compared to people with mild or moderate ID. However, Garcia-Dominguez et al72 found that adults with milder intellectual disability were twice as likely to experience psychotropic polypharmacy as those with severe intellectual disability.

Residence

A statistically significant increased prevalence of medication or risk of medication burden was reported by place of residence in 17 studies. Higher polypharmacy was associated with living in supervised housing.40 The risk of being prescribed an antipsychotic was related to living in a congregate setting.47,73,82 The risk of being prescribed more than one psychotropic medication at a time was also related to living in a group home.71,72 De Vreese et al55 reported that anticholinergic burden was higher in those living in community housing or a nursing home compared to adults with an ID living independently or with family members. AlMutairi et al43 found a higher risk of prescriptions for proton pump inhibitors for adults with an ID living in residential care homes compared to those living independently or with family. The risk of medication errors in people with an ID was also associated with living in supervised housing.42,86,87,90

Gender

A statistically significant increased prevalence of medications or risk of medication burden was reported by gender in fifteen studies. Bowring et al47 and Gomes et al73 reported that males with an ID had a higher risk than females of being prescribed an antipsychotic medication. However, Lunsky and Modi87 found that psychotropic polypharmacy was more common among women compared to men with an ID. Lim et al84 found higher prevalence of the use antipsychotic, antidepressant, laxative, and antihypertensive medications in women with an ID compared to men with an ID. One study reported a higher risk of an adverse medication event resulting in hospitalization in adult females compared to adult males with an ID.65 Higher anticholinergic burden in females compared to males with an ID was also reported.118

Mental Health

Nineteen studies found that a mental health diagnosis was associated with various measures of medication burden. These included polypharmacy,50 frequent use of psychotropic prescriptions,16,59,72,73,77,82,86,116 psychotropic polypharmacy,71,87,90,98 anticholinergic burden41,55,97 and a high Drug Burden Index.117

Challenging Behavior

Sixteen studies found an association between the diagnosis of challenging behavior and psychotropic medication prescriptions. For example, Bowring et al47 reported that adults with challenging behavior were nearly twice as likely to be prescribed psychotropic medication and three times as likely to be prescribed antipsychotics compared with adults with an ID who did not present with challenging behavior.

Comorbidity/Multimorbidity

Eleven studies found a link between comorbidity or multimorbidity and polypharmacy. Co-morbid seizure disorders were a strong predictor of polypharmacy in one study.50 Comorbid seizure disorders were also a predictor of high Drug Burden Index.117 Autism spectrum disorder was associated with use of psychotropic medications in several studies.101,103,108 One study reported an association between multimorbidity and the use of psychotropic medications.68 It is not unexpected that multimorbidity should be associated with medication use, however, there was also evidence that people with an ID receive a greater number of medications per chronic condition when compared to adults without an ID.100

Income/Social Economic Status

Very few studies (5) reported on the variable income or social economic status. Gomes et al73 found no differences in prescribing or polypharmacy associated with income levels However, McMahon et al90 reported that lower social economic status was related to a higher risk of polypharmacy and psychotropic polypharmacy. Erickson et al65 found a higher risk of hospitalization due to an adverse medication event in adults with an ID with lower incomes compared to adults with higher income.

Race

Only three studies examined the variable of race. Erickson et al65 found that non-white people with an ID had fewer hospitalizations due to an adverse medication event. Yoshida et al38 reported that among autistic adults, race other than white had significantly lower odds of being prescribed a psychotropic medication or experiencing psychotropic polypharmacy. Kim et al81 also reported that among adults with an ID, races other than white received fewer opioid prescriptions.

Discussion

This paper used a rapid mapping review methodology to elucidate what is known and what is not known about medication burden and its effects on adults with an ID. Ninety-one papers were reviewed. These papers covered a wide range of medications, measures of medication burden and topics of concern.

What We Know

Adults with an ID Have a High Prevalence of Medication Use

Evidence shows that compared to adults without an ID, adults with an ID are at risk of a higher prevalence of medication use, as well as experiencing more polypharmacy, excessive polypharmacy, more frequent use of psychotropic medications and more frequent psychotropic polypharmacy. These findings confirm the reports of other literature reviews on laxatives,12 psychotropic medications11 and anti-seizure medications.9

Adults with an ID are Prescribed off-Label Medications for Behavior Control

Although use of psychotropic medications is related to a mental health diagnosis and use of anti-seizure medication is related to seizure disorders in adults with an ID, there is also considerable evidence that these medications are frequently used “off-label” for behavior control. The use of this “treatment” occurs despite lack of agreement in defining “challenging behavior”. Psychotropic medications are used to treat a wide range of behaviors including irritability, stereotypical behaviors, violence and self-injury. However, studies have found that off label use has little128 to no effectiveness129 in reducing challenging behavior. There is also evidence that in many cases options to treat challenging behaviors, other than medications, have not been considered for people with an ID.95

Adults with an ID are at Risk for a High Anticholinergic Burden

Adults with an ID are frequently prescribed a large variety of medications that contribute to anticholinergic burden. Some medications commonly used by people with an ID are prescribed for their anticholinergic effect. For example, scopolamine is prescribed to treat nausea and vomiting. Atropine, scopolamine and glycopyrrolate are prescribed for chronic severe drooling.130,131 Severe drooling can be the result of neurodevelopmental conditions such as brain injury and cerebral palsy, but severe drooling can also be a side effect of psychotropic medications such as benzodiazepines and atypical antipsychotics.132,133 Many psychotropic medications, have anticholinergic side effects that contribute to anticholinergic burden.

Anticholinergic burden has a wide range of effects on health. Recent research has found a significant relationship between anticholinergic burden and cognitive impairment or dementia in the general population.134,135 However, there is very little research on polypharmacy of medications with anticholinergic effects and the long term effects of anticholinergic burden in people with an ID.136

Adults with an ID Frequently Experience Inappropriate Prescribing

Inappropriate prescribing, particularly dosage amounts over Defined Daily Dosage levels, were commonly reported in this population.

Adults with an ID are at High Risk of Adverse Medication Events

As a result of the high prevalence of medication use, polypharmacy and prescribing errors, adults with an ID experience a wide range of adverse medication events including detrimental side effects and medication related hospitalization.

Particular Sub-Groups of Adults with an ID Have a Higher Risk of Medication Burden

People with an ID at the highest risk for polypharmacy, over medication and serious side effects are those with multiple comorbidities, behavior challenges, mental health diagnoses, those living in congregate settings and older adults. Older age is of particular concern. People without an ID experience increased polypharmacy as they age.137 However, compared to older people without an ID, older people with an ID may have been on medications and experienced polypharmacy for longer periods of time; they are also more likely to have problems identifying and communicating adverse medication events.

What We Do Not Know

What Is the True Scale of Medication Burden Among Adults with an ID?

No studies encompassed the total overall medication burden in adults with an ID. The majority of studies looked only at psychotropics or used a single indicator of medication burden such as polypharmacy or the Anticholinergic Burden calculator.114 Only one study used multiple measures of medication burden for a population of older adults.35

Furthermore, the literature provides evidence that medication problems and adverse medication events may be under-reported for adults with an ID. This is due to communication difficulties, lack of training of health care professionals, lack of patient self-reports and reporting of only immediately obvious side effects (eg vomiting).27,138

At What Age are Medications First Prescribed and What Is the Duration of Their Use?

Very few papers in this review looked at duration of medication use. Only one study reported on the age at which people with an ID were first prescribed a medication with serious and long-lasting side effects.79 Several recent studies have shown that a large variety of psychotropic medications are prescribed at an early age to people with an ID.139–143 However, there is little information on medications other than psychotropics. No studies in this review examined the age of initial use and duration of use of other medications that contribute to anticholinergic burden, for example laxatives.

What are the Long-Term Effects of a High Anticholinergic Burden in Adults with an ID?

Recent research has found a significant relationship between anticholinergic burden and cognitive impairment or dementia in the general population.134,135 However, there is very little research on polypharmacy of medications with anticholinergic effects and the long term effects of anticholinergic burden in people with an ID.136

What are the Risks and Benefits of off-Label Use of Psychotropics and Anti-Seizure Medications for Adults with an ID?

Several literature reviews have described off-label use of psychotropic and anti-epilepsy medications by people with an ID, and concluded that there is insufficient information on the results of long-term use of these medications.11,14,18 Furthermore, the risks and benefits of more than one psychotropic medication and differing combinations of psychotropic medications are largely unknown in the ID population.10

Are Adults with an ID Under-Prescribed Pain Medication?

There are indications in the literature that people with an ID receive less pain medication than people without an ID.144–146 In a systematic review, Pacitti et al147 found that adults with an ID receive simple analgesic medication more frequently than other types of pain medications compared to the general population. Poor pain management may be due to under-reporting of pain and difficulties with pain assessment in adults with an ID.148,149 Amor-Salamanca et al150 found that compared to people without an ID, people with a severe ID attending an emergency department were significantly less likely to be diagnosed with pain. However, Wark et al151 studied pain in older adults with an ID compared to adults without an ID and reported a higher prevalence of pain in the adults with an ID; the adults with an ID also had higher rates of osteoarthritis, falls and oral health problems.

How Do Other Demographic Factors Such as Income and Race Impact Medication Burden in Adults with an ID?

Although a number of studies looked at the effect of increasing age, gender and residence on medication burden, very few studies included other variables such as income and race. These social determinants have important effects on health and understanding of medication burden is incomplete without a consideration of these factors.

Why Aren’t Medication Reviews for Adults with an ID More Common?

Thirty-two papers in this review recommended regular medication reviews for people with an ID. Three studies reported that medication reviews were successful in reducing inappropriate prescribing and the harmful side effects of polypharmacy.74,106,115 However, there is little evidence that medication reviews are occurring as recommended or that the medication regimens of adults with an ID are improving. Many factors may be preventing these reviews, but have yet to be rigorously explored. Ableism within society and particularly within health care professions may be a factor. Healthcare professionals lack knowledge and experience of the health of people with an ID.152–154 Dunn et al155 also observed that health professionals lack adequate training in valid informed consent and making reasonable adjustments for people with intellectual disability, and continue to perpetuate assumptions of incapacity.

Another barrier is how physicians are paid and funding formulas that may affect the frequency of medication reviews for people with an ID. For example, physicians in Ontario, Canada were more likely to conduct health checks of ID patients when they had a billing code that reflected the time needed for the visit.156 Pharmacists also lack training and experience in appropriate pharmaceutical care and de-prescribing for people with an ID.157,158 Thayer et al115 recommended more collaboration between physicians and pharmacists to ensure effective medication reviews and de-prescribing.

Medication reviews are also hampered by a lack of appropriate screening tools and protocols for deprescribing for people with an ID.21,159,160 O’Dwyer et al161 concluded that screening for inappropriate prescribing and polypharmacy was inadequate for people with an ID and that instruments for screening used in the non-ID population were not suitable for use in the ID population.

At the same time, adults with an ID and their care givers have reported that they have little knowledge about possible adverse side effects or drug-drug interactions of their medications.99,158,162 Sheehan et al163 and Crossley and Withers164 observed that people with intellectual disabilities were highly compliant with psychotropic medication prescribing and unaware of their right to be involved in medication decisions. However, people with an ID are demanding more information and better care. They want to be included in decisions regarding their medications.54,165,166

Strengths

This study is the first to review a wide variety of medications and medication use, assessing overall medication burden in adults with an ID. The majority of previous studies have focused exclusively on psychotropic medications. Many previous studies have also not considered the social determinants of health such as gender, age, race and income on medication use in adults with an ID. This study can therefore serve as a useful reference catalogue for researchers, clinicians and policy makers seeking information on total medication burden in adults with an ID.

We were able to conduct a robust rapid review because of our review team.33 Internal expertise in the topic and the initial author library provided an initial estimate of the number of studies and their scope. Internal expertise on medications facilitated understanding of medications and medication use. A methods expert guided the review in best practices. Some members of the review team had experience working together on two prior reviews. Finally, we had our final manuscript reviewed by a pharmacist.

Limitations

Limitations of this review come from both the methodology of the review and the limitations of the studies included in the review. Many of the studies covered in this review used convenience samples of people with an ID who were receiving care (eg people with an ID who were hospitalized or receiving medications for mental health diagnoses). Only ten studies used a control group. Without a risk of bias assessment, accuracy of the individual studies is unknown. The majority of studies were conducted in North America and Europe. There was very little information from countries outside these two areas. The majority of studies examined the use of psychotropic medications; there was considerably less information available on other types of medications such as analgesics and medications to treat constipation.

The methodology limited the number of databases searched which may have resulted in some studies being overlooked. Mapping reviews do not provide a comparison or analysis of differences between the studies’ findings, limiting the analysis to a tabulation of studies.33 Lastly, this review excluded studies that looked at medications used by autistic people who do not have an ID. There is considerably more research in this area that could potentially add to the findings in this review.

Conclusions

Because of the high prevalence of use of medications, polypharmacy, excessive polypharmacy, psychotropic polypharmacy, high anticholinergic burden and errors in prescribing, adults with an ID are at high risk of experiencing serious harm from their medications. Harms include detrimental effects on their physical and mental health, reduced quality of life and shorter lifespans. Concerns about these harms have been discussed for some time, however, little progress has been made in improving the medication burden borne by adults with an ID. Progress is hampered by lack of accurate information on total medication burden. Particularly there is a lack of information on the contribution of medications other than psychotropics to medication burden in this population. As well there is a lack of information on the age at which medications are first prescribed and the length of time that people with an ID are taking medications. There is a lack of appropriate screening tools and protocols to assess medication burden in this population. There is a lack of alternative therapies to medications offered appropriately to people with an ID. Lastly, people with an ID are historically subject to societal and health professional ableist attitudes which exclude them from input into their own health.

Now is the time to change the focus from repeatedly measuring a limited scope of medication issues in adults with an ID to making real changes to improve their health and lives. People with an ID need accurate information regarding their medications and must have input into their own health. Medication burden must be assessed with measures of all medications and the duration of their use. Medication reviews must be conducted regularly and appropriately to prevent over-medication, inappropriate use of medications for behavior control, and adverse side effects. The health and well-being of adults with an ID depend upon progress in research, policy and practice to prevent medication burden.
