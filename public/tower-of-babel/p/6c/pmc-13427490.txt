Low number of chikungunya cases in the Yogyakarta Region, Java, Indonesia – hallmark of a public health problem in the making, or not?
Authors: Eggi Arguni, Martin P. Grobusch
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Arboviral diseases dominate the acute febrile illness differential diagnoses list on Java, Indonesia, with dengue by far predominating. A recent study among 8000 cases in the greater Yogyakarta area yielded 400 dengue-PCR-positive cases [1]. Of note, in the same cross-sectional sample, we report here that only four patients of the remaining 7600 were chikungunya-PCR-positive. The epidemiological pattern of chikungunya and its burden of disease still need to be elucidated. Whilst Bali is a known hotspot of chikungunya transmission with occasional outbreaks (2021–2022: 20.6 cases/100,000 population) [2] posing a health risk for both the local population and travellers alike, public health data and peer-reviewed publications draw a patchier picture for neighbouring Java, with many unknowns (see Fig. 1).Fig. 1The map of the Republic of Indonesia depicts the provinces of North Sulawesi, South Sulawesi, and Bali; the cities of Ambon, Banjarmasin, and Batam; and the Yogyakarta cities, where the chikungunya studies were done.

Establishing the true burden of chikungunya on Java is complicated by several intersecting public health challenges. First, chikungunya shares an essentially indistinguishable clinical presentation with dengue and other causes of acute febrile illness. So, in settings where aetiological confirmation is not routinely pursued, cases are readily misclassified or missed altogether. Second, passive, hospital-based surveillance - the backbone of national reporting - captures only care-seeking, symptomatic disease, while a substantial proportion of chikungunya infections are thought to remain subclinical; national case counts reported to Indonesia’s Ministry of Health have fluctuated markedly from year to year (2974 cases nationally in 2022, rising to 6049 cases across 29 provinces in 2023, before falling to 571 cases in seven provinces in 2024 [3]); a pattern more consistent with uneven detection than with true epidemic dynamics. Third, RT-PCR - as used in the present study - only captures infection during the brief viraemic window of a few days, so a single cross-sectional sampling frame will systematically underestimate cumulative exposure; seroprevalence studies, better suited to capturing past infection, are themselves susceptible to cross-reactivity with other alphaviruses and have rarely been performed on Java. Where they have been conducted elsewhere in the archipelago, results vary strikingly: adult IgG seroprevalence exceeded 50% in North and South Sulawesi [4], yet chikungunya was rarely detected in multi-site, hospital-based sampling from Ambon, Banjarmasin and Batam [5], underscoring that CHIKV transmission intensity in Indonesia is patchy and, outside of Java and Bali, poorly characterised.

Few cases in a large cohort of febrile patients therefore raise several questions of public health relevance. First, might limited recognition have led to under-diagnosis in the past, with asymptomatic infections on a considerable scale having produced a degree of population immunity and hence limited vulnerability? Or has chikungunya been by-and-large absent, irrespective of the close proximity to neighbouring Bali, with a large-scale outbreak remaining a possible public health threat of note in the near future? One way to find out is to further examine the seroprevalence of past chikungunya infection in the collection of the abovementioned 8000 clinical specimens. A random-sample assessment is currently underway to provide a seroprevalence estimate for past chikungunya exposure and resulting seroprotection (Arguni et al., unpublished).

These uncertainties carry direct consequences for vaccine policy. Two chikungunya vaccines have now reached licensure - the live-attenuated IXCHIQ and the virus-like-particle vaccine Vimkunya - but with the first, post-marketing surveillance identified serious adverse events, predominantly in older adults, prompting a temporary restriction on IXCHIQ use in this age group in 2025 and its voluntary withdrawal from the United States market in January 2026, while the vaccine remains in use elsewhere, including through a Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations-supported access programme for low- and middle-income countries and a pilot vaccination programme in Brazil. Deploying either vaccine at scale on a population-dense island such as Java, where the true incidence and age distribution of infection remain unknown, therefore poses a genuine dilemma: without reliable burden and seroprevalence data, it is not possible to model the number needed to vaccinate, to weigh the modest but real safety signal against the disease averted, or to make a defensible cost-effectiveness case to funders and policymakers.

Resolving whether the low PCR-positivity reported here reflects true rarity, a high degree of pre-existing population immunity, or under-ascertainment is thus not a matter of academic interest alone, but a prerequisite for rational, evidence-based public health decision-making on population-rich, economically vulnerable Java island and beyond - particularly with an effective vaccination now on the horizon.
