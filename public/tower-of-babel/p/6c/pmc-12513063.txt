Playing sports in higher education as a protective factor against social jetlag: An exploratory study
Authors: Guilherme Agra Costa, Michael A. Grandner, Daniel Ruivo Marques
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudent-athletes face challenges balancing academic and athletic commitments, often leading to dysfunctional sleep patterns. Social jetlag - a misalignment between biological and social clocks - has emerged as a potential contributor to mental and physical strain. The current study is aimed at determining the social jetlag frequency and its associations with other sleep and health variables among student-athletes in higher education.MethodsSixty-seven European Portuguese student-athletes (mean age = 21.4 years) were evaluated for chronotype, social jetlag, sleep effort, daytime sleepiness, psychological distress, and perceived academic and athletic performance.ResultsSocial jetlag was computed using a corrected midpoint of sleep approach (SJLsc). Most participants (62.7 %) reported moderate social jetlag (1–2 h), and only 6 % of the sample presented high social jetlag (>2 h). Higher social jetlag was negatively correlated with academic performance and positively associated with evening chronotypes. Although group differences across social jetlag levels and chronotypes were not statistically significant, there is a trend suggesting morning types experience lower misalignment. Compared to normative data derived from Portuguese samples, participants showed higher sleep effort and lower perceived daytime sleepiness.ConclusionsThe prevalence of significant social jetlag among student-athletes was low, suggesting that participation in sports may serve as a protective factor, possibly due to greater health awareness and monitoring. However, further studies with larger samples are needed.

Introduction

Student-athletes, defined as full-time university students who engage in sports at a competitive level [1,2], must balance academic responsibilities with the physical and psychological demands of training and competition. This dual career can promote health and personal development but also increases vulnerability to stress, fatigue, and mental health challenges [3,4]. Sleep plays a crucial role in physical recovery and cognitive performance, yet student-athletes frequently report insufficient sleep due to conflicting demands [5,6]. Chronic sleep deprivation has been linked to poorer athletic recovery, higher injury risk, and reduced academic outcomes such as impaired memory, concentration, and learning [7,8]. Studies have also identified a high prevalence of insomnia, daytime fatigue, and poor sleep quality among university athletes, often linked to demanding schedules and high levels of stress [9,10]. From a chronobiological perspective, sleep patterns are regulated by circadian rhythms, which align biological processes with environmental cues [11,12]. A misalignment between internal biological time and external social demands - especially in individuals with evening chronotypes - can result in a condition known as social jetlag [13,14]. This phenomenon has been associated with cognitive impairments, mood disorders, and adverse health outcomes [15,16].

Although the effects of social jetlag have been explored in general student populations and in elite athletes separately, its impact on student-athletes remains largely unexamined. Research suggests that athletes with higher social jetlag may show reduced neuromuscular performance and recovery potential [17]. In non-athlete university students, irregular wake times and mismatched sleep schedules have been identified as key contributors to increased social jetlag [18]. However, there appears to be a lack of studies specifically addressing social jetlag in student-athletes - a group that may face unique risks due to rigid training and academic schedules. A recent search in PubMed using the terms “social jetlag” and “student-athletes” revealed no relevant results (search conducted on April 9, 2025). This represents a critical gap in the literature, especially considering that student-athletes are subjected to structured routines that may exacerbate circadian misalignment and its consequences.

Given the absence of targeted research, this study aims to examine the frequency and effects of social jetlag in a sample of higher education student-athletes in Portugal. We hypothesize that this population experiences levels of social jetlag comparable to or higher than those of regular university students, due to the cumulative demands of academic, training, and competition schedules. Based on the theory of allostasis [19], we expect that chronic circadian misalignment may lead to increased allostatic load, manifesting as sleep dissatisfaction, greater sleep onset difficulties, higher psychological distress, and lower self-perceived athletic performance [20,21]. We also hypothesize that evening-type student-athletes are more likely to experience higher levels of social jetlag, given their natural tendency to delay sleep and the greater mismatch with early academic or training obligations.

Participants

The sample comprised a convenience sample of 67 student-athletes who attended a higher education institution in Portugal. Approximately 50 % of the participants were female (50.7 %), single (98.5 %) and aged between 18 and 50 years old (M = 21.4; SD = 4.18). Sixty-five (97 %) of the participants were European Portuguese. Regarding academic qualifications, most of the members attended a bachelor's degree (70.1 %) and a master's degree (28.4 %). As to the field of study, most participants were students of Sciences and Engineering (37.3 %), followed by Health and Biomedical Sciences (22.4 %) and Behavioral Sciences (16.4 %). Measures

Sociodemographic characteristics

Participants completed a general socio-demographic questionnaire that collected information on age, sex, marital status, nationality, level of education (cycle of studies) and general field of study.

Satisfaction and perception of academic performance

Participants also reported their self-perceived academic performance at the time of survey completion and their level of satisfaction with this performance. Academic performance was assessed using a Likert-type scale with the following categories: less than 10 values, 10–11 values, 12–13 values, 14–15 values, 16–17 values, and 18 or higher values. In Portugal, the grading scale ranges from 0 to 20. Satisfaction was rated on a five-point Likert-type scale: “Very unsatisfied”, “Unsatisfied”, “Neither satisfied nor unsatisfied”, “Satisfied”, and “Very satisfied”.

Sports related questions

The questionnaire also included sports-related questions concerning the type of sport played by the participants that granted them the student-athlete status, the number of years they had been engaged in that sport, their weekly training hours, and their typical training schedule. Training schedules were analyzed using a multiple-response format, in which participants could select all options applicable to their situation: early morning, mid-morning, mid-day, mid-afternoon, late afternoon, and early evening.

Body mass index and substance consumption habits

To assess health-related factors, participants self-reported their height and weight, which were used to calculate their Body Mass Index (BMI). Additionally, they responded to three substance use questions adapted from Gomes [22], concerning the average number of cigarettes smoked per day, the average number of alcoholic beverages consumed daily, and the average daily intake of caffeinated drinks.

Social jetlag and sleep satisfaction

Social jetlag was assessed through participants’ self-reported sleep-wake schedules using questions adapted from Ghotbi et al. [23] concerning to an ultra-short version of the Munich ChronoType questionnaire. Participants were asked the time they went to sleep and woke up both on workdays (i.e., academic/training days) and free days (i.e., days without academic or training obligations). These data were used to compute a corrected measure of social jetlag based on the formula proposed by Jankowski [24]. To obtain a biologically meaningful estimate of social jetlag, the sleep-corrected midpoint of sleep was calculated for both free days (MSFsc) and workdays (MSWsc). The formula used was SJLsc = |MSFsc – MSWsc|, where MSFsc = sleep onset on free days + half of the average weekly sleep duration; MSWsc = sleep onset on workdays + half of the average weekly sleep duration. This corrected approach (SJLsc) overcomes limitations of the original social jetlag measure by accounting for oversleeping on free days, which is often a compensation for accumulated sleep debt [24]. Thus, SJLsc provides a more accurate representation of circadian misalignment. For the purposes of our study, and in line with most of the literature, social jetlag was considered significant or chronic when it was greater than or equal to 2 h [24,25].

Sleep satisfaction was assessed using a five-point Likert-type scale: “0-Very unsatisfied”, “1-Unsatisfied”, “2-Neither satisfied nor unsatisfied”, “3-Satisfied”, and “4-Very satisfied”. This item was adapted from the Insomnia Severity Index [26].

Sports Performance Perception Questionnaire (SPPQ, [27])

The perception of sports performance was assessed using the Sports Performance Perception Questionnaire (SPPQ), developed by Gomes et al. [27]. This instrument consists of 10 items divided into two dimensions: perception of individual performance and perception of team performance, with five items assigned to each dimension. For the purposes of our study, only the five items related to individual performance were utilized, which measure an individual's perception of their personal performance achievements. The instrument has two versions depending on the research objectives: a retrospective version, which assesses the individual's perception of previous performance, and an anticipatory version, which evaluates their perception of future performance. The present study used the “Retrospective Version – Until this moment”. Responses are provided on a five-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree; 5 = Strongly Agree), with final scores calculated as the means of the responses. Higher scores reflect a greater perception of the individual's performance.

In the present study the Cronbach's alpha was .88 and McDonald's omega was .90.

Reduced Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ; [28]; European Portuguese adaptation by Ref. [29])

Chronotype was assessed using the reduced version of the Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ), originally developed by Adan & Almirall [28] and later adapted to European Portuguese by Loureiro & Garcia-Marques [29]. The instrument comprises five items with scores ranging from 4 to 25, reflecting a continuum from low to high morningness. Based on these scores, participants can be classified into three chronotypes [28]: Evening-type (4–11), Neutral-type (12–17), and Morning-type (18–25). In the present study the Cronbach's alpha was .61 and McDonald's omega was .62.

Glasgow Sleep Effort Scale (GSES; [30]; European Portuguese adaptation by Ref. [31])

The Glasgow Sleep Effort Scale (GSES) measures the effort to initiate sleep [30]. It consists of seven items referring to sleep patterns from the past week, evaluated on a 3-point Likert scale (0 = Not at all, 1 = To some extent, 2 = Very much). Total scores range from 0 to 14 and are calculated by summing the item scores with higher scores indicating greater sleep effort [31,32]. In the present study the Cronbach's alpha was .80 and McDonald's omega was .79.

Daytime Sleepiness Perception Scale - revised (DSPS-R; [34])

The DSPS-R is a brief three item measure assessing daytime sleepiness perception as a trait, thus, no specific time frame is indicated in the instructions. This instrument is a revised shorter version of the DSPS-4 [33]. Score results are obtained from summing the 3 items, resulting in a total composite score [34]. The available response options are never (0), rarely (1), often (2), almost always (3), and always (4) with scores ranging from 0 to 12 points [34]. In the present study both the Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega values were .85.

Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS; [35]; European Portuguese adaptation by Ref. [36])

The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) is meant to provide a measurement of the subject's general level of daytime sleepiness propensity [35]. Participants rate, on a scale from 0 (would never doze) to 3 (high chance of dozing), their likelihood of dozing off or falling asleep in eight specific situations, based on their typical lifestyle and recent experiences. The total score, ranging from 0 to 24, differentiates individuals and diagnostic groups across the full spectrum of daytime sleepiness. Higher scores on the ESS denote a greater level of daytime sleepiness propensity. In the present study the Cronbach's alpha was .74 and McDonald's omega was .75.

Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10; [37]; European Portuguese adaptation by Ref. [38])

The Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10) is a 10 item self-report instrument designed to assess non-specific psychological distress. Originally developed by Kessler et al. [37] and later adapted to European Portuguese by Pereira et al. [38], the scale evaluates the frequency of symptoms associated with anxiety and depression over the past 30 days. Participants respond to each item using a 5-point Likert scale (1 = None of the time to 5 = All of the time), yielding total scores ranging from 10 to 50. Higher scores on the K10 reflect greater levels of psychological distress. In the present study both the Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega values were .92.

Procedure

Data collection took place between February 7th and March 10th, 2025. To be included in this study, participants must be 18 years of age or older, be enrolled in higher education, and hold official “student-athlete” status. Potential participants were invited to take part in this study by publicizing it in Portuguese higher education institutions, through posters, flyers and on social networks. Additionally, the study was shared with athletes of various sports teams representing some of these institutions, and coaches who were asked to pass the information on to their athletes. The questionnaire was completed online using the LimeSurvey platform. It should be noted that a total of 152 individuals initiated the questionnaire; however, only 67 participants (44.1 %) completed it in full.

In line with the ethical principles of scientific research with humans, the first page of the questionnaire contained a declaration of informed consent, accompanied by a brief mention of the general objectives of the study and the estimated response time. It was also stated that all answers provided would be treated confidentially and used exclusively for research purposes, reserving the right for participants to stop taking part in the study at any time. The following pages revealed the socio-demographic form and the measurement scales one by one. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee (67-CED/2024) and by the data protection officer of the University where the first and the last authors belong.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS® software version 30.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) for Windows. Descriptive statistics, including absolute and relative frequencies, means, and standard deviations, were computed to characterize the sample and provide a preliminary overview of the data distribution. The reliability of the instruments was estimated by analyzing their internal consistency, calculating the respective Cronbach's alpha and McDonald's omega coefficients. To examine relationships between social jetlag and other continuous variables (e.g., sleep effort, psychological distress, academic and sports performance), Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated [39]. To interpret the strength of the correlation coefficients (r), the present study adopted the guidelines proposed by Cohen [40]. According to these conventions, correlations were classified as very small when r < .10, small when .10 ≤ r < .30, moderate when .30 ≤ r < .50, and large when r ≥ .50 [39].

The Kruskal–Wallis H test, which is a non-parametric alternative to one-way ANOVA, was applied to examine differences in social jetlag across chronotype groups. This method allows for the comparison of independent samples without assuming a normal distribution, ensuring the reliability of the findings under non-parametric conditions [39]. This technique was used in our study due to the relatively small sample size of some subgroups. In addition, one-sample t-tests were used to compare the mean scores between the current sample and normative samples. Effect sizes for between-group comparisons were reported using Cohen's d [40].

It is important to note that one of the participants in our sample was 50 years old, which is significantly above the average age of a typical higher education student. We repeated the analyses excluding this participant and verified that the results were not affected. Therefore, given the relatively small sample size, we decided to retain this participant's data in the presentation of results.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level set at p < .05 [39].

Results

Descriptive analysis

In terms of the reduced Morningness-Eveningness Questionnaire (rMEQ), participants presented a mean score of 13.78 (SD = 3.01), ranging from 8 to 22. For the Glasgow Sleep-Effort Scale (GSES), the mean score was 4.60 (SD = 3.07), with a minimum of 0 and a maximum of 12. Daytime sleepiness perception, measured by the DSPS-R, had a mean score of 4.43 (SD = 2.15), with scores ranging from 1 to 12. Daytime sleepiness propensity, assessed by the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS), showed a mean of 8.60 (SD = 4.23), with values between 0 and 18. Finally, psychological distress, measured by the Kessler Psychological Distress Scale (K10), presented a mean of 20.03 (SD = 7.20), ranging from 11 to 43.

In addition, health-related behaviors were analyzed. The participants’ mean Body Mass Index (BMI) was 22.70 (SD = 2.97), with values spanning from 15.4 to 33.6. According to the National Institutes of Health, the BMI categories are defined as follows: underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5–24.9), overweight (25–29.9), and obesity (≥30). This indicates that, on average, the student-athletes in this sample fall within the normal BMI range, suggesting generally healthy weight status according to these established guidelines. Cigarette consumption was low, with a mean of .30 cigarettes consumed daily (SD = 1.44), ranging from 0 to 10. Alcohol consumption showed a mean of .13 beverages consumed daily (SD = .55), ranging from 0 to 4. Regarding caffeine consumption, the mean was 1.01 beverages consumed daily (SD = 1.19), with values between 0 and 5.

Of the 67 participants, a total of 96 training sessions were reported, suggesting that several participants engaged in multiple training sessions or trained at different times across the day. The most common training periods were late afternoon (58.2 % of participants) and early evening (49.3 %). Training during early morning hours was reported by 11.9 % of the participants, mid-morning by 9.0 %, midday by 1.5 %, and mid-afternoon by 13.4 %.

In terms of performance-related perceptions, participants reported a mean score of 3.76 (SD = 1.12) on the Sports Performance Perception Questionnaire (SPPQ), with scores ranging from 1.40 to 6.00, indicating a moderate to high perception of individual performance. Regarding academic performance, the most prevalent grade range reported was 14–15 values (43.3 % of the sample), followed by 16–17 values (25.4 %) and 12–13 values (23.9 %). A smaller percentage of participants reported their grades to be in the 10–11 range (6.0 %), and only 1 participant reporting a grade average equal to or above 18 on a 0–20 scale. Concerning satisfaction with academic performance, participants described themselves as satisfied (53.7 %) or very satisfied (13.4 %). Additionally, 19.4 % reported being neither satisfied nor dissatisfied, 10.4 % reported being dissatisfied, and 3.0 % reported being very dissatisfied. These distributions suggest an overall positive perception of academic performance, but with some variation throughout the sample.

A moderate positive correlation was found between caffeine and alcohol consumption (r = .46, p < .01) indicating that individuals who consumed more caffeine also tended to consume more alcohol. Chronotype (rMEQ) was negatively correlated with both caffeine (beverages per day) (r = −.25, p < .05) and alcohol consumption (beverages per day) (r = −.30, p < .05), as well as with daytime sleepiness perception (DSPS-R; r = −.25, p < .05), suggesting that evening-types were more likely to consume substances and perceive more daytime sleepiness.

Tobacco use (cigarettes per day) was positively associated with the GSES (r = .32, p < .01), DSPS-R (r = .36, p < .01), ESS (r = .29, p < .05), and K10 (r = .34, p < .01); conversely, it was negatively associated with the participants satisfaction with academic performance (r = −.34, p < .01). This pattern suggests that participants that reported higher tobacco use also tended to experience greater sleep effort, increased perception of daytime sleepiness, higher propensity to fall asleep, higher psychological distress, and lower satisfaction with their academic performance.

A significant positive correlation was found between the GSES and the DSPS-R (r = .31, p < .05). No significant correlation was observed between the GSES and the ESS (r = −.08). These results suggest that individuals who report greater sleep effort also tend to perceive more daytime sleepiness but not sleepiness propensity.

Notably, lower social jetlag, less daytime sleepiness perception, and a morning-type chronotype were all associated with better academic performance. Also, satisfaction with academic performance was positively associated with perceived sports performance (SPPQ; r = .44, p < .01) and sleep satisfaction (r = .56, p < .01), highlighting the interplay between sleep, performance perception, and subjective academic satisfaction.

Finally, academic performance (grade average) was negatively associated with social jetlag (r = −.26, p < .05), DSPS-R (r = −.25, p < .05), and positively with rMEQ (r = .27, p < .05), suggesting that students with better alignment between biological and social time reported higher academic outcomes. Satisfaction with academic performance also correlated positively with SPPQ (r = .44, p < .01) and sleep satisfaction (r = .56, p < .01).

A Kruskal-Wallis H-test was carried out to assess whether there were significant differences between the categories of social jetlag (i.e., <1 h, 1–2 h, >2 h) for the self-report variables. No statistically significant differences were observed for any of the measures analyzed, including perceived sports performance (SPPQ: H(2) = 5.13, p = .077), sleep effort (GSES: H(2) = 0. 22, p = .898), daytime sleepiness (ESS: H(2) = .75, p = .686), psychological distress (K10: H(2) = .07, p = .968) and perceived daytime sleepiness (DSPS-R: H(2) = .00, p = .999). The only variable close to statistical significance was chronotype (rMEQ: H(2) = 5.81, p = .055), suggesting a potential trend in which individuals with lower social jetlag scores report a morning-type chronotype.

Distribution of social jetlag categories by chronotype

As to social jetlag across chronotype groups, it was observed that evening-types reported a mean social jetlag of 67.50 min (SD = 41.42), neutral-types 72.30 min (SD = 48.05), and morning-types 47.86 min (SD = 48.29). Rank comparisons from the Kruskal-Wallis H test showed mean ranks of 33.97 for evening-types, 35.24 for neutral-types, and 26.07 for morning-types. However, in general, these differences were not statistically significant, H(2) = 1.375, p = .503, suggesting that social jetlag was similarly distributed across chronotype groups.

Comparison of the measures between the current sample and normative samples

The average results of the scales used in the present sample were compared with the average results obtained in the original samples of the validation studies. The comparison between the SPPQ mean (M = 3.76) and the normative mean (M = 3.71, 470 federated young Portuguese athletes; 77 % men; aged 13 to 20) revealed no significant differences (t(66) = .397, p = .692, d = .05). In the rMEQ, the sample mean (M = 13.78) was statistically lower than the normative mean (M = 15.69; 134 undergraduates, 66.4 % women; MAge = 20.99), indicating an effect of moderate magnitude (t(66) = −5.197, p < .001, d = .64). The mean obtained in the GSES (M = 4.60) was statistically higher than the normative mean (M = 3.50; 2547 Higher Education students, 60 % women; MAge = 24, corresponding to a difference of small to moderate magnitude (t(66) = 2.929, p = .005, d = .36). Regarding the DSPS-R, the mean of the current sample (M = 4.43) was statistically lower than the normative mean (M = 5.92; 411 Higher Education students; 75 % women; MAge = 26.6), indicating a difference of moderate size (t(66) = −5.668, p < .001, d = .69). Regarding the ESS, the difference between the mean of the current sample (M = 8.60) and the normative mean (M = 7.85; 465 medical school undergraduates, 65.2 women; MAge = 18.8) did not reach statistical significance (t(66) = 1.446, p = .153, d = .18), suggesting an effect of small magnitude. As for the K10 (M = 20.03), there were no statistically significant differences (M = 20.55; 694 adults from community, 78 % women; MAge = 36.1) compared to the normative mean (t(66) = −.591, p = .556, d = .07).

Discussion

The present study sought to explore the frequency of social jetlag in a sample of higher education student-athletes and to examine its associations with chronotype, sleep-related variables, health behaviors, and academic and sports performance. Additionally, it aimed to investigate potential differences between groups with distinct levels of social jetlag. These objectives were designed to address the current gap in the literature regarding sleep-circadian misalignment among student-athletes, a population greatly subjected to both sports and athletic demands [4].

Social jetlag is typically considered relevant when exceeding a 1-h difference between sleep timing on workdays and free days [13]. However, higher levels (e.g., >2 h) have been associated with more pronounced negative health outcomes, such as increased depressive symptoms and worse endocrine and cardiovascular risk profiles [15,41]. In our sample, only 6 % of participants report more than 2 h of social jetlag, suggesting that this may not be a prevalent or particularly relevant phenomenon within this population of student-athletes. Based on previous studies highlighting the high prevalence of social jetlag [42,43], we hypothesized that the frequency in our sample could be like that found in general student populations. However, compared to prior studies, our results suggests a relatively healthier sleep-wake alignment among the student-athletes in this sample, since they reveal a lower level of misalignment. Most participants (62.7 %) report experiencing between 1 and 2 h of social jetlag, while 31.3 % report less than 1 h. In the study by Lopes et al. [42], 13.4 % of participants exhibited social jetlag greater than 2 h, which is more than double the frequency observed in our sample. Similarly, Qu et al. [43] reported that 14.1 % of their sample experienced social jetlag ≥2 h, and in the work by Chen et al. [25], 12 % fell into this category. Furthermore, the proportion of participants with less than 1 h of social jetlag in our sample (31.3 %) is similar to the one observed in Lopes et al. [42] (34.3 %), but notably lower than the 53 % reported by Chen et al. [25]. Student-athletes are often subject to strict training schedules, which may limit the variability in their sleep cycle, however these comparisons suggest that student-athletes in our sample may be someway protected against high levels of circadian misalignment. One possible explanation for this fact is that the structured and regulated routines imposed by dual career responsibilities may promote more stable sleep-wake patterns. In addition, these students are frequently more closely monitored in terms of health compared to their non-athlete peers. For instance, Shah et al. [44] highlighted the implementation of wellness tracking tools that assess sleep quality, muscle soreness, mood, and stress among student-athletes, enabling strength and conditioning coaches to tailor training loads accordingly. The emphasis on such monitoring practices makes evident the recognition of the unique lifestyle faced by student-athletes, including balancing academic responsibilities with athletic commitments. By proactively addressing these challenges through comprehensive health monitoring, institutions aim to support the dual pursuits of academic success and athletic excellence among student-athletes [3,44].

We hypothesized that higher levels of social jetlag would be associated with worse academic performance, greater effort to fall asleep, increased daytime sleepiness propensity and perception, lower satisfaction with sleep, higher levels of psychological distress, lower sports performance perception, greater substance use (tobacco, alcohol and caffeine) and a greater BMI. While some of these associations were partially supported by the data, the overall pattern was not consistent across all variables. Notably, social jetlag in our sample was negatively associated with academic performance, which is consistent with previous findings [45,46], whereas rMEQ scores, indicating greater morningness, were positively related to academic performance. These findings support the hypothesis that better alignment between an individual's chronotype, and socially imposed schedules may facilitate improved academic outcomes. This interpretation is further supported by Trockel et al. [47], who reported that students with later wake-up and bedtimes during both weekdays and weekends tended to have lower GPAs (Grade Point Average). However, no significant associations were found between social jetlag and the other variables. These mixed results suggest that while circadian misalignment may influence certain academic-related outcomes, its impact on other areas of functioning may be mediated or moderated by individual or contextual factors, such as chronotype, coping strategies, or lifestyle routines, pointing to a gap that future research should look into.

Even though no significant chronotype group differences were found based on levels of social jetlag, our results suggest that individuals with morning chronotypes tend to report lower social jetlag. This is consistent with the notion that morning chronotypes experience less conflict between biological and social schedules. On the other hand, evening chronotypes reported greater misalignment (67.50 min), which is in accordance with existing literature [48]. On the other hand, athletic commitments often favor early routines, which tend to suit morning chronotypes better. Evening types, by contrast, often “rebound” on weekends - sleeping in to recover from accumulated sleep debt - which increases social jetlag [13]. Morning types, however, are more likely to maintain consistent sleep and wake times throughout the week [[49], [50], [51]]. Additionally, morning types often exhibit greater circadian stability - being less prone to irregular sleep patterns - and practice better sleep hygiene, such as avoiding late-night light exposure or caffeine. These factors may contribute to smaller shifts between weekday and weekend sleep schedules, thereby further reducing social jetlag.

Our study presents several notable strengths. Firstly, it addresses an understudied population, the higher education student-athletes in Portugal. To our knowledge, this is the first study applying the sleep-corrected formula for social jetlag [24] to this population. Secondly, the inclusion of self-report measures, such as the GSES and the DSPS-R, provides new insights into underexplored aspects of subjective sleep experience in student-athletes.

Despite its contributions, this study presents several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although the sample size (n = 67) was sufficient for exploratory purposes, it remains relatively small, potentially limiting statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Similar constraints have been reported in other studies examining sleep in athletic populations [5,52]. Additionally, the exclusive use of self-report instruments may introduce biases, which could affect the accuracy of the reported sleep behaviors and psychological variables. It is worth mentioning that we did not control for medical or psychiatric conditions, nor for the use of medications (e.g., psychotropics) that could directly affect sleep patterns, alertness, or stress levels. Moreover, the absence of objective sleep assessments, such as actigraphy or other wearable-based monitoring, reduces the precision of sleep-related estimates and limits the ability to validate self-reported data.

Future research should aim to further investigate the protective factors that may buffer the effects of social jetlag in student-athletes, such as structured routines, institutional support, or health-related behaviors. Additionally, several potentially relevant variables were not assessed in the present study, including medication use, the regularity of training schedules, and the alignment between training times and individual chronotypes. These factors may play a meaningful role in sleep regulation and circadian alignment and should be considered in subsequent investigations. Moreover, future studies would benefit from the incorporation of wearable devices, which can provide more accurate and continuous assessments of sleep-wake patterns and a more comprehensive understanding of sleep and circadian functioning in student-athletes. Recent research has demonstrated the feasibility and value of using wearables to monitor circadian rhythms and sleep parameters in different contexts [[53], [54], [55]].

In conclusion, this study provides new insights into social jetlag among student-athletes in higher education. Although the overall frequency of significant social jetlag was low in this sample, the observed associations with academic performance and chronotype suggest that even moderate circadian misalignment may have some implications. These findings underscore the importance of considering biological rhythms in academic and athletic planning. Structured routines, institutional support, and regular training schedules may act as protective factors, helping student-athletes maintain better sleep alignment, academic performance and overall well-being [3]. Moreover, the inclusion of different variables, such as sleep effort, adds to our understanding of the sleep experiences of student-athletes. Nonetheless, given the limitations of the current study, future research should seek to replicate and extend these findings using more diverse and representative samples and wearable-based data collection. Investigating how training schedules align with individual chronotypes, as well as the role of sleep education and behavioral interventions, will be crucial in developing specific strategies to optimize sleep health and academic success in this population. Ultimately, this research contributes to the growing field of sport and sleep science by shedding light on how student-athletes manage the convergence between biological needs and social demands.
