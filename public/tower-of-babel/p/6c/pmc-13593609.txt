Artificial intelligence and machine learning in bone research☆
Authors: Mustafa Unal, Karl J. Lewis, Cheryl L. Ackert-Bicknell, Jeffry S. Nyman
Published: 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) are increasingly being adopted in bone research, offering computational tools that address longstanding challenges in skeletal biology. These methods have shown considerable potential across diverse tasks, including fracture risk prediction, bone microarchitecture assessment, metabolic bone disease monitoring, and drug target discovery. At the same time, the field faces well recognized obstacles related to data availability, model interpretability, and translational readiness. This special issue of Bone Reports brings together eleven contributions that collectively illustrate both the current capabilities and the remaining gaps at the intersection of AI/ML and bone research.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) poses unique challenges for skeletal assessment because it primarily affects cortical bone through mechanisms that conventional imaging often fails to capture. Aoki et al. (Aoki et al., 2024) address this problem from a laboratory analytics perspective by examining whether their ML based Progressive CKD Risk Classifier (PCRC), a random forest model trained on routine laboratory data, is associated with CKD mineral bone disorder (CKD MBD). Using longitudinal data from over 330,000 US outpatients, they show that the CKD progression group exhibited a 94.9% increase in parathyroid hormone and a 15.5% increase in phosphate over five years, consistent with CKD MBD. The study also reveals significant underutilization of CKD MBD laboratory testing in the general US outpatient population, and represents one of the first investigations linking AI-based predictive analytics for CKD progression with MBD - outcomes. Lee et al. (Lee et al., 2025) tackle the same disease from an imaging perspective, presenting a proof of concept study that merges deep learning with ML for CKD related cortical bone assessment in HRpQCT images. Their two stage approach uses U Net for cortical segmentation and Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM) texture features, which capture spatial patterns in pixel intensity distributions, classified by XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting). The diaphyseal tibia model achieved an F1 score and Area Under the Curve (AUC) of 0.99, outperforming standard HRpQCT parameters including BMD and cortical porosity in distinguishing CKD from non CKD bone across all anatomical sites. Together, these two studies demonstrate that AI can detect CKD related skeletal changes through both routine laboratory data and advanced imaging, each offering complementary clinical value.

Fracture prediction and classification remain central concerns in musculoskeletal research. Marullo et al. (Marullo et al., 2024) apply YOLOv8, a state of the art object detection architecture originally designed for real time recognition tasks, to classify femur fractures according to the AO/OTA system from X-ray images. With advanced data augmentation and fine tuning, the model achieved 90% accuracy across seven fracture classes. The study incorporates Eigen CAM attention maps, a visualization technique that highlights which image regions most influence the model's output, to improve interpretability, allowing clinicians to verify which anatomical regions drive the model's predictions. The work also reinforces that data augmentation, while beneficial, cannot fully compensate for class imbalance in rare fracture types. Shaik et al. (Shaik et al., 2024) introduce a staged ML approach for hip fracture risk prediction that integrates clinical variables with DXA imaging features. By using uncertainty quantification from a first stage ensemble model, their method determines whether DXA scanning is necessary for each patient. The staged model achieved an AUC of 0.85 while identifying that approximately 54% of patients did not require DXA, thereby reducing diagnostic cost and radiation exposure through a sequential decision framework that mirrors clinical reasoning.

Beyond imaging and clinical prediction, Greene et al. (Greene et al., 2025) examine how AI can accelerate the discovery of new drug targets for bone diseases. Although bone mineral density is strongly heritable and genome wide association studies have identified over 1100 genetic regions linked to it, turning these findings into effective treatments has proven difficult. A key reason is that bone related traits are shaped not by a few isolated genes but by large regulatory networks in which many genes indirectly influence the phenotype through a smaller set of central regulators, a concept known as the omnigenic model. The authors review computational methods, including knowledge graphs and data integration tools such as PLIER and BuDDI, that can map these networks and reveal drug targets that traditional genetic analyses miss. Drawing on the PCSK9 success story in cardiovascular medicine, where genetic evidence guided the development of cholesterol lowering drugs, they argue that similar AI-driven strategies hold promise for bone therapeutics. They also highlight important gaps, including the strong bias toward European ancestry populations in bone genetics research and the difficulty of capturing osteoclasts, the cells responsible for bone resorption, in current single cell profiling techniques.

Osteoporosis and osteopenia screening is another area where AI shows considerable promise. Harris et al. (Harris et al., 2025) evaluate several deep learning architectures for detecting low bone density in children using wrist X-rays from a publicly available pediatric dataset (GRAZPEDWRI DX). Their best performing model, DenseNet201 with transfer learning (a technique that adapts a network previously trained on general images to a medical task), achieved 95.2% classification accuracy and an AUC of 0.98 in distinguishing osteopenic from healthy bone. To make the model's reasoning transparent, the authors applied two explainability methods, GradCAM and LIME, which produce heatmaps showing which image regions most influenced each prediction. Medical experts confirmed that these highlighted regions corresponded to clinically meaningful areas of low bone density in the distal forearm. The study underscores that children have distinct skeletal characteristics compared to adults, and therefore diagnostic algorithms developed for adult populations may not transfer directly to pediatric settings. Huang et al. (Huang et al., 2026) apply a Vision Transformer (ViT) to hip X-rays for classifying bone status into three categories: normal, osteopenia, and osteoporosis. Unlike conventional convolutional neural networks, which analyze small local patches of an image and build up context gradually, ViT divides the entire image into patches and uses a self-attention mechanism to learn relationships between all patches simultaneously, making it well suited for detecting the diffuse, spatially distributed bone changes that characterize declining mineral density. The model achieved 97.0% accuracy on internal validation and 89.4% on an external dataset from a separate community clinic, outperforming both ResNet50 and InceptionV3. Both studies demonstrate that routine radiographs, which are far more widely available and less expensive than DXA, can be repurposed for bone density screening when combined with deep learning.

Several contributions expand the scope of AI applications into less explored domains of bone research. Birsel et al. (Birsel et al., 2025) develop a support vector machine (SVM) model, a type of supervised ML algorithm that finds optimal boundaries between categories, to classify hip conditions in children with cerebral palsy. Using migration percentage measurements from pelvic radiographs, which quantify how far the femoral head has shifted out of the hip socket, the model categorized 176 hips into normal, at risk, and dislocated groups with an overall accuracy of 92.9%. The dislocated hip category was identified with particularly high reliability (AUC of 0.999). Because manual measurement of migration percentage can vary between clinicians, the authors compared the model's classifications against those of an orthopaedic resident and a pediatric orthopaedic surgeon, finding no statistically significant differences (P = 0.9). This result supports the model's potential as a standardized decision support tool, especially in settings where specialist expertise is limited. Unal et al. (Unal et al., 2025) combine Raman spectroscopy, a non destructive technique that probes the molecular composition of tissue by measuring how laser light scatters off chemical bonds, with ML algorithms to predict the fracture resistance of human cortical bone. Working with femoral specimens from 118 cadaveric donors spanning ages 21 to 101, they fed Raman-derived bone matrıx features reflecting mineral quality and collagen integrity, together with clinical variables such as age and bone density, into several regression models. The best configurations explained approximately 62% of the variance in crack initiation toughness and 74% in energy to fracture, representing substantial improvements over models that used clinical parameters alone. This study is the first to integrate Raman spectroscopy with ML for predicting fracture toughness of human cortical bone, and it points toward future clinical applications in which non invasive spectroscopic probes could assess bone quality without requiring biopsy. Horenberg et al. (Horenberg et al., 2025) present a workflow for three dimensional imaging and spatial analysis of nerves in bone. Peripheral nerves within bone are difficult to study because they are extremely thin (approximately 4 μm in diameter) and embedded in dense, mineralized tissue. The authors combine lightsheet microscopy, which illuminates a thin plane of tissue to build high resolution 3D images, with ML based segmentation using the open source Ilastik software to identify nerve fibers automatically. The workflow was validated across three skeletal contexts, including calvarial skull defects, metatarsal long bones, and knee joint sections, achieving approximately 80% agreement with manually traced gold standard data. These three contributions collectively highlight that the scope of AI in bone research extends well beyond imaging based diagnosis into biomechanics, developmental orthopedics, and tissue level neuroscience.

Moreover, David et al. (David et al., 2026) provide a comprehensive narrative review of ML approaches for digital bone imaging using microCT and HRpQCT, accompanied by a novel scientometric and bibliometric software called SciNetX. The review covers the full analytical pipeline, from super resolution and segmentation to dimensionality reduction, prediction, classification, clustering, and model interpretability. Among their practical observations, U-Net emerges as the most consistently used architecture across these tasks, and dimensionality reduction and data integration are identified as underutilized techniques in bone research.

Taken together, this special issue underscores the growing role of AI and ML across bone research. Several cross cutting themes emerge from the collected contributions: the importance of model interpretability for clinical acceptance (Marullo et al., 2024; Harris et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2026; David et al., 2026), the persistent challenge of limited and imbalanced datasets (Marullo et al., 2024; David et al., 2026), the value of multimodal and staged analytical approaches (Aoki et al., 2024; Shaik et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025), and the need for external validation before clinical deployment (Lee et al., 2025; Birsel et al., 2025; David et al., 2026). As the field continues to evolve, these contributions provide a foundation for future studies that bring AI-based computational tools closer to routine use in bone research and clinical practice.
