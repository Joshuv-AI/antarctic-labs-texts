Arthroscopic‐Assisted Posterolateral Corner Reconstruction With Popliteus Tendon Repair
Authors: Quang Ton Quyen Nguyen, Ngoc Thang Pham, Nguyen Khanh Hung Truong, Dinh Vinh Pham, Kenneth D Montgomery, Dang Dai Long Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPosterolateral corner reconstruction is traditionally performed via an open approach but remains technically challenging due to limited accessibility and suboptimal intraoperative visualization. Arthroscopy offers improved visualization of the posterior compartment of the knee. While switching to arthroscopic‐assisted posterolateral corner reconstruction, we found that popliteus tendon repair could be performed alongside its reconstruction, potentially enhancing stability and enabling retensioning of the popliteofibular ligament—a structure that appears to be difficult to anatomically recontruct with current methods. This technical note describes an arthroscopic‐assisted posterolateral corner reconstruction and popliteus tendon repair, intended to reestablish the popliteofibular ligament function.

Since first described by Serra Cruz et al., 1 anatomic reconstruction of the posterolateral corner (PLC) has undergone several modifications. 2 , 3 , 4 These contemporary techniques have not fully addressed the anatomical characteristics of the popliteofibular ligament, which uniquely connects from tendon to bone. This technical note describes an arthroscopic‐assisted PLC reconstruction comprising percutaneous fibular collateral ligament (FCL) reconstruction, popliteal bypass, and popliteus tendon repair, which may help restore the native popliteofibular ligament.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

Patient Selection

Our indications for PLC reconstruction include grade III injuries and multiligament knee injuries involving the PLC.

Position and Landmark

The patient is positioned supine with the knee flexed at 90°. A tourniquet is applied proximally, and the operative leg is prepped and draped in standard fashion. Anatomical landmarks including the Gerdy tubercle, lateral epicondyle, fibular head, patella, and patellar tendon are identified.

Graft Harvesting and Preparing

Autografts are harvested from the contralateral leg to facilitate postoperative recovery. A tripled‐folded superficial quadriceps tendon graft, approximately 85 mm in length and 8 mm in diameter, is prepared for posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) reconstruction, with both ends secured using adjustable‐loop suspensory fixation devices. A double‐folded semitendinosus for FCL and a double‐folded gracilis for popliteus reconstruction are passed through a single suspensory loop device, with an average diameter of 4 to 5 mm.

Arthroscopic Inspection and Transeptal Portal Creation

Anterolateral, anteromedial, posteromedial portals are created, and diagnostic arthroscopy is undertaken. The intercruciate trans‐septal technique described by Chernchujit Bancha et al. 5 is used to access the posterior compartment of the knee joint (Figure 1, Video 1).

Posterolateral corner (PLC) and posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) injuries in the left knee are confirmed intraoperatively under anesthesia. Surgery begins with harvesting the superficial quadriceps tendon, which is tripled and designated for PCL reconstruction, the gracilis tendon for popliteus reconstruction, and the semitendinosus tendon for fibular collateral ligament (FCL) reconstruction. Following creation of arthroscopic portals, the PCL tibial footprint is first exposed, after which dissection proceeds laterally to expose the popliteus tendon. A tibial tunnel is drilled in the popliteus sulcus, followed by creation of the PCL tibial tunnel. Two small lateral incisions are made: one over the lateral femoral epicondyle and another over the fibular head, allowing for femoral tunnel drilling for both the popliteus and FCL grafts. The grafts are first fixed into their respective femoral tunnels, then secured in their tibial or fibular tunnels. Additionally, the native popliteus tendon is sutured and retensioned alongside the graft to enhance the reconstruction. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70011.

PCL and Popliteus Exposure

After establishing the trans‐septal portals, with the view from the posterolateral (PL) portal, introduce the shaver and radiofrequency device through the posteromedial portal (PM) to complete septum removal and identify the PCL tibial footprint. Dissection is then carried out laterally, releasing the popliteomeniscal fibers until the popliteus tendon are exposed (Figure 2, Video 1).

Popliteus and PCL Tibial Tunnels Creation

A PCL guide (Acufex Director PCL Tibial Aimer, Smith & Nephew Memphis, TN, USA) is introduced via the anterolateral portal and seated against the posterior cortex of the lateral tibial plateau, with its tip aligned in the popliteus sulcus at the level of musculotendinous junction of the popliteus tendon (Figure 3, Video 1). The angle of the guide is then fine‐tuned until the ratcheting bullet can engage the flat surface medial to Gerdy's tubercle. The locking knob is tightened to secure the aimer in place, then a guide wire is drilled, and a tunnel with desired diameter is created. Pass the shuttle suture through the tibial tunnel and withdraw it via the posteromedial portal.

Popliteus tunnel creation, (A) popliteus tendon retraction for sulcus exposure and tunnel drilling point identification, (B) positioning of the drilling guide in the popliteus sulcus, and (C) tunnel creation with desired diameter (left knee, supine position with knee in 90° of flexion, view from posterolateral portal).

The same PCL aiming device is subsequently introduced through the posteromedial portal and advanced to the PCL tibial insertion, with its tip situated at the distolateral aspect of the native footprint. A guide pin and cannulated reamers are used to create a tibial tunnel corresponding to the graft diameter. A shuttle suture is placed in the tunnel to draw through the posteromedial portal.

PCL Femoral Tunnel Creation

The arthroscope is redirected to the anteromedial portal. A drill bit is inserted through the anterolateral portal to use as a guide to place a 2.4 mm guide pin into the medial femoral condyle, followed by drilling a socket to a depth of 20 mm. The drill bit, with a diameter matching that of the PCL graft, is positioned 2 mm from the articular cartilage margin within the anterolateral bundle footprint. A shuttle suture is then passed through the tunnel and retrieved via the anterolateral portal.

First (distal) Incision

A 2‐3 cm vertical incision over the fibular head is used to identify the fibular FCL attachment. A tibial bullet aimer (Acufex Director 4‐point bullet) is used to drill a guide pin through the fibular head, directed slightly anteromedial and parallel to the joint line, which helps protect the common peroneal nerve (Figure 4). The tunnel is then overreamed with a 5 mm cannulated reamer, and a suture loop placed for subsequent graft passage.

Second (Proximal) Incision

A 3‐4 cm longitudinal incision is made spanning the lateral epicondyle to the joint line. The iliotibial band is split to expose the FCL and the popliteus femoral insertion, allowing placement of 2 parallel guide pins. Both tunnels are overreamed to 5 mm, and passing sutures are inserted (Figure 4).

Graft Passage and Fixation

Entering via the second (proximal) incision, a small curved clamp is directed around the lateral femoral condyle toward the posterior aspect of the lateral tibial plateau, where it is used to grasp and retrieve the shuttle suture from the popliteus tunnel, drawing it out through the incision (Figure 5, Video 1).

(B) Lasso‐loop (No. 2 ultrabraid suture, Smith & Nephew, Memphis, TN, USA) placement in the popliteus tendon. (C) Advancement of the popliteus graft into the tibial tunne, white arrow: popliteus graft, (left knee, supine position in 90° of flexion, view from (A) anteromedial and (B), (C) posterolateal portal).

A No. 2 ultrabraid suture (Smith & Nephew) is placed roughly into musculotendinous junction of the popliteus tendon with a Firstpass mini suture passer (Smith & Nephew). The 2 free ends of the suture are drawn through the closed end and tensioned to create a loop on the tendon. The closed end of the gracilis graft is pulled and secured into the femoral popliteus tunnel by tensionning the adjustment tails of the button (mean depth: 20 mm). The free end of the graft, and the loop suture of the popliteus tendon are drawn into the tibial tunnel (Figure 5, Video 1).

The closed end of semitendinosus graft is anchored to the femoral tunnel then routed beneath the iliotibial band and superficial to the gracilis graft, crossed the fibular tunnel, using the passing sutures previously left in place. Viewing through the medial portal, both shuttle sutures of the PCL are retrieved through the anterolateral portal, then used to guide the PCL graft first through the femoral tunnel, passing adjacent to the lateral edge of the PCL remnant, and subsequently through the tibial tunnel.

The semitendinous FCL graft is tensioned and secured in full extension and gentle valgus, using a 7.0 × 25 mm interference screw (Biosure HA, Smith & Nephew) inserted into the fibular head. The gracilis PT graft and the loop suture are fixed to the tibial tunnel with a 7.0 mm interference screw at 90° of knee flexion while applying anterior translational force to the tibial plateau (Figure 6).

After fixation on the femoral side with a suspensory device (Ultrabutton Device, Smith & Nephew), the PCL graft is tensioned and a guide pin for interference screw is inserted through tibial tunnel. Under direct vision via the posterolateral portal, a 7.0 × 30 mm interference screw is introduced deep into the tunnel until its tip slightly protruded beyond the tunnel aperture. During graft fixation, an anterior draw is applied to the tibia while traction is exerted on the graft with the knee in 90° of flexion. An 4.5 mm accessory tunnel is created and the suspensory fixation device is redirected and secured through this tunnel to enhance graft stability and load distribution. The arthroscope is then switched to the anteromedial portal. A 7.0 × 25 mm bioabsorbable screw is inserted into the femoral tunnel to reinforce graft fixation (Figure 6, Video 1).

Following fixation of all grafts, knee stability and range of motion are evaluated to ensure absence of residual laxity or excessive tightness. The capsule, iliotibial band, and skin are then closed in a standard fashion. A summary of the surgical technique is illustrated in Figure 6.

Postoperative Rehabilitation

Rehabilitation begins on postoperative day one, focusing on swelling control, prevention of posterior tibial sag, and quadriceps activation. Patients remain nonweight‐bearing for 6 weeks using a dynamic PCL brace (Medi M.4s PCL dynamic). In the first 2 weeks, therapy emphasizes prone knee flexion (0°‐90°) and early quadriceps reactivation, while avoiding hamstring engagement. After 2 weeks, flexion beyond 90° is gradually introduced as tolerated. At 6 weeks, patients begin partial weight‐bearing and low‐resistance cycling (up to 70° flexion). Low‐impact activities are added at 12 weeks based on progress. At 6 months, clinical and radiographic evaluations determine whether the brace can be gradually discontinued, allowing progression to jogging and proprioceptive training. Functional testing at 9 and 12 months guides return to full activity.

DISCUSSION

Various techniques have been described for PLC reconstruction of the knee, with anatomic reconstruction showing superior outcomes compared with nonanatomic methods. 4 However, current fibular tunnel drilling techniques often fail to accurately reproduce the anatomical attachment site of the FCL and PFL. 6

The described technique enables an anatomic, arthroscopy‐assisted PLC reconstruction that minimizes soft‐tissue dissection, reduces the risk of neurovascular complications, and potentially restores the native popliteofibular ligament.

The repair technique serves to re‐tension the popliteus complex (popliteus tendon and popliteofibular ligament), which, in most cases, is structurally continuous but elongated due to injury. This procedure can be carried out with relative ease and does not consume considerable additional time. Popliteus tendon retensioning may function as an internal brace for the popliteus graft and enhance the stability of the reconstruction.

Given that distance between the attachment points of the PT and FCL is typically less than 18.5 mm in Asian individuals, 7 we employ suspensory fixation on the femoral side to mitigate the risk of femoral tunnel blowout (Figure 6).

Our approach—percutaneous FCL reconstruction together with arthroscopically assisted popliteus bypass—might provide superior mechanical stability compared with the Larson technique. 8 This anatomic PLC reconstruction, when combined with popliteus repair, may achieve biomechanical stability similar to the traditional open LaPrade reconstruction while causing less soft‐tissue damage, resulting in reduced postoperative pain and a lower risk of arthrofibrosis.
