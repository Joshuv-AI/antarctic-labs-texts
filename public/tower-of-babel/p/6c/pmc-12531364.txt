Saliva Versus All‐Site Microbiome and Proteome Mapping in Periodontitis
Authors: Kai Bao, Beral Afacan, Jonas Grossmann, Angelika Silbereisen, Veli‐Özgen Öztürk, Gulnur Emingil, Georgios N Belibasakis, Nagihan Bostanci
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimSaliva is a diagnostic surrogate for microbial and host biomarkers in periodontitis, but whether it reflects subgingival plaque, gingival crevicular fluid (GCF) or serum remains debated. This study profiled GCF and subgingival plaque from all sites of periodontitis patients, comparing them to saliva and serum.Materials and MethodsSaliva, serum, subgingival plaque and GCF were obtained from three patients with stage III, grade C periodontitis, having 23, 25 and 27 teeth, respectively, with six sites sampled per tooth. All plaque and GCF samples were pooled per patient. Shotgun sequencing and mass spectrometry proteomics were used for microbiome and proteome analysis, respectively.ResultsTotally 277 microbial taxa were collectively identified in saliva and plaque, 93 of which were differentially abundant between the two. Saliva exhibited higher overall species diversity, but lower periodontal pathogen abundance. A total of 1153 host proteins were identified (saliva: 803; GCF: 932; serum: 195) across the three biological fluids, with 685 shared among saliva and GCF and 109 among all three. Saliva contained slightly fewer proteins than GCF, but shared several common immune, metabolic and enzyme regulation pathways.ConclusionSaliva is effective for broad microbiome and proteome screening; whereas plaque delivers greater precision in identifying specific periodontal pathogens directly associated with a periodontal pocket.Keywords: gingival crevicular fluid, liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry‐based proteomics, plaque, saliva, serum, whole genome shotgun sequencing

Introduction

The diagnosis and monitoring of periodontitis is still largely dependent on clinical examination, patient history and radiographic assessment (Tonetti et al. 2018). However, these methods primarily reflect cumulative tissue damage, error‐prone measures and provide limited insight into current disease activity or the potential for disease progression. This limitation has led to growing interest in biological fluids as complementary diagnostic tools, as they provide a real‐time snapshot of the host response and microbial activity within the periodontal environment, offering greater potential for early detection, disease classification and monitoring of treatment response (Bostanci and Belibasakis 2018, 2023; Giannobile 2012; Kumar et al. 2021; Manoil et al. 2024; Teles et al. 2024).

Gingival crevicular fluid (GCF), subgingival plaque and saliva are considered the most applicable biological samples for the analysis and development of diagnostic and prognostic microbial and protein biomarkers for periodontitis (Bostanci et al. 2010; Chapple et al. 2007; Teles et al. 2024). While GCF and subgingival plaque provide site‐specific diagnostic information and detailed insight into the local inflammatory and microbial profile, their utility in clinical practice is limited by challenges in sample collection (difficulty in accessing all tooth sites), low volume and technical complexity (Bostanci et al. 2011; Grant et al. 2022; Kumar et al. 2021; Molinsky et al. 2025). Full‐mouth site‐specific GCF and plaque sampling presents clinical challenges as it would require extensive chair time and significant patient discomfort, making it impractical for routine use (Bostanci and Belibasakis 2023; Lamster and Ahlo 2007). This often results in partial sampling at selected teeth/sites, whether from deep or shallow periodontal pockets, which may not provide a comprehensive representation of the overall periodontal status (Belibasakis et al. 2014; Romano et al. 2019; Teles et al. 2010). Saliva, on the other hand, offers a more practical, non‐invasive and scalable option for periodontal disease diagnosis due to its proximity to periodontal tissues, ease of collection, larger sample volume and potential to reflect both local and systemic disease activity (Bao et al. 2024; Bostanci et al. 2013; Detzen et al. 2017; Greenwood et al. 2020; Lira‐Junior et al. 2024; Silbereisen et al. 2024). However, transitioning from site‐specific samples to saliva biomarkers requires careful consideration (Giannobile 2012). Despite the increasing availability of point‐of‐care platforms for salivary microbial‐protein based biomarker detection, their integration into periodontal practice remains limited, primarily due to the absence of well‐performing biomarkers and ongoing concerns regarding the ability of salivary diagnostics to reliably reflect site‐specific periodontal disease activity or systemic health status (Baumgartner et al. 2021; Herr et al. 2007). To date, no single biomarker has demonstrated 100% specificity and sensitivity for the accurate diagnosis of periodontitis; instead, a combination of microbial and host‐response markers is considered more effective for capturing the disease's complex pathobiology (Bostanci and Belibasakis 2023; Bostanci, Mitsakakis, et al. 2021; Bostanci, Selevsek, et al. 2018; Grant et al. 2022). Recent microbiological studies have identified a ‘disease‐provoking microbiota’, indicating that periodontitis may begin with a dysbiotic shift detectable before the clinical onset of inflammation and bone loss (Kumar et al. 2021; Manoil et al. 2024). In recent years, a few emerging studies have highlighted that protein signature profiling in saliva through proteomics has advanced the identification of periodontitis subtypes from gingivitis, well positioning these approaches as central players in the advancement of personalised periodontology (Bostanci, Mitsakakis, et al. 2021; Bostanci, Selevsek, et al. 2018; Bostanci et al. 2020; Grant et al. 2022). Thus, we evaluated GCF and subgingival plaque at the full‐mouth scale in patients with periodontitis using advanced proteomic and microbiome analyses and compared these profiles with saliva and serum samples collected from the same patients.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Sample Collection

Three systemically healthy, non‐smoker males with Stage III Grade C periodontitis were enrolled from the population seeking periodontal care at the Department of Periodontology, School of Dentistry, Aydın Adnan Menderes University, Turkey between December 2019 and February 2020. The participants were requested to refrain from eating, drinking (except water), chewing gum, brushing and flossing 2 h before sample collection. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected using passive drooling for 5 min between 8:30 am and 10:30 am, 24–48 h after the prescreening visit. The samples were centrifuged to separate the pellet and supernatant for microbiome and proteomic analyses (Figure 1). Venous blood (5 mL) was collected from the antecubital vein by venipuncture and allowed to clot at room temperature for 15 min. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 15 min at 4°C. Subgingival plaque (two paper points per site, 80 teeth × 6 sites × 2, n = 960) and GCF samples (one filter paper strip per site, 80 teeth × 6 sites, n = 480) were collected from six sites. All samples per patient were pooled prior to analysis, resulting in n = 3 pooled samples (Supporting Information).

Illustration of sample collections and their corresponding high‐throughput analyses. Samples collected from paper points strips, unstimulated saliva, GCF and serum were first processed by centrifugation. The pellets from saliva and paper points were subjected to whole genome sequencing to examine the oral microbiota. The supernatants of saliva, GCF and whole serum were subjected to LC/MS2 to determine host protein profiles.

Metagenomic and Proteomic Analyses

DNA was extracted and quantified as per established protocols (Bao et al. 2017). A label‐free quantitative proteomic approach was used to dissect host protein composition (Bao et al. 2024). Details for the methods and bioinformatic pipelines are provided in Supporting Information.

Microbial Profiles From Subgingival Plaque, Saliva and Serum

Serum did not yield a quantifiable DNA. An average of 19.21 ± 4.86 × 106 reads per sample, with 18.07 ± 6.50 × 106 reads from plaque and 20.35 ± 1.57 × 106 reads from saliva, was identified, assigned to 1 archaeon, 271 bacteria, 1 orthornavirus, 1 protist and 3 viruses (Table S1). Two hundred and nine species (207 bacterial, 1 orthornaviral, 1 protistal) were identified in both sources, while 47 species (45 bacterial, 3 viral) and 21 species (20 bacterial, 1 archaeal) were found exclusively in plaque and saliva, respectively (Figure 2A). Alpha diversity indices analysis, including Chao1 (p = 0.827, Figure 2B), Simpson (p = 0.827, Figure 2C) and Shannon (p = 0.513, Figure 2D), revealed no significant differences in species richness and evenness between plaque and saliva. Beta diversity analysis using permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) also failed to indicate statistically significant differences in microbial composition (p = 0.12). LEfSe identified 92 oral species with significant differences between plaque and saliva (Table S2). These differences were particularly evident with Porphyromonas gingivalis and Tannerella forsythia , cumulatively accounting for 42.14% ± 5.9% of total abundance (Figure 2E). These two species had the highest linear discriminant analysis (LDA) scores, with P. gingivalis (LDA = 5.16) and T. forsythia (LDA = 4.78) showing a strong association with plaque. Treponema denticola (LDA = 3.27) also showed a greater discriminatory presence in plaque. In contrast, only seven salivary species exceeded 3% in relative abundance, constituting 29.70% ± 9.47% of total abundance (Figure 2D). Unclassified Porphyromonas sp. (LDA = 4.37), unclassified Rothia sp. (LDA = 4.30) and Prevotella melaninogenica (LDA = 4.27) were more prevalent in saliva (Figure 2E).

Host‐Derived Proteomic Profiles From Saliva, GCF and Serum

Totally 1153 proteins were identified, with an FDR of 2.93%; 803 proteins were identified in saliva, 932 in GCF and 195 in serum (Figure 3A, Table S3); 109 proteins were common across all three body fluids, while 97 were unique to saliva, 242 to GCF and 60 to serum (Table S4). Despite the lower number of proteins identified in serum, principal component analysis (PCA) analysis based on protein counts showed a distinct clustering of serum from the other two body fluids (Figure 3B). The PERMANOVA analysis revealed statistically significant overall differences among the three groups (p = 0.012), with the grouping factor accounting for a large proportion (87.99%) of the total variation observed. Serum samples showed greater compositional differences compared to both GCF (88.76%, p = 0.1) and saliva (93.65%, p = 0.1), indicating large effect sizes despite these differences not reaching statistical significance. Conversely, GCF and saliva were compositionally similar, with sample type explaining only 2.84% of the variation (p = 0.4) as visually depicted in Figure 3B. This may be attributed to the high percentage of abundant proteins in serum (Figure 3C), where nearly 50% of the biomass was accounted for by seven proteins (each exceeding 3%), led by albumin, which constituted 13.36% of the total biomass. Two proteins in saliva (albumin; S100‐A9) and three in GCF (albumin, S100‐A9; and haemoglobin subunit alpha) exceeded 3%.

We performed over‐representation analysis (ORA) based on the Gene Ontology (GO) biological process database. A total of 69 proteins were mapped to all 103 enriched functional categories derived from shared proteins across the three biofluids. To illustrate the collective functional roles of shared proteins, we selected the top 10 enriched GO biological process terms among proteins common to saliva and GCF (Figure 3D). These were immune‐related, including ‘defence response’, ‘regulation of immune system’, ‘complement activation’, and ‘humoral immune response’ (Figure 3E). The other GO terms (93 in total; Table S5) revealed clustering into metabolic processes, enzyme regulation and blood‐related functions.

The ORA of proteins shared exclusively between GCF, and saliva identified 12 significantly enriched GO terms (Figure 3F). Among the enriched terms, ‘developmental process’ and ‘anatomical structure development’ exhibited the highest gene ratios, followed by ‘cell death and apoptotic process’, highlighting key roles in tissue development (i.e., epithelial turnover, wound healing, tissue remodelling, extracellular matrix organisation, cell adhesion) and programmed cell death. Other enriched terms, including ‘cellular response to stress or chemical stimulus’, suggest that many of the shared proteins (i.e., annexin, actin, MMP‐8, CAMP) between GCF and saliva participate in host defence mechanisms (Figure 3G,H). Serum‐specific proteins were enriched in nine GO terms (Figure 3I), with top terms like ‘blood coagulation’ accounting for over 33% of this group. These were associated with coagulation factors and SERPINs. Lipid metabolism was also enriched, suggesting altered lipid transport and immune regulation by apolipoproteins (Figure 3J).

Discussion

Saliva is a diagnostic surrogate for microbial and host biomarkers in periodontitis, but it remains highly debatable whether its content reflects that of subgingival plaque, GCF or serum. This study evaluated GCF and subgingival plaque at the full‐mouth scale in patients with periodontitis using advanced microbiome and proteomic analyses and compared these profiles with saliva and serum samples collected from the same patients. The findings confirmed previous observations of the presence of subgingival bacteria in saliva. However, subgingival plaque showed greater periodontal pathogen dominance, while saliva exhibited higher microbial diversity and captured host and microbial components also found in GCF and plaque. Serum displayed distinct proteomic profiles compared to both GCF and saliva. While these differences were not statistically significant, likely due to limited sample size or biological variability, the large effect sizes indicate biologically relevant protein variation. In contrast, GCF and saliva proteomes were highly similar, with sample type accounting for only 2.8% of the variation. Thus, saliva is easily obtainable, non‐invasive and quick to collect, containing a broad range of microbiome components and host proteins, including those typically found in GCF and subgingival plaque. This makes it a valuable oral fluid for disease screening in both dental and non‐dental settings.

Microbiological testing is increasingly used to support in the diagnosis and monitoring of patients with periodontitis (Manoil et al. 2024). Selecting appropriate sites and determining the optimal number of samples can be challenging. Earlier studies have typically selected only a few sites with the deepest periodontal pockets for subgingival plaque sampling (Belstrom et al. 2017; Duran‐Pinedo et al. 2023). However, periodontitis is a heterogeneous, site‐specific disease and microbial populations can vary significantly even within the same patient, depending on pocket depth and location within the dentition (Mason et al. 2018; Tanner et al. 2006). Oral bacteria have been shown to demonstrate specific tropisms towards different biological surfaces in the oral cavity (Baker et al. 2024). Theoretical models suggest that microbes with high plaque specificity could still appear in saliva at ~100‐fold lower abundance, consistent with passive transfer rather than active colonisation (Bostanci, Krog, et al. 2021; Mark Welch et al. 2020). Previous studies have indicated that saliva may be as effective as, or even superior to, subgingival plaque samples from the four deepest periodontal pockets in detecting selected periodontal pathogens (Umeda et al. 1998). Sampling only the deepest pockets may introduce bias, as it assumes that these sites are representative of the entire subgingival microbiome in a periodontitis patient. In contrast, sampling all teeth, as in the present study, offers a more complete representation of the microbiota across the entire periodontium. However, pooling healthy and diseased sites in periodontitis patients may introduce some variability in biomarker analysis, though the small proportion of healthy sites in the present study minimises their impact. Given the higher concentrations of inflammatory markers or periodontal pathogens in diseased sites, the inclusion of healthy sites is unlikely to significantly dilute disease‐specific signals (Abusleme et al. 2021; Grant et al. 2022). Nonetheless, site‐specific sampling offers greater sensitivity in detecting localised disease activity. Future studies could consider integrating both pooled and site‐level analyses to enhance diagnostic resolution.

The present findings confirmed that saliva and subgingival microbiomes share similarities but also exhibit notable differences (Mason et al. 2018; Tanner et al. 2006). However, neither saliva nor subgingival plaque alone is adequate for detecting the presence of certain periodontal pathogens. Thus, saliva may serve as a qualitative diagnostic indicator (presence/absence) for periodontal species, while subgingival plaque is more reliable for bacterial quantification. Interestingly, recent studies suggest a potential role for protozoan species in periodontal health and disease (Yaseen et al. 2021). In our study, Entamoeba nuttalli was detected in the subgingival plaque of one patient and in the saliva of another, with a mean relative abundance of 0.0003% across patients in each sample group. Importantly, the severity of periodontitis influences the overlap between salivary and subgingival microbiomes, with greater similarity observed as severity increases (Iniesta et al. 2023).

Within the context of periodontitis, the most relevant biofluid for protein analysis is GCF, which is a proximal tissue exudate (Bostanci and Bao 2017; Bostanci and Belibasakis 2018). While it is firmly established that GCF is the most appropriate local diagnostic medium for assessing periodontal inflammation, there are tangible impracticalities in its collection for a full‐mouth assessment. We used an MS‐based proteomics approach to measure the proteome and found 932, 803 and 195 proteins in GCF, saliva and serum, respectively. One hundred and nine proteins were common across all three body fluids; 685 were found both in GCF and saliva. The number of proteins identified in GCF and saliva was similar to or higher than previous reports, despite the small sample size, indicating good analytical depth (Bao et al. 2015; Bostanci et al. 2010; Bostanci, Selevsek, et al. 2018; Grant et al. 2022). Collecting GCF from both deep and shallow sites may have diluted periodontitis‐associated proteins, potentially reducing sensitivity and limiting the detection of disease‐specific biomarkers (Bao et al. 2020). However, the proportion of healthy sites within the periodontitis group was low, indicating that the contribution of non‐inflamed sites to the overall biomarker profile was likely minimal (Bostanci, Mitsakakis, et al. 2021; Lamster and Ahlo 2007).

Serum, on the other hand, yielded far fewer proteins than saliva and GCF. Although blood is estimated to contain over 10,000 distinct proteins, individual studies of the plasma proteome have typically identified fewer than 1000 proteins with high confidence (Keshishian et al. 2017). This disparity in detection depth is likely due to the wide range of protein abundance in serum and the dominance of a few highly abundant proteins constituting about 99% of the protein mass, which produce numerous peptides after digestion, masking those from low‐abundance proteins (Anderson and Anderson 2002). To alleviate this, many studies employed fractionation and depletion of highly abundant proteins; however, this tended to increase experimental variability (Bader et al. 2023). Although GCF samples with visible blood contamination were excluded during sample collection, the presence of haemoglobin in our samples aligns with previous findings that haemoglobulin can be detected in GCF even without clinically apparent bleeding (Ito et al. 2024). Due to its high abundance in GCF and saliva, haemoglobin may interfere with mass spectrometry‐based proteomic analysis by dominating ionisation and suppressing signals from low‐abundance peptides, thereby reducing analytical sensitivity and limiting the detection of other proteins, similar to the challenges commonly encountered in serum‐based proteomics (Bao et al. 2015; Bostanci, Selevsek, et al. 2018).

The ORA is used to determine which a priori defined protein sets are more present in a subset of ‘interesting’ proteins than what would be expected by chance (Huang et al. 2009). These enriched networks represented specific functional pathways including immune regulation, lipid metabolism, cell death and apoptotic process and tissue remodelling, potentially revealing shared biological aspects of the periodontitis process (Hajishengallis and Lamont 2021). A large portion of these shared immune‐related proteins were complement proteins. These results align with previous findings that the complement system is activated in the periodontal pocket or saliva of periodontitis patients (Grande et al. 2021; Hajishengallis et al. 2021). Our GCF proteomics studies in non‐human primates have also confirmed that complement is hyperactivated during periodontitis and targeting its central component, C3, has demonstrated therapeutic benefits (Bostanci, Bao, et al. 2018). The other proteins shared between saliva and GCF were significantly associated with key biological functions such as cell death and cellular responses to stress and included well‐characterised molecules like MMP‐8, cathelicidin antimicrobial peptide and high‐mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) (Bostanci, Mitsakakis, et al. 2021). The interplay between complement and HMGB1 is likely to play a pivotal role in inflammatory diseases such as periodontitis, given that both are potent soluble mediators involved in host defence and immune surveillance (Gaboriaud et al. 2022). In addition to immune‐related proteins, the shared proteins were enriched in metabolic processes, especially lipid metabolism, due to several apolipoproteins. This may be particularly relevant for monitoring the host's metabolic‐inflammatory status in periodontitis patients with diabetes mellitus, as elevated apolipoprotein B levels have been reported in both plasma and GCF (Noguchi et al. 2014). Collectively, these data also provided novel insights into periodontitis pathophysiology.

There were limitations to the current investigation. Only three patients were included for full‐mouth gingival and subgingival sampling, as the procedure is time‐consuming and can cause discomfort to the patient; therefore, the sample size was kept to a minimum. Small sample sizes may not adequately capture the natural variability within populations, leading to non‐generalisable results. Yet, calculating sample size in multi‐omics experiments is complex due to the high‐dimensional nature of the data. Since the current experiments were exploratory and discovery‐driven, smaller sample sizes with full‐mouth scale sampling are acceptable for initial discovery. Testing thousands of individual features (e.g., proteins) increases the risk of false positives. Thus, to strengthen our findings beyond simple statistical differences, we performed enrichment analysis with stringent multiple testing corrections (q < 0.05), which enabled us to identify unique biological functions associated with each sample source in comparison to all identified proteins (Huang da et al. 2009). However, it is important to note that well‐characterised proteins often have more extensive annotations than lesser‐known proteins, which may introduce bias and limit the comprehensiveness of pathway analyses, potentially influencing the interpretation of the results. Another limitation of this study is the lack of functional annotation of the predicted genes in the metagenomic datasets, which may have provided additional insights into the metabolic and functional potential of the microbial communities.

Conclusion

Saliva analysis is effective for broad screening of the periodontal microbiome profile, whereas subgingival plaque delivers greater precision in identifying specific periodontal pathogens directly associated with a periodontal pocket. Saliva contains slightly fewer but comparable number of host protein components compared to GCF, with similarly enriched functions. This makes it a valuable fluid for disease screening in both dental and non‐dental settings.
