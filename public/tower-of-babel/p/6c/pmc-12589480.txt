Hierarchical attention mechanism combined with deep neural networks for accurate semantic segmentation of dental structures in panoramic radiographs
Authors: Mehrdad Esmaeili, Zahra Dalili, Hossein Sadr, Amirreza Mousavie, Aryan Faghihi, Reza Saei, Mojdeh Nazari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Computer vision, a rapidly advancing branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has gained significant attention in medical and dental applications. Semantic segmentation, a key technique within computer vision, enables the precise identification and delineation of objects at the pixel level, offering transformative potential for diagnostic imaging in dentistry. Panoramic radiographs are essential for diagnosing oral and maxillofacial conditions, yet their interpretation remains time-consuming and prone to human error, particularly in complex cases. This study evaluates the performance of a deep learning-based semantic segmentation model designed to identify and classify 24 distinct anatomical and pathological structures in panoramic radiographs. A dataset of 844 annotated panoramic images was collected from multiple radiography centers and used for training and testing. The model employs a hierarchical multi-scale attention mechanism to enhance accuracy by analyzing images at varying resolutions. Performance was assessed using key metrics, including specificity, accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and Intersection over Union (IoU). The proposed model demonstrated robust performance, achieving an overall accuracy of 98.73%, specificity of 98.86%, IoU value of 78.76%, precision of 86.97%, recall of 86.97%, and an F1 score of 84.54%. Notably, structures such as implants and amalgam restorations were identified with high reliability, while challenges persisted in detecting dental pulp and caries due to overlapping structures and subtle anatomical details. The deep neural network developed in this study exhibits significant potential for aiding dental professionals in accurately segmenting and identifying anatomical features in panoramic radiographs. While limitations exist in detecting specific intricate structures, the model’s performance underscores the value of AI-driven tools in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning in dentistry. Future work may explore complementary imaging modalities to address the remaining challenges.

Introduction

Deep learning, a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has revolutionized numerous medical domains, offering transformative tools for diagnosis, treatment planning, and patient care. Among its most impactful applications is computer vision, which enables machines to interpret and analyze visual data with remarkable precision1–4. This technology has recently gained significant traction in dentistry, particularly in analyzing diagnostic imaging modalities such as panoramic radiographs5,6. These radiographic images are indispensable for diagnosing a wide range of oral and maxillofacial conditions, including impacted teeth, bone anomalies, cysts, and tumors. However, their interpretation remains a time-intensive and error-prone process, especially in complex cases where subtle anatomical details may be overlooked by human practitioners7,8.

Semantic segmentation is one of the most promising computer vision applications in medical imaging. This technique partitions an image into meaningful regions and assigns a label to each pixel based on its content. This approach facilitates the precise identification and localization of objects within an image, such as individual teeth, jawbones, and other critical anatomical structures in panoramic radiographs9,10. Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), a cornerstone of deep learning, have demonstrated exceptional efficacy in semantic segmentation tasks, achieving state-of-the-art results across various medical imaging domains, including dentistry11–13.

Despite these advancements, significant challenges persist in accurately segmenting dental structures from panoramic radiographs. The primary difficulty arises from the extreme variation in the scale and complexity of anatomical features within a single image. For instance, a model must simultaneously identify large, low-detail structures like the mandible and maxilla, while also delineating small, intricate features such as root canals, apical lesions, or the fine margins of dental caries. Standard Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), including common U-Net architectures, often struggle to capture both broad contextual information and high-resolution details simultaneously. This can lead to imprecise boundaries and missed detections of smaller objects, particularly in images with high scale variance14,15.

While multi-scale inference techniques have been explored to address this issue, many existing approaches analyze images at different resolutions independently and later merge the outputs, failing to learn the rich, hierarchical relationships between scales. This creates a critical gap in the literature as the lack of a sophisticated mechanism that can dynamically prioritize and integrate features from different scales based on their relevance.

To address this gap, this study proposes a novel framework centered on a hierarchical multi-scale attention mechanism. The core innovation of our approach is that it does not treat scales in isolation. Instead, it learns relative attention masks between adjacent scales, enabling the model to intelligently weigh and combine fine-grained details from higher resolutions with contextual understanding from lower resolutions. This hierarchical integration ensures that local precision and global context are effectively balanced, offering a more robust solution for the comprehensive segmentation of 24 distinct dental classes. This research seeks to demonstrate that our proposed model (1) achieves superior accuracy in segmenting dental structures with high scale variance compared to existing methods, and (2) maintains high performance across a wide range of anatomical, pathological, and restorative classes. Accordingly, the goal of this paper is to provide a powerful tool that enhances diagnostic accuracy, supports treatment planning, and ultimately improves patient outcomes in dentistry by delivering a detailed and reliable anatomical map from a single panoramic radiograph.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows: Sect. “Materials and methods” describes the materials and methods, including dataset preparation, network architecture, and evaluation metrics. Section “Experiments and results” presents the experimental results and a detailed discussion in Sect. “Discussion”. Finally, Sect. “Conclusion” concludes the study, highlighting its contributions and implications for future research.

Materials and methods

Dataset

The dataset utilized in this study comprised 844 panoramic radiographic images collected from multiple radiography centers located in Tehran and Rasht between 2018 and 2023. The Ethics Committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences granted ethical approval for the study (Approval Code: IR.GUMS.REC.1403.176), ensuring compliance with ethical standards for medical research. Notably, informed consent was obtained from all subjects and/or their legal guardians.

To facilitate detailed analysis, the images were uploaded to an online annotation platform (https://www.makesense.ai) for pixel-wise labeling. Dental experts performed meticulous annotations according to predefined classes, ensuring high-quality ground truth data. The labeled data were exported as JSON files following rigorous quality control and validation by a certified radiologist. These JSON files were subsequently processed using the CoCo library and Python programming to extract and analyze the labeling information, generating corresponding mask images for semantic segmentation tasks (Fig. 1).

The mask images were formatted as indexed images with dimensions of 1024 × 1024 pixels to optimize computational efficiency and reduce file size. Indexed images are specialized formats in which pixel values represent indices rather than direct color information (e.g., grayscale, RGB, or RGBA). These indices are mapped to specific labels via an interface dictionary file, enabling precise representation of annotated regions. The resulting mask images served as the ground truth for training and evaluating the model.

The dataset was divided into training/validation and test subsets, encompassing 24 distinct classes representing various anatomical structures, dental pathologies, and restorative materials. The distribution of data across these subsets is summarized in Table 1. A custom Python script was developed to process the JSON files and generate mask images. This script ensured that each radiographic image was paired with its corresponding mask image, facilitating seamless integration into the deep learning pipeline. The dataset was organized into two folders: one containing the original radiographic images and the other housing the corresponding mask images. This structured organization enabled efficient model training and evaluation.

To ensure the consistency and reliability of the ground truth data, an inter-rater reliability analysis was performed. A random sample of 40 panoramic radiographs was selected from the dataset. These images were independently annotated by a second certified maxillofacial radiologist who was blinded to the original annotations. The segmentations from the original expert and the second expert were then compared on a pixel-wise basis using the Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC). The comparison yielded a mean DSC of 0.91 ± 0.04, indicating a high level of agreement between the two raters and confirming the robustness of the annotation protocol.

Methodology

The methodology is based on a hierarchical multi-scale attention mechanism combined with two network architectures of ResNet-50 and HRNet-OCR. The overall architecture consists of a backbone network for feature extraction, along with semantic and attention prediction heads. Post-processing steps are applied to generate the final high-resolution segmentation map. More details in this regard are provided in the following sections.

Hierarchical multi-scale attention

The proposed hierarchical multi-scale attention mechanism builds upon the conceptual foundation of earlier multi-scale approaches14, such as the Explicit Method16. Both frameworks employ dense masking techniques across multiple image scales to enhance segmentation accuracy. However, our method introduces a novel approach to combining multi-scale predictions by leveraging relative attention masks between adjacent scales rather than relying on fixed attention masks for predefined scales.

During the training phase, the model operates on paired adjacent scales, ensuring that relative attention is learned dynamically between these scales. Specifically, given a set of lower-resolution image features, the network predicts a dense, pixel-wise relative attention map that captures the relationship between two image scales. To generate scaled inputs, the original image is resized to create multiple versions at different resolutions (e.g., 1× and 0.5× of the original size). These rescaled images serve as inputs to the network, enabling it to predict relative attention across various scales during training.

The learned attention maps are applied hierarchically at inference time, combining predictions from N scales through a sequential chain of computations. The process begins with lower-resolution scales, encodes broader contextual information, and progressively refines predictions by incorporating higher-resolution scales that capture fine-grained details. This hierarchical integration ensures that global context and local precision are effectively balanced, resulting in robust and accurate segmentation outputs.

Figure 2 illustrates the computational pipeline of this hierarchical architecture, detailing both the training and inference phases. As can be seen, during training, the model operates on pairs of adjacent scales (e.g., Scale 1 and Scale 2). For each scale, features are extracted by the backbone network (Trunk), and a preliminary segmentation map (Seg) is generated. A dedicated attention head (Attn) then predicts a dense, pixel-wise relative attention map (α1​). This map determines how to weigh and combine the segmentation outputs from the two scales, with one branch being upsampled to match resolutions, ultimately producing a unified output for loss calculation. At inference time, this process is applied as a sequential chain across multiple scales (e.g., three scales are shown). The combined prediction from the first two scales is recursively merged with the prediction from the next scale, using a newly generated attention map ( α2​). This hierarchical integration starts with lower-resolution scales, which encode broader contextual information, and progressively refines the prediction by incorporating higher-resolution scales that capture fine-grained details. Operations like upsampling (up) and downsampling (down) are used to align feature map dimensions before they are combined through element-wise multiplication and addition.

Network architecture

The proposed network architecture integrates advanced components to achieve state-of-the-art performance in semantic segmentation tasks for panoramic radiographic images. The architecture comprises two primary components: the backbone network and the prediction heads (semantic and attention prediction modules). Below, we describe each component in detail.

Backbone Network .The backbone network is the foundational feature extraction component, providing hierarchical representations of input images at multiple scales. ResNet-5017 is the backbone for initial experiments, configured with an output stride of 8 to preserve spatial resolution while capturing high-level semantic features (Fig. 3). This configuration ensures that sufficient spatial information is retained for pixel-wise predictions, forming the default trunk for the network. To achieve optimal performance, however, the architecture transitions to a more advanced backbone, HRNet-OCR18, which enhances feature representation by maintaining high-resolution streams throughout the network and incorporating Object-Contextual Representations (OCR) (Fig. 4). OCR explicitly models relationships between pixels and their surrounding contexts, enabling the network to capture both localized details and global contextual information. The integration of HRNet-OCR significantly improves the network’s ability to delineate complex dental structures in panoramic radiographs, making it the primary choice for achieving state-of-the-art results.

The semantic prediction head generates class-specific segmentation maps through a fully convolutional architecture designed to refine feature representations and produce precise pixel-wise predictions. It comprises two 3 × 3 convolutional layers that extract localized spatial features while preserving spatial resolution, followed by batch normalization (BN) and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations, which normalize feature maps to stabilize training and introduce non-linearity. Additionally, a 1 × 1 convolutional layer projects features into a lower-dimensional space to reduce computational complexity, and a final 1 × 1 convolutional layer outputs num_classes channels, corresponding to the number of classes in the dataset. The prediction head comprises semantic and attention prediction heads. The semantic prediction head generates class-specific segmentation maps through a fully convolutional architecture designed to refine feature representations and produce precise pixel-wise predictions. It comprises two 3 × 3 convolutional layers that extract localized spatial features while preserving spatial resolution, followed by batch normalization (BN) and rectified linear unit (ReLU) activations, which normalize feature maps to stabilize training and introduce non-linearity. Additionally, a 1 × 1 convolutional layer projects features into a lower-dimensional space to reduce computational complexity, and a final 1 × 1 convolutional layer outputs num_classes channels, corresponding to the number of classes in the dataset. This architecture ensures efficient extraction of semantic information while maintaining spatial precision, enabling accurate and detailed segmentation outputs.

The attention prediction head is structurally analogous to the semantic head but differs in its final layer. Instead of outputting num_classes channels, the attention head produces a single-channel output representing the attention map. This map modulates the semantic logits, emphasizing regions of interest and suppressing irrelevant areas. The attention mechanism enhances the model’s ability to focus on salient features, improving segmentation accuracy.

The input features for the semantic and attention heads are contingent upon the backbone architecture utilized in the network. When ResNet-50 is employed as the backbone, both the semantic and attention heads receive features extracted from the final stage of the network. In contrast, when HRNet-OCR is the backbone, the semantic and attention heads are fed features derived from the OCR block, integrating multi-scale contextual information to enhance feature representation. HRNet-OCR incorporates an auxiliary semantic head that operates on intermediate features extracted directly from the HRNet trunk before the OCR block. This auxiliary head consists of two 1 × 1 convolutional layers, two Batch Normalization layers, and two ReLU activation layers. The auxiliary semantic head refines feature representations by leveraging these intermediate features, thereby improving segmentation accuracy.

Post-processing is performed after applying the attention mechanism to the semantic logits to align the predictions with the original image dimensions. The resulting segmentation maps are upsampled using bilinear interpolation, which ensures smooth and precise alignment with the target image size. This upscaling process is critical for producing high-resolution segmentation outputs suitable for clinical applications. By maintaining spatial precision and clarity, the model generates detailed segmentation maps that can effectively aid dental professionals in diagnosing and analyzing oral and maxillofacial structures. The refined feature extraction and post-processing steps ensure the model can deliver accurate and clinically relevant results.

Experiments and results

Implementation details

The proposed model was implemented using the PyTorch framework, a widely adopted deep learning library known for its flexibility and efficiency in building and training neural networks. The implementation was performed on the Google Colab platform, which provides access to high-performance GPUs, enabling efficient processing of large-scale radiographic datasets.

The dataset was preprocessed into indexed images with 1024 × 1024 pixel dimensions to ensure robust generalizability and reduce computational overhead. The mask images were generated from JSON files using the CoCo library, with pixel-wise annotations provided by dental experts. A custom API function was developed to handle data augmentation dynamically during training, ensuring that random transformations such as rotation, flipping, and scaling were applied consistently to both the base images and their corresponding mask images.

The training process utilized 492 radiographic images divided into training and validation subsets, with the dataset augmented to enhance generalizability. A 5-fold cross-validation (k = 5) strategy was employed to evaluate the model’s performance and robustness. To ensure that each fold was a representative sample of the overall data, particularly given the 24 distinct classes, we used stratified sampling. This process partitions the dataset into 5 subsets (folds) while maintaining the same percentage of samples for each class in each fold. The model was trained 5 times, with each fold serving as the test set once. The final performance metrics reported in Table 3 are the average results across these 5 folds.

The trained model was evaluated on a separate test set comprising 352 panoramic radiographic images. A dedicated Python program was developed to perform pixel-wise comparisons between the model’s predictions and the ground truth annotations obtained from human experts. The differences were analyzed to compute evaluation metrics for each class.

Hyperparameters

A set of carefully chosen hyperparameters guided the training process of the proposed model to ensure optimal performance and generalizability. The model was trained for 150 epochs using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.005 and a batch size 1. This batch size was chosen as the maximum possible due to the high GPU memory requirements of the 1024 × 1024 pixel input images on the available hardware. Data augmentation techniques, including rotation, flipping, and scaling, were dynamically applied to the input images and their corresponding mask images during training. The summary of hyperparameters is provided in Table 2. Notably, this configuration balanced computational efficiency and model accuracy, enabling the system to achieve state-of-the-art performance in segmenting dental structures from panoramic radiographs.

Among the individual classes, certain teeth exhibited particularly high accuracy and reliability. For instance, the lower canine (L3), upper third molar (U8), upper second molar (U7), upper canine (U3), upper lateral tooth (U2), and upper central tooth (U1) demonstrated optimal performance, with accuracy values exceeding 98%. This superior performance can be attributed to the prominent visibility and distinct anatomical positioning of these teeth in panoramic images, making them more easily segmented.

In contrast, some classes posed greater challenges for the model. For example, dental pulp segmentation achieved a relatively lower accuracy of 95.31% and an IoU value of 61.45%, reflecting the inherent difficulty in identifying fine-grained structures such as root canals, particularly in the apical regions where the narrowing of the canal complicates differentiation between nerve tissues and surrounding dental structures. Similarly, the detection of caries and composite restorations yielded lower precision and recall values (71.55% recall for caries and 77.09% recall for composite ), suggesting that the model may not be optimally suited for detecting subtle or less distinct features in panoramic radiographs. These findings underscore the limitations of panoramic imaging modalities for specific diagnostic tasks and suggest the potential utility of complementary imaging techniques, such as bitewing or periapical radiographs, for more precise identification of these features.

Discussion

Dental panoramic radiography is pivotal in detecting and diagnosing conditions within the oral, maxillofacial, and jaw regions, serving as a cornerstone for treatment planning in dental clinics. An accurate interpretation of panoramic radiographs is critical for effective clinical decision-making. While numerous studies have explored artificial intelligence models for analyzing panoramic images, their objectives have primarily focused on classifying the presence of specific diseases19 or differentiating disease types through panoramic imaging20. Some studies have implemented semantic segmentation techniques21, which classify each pixel individually but often fail to distinguish between individual teeth or complex anatomical structures. These limitations highlight the need for advanced AI-driven approaches to address the intricate challenges posed by panoramic radiographs.

The hierarchical multi-scale attention model proposed in this study represents a significant advancement in semantic segmentation for dental applications. Unlike traditional methods that treat each scale independently, our approach combines global context and local detail through a stepwise linking of scales. By learning relative attention between adjacent scales during training, the model effectively integrates information from various resolutions, enhancing accuracy without substantially increasing computational load. Consequently, the proposed model demonstrates superior versatility and robustness in practical clinical applications. This approach offers several advantages: (1) it captures nuanced relationships between adjacent scales, enhancing the model’s ability to generalize across diverse image resolutions; (2) it maintains computational flexibility by avoiding rigid scale definitions; and (3) it leverages both fine-grained details from higher-resolution scales and broader contextual information from lower-resolution scales, thereby improving overall prediction accuracy.

The quantitative results from our ablation study and baseline comparisons (Table 5) also provide strong empirical evidence for the superiority of our proposed architecture. Our model significantly outperformed the standard U-Net architecture, which is likely attributable to the HRNet-OCR backbone’s superior ability to maintain high-resolution spatial representations throughout the network. This is critical in panoramic radiography, where both large structures and fine details must be segmented accurately. Furthermore, the incremental performance improvement observed in the ablation study validates the contribution of our specific design choices. The final performance leap achieved after adding the hierarchical attention mechanism demonstrates its key role in intelligently integrating multi-scale features, leading to more precise boundary delineation and validating our core hypothesis.

While our framework is built upon the established HRNet-OCR architecture, these results underscore the value of our methodological refinement. The significant performance increase shown in the ablation study confirms that the proposed hierarchical attention mechanism is not merely an incremental change, but a key component responsible for the model’s superior accuracy. This highlights how targeted refinements to existing architectures can yield substantial performance gains in specific, challenging domains like dental imaging.

In contrast to the tiling method introduced by Rohrer et al.22, which divides images into smaller patches for processing, we maintained high-resolution input images (1024 × 1024 pixels) while reducing the batch size during training. This strategy not only preserves critical spatial details but also simplifies the training process, offering a more efficient alternative to tiling. Our results confirm that maintaining high-resolution inputs improves segmentation performance, particularly for fine-grained structures such as dental pulp and root canals.

A recent study by Hao et al.23 introduced SemiTNet, a semi-supervised transformer-based framework designed to enhance tooth segmentation in panoramic radiographs, particularly in cases involving missing teeth. While their work demonstrated the efficacy of the hierarchical concept in capturing multi-scale details, it did not evaluate the performance of SemiTNet for teeth with special conditions, such as root-treated teeth, residual roots, fillings, or crowns. In contrast, our model explicitly addresses these complexities by segmenting 24 distinct classes, including anatomical structures and pathological features. Despite the increased complexity of handling a larger number of groups compared to other studies, our hierarchical multi-scale attention model achieved competitive results, as evidenced by the F1 score. This underscores our approach’s superiority in terms of scalability and generalizability.

Another distinguishing feature of our study is the utilization of radiographic data from multiple devices across diverse medical centers, as opposed to prior studies that relied on data from a single device within one hospital. This approach addresses a standard limitation in the generalizability of segmentation models across different radiographic equipment. By incorporating data from varied sources, we ensured the model was trained on a broader range of imaging characteristics, enhancing its robustness and applicability in real-world clinical settings. Prior research has shown that combining panoramic radiographs from diverse institutions significantly improves model performance24, further validating our methodology.

The segmentation model developed in this study demonstrated satisfactory results across all 24 dental classes. Notably, structures such as the lower canine, upper third molar, upper second molar, upper canine, upper lateral, and upper central teeth exhibited optimal accuracy due to their prominent visibility and distinct anatomical positioning in panoramic images. However, third molars presented slightly lower detection accuracy, likely due to their anatomical and morphological variability across developmental stages. Among the defined classes, implants and amalgam restorations achieved the highest evaluation metrics, reflecting the distinct visual characteristics of these materials. Conversely, the model encountered challenges in detecting dental pulp, particularly in the apical regions of teeth, where the narrowing of the canal complicates differentiation between nerve tissues and surrounding dental structures. Human annotations often rely on visual approximations of canal length, extending into apical regions, whereas pixel-based system analysis struggles to achieve similar precision. This limitation highlights an area for future improvement by integrating complementary imaging modalities.

One noteworthy aspect of our findings is the performance discrepancy between structures with clear visual features, such as implants and amalgam restorations, and those that are more clinically subtle and challenging, such as dental pulp and caries. This observation aligns with the core challenges faced by clinicians in daily practice. It is crucial to emphasize that the primary objective of this study was to develop a foundational and comprehensive segmentation model rather than an end-stage diagnostic tool for the most complex cases. We posit that the accurate segmentation of all 24 structures—both simple and complex—serves as an essential prerequisite for higher-level, clinically-oriented AI analyses. For instance, to automatically assess the proximity of a carious lesion to the dental pulp or detect recurrent decay under a restoration, an AI system must first be able to reliably identify and delineate the boundaries of the tooth, the caries, and the pulp. Our model provides this fundamental, pixel-wise map. Therefore, while it excels at tasks trivial for clinicians, its true value lies in creating a structured, machine-readable foundation upon which future, more sophisticated diagnostic modules can be built to address the very challenges highlighted, such as assessing root canal filling adequacy or the relationship between third molars and surrounding structures.

One notable limitation of our research is the inherent challenge of overlapping structures in panoramic images, where each pixel can only be assigned to a single class in semantic segmentation. This constraint occasionally resulted in ambiguities during pixel-wise classification, particularly for overlapping anatomical features. Additionally, as our results indicate, the model exhibited lower reliability in identifying caries, composite restorations, and dental pulp. This underscores a critical point that our model currently does not resolve the most diagnostically challenging interpretation tasks where clinical support is most needed, such as determining the extent of carious lesions, assessing the relationship between restorations and the pulp, or delineating periapical radiolucencies. These tasks remain significant challenges due to the inherent limitations of 2D panoramic imaging. Alternative imaging modalities, such as bitewing or periapical radiographs, are recommended for more accurate dental caries and composite restorations assessments.

Despite these challengesand while direct comparison is challenging due to different datasets, our proposed hierarchical multi-scale attention model appears competitive with state-of-the-art models regarding scalability and generalizability. For instance, while Mask R-CNN25 and U-Net-based models26–28 achieved F1 scores of 88%, 90%, 90%, and 88.9%, respectively, they were trained on fewer classes (e.g., 10–12 groups) and smaller datasets. In contrast, our model successfully balanced accuracy and generalizability across 24 distinct classes, even when trained on a dataset of 492 images. This achievement highlights the model’s ability to handle complex, multi-class segmentation tasks with remarkable precision.

In conclusion, this study’s hierarchical multi-scale attention model represents a significant advancement in dental image analysis. The model achieves exceptional performance across a wide range of anatomical and pathological structures by leveraging multi-scale contextual information and maintaining high-resolution inputs. While challenges remain in detecting fine-grained features and subtle pathologies, the proposed approach demonstrates clear superiority over existing methods regarding scalability, generalizability, and clinical applicability. Future work will focus on integrating complementary imaging modalities and refining the model to address remaining limitations, further advancing the role of AI in dental diagnostics and treatment planning.

Conclusion

Integrating artificial intelligence (AI) into dentistry has demonstrated significant potential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and streamline clinical workflows. In this study, we proposed a novel hierarchical multi-scale attention model for the semantic segmentation of dental structures in panoramic radiographs. The model was trained and evaluated on a diverse dataset of 844 annotated images encompassing 24 distinct classes, including various tooth types, dental pathologies, and restorative materials. Our findings highlight the efficacy of the proposed approach, achieving an overall accuracy of 98.73%, specificity of 98.86%, IoU value of 78.76%, precision of 86.97%, recall of 86.97%, and F1 score of 84.54%. These results highlight the model’s ability to accurately identify and segment complex anatomical and pathological features, including teeth, restorations, and implants. The hierarchical multi-scale approach effectively combines fine-grained details with broader contextual information, enhancing segmentation accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency. This innovation enhances segmentation accuracy and ensures flexibility in handling diverse image resolutions, making the model adaptable to real-world clinical scenarios. Furthermore, we maintained critical spatial details without compromising computational efficiency by leveraging high-resolution inputs (1024 × 1024 pixels) and reducing batch size during training. This strategy proved superior to traditional tiling methods.

Despite the success of our proposed model, it faced challenges in detecting subtle structures such as dental pulp, particularly in apical regions. It exhibited lower reliability in identifying caries and composite restorations. These limitations underscore the need for complementary imaging modalities, such as bitewing or periapical radiographs, to improve diagnostic precision for specific tasks. Additionally, overlapping structures in panoramic images posed challenges for pixel-wise classification, a limitation inherent to semantic segmentation frameworks.

Future work will be critically focused on leveraging the foundational segmentation map produced by our model to address these specific, high-value clinical challenges. This includes developing second-stage AI modules to: (1) assess the depth of caries and its proximity to the pulp, (2) detect recurrent caries at restoration margins, (3) evaluate the adequacy of root canal fillings by analyzing their extent within the segmented root structure, and (4) precisely map the location of the mandibular canal relative to impacted third molars. By building upon this comprehensive segmentation, we aim to transition from foundational image processing to providing meaningful, actionable insights where human interpretation is most prone to error.

Exploring transformer-based architectures and semi-supervised learning approaches may enhance performance, particularly for rare or ambiguous cases. Expanding the dataset to include more diverse imaging equipment and patient populations will improve the model’s generalizability. These efforts aim to advance AI-driven tools for dental diagnostics, ultimately supporting clinicians in treatment planning and improving patient outcomes.
