Infection of Endobronchial Valves by Mycobacterium kansasii Following Endoscopic Volume Reduction
Authors: Stephany Ivonne Briones Alvarado, Javier García López, Francisco José Caballero Segura, Javier De Miguel-Díez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndoscopic lung volume reduction (ELVR) using endobronchial valves (EBV) has become established as a therapeutic option in patients with advanced COPD. Although infectious complications associated with this procedure are rare, the creation of functionally isolated lung segments and the presence of prosthetic surfaces can promote secretion retention and biofilm formation, predisposing patients to the development of persistent infections caused by opportunistic microorganisms. We present the case of a patient who underwent ELVR with EBV implantation and developed functional deterioration secondary to a Mycobacterium kansasii infection as a complication of the procedure. This case suggests that relative hypoxia induced by EBVs, theoretically a limiting factor for bacterial growth, could paradoxically contribute to creating a niche for slow-growing bacteria such as mycobacteria.Keywords: Endobronchial, Valves, Mycobacteria, Volume reduction

To date, limited information is available regarding biofilm-associated infections in patients undergoing endoscopic lung volume reduction (ELVR).1 Available evidence suggests that patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) experience a higher number of exacerbations after ELVR when lower airway bacterial colonization is present. However, only a few studies have directly assessed the presence of biofilms on endobronchial valves (EBVs).1, 2 We report the case of a 67-year-old woman, a former smoker with a 42-pack-year, diagnosed with high-risk, non-frequent-exacerbator COPD, who underwent ELVR with EBV implantation in the anterior and apicoposterior segments of the left upper lobe, as well as in the lingula. Eight months after the procedure, the patient developed progressive worsening of dyspnea to grade 3 on the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) scale, accompanied by productive cough with mucopurulent sputum, requiring three hospital admissions for exacerbations of COPD. Given the suspected causal relationship between EBV implantation and clinical deterioration, repeat bronchoscopy was performed.

The EBVs were found to be slightly displaced, with abundant mucopurulent secretions and granulomas. The valves were removed, and bronchoalveolar lavage cultures yielded Mycobacterium kansasii. Treatment with ethambutol, rifampicin, and azithromycin was initiated. Approximately three months later, the patient showed progressive clinical improvement, and follow-up bronchoscopy demonstrated a marked reduction in granuloma size (Fig. 1). A new bronchoalveolar lavage was performed, this time without microbiological isolation. Previous studies have shown that patients with pre-existing bacterial colonization in the lower respiratory tract experience a higher number of exacerbations after LVRT, probably due to secondary colonization of the EBV by these microorganisms. However, our patient showed no evidence of colonization or chronic bronchial infection in sputum cultures (requested during follow-up visits) prior to EBV placement. As with other medical devices, such as dental implants or cardiovascular prostheses, bacteria can adhere to the surface of BAVs and form biofilms, which promote persistent infection 1–2. Although BAV-associated infection after BRV is rare, it can lead to recurrent exacerbations.3

Moreover, COPD patients with active biofilms on EBVs have been reported to exhibit higher residual volume, lower forced expiratory volume in one second, worse diffusing capacity, shorter six-minute walk distance, more frequent exacerbations, and increased mortality compared with patients without biofilm formation, with no significant differences in mMRC or COPD Assessment Test scores.1, 2, 3, 4 In addition to pre-existing colonization, other factors influencing biofilm formation include the patient's immune status, the number of implanted EBVs, and the duration of implantation.5 Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus, fast-growing pathogens, are the microorganisms most frequently identified in EBV biofilms.1 Currently, the only direct method for detecting biofilm-associated infections on medical devices is FISHseq, which combines fluorescence in situ hybridization with polymerase chain reaction and sequencing, allowing characterization of microorganisms on the implant or surrounding tissue and differentiation between colonization and active infection.1 This technique is limited to advanced research laboratories; consequently, it is not available in most centers, including ours, which is why we were unable to perform this test.

The inability to perform these tests points to a significant diagnostic gap in cases such as this. To date, no studies have reported the detection of mycobacteria on EBVs. Nevertheless, both Mycobacterium tuberculosis and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are well known to form biofilms on prosthetic surfaces.6 In theory, reduced oxygenation in EBV-treated lung segments could impair the growth of aerobic microorganisms- and this principle has even been explored as an adjunctive treatment in multidrug-resistant tuberculosis. Although M. kansasii is strictly aerobic, it tolerates relative hypoxia.7 EBV-induced hypoventilation and relative hypoxia do not prevent mycobacterial persistence or proliferation, as they are slow-growing pathogens. On the contrary, NTM, including M. kansasii, tolerate hypoxic conditions and adopt a low-metabolic, biofilm-associated phenotype that favors persistence under stress conditions.8 Although prior sputum cultures did not demonstrate chronic colonization, systematic screening for nontuberculous mycobacteria before EBV implantation was not performed.

Therefore, pre-existing low-grade colonization cannot be definitively excluded. Chest computed tomography prior to EBV implantation showed no cavitary lesions, bronchiectasis, or structural abnormalities in the treated segments that could serve as a preexisting niche for mycobacterial colonization. Furthermore, follow-up imaging after clinical deterioration revealed no new lesions. However, the temporal relationship between EBV implantation, clinical deterioration, microbiological isolation of M. kansasii, and subsequent improvement after valve removal and antimicrobial therapy suggests a probable device-associated infectious process. This case illustrates that endobronchial valve implantation for lung volume reduction may create a permissive microenvironment for opportunistic pathogens such as M. kansasii, even in the absence of pre-existing cavitary lung disease. These findings underscore the need for further research into biofilm formation on endobronchial devices and for the development of more accessible diagnostic strategies to detect and monitor device-associated infections.
