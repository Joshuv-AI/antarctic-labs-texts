Relationship Between Quality of Reproductive Life and Semen Quality: The Moderating Roles of Social Support and Fertility Stress
Authors: Jumanali Mireyi, Lihuan Zhi, Abulizi Maierhaba, Hua Xu, Lijuan He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReports on whether social support and fertility stress moderate the association between quality of reproductive life and semen quality are limited. This study explored the relationship between quality of reproductive life and semen quality, focusing on the moderating roles of social support and fertility stress. This case–control study involved 1024 adult males who attended our institution’s Reproductive Medicine Centre. Participants were categorized into the abnormal (n = 467) and normal semen groups (n = 557) based on World Health Organization standards. Data on quality of reproductive life, social support, and fertility stress were collected via questionnaires, and semen quality parameters were assessed. Relationships and moderating effects were evaluated using nonparametric tests and grouped linear regression models. In the abnormal semen group, quality of reproductive life positively correlated with sperm concentration, total sperm count, sperm progressive motility, and sperm total motility. Quality of reproductive life positively and negatively correlated with social support and fertility stress, respectively. Social support positively influenced sperm total motility, whereas no similar results were found in the normal semen group. In the abnormal semen group, low fertility stress improved the relationship between quality of reproductive life and sperm total motility; however; such significance was not found in the control group. In conclusion, quality of reproductive life is positively associated with semen quality and social support but negatively correlated with fertility stress. Enhancing social support and reducing stress is critical for improving the quality of reproductive life and semen quality, particularly in men with abnormal semen parameters.Keywords: Semen quality, Quality of reproductive life, Social support, Fertility stress

Introduction

Infertility is a major issue in reproductive health that negatively impacts the psychological well-being and quality of life (QoL) of individuals. It poses a major challenge to the marital life of the couple and the family and is a global public health concern affecting couples of childbearing age.

World Health Organization (WHO) defines infertility as the absence of any contraception for > 12 months and normal sexual life without successful pregnancy (Sørensen et al., 2024). Research indicates that 10–15% of couples in their childbearing years worldwide face infertility issues, and male factors contribute to roughly 50% of these instances (Kleiman et al., 1999). The seminal study by Carlsen et al. (1992) first proposed a declining trend in male semen quality over the past five decades, a conclusion that has sparked extensive academic debate. While numerous studies have indicated progressive deterioration in semen quality across global regions, methodological and demographic heterogeneities (e.g., geographic disparities and population selection criteria) have hindered the establishment of a robust consensus. A meta-analysis revealed marked declines in sperm concentration and total sperm count among male populations in North America, Europe, and Australia from 1973 to 2011 (Levine et al., 2017). However, a Belgian longitudinal study investigating temporal trends in semen quality demonstrated no statistically significant alterations in sperm concentration or fertility potential among candidate sperm donors between 1995 and 2017 (Sugihara et al., 2021). A retrospective Argentine study similarly reported no significant deterioration in semen quality within the studied region from 2001 to 2020 (Ramírez et al., 2022). Chinese epidemiological investigations have documented a progressive decline in semen quality among Chinese males over the past 35 years (Li et al., 2021). China has witnessed a rising prevalence of infertility, with 12–15% of couples of childbearing age affected—translating to over 50 million cases nationwide—where male etiological factors account for approximately 30% of these cases. Consequently, male infertility has emerged as a critical public health priority, demanding urgent interdisciplinary interventions (Sun & Hu, 2019).

QoL, which is a comprehensive measure of an individual’s physical, mental, and social functioning as well as their subjective judgments and satisfaction, is defined by the WHO as “individual’s perception of their position in life in the context of the culture and value systems in which they live and in relation to their goals, expectations, standards and concerns” (The World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment, 1998). It is a broad concept that encompasses an individual’s physical health, psychological state, level of independence, social relationships, personal beliefs, and their relationship to salient features of the environment (Palomba et al., 2018). QoL assessment has long been an important component of health-related research (Bakas et al., 2012) since it provides valuable insights into an individual’s subjective experience, functioning, and well-being, as well as helps to evaluate the impact of interventions and medical outcomes (Vederhus et al., 2024). Relevant studies have shown that infertility may reduce the QoL of couples (Lakatos et al., 2017; Li et al., 2019), and impaired QoL is already a significant issue among couples experiencing infertility (Boivin et al., 2011). Therefore, focusing on and measuring QoL in the context of fertility disorders is particularly important.

In the context of infertility, the Fertility Quality of Life Scale (FertiQoL), designed by Boivin et al. (2011), is recognized as a reliable and sensitive instrument. Its development involved contributions from 2000 patients with fertility issues and was based on a comprehensive mixed-methods approach, including a literature review, international expert consultation, patient focus groups, cross-cultural feasibility and acceptability surveys, and psychometric survey assessments. The FertiQoL aims to assess the QoL of individuals with infertility-related conditions. Research has assessed the impact of fertility issues on general health, self-perception, emotions, partner relationships, family and social relationships, work life, and future life plans (Ahmadi et al., 2011; Goker et al., 2018; Lakatos et al., 2017; Li, et al., 2019; Mael-Ainin et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2024). However, it is unclear whether reduced quality of reproductive life has further implications for semen quality in men with infertility.

Social support refers to the support that an individual receives, perceives, and utilizes from family, friends, and others(Zhang, 2017). Research has shown that, for couples with fertility issues, social support helps promote optimism and positive cooperation during diagnosis and treatment(Martins et al., 2014). A study examining the impact of social support on infertility treatment concluded that strong social support played a moderating role in anxiety and marital satisfaction (Iordachescu et al., 2021). More social support had a positive effect on the quality of reproductive life of men with infertility (Bhamani et al., 2021; Maierhaba et al., 2024). Therefore, obtaining a high level of social support—through creating a harmonious and supportive family environment, as well as encouragement and comfort from family and friends—can help alleviate the low self-esteem and mindset usually experienced by men with infertility and effectively improve their QoL and reduce the pressures associated with infertility (Alves-Leite et al., 2025).

Fertility stress refer to a series of psychological pressures experienced by individuals due to infertility or during the process of seeking treatment stemming from factors such as the impact of social opinion, sexuality, marital conflicts, the need for parenthood, and childlessness (Li et al., 2019). Infertility has a significant negative impact on couples, families, and society as a whole (Bornstein et al., 2020). Couples face pressures that affect their well-being, their relationship with each other, their interpersonal relationships, and society at large. These challenges can have a profound effect on their marriage, affect both partners’ physical and psychological state, and influence the functioning of their social relationships, ultimately leading to an impaired QoL. Stress is experienced and perceived differently depending on gender differences and societal roles (Feijing et al., 2016). Recent research has focused more on fertility stress and QoL in women experiencing infertility than in men (Liu et al., 2024; Swift et al., 2022); however, men with infertility also experience significant negative emotions and fertility stress. Therefore, considering the QoL of men with infertility is important. Some studies have confirmed that fertility stress affects the quality of reproductive life of men with infertility (Braverman et al., 2024; Dehghan et al., 2020; Koert et al., 2021). Men with abnormal semen quality tend to be under more pressure to conceive, with varying degrees of impact on their quality of reproductive life (Cai & Dong, 2021). Besides, men’s quality of reproductive life can also be affected by fertility problems and fertility treatments (Wdowiak et al., 2021). Infertility is a source of stress that can cause men to question their fertility, negatively affecting their self-esteem and self-confidence, which in turn can lead to a reduction in their quality of reproductive life. Moreover, increased social support can alleviate fertility stress and indirectly improve men’s quality of reproductive life (Maierhaba, 2023).

However, only a few studies exist on whether social support and fertility stress moderate the relationship between quality of reproductive life and semen quality. Therefore, this study explored the relationship between the quality of reproductive life and semen quality, as well as analyzed the potential moderating roles of social support and fertility stress.

Method

Participants

Overall, 1,024 adult males who visited the outpatient clinic of the Centre for Reproductive Medicine at our institution between July 2021 and July 2024 were recruited in this case–control study. The case and control groups comprised men with abnormal semen quality (n = 467) and those with normal semen quality (n = 557), respectively. An anonymous electronic questionnaire and routine semen examinations were administered to all participants. The inclusion criteria for abnormal semen quality were as follows: ① According to the WHO (fifth edition) Laboratory Manual for Human Semen Examination and Processing as the diagnostic criteria for semen quality: sperm concentration ≥ 15 × 106 mL−1, total sperm count ≥ 39 × 106, sperm progressive motility ≥ 32%, and sperm total motility ≥ 40%. Semen was defined as abnormal when it failed to satisfy one or more of the above requirements. ② Patients were informed and agreed to participate in the study voluntarily. Participants provided informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study. In contrast, the exclusion criteria were (1) presence of major diseases (severe mental illness, severe chronic diseases, or tumors) and (2) difficulty in comprehending the questionnaire content or inability to complete it.

Measures and Procedure

Semen Collection and Analysis

All men undergoing routine semen examination were instructed to abstain from sexual intercourse for 2–7 days before sampling to obtain qualified semen specimens and ensure the reliability of the routine semen analysis results. Semen samples from a single ejaculation were obtained through masturbation in a special semen extraction chamber and deposited in a sterile collection cup. The net mass of the semen sample was determined by weighing the total mass of the container with the semen and subtracting the mass of the empty container. Subsequently, the semen volume was calculated from the density of the semen (1 g/mL) to ensure that the results reflected the true condition of the person. The semen was stored in a thermostatic incubator at the optimal liquefaction temperature of 37 °C. Specifically, the incubator temperature was monitored and calibrated periodically, and a thermostat was placed under the counting plate during semen testing to prevent altering the temperature, which could lead to reduced spermatozoa quality. Semen samples were routinely analyzed after complete liquefaction for 30–60 min. The incompletely liquefied specimens were mixed with an equal volume of Dulbecco’s phosphate buffer, and the mixture was liquefied by repeated pipetting using a sampling device. The resulting sample was then processed for detection using the sperm quality analyzer BEl0Ns3-3 (a computer-assisted sperm analysis system, Shanghai BeiAng Pharmaceutical Science and Technology Co., Shanghai, China). All semen quality analysis was performed according to the fifth edition of the WHO standards for semen analysis.

Fertility Quality of Life Scale

The FertiQoL is a self-assessment scale designed by experts from the European Society for Human Reproduction and Embryology and the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (Fertility Quality of Life Questionnaire) to more accurately assess the QoL of patients with infertility, with a total scale Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92. It contains 36 items and comprises the following two main modules: the core and treatment dimensions. The core dimension comprises emotional response (six items), physical and mental relationships (six items), marital relationships (six items), and social relationships (six items). In contrast, the therapeutic dimension comprises environment (six items) and tolerance (four items). Each option is rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0–4). Emotional response entries in the core dimension (six items; e.g., “Do your fertility problems cause you to feel jealousy or resentment”?), physical and mental relationships (six items, e.g., “Is your concentration and focus affected by thoughts of infertility”?), marital relationships(six items, e.g., “Despite having fertility problems, do you and your partner remain emotionally close?”),Social relationships (six items, e.g., “Are you satisfied with the support you receive from friends regarding fertility”?), entries in the tolerance dimension (Four items, e.g., “Did infertility treatment negatively affect your mood”?), and the treatment environment dimension (six items, e.g., “Did you feel that the health care provider treating your infertility understood what you were going through”?) and so on. The subscale and total scale can be converted to a score of 0–100 by calculation, with higher scores indicating a better quality of reproductive life. Notably, the Chinese version of the scale has also demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of > 0.7 (Maierhaba et al., 2024).

Social Support Rating Scale

The Social Support Rating Scale was designed to assess the degree of social support for patients (Shuiyuan, 1994). It consists of three dimensions (10 items): subjective support (the emotional support experienced or felt by an individual), objective support (visible or tangible support, including direct material assistance, presence in group relationships, and participation in group activities), and utilization of support (active utilization of support, such as confiding and seeking help). Items 1–4 and 8–10 are single-choice questions, each item scoring from 1 to 4. Item 5 contains four sub-items A, B, C, and D, each sub-item scored from 1 to 4, representing no support to full support, respectively. For items 6 and 7, if the answer is "no support source at all," it is scored as 0. Calculate the total score of 10 items, with a higher score indicating a better level of social support.

Fertility Pressure Inventory

The Fertility Problem Inventory (FPI) comprises 46 items, including the following five subscales: social stress (10 items), sexual stress (8 items), parental role needs (10 items), couple relationships (10 items), and childless lifestyle (8 items). Each item was rated on a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from “completely disagree” (1 point) to “completely agree” (6 points), with a total scale score of 46–276. The social stress dimension included 10 items such as “It does not bother me when asked about my children,” the sexual stress dimension included 8 items such as “I have lost my enjoyment of sex due to fertility problems”, and the parental role needs dimension included 10 items such as “Parenting is far more important to me than having a satisfying career.” The couple relationship dimension included “I can’t express my feelings to my partner because it would be difficult for him.” The dimension of childless lifestyle includes “The only thing I think about when having sex, the only thing I think about is having a child (or having one more child).” Higher scores indicate greater fertility stress. The total scores of the FPI scale and each subscale had high reliability and validity, with Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.77 to 0.93.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed, with mean ± standard deviation and median with interquartile range for normally and non-normally distributed continuous variables, respectively. Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages. Depending on the type of data distribution, we used the chi-square (categorical variables) or Mann–Whitney U (continuous variables) test to compare differences in the distribution of variables between the two groups. The Spearman correlation coefficient for continuous variables was used to test the correlation between quality of reproductive life and social support, fertility stress, and semen parameters. To further investigate our hypotheses, we performed moderating effect analyses for the two moderating variables (social support and fertility stress) and quality of reproductive life and semen parameters using grouped regressions in a linear regression model (Adjustments were made for reported covariates associated with semen quality, including age, smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep quality, and educational attainment that were statistically significant in univariate analyses). In the case of significant interactions, the analysis allowed us to determine the level at which the moderators (social support and fertility stress) were associated with quality of reproductive life using semen parameters. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0, and significant moderating effects were interpreted as significant at p < 0.05.

Sperm concentration, total sperm count, sperm progressive motility, and sperm total motility were significantly lower in the abnormal semen group than in the control group (p < 0.001). The social support score was slightly lower in the abnormal semen group than in the normal group (p = 0.045), as shown in Table 1.

Correlation of Quality of Reproductive Life, Social Support, and Fertility Stress with Semen Parameters

We conducted a correlation analysis between quality of reproductive life, social support, fertility stress, and semen parameters in patients with abnormal semen quality. Quality of reproductive life was positively correlated with sperm concentration (ρ = 0.12, p = 0.013), total sperm count (ρ = 0.14, p = 0.002), sperm progressive motility (ρ = 0.18, p < 0.001), and sperm total motility (ρ = 0.19, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher quality of reproductive life results in better semen quality. A positive correlation was observed between quality of reproductive life and social support (ρ = 0.27, p < 0.001), while a negative correlation was found with fertility stress (ρ = − 0.23, p < 0.001). Social support exhibited a significant positive correlation with sperm progressive motility (ρ = 0.09, p = 0.050) and sperm total motility (ρ = 0.10, p = 0.032) but a negative correlation with fertility stress (ρ = − 0.17, p < 0.001). Fertility stress was significantly negatively correlated with sperm concentration (ρ = − 0.10, p = 0.033) and total sperm count (ρ = − 0.10, p = 0.028), as shown in Table 2. The above results suggest an association between quality of reproductive life and semen quality, social support, and fertility stress in childbearing. Fertility quality tends to be higher with increased social support and lower fertility stress.

Moderating Effects of Social Support and Fertility Stress on the Relationship Between Quality of Reproductive Life and Semen Quality

We divided social support and fertility pressure into high social support and low social support, high fertility pressure and low fertility pressure based on scores falling one standard deviation above the mean and one standard deviation below the mean, respectively, and analyzed the moderating effect of different semen quality groups.

Regarding the moderating effect of social support in the abnormal semen group, high social support, quality of reproductive life, and sperm total motility were significant (β = 0.189, SE = 0.034, 95% CI [0.028, 0.163], p = 0.006); however, such difference was not found in the normal group (β = 0.011, SE = 0.019, 95% CI [− 0.035, 0.042], p = 0.861) (Tables 3 and 4). These findings indicate that high social support played a moderating role in the relationship between quality of reproductive life and the abovementioned semen parameters for the abnormal semen group. Specifically, high social support contributed to a positive correlation between quality of reproductive life and semen quality.

Discussion

Studies have shown that infertility can lead to severe psychological distress and burden in different cultures and countries, jeopardizing patients’ emotional and social functioning and affecting their QoL (Martins et al., 2014). In our study, we found that the quality of reproductive life in the abnormal semen group was significantly and positively correlated with sperm concentration, total sperm count, sperm progressive motility, and sperm total motility. Abnormal or impaired semen quality may cause significant psychological distress and fertility stress, negatively affecting the quality of reproductive life (Feijing et al., 2016; Zou, 2019).

Social support as a positive resource, perceived as greater social support from partners or family, can help improve their quality of reproductive life (Warchol-Biedermann, 2021). We analyzed the moderating effect of social support and found that a high level of social support had a moderating effect on the relationship between quality of reproductive life and semen quality in the abnormal semen group; however, such results were not found in the normal group. Perhaps the distress caused by the disease and the motivation for manhood makes men with infertility reluctant to express inner anxiety and feelings to their families, friends, and partners. Therefore, more social support should be provided to promote the quality of reproductive life in patients with abnormal semen quality. Our results align with those of a study conducted by Zou (2019) on the relationship between social support for regulating fertility stress and semen quality. A study suggests that self-efficacy and social support are effective in reducing anxiety in men (Casu et al., 2019). High levels of social support can help regulate negative emotions in men with infertility (Iordachescu et al., 2021). The association between high levels of family functioning, self-efficacy, and social support and low levels of anxiety in psychological interventions highlights the importance of these factors in the male infertility population.

A study showed that men with infertility in China face significantly higher fertility pressure than those abroad; influenced by traditional concepts, men with infertility experience greater stress, reducing their QoL (Bolsoy et al., 2010). We found a negative correlation between fertility stress and fertility QoL, consistent with the findings of Li et al. (2019). The model analysis of the moderating effect of fertility stress showed that low fertility stress had a moderating effect in the abnormal semen group, indicating that low fertility stress can protect against the positive relationship between their quality of reproductive life and semen quality. Numerous studies have confirmed that fertility stress affects the QoL of couples with infertility and semen quality in men (Zhong et al., 2024). The WHO definition of health clearly states that health is not only the absence of disease and infirmity but also a good state of physical, mental, and social adjustment. Mental health is a crucial and indispensable aspect of physical and mental health. Severe psychological problems or distress can have a huge impact on human health and life. Men with infertility are likely to score higher on depression, anxiety, and stress than those without infertility due to the desire for children and the pressure from families to become fathers (Dourou et al., 2023). Therefore, the psychological problems and stresses, such as anxiety and depression, faced by this cohort of men with infertility are even more important to emphasize (Roudbaraki et al., 2025).

This study underscores the critical need for increased support from family members, society, and medical professionals in addressing the mental health of men with infertility. This support is crucial to help them navigate infertility with a healthy and optimistic mindset and actively engage in treatment.

However, this study has some limitations. First, since the data were obtained from a single center, the findings may lack broader representativeness. In future, we aim to conduct deeper research on multicenter populations. Second, only one semen sample was collected from each participant; therefore, intra-subject variability in semen quality may not have been captured, potentially leading to the inclusion of participants with abnormally low semen quality in the abnormal semen group. Additionally, only parameters such as semen concentration, total sperm count, sperm progressive motility, and sperm total motility were used in our study. However, semen quality-related indexes are not limited to the ones we used above but also include, slow progression motility, sperm survival, and sperm morphology. Therefore, more semen parameters should be investigated in future studies. While several studies have confirmed the influence of lifestyle factors on semen quality (Amoah et al., 2025; Cargnelutti et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2021; Rahban et al., 2023), information on lifestyle factors should be collected for future in-depth exploration. Finally, although we tested for associations, we could not make causal inferences. Therefore, deeper longitudinal studies are needed in future to further reveal the causal relationships.

Conclusion

This study identified significant positive correlations between the quality of reproductive life and various semen parameters, as well as positive and negative associations with social support and fertility stress, respectively. Exploring the moderating effects of social support and fertility stress on the relationship suggests that having a high level of social support contributes to a positive relationship between the quality of reproductive life and sperm total motility in patients with abnormal semen quality and that low fertility stress has a protective effect on the relationship in the abnormal semen group. Therefore, these findings highlight the critical role of external support from family members, partners, relatives, and friends, alongside stress reduction, in improving the quality of reproductive life for men with infertility.
