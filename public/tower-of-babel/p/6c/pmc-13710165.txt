Early maturation of host antimicrobial peptide defences is associated with host–pathogen coexistence

ABSTRACT
Epizootic outbreaks of invasive pathogens, such as the fungal pathogen Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd), are increasingly recognized as a global threat to biodiversity. However, not all susceptible host populations are equally impacted by these disease outbreaks. While some populations persist despite intense infections and high mortality rates, others can transition to enzootic coexistence with the pathogen. Here, we use comparative peptidomics to identify differences in antimicrobial peptide (AMP) maturation in Bd-susceptible common midwife toads (Alytes obstetricans) through metamorphosis. Here, we show that animals that metamorphose before AMP immune maturation display a low-diversity AMP phenotype deficient in anti-Bd AMPs. Where a high incidence of postmetamorphic animals occur with immature AMPs, populations are associated with epizootic disease dynamics. Conversely, populations associated with mature AMPs are stable following invasion of the pathogen. Our results show that even intrinsically susceptible amphibian species can possess the tools that allow populations to recover following a severe Bd epizootic.

Main

When highly transmissible pathogens are introduced into a naive community of susceptible hosts, these often experience one or a series of epizootic waves1. Over time, barring extinction of the pathogen or the host, epizootic disease dynamics will usually subside into more stable enzootic equilibria. Divergent trajectories and asynchronous rates of recovery can generate contemporary patterns of epizootic and enzootic disease dynamics between persisting host populations2,3. Comparative studies between these populations can offer insights into the role of host immunity in shaping population-level disease outcomes4. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are a crucial and near-ubiquitous component of innate immune defense across the tree of life5 and have been shown to inhibit the growth of bacterial, viral and fungal taxa in vitro6. Innate immune effectors, including AMPs, are traditionally considered a primitive, broad-spectrum antimicrobial barrier for preventing infection7. Despite this long-held perception, there is a growing body of work challenging this oversimplification5,8. While the innate immune system does constitute the first line of defense against novel pathogens, the complexity and specificity of the constituent AMPs has been long overlooked.

As anthropogenically spread emerging infectious diseases (EIDs) increasingly impact humans, agriculture and wildlife9, there is a pressing need to understand the interactions between host immunity and these novel pathogens. Here, the amphibian-infecting chytrid fungus, Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis (Bd), is one of the most impactful EIDs known and is a recognized threat to amphibian diversity worldwide10. Chytridiomycosis, the disease caused by Bd, causes acute epidermal disruption, leading to pathophysiological changes associated with high rates of mortality in susceptible species11. The resulting population declines have been monitored over the past forty years and the loss of amphibian taxa, particularly in tropical montane ecosystems, results in the loss of ecosystem function12,13 and the collapse of predator communities through trophic cascades14. Despite these localized catastrophic events, at a global level, the emergence of Bd is associated with heterogeneous patterns of declines of susceptible amphibians at both interspecific11 and intraspecific levels2,3.

There is some evidence to suggest that increased host immunity is contributing to the persistence of susceptible host populations11,15. As a pathogen of amphibian skin, Bd must first survive contact with the epidermal mucosal layer before it can infect the host. Variation in the efficacy of these mucosal defenses may explain the observed heterogeneity in population-level responses to a Bd epizootic. The highly diverse mixtures of both host16 and microbial compounds17 present in the mucosal layer have been shown to inhibit the growth of Bd in vitro18 and in vivo15. AMPs, in particular, are thought to be a critical component of an amphibian’s mucosal innate immune defense against pathogens such as Bd. This hypothesis owes much to previous in vitro work showing increased anti-Bd efficacy of mucosal samples from resistant amphibian species and the corresponding in vitro activity of synthetic amphibian AMPs19. However, there has been substantial difficulty in linking these in vitro AMP experiments to population-level studies in the field.

In many amphibian species, the metamorphosis from the larval to postmetamorphic stages carries an elevated risk of mortality from chytridiomycosis20. This elevated risk is generated through two simultaneous developmental processes, keratinization of the skin and immune maturation. As a keratinophilic parasite, Bd requires keratinized amphibian skin as a substrate for its survival in vivo21. In fatal chytridiomycosis, the osmoregulatory capacity of the amphibian’s skin breaks down, leading to cardiac asystolic death through electrolyte imbalance11. However, tadpoles do not possess keratinized skin22. Whilst Bd can infect their keratinized mouthparts, this does not develop into chytridiomycosis and is generally not fatal23. During metamorphosis, as keratinization of the skin occurs, the pathogen can spread to the skin, which can then develop into chytridiomycosis. This transition from a Bd-tolerant to a Bd-susceptible life stage coincides with a critical period of immune reorganization24. The immune system of tadpoles is considered immature and less effective in comparison to postmetamorphic animals25. At an organismal level, the development of the constitutive, innate and adaptive immune defenses takes places at discrete stages before, during and after metamorphosis as the requisite organs develop26. At a skin level, the AMP repertoire of tadpoles is considerably different to postmetamorphic life stages25,27, with further changes potentially occurring between recent metamorphs and sexually mature adults28. The inhibitory potential of these limited AMP defenses in larval amphibians has been reported to be significantly lower than those of postmetamorphic animals27. However, there has been no research into the population-level variation of these immune profiles across life stages in nature.

The common midwife toad (Alytes obstetricans) is susceptible to Bd but its population-level response to Bd introduction has been highly variable2,29–31. This species is particularly susceptible to chytridiomycosis during or shortly after metamorphosis, often leading to seasonal mass mortality events in montane regions31. In the Western Pyrenean Mountain range, all populations of A. obstetricans experienced heavy declines following Bd introduction, yet four of these populations have persisted 19 years later, with three transitioning to stable, enzootic disease dynamics2. At Lac d’Arlet, one population continues to exhibit epizootic dynamics characterized by high Bd infection intensity, seasonal mass mortality events and near extirpation. This observed heterogeneity in disease dynamics is known to be associated with amongst-lake variation in host microbiome2,4; however, an underlying immunological mechanism driving these host–microbe interactions is missing.

Here, we aimed to investigate the extent to which the observed disease dynamics were associated with variation in host AMP defenses. The AMP defenses of A. obstetricans include the alyteserin family, which consists of 12 cationic, α-helical cytolytic AMPs that are C-terminally amidated32. However, it remains unknown whether some or all the known alyteserins inhibit Bd or whether they could operate synergistically as is the case in other amphibian AMP systems33,34. If there is variation in anti-Bd efficacy between AMPs, then population-level responses to chytridiomycosis could occur through differences in composition or concentration of their AMP arsenal.

Since the description of the alyteserin AMPs in A. obstetricans in 2009, recent advances in mass spectrometry (MS)-based proteomics35,36, when integrated with transcriptomic references, has allowed for the high-throughput characterization of complex peptidomic mixtures. While these methods are typically used in human health research37,38, we were able to apply these analytical techniques to this wild amphibian disease system. In combination with molecular diagnostics, in vitro AMP Bd inhibition assays and confocal microscopy, we leveraged a multidisciplinary approach to investigate whether these differences in contemporary disease dynamics in the Western Pyrenees are associated with (1) the composition and efficacy of host AMP defenses in recently metamorphosed animals; (2) preinvestment in AMPs at the larval stage; and (3) the varied epidemiological outcomes that we observe across this long-term study system.

The mucosal peptidome is a highly diverse immunophenotype

We sampled skin secretions from a total of 154 A. obstetricans tadpoles and 123 postmetamorphic individuals across the four survey sites. Peptidomic analysis of the samples was then carried out by liquid chromatography (LC)–MS/MS under conditions that allowed for detection and sequencing of all peptide components. To identify these peptides, we performed a peptidomic workflow in PEAKSStudio12 (Bioinformatic Solutions). In summary, de novo peptide sequences went through a two-round database search (first against the AMP database (APD3 database39) and then against a skin transcriptome reference library generated from captive A. obstetricans). All unaligned sequences were then assembled into de novo features using the DeepNovo algorithm40, before quality filtering and quantification of all identified peptide features. We found that the mucosal peptidome of A. obstetricans skin is far more diverse than the existing literature suggests (Fig. 2a)32. The field mucosal peptide samples yielded 1,820 putative peptides (inclusive of post-translational modifications), with 1,152 of these peptides passing the quality filtering stage for label-free quantification (Fig. 2a). Amongst these peptides are six previously described alyteserin AMPs (Supplementary Table 1)32. We also found alyteserin 2 Mb, which was only previously described in the closely related A. maurus41. To enrich the dataset for peptides likely to possess antimicrobial activity, we removed peptide sequences without significant (−logP > 20) alignment to alyteserin 1 or alyteserin 2 AMPs (Fig. 2c). These removed sequences were predicted to have a significantly lower probability of AMP activity (Kruskal–Wallis: χ2(2) = 448.83, P < 0.001; pairwise Wilcoxon test: P < 0.001). A surprisingly large number of peptides had alignment with alyteserin 1 (n = 398) or alyteserin 2 (n = 121) AMPs, which is far higher than expected given the limited diversity of reported AMPs in the species. Much of this diversity appears to have been introduced after translation. This variation includes C-terminal acid and C-terminal amide analogs of all alyteserin AMPs, fragments of alyteserin AMPs and likely variation in the cleavage site from the propeptide observed as additional amino acids at either the C or N terminus of the AMP. This post-translational processing generates a large diversity of alyteserin peptide variants (alyteserin-PVs).

Alyteserin AMPs show variable ability to inhibit Bd in vitro

To investigate whether the AMPs from A. obstetricans represent an effective defense against Bd, we performed in vitro Bd growth inhibition assays against a panel of known AMPs in the species. This included all seven previously described alyteserin 1 and alyteserin 2 AMPs described previously32 (two of which were not found in our study system) and alyteserin 2 Mb (which we found in A. obstetricans within this study). Despite their reported universal efficacy against bacteria32,41, they showed variable ability to inhibit the growth of Bd in vitro (Supplementary Table 2). Only alyteserin 1a (half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) = 8.47 μmol), alyteserin 1b (EC50 = 41.03 μmol), alyteserin 2c (EC50 = 37.14 μmol) and alyteserin 2 Mb (EC50 = 7.53 μmol) were found to be inhibitory against Bd.

We then carried out a preliminary investigation into elucidating the mode of action of alyteserin AMPs against Bd. It is generally thought that AMPs from frog skin secretions exert their antibacterial activity by disruption of the bacterial membrane43. Both the alyteserins and the temporins contain amphipathic sequences that adopt α-helical structures in membranes and in membrane-mimicking environments, leading to cell lysis; furthermore, changes in the sequences that promote helicity and membrane interactions enhance antibacterial activity44. The activity of temporin family peptides against Bd has also been hypothesized to depend on membrane disruption. An all-D-analog of temporin A was found to adopt an α-helical conformation in trifluorethanol (TFE) and had enhanced potency against Bd16,suggesting a membrane disruption rather than a receptor-binding mode of action. As similar structure–activity studies were not previously carried out with the alyteserins, we synthesized two of the most potent analogs (alyteserin 1a and alyteserin 2c) and a scrambled analog (scr-alyteserin 2c) as a control to study their conformational properties (Supplementary Documents 1 and 2). Circular dichroism (CD) measurements in both TFE and in 10 mM SDS showed that alyteserin 1a adopted a predominantly α-helical conformation in both solvents. This was less pronounced for alyteserin 2c and scr-alyteserin 2c had little or no secondary structure (Supplementary Document 3). To further probe the binding localization of the alyteserin informing on potential mode of action, we stained killed Bd-GPL isolate IA042 against Cy3-labeled versions of Alyteserin 1a, alyteserin 2c and scr-alyteserin 2c (Supplementary Document 1). Contrary to expectations, both alyteserin 1a and alyteserin 2c appeared to stain intracellular structures of Bd inside both the mature zoosporangia and the encysting zoospores (Fig. 3). The control scrambled alyteserin 2c did not expose such staining patterns, as only random fluorescent puncta were observed across the imaging areas. As these puncta did not seem to consistently locate with a killed chytrids, such signals were considered as background signal (Fig. 3). Maturity of AMP defenses is not intrinsically linked to metamorphosis

To characterize the changes in the skin-secreted AMP defenses through metamorphosis, we performed differential abundance analysis of the alyteserin-PVs between larval and postmetamorphic life stages within each population. We found that the numbers of differentially abundant peptides (log2 fold change > 1, Hochberg corrected P value < 0.05) varied substantially between populations (Fig. 5a). There were a total of 121 unique alyteserin-PVs with significantly increased abundance in postmetamorphic individuals at Puits d’Arious (n = 98) and Lac de Lhurs (n = 58). However, there were no differentially abundant peptides between life stages at either Lac d’Arlet or Ibon de Acherito. We then performed a hierarchical, complete-linkage clustering analysis on these differentially abundance peptides (Fig. 5b). Surprisingly, the two clusters identified by the basal node of this analysis did not clearly separate larval and postmetamorphic life stages. Instead, each of these two clusters comprised a mixture of both life stages. The first cluster comprised mostly the postmetamorphic life stage (123/184 individuals) and was characterized by a higher compositional abundance of the differentially abundant secreted peptides. The second cluster comprised mostly the larval life stage (93/123 individuals) and was characterized by a lower compositional abundance of the differentially abundant secreted peptides. We then performed a principal component analysis (PCA) to investigate the relative contributions of life stage, cluster membership and Bd infection status on the composition of these differentially abundant peptides (Fig. 5c). We fitted a mixed-effect model of observed peptide diversity with cluster membership as an interacting effect, bodyweight and life stage as fixed effects and site as a random effect. We found that cluster membership was a strong predictor of observed peptide diversity (GlmmTMB: estimate = −152.68, s.e. = 11.85, z = −12.89, P < 0.001). Bodyweight (GlmmTMB: Est = 47.44, s.e. = 27.17, z = 1.75, P > 0.05) and life stage (GlmmTMB: Est = −32.08, s.e. = 17.69, z = −1.81, P > 0.05) were marginally nonsignificant. Across both life stages, complex AMP phenotypes possessed significantly higher alyteserin-PV alpha diversity than simple AMP phenotypes (Fig. 6b). Lastly, postmetamorphic animals with simple AMP phenotypes produced significantly smaller (bodyweight-normalized) quantities of Bd-inhibitory alyteserin 1a (Wilcoxon test: Z = 3.56, P < 0.001), alyteserin 1b (Wilcoxon test: Z = 3.36, P < 0.001) and alyteserin 2 Mb (Wilcoxon test: Z = 2.46, P = 0.013) (Fig. 6c).

Discussion

This study challenges the paradigm of immune maturation being a fixed process through the ontogeny of organisms with a biphasic life history. This maturation process was presumed to be a transition from an immature (‘simple’) larval AMP phenotype to a mature (‘complex’) postmetamorphic AMP phenotype. We show that the timing of innate immune maturation relative to metamorphosis is associated with population-level disease dynamics in A. obstetricans. Animals that metamorphose before AMP immune maturity display a simple, low-diversity alyteserin-PV phenotype deficient in Bd-inhibitory AMPs. Study sites that have perennially declined following the emergence of Bd are characterized by a high prevalence of A. obstetricans manifesting this simple, low-diversity alyteserin-PV phenotype.

We show that the timing of immune maturation of amphibians is not intrinsically tied to metamorphosis. We further show that immune maturation may occur before metamorphosis and metamorphosis may occur without immune maturation. However, little is known about the factors that determine this timing. Whilst we acknowledge that a genetic component of this variation is likely, larval development in amphibians is known to be a highly plastic process. A wide range of interacting ecological factors experienced at the larval stage such as temperature, larval density, the presence of predators and food availability can influence many host traits, including skin chemistry. Although we are unable to produce definitive evidence to support our hypothesis, we believe that the process of larval overwintering in A. obstetricans likely explains this variation in immune investment before metamorphosis. Rather unusually for amphibians, A. obstetricans tadpoles can either achieve metamorphosis within a single year or potentially overwinter for one or more years before metamorphosis45. Overwintering is more common in high-elevation populations of A. obstetricans, likely because of constraints on the annual period of activity by the lower temperatures in montane habitats. This lack of overwintering and the shorter annual activity periods may leave tadpoles with insufficient resources to invest in immune development; accordingly, they metamorphose without mature AMP defenses. Our results highlight the importance of studying both amphibian life stages to understand population-level disease dynamics.

Through the application of MS-based peptidomics, we revealed that the diversity of secreted peptides from amphibian skin is far higher than was previously appreciated32. We found that 45% of these peptides had significant alignment with six known alyteserin AMPs. Interestingly, much of this variation appears to be driven by post-translational modifications. This includes the characterization of both C-terminal acid and C-terminal amide analogs of all six Alyteserin AMPs in these populations, modifications known to be functionally important in membrane interactions with bacteria46. The remaining modifications were likely driven by hydrolysis of mature AMPs and variation in the cleavage sites of mature AMPs from their protein precursor.

This increased complexity of amphibian AMP phenotypes also has broad implications for the study of immunity and eco-evolutionary theory. The innate immune system has long been thought of as a primitive, blunt instrument comprising a limited number of broad-spectrum antimicrobial effectors. However, this assumption is increasingly recognized as being inaccurate5,8. We demonstrated that alyteserins show antimicrobial selectivity in their inhibitory activity and post-translational modifications are driving a vast diversification of the peptidome from the transcribed proteome. However, despite these insights, we are currently unable to assess whether skin AMPs represent an inducible or constitutive defense against Bd. While Bd infection was a poor predictor of the overall composition of alyteserin-PVs, further work under controlled, laboratory conditions will be needed to assess whether individual AMPs are modulated in response to Bd infection.

The implications for this increase in characterized peptide diversity are complex. Currently, our ability to characterize these complex biological mixtures far exceeds our ability to understand their function. Machine-learning tools are now available that aim to predict the conformation47 or function42 of peptides from amino acid sequence. However, the current tools come with three primary limitations with respect to this study system. Firstly, many of these tools treat antimicrobial activity as a binary response variable, which ignores the nuances of the antimicrobial selectivity of host AMPs. All six known alyteserin AMPs found within this study are reported to show inhibitory activity to bacteria32 while only three of these peptides inhibit the growth of Bd in vitro. Secondly, the predicted tertiary structures and physiochemical characteristics derived from amino acid sequence are an integral part of predicting AMP activity (Supplementary Fig. 1). These properties are optimized for identifying AMPs that will interact with bacterial membranes, not the intracellular fungal inhibition demonstrated in this study. Further work is needed to understand the physiochemical properties of AMPs that inhibit fungi and how fungal pathogens are targeted by AMPs rather than the host’s epithelial cells. The traditional models of AMP activity show that initial interactions take places by electrostatic attraction between cationic AMPs and negatively charged phospholipid head groups of bacteria48. However, the keratinized tissue in which Bd encysts is also negatively charged49. A greater understanding of the properties of the membranes of amphibian epithelial cells and Bd will greatly improve our ability to perform in silico screenings of putative AMPs from large peptidomic datasets. Lastly, these tools assume ‘independence of action’ of AMPs. Although this is currently an unreasonable expectation of machine-learning tools, AMPs are known to interact with pathogens synergistically33,34 and ‘noninhibitory’ AMPs may act synergistically with other peptides to inhibit pathogen growth. However, with the rapid developments in this field, our ability to generate in silico predictions is constantly improving and future studies will be able to start to overcome these barriers.

The low alyteserin-PV diversity of the epizootic Lac d’Arlet population relative to the enzootic populations mirrors previous work with skin microbiomes in these populations2,4. The skin microbiome acts as extended immunity through the inhibition of Bd growth as a function of the production of anti-Bd metabolites17. These mucosal microbial communities are shaped and regulated through host AMP production50. However, in many epithelial systems, there is extensive bidirectional crosstalk between the host’s innate immunity and the microbiome51. This can include host immune priming modulated by the secretome of the skin microbiome when confronted by pathogens52. Future work should address these parallel observations through an integrative ‘holobiont’ approach to skin mucosal immunity.

Our results show that even intrinsically susceptible amphibian species can possess the tools that allow populations to recover following a severe Bd epizootic outbreak. In this study, we show that the timing of innate immune maturation relative to metamorphosis is associated with population-level disease dynamics in A. obstetricans. Animals that metamorphose before AMP immune maturity display a low-diversity AMP phenotype deficient in anti-Bd AMPs. A high incidence of these postmetamorphic animals with immature AMPs is associated with epizootic disease dynamics and population decline. These observed differences in host immune phenotype development are likely a crucial mechanism for establishing stable host–pathogen coexistence. As these immune phenotypes and biphasic history are highly generalizable across the class Amphibia, our results suggest that the timing of innate immune maturation may underlie the observed rebound in other Bd-susceptible amphibian species.

Methods

Sample collection: field surveys

Field sampling took place between July 29 and August 6, 2019, and between August 3 and August 9, 2020. Animals were captured by hand or net and we sampled a maximum of 20 tadpoles and 20 postmetamorphic individuals per site (Supplementary Table 3). Recently metamorphosed individuals were found beneath cover objects bordering the lakes and any observed mortalities were recorded. These postmetamorphic animals only congregate near water for a few weeks before dispersing into strictly terrestrial habitats, which limits demographic structuring of the animals sampled45. Tadpoles had their mouthparts swabbed with an MW100 cotton-tipped swab (Medical Wire and Equipment) and postmetamorphic animals were swabbed according to a standard protocol; each specimen was swabbed by taking five strokes down the center of the underside, on each flank, the inside of the hind legs and bottom of the feet. Swabs were stored below 4 °C until DNA extraction. Peptide sampling was performed using a bath exposure of norepinephrine53. Briefly, each individual was exposed to a 200 μM bath of norepinephrine bitartrate (A0397, Merck) and high-performance LC (HPLC)-grade water (V270733, Merck) in a sterile Whirlpak bag (Nasco Sampling). After 15 min, the animal was removed from the Whirlpak and returned to its individual capture bag. The sample was then acidified by addition of 0.2 ml of trifluoroacetic acid (302031, Merck), returned from the field on ice and stored at −18 °C until purification for MS analysis. Each animal was contained in a new plastic bag and handled with a new pair of powder nitrile gloves to prevent cross-infection of individuals54.

DNA extraction and Bd diagnostics

DNA from skin swabs was extracted in a PrepMan Ultra (Applied Biosystems). To assign the infection status and to quantify the disease burden of each individual, we performed Bd-ITS qPCR55. Extractions were diluted 1:10 before qPCR amplification, run in duplicate and considered to be positive if both wells amplified and were over 0.1 genomic equivalents (GE; where 1 GE is the quantity of genetic material present in a single zoospore). All samples were run against positive standards of 0.1, 1, 10 and 100 GE of Bd-GPL (isolate IA042) and negative control standards. Plates with a standard curve of R2 < 0.95 were repeated.

Skin secretion sample prep

Skin secretion samples were concentrated and desalted through a C18 Sep-Pak cartridge (WAT020515, Waters) partial purification procedure53. Briefly, the Sep-Pak cartridge was primed with 2 ml of acetonitrile (270717, Merck), equilibrated with 2 ml of a 0.1% TFA wash solution before the sample was loaded. To maximize the yield, the wash and loading steps were repeated. Samples were then stored on the cartridges at 4 °C until analysis. Before MS analysis, the cartridge was washed with 4 ml of a 0.1% TFA wash solution before being eluted with 2 ml of 70% acetonitrile and 30% HPLC water with 0.1% TFA. The eluent was then frozen overnight at −80 °C and dried under vacuum using a Speedvac (Thermo-Savant).

LC–MS/MS analysis of field samples

Proteomic analysis of the semipurified amphibian skin secretions was performed using a Vanquisher LC system connected to a Q-Exactive plus quadrupole-orbitrap MS instrument (Thermo Scientific). Lyophilized skin secretions were resuspended in 100 μl of solvent (5% acetonitrile in water, 0.1% formic acid) and centrifuged for 5 min at 11,300g. Then, 20 μl of each sample was injected onto a Hypersil Gold column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 3-µm particle size; 25002-1521300). The chromatographic peptides separation on this column was achieved using mobile phase A (water, 0.1% formic acid) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid). The 20-min gradient was as follows: 2 min with 5% B, before increasing to 99% B over the next 15 min, maintaining at 99% B for a further 3 min and then decreasing to 5% B within 6 s, maintained for 1.9 min to equilibrate the Hypersil C18 column. The flow rate was 0.2 ml min−1. The column effluent was continuously directed to the heated electrospray ionization (HESI) source of the Q-Exactive MS instrument. The HESI probe was operated with sheath gas flow at 25, auxiliary gas flow at 5, spray voltage at 3.80, capillary temperature at 270 °C, S-lens radiofrequency level at 55 and auxiliary gas heater temperature at 50 °C. The Q-Exactive MS instrument was operated in a positive ion mode. MS data were acquired using a data-dependent acquisition mode and operated at 70,000 resolution (full width at half-maximum height definition). The top eight most abundant multiply charged (z ≥ 2) ions in the 300–1,800 m/z range were selected for MS/MS, with dynamic exclusion enabled for 30 s. The automatic gain control for the Q-Exactive was set to 500,000 ions and the automatic gain control for the MS/MS in the ion trap was set at 8,000 ions. For MS/MS, the isolation width was set at 1.4 and the collision energy was 35%. MS/MS scans were acquired at a resolution of 17,500 at 200 m/z and the fixed first mass was set to 120 m/z. Three MS/MS microscans for each precursor were accumulated. Dynamic exclusion was enabled and selected ions were excluded for 20 s before they could be selected for another round of MS/MS.

Peptidomic reference libraries

To align the peptide sequences generated through MS, we used two references libraries. To identify known AMPs, we aligned all de novo sequences against the APD3 database39.

To generate a full mucosal peptidome, we generated an RNA-seq reference library for both larval and postmetamorphic A. obstetricans. Skin samples (of three larval and three postmetamorphic A. obstetricans) up to 25 mg were collected and immediately stored in Trizol reagent. Samples were homogenized using a pestle in 1.5-ml tubes and total RNA was extracted using the standard Trizol RNA extraction protocol. Pelleted RNA was resuspended in 30 µl of RNase-free water. RNA integrity was quantified using an Agilent TapeStation 2100 bioanalyzer. Total RNA was quantified on a Qubit using the RNA high-sensitivity kit. Sample replicates were pooled equally to produce a single sample for library preparation. Samples were submitted to Novogene for mRNA library preparation (poly(A) enrichment) and paired-end 150-bp sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq for 80 million reads per sample.

De novo assembly was performed using Trinity (version 2.8.5) using default parameters. Trimmomatic (version 0.39)56 was used to quality trim reads and in silico normalization of reads was carried out. Resulting assemblies were quality assessed by examining the number of transcripts that appear full-length or nearly full-length. BLASTx (version 2.2.30), using the Xenopis laevis (XENLA_10.01_GCF_XBmodels.transcripts.fa) reference transcript set as a frog-specific comparison, was performed to assess the quality of the assemblies. It is sometimes the case that a single transcript will align to a single protein sequence with several discontinuous alignments (that is, a BLAST hit containing multiple hit-scoring segment pairs (HSPs)). Therefore, multiple HSPs were grouped per transcript and database hit and the alignment coverage based on grouped HSPs was also assessed. Bowtie2 (version 7.3.0)57 was used to assess the alignment rate of RNA-seq reads back to the assembly. The two assemblies were then concatenated and underwent six-frame translation in EMBOSS sixpack58 before being clustered into 95% similar sequences using CD-HIT59.

Peptidomic pipeline

Downstream analysis of the MS data was conducted using PEAKs Studio (version 12.0; Bioinformatic Solutions). From 697,265 MS scans and 1,109,355 MS/MS scans, 1,073,825 peptide spectrum matches were identified using the DeepNovo algorithm40 with the following parameters: no enzyme digestion, parent mass error tolerance of 10.0 ppm, fragment mass error tolerance of 0.02 Da and a maximum of three post-translational modifications per peptide. We then performed a two-round search of de novo sequences with a quality score > 70 against APD3 and then our transcriptome-derived A. obstetricans peptide library with an identification threshold of −logP > 20. Lastly, high-quality unaligned sequences were assembled into de novo peptide features. Label-free quantification using PEAKs Q (version 12.0; Bioinformatic Solutions) was used to quantify the expression levels of the peptides in each sample using the following parameters: retention time, 4–22 min; peptide quality, ≥5; peptide average area, ≥1 × 106; peptide charge, 1–5; protein significance, ≥20; fold change, ≥1.

AMP quantification

Relative quantification of AMPs was achieved through generating a standard curve using synthetic peptides supplied by Cambridge Research Biochemicals. Standards were made up at concentrations of 100 μM, 50 μM, 25 μM and 12.5 μM in 5% acetonitrile and 95% water with 0.1% formic acid using the same LC–MS method used for the amphibian secretion samples. Peak areas of the standards were measured using Xcalibur Qual browser (version 2.2; Thermo Scientific). All standard curves had an R2 > 0.95.

In vitro Bd inhibition assays

Growth inhibition assays were conducted by challenging Bd-GPL343 with a dilution series (six replicates of 100 μM, 50 μM, 25 μM, 12.5 μM, 6.25 μM, 3.125 μM and 1.60 μM) of each AMP in a FLUOstar Omega optical density plate reader (BMG Labtech). The experimental set up included a positive control (50 μl of Bd and 50 μl of sterile water), negative control (50 μl of heat-killed Bd and 50 μl of sterile water) and blanks (50 μl of mTGHL medium and water)60. Blank-compensated optical density readings were taken daily and each well examined with a Leica inverted microscope to check for contamination. Contaminated wells were removed from the experiment. Readings on each plate were taken until the growth rate of the positive control had reached a plateau. Growth was measured as a percentage of the mean growth of the positive control wells on the day growth reached a plateau. Best fit log–log models were determined and fitted using R package DRC61.

Confocal microscopy imaging of Cy3-labeled alyteserins with Bd

We challenged killed Bd-GPL isolate IA042 against Cy3-labeled alyteserin 1a, alyteserin 2c and scr-alyteserin 2c (Supplementary Document 1). Briefly, zoospores of Bd-GPLIA042 were isolated from a 3-day old culture by filtration of the mTGhL on a 10-µm filter. Then, 300 µl of the zoospore suspension were inoculated in Mattek petri dishes for 2 days at 17 °C. Young zoosporangia were then killed and blocked by replacing the medium with a solution of 0.5% sodium azide and 1× carbofree blocking solution (Vectorlabs) in 1× PBS and incubating at 37 °C for 15 min. As dead zoosporangia were used, the potential bias raised by the different biological activities of the alyteserins was alleviated and all zoosporangia could be stained with a similar concentration of alyteserins and fluorophores. Blocked zoosporangia were then incubated with 10 µM Cy3–alyteserins in 1× PBS overnight at 4 °C and counterstained with 10 µg ml−1 calcofluor white for 15 min. After two washes, zoosporangia were imaged on Zeiss LSM780 laser scanning confocal microscope accumulating Z stacks covering the entire organism with a ×63 objective.

Statistical methods

All statistical analyses were carried out in R (version 4.2.0)62. Proteomic data were handled in the R package phyloseq63 and CLR data transformations were performed in the R package microbiome. Curve fitting of peptide inhibition data was performed in the R package DRC. All plots were generated using ggplot2 (ref. 64). The map of field sites was generated using the ESRI National Geographic (https://services.arcgisonline.com/ArcGIS/rest/services/NatGeo_World_Map/MapServer) and ESRI Gray (light; https://www.arcgis.com/home/item.html?id=8b3d38c0819547faa83f7b7aca80bd76) base layers in QGIS65.
