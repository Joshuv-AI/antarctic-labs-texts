Perspectives of gastroenterologists on an educational curriculum to promote self-management in young adults with inflammatory bowel disease
Authors: Zuhal Mohmand, Ashleigh Miatello, Brooke Allemang, Aalia Tausif, Yi Ran Wang, Sally Lawrence, Colette Deslandres, Canadian Children IBD Network Education Committee, Melanie Barwick, Kate Lee, Jacqueline de Guzman, Nancy Fu, Eric I Benchimol, Natasha Bollegala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents and young adults (AYAs) with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) face challenges transitioning from pediatric to adult healthcare, where they assume greater responsibility for their care. The Transition Adolescent & Young Adults implementioN (IBD-TrAYN) intervention is part of a multisite hybrid effectiveness-implementation randomized controlled trial launched in 2022 to address gaps in transition care. Online educational modules were developed to enhance disease knowledge and self-management skills among AYAs.AimTo explore adult gastroenterologists’ perspectives on transition-related education and curriculum developed as part of the IBD-TrAYN intervention.MethodsAdult gastroenterologists across Canada with experience treating AYAs with IBD were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling. Three virtual focus groups were conducted with 11 gastroenterologists to explore perspectives on the curriculum. Transcripts were analyzed by 2 independent reviewers using content analysis.ResultsParticipants identified the IBD-TrAYN curriculum as a valuable tool for addressing common educational barriers in AYA care. These included provider- and system-level barriers, such as limited clinical time and allied health support, and differences in patient readiness. Priority content included disease knowledge, self-management skills, mental health, and sexual health. Caregiver education was highlighted as an important part of transition care. Suggested improvements included consolidating content, increasing interactivity, and ensuring flexible, self-paced access to support individualized engagement.ConclusionAdult gastroenterologists endorsed the value of the IBD-TrAYN curriculum in bridging educational gaps during transition to adult care. Incorporating these insights, particularly prioritizing key topics and enhancing interactivity, may strengthen future iterations and promote effective, patient-centred transition care for AYAs with IBD.Keywords: IBD, transition of care, education, self-management

Introduction

Transition from pediatric to adult care represents a critical period for adolescents and young adults (AYAs) living with chronic health conditions. It is defined as the purposeful, planned movement from pediatric to adult-oriented healthcare systems and is characterized by a shift from family-centred models to greater autonomy, self-management, and independent communication with healthcare providers.1,2 This reflects a change in the care environment and a developmental shift toward independent management of a complex disease.

Canada has one of the highest global rates of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), with the incidence of pediatric-onset IBD increasing by approximately 1.27% annually.3 Children and adolescents with IBD often experience more extensive disease, higher inflammatory burden, and a greater risk of complications than adults.4 As youth with IBD transition to adult care, they are expected to independently monitor symptoms, manage medications, and participate in shared decision-making.5 Education therefore plays a central role in preparing AYAs to assume new responsibilities and engage effectively with the adult healthcare system. The Canadian Consensus Statements on transition of AYAs with IBD emphasize structured, developmentally appropriate education as a core component of effective transition care. The statements recommend proactive education for AYAs and their families to support disease knowledge, self-management skills, and readiness for adult care.6

In practice, the transition to adult IBD care can fall short of these ideals. Pediatric and adult gastroenterologists have identified gaps in disease-specific knowledge and self-management readiness at the time of transition, with limited structured tools to support patient education.2,7 As a result, AYAs enter adult care with limited understanding of their diagnosis, treatment history, differences in care practices, and monitoring requirements, despite years of pediatric follow-up.8–11 These gaps highlight the need for educational interventions that equip AYAs with the knowledge, skills, and confidence for successful transition and engagement in adult care.

To address these gaps, we developed the IBD-TrAYN (Transition Adolescent & Young Adults implementatioN) randomized controlled clinical trial (RCT) and launched a hybrid effectiveness and implementation trial in 2021 (NCT05221281).12 This national, multisite initiative aimed to provide evidence for transition interventions that improve outcomes for AYAs with IBD. A component of IBD-TrAYN was 21 online modules that were developed with input from pediatric and adult gastroenterologists, IBD nurse specialists, and patient partners across Canada (Figure 1). Topics covered disease knowledge and health behaviours, with the overarching goal of empowering AYAs to self-manage their condition and engage confidently in adult care. The aim of this quality improvement (QI) project was to explore the perspectives of Canadian adult gastroenterologists on transition-related education for AYAs with IBD and on the educational curriculum developed as part of the IBD-TrAYN intervention.

Methods

Design

This quality improvement project was nested within the IBD-TrAYN multisite type 1 hybrid effectiveness-implementation RCT of a multimodal transition intervention for AYAs with IBD across 3 Canadian tertiary care centres (NCT05221281).12 A qualitative descriptive approach with inductive content analysis was used to explore gastroenterologists’ perspectives on transition-related education and the educational curriculum. Approval was obtained through the Women’s College Hospital Assessment Process for Quality Improvement Projects (APQIP) (APQIP #2023-0063-P).

Recruitment

Eligible participants were English-speaking adult gastroenterologists practicing in Canada with experience managing AYAs (18-25 years old) with IBD. Participants were recruited using purposive and snowball sampling methods to ensure diverse perspectives from community-based practices and tertiary care settings. A total of 37 participants were invited to participate in focus groups by email. Individuals who expressed interest received an informed consent form for completion prior to being scheduled for focus groups. Of these, 12 individuals consented and 11 participated. Recruitment was guided by information power, which emphasizes that sample size adequacy depends on the richness and relevance of data. Recruitment concluded once sufficient information power was achieved to meaningfully address the objectives as determined based on ongoing review of interview transcripts and preliminary thematic analyses.13

Data collection

Demographic information was collected via a pre-survey that was emailed to participants at enrollment. Virtual focus group interviews were conducted during the summer of 2024 by 4 qualitative researchers (A.M., B.A., N.B., and N.F.) using a semistructured interview guide (Appendix S1). The guide was designed to explore gastroenterologists’ perspectives on education for AYAs with IBD during the transition to adult care. Participants were asked to comment on current approaches to transition-related education in clinical practice, strengths and barriers to education delivery, and strengths and limitations of the IBD-TrAYN educational curriculum.

The IBD-TrAYN curriculum comprises of 21 online modules organized into themes of disease knowledge, self-management and health behaviours, psychological well-being, and navigation of adult care. Each module includes written content, embedded videos, and brief knowledge checks. Prior to each focus group, participants received a structured overview of all modules, including learning objectives and content outline, and were given access to the platform for independent review between recruitment and the focus group.

Each focus group lasted 60-90 minutes and included 2-6 participants to facilitate balanced participation and in-depth discussion. Focus groups began with a brief overview of the education topics and modules as a refresher prior to discussion. Sessions were facilitated by an experienced qualitative researcher, with a second team member monitoring the group dynamic. Focus groups were conducted using a secure institutional Zoom account, digitally recorded, transcribed verbatim, and de-identified before analysis. Reflexive journals documented analytic insights, patterns, and reflections on the interview data.14

Data analysis

Transcripts were analyzed using an inductive content analysis approach to identify key patterns and themes.15,16 Interview transcripts were independently reviewed by 2 coders (Z.M. and Y.R.W.). Using an inductive line-by-line coding approach, initial codes were developed using participants’ own language where possible. Bi-weekly meetings were held to consider emerging patterns, refine the coding structure, and consolidate categories. A third team member with qualitative expertise (B.A.) was available to support this process. Data collection and analysis proceeded iteratively until the research team agreed the dataset contained sufficient analytic depth to address the study aims, with later focus groups producing repetition rather than new concepts. Through iterative discussions, codes were organized into broader themes and relationships, which were reviewed collaboratively among the larger team to refine the final thematic framework.

Results

Three virtual focus groups were conducted with 11 adult gastroenterologists from community (n = 3) and tertiary care settings (n = 8) in Canada. Four participants were female and 7 were males. Participants were primarily from Ontario (n = 6) and British Columbia (n = 3), with others from Nova Scotia (n = 1) and Alberta (n = 1). There was a greater predominance of mid-career (≥10 years in practice; n = 8) compared to early-career gastroenterologists (4-10 years; n = 3). All participants treated AYAs in their practice (n = 11). Almost all described seeing 100 or more AYAs in a year (n = 9), with 1 participant seeing over 500. Ten participants had access to at least 1 allied health professional for support. Demographic characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1.

Mapping of themes and subthemes.

Domain A: current practice gaps in transition-related education

Systemic and resource barriers

Time limitations were consistently cited as a barrier to education delivery in the clinic setting (A01, A02, and A03). Participants described clinic appointments as brief, primarily focused on disease management, leaving little time to address topics such as mental health, nutrition, lifestyle, or reproductive health (C02). In the absence of dedicated transition clinics, participants reported distributing teaching across multiple visits. They described the variability, depth and scope of education provided. Limited access to allied health professionals, such as dietitians, psychologists, and IBD nurse specialists, was reported as a barrier, particularly outside of academic centres. Participants described multidisciplinary support as “extraordinarily deficient” and relied on ad hoc strategies such as printed materials and online searches to supplement patient education (C02).

Communication barriers

Participants described that variability in patient engagement, comfort levels, and health literacy makes it challenging to gauge patient understanding of their disease (A04). Participants observed that many AYAs were hesitant to ask questions or share concerns regarding their health during clinic visits, which they attributed to embarrassment, distrust, or dependence on caregivers (A01). Several reported directing explanations to caregivers rather than patients, particularly when caregivers were present (A02 and A04). Participants also described challenges related to establishing rapport and managing resistance to changes in medical therapy, which they attributed to prior care experiences and differing expectations entering adult care.

Sensitive topics

Participants named mental health, sexual health, and body image as important areas of care that were infrequently addressed in clinical encounters (B04). Many described feeling constrained by time, discomfort, or uncertainty about how to approach these topics (B05). They reported that such discussions were often deprioritized in favour of immediate medical concerns and typically occurred only when patients raised them directly. Topics related to sexual health, including fertility, contraception, and intimacy, were described as particular areas of discomfort, with some physicians hesitant to initiate discussions due to uncertainty around timing, perceived sensitivity, or lack of training (A02). Several also noted the limited availability of structured educational resources or psychosocial supports to address these topics (B04 and C02).

Developmental and life stage

Participants described the transition period as coinciding with a life stage characterized by competing priorities, including academic demands, social relationships, employment, and lifestyle changes (A03 and B01). They reported that, as parental oversight decreased, some young adults placed less emphasis on health management, which they associated with inconsistent engagement in care (A02). They described observations of delayed symptom reporting, missed medication doses, and gaps in patients’ understanding of disease triggers during this period. Participants also noted variability in AYAs’ readiness for self-management (C02). While some patients were described as confident and independent in managing their care, others were reported as reliant on caregivers for appointment scheduling, medication organization, and communication with the healthcare team. Participants described these differences in readiness in relation to family involvement, individual characteristics, and prior preparation within pediatric care settings.

Domain B: gastroenterologist feedback on the IBD-TrAYN curriculum

Perceived value of education curriculum

Participants emphasized the importance of a structured approach to education for young adults with IBD. They noted that educational practices differed in content, depth, and format, and described variability in patients’ understanding of their condition and preparedness for transition (A01, A04, and B02). They described variation in how disease-related information was delivered across providers, healthcare centres, and between pediatric and adult care settings (A01 and B04). Participants also expressed support for a centralized, evidence-based educational resource that could be used across regions and practice settings. Several described such a resource as a way to provide more consistent information to patients. Current educational approaches were described as fragmented, with participants reporting reliance on printed materials, verbal explanations, and internet resources shared during clinic visits (C02). Participants described the modules as addressing topics that were not routinely covered during clinical encounters (A02). They noted the value of materials that patients could review independently and revisit over time, depending on their level of readiness and understanding (C02).

Core educational topics

Participants prioritized modules on disease knowledge, mental health, sexual health, and disease self-management as essential components of an educational curriculum for AYAs with IBD.

Disease knowledge

Participants emphasized the importance of foundational knowledge about IBD, including disease pathophysiology, symptom recognition, medication uses, and surgical considerations such as stomas (A02). Participants described encountering patients who lacked a clear understanding of their chronic diagnosis or the rationale for ongoing treatment, even after prolonged engagement in pediatric care (A03).

Mental health

Mental health was consistently identified as an important topic. Participants described anxiety, depression, and emotional distress as common among AYAs with IBD, but they noted that these issues were not routinely discussed in depth during clinic visits (A03). Several participants reported that patients appeared to have limited coping strategies for managing the psychological impact of their disease, and that they themselves had few resources available to offer support in this area (B04 and C02).

Sexual health

Participants identified sexual health as an area that was infrequently addressed in clinical practice (A02). Participants noted that issues related to relationships, family planning, and body image were particularly relevant for young adults with IBD (A04). However, discussions were deferred, often due to limited time, participant discomfort, or uncertainty about patient readiness (B05).

Self-management and resilience

Participants described self-management and resilience as important skills during transition (A01). They reported that patients who demonstrated confidence, organization, and self-advocacy were more engaged in their care (A03 and B01). Participants also noted that competing demands, such as academic and social commitments, commonly interfered with patients’ ability to prioritize health-related tasks.

Content delivery and future directions

Participants suggested simplifying content to focus on high-priority topics and avoid overwhelming patients (A04). They described the value of flexible content that patients could explore as needed and that providers could reference selectively during clinical encounters (B01) (Figure 2). Participants also identified interactive and multimedia elements, such as videos, quizzes, and visual aids, as valuable features to support engagement (B04). Some suggested that mobile access or integration with social media platforms could better align content delivery with how young adults typically consume information.

Priorities for refinement of IBD-TrAYN Educational Curriculum.

Several gastroenterologists recommended expanding the curriculum to include modules for caregivers and families, as well as additional topics such as travel and medication management (B03, B04, and C02). Participants also described interest in incorporating mechanisms to evaluate the curriculum’s use or impact, including metrics such as appointment attendance.

Discussion

Using qualitative research methodology, we explored adult gastroenterologists’ perspectives on transition-related education and the IBD-TrAYN curriculum designed to support AYAs with IBD. Our findings echo previously documented barriers to transition education and describe novel contributions, namely adult gastroenterologist appraisal of a structured, transition curriculum with recommendations for its refinement. Gastroenterologists described challenges in delivering comprehensive transition-related education within routine clinical practice. Within this context, gastroenterologists viewed the IBD-TrAYN curriculum as a valuable adjunct to clinical care, offering a structured and accessible approach to addressing educational gaps that commonly arise during the transition from pediatric to adult care.

Gastroenterologists consistently described current transition education as variable across practice settings, shaped by differences in provider time, experience, and access to institutional and allied health resources. Time constraints emerged as the most pervasive challenge, with clinic visits often focused on immediate disease management rather than broader educational or psychosocial needs. As a result, discussions related to mental health, nutrition, lifestyle, and reproductive health were frequently deferred or addressed inconsistently. Limited access to dietitians, mental health providers, and IBD nurse specialists, particularly in community practices, further constrained the ability to deliver comprehensive transition education. In contrast, those practicing in settings with integrated multidisciplinary teams described fewer barriers. These observations are largely confirmatory of prior Canadian and international literature documenting fragment transition processes, particularly outside of tertiary pediatric centres.6,17 Variability in disease knowledge at the time of transition was also commonly noted, consistent with prior literature that highlights gaps in AYA understanding of disease mechanisms, treatments, and monitoring strategies.6,17 The curriculum was therefore viewed not as a replacement for clinical visits, but as a complementary tool that could reinforce and standardize delivery of education regardless of local resources, helping to reduce inequities in transition education.

Across focus groups, gastroenterologists identified a core set of educational priorities for AYAs transitioning to adult care, including disease knowledge, self-management skills, mental health, and sexual health. These domains highlight that effective transition extends beyond disease control to also encompass autonomy, confidence, and psychosocial well-being. Mental health and sexual health emerged as particularly important yet inconsistently addressed areas of transition care. Individuals with IBD experience psychiatric disorders at rates 1.5 to 2 times higher than the general population, with transition-aged youth facing up to a sixfold increased risk of depression.18 Sexual dysfunction is also common but frequently underreported among individuals with IBD, with estimates suggesting that 40%-60% of patients are affected.19 Despite this prevalence, gastroenterologists described these topics as challenging to address during routine visits due to time constraints, discomfort initiating discussions, and limited access to specialized supports. Gastroenterologists interpreted these gaps as having downstream implications for AYAs, noting that unaddressed mental health concerns can negatively influence treatment adherence, symptom perception, and overall engagement in care. This is consistent with literature linking psychological distress in AYAs with IBD to school absences, social isolation, restrictive dietary behaviours, and heightened symptom-related anxiety.19,20 As a result, the inclusion of mental and sexual health content within the curriculum can normalize these discussions, provide shared language for patients and providers, and support ongoing dialogue over time. This supports previous qualitative work demonstrating that AYAs prefer proactive initiation of mental health discussions by IBD providers, underscoring the value of structured tools that normalize these conversations.20

Beyond disease knowledge, self-management capacity emerged as an important determinant of sustained engagement in adult care. Gastroenterologists emphasized the importance of competencies such as medication adherence, symptom monitoring, appointment coordination, and navigation of healthcare systems as responsibility shifts to the AYA. Educational content supporting self-advocacy and communication was viewed as valuable for fostering the language and confidence needed to articulate concerns and engage in shared decision-making. This interpretation aligns with broader chronic disease literature, which identifies communication and self-management capacity as key drivers of engagement and long-term outcomes during transition to adult services.21,22

Gastroenterologists provided feedback on the curriculum, along with recommendations for refinement. Key priorities included maintaining concise content, increasing interactivity, and ensuring flexible access. While prior literature has highlighted AYA preferences for interactive, self-paced learning tools, adult gastroenterologist perspectives on the design and integration of such resources have been less well characterized until now.23 Flexibility was identified as a strength of the platform, allowing AYAs to engage with content according to readiness, learning style, and stage of transition. Many gastroenterologists also advocated for the inclusion of caregiver-focused content, recognizing the evolving role of caregivers and the need to support families during this period. Existing literature in IBD and other chronic diseases suggests that while caregivers play a critical role in support and advocacy, many can experience emotional vulnerability and uncertainty as responsibility shifts to the AYA.24,25

Our work has several limitations. Participants were volunteer, English-speaking gastroenterologists, which may introduce selection bias toward those with an interest in education and transition care. Although gastroenterologists from multiple provinces and practice settings were invited, the final sample was concentrated in Ontario and British Columbia and weighted toward academic centres. This may limit transferability to other regions, including those with different health infrastructures, lower patient volumes, or fewer pediatric-adult interfaces. Given that community settings often have fewer allied health resources, many of the identified gaps and recommendations are likely applicable across contexts. The time constraints reported here may in fact underestimate the challenges in community settings, where curriculum-based supports may be even more valuable. Given their central role in receiving AYAs into adult care, this project focused on adult gastroenterologists and does not capture the perspectives of pediatric gastroenterologists or allied health professionals. These findings therefore reflect a single stakeholder group and may not align with the priorities or experiences of other groups.

Future work should aim to recruit more geographically diverse and community-based stakeholders, triangulate findings across groups, and evaluate the impact on measurable outcomes, such as patient-reported confidence and engagement in care.

Conclusions

Adult gastroenterologists identified the IBD-TrAYN curriculum as a valuable resource for supporting transition-related education among AYAs. The curriculum offers a promising approach to delivering standardized evidence-informed educational content that addresses both medical and psychosocial domains of IBD care. Thoughtful integration into clinical practice, alongside ongoing refinement informed by stakeholder feedback, may enhance its utility and support transition education across diverse care settings.
