Cilostazol Conduction Effects
Authors: Justin Haloot, Yochai Birnbaum
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey words: arrhythmias, cilostazol, conduction system

Cilostazol, a phosphodiesterase-3 (PDE3) inhibitor, is widely recognized for its vascular and antiplatelet effects. The case by Aryal et al1 describing rapid improvement in Lyme carditis–related atrioventricular (AV) block after cilostazol invites renewed examination of this agent within conduction-system physiology.

Cilostazol selectively inhibits PDE3, increasing intracellular cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP) and activating protein kinase A,2 which lead to enhancement of L-type calcium current, modulation of potassium currents, and increased spontaneous depolarizations of pacemaker cells. These features make cilostazol a drug whose pleiotropy extends across platelets, vascular smooth muscles, and the cardiac conduction system.2,3 Popeye domain-containing (POPDC) proteins mediate the effects of cAMP on the conduction system.4

In addition, cilostazol inhibits adenosine reuptake, augmenting A1-mediated hyperpolarization exerted on pacemaker tissue.3 This dual mechanism of enhancing cAMP while augmenting adenosine's inhibitory signal produces a balanced chronotropic and dromotropic profile.3 The direct PDE3-mediated enhancement of L-type calcium currents may override adenosine-mediated suppression in the AV node, leading to dromotropic effects.

Toyonaga et al5 showed that cilostazol significantly increased minimal, mean, and maximum heart rate and reduced pauses in bradycardic atrial fibrillation. These effects were attributed to shortening of the AV nodal refractory period and improved conduction.5 Earlier Japanese studies similarly demonstrated accelerated ventricular response owing to enhanced nodal conduction.6,7

Sonoura et al7 found that patients treated with 100 to 200 mg/d of cilostazol had markedly lower pacemaker implantation rate at 6 months (OR: 0.22, 95% CI: 0.08-0.55) when compared to controls with sick sinus syndrome. Jin et al8 showed that cilostazol produced chronotropic benefit comparable to theophylline, improving intrinsic sinus rate by 12 to 17 beats/min and reducing symptoms.

In idiopathic complete AV block, Kodama-Takahashi et al9 reported accelerated ventricular escape rhythm and shorter R-R intervals with cilostazol therapy, accompanied by reduced natriuretic peptides, suggesting improved hemodynamics driven by faster escape rates. Nimura et al10 documented recovered AV conduction in a patient with complete block treated with cilostazol 200 mg/d.

While cilostazol is avoided in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, favorable effects have been reported in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). The CLIP-HFpEF trial showed that cilostazol reduced NT-proBNP and, in patients with implanted pulmonary artery monitors, lowered diastolic pulmonary arterial pressure associated with modest heart rate increases.11 These findings support the concept that chronotropic augmentation may modestly improve hemodynamics in select patients.

In patients with peripheral arterial disease, cilostazol shortened total atrial conduction time from 121 to 109 ms (P < 0.001), suggesting enhanced intra-atrial conduction velocity.12 Uchikawa et al13 reported improved heart rate and cardiac output in a patient with symptomatic post–heart transplant bradycardia.

Collectively, these studies portray that cilostazol may be associated with an increased sinus rate, enhanced dromotropy, increased ventricular escape pacemaker rate, enhanced intra-atrial conduction velocity, and improved hemodynamics in patients with HFpEF from heart rate–mediated effects.

In the case by Aryal et al,1 the rapid improvement in AV conduction temporally after initiating cilostazol aligns with the drug's reported effects. While spontaneous recovery is common in Lyme carditis, cilostazol's potential mechanisms—shortening AV nodal refractoriness, attenuating adenosine-mediated suppression, and enhancing nodal automaticity—may have accelerated the recovery process.

Cilostazol's oral formulation, relative safety, and physiologic profile position it as a potential adjunctive therapy in selected patients with reversible conduction disturbances. The present case provides interesting evidence that cilostazol may serve as a useful adjunct for reversible AV conduction system disease. Cilostazol's chronotropic and dromotropic effects warrant further systematic study as a targeted bridge for conduction system disease where full recovery is expected.
