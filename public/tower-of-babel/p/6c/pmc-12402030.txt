Artificial intelligence-based approaches for sleep-related breathing events identification using EEG and ECG signals
Authors: Nguyen Thi Hoang Trang, Tran Thanh Duy Linh, Do Quoc Vu, Bui Thi Hong Loan, Nguyen Nhu Vinh, Tran Ngoc Dang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposesSleep apnea or hypopnea is a sleep-related breathing disorder characterized by insufficient ventilation during sleep. Sleep apnea is classified into two major forms: obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and central sleep apnea (CSA). The conventional diagnosis with Polysomnography (PSG) is time-consuming, uncomfortable, and costly in the clinical setting. To address these issues, wearable devices and AI techniques have been developed, utilizing single or multi-modal physiological signals. This study aims to deploy a multi-modal approach by analyzing both EEG and ECG signals derived from home sleep testing devices for OSA/CSA/hypopnea identification. A robust ensemble learning model is proposed to compare with the performance of the deep learning model in event classification.MethodsEEG and ECG signals from 201 PSG were collected. Non-linear features extracted by wavelet transform methods and machine learning were used to develop a classification algorithm. ECG spectrograms and the deep learning model were also deployed to compare with traditional method. Two classification strategies including 3-class (OSA-hypopnea-normal, OSA-CSA-normal) and 2-class (OSA-hypopnea, OSA-CSA) were also examined.ResultsThe highest classification performance was achieved using the combined signal-based model with 98.8% accuracy, 99.1% sensitivity, and 98.5% specificity for classifying OSA and CSA. When compared with the deep learning model, the classification accuracy of the combined signal-based machine learning model was significantly higher in almost all classification strategies.ConclusionThe findings highlight the effectiveness of combining non-linear features from ECG and EEG signals for classifying various sleep-related breathing events. A proposed machine learning model provides significantly precise classification compared to a deep learning approach, offering improved reliability in-home sleep setting.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea, Central sleep apnea, Hypopnea, Wavelet transform, Machine learning, Deep learning

Introduction

Sleep apnea (SA) affects millions worldwide and poses significant health burdens, including cardiovascular diseases, cognitive impairment, daytime sleepiness, reduced quality of life, traffic accidents, and overall mortality [1–6]. Timely and accurate diagnosis could mitigate these risks.

Sleep apnea is characterized by the temporary reduction (hypopnea) or complete closure (apnea) of breathing during sleep. Apnea can be classified into three primary apnea types: obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) caused by airway blockages with respiratory effort; central sleep apnea (CSA) due to lack of respiratory drive; and mixed sleep apnea (MSA), combined the features of OSA and CSA [7, 8]. Hypopnea, though less severe, disrupts sleep quality and overall health [9]. Differentiating these respiratory sleep-related event types is critical for precise diagnosis and targeted treatment strategies.

Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard for diagnosing sleep apnea, reliably identifying sleep-related respiratory events [10]. However, it is costly, time-consuming, and requires an overnight stay in a clinic, driving an interest in developing automation. With advancements in home-based methods, several works have been focused on utilizing sleep assessments with fewer physiological signals such as home sleep testing (HST) type III or type IV to achieve automated detection of sleep apnea. Research by Facco et al. [11] found that HST demonstrates a high intraclass correlation and strong agreement with in-lab PSG for diagnosing sleep apnea. Whereas more research has proven the effect of HST tools and wearable devices in patients with the risk of CSA [12]. Additionally, Collop and Corral [13, 14] observed that the HST, which monitors less number of respiratory channels at home, measures as well as in-lab PSG in OSA patients. The development of sleep technologies could enhance sleep apnea detection due to their convenience compared to PSG.

Single-modality ECG-based algorithms have demonstrated strong performance, with accuracies exceeding 90%, in detecting OSA versus normal breathing [15, 16]. However, ECG is prone to movement artifacts and noise, limiting its reliability. In contrast, EEG captures brain activity, offering unique advantages for identifying CSA and analyzing sleep stages that influence respiratory event characteristics [17]. Combining ECG and EEG can leverage these complementary strengths to improve classification accuracy and overcome the limitations of single-modality approaches. Saghafi et al. used the combined features from EEG and ECG signals to distinguish apnea and other events with a high accuracy of 94.4% [18].

Most studies focus on classifying apnea versus normal breathing or grouping apnea types and hypopnea into a single category [19–22]. Only a few studies have classified different respiratory event types such as OSA, CSA, and hypopnea, with an accuracy ranging from 79.8% [20] to 83% [21]. Two studies classified OSA, CSA, or MSA using thorax or abdominal movement, achieving the accuracy of 83.8% and 85.6% [23, 24], respectively. Zhao et al. (2021) achieved 88% accuracy using EEG sub-band signals to classify OSA, CSA, and normal breathing events [25]​​.

Previous studies have used hand-crafted features derived from heart rate variability (HRV), electrocardiogram-derived respiratory (EDR), and cardiopulmonary coupling (CPC) for identifying SA. However, RR interval and EDR data collection is time-consuming, reducing the temporal resolution of algorithms. On the other hand, CPC can visualize RR interval and EDR coupling to assess sleep quality or breathing disorders, but its temporal resolution is also poor due to the high variability in breathing patterns. Wavelet analysis, a time-frequency decomposition technique, provides better temporal localization and well-suit for non-stationary signals such as EEG, and ECG. It can also generate 2D spectrograms to visualize frequency variations in sleep events. Penzel et al. [10] first observed differences in ECG spectrograms between SA and normal breathing. Sinam et al. [26] later used continuous wavelet transform (CWT) to create 60-second ECG scalograms and fed them into a pre-trained AlexNet model to detect OSA. Their result showed that this method achieved satisfactory performance with low cost and less complexity.

As shown above, this study aimed to develop an optimized algorithm to classify OSA, CSA, and hypopnea from normal breathing. We approach this in two steps: first, applying a 3-class classification to differentiate abnormal breathing events from normal breathing, and second, using a 2-class classification to distinguish abnormal event types. By combining features from ECG and EEG and employing advanced signal processing techniques with wavelet transforms, we hypothesize improved classification performance compared to single-modality approaches. We also conduct machine learning and deep learning algorithms in classification tasks to investigate the optimized and reliable model for sleep-related respiratory event classification.

Materials and methods

Sleep data used in this study were collected from two distinct databases: a private Sleep Clinic and the Sleep lab of the University Medical Center in Ho Chi Minh City. The data was recorded on a Nox A1-portable full PSG recorder and scored with Noxturnal software version 6.2. The data utilized in this study were not collected in a sleep laboratory setting but were primarily attempted to do the home testing. Nox A1, which has been previously validated [27], was configured to identify sleep-related breathing events according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) manual. This experimental protocol emphasizes realistic medical scenarios by accelerating the diagnostic process and simplifying assessments to improve treatment effectiveness. A schematic of the proposed sleep-related breathing events classification algorithm based on EEG and ECG signals details described in Fig. 1.

The flowchart of the proposed classification algorithm. The process of sleep-related breathing events classification based on EEG and ECG signals consists of signal preprocessing, feature extraction and selection, and model construction. These signals are decomposed into different frequency sub-bands using wavelet transform technology which is a two-dimensional signal analysis to obtain time and frequency components simultaneously. In response to reducing feature dimensions, a feature selection method using Neighborhood Component Analysis (NCA) is applied to choose the optimal feature set. The obtained features were then utilized as inputs for the classifiers. Finally, we developed two distinct models: the first one used machine learning for classifying features derived from signal decomposition, while the deep learning approach employed direct ECG spectrograms for sleep-related breathing events classification. The models are evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation and compared to their performance. Additionally, two classification approaches include 3-class (OSA-hypopnea-normal; OSA-CSA-normal) and 2-class (OSA-hypopnea; OSA-CSA). Study participants

In this work, we excluded the participants with the following criteria: PSG recordings for continuous positive airway pressure ventilation titration, the use of hypnotic medicine during the measurement, and worse signal quality. As a result, the study analyzed 201 participants including 55 women and 146 men, who underwent overnight PSG recordings using the Nox A1-portable device and Noxturnal software version 6.2. The characteristics of those were aged 51 ± 15.7 years, BMI 26.8 ± 4.7 kg/m2, and AHI 38.9 ± 27.1 (mean ± SD). The data deployed were collected in home-based testing and the measurement process was prepared by technical specialists. The protocol was approved by the Board of Ethics in Biomedical Research at the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (protocol number 585/UMP-BOARD).

Data preprocessing

From each PSG recording, two channels C3M2 and C4M1 of EEG and single-lead ECG data were exported at 200 Hz sampling frequency for respiratory event types classification. EEG and ECG signals were first preprocessed with noise removal and segmented into consecutive 30-second epochs. With annotations scored for each 30-second epoch, it was labeled as either OSA, CSA, hypopnea, or normal breathing following the criteria of the AASM manual [28]. An OSA event was defined as ≥ 90% oronasal airflow reduction for ≥ 10 s, associated with the presence of respiratory effort. Score a CSA event, on the other hand, was associated with absent respiratory effort during the duration of ≥ 90% airflow decrease lasting at least 10 s. Hypopneas were scored when there was a drop by ≥ 30% of nasal pressure or with a > 3% reduction in oxygen saturation.

In addition, it is particularly important to describe the effect of the sleep stage on the occurrence of sleep-related breathing events. We classified the data into two categories: REM and NREM sleep stages concerning respiratory event types. Due to a high imbalance between normal and other events, the input data were then balanced using the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE) to reduce bias toward the majority class. The SMOTE technique creates synthetic samples for the minority classes rather than duplicating the data. It is implemented by determining its k-nearest neighbors using an Euclidean distance in the feature space and randomly selecting one of the k-nearest neighbors for the current sample. Finally, a new synthetic sample is created by interpolating between the current sample and the selected neighbor. This process is repeated until the desired level of oversampling is achieved for the minority classes [29]. In this study, to perform SMOTE, k = 10 and an oversampling rate based on the imbalanced ratio were set (Table 1).

Data analyses approach

In this work, EEG and ECG signals were initially preprocessed by artifact removal, standardization, and moving average before segmenting into consecutive 30-second epochs as labeling OSA, CSA, hypopnea, or normal. Subsequently, a wavelet transform technique is selected to transform time-domain signals into the time-frequency domain which facilitates better recognizing different signal patterns. This method allows simultaneous localizing in both the time and frequency domain, therefore is suitable for analyzing dynamic signals such as EEG and ECG. Accordingly, we performed this approach including Wavelet Packet Analysis (WPA) and Discrete Wavelet Transform (DWT) to extract the features from original ECG and EEG signals based on experimental analyses described in previous studies [19, 25]. Several statistical functions were then used to calculate non-linear parameters for all obtained features before being fed to classifiers.

EEG signals analysis using DWT

A Butterworth bandpass filter of 0.5–64 Hz and a 50 Hz notch filter were applied to remove artifacts from two C3M2 and C4M1 channels. Due to the random appearance on the time scale of the EEG signals, the data were then standardized with the range [−1,1] to eliminate the improper fluctuation occurring in the amplitude of EEGs. DWT was performed on the standardized EEG signals to decompose into five sub-bands corresponding to the brainwaves: gamma (32–64 Hz), beta (16–32 Hz), alpha (8–16 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), and delta (0–4 Hz) [30, 31] (Figure S1-S3 in the supplemental material).

Non-linear statistical measures for evaluating time series complexity are appropriate for managing the inherent randomness of EEG signals [32]. Therefore, we computed 5 statistical indices including mean, standard deviation, skewness, kurtosis, and median. As a result, a total of 75 feature elements were extracted for each channel of EEGs.

ECG signals analysis using WPA

The morphology of ECG signals is varied due to heart rate change and blood oxygen saturation. These variations are particularly evident during sleep-related breathing events such as OSA, hypopnea, or CSA, where disruptions in airflow and thorax movement. It has been observed that the heart rate intervals of the ECG signal exhibit specific patterns that are associated with events occurring during sleep apnea [33]. For removing artifacts from the ECG signals, the signal was preprocessed by a bandpass filter with a low cutoff frequency at 1 Hz and a high cutoff at 49 Hz before applying a moving average filter (MAF) with a specific window length (m = 5) [34] for smoothing the signal. Next, the data was deconstructed into different frequency components using wavelet packet analysis (WPA). From each obtained component, a variety of non-linear features were computed, including the entropy of each sub-band [35] and four statistical parameters: interquartile range (IQR), variance, standard deviation, and mean absolute deviation (MAD) [19]. This process resulted in a feature vector of length 310.

ECG spectrogram

The spectrogram is a 3D image that includes the time axis, frequency axis, and energy distribution. Legarreta et al. [36] used a spectrogram to display the energy distribution of a different wave of ECG signal, and the QRS complex wave is the most powerful characterization. To generate a spectrogram, CWT is widely performed, which adaptively adjusts time lengths to optimize resolution across different frequency ranges. In CWT, the signals are analyzed using a set of basis functions that relate to each other by simple scaling and translation. Therefore, the CWT can localize frequency components in time, making it particularly useful for analyzing non-stationary signals. We applied CWT with the analytical Morlet wavelet for this study to compute ECG spectrograms. A specific frequency band at 0.5-50 Hz was selected based on the previous study [26]. Feature selection

In developing a classification model, feature selection is an essential step because it eliminates redundant features in the dataset that lead to reducing the complexity of the computations and optimizing the classification performance. In our study, we deployed Neighborhood Component Analysis (NCA) to select robust feature subsets from ECG and EEG signals. NCA selects the best feature subsets by calculating parameter λ. At each subset, a value of λ is assigned to estimate the loss value. The best λ value is defined as corresponding to the minimum average loss value [37]. Consequently, a new feature subset is selected by maximizing the average classification performance over the training data. As a result, 20 ECG-based features and 45 EEG-based features which have the highest weights were chosen and fed to the classification model. Model building

The classification models represented for machine learning and deep learning approaches were constructed: Ensemble Learning (EL) and ResNet50. The classifiers used for model building were implemented in MATLAB 2024a using Deep Learning and Classifier Learner Toolbox.

Ensemble learning

A machine learning model and features derived from 30-second EEG and ECG epochs were developed to classify sleep-related breathing events as OSA, CSA, hypopnea, and normal breathing. Ensemble learning (EL) is a robust technique that combines several models to obtain better predictions and generate a strong classifier. By aggregating predictions from multiple classifiers, the ensemble method can reduce the error rate of a weak classifier and capture diverse feature interactions. However, training on multiple models requires extensive computational resources and leads to slower inference times. Two main ensemble models have been introduced, including bootstrap aggregation (bagging) and boosting [38], which are less complex and fast algorithms. An Ensemble Bagged Tree (EBT) model was proposed in this work for automatic sleep events classification with NumberLearning of 30. EBT creates subspaces from the random training data and is used to train independent decision trees. The probabilities of the individual trees are aggregated to determine the final prediction by computing the weighted average [39]. This algorithm proposed an easy computational model, reducing the complexity and optimizing the classification accuracy.

ResNet-50

We attempted a deep learning-based model using 30-second ECG spectrograms for respiratory event classification. A deep learning architecture based on ResNet50 has been shown to learn directly from the data input without human feature extraction and advance the performance. In this work, we optimized the ResNet50 structure by replacing the last three layers with new layers tailored to the input data and classification tasks (Figure S4 in the supplemental material). ResNet50 model was trained using Stochastic gradient descent with momentum (sgdm) training option, learning rate of 0.0001, mini-batch size of 50, and maximum epochs of 50. Output was obtained by computing the probability of each class. This provides a comprehensive perspective on the different feature types that can contribute to sleep-related breathing event classification.

Validation method

The input data were divided into a ratio of 8:2 corresponding training and tuning sets, and a separate testing set. First, training and tuning sets were utilized to train machine learning and deep learning models with a 5-fold cross-validation technique. The best performance of models was evaluated on the tuning set using models trained on the training set across all cross-validation folds. The models achieving the optimal performance on the tuning set were selected as the final models used for the test set. Data processing and analyses were executed using MATLAB R2024a software on a computer with 32 GB installed RAM, Intel® Core™ i7-13700 K @3.40 GHz, and NVIDIA Geforce GTX 3060 12GB mounted graphic card.

Model assessment

F1-score measures the weighted average between precision and recall. It has a range [0, 1] with 1 for the best efficient model and 0 for the failure.

Discussion

The main aim of the present study was to distinguish different sleep-related breathing events using non-linear features derived from EEG and ECG signals. The findings highlight the potential of the selected feature dataset can contribute to optimizing model performances. Additionally, the machine learning approach offers an advantage in clinical practice by understanding important features that contribute to output decisions.

Feature combination of different signals to sleep-related breathing events classification

We hypothesized that combining features extracted from wavelet transform analysis of both signals would enhance classification outcomes. From the experimental results, the proposed method showed an improvement of 2–18% on average compared to single signal-based models. Although the performance of the EEG-based model did not yield as high as other models, it complemented the accuracy when integrated with ECG features. These findings suggest that a dual-signal approach, leveraging both ECG and EEG, contributes to presenting the differential in characteristics of these sleep-related breathing events and offers a more effective solution for real-time detection than relying on a single signal. There have been current studies relating to home sleep testing (HST) and portable devices for real-time apnea detection [40, 41], which attempt to use the advancement of sensor technology in wearable detection systems. Therefore, a valuable attribute of this study is that it was conducted with a real-life dataset from participants whose PSG recordings were acquired in-home sleep testing. Moreover, the proposed method used in this work presents a robust combined signal-based model with high reliability and performance, making it a promising approach for real-time apnea detection.

The comparison between EEG and ECG signals demonstrated the potential of features derived from ECG signals in recognizing respiratory event categories. In particular, the results in OSA-CSA and OSA-CSA-normal classifications are consistently higher than in the classification of OSA-hypopnea and OSA-hypopnea-normal. This indicates that the ECG signal may be more effective in capturing changes in characteristics of sleep events, especially between OSA and CSA, compared to the EEG signal. Conversely, the lower performance in detecting OSA-hypopnea or OSA-hypopnea-normal can caused by a high degree of overlap in characteristics between OSA and hypopnea. This challenge could be attributed to the data acquisition process, which was conducted outside a controlled laboratory environment.

CSA and OSA differ fundamentally in their underlying pathophysiology: CSA is characterized by the absence of respiratory effort due to lack of neural drive, while OSA involves persistent respiratory effort against an obstructed airway. This physiological distinction can be captured using both EEG and ECG signals.

In addition to physiological distinctions, the ability to discriminate OSA from CSA using EEG and ECG is enhanced by the feature extraction strategies employed. EEG signals were decomposed using wavelet transforms to extract frequency-specific sub-band activities (delta, theta, alpha, beta, gamma), which reflect different sleep stages and arousal patterns. CSA is more likely to occur during lighter sleep stages (e.g., N1, N2) or during transitions, and is associated with periodic arousals, resulting in distinct EEG dynamics. In contrast, OSA may occur across all stages but typically induces stronger arousals following obstruction-related hypoxia. These characteristics are reflected in non-linear statistical measures extracted from EEG, such as skewness, kurtosis, and entropy within specific bands.

Similarly, ECG wavelet-based features capture autonomic regulation differences: CSA tends to have more regular RR intervals due to central drive loss, while OSA causes irregularity through sympathetic surges. The combination of these time-frequency and non-linear features enables the model to distinguish OSA and CSA patterns without requiring direct respiratory effort measurements.

Therefore, by extracting non-linear features from EEG and ECG, the proposed model can detect the physiological signatures specific to each apnea subtype. Prior studies (Saghafi et al., 2021 [18]; Gubbi et al., 2012 [19]) have also demonstrated successful CSA/OSA discrimination using these two signal modalities.

In this study, participants with known cardiovascular diseases were not intentionally recruited, as our sample primarily consisted of individuals undergoing diagnostic evaluation for sleep-disordered breathing. However, some cases of CSA were incidentally identified. It is important to acknowledge that, in clinical populations, CSA is often secondary to conditions such as heart failure or atrial fibrillation. This may result in underrepresentation of such phenotypes in our dataset. Despite this limitation, the model’s ability to discriminate CSA from OSA using only EEG and ECG features suggests that even non-cardiac CSA events may manifest distinct neurophysiological and autonomic patterns. This supports the hypothesis that CSA, regardless of etiology, produces detectable signatures in ECG and EEG signals. Nevertheless, future studies should consider including a broader CSA population, particularly those with cardiovascular comorbidities, to further validate and generalize the findings.

In addition, a robust deep learning algorithm using ResNet50 structure learning direct information from ECG spectrogram was employed for better performance. Previous studies have developed a deep learning framework based on Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and ResNet models with inputting spectrogram images [4, 42]. These techniques yielded good accuracy estimates in sleep apnea detection. Similarly, our attempt to use the deep learning model also achieved acceptable high results in classification strategies (accuracy values range from 79.3 to 97.2%) but did not surpass the traditional machine learning model. This finding highlights the advancement in simplified methods for the identification of the important features related to respiratory events that contribute to a precise classification model.

Methodology assessment with existing literature

There are several current studies on classifying different types of sleep apnea events. These studies vary in terms of datasets, methods, and the number of classes analyzed. While some studies considered apnea types like central or mixed central apnea [23, 24], others focused primarily on two classes [19, 43], such as obstructive apnea and non-apnea. Central sleep apnea, being less frequent, poses challenges in obtaining sufficient patient data, leading to a predominant focus on obstructive sleep apnea in most research. Our results for the 2-class classification approach were higher than [19, 43]. The study of Saghafi et al. [18] and Paul et al. [43] demonstrated that utilizing combined signals generally achieves better performance than a single signal. This underscores the advantages of integrating multiple signals, as complementary information from each signal enhances classification accuracy and robustness. By incorporating EEG in addition to ECG, our method achieved superior performance, with the highest accuracy of 98.8%. Processing methods in Wang et al. [22] and Saghafi et al. [18] used DWT to extract non-linear features, similar to our study. However, their accuracy, at 90.4% and 94.4%, respectively, was lower than that achieved by our presented approach. Gubbi et al. [19] proposed WPA to analyze ECG signals and used Shannon entropy as a feature obtaining 91% accuracy, 88.1% sensitivity, and 91.1% specificity. By also employing WPA but incorporating additional statistical features, our method achieved better performance in accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity, indicating the effectiveness of the proposed approach and feature selection in capturing signal variations. Various methods exist for detecting sleep apnea, mainly features based on time, frequency, non-linear domain, and machine learning methods (ANN, MLP, KNN, SVM, etc.) and deep learning (CNN, etc.). A summarized comparison between our proposed study and existing literature is shown in supplemental material Table S9. In this study, the EBT model is preferred a clearly strengh by its simple structure and low computation cost. Another strength is the processing signal techniques which facilitate the complex and non-stationary of EEG and ECG signals, resulting in improving respiratory event types classification.

Despite its advantages, this proposed method has certain limitations that should be considered. For example, the sample size was quite small, so we did not utilize an independent dataset for the evaluation model. However, high performance in our approach compared to other recent studies provides a potential classification model that would be considered and tested on a large-scale group of participants in future assessments. Additionally, the performance in classifying OSA and hypopnea of the EBT model was not satisfactory. It can be attributed to a considerable overlap in traits shared by OSA and hypopnea due to insufficient PSG collection process. Therefore, it is important to control and optimize the data collection process, especially in home testing, reducing the impact of interference and improving data quality.

Our study demonstrated that the combined features from EEG and ECG signals are suitable for classifying sleep-related breathing events. These non-linear features provide valuable information corresponding to abnormal event traits. Moreover, the proposed EBT model offers reduced complexity and cost-effective computation, making it practical for a more realistic scenario for medical applications. The acceptable accuracy achieved in this study highlights its potential for clinical practice, particularly in the development of wearable devices for home-based sleep testing.
