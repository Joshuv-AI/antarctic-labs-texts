Association of dietary fiber intake with Helicobacter pylori infection and all-cause mortality among US population
Authors: Run Zhang, Sen Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite limited researches revealed potential relationship between dietary habits and diseases in recent years, there is still a lack of systematic understanding of the relationship between dietary fiber intake and Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection. This study aim to investigate the relationship between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection, and to evaluate their impact on all-cause mortality.MethodsThis study was conducted based on data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999-2000). We employed logistic regression analyses, cox regression analysis and restricted regression cubic spline(RCS) analysis to assess the association between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection, as well as all-cause mortality in individuals with or without H. pylori infection.ResultsA total of 3803 participants were included in our analysis. We found that dietary fiber intake was negatively associated with seropositive for H. pylori even after adjusting for potential confounders. In addition, higher dietary fiber intake was collated to the lower all-cause mortality in seropositive to H. pylori patient with HR of 0.60 (95% CI 0.47, 0.75). In addition, there is a nonlinear relationship between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality only in seropositive for H. pylori individuals.ConclusionAn increase in dietary fiber intake was correlated with a lower risk of seropositive for H. pylori and lower all-cause mortality in US population. Additional work is needed to clarify the precise mechanisms underlying the association between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40795-025-01177-0.

Introduction

Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) is a common gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa and the surface of gastric epithelial cells in more than half of the world’s population [1]. H. pylori have a highly complex and diverse etiology and causes diverse non-specific clinical signs and symptoms. Although numerous medical therapies have achieved good efficacy for treating H. pylori infection, drug-resistance of H. pylori has still led to a great decrease in H. pylori eradication and an increase in reinfection rate over these years [2]. Studies found that persistent H. pylori infection has been considered as an important non-genetic factor contributing to a variety of gastric pathologies, including gastric cancer, peptic ulcers and gastric mucosa-associated tissue lymphoma (MALT) [3, 4]. Therefore, investigating the influencing factors of Helicobacter pylori (Hp) infection is of great significance.

pylori infection has been found to be influenced by numerous exogenous or endogenous elements, such as genes, diet patterns, drugs, antibiotics and stress [5, 6]. The dietary factor, as one of the most important exogenous elements, has been considered to play an important role in H. pylori infection. Previous studies have found that changes in dietary patterns significantly affected the intestinal flora, leading to changes of its structure [7]. Also, the intestinal microbiota could affect the composition of the gastric microbiota that influences H. pylori infection or gastric carcinogenesis [8, 9]. It has also been reported that the balanced diet patterns, especially those rich in fruits and vegetables, could help reduce the infection of H. pylori [10]. In addition, diets rich in carbohydrates or excessively sweet could increase the prevalence of H. pylori infection. However, further experiments were distressed to make better understanding for the role of the specific dietary components in the pathogenesis and development of H. pylori infection and the related disorders.

Dietary fiber is a general term for edible plant components, carbohydrates, with analogues being not easily digested and absorbed in the small intestine, but being fermented in the large intestine and have physiological effects. Dietary fiber has been acknowledged to have a significant impact on the composition, diversity and abundance of the microbiota, and therefore has an important role in regulating metabolism and preventing chronic gastrointestinal diseases [11]. High fiber intake has been discovered to be associated with a significant reduction in the risk of esophageal cancer and gastric adenocarcinoma [12, 13]. The above evidence has suggested a very important association between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection, as well as its associated diseases.

To the best of our knowledge, there have been few studies on the association between dietary fiber and H. pylori infection. Rikard Landberg’s study shown that high-fiber wholegrain rye foods did not affect Helicobacter pylori bacterial load [14]. Therefore, on the basis of the previous studies, we utilized data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES 1999–2000) to examine the association between diet fiber intake and the risk of seropositive for H. pylori after adjusting for some potential confounding. In addition, this study also analyzed whether H. pylori infection status modifies the association between dietary fiber intake and survival time. Better understanding of the importance of dietary factors in seropositive for H. pylori and their association with all-cause mortality might provide insights for future public health interventions for the prevention and treatment of H. pylori infection.

Methods

Study design and data source

All data used for our study were collected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES, 1999–2000), which contains a wide range of data including demographics, dietary patterns, physical examination results, laboratory results, and responses to questionnaires. More details about the NHANES design are available on the NHANES Web site (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm). NHANES was developed by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) using a complex, multi-stage, hierarchical, clustered probability technique to select a representative sample of US populations with the primary goal of assessing the health and nutritional status of individuals. CDC’s Institutional Institutional Review Board approved the survey protocol. Each participant submitted informed consent in NHANES. During NHANES from 1999 to 2000, individuals aged 20 years or older who had participated in consecutive study cycles were surveyed. In our analysis, exclusion criteria were participants with missing H. pylori serologic data and unclear serologic optical values. A total of 9965 participants were enrolled in the 1999–2000 cycle as an initial screening. After individuals with excluding variables recalling missing information, including dietary fiber, education level, hypertension history, cancer history, smoking status, the mortality status, and participants younger than 20 years of age. Finally, a total of 3803 individuals were included in this study. The sample selection flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Assessment of H. pylori infection

Wampole Laboratories’ Helicobacter pylori IgG Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) kit was used to detect the concentration of IgG antibodies to Helicobacter pylori. This method provides greater sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility than other serological tests, such as immunofluorescence, complement fixation, hem agglutination, and radioimmunoassay. Serum lgG antibody values less than 0.9 were considered negative, while lgG values greater than or equal to 1.1 were considered positive. As described in the previous studies, serum lgG antibody values (0.9–1.1), which are considered controversial, were excluded from the analysis in this study to ensure statistical accuracy [15].

Covariables

In our study, we extracted covariables that may have a potential impact on the relationship between dietary fiber and H. pylori, including total energy, age, gender, ethnicity, educational attainment, alcohol intake, cancer, diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, stroke and BMI. Data were collected through standardized participant questionnaires in a home interview combined with physical examination information and laboratory findings for medical assessment of demographic information, lifestyle, dietary habits on the potential influencing factors of H. pylori.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R (version 4.2) or Empowerstats (version 4.1). In this study, we followed the NHANES survey’s design and used circular weights, stratification, and clustering for analysis. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (± SD) and compared using t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentage and compared by chi-square test. The confidence interval for the relationship between dietary fiber intake and seropositive for H. pylori was 95%. Participants were categorized into two groups based on H. pylori IgG antibody test results (negative or positive) or into four groups based on dietary fiber intake quartiles. Differences in baseline variables were tested using Student t-tests and chi-square tests. Logistic regression modeling was used to explore the relationship between dietary fiber and H. pylori infection. Model 0: the model was not adjusted for any covariates; Model 1: adjusted for age, gender, and race; Model 2: adjusted for age, gender, race, education level, alcohol intake, cancer, diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, stroke and BMI; in addition, we applied restricted cubic splines (RCS) to adjust for potential confounding factors, calculated smooth curve-fitting nonlinear p-values were used to explore potential nonlinear associations between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in H. pylori positive and negative patients. The event-free survival rates between groups were calculated by use of the Kaplan-Meier method to further estimate association between dietary fiber and all-cause mortality and were compared by the log-rank test. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristic

Baseline characteristics of seropositive and seronegative for H. pylori individuals are summarized in Table 1. The results showed that a total of 1684 participants (44.28%) out of 3803 participants were seropositive for H. pylori with a mean age (SD) of 52.95 ± 17.83 years. Of these, 51.25% were women and 48.75% were men, with no statistical difference in gender. Compared to H. pylori negative individuals, H. pylori seropositive individuals showed significant differences in age, body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, education, economic status, drinking status, smoking. Compared to H. pylori negative population, H. pylori seropositive individuals exhibited higher age(P < 0.001), higher BMI(P = 0.019), lower educational level(P < 0.001), smoking(P = 0.036), non-alcohol consumption(P = 0.004), lower level of fiber intake(P < 0.001), and higher rates of diabetes(P < 0.001), hypertension(P < 0.001), heart failure(P < 0.001), and stroke(P = 0.021). We further analyzed the relationship between fiber intake and population characteristics. We quantified their dietary fiber intake into 4 groups (Q1 (Fiber ≤ 8.54), Q2 (8.54 < Fiber ≤ 13.44), Q3 (13.44 < Fiber ≤ 20.16), and Q4 (Fiber > 20.16). Participants in the highest fiber intake quartile exhibited a higher proportion of male population, higher education, and higher income. In terms of lifestyle, participants in the high fiber quartile exhibited a lower frequency of smoking and a higher rate of alcohol consumption. In addition, H. pylori seropositivity was also lower in individuals with higher fiber intake, while was not associated with the prevalence of diabetes, hypertension, heart failure, and stroke (Table 2). Associations between fiber intake and seropositive for H. pylori

To further examine the correlation between dietary fiber intake and the risk of seropositive for H. pylori, we performed multivariate logistic regression analyses (Table 3). When the level of dietary fiber intake was examined as a continuous variable, in Model 0 group, the corrected ratio of ratios (OR) for seropositive for H. pylori was 1.00 (95% CI: 0.98,1.01) for an SD increase in fiber intake. When dietary fiber intake was assessed as quartiles in Model 0, participants with higher dietary fiber intake (Q2, Q3, Q4) in the quartiles showed a significantly lower risk of seropositive for H. pylori compared with the lower quartile (Q1). This association persisted outside of adjusting for age, gender, and race factors in Model 1. As shown in Model 2, similar results were also observed after adjusting for age, gender, race, education, alcohol drink, cancer, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, stroke and BMI. Correlation between dietary fiber and all‑cause mortality

To assess the relationship between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in the US population, we plotted the RCS curves. RCS analysis showed that dietary fiber intake, as a continuous variable, was negatively correlated with the risk of all-cause mortality in overall populations (p < 0.0001). Additionally, we also observed a significant negative correlation between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in H. pylori-positive and H. pylori-negative patients, as well as all the patients included, while a non-linear correlation was only observed only in H. pylori-positive patients (Fig. 2A and C). We further used Kaplan-Meier survival curves to clarify the relationship between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in the US population and showed that lower dietary fiber intake was associated with higher all-cause mortality in H. pylori-positive individuals, while there was no significant correlation between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in H. pylori-negative participants (Fig. 3A and C). As a continuous variable, in Model 0, for one SD increase in dietary fiber intake, the hazard ratio HR for mortality was 0.99 (95% CI 0.99,1.00), and this significant association remained in Model 1 and Model 2. However, no statistically significant difference was found in Model 0, when considering the effect of an increase of dietary fiber intake on the hazard ratio HR for mortality in the H. pylori-negative population. In adjusted Model 2, for an SD increase in dietary fiber, an adjusted hazard ratio for mortality was 0.99 (95% CI 0.98,1.00). As a categorical variable, Q3 and Q4 showed a lower risk of mortality in the H. pylori positively infected population compared to Q1 in Model 0, with hazard ratios of 0.77 (95% CI 0.62, 0.96) and 0.78 (95% CI 0.63, 0.97), respectively. This correlation persisted in both the adjusted Model 1 and Model 2 groups. However, in H. pylori-negative populations, increasing dietary fiber intake failed to reduce all-cause mortality in the participating populations(Tables 4, 5, 6). Then we run a sensitivity analysis excluding deaths in the first two years. The results were consisting with original data(Tables S1 and S2). These findings suggest that high dietary fiber intake decreased all-cause mortality in H. pylori-positive patients after adjusting for confounding factors, which could support the role of dietary fiber intake in preventing the progression of H. pylori infection. Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between dietary fiber intake and seropositive for H. pylori using data from NHANES 1999–2000. In addition, we explored the association between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in patients infected or uninfected with H. pylori. Our findings suggest that subjects with lower levels of dietary fiber intake have a higher risk of seropositive for H. pylori after adjusting for age, gender, race, education, alcohol drink, cancer, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, stroke and BMI. However, in exploring the relationship between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality, we found that lower dietary fiber intake was associated with higher all-cause mortality in H. pylori-positive individuals, while there was no significant correlation between dietary fiber intake and all-cause mortality in H. pylori-negative participants.

During the recent years, a large number of studies have suggested an intrinsic association between dietary patterns and the risk of H. pylori infection [5, 16]. A frequent dietary intake of fresh fruits and vegetables has been found to be associated with the prevention of H. pylori infection [17]. In addition, greater intake of whole grains, roots and tubers, vegetables, mushrooms, various legumes, vegetable oils, nuts and seeds were also associated with a lower risk of H. pylori infection [18]. The correlation between dietary patterns and H. pylori infection may be related to differences in the content of specific compound components in foods, such as vitamin C, polyphenols and flavonoids [19]. Dietary fiber, as an important food component, was acknowledged to play a key role in maintaining gastrointestinal homeostasis [20]. Meanwhile, dietary fiber was found to not only prevent constipation and alleviate inflammatory bowel disease [21], but also control body weight [22], reduce the incidence of other extra-gastric diseases, including chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [23], non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [24].

Studies found that dietary fiber regulates intestinal microbiota balance by promoting probiotic growth and modifying harmful bacterial counts, with soluble fiber enhancing probiotic proliferation and fermentation to produce short-chain fatty acids that support intestinal integrity [25–29]. Though Helicobacter pylori primarily colonize the gastric epithelium, intestinal microbiota imbalance can impair gastric immune responses and mucosal barrier function, favoring H. pylori colonization. Studies note that intestinal dysbiosis disrupts immune function, weakening defenses against H. pylori and facilitating gastric colonization and disease. Additionally, altered intestinal microbial metabolites indirectly affect gastric immunity and microenvironment, promoting H. pylori growth [30]. H. pylori survive in the acidic environment of the stomach. The cellulose in dietary fiber could increase the viscosity of food and could directly neutralize gastric acid, while crude fiber could accelerate gastric emptying and reduce gastric acid secretion, lowering the acid concentration in the stomach [31]. Therefore, the inhibitory effect of dietary fiber on H. pylori might be related to its regulatory effect on gastric acid. Our findings align with mechanisms by which dietary fiber may influence Helicobacter pylori dynamics. These pathways support the observed negative association between fiber intake and H. pylori infection in our study.

A recent case-control study in Sabah confirmed this association and showed that high-fiber pattern diet significantly reduced the risk of H. pylori infection [32]. Our findings were consistent with the previous studies that showed a negative association between fiber intake and H. pylori infection. To the best of our knowledge, our study was the first to explore the relationship between dietary fiber intake and seropositive for H. pylori at the large sample population level, providing strong support for further understanding of the intrinsic link between dietary patterns and gastrointestinal health. In addition, dietary fiber has also been found to be closely related to the occurrence of cardiovascular diseases [33]. An increase in the daily intake of dietary fiber by 7 g could reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease by 9% [34]. In the present study, we conducted Kaplan-Meier survival curves and found that lower dietary fiber intake was associated with higher all-cause mortality only in H. pylori-positive infected individuals, which indicated that the modulating effect of dietary fiber on the regression of the above diseases may be related to its modulation of H. pylori, which was independent of other pathogenic factors.

Although the specific mechanism underlying the intrinsic relationship between dietary fiber and Helicobacter pylori infection has not been fully elucidated, the high consistency between results of this study and previous evidence suggested the biological validity of high dietary fiber intake in reducing the incidence of seropositive for H. pylori and the corresponding disease mortality. This evidence suggested the necessity of increasing attention to dietary fiber intake in future researches.

Our study has several strengths and limitations as described below. This was the first study to assess the association between dietary fiber and seropositive for H. pylori and all-cause mortality based on a large sample population. We showed more reliable results after adjusting for confounders such as age, gender, race, education, alcohol drink, cancer, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, heart failure, stroke and BMI. However, as with other observational and cross-sectional study designs, our study has several limitations. Firstly, as a cross-sectional analysis, it is difficult to obtain a definitive causal relationship between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection, and therefore further prospective studies are warranted. Secondly, although we have adjusted for several confounders, we still cannot completely exclude the influence of other confounders on the study results. For instance, the other methodology to diagnose H. pylori and the data of antibiotic use was unavailable. Thirdly, we used data from the 1999–2000 NHANES, which may differ from the current population’s dietary patterns. Our study may not be able to distinguish whether the observed correlation between dietary fiber intake and H. pylori infection is due to current infection or past infection. Results of this study might not be extremely applicable to populations outside of the US. By comparing data with those published researches in the recent years, we could still draw conclusions of instructive value.

Conclusion

Based on data from NHANES (1999–2000), lower dietary fiber intake was associated with a higher risk of H. pylori infection. Dietary fiber intake levels were significantly associated with the risk of all-cause mortality in H. pylori positively infected individuals. These results emphasize the predictive value of dietary fiber intake for the prognosis of seropositive for H. pylori and related diseases, suggesting that effective management of the dietary patter in H. pylori-infected individuals is important for decreasing morbidity and mortality rates of infected populations, but the actual mechanism is still unknown.
