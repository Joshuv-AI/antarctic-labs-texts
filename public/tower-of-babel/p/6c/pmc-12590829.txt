Exploring self-perceived preparedness for practice among pre and post Covid-19 graduates of Nigerian dental schools
Authors: Fadekemi Olufunmilayo Oginni, Laura E Hirshfield, Alan Schwartz, Rachel Yudkowsky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExamination results do not necessarily correlate with competence or preparedness for clinical practice. The overall preparedness of Nigerian Dental graduates for practice has never been evaluated; it is important to do so on a regular basis to allow for continuous improvement. We sought to elicit the perceptions of Nigerian dental students and interns regarding the roles of interns and their preparedness for intern tasks using the modified American Dental Education Association (ADEA) Graduation Questionnaire (GQ).MethodsA survey of dental students at time of graduation, and interns (pre- and post-Covid-19) six months into internship, based on a modified ADEA GQ, explored respondents’ understanding of the roles of interns, self-reported preparedness for internship at graduation, and self-assessed competence as interns. Data was analyzed using descriptive statistics. Chi-Square, and student’s t-test were employed to compare students’ and interns’ responses.ResultsRespondents were 49 graduating students and 167 interns (113 pre-Covid-19 and 54 post-Covid-19). Graduating students overestimated the responsibilities of interns. However, they considered themselves significantly more prepared for specific tasks at graduation than interns looking back to when they graduated. Gaps in perceived preparedness included both basic clinical dental skills (e.g., management of tooth wear, and post extraction complications), and some general medical skills (e.g., management of common emergencies). Narrative comments suggested a need for additional skills training, active and supervised participation in clinical care, and pre-internship transitional programs. Although preparedness was not different between pre/post Covid-19 groups, Covid-19 may have enhanced trainees’ opportunities during internship.ConclusionGraduating students overestimated interns’ expected clinical roles and expressed better preparedness prior to internship than did interns based on their actual experience. At the point of graduation, respondents were neither fully aware of nor fully prepared for their roles as interns. The pre and post-Covid interns did not differ significantly from each other, however post-Covid-19 interns endorsed undertaking more tasks (including validity check items) than their pre-Covid-19 counterparts. Repeated National GQ surveys can provide institutions and the nation with critical country-specific feedback needed to facilitate advancement of dental education. The ADEA GQ can be modified easily and successfully for international settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08169-5.Keywords: Self-perceived, Preparedness, Graduating students, Interns, Pre-Covid-19, Post-Covid-19, Dental

Background

Training programs for dentists are expected to equip them adequately for their immediate expected roles as interns. At present, there is no process for obtaining systematic and ongoing feedback on the effectiveness of dental training in Nigeria. Outside Nigeria, studies have shown varying levels of unpreparedness expressed by graduates in practice [1–3], their employers [4–6] or both [7] over time. Specifically, inadequate preparedness has been reported for specialized care in dentistry [8], less frequently encountered clinical scenarios in dentistry [9, 10], and aspects of dental practice considered to be complex or characterized by difficult skill transfer [11–13]. In contrast, adequate preparedness was reported for most commonly encountered clinical conditions [9–11, 13]; with a few exceptions [14]. Further, no single examination assesses fully how well prepared a student is for undertaking the dentist’s job [2]. Indeed while the tendency is to equate passing exams with preparedness and competence, examination results do not necessarily correlate with competence or preparedness for practice [15, 16].

Literature on educational development is replete with the importance of feedback for monitoring any curricular program [10]. An important yardstick employed in measuring the success of an educational program is graduates’ preparedness, their ability to meet the demands of practice and expectations of their employers [17]. Such feedback may emanate from trainees’ self-assessment [1–3, 18], patients [19], supervisors, and clinical teams (in isolation or combination) [4–6, 19–21].

Nigeria is the seventh most populous country in the world and Africa’s most populous nation with over 220 million people [22] and about 4,000 dentists. Training of medical and dental practitioner in Nigeria has its unique features (Appendix F).

With a large, underserved Nigerian population, national efforts geared towards training more dentists are in place [23]. Inadequate internship training positions identified in 2010 [24] informed an extension of internship training locations to private hospitals, general hospitals and similar facilities. In 2020, Covid-19 impacted dental education globally with the introduction of remote classes, limited access to patients, restrictions in clinical procedures, and uncertainties [25, 26]. A survey of Australian medical students revealed a significant decline in overall self-perceived preparedness among participants in 2020 (mean preparedness, 3.55± 0.88) compared with pre-Covid graduates from the same medical school in 2019 (mean preparedness, 4.28 ± 0.64, P < 0.001) and the national average (mean preparedness, 3.81 ± 0.93, P = 0.04) [27].

To the best of our knowledge, the overall preparedness of Nigerian Dental graduates for practice has never been evaluated.

The ADEA GQ [28] is a national questionnaire, administered to graduating dental students in the United States to help gain an understanding of the strengths and weaknesses of individual training programs from the graduate’s perspective. We adapted the ADEA GQ for the Nigerian setting. Based on Muthaura’s findings [7, 13] in the context of Kenyan medical school graduates, we hypothesized that Nigerian GSs’ expectations of their roles as interns would differ from the interns’ experience, with respondents expressing a high level of preparedness at the point of graduation but lower levels of preparedness by the sixth month of internship experience. We also expected post-Covid-19 interns to express lower level of preparedness than pre-Covid-19 interns due to their more limited access to patients and procedures. We expect our findings to constitute baseline values for subsequent routine national surveys that will reveal the current situation of preparedness and inform curriculum review processes throughout the nation.

We sought to answer the research questions, “Do graduating students (GS) and graduates of Nigerian dental schools (Interns) (Pre-and Post-Covid-19) feel prepared for their roles as interns? Do Nigerian dental GS and interns differ in their understanding of the intern’s role and their self-perceived preparedness? Does the self-perceived preparedness of immediate post-Covid dental interns differ from that of pre-Covid interns?”

Methods

This was a three-stage survey study conducted over a period of 5 years (2018–2023). We explored respondents’ understanding of the roles of an intern, interns’ actual experiences of their roles, self-perceived preparedness for the roles of intern, anticipated challenges, actual challenges encountered during internship, and relationships between training and internship experiences. GS and interns were studied because they are the accessible custodians of the information required.

Modifications (with permission) to the ADEA GQ were proposed by author FO, who is deeply embedded in Nigerian dental training, in order to adapt the GQ to Nigerian context and culture Supplemental Digital Content (SDC): Appendix A). The modified instrument was piloted with seven junior residents, eight local faculty and ten outgoing interns; and revised based on their feedback. Final modifications included (1) the exclusion of eleven GQ items that were neither helpful in answering the research question, nor relevant to the Nigerian cultural setting; (2) the addition of Nigerian-specific questions addressing the objectives of training Nigerian dentists [29] and the Nigerian culture; and (3) the addition of nine validity-check items (tasks Nigerian interns are not trained or expected to do). Final items were endorsed by the eight local faculty involved in the pilot. The final survey can be seen in Appendix B. Items were rated on a six-point scale (1 = Strongly disagree to 6 = Strongly agree). Pilot data was not included in the final dataset.

Stage 1: Graduating students

The wording of the final survey instrument was slightly modified for graduating dental students (SDC: Appendix C) and was administered on paper to consenting final-year students from three dental schools (out of nine in the nation) - Lagos State University Dental School (South West Urban location); University of Benin Dental School (South South- Urban location) and Obafemi Awolowo University Dental School (South West, Semi Urban location). These schools were selected based on the feasibility of their calendar for our research schedule – i.e. their academic session ended (students were graduating) at the time the study was scheduled to commence. Students were recruited over a period of two months with the help of individual Deans who facilitated information dissemination and students’ participation but were not involved with the conduct of the survey. Questionnaires were administered in classroom settings at the conclusion of their last clerkship, just before final exams in the rain semester of 2017/2018 academic year. The frequency of direct observation of student-patient interaction by supervising Registrars, and Consultants was requested from GS only, because of decreased likelihood of accurate recall by interns over 6 months post graduation .

Stages 2 and 3 (Interns) were conducted over a period of 6 months each. Pre-Covid and Post-Covid participants were recruited through the list of dental graduates published by the MDCN in the respective years. The intern survey instrument (Appendix B) was created on Qualtrics on Qualtrics [30]. A link to the survey was sent to all eligible interns (graduates of all Nigerian dental schools) by means of email and WhatsApp [31].

Eligible Stage 2 interns were Pre-Covid-19 interns: 2017/2018 graduates of all Nigerian dental schools [September to December 2018] with at least six months internship experience.

Eligible Stage 3 interns were Post-Covid-19 interns: 2022/2023 graduates of all Nigerian dental schools [October 2022 to January 2023] with at least six months internship experience.

Reminders were sent at two weeks and four weeks. All data were collected anonymously. Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 [32]. Descriptive analyses and Chi-square test, Student’s t-test and ANOVA were employed to describe and compare parametric socio-demographic data. Statistical significance was inferred at p-value ≤ 0.05, with a Bonferroni adjustment for post-hoc comparisons. We did not adjust for multiple comparisons across items, since our analyses were exploratory. Open-text responses were analyzed and categorized into themes. Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Review Board at the Obafemi Awolowo University Teaching Hospital Nigeria and University of Illinois at Chicago.

Results

All forty-nine GS in the three selected institutions participated in the first stage of the survey; intern response rates in stage 2 and 3 were 73% (113/155 interns) and 55% (54/98 interns) respectively. (Fig. 1)

The 216 respondents were aged 22–38 years with mean (SD) at 26.9(2.87). We observed inconsistent gender preponderance across groups and this did not attain statistical significance overall (M: F 115:101) (p = 0.08). There was no significant difference in training duration for pre- and post-Covid-19 interns, 7.0(1.05) and 7.04(0.08) years respectively (Table 1). 78.4% of pre-Covid-19 interns and 81.5% of post-Covid-19 interns had rotated through two of the four mandatory postings; the rest had rotated through more than two postings.

Table 3 shows GS’ expectations of their clinical roles as interns, and interns’ reports of their actual roles, grouped into six categories of clinical practice and a seventh category comprising validity-check items. GS’ role expectations did not differ significantly from interns’ actual experience in oral surgery, paediatric dentistry and orthodontics. Statistically significant disparities between GS expectations and intern experiences were found for individual items in general dental procedures, restorative dentistry, prosthetic dentistry and oral medicine/periodontology, indicating that GS generally expected to perform the listed procedures, while actual intern experience was variable. Post-Covid-19 interns tended to be more likely to report that they were expected to perform individual tasks than were pre-Covid-19 interns. The validity-check tasks, which faculty thought were not expected of interns, were least often endorsed; however, a substantial number of interns endorsed individual tasks (e.g., 85% of post-Covid-19 interns endorsed doing complex endodontic treatment), with high variability across tasks and groups.

Table 3 also shows respondents’ self-perceived preparedness for each task, rated on a four-point scale (1 = Not at all prepared to 4 = very prepared). Apart from patient preparation for surgery under general anaesthesia and six validity-check items, GS consistently expressed higher levels of perceived preparedness than the interns. The differences attained statistical significance with all general dental procedures, most (5/7) of restorative dentistry procedures, and 3/5 paediatric dentistry/orthodontic procedures. Low level preparedness (score < 3) were recorded by interns in several expected tasks: composing referral letters, recognizing and treating emergencies, tooth whitening, management of tooth wear, hypersensitivity, complications of dental extractions, mixed dentition, traumatized teeth, malocclusion with removable appliances and oral lesions and preparing patients for procedure under general anaesthesia.

Interns and GS alike perceived that lectures in dental sciences, clinical sciences, outpatient clinics, basic medical sciences, and ward rounds were most helpful in preparing them for their roles, while adult/pediatric emergency calls, and electives were rated least helpful (Appendix D).

Students reported being directly observed from 3 to 30 times per posting in oral diagnosis, 0–25 times in pedodontics, and 0–10 times each in others. (Appendix E).

Anticipated challenges

51.0% of GS anticipated challenges during internship, while 69% and 100% of pre and post-Covid-19 interns respectively reported actual challenges encountered in their role of interns. Content analysis for themes found that challenges common to all groups were related to increased responsibility, handling emergencies, and working independently with limited basic skills. Challenges that were not anticipated by GSs include challenges with accommodation, lack of confidence, heavy workload, multitasking, bullying by senior colleagues, language barrier, and securing placement for internship. Overall, the respondents felt that their training institutions helped them to develop as a dentist (90.4%) more than as a person (78.4%), with no significant difference between students and interns (p = 0.29).

Respondents expressed desire for more clinical skills training (especially basic emergency procedures), electives, conducive learning/working environment, improved course structure (to include entrepreneurship and leadership), and better trainer-trainee relationships across all groups. Interns particularly requested an orientation program, while GS requested elimination of afternoon lectures.

Discussion

A dearth of information on healthcare professionals’ preparedness for practice at the point of graduation deprives the education system of valuable information on training outcomes and evidence towards curriculum improvement. The interaction between provision of educational services and supply of an educated, up-to-date workforce that meets the demand for professionals in the health system labor market [33] makes this information most critical.

For the first time in the history of Nigeria’s dental education, we sought to understand the perceived preparedness of graduating dental students and interns for their roles post-graduation, by administering a culturally appropriate modified version of the ADEA GQ. We found that the modified GQ was able to identify perceived areas of relative strength and weakness, which could serve as valuable program feedback to dental schools and internships. GS tended to overestimate interns’ expected clinical roles, and felt better prepared prior to internship than did interns based on their actual experience. Both GS and interns expressed the need for more clinical hands-on experience, and felt less prepared to lead a team and manage a dental business; while interns felt less prepared for decision-making and applying evidence-based information to dental practice.

Understanding their roles

Students’ anticipation of the tasks expected of an intern was generally in line with interns’ experience, however interns’ experience was highly variable. The narrative responses confirmed the absence of formal sessions dedicated to introducing undergraduates to the expected roles of interns. Communicating these roles clearly at the onset of their clinical years would enhance learners’ goal setting and priorities. The differences in roles experienced by pre and post-Covid-19 interns may be attributed partly to changes in the health sector around Covid-19 pandemic. The post-Covid-19 interns’ endorsement of more tasks/activities in the general dental list than the pre-Covid-19 interns could be a result of busier supervisors handling more complex cases and delegating more activities to interns. In restorative dentistry and most of Oral surgery, paediatric dentistry, orthodontic, periodontology and oral medicine procedures post-Covid-19 interns were more involved in all activities except tooth whitening. This is at variance with the negative perceived impact of Covid-19 in an Australian population [27]. The need for clear/standardized presentation and appropriate communication of interns’ expected roles to trainees early is apparent.

Preparedness for tasks and roles

Interns’ self-perceived preparedness, retrospective to the beginning of internship, was generally lower than the perceived preparedness of GS just prior to beginning internship. This reflects the impact of interns’ contact with the reality of internship experience, and GS’ lack of insight into their own skills. This underscores the gap between perceptions and reality as reported by Sarhan and Alwadi [34]. Our respondents felt least prepared to manage a business and lead a team. Accordingly, their narratives requested inclusion of entrepreneur and leadership courses in the training curriculum to bridge this gap. One objective of Medical Education in Nigeria is “To produce practitioners with sufficient managerial ability to play a leadership role in health care delivery” [22]. Training programs are therefore expected to accommodate “Basic Principle of Business management, Planning, and Administration at the junior and intermediate clinical years” [22]. Our findings suggest the need for inclusion or enhancement of this aspect of training nationally. Respondents also felt unprepared for understanding the social sciences of medicine, the application of evidence-based information to practice, and basics of clinical decision-making. The relevance of these in dental education is well established, documented, and compelling [34–36]. Like in our subjects, knowledge, attitude and perception of evidence-based practice also was rated low among Saudi medical students and attributed mostly to difficulty with comprehension, lack of journal subscription, and lack of time [37]. These gaps also demand due attention in the dental education curriculum nationally. Recommendations to improve dental interns’ preparedness include implementing a curriculum focused on competence with intensive clinical skills development training activities in small learning groups; increasing clinic hours with sufficient patient volume and hands-on, active call duty roles-management of emergencies in clinical years; and electives devoted to skills training. Dental students and interns indicated they felt under-prepared to undertake a variety of specific dental procedures, and tasks. Like Turkish medical students [38], their narrative comments expressed desire for more clinical/hands-on experiences, under a conducive learning environment, and with supportive equipment and facilities – i.e. training experiences that correspond to expected clinical practice. Our findings are consistent with Ali et al in Pakistan who found low preparedness in ability to undertake intraoral radiographs, treatment planning, crowns, multirooted endodontics, research skills, and referral for suspected oral cancer [39]. Validity-check items (tasks for which students were not trained and that faculty did not expect interns to perform) had low rates of endorsement, supporting respondents’ attentiveness to the survey task. However, an appreciable number of interns (particularly post-Covid-19) were in fact expected to carry out some of these tasks. For example, about 50% of interns performed extraction of impacted teeth, suggesting the need for curriculum update (inclusion). A closer look at the responses of post-Covid-19 interns suggests an emerging trend wherein interns are allowed to engage in more procedures now. Over half of respondents anticipated or experienced challenges settling into the role of an intern. Difficulty adjusting into their new roles, handling clinical emergencies and basic procedures, lack of confidence, fear of making mistakes, and inadequate skills suggest the need for a re-structured undergraduate training program that prioritizes clinical skills acquisition [38]. Similarly, Leelavathi et al. reported low level of awareness and preparedness for handling medical emergencies among dental interns in India [40]. Better preparedness could be accomplished through simulated and real-life experiences, with quality time dedicated to individual practice and confidence-building. Provision of a pre-internship experience or internship transition courses that allows for “independent practice” are well known and practiced in many countries of the world [41–43] Trainees’ active contributions to decision-making processes, and observance of outcome of care can help build their confidence and skills. Reporting emergency call experiences as least helpful in their training may be because interns’ deficiencies are often revealed in the emergency units [44]. Structured and coordinated participation in emergency room activities, however, could be an effective preparatory experience and therefore should be encouraged. Interns’ host institutions should systematically orient them to their new work environment; as orientation to a new work environment may not happen on the job seamlessly [13].

Roles of different aspects of training

Respondents rated operating room experiences, community rural postings, and electives as least helpful towards preparing them for internship. Focus group discussions or interviews of students, interns and trainers and an audit of the experiences may help elucidate the reasons. Faculty development geared towards active engagement of learners within these settings may help. GS reported clinical science lectures and ward rounds as being more helpful in preparing them for internship while interns considered basic sciences lectures more helpful. The differences expressed may be attributed to GS’ lack of contact with the reality of interns’ frequent need to recall basic sciences when managing clinical conditions.

Direct observation of learners is a necessary precursor to accurate assessment and formative feedback. The highly variable extent of direct observation reported may have contributed to the lack of preparedness and confidence among respondents. The frequency of direct observation in oral diagnosis is possibly due to receipt of all new cases, clerked by students and presented for revision and endorsement by trainers. The lower rate of direct observation in other units is a clear indication of need for more faculty development aimed at encouraging consistent direct observation and feedback.

Pre- and Post-Covid-19 interns

Training and practice during Covid-19 in Nigeria, as elsewhere, came with restrictions and fears [25, 26]. One might expect that post-Covid-19 interns would express lower level of preparedness than the pre-Covid-19 but there were no significant differences between the two groups. The Covid-19 pandemic resulted in compulsory holiday, and eventual resumption of classes with a revised and backdated timetable to compensate for loss of touch with clinics. Perhaps the relatively longer stay of the post-Covid-19 interns in training (despite the restrictions) afforded them the opportunity to feel well-prepared for internship. The post-Covid-19 interns reported doing more than their pre-Covid-19 counterparts, including previously uncommon (validity-check) tasks. A positive side of Covid-19 pandemic may be overworked trainers’ [26] increased willingness to entrust interns while trainers concentrated on major tasks.

Benefits of a National graduation Questionnaire(GQ)

The rich information gathered by this study suggests that a National GQ could help detect lack of preparedness early and inform institutions and faculty of required curricular reviews. Repeating the GQ regularly could reveal the strengths and weaknesses of training programs; effects of prior curricular interventions; and areas requiring further attention locally and nationally in real-time. Our respondents’ narrative feedback and enthusiasm despite the lengthy survey supports instituting a similar survey at various stages of dental education and internship. Our experience shows that leveraging popular or emerging communication trends (e.g. WhatsApp as opposed to Email) in contacting respondents could facilitate maximum participation. Given the variations in schools’ calendar, the support of the respective Provosts of colleges and Deans of faculty would enhance a successful implementation of a national GQ. Despite a marked dearth of African literature assessing dental graduates’ preparedness for practice [45, 46], this study demonstrates that a GQ-type survey can provide important and credible information towards evaluating dental education curricular outcomes periodically. In particular, the ADEA GQ can be modified, easily, for international country-specific contexts.

Limitations of this study and opportunities for further research

A survey of the entire GS and intern population was desirable, but not logistically feasible. Our survey results are based on self-report and self-perceived preparedness, which may not correspond to actual competence [4]. Supervisors’ evaluations could provide a triangulated view of our subjects. The absence of baseline data offers no basis for comparing our data within the Nigerian context. The information provided by this data holds true for the 2018 and 2022/2023 graduates, thus underscores the need for frequent and consistent GQ administration, in obtaining up to date information. Further research may include surveys of supervisors/trainers, objective assessment of interns’ preparedness; observational studies of interns’ performance at workplace; and surveys/interviews of students and interns to explore determinants of helpful, and unhelpful educational experiences.

Conclusion

The internship experience, designed to immerse trainees into practice under the supervision, of experienced clinicians after undergraduate training, is a launch pad into life-time practice. Feedback from students and interns can serve as an important source of data towards appraisal of the impact of teaching and curriculum review. In this study, graduating students overestimated interns’ expected clinical roles and expressed better preparedness prior to internship than did interns based on their actual experience. At the point of graduation, students were neither fully aware of, nor fully prepared for their roles as interns. The pre and post-Covid interns did not differ significantly from each other, however post-Covid-19 interns endorsed undertaking more tasks (including validity check items) than their pre-Covid-19 counterparts. Regular GQ surveys can provide faculty, institutions, and the nation with country-specific curricular feedback and facilitate the advancement of dental education. Furthermore, the adapted instrument proved effective for international use; and was able to reflect changes in environmental condition/curriculum.
