Comparative efficacy and safety of first-line immunotherapy- and targeted therapy-based regimens for advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Wanting Wang, Xiaoning Zhang, Xiaohu Sun, Lu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe optimal first-line immunotherapy- or targeted therapy-based regimen for advanced gastric cancer (GC) and gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) remains uncertain, largely because direct head-to-head comparisons are limited. We therefore conducted a network meta-analysis to compare the efficacy and safety of currently available first-line treatment strategies.MethodsPubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were systematically searched from inception to January 10, 2026, for randomized controlled trials evaluating first-line immunotherapy- or targeted therapy-based regimens for advanced GC or GEJC. Overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) were analyzed using hazard ratios (HRs), and dichotomous outcomes were analyzed using odds ratios (ORs), both with 95% credible intervals (CrIs). Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool. Bayesian network meta-analysis performs by R4.3.1.ResultsNineteen randomized controlled trials involving 10,808 patients were included. No statistically significant differences were observed among treatment regimens for OS or PFS. Several immunotherapy- and targeted therapy-based regimens demonstrated improvements in short-term tumor response outcomes, including ORR and DCR; however, these improvements were not consistently associated with survival benefits. SUCRA-based ranking results suggested variability in probabilistic treatment ordering but should be interpreted as exploratory rather than definitive evidence of clinical superiority. Substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was observed across trials, including differences in biomarker status (PD-L1, HER2, CLDN18.2, FGFR2b), geographic regions, chemotherapy backbones, and subsequent treatment strategies. In safety analyses, EGFR-targeted agents showed relatively favorable profiles in selected outcomes.ConclusionsAlthough certain regimens were associated with improved short-term tumor responses, there is insufficient evidence to support definitive superiority in terms of long-term survival outcomes. Given biomarker heterogeneity, clinical variability, and limitations in network structure, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Further biomarker-stratified and head-to-head randomized trials are warranted to better define optimal treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16434-z.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks among the most prevalent malignant tumors of the digestive system globally, with its incidence and mortality rates consistently placing it among the leading causes of cancer-related mortality [1, 2]. Epidemiological data indicates that GC exhibits particularly high prevalence in East Asia, imposing a significant burden on public health systems [3]. The incidence of gastroesophageal junction cancer (GEJC) has shown an upward trend in recent years. Its biological characteristics and treatment strategies highly overlap with those of GC, leading to their management as a continuum in clinical practice [4]. Due to the lack of specific early symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at locally advanced or metastatic stages, losing the opportunity for curative surgery [5]. Systemic therapy thus becomes the primary treatment approach. However, the efficacy of traditional dual-agent chemotherapy (CHE) regimens based on fluoropyrimidine plus platinum remains limited, with median overall survival typically less than one year, resulting in poor overall prognosis for advanced patients [6]. Consequently, exploring more effective and safer first-line treatment strategies remains a key research priority. With advances in tumor molecular biology research, advanced GC is increasingly recognized as a highly heterogeneous disease [7]. Genomic studies reveal that GC can be classified into distinct molecular subtypes based on characteristics such as HER2 overexpression, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), EB virus-associated, and chromosomal instability [8]. These molecular subtyping approaches provide the theoretical foundation for precision medicine. The successful application of anti-HER2 targeted therapy marks the advent of molecular targeted treatment for advanced GC [9]. For HER2-positive patients, trastuzumab combined with CHE significantly improves survival outcomes, establishing targeted therapy as the standard first-line regimen for this specific population [10]. Additionally, anti-angiogenic agents and other novel targeted drugs have demonstrated clinical value in various studies.

In recent years, the rapid advancement of immunotherapy has further transformed the treatment landscape for advanced GC and GEJC. Immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting programmed death receptor-1 (PD-1) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) have demonstrated survival benefits in multiple large-scale phase III randomized controlled trials [11]. Immunotherapy demonstrates particularly pronounced efficacy in PD-L1-positive or MSI-H populations. Based on these findings, immunotherapy combined with CHE has gradually become a key first-line treatment option for advanced GC [12, 13]. Concurrently, immunotherapy combined with targeted therapy or dual-immunotherapy strategies are actively being explored, reflecting a trend toward treatment diversification. However, despite expanding treatment options, there remains a lack of sufficient direct comparative evidence between different first-line immunotherapy and targeted therapy regimens [14]. Most randomized controlled trials have only compared immunotherapy with traditional CHE, lacking head-to-head studies between immunotherapy combinations or between immunotherapy and targeted therapy combinations [15]. This limitation in the evidence structure creates uncertainty for clinicians when making specific treatment decisions. Furthermore, variations across studies in enrollment criteria, PD-L1 detection methods, HER2 status proportions, geographic distribution, and subsequent treatment strategies complicate result interpretation. Relying solely on traditional pairwise meta-analyses proves insufficient for systematically comparing and ranking multiple treatment strategies.

Against this backdrop, this study aims to systematically retrieve and include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) related to first-line treatment for advanced GC and GEJC. By constructing a network meta-analysis model, we will compare and rank the efficacy and safety of different immunotherapy and targeted therapy regimens, clarify the relative advantages and risks of each strategy, and provide more comprehensive, evidence-based, and quantifiable reference for clinical treatment decisions. Concurrently, this study seeks to provide theoretical support for designing future head-to-head clinical trials, thereby advancing the optimization of precision treatment strategies for advanced GC.

Materials and methods

Study design and registration

This study is a Bayesian network meta-analysis based on randomized controlled trials (RCTs), reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines [16].

Literature search strategy

Systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases from their inception to January 10, 2026. Search keywords included “gastric cancer,” “gastroesophageal junction cancer,” “immunotherapy,” “immune checkpoint inhibitor,” “PD-1,” “PD-L1,” and “randomized controlled trial.” The search employed a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text keywords. Additionally, reference lists of relevant publications and abstracts from major international oncology conferences were manually reviewed to prevent omissions. Detailed search strategies are provided in Supplementary Material Table S1.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

Study Type: randomized controlled trials.

Study Population: Patients with histologically or cytologically confirmed advanced or metastatic gastric cancer or gastroesophageal junction cancer.

Intervention: First-line immunotherapy, targeted therapy, or combination regimens thereof.

Control: CHE or other immunotherapy/targeted therapy combination regimens.

Outcome Measures: Primary Outcomes: Overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), Objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), Secondary Outcomes: Serious adverse events (SAE), neutropenia.

Exclusion criteria

Non-randomized studies. Studies evaluating adjuvant or perioperative therapies. Duplicate publications or studies with incomplete data. Studies from which valid data cannot be extracted.

Data extractions

Two authors independently screened the literature for inclusion by importing the literature into endnote according to the literature inclusion and exclusion criteria, the final included studies were used for data extraction using excel software and if there was a dispute about the literature screening then it would be discussed, or a third person would be sought to adjudicate. The extracted data contained basic characteristics of the study (author, year of publication, Trial Registration Number), basic characteristics of the population (sample size, gender, age), Stage of tumor, intervention, and outcome.

Risk of bias

In the Meta-analysis of this study, we used the ROB 2.0 (Risk of Bias 2.0) tool [17] to assess the risk of bias of the included studies. Developed by the Cochrane Collaboration, the ROB 2.0 tool is a standardized tool for assessing the risk of bias in RCTs, which is aimed at systematically identifying and evaluating bias factors that may affect the validity of the results of the study. This improves the accuracy and reliability of Meta-analyses. The ROB 2.0 tool contains five key assessment domains: randomization process, intervention implementation, outcome measures, data reporting, and other sources of bias. Each domain is scored according to the transparency, reasonableness, and potential for bias in the study design and implementation, and is categorized as low risk, high risk, and uncertain risk. In conducting the assessment, two independent reviewers will score each domain based on the specifics of the study.

Statistical analysis

A Bayesian network meta-analysis [18] was conducted to compare the relative efficacy and safety of first-line immunotherapy- and targeted therapy-based regimens for advanced gastric cancer and gastroesophageal junction cancer. The analysis was performed within a hierarchical Bayesian framework, which allowed simultaneous synthesis of direct and indirect evidence across the treatment network.

For time-to-event outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS), hazard ratios (HRs) with corresponding 95% credible intervals (CrIs) were used as the effect measure. For dichotomous outcomes, including objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), serious adverse events (SAEs), and neutropenia, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CrIs were calculated. When available, effect estimates reported in the original trials were extracted directly. For binary outcomes, event numbers and total sample sizes were used to estimate treatment effects.

Considering the expected clinical heterogeneity across trials, including differences in CHE backbones, molecular eligibility criteria, geographic regions, and baseline patient characteristics, random-effects models were used as the primary analytical approach. A fixed-effect model was also examined where appropriate as a sensitivity assessment, and model fit was compared using the deviance information criterion (DIC). Lower DIC values were considered to indicate better model fit, although differences were interpreted cautiously when small.

Non-informative or weakly informative prior distributions were used for treatment effects. The between-study heterogeneity parameter was assigned a vague prior distribution to avoid imposing strong assumptions on the posterior estimates. Bayesian models were estimated using Markov chain Monte Carlo methods. Four independent chains were run with an initial burn-in phase, followed by additional iterations for posterior inference. Model convergence was assessed using trace plots and the potential scale reduction factor, with values close to 1.00 considered indicative of acceptable convergence.

The validity of indirect comparisons in a network meta-analysis relies on the assumptions of transitivity and consistency. Transitivity was assessed clinically by comparing key study and patient characteristics across treatment comparisons, including disease setting, line of therapy, CHE backbone, and eligibility criteria. Consistency between direct and indirect evidence was evaluated by comparing consistency and inconsistency models using DIC. Where closed loops were available, inconsistency was considered; however, formal local inconsistency testing was limited when loops were sparse or supported by few studies.

Treatment ranking probabilities were estimated using posterior ranking distributions. Ranking results were summarized using cumulative ranking probabilities. However, rankings were interpreted cautiously, especially when treatment nodes were informed by single studies, when credible intervals were wide, or when the network contained limited direct head-to-head evidence. Therefore, treatment rankings were considered supportive and exploratory rather than definitive evidence of clinical superiority. All analyses were conducted using R 4.3.1 software with the gemtc package.

Results

Literature screening and study characteristics

A systematic search yielded 4,263 records: 436 from PubMed, 383 from Embase, 2,008 from the Cochrane Library, and 1,436 from Web of Science. After removing 479 duplicate records, 3,763 full-text articles were excluded during the title and abstract screening process. After full-text review, two studies were excluded: one failed to report the predefined outcome measures of this study, and the other combined the intervention with other treatments, making it impossible to independently extract data on first-line therapy. Ultimately, 19 randomized controlled trials [19–37] were included. The literature screening process and exclusions at each stage are detailed in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Literature search flow chart

Literature search flow chart

This study ultimately included 19 randomized controlled trials, comprising a total of 10,808 patients. The studies were published between 2011 and 2025 and were conducted across multiple centers in Asia, Europe, and North America. The overall study population was predominantly male (approximately 70%), with a mean age clustered around 60 years. Most trials enrolled patients with an ECOG performance status of 0–1, while a small number of studies allowed inclusion of patients with ECOG 2, indicating generally comparable baseline characteristics across studies. The treatment regimens evaluated included nivolumab, pembrolizumab, tislelizumab, cadonilimab, sugemalimab, trastuzumab, pertuzumab, ramucirumab, bevacizumab, zolbetuximab, bemarituzumab, cetuximab, panitumumab, rilotumumab, nimotuzumab, and the combination of nivolumab plus relatlimab. Detailed baseline characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1. Biomarker information, including PD-L1 expression status, HER2 status, CLDN18.2 expression, FGFR2b status, and MSI status were extracted from each included trial based on original publications. These variables were recorded to characterize study populations and to facilitate interpretation of potential clinical heterogeneity across treatment comparisons (Supplementary Materials Table S2).

Risk of bias results

This study employed the Cochrane RoB 2 tool to assess risk of bias across 19 randomized controlled trials. Overall assessment results (Supplementary Materials Figures S1-S2) indicate most studies were judged to have a low risk of bias, with a smaller proportion rated as having some concerns, and only a few studies identified as high risk. Across the 23 included trials, 17 studies were rated as low risk of bias, 5 studies were classified as having some concerns, and 1 study was considered high risk. The main sources of bias were related to deviations from intended interventions and incomplete outcome data. Randomization processes and outcome measurement were generally of good quality across most studies. Overall, the included evidence was of moderate to high methodological quality.

Pairwise meta-analysis results

The results of pairwise meta-analyses (Table 2) indicate that in terms of efficacy, no statistically significant differences were observed between any immunotherapy or targeted therapy regimen and CHE alone, whether in ORR or DCR. Most comparisons were based on single studies, and the confidence intervals for pooled analysis results all spanned 1. Among these, pembrolizumab and ramucirumab showed a trend toward benefit but failed to reach statistical significance. Regarding safety, only nimotuzumab plus CHE demonstrated a significantly higher incidence of SAE compared to CHE alone (OR = 2.14, 95% CrI 1.27–3.69). The remaining regimens showed no significant differences in serious adverse events or incidence of neutropenia compared to CHE. Overall, direct comparative evidence did not demonstrate clear superiority of most treatment regimens in short-term efficacy outcomes, with only isolated regimens showing increased safety risks.

Discussion

This study indicate that no statistically significant differences were observed between different intervention strategies in terms of long-term survival outcomes, whether OS or PFS. In contrast, certain regimens demonstrated advantages over CHE in short-term efficacy metrics such as ORR and DCR. Regarding safety, differences existed among drugs, with some EGFR monoclonal antibodies exhibiting relatively lower risks of severe adverse events or neutropenia.

The lack of significant differences in OS and PFS should be interpreted cautiously within the current treatment landscape. The absence of statistically significant differences in OS and PFS across treatment strategies should be interpreted within the context of both clinical and methodological limitations. First, substantial heterogeneity across included randomized trials, including differences in chemotherapy backbones, patient characteristics, subsequent lines of therapy, and study design, may have contributed to dilution of observable survival differences between treatment regimens [38, 39]. Second, many included therapies were evaluated in distinct biomarker-defined populations (HER2-positive, CLDN18.2-positive, FGFR2b-positive subgroups) or in unselected immunotherapy populations. Pooling these biologically heterogeneous groups within a single network may have attenuated treatment-specific survival effects, thereby reducing the ability to detect statistically significant differences at the overall population level [40]. Third, the network structure was relatively sparse for several active treatment comparisons, with limited direct head-to-head evidence among novel agents [41, 42]. As a result, many comparisons relied primarily on indirect evidence through a common chemotherapy comparator, which may reduce precision and statistical power for detecting differences in long-term survival outcomes.

Regarding short-term efficacy, several immunotherapy-based combination regimens were associated with higher ORR compared with chemotherapy, including cadonilimab, sugemalimab, pembrolizumab, and nivolumab. SUCRA-based ranking suggested that sugemalimab, cadonilimab, and pembrolizumab had relatively higher probabilities of achieving better tumor response; however, these findings should be interpreted as probabilistic estimates rather than evidence of definitive superiority [43]. Cadonilimab, a bispecific antibody targeting PD-1 and CTLA-4, has been proposed to exert synergistic immunomodulatory effects that may enhance antitumor immune activity; nevertheless, the present study provides no direct evidence linking mechanistic properties to comparative clinical superiority, and these observations should therefore be interpreted cautiously [44, 45]. Similarly, sugemalimab demonstrated a higher-ranking probability for ORR; however, such ranking results reflect relative positioning within the network and should not be interpreted as absolute efficacy differences.

For DCR, ramucirumab, cadonilimab, and bevacizumab were associated with higher probabilities of disease stabilization compared with chemotherapy, with ramucirumab showing relatively higher ranking. Anti-angiogenic therapies may contribute to tumor stabilization through modulation of tumor microenvironment and inhibition of angiogenesis; however, these short-term disease control benefits did not translate into consistent improvements in OS or PFS [46]. This discrepancy suggests that DCR should be interpreted as an indicator of early disease control rather than long-term clinical benefit.

Substantial differences in study populations may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity across included trials. These differences include geographic variation in patient populations, inconsistent PD-L1 assessment methods (including assay platforms and cut-off values), variability in chemotherapy backbones, and differences in subsequent treatment strategies or crossover designs. Such factors may influence baseline prognosis and treatment responsiveness, particularly in immunotherapy-based regimens, and may therefore affect the comparability of survival outcomes across studies. These issues should be considered when interpreting the robustness and generalizability of the network estimates.

Regarding safety, the study findings indicate that Panitumumab carries a lower risk of serious adverse events compared to CHE and ranks higher in the sequence; Cetuximab demonstrates a lower risk of neutropenia and outperforms certain immune and anti-angiogenic regimens [47]. This suggests that, in the context of limited efficacy differences, the toxicity profiles of different treatment strategies may become important considerations for clinical selection [48]. It should be noted, however, that this study primarily analyzed ≥Grade 3 serious adverse events and neutropenia, without conducting separate stratified analyses for immune-related specific adverse events. Consequently, the assessment of overall safety remains somewhat limited.

From a clinical perspective, these findings underscore the importance of personalized treatment decisions. In the absence of clear OS differences, treatment choices may increasingly be guided by patient-specific characteristics and therapeutic objectives. For instance, immunotherapy combinations demonstrating superior ORR may be considered for patients requiring rapid tumor burden reduction or symptom relief. Conversely, treatment strategies with lower toxicity profiles may be prioritized for patients with comorbidities or poor hematologic tolerance. Concurrently, biomarker-driven precision medicine remains a key direction for optimizing future treatment strategies, as population-level comparative results cannot replace individualized regimen selection guided by molecular subtyping.

A key methodological consideration in this network meta-analysis is the potential violation of the transitivity assumption due to biological heterogeneity across included treatment populations. The evaluated regimens were developed for distinct molecularly defined subgroups, including HER2-positive, CLDN18.2-positive, FGFR2b-positive populations, as well as unselected populations receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors.

These biomarker-driven differences may introduce systematic variation in baseline prognosis and treatment responsiveness, which could affect the validity of indirect comparisons across the network. Comparing targeted therapies designed for specific molecular alterations with immunotherapy regimens tested in unselected populations may not fully satisfy the assumption of exchangeability required for network meta-analysis.

Therefore, the pooled estimates in this study should be interpreted as population-averaged comparative effects rather than precise biomarker-stratified treatment effects. Future studies incorporating individual patient data or biomarker-stratified randomized trials are needed to improve the validity of cross-population comparisons and support truly precision-guided network meta-analytic frameworks.

Clinical heterogeneity section

A notable source of heterogeneity in this network meta-analysis arises from differences in CHE backbones, subsequent treatment strategies, and trial design across included studies. CHE regimens varied substantially, including fluoropyrimidine- and platinum-based combinations such as FOLFOX, CAPOX, and cisplatin-based doublets, which may differentially influence treatment efficacy and tolerability.

In addition, variations in post-progression therapies and treatment crossover were common in several phase III trials, which may have attenuated observed differences in overall survival and progression-free survival between experimental regimens. These factors could partially dilute treatment effects and reduce the ability to detect statistically significant differences at the network level.

Furthermore, the inclusion of both phase II and phase III trials may introduce additional methodological variability, as phase II studies are typically exploratory with smaller sample sizes and less rigorous survival endpoint validation compared with confirmatory phase III trials.

Although quantitative sensitivity analyses were not feasible due to insufficient numbers of studies within homogeneous subgroups, these sources of heterogeneity should be considered when interpreting the robustness and generalizability of the present findings.

Strength and limitations

This study possesses certain strengths. First, the inclusion of many studies and substantial sample sizes enhances the statistical robustness of the analysis. Second, the Bayesian network meta-analysis method integrates direct and indirect evidence in the absence of direct head-to-head comparisons, providing a systematic framework for multi-arm comparisons. Additionally, the simultaneous evaluation of efficacy and safety outcomes facilitates a comprehensive assessment of different strategies from a benefit-risk balance perspective.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, substantial clinical and biological heterogeneity existed across included trials, particularly in relation to biomarker status, including PD-L1 expression, HER2, CLDN18.2, FGFR2b, and MSI. The lack of consistent biomarker stratification limited the ability to perform subgroup analyses and may have affected the validity of indirect comparisons. Second, variability in study populations, including geographic distribution, chemotherapy backbones, and differences in subsequent therapies and crossover strategies, may have contributed to heterogeneity and influenced survival outcomes. Third, the network was relatively sparse, with several treatment nodes supported by single studies and a limited number of closed loops, preventing formal assessment of inconsistency. This reduces the robustness of indirect comparisons and increases uncertainty in ranking estimates. Fourth, several included agents are no longer part of contemporary clinical practice, which may limit the direct applicability of some comparisons to current treatment algorithms. Fifth, although improvements were observed in ORR and DCR, these surrogate endpoints did not translate into consistent OS or PFS benefits, highlighting the limited clinical interpretability of short-term response outcomes. Finally, SUCRA-based rankings should be interpreted as probabilistic estimates rather than definitive evidence of treatment superiority.

Future research should strengthen head-to-head randomized controlled trials to validate true differences between various immunotherapy or targeted combination regimens. Concurrently, biomarker-stratified network meta-analyses should be conducted to identify optimal first-line treatment strategies for specific molecular subtypes. Furthermore, extending follow-up periods and incorporating patient-reported outcomes such as quality of life will facilitate a more comprehensive assessment of treatment value.

Conclusions

In this network meta-analysis, several immunotherapy- and targeted therapy-based regimens demonstrated improvements in short-term tumor response outcomes, including ORR and DCR, compared with chemotherapy in patients with advanced gastric and gastroesophageal junction cancer. However, no statistically significant differences were observed in OS or PFS across treatment strategies.

Given the substantial clinical heterogeneity among included studies, including differences in patient populations, treatment regimens, and study designs, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Overall, while certain regimens may be associated with improved tumor response, there is insufficient evidence to support definitive superiority in terms of long-term clinical benefit. Further high-quality head-to-head randomized trials and biomarker-stratified analyses are warranted.
