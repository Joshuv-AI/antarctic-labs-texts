T cell exhaustion methylation signature drives differential immune responses in glioblastoma
Authors: Feng Chen, Wen-Bo Qian, Zhen-Hua Chen, Jun Qian, Chun Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMethylation-related signatures play crucial roles in tumorigenesis and progression. However, their roles in the immune response in primary glioblastoma (GBM) remains unclear.MethodsWe analyzed the differential expression of specific members of T cell exhaustion-related pathways in GBM from the perspective of T cell exhaustion. We further screened for significantly negatively correlated methylation sites as candidate methylation markers for T cell exhaustion. Using consensus clustering, we divided the samples into two categories with significant differences in overall survival (OS). We then performed univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses to construct the T Cell Exhaustion Methylation (TEXM) signature. Finally, we confirmed that this signature served as an independent prognostic factor, and further characterized it in terms of drug resistance and immunotherapy.ResultsWe identified 95 significantly differentially expressed T cell exhaustion-related genes and 51 methylation markers associated with T cell exhaustion. The cancer samples were classified according to methylation site markers, thus indicating two subtypes with significant differences in OS: subtype A and subtype B. Tumor scores, stromal scores, tumor purity, and ESTIMATE scores all showed significant differences between subtypes (P < 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified five methylation sites significantly associated with OS, and multivariate Cox regression analysis was used to construct the TEXM signature model by using these five methylation sites. Significant differences in OS were found between the groups with high and low TEXM signature scores, on the basis of calculation of the TEXM signature scores of tumor samples and using the median score to divide them into high and low score groups. Survival analysis revealed that the high score group had poorer OS and DFS than the low score group in the validation set. Notably, we observed a significant difference in drug sensitivity between the high and low TEXM signature score groups, with the high score group showing higher drug resistance and poorer prognosis. The tumor immune state, as predicted with Tracking Tumor Immunophenotype (TIP), revealed significant differences in antitumor immune scores between the high and low TEXM signature score groups. Finally, we identified 43 significantly differentially regulated metabolism-associated biological processes.ConclusionThe epigenetic methylation-related TEXM signature plays a key role in driving differential immune responses in GBM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01412-3.Keywords: Consensus clustering, Glioblastoma, Survival analysis, T cell exhaustion, TEXM signature

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common primary malignant brain tumor. Despite current aggressive standard treatments, including surgical resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide chemotherapy, the prognosis for patients with GBM remains poor [1–4]. In recent years, immunotherapy has garnered increasing attention [5, 6]. T cells, as pioneers and primary effector cells in immunotherapy, play a crucial role in tumor immunotherapy [7, 8]. However, T cells are inhibited by the highly immunosuppressive GBM environment, thus posing a major challenge for T cell-based GBM immunotherapy [9, 10].

T cell function is impaired in many tumors, and addressing this issue has been a focus of many immunotherapies. T cell exhaustion refers to a state of T cell dysfunction characterized by high expression of inhibitory receptors, poor effector function, and diminished proliferative potential, mediated by epigenetic remodeling [11]. Chronic antigen stimulation in infections and cancer induces T cell exhaustion, thereby impairing disease control [12]. Exhausted T cells consist of two distinct subsets: stem-like/progenitor exhausted T cells (PEX)—which have self-renewal properties, maintained by the transcription factor TCF-1 and controlled by the PD-1 receptor, and mainly lack an inhibitory phenotype—and terminally exhausted T cells (TEX)—which show an irreversible stage of exhaustion, with co-expression of PD-1 and TIM-3, including typical features of exhaustion and an inhibitory phenotype, including multifunctional cytokines, decreased proliferation ability, and elevated TOX protein expression. TEX is believed to be associated with poor prognosis after anti-PD-1 therapy [13].

DNA methylation, an epigenetic modification of cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides, is crucial in cancer development and progression [14]. This modification plays major roles in regulating gene expression, RNA processing, and protein function [15]. Chromatin accessibility associated with functional DNA demethylation has been demonstrated to be diminished in the transcription initiation regions of exhausted T cells [16]. Recent research has demonstrated that de novo DNA methylation acquired during and after the peak T cell response caused by chronic viral infections or tumor assaults is critical for establishing T cell exhaustion [17].

To date, few studies have used methylation sites as features to predict GBM prognosis [18]. To explore potential DNA methylation sites associated with GBM survival, this study established a new model based on five methylation site features selected from TCGA-GBM methylation chip data. The model was then applied to evaluate training and validation data sets for 114 patients with GBM, with an aim of improving GBM survival prediction.

Methods

Data and preprocessing

TCGA data

mRNA expression profiles and methylation microarray data (27k, 450k, corrected for batch effects with the sva combat function) for TCGA-GBM glioblastoma samples and GTEx (brain cortex, n = 290) were downloaded from the Xenabrowser database (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/) [19]. Clinical information was downloaded with the R package cgdsr, and samples with survival information, RNA-seq data, and methylation data were selected, thus resulting in 114 glioblastoma samples.

CGGA data

Methylation datasets and corresponding survival data for glioblastoma were downloaded from the Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) database (http://www.cgga.org.cn/index.jsp) [20] and served as the validation set. To match the survival time criteria used in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA, https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) [21], we selected 83 tumor samples with survival data (overall survival [OS] < 55). T cell exhaustion genes

T cell exhaustion-related genes were obtained by downloading four pathways (REACTOME_TNF_SIGNALING, REACTOME_INTERLEUKIN_2_SIGNALING, REACTOME_INTERFERON_GAMMA_SIGNALING, BIOCARTA_TCYTOTOXIC_PATHWAY) from the MSigDB database and relevant genes from the literature (PMID: 35961204), thus resulting in 192T cell exhaustion-related genes with expression data in TCGA-GBM. The 192T cell exhaustion-related genes are listed in Supplementary Table 2.

Identification of methylation sites involved in T cell exhaustion

Using the limma package, we identified differentially expressed genes associated with T cell exhaustion between tumor and normal tissues (ajd. P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1). The psych package's corr.test function was used to detect the correlation between differentially expressed genes and their corresponding methylation sites. Significantly negatively correlated sites were identified as T cell exhaustion-related methylation markers (Pearson P < 0.05 and correlation < 0).

Identification of T cell exhaustion-related methylation subtypes

The methylation site markers identified in the previous step were used for disease subtyping of cancer samples through consensus clustering analysis with the ConsensusClusterPlus package. The clustering distance was Euclidean, and the clustering method was KM, with 1000 repetitions to ensure stability. The K-elbow method was used to aid in determining the number of clusters, and survival analysis was performed on different subtypes.

Immune infiltration assessment

CIBERSORT combined with the LM22 signature matrix was used to estimate the proportions of 22 cell phenotypes in the samples, with the sum of all estimated immune cell type proportions in each sample equaling 1. The ESTIMATE algorithm was used to calculate immune scores, stromal scores, and tumor purity for each tumor sample. Differences in these scores between groups were compared with the Wilcoxon test.

Construction and validation of the T cell exhaustion methylation (TEXM) signature

On the basis of the TCGA training set's methylation and OS data, we performed univariate Cox regression analysis on the methylation site markers identified in the previous step (samples were divided into high and low methylation groups according to median expression values). Independent prognostic performance and nomogram construction based on the TEXM signature

Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed on the clinical characteristics and the TEXM signature scores in both the training and validation sets to determine whether the TEXM signature might serve as an independent prognostic factor. In the training set, the TEXM signature was analyzed across various clinical characteristics, and a nomogram model combining the TEXM signature and clinical factors was constructed to predict cancer progression and guide clinical practice.

Drug resistance assessment associated with the TEXM signature

The pRRophetic package's pRRopheticPredict function was used to predict the resistance of each sample to two drugs (methotrexate and doxorubicin). The Wilcoxon test was used to analyze differences in drug sensitivity between the high and low TEXM signature score groups.

Immune analysis associated with TEXM signature

The Time-series Dense Encode (TIDE) database (http://tide.dfci.harvard.edu/) was used to predict the efficacy of immunotherapy and further characterize differences among TEXM signature groups. The TIP tool (http://biocc.hrbmu.edu.cn/TIP/index.jsp) was used to predict antitumor immune process activity and analyze differences in antitumor immune process activity among the TEXM signature groups.

Metabolic process analysis associated with the TEXM signature

To explore metabolic processes, we used 470 metabolism-associated biological processes (keyword "metabolic") from the GOBP pathways in the MSigDB database. Enrichment of these processes in tumor samples was calculated with ssGSEA, and differences between groups with high and low TEXM signatures were analyzed with the Wilcoxon test.

Statistical analysis

Pearson’s test was used to evaluate the independence or correlation between two variables. All survival analyses were performed with Cox proportional risk models, KM analysis, and log-rank tests. The statistical significance threshold was set at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification of methylation markers involved in T cell exhaustion regulation

First, we used the limma package to identify differentially expressed genes associated with T cell exhaustion between tumor and normal tissues, with adj.P < 0.05 and |log2FC| > 1 as the threshold. This process yielded 95 significantly differentially expressed T cell exhaustion-related genes (Fig. 1A, B). Next, we used the corr.test function from the psych package to detect the Pearson correlations between these 95 differentially expressed genes and their corresponding methylation sites. Using P < 0.05 and correlation < 0 as the threshold, we identified 51T cell exhaustion-related methylation markers. Using the methylation site markers obtained in the previous step, we classified the cancer samples (ConsensusClusterPlus, with Euclidean distance and the KM clustering method, repeated 1000 times). We calculated the TEXM signature scores for tumor samples and divided them into high and low score groups according to the median TEXM signature score. The expression of these five genes differed between the high and low TEXM signature score groups. Survival analysis revealed that the high score group had poorer OS and DFS survival than the low score group. The combined ROC curve indicated good predictive power of the model, with AUCs of 0.79 at 2.5 years, 0.58 at 1 year, and 0.76 at 2 years (Fig. 3F–K).

Similarly, we calculated the TEXM signature scores in the validation set by using the same formula, and divided the samples into high and low score groups with the median score as the threshold. Survival analysis indicated that the high score group had significantly poorer OS, with AUCs of 0.61 at 2.5 years, 0.58 at 1 year, and 0.57 at 2 years (Fig. 3L, M). We then compared the TEXM signature with clinical characteristics in the training set and found some variations but no statistically significant differences (Fig. 3N–R).

Independent prognostic performance of the TEXM signature and nomogram construction

In the training set, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on clinical characteristics (age, sex, and MGMT) and TEXM signature groups. Age, MGMT, and TEXM signature groupings were significantly associated with OS. Multivariate Cox regression analysis incorporating these four features indicated that the TEXM signature grouping remained significantly associated with OS and therefore was confirmed as an independent prognostic factor (Fig. 4A). Similar results were observed in the validation set (Fig. 4B). We then constructed a nomogram model combining the TEXM signature and clinical factors; this model showed slightly higher predictive ability than the TEXM signature alone (Fig. 4C–E).

To further characterize the relationship between the TEXM signature and drug resistance, we used the pRRopheticPredict function from the pRRophetic package to predict the resistance of each training set tumor sample to two drugs (methotrexate and doxorubicin). Significant differences in drug sensitivity were observed between the high and low TEXM signature score groups, with the high score group exhibiting higher drug resistance and poorer prognosis (Fig. 5).

Immune analysis associated with the TEXM signature

First, we used TIDE to predict the immune characteristics of tumor samples. Compared with the low score group, the high TEXM signature score group had significantly higher myeloid-derived suppressor cell (MDSC) values, whereas tumor mutation burden (TMB), TIDE, and microsatellite instability (MSI) showed no significant differences between groups (Fig. 6A–D). Next, we used TIP to predict the antitumor immune status of tumor samples, and observed significant differences in antitumor immune scores between the high and low TEXM signature score groups in most steps (Fig. 6E).

Differences in TMB (A), TIDE (B), MSI (C), and MDSC (D) between groups. E Differences in antitumor immune scores between the high and low TEXM signature groups. F Differences in enrichment in metabolism-related biological processes between the high and low TEXM signature groups

Finally, to characterize the involvement of metabolic processes in T cell exhaustion, we calculated the enrichment of tumor samples in 470 metabolism-associated biological processes (keyword "metabolic") from the GOBP pathways in the MSigDB database with ssGSEA. The Wilcoxon test identified 43 significantly differentially regulated metabolism-associated biological processes between the high and low TEXM signature score groups (Fig. 6F, adj. P < 0.05).

Discussion

DNA methylation plays a crucial role in both physiological and pathological processes in gliomas and neurodevelopment. An urgent need exists to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the GBM microenvironment and identify new prognostic biomarkers. Epigenetics, particularly DNA methylation, shows promise in understanding carcinogenesis, cancer progression, and immune surveillance in malignant tumors [22, 23]. However, DNA methylation can either drive malignancies or result from genomic dysregulation in these malignancies [24]. In this study, methylation sites on the same gene were found to have different effects, thus highlighting the heterogeneity and complexity of these epigenetic changes. Therefore, distinguishing epigenetic changes that promote phenotypes from "passenger" changes that have no biological effects is essential [25, 26]. Additionally, the challenging task of interpreting their biological effects hinders the practicality of purely epigenetic biomarkers.

Identifying Millennium Development Goals may provide new perspectives for exploring the biological effects of epigenetic regulation. DNA methylation is a well-known epigenetic modification that alters the expression of key tumorigenesis-related genes without any changes in gene sequence [21]. These modifications are closely associated with cancer occurrence and development, and many associated biomarkers have been reported [27, 28]. Tumor-specific methylation sites are crucial for accurate diagnosis of cancer [29].

As increasing evidence suggests that T cell differentiation can be defined by the cell’s DNA methylation state, the field is moving away from the historical reliance on surface molecule expression, including the use of inhibitory receptors to classify T cell exhaustion [30]. To investigate whether T cell exhaustion might regulate methylation markers affecting glioma progression, we first performed differential gene expression analysis on TCGA and CGGA database samples from and correlated the findings with methylation sites. We identified 51 methylation markers among T cell exhaustion-related genes. Subsequently, we used these markers to classify glioma samples, thus identifying subtype A and subtype B, which showed significant differences in OS. Additionally, the significantly different tumor scores, stromal scores, tumor purity, and ESTIMATE scores between subtypes suggested that methylation markers might be involved in T cell regulation. Finally, we constructed and validated a T cell exhaustion epigenetic prognostic signature, and demonstrated its potential in clinical diagnosis and patient prognosis.

Although immunotherapy has achieved encouraging response rates in various patients with cancer, its efficacy in patients with GBM remains relatively unclear. One reason is that GBM is in a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. The tumor microenvironment (TME) of GBM is characterized by extensive infiltration of inhibitory myeloid cells, including macrophages, MDSCs, and microglia, thus resulting in a highly immunosuppressive state, with severely limited immune monitoring and response functions [31, 32]. Tumor infiltrating CD8+ T cells are an important component of the tumor immune microenvironment, and play critical roles in identifying and killing tumor cells. However, because of the triggering of complex immune suppression signaling networks, most infiltrating CD8+ T cells become "exhausted," thus leading to cancer immune evasion [33]. In the complex tumor immune microenvironment, the T cell exhaustion markers are not specific to T cells and also represent dysregulation of other important immune cells, such as NK cells and macrophages. For example, the depletion state of CD8+ T cells is also influenced by NK cells. NK cells inhibit the immune function of antiviral CD8+ T cells in direct killing, limiting antigen presenting cell activation, producing immune regulatory cytokines such as IL-10, and eliminating CD4+ T cells that contribute to the immunotherapy response [34]. Increasing evidence suggests that resistance is associated with the complex dual role of the tumor microenvironment, which simultaneously promotes and suppresses GBM [35]. Our findings indicated a significant difference in drug sensitivity (to methotrexate and doxorubicin) between the high and low TEXM signature groups, with the high TEXM score group exhibiting greater drug resistance and poorer prognosis. These findings suggested that the TEXM signature plays an important role in glioma drug resistance.

However, our model has several limitations. First, our study was a retrospective analysis of patients with GBM. Prospective cohorts should be used in the future to validate the prognostic characteristics and confirm the stable performance of the gene prognostic model. Research on the roles of these five genes in tumor models is limited. Although some studies have suggested that certain genes play roles in regulating T cell function, clinical and fundamental experimental validation of the specific mechanisms of these genes remains lacking [36, 37]. This area will require further improvement in our future work.

Conclusion

In this study, we screened tumor tissues and normal tissues for differentially expressed genes in four T cell exhaustion pathways and T cell exhaustion-related genes from the published literature, and further identified methylation markers. Subsequently, we constructed a TEXM signature with univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. This model demonstrated good prognostic value for OS in glioblastoma and performed well on both the training and validation sets. Finally, we characterized drug resistance and immune therapy responses. These results suggest promise in identifying effective methods for glioblastoma treatment in the context of immunotherapy.
