Substantiating the Short Burst Duration in Cortical Myoclonus
Authors: Sterre van der Veen, Amber Maliepaard, Madelein van der Stouwe, Jelle Dalenberg, Inge Tuitert, Jan Willem J Elting, Marina AJ Tijssen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyoclonus is characterized by involuntary, shock‐like movements, of which cortical (CM) and non‐cortical myoclonus (NCM) are most common. Electrophysiology can help differentiate between these subtypes; however, the diagnostic value of several features is largely unknown.ObjectiveThis study aims to determine the diagnostic value of the burst duration in distinguishing CM and NCM.MethodsWe manually identified the burst duration of 8 patients with CM, confirmed by electromyography–electroencephalography registration or somatosensory‐evoked potentials, and 19 patients with NCM, suspected due to a myoclonus‐dystonia phenotype (MYC/DYT‐SGCE positive and negative).ResultsThe sensitivity and specificity were calculated to assess the diagnostic value. The burst duration of CM (31.1 ms) was significantly shorter than that of NCM (56.7 ms), with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 89.5% at a threshold of 45.0 ms. A minimum of 10 randomly selected bursts were sufficient for reliable diagnostic accuracy.ConclusionThe burst duration seems a valuable supportive diagnostic criterion for distinguishing CM and NCM.Keywords: myoclonus, electromyography, burst duration, anatomical classification, cortical myoclonus

Myoclonus refers to brief shock‐like involuntary movements that occur due to hyperexcitable neurons in the central and peripheral nervous system. The classification of myoclonus is commonly based on its anatomical origin, including cortical, subcortical (or non‐cortical), brainstem, spinal, and peripheral myoclonus. 1 Among these subtypes, cortical myoclonus (CM) and subcortical myoclonus are the most common. 2 This anatomical classification aids physicians in diagnosing and choosing the most effective treatment strategy. 3

CM can occur at rest, during voluntary actions, or in response to stimuli. It typically manifests in a multifocal distribution with a preference for distal body parts. CM is commonly associated with conditions such as progressive myoclonus epilepsies/ataxias and postanoxic encephalopathy. On the contrary, non‐cortical myoclonus (NCM) tends to have a more proximal body distribution compared to CM. Reflex features are not commonly observed. Many patients with NCM are diagnosed with myoclonus dystonia syndrome. 1

Electrophysiological testing is frequently employed to differentiate myoclonic jerks originating from different anatomical sources. These tests primarily focus on identifying a cortical correlate on electroencephalography (EEG) that precedes myoclonic bursts observed with electromyography (EMG), but also include muscle recruitment and EMG burst duration. 4 However, the diagnostic value of these electrophysiological features is largely unknown, particularly regarding burst duration. 5 Experts have suggested threshold values for CM ranging from 20 to 100 ms without sufficient evidence to support their recommendations, due to the absence of a study with direct comparison between the subtypes.3, 6, 7

Therefore, our aim was to determine the diagnostic value of burst duration in distinguishing between CM and NCM. This study is the first to present evidence demonstrating that burst duration is a highly sensitive and specific electrophysiological feature that can reliably be used in clinical practice.

Patients and Methods

Patient Selection

This study is part of the Next Move in Movement Disorders (NEMO) study protocol. 8 Patients with CM and NCM were selected from the University Medical Center Groningen (UMCG) hyperkinetic movement disorder database or referred from other hospitals in the Netherlands, and recruited at the UMCG outpatient clinic. CM was confirmed with (1) simultaneous video EMG–EEG registration to demonstrate a positive correlate with jerk‐locked back‐averaging (JLBA) or significant cortico‐muscular coherence (CMC) analysis, or (2) a giant somatosensory‐evoked potential (SSEP). NCM was suspected when patients were diagnosed with the myoclonus‐dystonia phenotype, either MYC/DYT‐SGCE positive or negative. Participants were eligible for inclusion only if they were at least 16 year old. Exclusion criteria consisted of comorbid conditions that led to impaired upper‐extremity function, silver allergy, or implanted pacemaker due to incompatibility with the EMG device. The patients were carefully selected by two movement disorders experts (M.A.J.T. and M.S.).

Data Collection

Clinical information collected included age of onset, family history, disease progression, medication use, and symptom‐influencing factors. Surface EMG data were obtained from the musculus (m.) biceps, m. extensor carpi radialis, and m. flexor carpi ulnaris, and participants performed three tasks (conditions): rest for 20 to 30 seconds, posture (pronated outstretched arms and extended wrists) for 20 to 30 seconds, and action (finger‐to‐nose maneuver) with five repetitions. Surface EMG was recorded using the Trigno system (Delsys, MA, USA) with a frequency of 2000 Hz.

Burst Analysis

Burst Selection

The signal of the surface EMG was filtered using a sixth‐order 10 to 500 Hz Butterworth band‐pass filter and analyzed in MATLAB 2021a. The myoclonic bursts were manually (S.V. and A.M.) identified on the EMG signal (Data S1). To confirm that these bursts were indeed myoclonic jerks, a corresponding abnormal brief shock‐like movement had to be simultaneously observed on the video recording. The onset and offset was visually set for each myoclonic burst with a maximum of 50 bursts per condition per muscle. This approach ensures a manageable and feasible burst selection process, even for high‐frequency myoclonus. To mitigate visual perception bias, burst selection was conducted within a predetermined time frame of the filtered EMG data (0.8 seconds). The burst duration was defined as the difference in milliseconds between onset and offset. The average burst duration was calculated for each patient, including all muscles and all conditions. The frequency in the number of bursts per minute was calculated per condition per muscle for all patients. As a maximum of 50 bursts per condition per muscle was set, the frequency will be underestimated in those patients who exceeded this maximum of 50 bursts.

Statistical Analysis

Mann‐Whitney U test was used to test for statistical differences in average burst duration between patients with CM and NCM. The receiver operator curve (ROC) was calculated for the average burst duration per patient to determine the sensitivity and specificity. We did not perform a power analysis a priori due to the rarity of both myoclonus subtypes.

Bootstrapping Analysis

To determine the minimal number of bursts per patient, we conducted a bootstrapping analysis. We randomly selected k number of bursts per patient (all muscles, all conditions) to calculate the average burst duration, with replacement, for 1000 iterations. With each iteration, we calculated the ROC, area under the receiver operator curve (AUC‐ROC), and optimal threshold. This resulted in a mean ROC, AUC‐ROC, and optimal threshold with 95% confidence intervals (CI) based on the standard deviation, for k = 5, 10, 15, and 20 bursts.

Results

We included 8 patients with CM and 19 with NCM (8 MYC/DYT‐SGCE negative and 11 positive). CM was confirmed with significant CMC (n = 6), positive JLBA (n = 1), or a giant SSEP (n = 1). A total of 1325 bursts were identified in patients with CM and 598 in patients with NCM. We found a mean burst duration of 31.1 ms (±6.7 ms) on average per patient with CM (Fig. 1; Table S1). This was significantly lower than the mean duration of 56.7 ms (±11.5 ms) on average per patient with NCM (U = 44, z‐value = −3.58, P < 0.001). An “excellent” AUC‐ROC of 0.947 was found in differentiating CM from NCM based on the average burst duration per patient, with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 89.5% at a threshold of 45.0 ms.

The burst duration of MYC/DYT‐SGCE negative (mean duration: 58.1 ± 7.4 ms) and MYC/DYT‐SGCE positive (55.7 ± 14.1 ms) was similar.

The average number of burst per minute was higher for patients with CM than NCM, during all conditions and in all muscles (Table S1). The highest number of bursts was identified during posture in both groups. Totally 4 of 8 patients with CM reached the maximum of 50 bursts per condition per muscle in 9 of 72 (13%) EMG signals. None of the patients with NCM reached this maximum.

To determine the average burst duration most accurately for an individual patient, only 10 bursts are required ( Figure S1). The average duration of 10 randomly selected bursts across multiple EMG signals of different conditions and muscles has a high diagnostic accuracy, with a mean AUC‐ROC of 0.9375 (95% CI: 0.89–0.98). The threshold for distinguishing the two types of myoclonus was 41.7 ms (95% CI: 35.4–47.9 ms). Interestingly, the diagnostic performance (AUC‐ROC) was highly similar whether the average duration was calculated using 5, 15, or 20 randomly chosen bursts (Table S2).

Discussion

We found that the burst duration of CM is significantly shorter than that of NCM. At a threshold of 45 ms, the average burst duration per individual in an electrophysiological test has an excellent diagnostic accuracy (94.7%) with a sensitivity of 100% and a specificity of 89.5% for distinguishing between the two myoclonus subtypes. This diagnostic test requires only 10 burst to be randomly selected, during rest, posture, or action, to calculate the average burst duration. In conclusion, the burst duration should indeed be a supportive criterion for the diagnosis of CM and NCM.

The average burst duration (57 ms) in our cohort of patients with myoclonus dystonia is shorter than the burst duration described in two other case series, which showed an average duration of 95 ms (range: 25–256) and 102 ms (range: 30.4–750.6 ms).9, 10 The longer burst durations were hypothesized to be likely related to dystonia. An explanation for the relative short duration of NCM in our cohort could be that we minimized the inclusion of longer dystonic movements due to validation of each individual EMG burst with a co‐occurring myoclonic movement on video.

The observed difference in burst duration between the subtypes of myoclonus raises the question as to why one is shorter than the other. The simplicity and brevity of a cortical myoclonic jerk resemble the action potentials of those evoked by eliciting a reflex jerk with a tendon tap. 11 The longer duration of bursts in NCM in myoclonus dystonia could be attributed to the involvement of subcortical structures (eg, thalamus, basal ganglia, brainstem, and cerebellum) because of its common co‐occurrence with dystonia. 12 The abnormal electrical activity may travel through more complex pathways, leading to prolonged bursts of muscle contraction compared to the relatively direct and rapid discharges seen in CM.

Interestingly, not only did the burst duration differ between CM and NCM, but the frequency of bursts per minute differed as well. The difference in frequency is clear despite the presumed underestimation of the frequency of CM, as we had set a maximum of 50 bursts. Therefore, the frequency of myoclonic bursts could be an additional diagnostic marker in distinguishing between CM and NCM.

Our study has a limitation concerning the visual determination of the onset and offset of each myoclonic burst, which was done in a nonblinded manner, introducing the possibility of selection bias. Unfortunately, no automatic method of burst detection is currently available to address this issue. A strength of the study has been the careful patient selection and strict inclusion criteria for CM based on a clear cortical correlate or giant SSEP. Unfortunately, we were not able to perform JLBA or CMC in the patients with myoclonus dystonia.

In conclusion, we have provided evidence for the burst duration as a supportive electrophysiological diagnostic criterion in distinguishing between CM and NCM. This is the first supportive diagnostic criteria of CM that has been directly studied between the subtypes of myoclonus. Future studies should focus on validating other supportive criteria, as simultaneous registration of EEG–EMG to confirm a cortical correlate is not available in all centers or feasible in all patients. Over time, the utilization of the anatomical classification will become more accessible for physicians worldwide, facilitating the diagnostic approach and therapy strategies for patients with myoclonus.
