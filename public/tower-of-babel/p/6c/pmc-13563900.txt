Birthweight and cardiometabolic risk factors in offspring: A systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies
Authors: Chengbi Zhu, Yali Ding, Li Chen, Siming Zhai, Yaqun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association between birthweight and cardiometabolic risk factors in offspring remains incompletely elucidated. Clarifying this relationship is crucial for understanding the origins of developmental disorders and identifying early risk factors. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to assess the association of birthweight with anthropometric nutritional status and blood pressure in offspring, and secondarily to evaluate its association with lipid and glucose metabolism.MethodsPubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science were searched from inception to September 30, 2025. Data were extracted using a standardized form, which included the title, first author, publication year, country, baseline characteristics of participants, exposure variables, grouping method, and outcome measures. The quality of the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale(NOS). Primary endpoints (obesity, overweight, and blood pressure) were pooled and reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals. A fixed-effects model was used when between-study heterogeneity was low (I²<50%); otherwise, a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model was used.ResultsA total of 33 cohort studies involving 8,241,597 children were included. High birthweight (≥ 4,000 g) was associated with a higher odds of obesity (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.33–1.95; I²=96.9%) and overweight (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.22–1.86; I²=77.5%). Conversely, low BW (< 2,500 g) was associated with a lower odds of obesity (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.71–0.81; I²=0.0%) and overweight (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.71–0.89; I²=0.0%). Birthweight was negatively associated with systolic blood pressure (SBP) (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.42–0.94; I²=4.2%), although no association was observed between birthweight and diastolic blood pressure (DBP).ConclusionHigh birthweight was associated with a higher likelihood of developing obesity and overweight during childhood and adolescence, while birthweight was negatively correlated with SBP.Trial registrationCRD42024565031.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-02008-9.

Background

Metabolic syndrome represents a series of cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk factors arising from systemic metabolic disturbances [1, 2]. Epidemiological evidence has shown a steadily increasing number of deaths attributable to CVD, imposing a substantial burden on many countries worldwide [3, 4]. In fact, several cardiovascular risk factors commonly observed in adults have already emerged during childhood and adolescence. Therefore, early identification and timely intervention among these vulnerable populations have been recognized as critically important [5].

Birthweight (BW) is an important indicator of intrauterine growth and nutritional status and is also one of the key predictors of future growth and health outcomes [6]. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), BW is classified into three categories: low BW (< 2,500 g), normal BW (2,500-4,000 g), and macrosomia (> 4,000 g) [7]. Over the past two decades, the prevalence of low BW has remained high worldwide [8]. In China, the prevalence of macrosomia has shown an increasing trend [9, 10] approaching the levels reported in the United States over the past four decades [11]. This trend may be related to advanced maternal age, socioeconomic disparities, healthcare conditions, and environmental exposures [12].

The International Diabetes Federation has defined pediatric metabolic syndrome based on the presence of obesity, elevated blood pressure (BP), abnormalities in glucose metabolism, and dyslipidemia [13]. Consequently, considerable attention has been directed toward the associations between BW and these cardiometabolic risk factors. However, the available evidence remains inconsistent. Furthermore, most previous reviews have focused on a single outcome, such as obesity or diabetes, rather than comprehensively evaluating the full spectrum of cardiometabolic risk factors. In addition, substantial heterogeneity exists in the classification of BW across studies.

Therefore, a contemporary systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies were conducted using rigorous methodological criteria. Multiple cardiometabolic risk factors, including body composition, BP, lipid metabolism, and glucose metabolism, were comprehensively evaluated. By systematically examining the associations between BW and cardiometabolic outcomes in children and adolescents, this study aimed to provide robust evidence for the early identification of high-risk populations and the prevention of future CVDs.

Methods

This report followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [14] and the Meta-analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) [15]. This meta-analysis has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024565031).

Search strategy

PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Web of Science databases were searched from inception to September 30, 2025. There is no restriction on the publication date for any literature. MeSH terms and free-text keywords were used in our search, including child, adolescent, BW, cardiometabolic risk factors, obesity, and BP. The search strategy is presented in Table S1. The references of included studies and pertinent review articles were manually reviewed to identify additional articles.

Study selection

The eligibility criteria were defined based on the PECOS framework.

Inclusion criteria

(1) Population: Children or adolescents (< 18 years); (2) Exposure and Comparator: BW categorized according to WHO criteria as low BW (< 2,500 g), normal BW (2,500-4,000 g), and macrosomia (≥ 4,000 g); (3) Outcomes: Primary outcomes included obesity, overweight, and BP. Secondary outcomes included IR, fasting plasma glucose, diabetes, cholesterol, and TG (triglyceride). No restrictions were imposed based on diagnostic criteria.

Study Design: Cohort studies published in English. Cohort studies, through their prospective or retrospective longitudinal design, could establish a clear temporal sequence between exposure (BW) and outcome (cardiometabolic risk factors), thereby providing stronger evidence for causal inference than cross-sectional or other observational designs. Although a cross-sectional study was cited in the introduction, no such studies were included in the meta-analysis.

Exclusion criteria

The following studies were excluded: (1) Reviews, guidelines, case reports, study protocols, or conference abstracts; (2) Clinical trials, animal studies, or in vitro research; (3) Studies without available full texts or a duplicate publication; (4) Studies lacking outcome data or with insufficient data; (5) Studies employing inappropriate statistical methods, including those from which effect estimates could not be extracted or calculated, exposure categories were incompatible with predefined BW classifications, outcome data were insufficient for quantitative synthesis, or statistical analyses did not adequately evaluate the association between BW and cardiometabolic outcomes.

Two independent reviewers (CB Z and YL D) screened the searched studies. All potentially relevant studies were imported into EndNote 20. After deduplication, the titles and abstracts were screened to remove ineligible studies. Subsequently, the full texts were checked to identify eligible studies. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (YQ L) when necessary.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction and quality assessment were performed independently by two reviewers (CB Z and YL D). Discrepancies, if any, were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (YQ L). Extracted data included title, first author, publication year, country, baseline characteristics of participants, exposure variables, methods for ascertaining exposure levels, and outcome measures.

The quality of included studies was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) [16]. This tool covered eight domains: representativeness of the exposed cohort, selection of the non-exposed cohort, exposure ascertainment, confirmation that the outcome of interest was not present at study initiation, comparability of cohorts according to the study design or analysis, outcome assessment, adequacy of follow-up duration, and completeness of follow-up. Each domain (except for comparability) was assigned a maximum score of 1, with comparability receiving a maximum of 2 points. The total score ranged from 0 to 9. A score ≥ 7 denoted high quality.

Data synthesis and analyses

Effect estimates were presented as ORs, relative risks (RRs), linear regression coefficients (β), or hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) obtained from adjusted analyses. When these measures were not directly reported in the articles, they were obtained from back calculations using the raw data or extracted from figures and Table (4) Results from included studies were meta-analyzed for the primary outcome measures, while the findings for the secondary outcomes were not pooled and summarized narratively.

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the χ² test and the I² statistic. I² < 50% indicated low heterogeneity; accordingly, a fixed-effects model (Mantel-Haenszel method) was used. Otherwise, a random-effects model (DerSimonian-Laird method) was applied. If large heterogeneity was present, subgroup analyses and meta-regression were conducted to find potential sources of heterogeneity. If an outcome measure was reported in at least 5 studies, sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the stability of meta-analytic results by excluding the studies one by one. Publication bias was evaluated through funnel plots and Egger’s test. Analyses were performed using Stata 15.0.

Ethics approval

The data are derived from published literature. Hence, additional ethics review is not required.

Results

Screening results & flowchart

A total of 60,100 records were identified from PubMed (n = 8,785), Embase (n = 32,903), Cochrane (n = 2,435), and Web of Science (n = 15,976), with an additional study identified through reference screening. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 45,787 articles were screened. Subsequently, 136 full-text articles were reviewed. After the removal of studies with inaccessible full texts (n = 3), inappropriate statistical methods (n = 94), and incomplete data (n = 6), 33 studies [17–49] were included. The selection process and results are detailed in Fig. 1.

Characteristics of included studies

The included studies originated from multiple countries and regions, including 8,241,597 individuals. The sample sizes ranged from 143 [25] to 3,834,405 participants [24]. Except for two studies [30, 45], all were multicenter studies. Most studies adjusted for potential confounding factors, including sex, gestational age, and height, as well as maternal age, parental education, and household income. Study characteristics are summarized in Table S2.

Quality assessment

Included studies were generally of high quality. Among them, 30 studies scored 7 or higher, while two scored 6 one scored 5. Details of the quality assessment are provided in Table S3.

Meta-analysis of primary outcomes

Association between BW and anthropometric nutritional status in offspring

Obesity

Eleven studies investigated the association between BW and obesity [19–21, 25, 29, 31–33, 35, 39, 40]. The pooled results for continuous BW measures using a random-effects model (I2 = 64.2%) demonstrated a notable positive association between BW and obesity (OR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.22–2.52) (Fig. 2). For categorical variables, a random-effects model (I2 = 96.9%) was used. The results demonstrated that children with BW ≥ 4,000 g had a greater odds of obesity (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.33–1.95). Conversely, BW < 2,500 g was associated with a lower risk of obesity (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.71–0.81) (Fig. 3).

Overweight

Eight studies examined the association between BW and overweight [20, 21, 27, 31, 32, 36, 38, 44, 48]. The meta-analysis for continuous BW was performed using a random-effects model (I2 = 71.0%) and revealed a significant positive association of BW with overweight (OR = 1.46, 95% CI: 1.13–1.89) (Fig. 2). A random-effects model was used to pool results (I2 = 73.9%) for categorical BW. The results indicated that children with BW ≥ 4,000 g had a higher odds of overweight (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.22–1.86). Additionally, BW < 2,500 g was associated with a lower odds of obesity (OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.71–0.89) (Fig. 3).

Association between BW and blood pressure in offspring

Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP)

Ten studies explored the association between BW and SBP [17, 18, 22, 28, 30–32, 37, 41, 42]. A fixed-effects model was utilized to perform the meta-analysis (I²=4.2%) for continuous BW. The results indicated that each 1 kg increase in BW was associated with 35% lower odds of elevated SBP (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.42–0.94) (Fig. 2). The meta-analysis for categorical BW was performed to evaluate the linear association between a 1 kg increase in BW and the odds of elevated SBP, as defined by the respective diagnostic criteria in each included study. The pooled analyses for categorical BW were conducted using a random-effects model (I²=56.3%). The results revealed no difference in the odds of elevated SBP between infants with high BW (≥ 4,000 g; OR = 2.80, 95% CI: 0.64–12.24) or low BW (< 2,500 g; OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 0.58–3.08) and those with normal BW (Fig. 3).

Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP)

Nine studies reported on the association between BW and DBP [17, 18, 22, 28, 30–32, 37, 42]. The meta-analysis of continuous BW was performed using a fixed-effects model (I2 = 0.0%). The results revealed no association between BW and DBP (OR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.61–1.13]) (Fig. 2). The meta-analysis of categorical BW was performed utilizing a random-effects model (I2 = 61.4%). The results indicated that neither BW ≥ 4,000 g (OR = 0.86, 95% CI: 0.39–1.88) nor BW < 2,500 g (OR = 0.98, 95% CI: 0.29–3.35) was associated with increased DBP (Fig. 3).

Secondary outcomes

Association Between BW and Lipid Profiles in Offspring

Total Cholesterol (TC) and Triglyceride (TG)

Two studies investigated the relationship between BW and TC. The study by Bekkers et al. [45] examined the association between BW and elevated plasma TC levels. Multiple linear regression analysis results show that neither BW < 2,500 g (β=−0.05, 95% CI: −0.38 − 0.28) nor BW ≥ 4,000 g (β=−0.001, 95% CI: −0.13–0.13) was associated with TC levels. Similarly, the study by Azadbakht et al. [32] analyzed 5,528 school-aged children using multivariable logistic regression and reported no differences in TC levels between the low BW group (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.60–1.50) and the macrosomia group (OR = 0.70, 95% CI: 0.30–1.30). This study also examined the association between BW and TG, revealing no associations for either BW ≥ 4,000 g (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.60–1.60) or BW < 2,500 g (OR = 1.20, 95% CI: 0.80–1.80). The study by Suh et al. enrolled 276 preterm infants with low BW born before 32 weeks of gestation, among whom 104 were diagnosed with hypertriglyceridemia (HTG) 10 days after birth. Multivariate regression analysis indicated that lower BW was a predictor of HTG (OR = 0.75, 95% CI: 0.66–0.85) [49].

Association Between BW and Glucose Metabolism in Offspring

Diabetes Mellitus

Only one study reported on the occurrence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). The study by Wei et al. [43] split the participants into five groups based on BW: <2,500 g, 2,500-2,999 g, 3,000–3,499 g (reference group), 3,500-3,999 g, and ≥ 4,000 g. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that low BW (< 2,500 g) elevated the likelihood of T2DM (OR = 2.87, 95% CI: 1.19–6.92), whereas no associations were observed for other BW levels.

Four studies examined type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM). However, due to methodological heterogeneity, a meta-analysis could not be conducted. The study by Ponsonby et al. [34] investigated the association between BW and T1DM and found a positive association (OR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.31–6.09). Macrosomic infants exhibited a greater likelihood of T1DM than children with a BW < 4,000 g (OR = 3.09, 95% CI: 1.15–8.29). However, subsequent studies yielded conflicting conclusions. According to the study by Khashan et al.[46] a BW < 1,500 g was associated with a lower risk of T1DM (RR = 0.66, 95% CI [RR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.48–0.91), while other BW levels showed no association with the risk of T1DM. Goldacree et al. [24] utilized the same BW classification as Wei et al. [43], and found that BW above the average level (3,500-3,999 g) elevated the risk of T1DM (HR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.02–1.22), whereas BW < 2,500 g served as a protective factor (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.67–0.99). Conversely, Algert et al. [47] found no association between BW and the risk of T1DM (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 0.97–1.22). Additionally, neither BW < 2,500 g (RR = 0.74, 95% CI: 0.38–1.44) nor BW ≥ 4,000 g (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.70–1.43) was associated with T1DM.

Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG)

Two studies reported the association between BW and FBG levels. Due to differences in statistical methodologies, these studies could not be pooled. With normal BW as the reference group, the study by Azadbakht et al. [32] found no association between FBG levels and low BW (OR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.70–1.40) or macrosomia (OR = 0.90, 95% CI: 0.60–1.30) after adjusting for potential confounders. The study by Bianco et al. [23] assessed FBG levels in 4,155 children, and found a negative association between BW and FBG levels (β=−0.015, 95% CI: −0.029- −0.001).

Insulin Resistance (IR)

Two studies reported the association between BW and IR. Mardones et al. [19] categorized BW and used the 2,500 g-2,999 g as the reference group. The results indicated that the prevalence of IR declined as BW increased, but BW < 2,500 g did not appear to be a risk factor for IR (OR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.52–1.29). Zhuo et al. [26] explored the association between BW and the homeostasis model assessment for IR (HOMA-IR) in US adolescents. BW > 4,000 g was negatively associated with the prevalence of abnormal HOMA-IR (OR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.47–0.83). Additionally, low BW showed no association with IR (OR = 1.26, 95% CI: 0.99–1.60. They also found that for each kilogram increase in BW, the prevalence of abnormal HOMA-IR decreased by 21% (adjusted OR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.69–0.90).

The detailed results of the secondary outcome measures are presented in Table S4.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted for outcome measures reported in five or more studies, using a leave-one-out method to evaluate the influence of individual studies on aggregated results. The results for obesity, overweight, and DBP did not change after excluding any single study, suggesting the results were relatively reliable (Figure S2). For SBP as a continuous variable, the results changed when the study by Lule et al. [30] OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.44–1.03) or Pereira et al. [37] (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.45–1.01) was excluded, suggesting that the results were not stable (Figure S3).

We used the same approach to identify sources of heterogeneity. In the BW ≥ 4, 000 g group, heterogeneity was significantly reduced after excluding the study by Moreira et al.(I²=0.2%), possibly because it did not describe the adjustments for influencing factors. Unfortunately, no other sources of heterogeneity were found in the results of the meta-analyses (Figure S3).

Publication bias

Publication bias was evaluated using the funnel plot (Figure S4) and Egger’s test. Publication bias was not detected for all six outcome indicators.

Discussion

Extreme BW elevates the risk of CVDs [50]. CVD in childhood may lead to irreversible damage later in life [51–53]. Hence, early prevention before CVDs occur is essential [5].

This meta-analysis investigated the association between BW and the risk factors of CVDs in offspring. BW ≥ 4,000 g was identified as a risk factor for obesity and overweight in offspring, whereas low BW was associated with a lower likelihood of these conditions. A potential negative correlation was observed between BW and both SBP and IR. However, evidence was insufficient to establish associations of BW with lipid and glucose metabolism.

These findings indicate that macrosomia significantly increases the risk of obesity, potentially through shared genetic susceptibilities, excessive maternal nutrition, or biomarkers like leptin [54–56]. Newborns with macrosomia thus require focused early interventions to prevent excessive weight gain in early childhood. Moreover, although BW below 2,500 g was associated with lower odds of obesity and overweight, this observed association should not be interpreted as a beneficial effect, as extremely low BW infants may face more severe challenges in growth and development [57, 58]. It may be influenced by other factors frequently associated with low BW, such as intrauterine growth restriction, prematurity, or underlying pathological conditions, which themselves carry significant health risks.

The relationship between BW and hypertension in offspring remains inconclusive. While one study indicated that high BW (≥ 4,000 g) was associated with elevated BP in childhood [59], others found no significant association with primary hypertension [60]. Our analysis found no association between BW and DBP. Furthermore, SBP as a continuous variable was found to be negatively associated with BW. However, in the analysis of categorical BW, neither high nor low BW was associated with SBP. Sensitivity analysis (Figure S2E) revealed that the pooled results for continuous SBP were influenced by the Pereira [37] and Lule [30] studies. After excluding these two studies, the results indicated no association between BW and SBP. This suggested that the meta-analysis results were not robust, possibly owing to different research methods or sample sizes. How BW influences hypertension in children has not yet been unveiled. Nevertheless, the association between obesity and hypertension and its associated mechanisms has been documented across pediatric and adult populations [61, 62]. Given the association of BW with obesity and overweight observed in our study, significant attention should be paid to childhood hypertension.

Dyslipidemia is closely associated with metabolic abnormalities in TC and TG [63]. A meta-analysis [9] revealed that children with LGA status do not exhibit a higher risk of HTG or hypercholesterolemia. In our systematic review, two cohort studies showed no significant association between BW and TC levels in offspring during adolescence, although both studies had limitations. Bekkers [45] reported a large sample loss, and the low BW group comprised only 15 individuals, which may have biased the results due to the considerable differences in sample sizes between groups. Azadbakht et al. [32] did not report sample sizes for the different weight groups. Notably, participants in Azadbakht's study were instructed to fast for at least 12 h before blood sampling, whereas Bekkers et al. did not implement this protocol.

The relationship between BW and childhood T1DM remains unclear. While a 2010 meta-analysis indicates that high BW increases the risk of T1DM [64], this finding needs to be further validated. One included study [43] found that children with low BW were more prone to T2DM, whereas a higher BW did not increase this risk. However, the crude OR in this study indicated a U-shaped relationship of BW with the risk of T1DM, while both low and high BW elevated the prevalence of T2DM. The foregoing finding has also been validated in other studies [65, 66]. Due to methodological differences among studies, it is difficult to draw a unified conclusion based on the existing evidence. The fetal insulin hypothesis proposes that low BW and T2DM represent distinct phenotypic expressions of a shared genotype. Genetic variations impact fetal pancreatic development, causing intrauterine growth restriction, resulting in both low BW and T2DM [67]. Subsequent studies have also confirmed this viewpoint [68, 69]. However, an alternative thrifty phenotype hypothesis posits that adipose tissue may play a pivotal role in the association between low BW and T2DM [70].

FBG and IR are critical markers for assessing glucose metabolism in the body. Elevated blood glucose impacts cardiovascular health through multiple mechanisms, such as endothelial cell damage [71]. One study indicates a negative association between BW and FBG levels in adolescents, while another study, after grouping BW, found no correlation between BW and FBG levels. Additionally, abnormal glucose tolerance is considered a pre-diabetic stage and is closely associated with IR. HOMA-IR is higher in low BW children than in those with normal BW [72]. In contrast, our systematic review found a potential negative correlation between BW and IR, though low BW may not be a significant determinant of IR.

BW is also influenced by multiple factors. Macrosomia is associated with maternal high BMI and gestational diabetes [73, 74], whereas low BW may be related to maternal height, incomplete prenatal care, and lower maternal education levels [75]. Furthermore, the length of the pregnancy may also determine the baby’s BW [76]. Therefore, when BW is used as a predictor of subsequent child growth and development, these contextual factors should be carefully considered. In any case, monitoring the physical condition of pregnant women and the intrauterine environment has a significant impact on the birth and future health of the child.

While most included studies adjusted for common confounders, such as child age, sex, gestational age, and residence, some studies employed incomplete adjustments or inconsistent approaches. Future studies should standardize and comprehensively address potential confounders, including BMI, diet, and socioeconomic status.

Methodological concerns also exist in the measurement and categorization of BW. First, although the WHO classification is internationally accepted and facilitates comparability across studies, it may not fully capture population-specific growth characteristics in some settings. This approach may exaggerate statistical effects near cutoff points. Second, continuous measures of BW employed 1-kg increments, which might obscure risk patterns at extreme BW. Given these limitations, the results need to be cautiously interpreted, and studies with more precise BW assessment methods or larger samples are warranted to minimize threshold-related bias.

Strengths and limitations

This systematic review possesses notable methodological strengths. For instance, we included a large sample size from 33 cohort studies (over 8.2 million children). Furthermore, we comprehensively assessed cardiometabolic risk factors. Clinically, intrauterine monitoring is pivotal for preventing extreme BWs, early intervention for macrosomic infants, and enhancing postnatal care for infants with low BW.

Our findings align with the perspective that BW is likely a marker of complex fetal metabolic and endocrine programming, rather than a direct causative factor for later cardiometabolic health. As discussed by Wilcox et al., observed associations may reflect the influence of underlying genetic, maternal, and environmental factors that shape both fetal growth and long-term metabolic trajectories [77].

This study has limitations. First, although a comprehensive search strategy was employed across multiple databases and supplemented by manual reference screening, the inclusion of numerous outcome-related search terms may have affected retrieval efficiency, and some relevant studies may have been missed. Second, selection and information bias may have arisen from self-reported BW data and incomplete follow-up. Third, residual confounding cannot be excluded despite adjustment for major confounders. Fourth, substantial heterogeneity was observed across several analyses, reducing confidence in some pooled estimates. Potential sources of heterogeneity were identified only for obesity outcomes. For other outcome measures, subgroup analyses are not performed due to limited studies and inconsistent reporting. Finally, given limited data, meta-analyses of secondary outcomes are not conducted.

Conclusions

BW over 4,000 g is associated with a higher odds of obesity and overweight in offspring, while BW less than 2,500 g is associated with a lower odds. Additionally, BW is negatively associated with SBP. Due to insufficient data, the relationship between BW and lipid and glucose metabolism remains unclear. These findings need to be further validated in future studies.
