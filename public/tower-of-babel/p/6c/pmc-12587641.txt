A nationwide cross-sectional study of antimicrobial resistance in Palestinian hospitals: insights from 10,000 clinical isolates
Authors: Ibrahim Amer Ghannam, Shams Riad Owdetallah, Sara Khaled Alkhatib, Deema Issa Zboun, Esra Ahmed Alhelale, Khalid Ayman Najjar, Yahya Ibrahim Ghannam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global health crisis, but its burden is magnified in conflict-affected, resource-limited settings. We present the first national-scale assessment of AMR in Palestinian hospitals, characterizing prevalence patterns and identifying demographic and clinical predictors of multidrug resistance (MDR).MethodsThis cross-sectional study examined 10,007 unique bacterial isolates from thirteen West Bank governmental hospitals (January–December 2023). Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing adhered to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2022 guidelines. Hospital-specific MDR rates ranged from 24.0 to 64.4%.ConclusionsPalestine faces a critical MDR burden, necessitating urgent antibiotic regulation, enhanced stewardship, and standardized diagnostics to mitigate AMR in this and similar conflict-affected settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01652-0.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, Multidrug resistance, Extensively drug-resistant, Gram-positive pathogens, Gram-negative pathogens, Palestinian healthcare, AMR surveillance

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is one of the greatest health challenges of the current era, rendering once-treatable infections difficult—and sometimes impossible—to cure, particularly in resource-limited settings such as Palestine [1]. In 2019, bacterial AMR caused 1.27 million deaths and was associated with nearly 5 million deaths worldwide [2]. Without urgent action, AMR is projected to cause significant economic losses, with a potential 3.8% drop in global GDP by 2050. Low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are likely to bear the heaviest burden [3].

In the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region, rising resistance to cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, and carbapenems threatens treatment efficacy, particularly for Acinetobacter baumannii, Enterobacterales, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [4, 5]. In Palestine, antibiotic misuse, unregulated over-the-counter access, and inadequate infection control—compounded by a fragmented healthcare system—fuel the spread of AMR [6, 7]. Ongoing conflict and restricted healthcare access exacerbate these challenges, limiting surveillance and infection prevention efforts [7, 8]. Comprehensive national AMR surveillance data are scarce, hindering evidence-based interventions [9].

Previous Palestinian studies were limited to single-center analyses or focused on specific patient groups [10, 11]. To address this gap, this study provides the first comprehensive cross-sectional analysis of AMR trends across over 10,000 clinical isolates from governmental hospitals in the West Bank. By analyzing AMR patterns, demographic factors, and hospital-specific variations, this study aims to inform national antimicrobial stewardship strategies and provide models for other resource-limited, conflict-affected settings worldwide.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional descriptive study retrospectively analyzed AMR data obtained from thirteen Palestinian governmental hospitals across the West Bank. The study included three tertiary care centers and ten district-level hospitals, collectively serving a diverse patient population.

Sample and data sources

Clinical microbiology records were obtained from the Ministry of Health’s centralized database. Data were sourced from routine diagnostic microbiology workflows conducted between January 1 and December 31, 2023. From an initial pool of 16,454 clinical specimens, only one isolate per patient per bacterial species was included to avoid duplication of infection episodes. This was confirmed using patient IDs and collection dates. After filtering, the final dataset comprised 10,007 unique bacterial isolates. These isolates were derived from urine, blood cultures, wound swabs, and other sterile body fluids collected in both inpatient and outpatient settings.

Bacterial identification

Bacterial identification was performed at each site using standardized microbiological techniques, including Gram staining, colony morphology, and biochemical assays such as catalase, oxidase, and carbohydrate fermentation tests. In addition, commercial identification systems (e.g., VITEK 2 and API 20E; bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France) were employed where available. Laboratories followed manufacturer protocols and institutional standard operating procedures to ensure diagnostic accuracy and consistency across facilities.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST)

AST was conducted in accordance with Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) 2022 guidelines [12]. Three hospitals utilized the automated VITEK 2 system, while the remaining hospitals primarily employed the manual disk diffusion method. Some laboratories also applied broth microdilution or disk elution methods for selected organisms or antibiotics, depending on clinical or logistical considerations.

Disk diffusion testing followed standardized procedures using pure cultures with morphologically identical colonies, inocula adjusted to a 0.5 McFarland turbidity standard, incubation at 35 ± 2 °C for 18–24 h, and Mueller-Hinton Agar as the testing medium. CLSI-defined breakpoints were used for interpretation. A broad range of antibiotic classes was included, guided by both CLSI recommendations and local prescribing patterns. Not all antibiotics were uniformly tested across facilities due to variations in laboratory capacity and clinical priorities. Detailed information on the antimicrobials tested, breakpoints, and organism-specific coverage is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Quality control

All laboratories implemented internal quality control procedures in accordance with CLSI standards. Reference strains (Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923) were used to verify the potency of antibiotic discs, media performance, and the accuracy of identification systems. These measures were part of routine laboratory operations throughout the study period.

Resistance classification

AMR classifications adhered to internationally accepted definitions. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms were defined as those resistant to at least one agent in three or more antimicrobial classes, while extensively drug-resistant (XDR) organisms were defined as those susceptible to only one or two antimicrobial classes, in accordance with WHO and CDC guidelines [13, 14].

As data were retrospectively collected for clinical rather than research purposes, antimicrobial susceptibility testing was not fully standardized across all isolates. In particular, not all laboratories tested the complete panel of antimicrobial classes required for definitive XDR classification. Consequently, only 14 isolates (< 0.2%) met the formal XDR definition. Due to their low prevalence and potential to bias analyses, these were grouped with MDR isolates in binary comparisons.

Isolates resistant to fewer than three antimicrobial classes or fully susceptible were categorized as non-MDR. Intermediate susceptibility results were considered non-susceptible and grouped with resistant results for statistical purposes. Final AMR classification was thus binary: MDR (including XDR) versus non-MDR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and AMR profiles. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify independent predictors of multidrug resistance (MDR), with adjustments made for potential confounding variables. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee of Al-Quds University. In accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, all patient data were anonymized prior to analysis to maintain confidentiality and protect participants’ rights.

Results

A total of 10,007 unique bacterial isolates were recovered from patients across 13 Palestinian governmental hospitals between January and December 2023. Urine cultures were the most prevalent specimen type (6916; 69.1%), followed by wound swabs (1981; 19.8%) and blood cultures (847; 8.5%). The majority of isolates were obtained from female patients (6414; 64.1%). Age distribution showed a higher prevalence of infections among children aged ≤ 9 years (2544; 25.4%) and elderly individuals aged ≥ 65 years (2379; 23.8%). Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics, including hospital distribution, microorganism types, and AMR patterns, are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

This study reveals distinct AMR profiles and culture source distributions among bacterial pathogens, highlighting the escalating challenge of multidrug resistance (MDR) in clinical settings. Escherichia coli was the most prevalent isolate (N = 4255). Non-MDR strains of E. coli comprised 77.2% of these isolates, while extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing strains exhibited a 58.3% MDR rate and were predominantly isolated from urine (51.7%) and wound swabs (15.8%). Among Gram-negative pathogens, Acinetobacter baumannii displayed the highest MDR rate (76.4%, N = 136/178), followed by Klebsiella pneumoniae ESBL (69.2%, N = 466/673) and E. coli ESBL (58.3%, N = 1306/2240), primarily from urine and wound specimens. Staphylococcus aureus, the predominant Gram-positive pathogen (N = 1295), showed a 29.5% MDR rate, with frequent isolation from wound swabs (35.9%) and blood cultures (25.9%). Pseudomonas aeruginosa (22.8% MDR, N = 125/548) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (34.9% MDR, N = 210/601) were also prevalent in these sources. Conversely, Salmonella spp. exhibited no MDR (0.0%, N = 0/30). In addition, Streptococcus pneumoniae had a low MDR rate (13.2%, N = 7/53). Detailed AMR patterns and specimen tested distributions are presented in Table 1.

Antimicrobial resistance patterns and culture sources among clinical bacterial isolates

Testing for methicillin resistance in Staphylococcus aureus was performed using cefoxitin disk diffusion (with an inhibition zone < 22 mm considered resistant) and/or MIC determination via the VITEK system using cefoxitin and oxacillin.

MDR patterns among bacterial isolates varied across the different hospitals and patient age groups. The overall MDR rate across hospitals was 36.7%, ranging from 24.0% (114/475) at Jericho Government Hospital to 64.4% (163/253) at Tubas Turkey Government Hospital. Among age groups, MDR rates ranged from 24.7% (123/497) in children aged 9.0–15.9 years to 48.4% (1,152/2,379) in patients aged ≥ 65 years, with the elderly exhibiting the highest prevalence. Full MDR and non-MDR distributions by hospital and age group are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Binary logistic regression analysis (Supplementary Table 4) revealed several significant associations between demographic and clinical variables and antimicrobial resistance. Male patients had 14% higher odds of MDR infection (adjusted OR 1.14; 95% CI 1.05–1.24; p = 0.003) compared to females. Age was a strong predictor, with children aged 2–8.9 years and 9–15.9 years having significantly lower odds of resistance (aORs = 0.69 and 0.66, respectively; p < 0.001 for both) compared to those under 2 years. In contrast, adults aged 44 years and older, particularly those aged 65 and above, showed progressively increasing odds of resistance, reaching an adjusted OR of 1.85 (95% CI 1.61–2.13; p < 0.001). A seasonal trend was observed, with December showing significantly lower odds of resistance compared to January (aOR = 0.79; 95% CI 0.65–0.97; p = 0.021). Regarding specimen types, urine cultures (adjusted OR = 1.49; 95% CI 1.27–1.75; p < 0.001), wound swabs (aOR = 1.29; 95% CI 1.09–1.53; p = 0.004), and other swabs (aOR = 1.39; 95% CI 1.01–1.89; p = 0.044) were all associated with significantly increased odds of resistance compared to blood culture. Marked variability was observed across hospitals compared to Al-Watani Hospital, with isolates from Tubas Turkey Hospital showing the highest odds (aOR = 3.41; 95% CI 2.48–4.68; p < 0.001), while significantly lower odds were found in Jericho Hospital (aOR = 0.61; 95% CI 0.46–0.81; p < 0.001) and Palestine Medical Complex (aOR = 0.81; 95% CI 0.66–1.00; p = 0.048).

Due to incomplete data across facilities, it was not possible to distinguish between community-acquired and hospital-acquired infections. As such, interpretations regarding infection source and institutional stewardship practices were made cautiously and are discussed further in the Limitations.

Discussion

This nationwide cross-sectional study, the first comprehensive assessment of AMR in Palestine’s conflict-affected governmental hospitals, analyzed over 10,000 clinical isolates from 13 facilities, revealing a critical MDR burden, particularly among Gram-negative pathogens. These findings demand urgent, context-specific interventions in this resource-limited setting.

Epidemiology of MDR pathogens

The highest MDR rate was observed in Acinetobacter baumannii (76.4%), followed by ESBL-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae (69.2%) and Escherichia coli (58.3%), consistent with WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region trends, where carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii exceeds 65% and third-generation cephalosporin resistance in K. pneumoniae surpasses 80% [5, 15]. While Jordan reports comparable carbapenem resistance in A. baumannii (79.4%), it shows lower ESBL prevalence in K. pneumoniae (19.0%), likely due to differences in surveillance methods or patient populations [16, 17]. The 100% MDR A. baumannii rate in well-resourced Saudi hospitals further illustrates the regional scope of resistance [18].

In contrast, Staphylococcus aureus showed a 29.5% MDR rate—slightly higher than in Jordan (21%) and Saudi Arabia (26.9%) [17, 19]. No MDR Salmonella isolates were detected, diverging from global trends and suggesting potential surveillance gaps, warranting further investigation using standardized, CLSI-compliant testing protocols [20, 21].

Drivers of AMR in Palestine

MDR patterns were shaped by demographic, institutional, and behavioral factors. Patients aged ≥ 65 had the highest odds of MDR infection (aOR = 1.85, 95% CI 1.61–2.13, p < 0.001), consistent with global evidence linking age to comorbidities and cumulative antibiotic exposure [22]. Hospital-level MDR rates ranged from 64.4% to 24.0%, reflecting disparities in resource allocation and antimicrobial stewardship program (ASP) implementation, as seen in other LMICs [23].

Systemic challenges further exacerbate resistance. Physicians report pressure to prescribe antibiotics for suspected viral infections, driven by patient expectations and diagnostic uncertainty in the absence of rapid testing tools [6, 24]. This issue is compounded by the widespread, unregulated availability of antibiotics without a prescription from community pharmacies, which facilitates self-medication [25]. Furthermore, significant gaps in prescriber knowledge regarding resistance and stewardship best practices continue to undermine containment efforts, highlighting a critical need for targeted educational and policy interventions [26, 27].

Implications for practice

Integrating antimicrobial stewardship into Palestine’s national health strategy is essential, particularly in its conflict-affected and fragmented health system. A dual focus is needed: one on curbing community-level misuse and another on strengthening hospital-based stewardship.

In the community, stricter regulations on non-prescription antibiotic sales—such as amoxicillin and azithromycin—must be enforced through mandatory audits and penalties. These efforts must be supported by public awareness campaigns to reduce inappropriate use for viral illnesses such as upper respiratory tract infections (URTIs) [28].

In clinical settings, hospitals should adopt antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) modeled on successful regional frameworks such as Jordan’s. Priority actions should include implementing carbapenem-sparing protocols and training providers on current CLSI guidelines. Success hinges on targeted investment in laboratory infrastructure—particularly in high-resistance areas like Tubas Turkey Government Hospital—for standardized antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and surveillance [29].

Study limitations

This study has key limitations. The retrospective design resulted in non-standardized AST panels, which prevented the formal classification of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) organisms and limited this analysis to MDR isolates. The absence of clinical metadata precluded differentiation between community- and hospital-acquired infections, limiting insights for targeted infection control. Furthermore, the use of some non-CLSI-compliant antibiotics may reduce global comparability. Finally, this analysis was restricted to positive culture isolates, as denominator data on the total number of specimens tested were not uniformly available, precluding the calculation of infection prevalence rates for specific specimen types. Future prospective studies with standardized protocols and detailed clinical data are essential to overcome these constraints.

Conclusions

This study reveals a substantial MDR burden in Palestinian governmental hospitals, disproportionately affecting elderly patients and smaller facilities. Resistance patterns varied by age, hospital, specimen type, and season, emphasizing the need for data-driven, context-specific interventions. Urgent, coordinated action—through regulatory enforcement, scaled-up ASPs, CLSI-compliant diagnostics, and public education—is critical to mitigate the AMR crisis in Palestine and similar conflict-affected settings. These findings provide a foundation for longitudinal surveillance and policy interventions to safeguard strained health systems.
