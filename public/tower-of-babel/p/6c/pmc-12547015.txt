Self-care practice and associated factors among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia: Umbrella review of systematic review and meta-analysis studies
Authors: Sefineh Fenta Feleke, Anteneh Mengist Dessie, Atitegeb Abera Kidie, Muluemebet Kassa, Tesfaye Engdaw Habtie, Natnael Amare Tesfa, Zenebe Daniel Getachew, Tadele Emagneneh, Wagaw Abebe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSelf-care practices are vital for managing high blood pressure. Four systematic reviews and meta-analysis in Ethiopia have shown inconsistent prevalence rates and varying quality scores for hypertensive self-care. This umbrella review aims to unify these findings into a comprehensive document for better comparison. It is the first of its kind in Ethiopia and seeks to support clinicians and policymakers in preventing hypertension-related complications.MethodsThis umbrella review incorporated four systematic reviews and meta-analyses identified through a literature search across PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects, specifically targeting systematic reveiw and meta-analysis) studies on hypertension self-care practices in Ethiopia. Data from the included systematic review and meta-analysis studies were extracted using a standardized data abstraction form created in an Excel spreadsheet. The quality of all relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses was assessed using the AMSTAR-2 (Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews) tool. R software version 4.3.2 was used to perform overall data synthesis and statistical analysis. The overall pooled prevalence and effect sizes were analyzed using a random-effects model.ResultsFour studies with 21,479 hypertensive patients were included. The overall pooled prevalence of good self-care practice among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia is 41 % (95 % CI 38 %, 44 %, I2 =96 %, P = 0.001). Attending formal education (OR=1.56, 95 % CI: 1.23, 2.74), good knowledge of hypertension (OR=1.98, 95 % CI: 1.45, 3.01) were significantly associated with self-care practice among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia.ConclusionsThis umbrella review revealed low self-care practices among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia, with the poorest adherence in physical activity and the highest in smoking cessation. Education and knowledge about hypertension were key influences on self-care. The review suggests promoting physical activity, targeted education, and integrating these strategies into public health policies, alongside conducting nationwide studies to improve interventions.Keywords: Ethiopia, Hypertension, Self-care practice, Umbrella review

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases cause 41 million deaths annually, accounting for 74 % of global deaths. Of these, 17 million occur before age 70, with 86 % in low- and middle-income countries. Cardiovascular diseases lead, causing 17.9 million deaths yearly, followed by cancers, chronic respiratory diseases, and diabetes. Together, these four groups account for over 80 % of premature non-communicable diseases deaths. Risk factors include tobacco use, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol consumption, unhealthy diets, and air pollution. Key responses include detection, screening, treatment, and palliative care(WHO basic facts about NC https://www.who.int/news-room/fact- sheets/detail/noncommunicable-diseases, accesed date, Augest 12, 2024).

Hypertension, a type of non-communicable disease commonly known as high blood pressure, is a chronic condition where the arterial pressure remains consistently elevated over time.(High Blood Pressure. In: High Blood Pressure Symptoms and Causes. Centere for Disease Control and Prevention.). Around 1.28 billion adults aged 30–79 worldwide are estimated to have hypertension, with two-thirds of them residing in low- and middle-income countries. Shockingly, about 46 % of adults with hypertension are unaware of their condition. Of those affected, less than half (42 %) are diagnosed and treated, and only about 21 % manage to keep it under control. Hypertension is a leading cause of premature death globally. One of the global non-communicable disease targets is to reduce the prevalence of hypertension by 33 % from 2010 to 2030. (WHO basic facts about hypertension https://www.who.int/news-room/fact- sheets/detail/hypertension, acessed date Augest, 12, 2024).

Currently, one-third of adults globally have hypertension, and by 2025, this number is expected to increase to 1.56 billion, with over 125 million of those affected residing in sub-Saharan Africa(Guwatudde et al., 2015; Tiruneh et al., 2020). A systematic review and meta-analysis from 2020 estimated that hypertension prevalence in Ethiopia is expected to rise to 21.81 percent.

Hypertension is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease contributing to stroke, heart attack, heart failure, cognitive decline, and death(Carey et al., 2018; Chia and Ching, 2014; Kaptoge et al., 2019). It is responsible for 45 % of global cardiovascular morbidity and mortality(P. K. Whelton et al., 2018). Often, hypertension remains symptomless until it leads to serious complications such as stroke, heart attack, kidney failure, or vision issues(Carey et al., 2018).

The exact cause of hypertension is unclear, but studies indicate that certain factors like excessive alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, high salt intake, smoking, and a higher body mass index can raise the risk. Managing these factors is a crucial part of self-care(Chobanian et al., 2003; Ogah and Rayner, 2013).

Effective management of hypertension involves key self-care practices such as regular exercise, maintaining a healthy weight, and adhering to a heart-healthy diet low in salt and fat. It is also critical to limit alcohol, quit smoking, take prescribed medications consistently, and attend regular check-ups with a healthcare provider(Aung et al., 2012). These measures are essential for controlling blood pressure and reducing the risk of related health complications.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends self-care activities to enhance the effectiveness of antihypertensive medications (Oza and Garcellano, 2015).

Adhering to these self-care practices assists hypertensive patients in managing their blood pressure, preventing and reducing complications, and mitigating associated morbidities, disabilities, and mortality(Amsterdam et al., 2016). Self-care practices are crucial in preventing and managing high blood pressure(Chobanian et al., 2003; McManus et al., 2010). These practices typically involve maintaining a healthy body weight (with a Body Mass Index of 18.5 to 24.9) and waist circumference, adhering to prescribed medications, engaging in moderate-intensity physical activity for 30–60 min on 4–7 days a week, following a healthy diet (including low salt intake, moderate alcohol consumption, and quitting smoking), and managing stress effectively(Lamb et al., 2018; Mancia et al., 2014).

Various factors influence adherence to self-care activities, such as socio-economic status, age, gender, education level, place of residence, co-morbidities, access to healthcare, health literacy, duration of treatment, culture, social support, self-efficacy, sources of self-care information, and knowledge of the disease and its treatment(Beigi et al., 2014; Hu et al., 2013; Labata et al., 2019; S. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016).

Various single studies conducted across Ethiopia reported regional estimates of good self-care practices among hypertensive patients, with prevalence ranging from 20.3 %(Gebremichael et al., 2018) to 68.9 %(Tiruneh et al., 2020).

To date, numerous systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Edmealem et al., 2022; Tadesse and Gerensea, 2021; Wondmieneh et al., 2021) have reported inconsistent prevalence rates of hypertensive self-care practices in Ethiopia, ranging from 36.98 % (Edmealem et al., 2022) to 44 % (Tadesse and Gerensea, 2021), with varying quality scores. Similarly, there is conflicting information regarding the impact of different factors on hypertensive self-care practices.

The decision to examine self-care practices for hypertension solely in Ethiopia was driven by a glaring absence of national level data: although several studies had explored self-care behaviors in regions like Oromia, Amhara, Tigray, Harari, Southern Nation and nationality of people region, and Addis Ababa, no evidence was available from Afar, Benishangul Gumuz, Somali, Gambella, or Dire Dawa.

The goal of this umbrella review was to consolidate the diverse findings from four systematic review and meta-analysesinto a single, comprehensive document that allows for comparison and contrast of the results. To the best of the authors' knowledge, this is the first umbrella review focusing on hypertensive self-care practices and their predictors in Ethiopia. The evidence gathered from this review aims to assist clinicians and policymakers in preventing hypertension- related complications.

Methods

This umbrella review was carried out following the methodology for reviewing multiple systematic reviews(Aromataris et al., 2015). It involved systematically synthesizing the eligible systematic review and meta-analysis reports on hypertension self-care practices and their predictors in Ethiopia. The protocol has been registered on the PROSPERO-International Prospective Register of systematic reviews under registration number CRD42024579606.

Search strategy

Between June 1 and 30, 2024, SFF and NAT conducted electronic searches across five international online databases PubMed, ScienceDirect, Web of Science, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, and the Database of Abstracts of Reviews of Effects seeking systematic review and meta-analyses on hypertension self-care practices in Ethiopia. The search strategy was designed using the Population Intervention Comparison and Outcome (PICO) framework. The search terms included a combination of free text and subject headings, along with appropriate Boolean operators. During the systematic search, the following terms were used: ((“self-care behavior”) OR “self-care adherence”) OR “self-care” OR “dietary practice” OR “weight management” OR “physical activity” OR “medication adherence”) AND “hypertensive patients”) OR “patients with hypertension”) AND “Systematic Review” OR “Meta-Analysis” AND “Ethiopia).

Population: Adult population who had primary or secondary hypertension.

Intervention: Adherence to all components of self-care for hypertensive patients: medication adherence, physical activity, low-salt diet, smoking abstinence, alcohol drinking abstinence, and weight management.

Comparison: Systematic reviews and meta-analysis were included irrespective of whether their primary studies had controls or not.

Outcomes: Variation in the proportion of a target population who undergo hypertension self-care practice and determinants of it.

Study design: Only systematic reviews and meta-analysis studies were included.

Study setting: Ethiopia

Eligibility criteria

All eligible systematic reviews and meta-analyses that utilized observational study designs (including cross-sectional, cohort, and case-control studies) to assess the prevalence of self-care practices and associated factors among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia were included. The predefined eligibility criteria were as follows: population adults; exposure risk factors or predictors of hypertension self-care practices; study area research conducted in Ethiopia; study design all systematic review and meta-analysis ; publication status both published and unpublished studies; and language studies reported in English. There were no restrictions on the publication dates of the systematic review and meta-analyses We excluded narrative reviews, editorials, correspondence, abstracts, methodological studies, and literature reviews that lacked a clear research topic, search strategy, or article selection method. Additionally, systematic review and meta-analyses that did not report the prevalence of self-care practices and associated factors among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia were excluded.

Measure of outcomes

In this umbrella review, the primary outcome is a self-care practice among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia. Self-care practice was assessed using Hypertension Self-Care Activity Level Effects (H-SCALE), which includes medication adherence, dietary management, smoking status, physical activity, weight management and alcohol intake. H-SCALE has provided a detailed description of the scoring method of each component (Shea et al., 2017). Good hypertension self-care adherence is described as adherence to all components of self-care for hypertensive patients (De Santis et al., 2023; Pieper et al., 2021). The second outcome was assessing the associated factor of self-care practice among hypertensive patients.

Medication adherence

Three items assessed the number of days in the last week that an individual takes medication, at the recommended dosage, and at the same time. Responses were summed (range 0–21). Score=21 was considered adherent.

Low-salt diet: six items assessed practices related to eating a healthy diet. A mean score is calculated. Scores of 6 or better were considered adherent.

Physical Exercise adherence: This included respondents who reported having exercised for 30min/day, at least three times per week.

Weight management: Seven items, strongly disagree (1) to strongly agree (5), assessed weight management. Responses were summed, creating a range of scores from 7 to 35. Scores of ≥28 were considered adherent to weight management practices.

Smoking cessation/ Quitting: This included respondents who reported having never smoked or stopped smoking.

Adherence to alcohol abstinence: Respondents who reported having never consumed alcohol.

Study screening and selection

To identify systematic reviews and meta-analyses that met the inclusion criteria, all search results were imported into the EndNote X20 citation management system, and duplicate articles were removed. Following this, publications were screened based on their titles and abstracts, and the full texts of the selected articles were thoroughly evaluated. The publications were reviewed independently in duplicate by SFF and TEH. Any disagreements among the reviewers concerning the inclusion of specific studies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer, AAK.

Data extraction

Data from the included systematic review and meta-analyses studies were extracted using a standardized data abstraction form created in an Excel spreadsheet. For each systematic review and meta-analysis study, the following information was collected: (a) identification details (first author's last name and publication year), (b) review objective, (c) prevalence or proportion of birth asphyxia, (d) risk factors for hypertension self-care practices, (e) odds ratios or relative risks with 95 % confidence intervals for these risk factors, (f) the number and design type of primary studies included in each systematic review and meta-analysis, (g) the total sample size, (h) methods and scores for assessing publication bias, (i) methods and scores for quality assessment, (j) data synthesis approach (random or fixed-effects model), and (k) the authors' main conclusions from the systematic review and meta-analysis study.

Quality assessment of the systematic review and meta-analyzed studies

The quality of all relevant systematic reviews and meta-analyses was assessed using the AMSTAR-2 (Assessment of Multiple Systematic Reviews) tool(Pieper et al., 2021). The tool consists of 16 items, divided into seven critical domains and nine non-critical domains. The critical domains encompass the registration of the review protocol before initiation, the adequacy of the literature search, the rationale for excluding certain studies, the risk of bias in the included studies, the suitability of meta-analytical methods, consideration of bias risk when interpreting findings, and the assessment of publication bias and its potential impact. The instrument's responses were categorized as "Yes," "Partial Yes," "No," or "No Meta-analysis Conducted." Two authors independently scored the 16 questions for each included systematic review and meta-analys is study. Any scoring disagreements were settled by a third reviewer. The overall findings of this umbrella review were rated as high, moderate, low, or critically low using AMSTAR-2(De Santis et al., 2023; Matthias et al., 2020; Pieper et al., 2021) (Supplementary Table 1).

High: no major flaw in critical domains or one non-critical weakness items.

Moderate: no major flaw in critical domains or more than one flaw in non-critical items.

Low: One flaw in critical items with or without non-critical weaknesses.

Critically low: More than one flaw in critical items with or without non-critical weaknesses.

We used the following criteria to manage overlap between reviews. When CCA was >11 %, preference was given to the review that met the following criteria: (i) had highest AMSTAR 2 rating and the rating was moderate or higher; (ii) was most recent; had conducted a meta-analysis or provided pooled summary estimates; and (iii) had the greatest sample size (including both the number of studies and participants); where CCA was <10 %, all reviews were retained and the findings compared(Pollock et al., 2019a).

Data analysis

R software version 4.3.2 was (metaumbrella package)Gosling CJ, Solanes A, Fusar-Poli P, Radua J: Metaumbrella: the first comprehensive suite to perform data analysis in umbrella reviews with stratification of the evidence. BMJ Ment Health 2023, 26(1). used to perform overall data synthesis and statistical analysis. We summarized the range of estimates of the prevalence and predictors of hypertension self-care practice reported in each systematic review and meta-analysis study. A narrative synthesis was used to present the findings of the included systematic review and meta-analyses , followed by an overall meta-analysis. The overall pooled prevalence and effect sizes were analyzed using a random-effects model. A meta-analysis of systematic review and meta-analyses was conducted using the recommendations of Higgins et al.’s(Higgins and Thompson, 2002) statistic (I2 = 75/100 % or higher, indicating significant heterogeneity). To address bias, comprehensive searches were carried out, and collaborative efforts among the authors were pivotal in maintaining objectivity and adhering to clear criteria during article selection, quality assessment, and data extraction. Qualitative scrutiny of the funnel plot ((Cherubini et al.) package, the funnel.meta) graph was employed to explore potential publication bias(Kossmeier et al., 2019). Furthermore, Egger's correlation tests were applied at a 5 % significance level to detect any presence of publication bias.

Result

Study screening and selection

A total of 1694 records were identified through database searching, of which 1689 were excluded—583 due to duplications and 1107 after title and abstract screening leaving 4 studies that were assessed for full-text review, all of which were included in the umbrella review. As a result, we included a total of 4 systematic review and meta-analyses(Abate et al., 2022; Edmealem et al., 2022; Tadesse and Gerensea, 2021; Wondmieneh et al., 2021)in this umbrella review after conducting a full-text assessment of the remaining articles. Discussion

At present, clinical researchers frequently utilize systematic reviews and meta-analyses to address the majority of clinical and epidemiological inquiries. However, there are times when researchers need to explore evidence on multiple questions within a specific topic. In such cases, umbrella reviews, which are systematic reviews of systematic review and meta-analyses, can be a suitable approach.

To our knowledge, this umbrella review is the first to evaluate self-care practices and their associated factors among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia. As of now, there are four systematic review and meta-analyses that report on self-care practices and their associated factors among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia.

The umbrella review revealed that the pooled prevalence of good self-care practices among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia was 41 % (95 % CI: 38 %–44 %, I² = 96 %, P = 0.001). Which is lower than study conducted in Saudi Arabia (74.4 %)(Bakhsh et al., 2017), Southwest Nigeria (61.2 %)(Ayodapo et al., 2020), a nationwide population-based study in Korea at 72.5 % (Ayodapo et al., 2020) and Ghana (72 %) (Obirikorang et al., 2018) but higher than study conducted in Kenya (13 %)(Mwenda, 2019), multicenter study conducted in China (32.9 %)(Qu et al., 2019). This variation could potentially be explained by differences in socioeconomic status, cultural practices, educational levels, access to healthcare facilities, and the lifestyles of the study participants. Furthermore, methodological differences and variations in the components of the hypertension self-care practice assessment tool may also have contributed.

The greater adherence to healthy lifestyle behaviors among Ghanaians can be attributed to the healthcare system's focus on task-shifting strategies for hypertension control(Iwelunmor et al., 2019).

Medication adherence is crucial for managing blood pressure in hypertensive patients. According to this umbrella review, the overall estimate of medication adherence among these patients is 63 % (with a 95 % confidence interval of 58 % to 67 %). This finding is lower than the adherence rates reported in previous studies conducted in Saudi Arabia (83.7 %)(Bakhsh et al., 2017), Uganda (79.5 %)(Tusubira et al., 2020), America in 2015 (69 %) (Chang et al., 2019)and Korea (81.7 %)(Choi et al., 2018). However, the finding from this study was higher compared to a study conducted in Southern Iran(S. F. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016), which reported an adherence rate of 36.1 %, Egypt (46.12 %)(Hussein et al., 2020), Palestine (45.8 %) (Al-Ramahi and health, 2015) and India (54 %)(Chacko and Jeemon, 2020). The difference in findings may be attributed to variations in the socioeconomic status of the participants. Additionally, methodological differences between this study and the others could account for the discrepancy. For instance, the study in Southern Iran focused on participants aged 30–90 years, excluding those under 30, unlike this study. Different tools used to assess medication adherence across studies might also contribute to the variation. Consequently, enhancing adherence to antihypertensive medications is essential to reduce complications associated with uncontrolled hypertension.

In this umbrella review, the subgroup analysis revealed that adherence to weight management was 51 % (with a range of 48 % to 54 %). This finding was in line with the result from a study conducted in Iran(S. F. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016), which reported an adherence rate of 39 % and in Nepal (48.6 %)(Satyal et al., 2020). A study involving African American hypertensive patients found that only 30.1 %(Warren-Findlow and Seymour, 2011) of participants engaged in weight reduction practices. Conversely, this finding was lower than the rate reported in Kenya, which was 90.5 %(Mwenda, 2019), Uganda (75.6 %)(Tusubira et al., 2020). On the other hand, this finding is higher than a study conducted in China (34.8 %) (Qu et al., 2019) and Southern Iran (39.2 %)(S. F. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016). The current finding is significantly higher than a study conducted in India, which reported a rate of 11.4 %(Chacko and Jeemon, 2020). This variation could be attributed to cultural differences. Additionally, there are methodological differences, as this study examined hypertensive self-care practices using a large sample size of 10,493 participants, whereas the studies mentioned above were conducted in specific locations with smaller sample sizes. Therefore, weight loss is among the most effective lifestyle modifications for managing blood pressure, with a reduction of one kilogram leading to a decrease of one millimeter of mercury (mmHg) in blood pressure (N. M. Kaplan, “Obesity and weight reduction,” 2015, http://www.uptodate.com/home.).

In this umbrella review's subgroup analysis, nearly three-fourths 74 % (95 % CI: 69, 79) of the hypertensive patients adhered to the recommended practice of abstaining from alcohol consumption. This finding is lower compared to the results of studies conducted in Nepal (84 %) (Worku Kassahun et al., 2020)and Iran (86.7 %)(S. F. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016), but higher than that of a study conducted in Kenya (49.5 %)(Mwenda, 2019). The American College of Cardiology advises that men should limit their daily alcohol intake to no more than two drinks, while women should limit theirs to one drink per day(W. J. J. A. C. C. Whelton, 2017). Lowering alcohol consumption reduces blood pressure in individuals who consume more than two drinks per day. Thus, cutting back on alcohol is crucial for lessening the impact of hypertension.

In this finding, subgroup analysis showed that adherence to the recommended non-smoking practice among hypertensive patients was 89 %, with a 95 % confidence interval of 87 % to 92 %. This finding is higher than a study conducted in Nepal (84.9 %) (Satyal et al., 2020), China (72.6 %)(Qu et al., 2019)and Saudi Arabia (31.2 %) (Bakhsh et al., 2017) but consistent with a study conducted in Israel (87 %)(Hava Tabenkin et al., 2011). The result of subgroup analysis revealed that 11 % of respondents continue to smoke cigarettes.

This finding is lower than a study conducted Uganda (98.7 %)(Tusubira et al., 2020). The difference could potentially be explained by socioeconomic disparities. Smoking cigarettes causes blood vessels to constrict and increases heart activity. Therefore, hypertensive patients should quit smoking to better manage their blood pressure.

In this umbrella review, 57 % of patients with hypertension adhered to a low-salt diet, with the 95 % confidence interval ranging from 50 % to 63 %. This finding is lower than those reported in studies conducted in Uganda (75.6 %)(Tusubira et al., 2020) and Saudi Arabia (79.3 %)(Bakhsh et al., 2017), but it aligns with the results of a study conducted in Kenya (63 %)(Mwenda et al., 2019). In contrast, the findings from this study were higher than those from a study conducted in Iran, which reported 12.3 %(S. F. Z. Motlagh et al., 2016) and Korean study (<20 %)(Kim and Kong, 2015). Evidence from the Cochrane Library indicates that reducing dietary sodium intake from 11.59 g per day to 3.89 g per day leads to a 3 % decrease in blood pressure among hypertensive patients(Graudal et al., 2020). Reducing daily salt consumption by 5 g lowers the risk of cardiovascular mortality by 17 % and stroke mortality by 23 %(Organization, 2017). Thus, reducing salt in food preparation and minimizing or avoiding salty foods may help lower both mortality and morbidity rates in individuals with hypertension.

In this umbrella review, the combined prevalence of good adherence to physical exercise was 45 % (with a 95 % confidence interval of 45 % to 49 %). Over half of hypertensive patients did not adhere to the recommended levels of physical activity. This finding is consistent with a study conducted in Nepal (44.8 %)(Satyal et al., 2020), and in the United States (52.2 %)(Warren-Findlow and Seymour, 2011). However, this finding is higher than the result of a study conducted in Saudi Arabia (20.1 %)(Elbur, 2015). On the other hand, it is lower than a study conducted in Uganda (63.1 %) (Tusubira et al., 2020) and Kenya (67 %)(Mwenda et al., 2019). The discrepancy might be explained by differences in the study populations. The study conducted in Uganda involved participants from a rural area, where people are more likely to engage in physical activity through daily tasks like farming. Aerobic exercise is an effective method for reducing blood pressure in patients with hypertension(Cao et al., 2019). Therefore, engaging in regular aerobic exercise for 30 min on 5–7 days per week can be important for both preventing and treating hypertension(Unger et al., 2020).

In this umbrella review, good knowledge of hypertension and the educational status of hypertensive patients were significant factors influencing hypertensive self-care practices in Ethiopia. Patients with formal education are 56 % more likely to engage in hypertension self-care compared to those without formal education (AOR = 1.56, 95 % CI: 1.23, 2.74). This finding is consistent with studies conducted in the Ashanti region of Ghana (Obirikorang et al., 2018) and West Bengal, India (Unger et al., 2020). A study in Nepal found that less-educated hypertensive patients were less likely to adhere to self- care practices compared to their more educated counterparts(Satyal et al., 2020). A possible explanation could be that educated individuals are able to comprehend information that is beneficial for hypertension self-care by reading, and they are likely to understand the recommended lifestyle changes needed to prevent hypertension and its complications(Ademe et al., 2019; Hussen et al., 2020). Therefore, improving education has a significant impact on increasing self-care practices among individuals with hypertension.

The findings from this umbrella review indicate that patients with a good knowledge of hypertension are 98 % more likely to engage in effective self-care practices compared to those with counterpart (AOR = 1.98, 95 % CI: 1.45, 3.01). Similar results were found in research conducted in Israel(Hava Tabenkin et al., 2011), Saudi Arabia (Bakhsh et al., 2017). This is because understanding the nature of hypertension encourages health-seeking behaviors, fosters the development of preventive, therapeutic, and rehabilitative strategies, and enhances the ability to apply knowledge in practice (Barr et al., 2014; Cohn et al., 2012; Dasgupta et al., 2017).

Strength and limitations

The risk of bias was mitigated by conducting a thorough search across multiple databases and having two researchers independently select studies, with a third researcher involved to resolve any disagreements. Additionally, the risk of bias in the systematic review and meta-analyses was evaluated using the AMSTAR-2 tool. Primary research within the systematic review and meta-analyses reports was also mapped to identify data overlap among the included studies. However, despite these strengths, summarizing data from multiple meta-analyses that include overlapping primary studies could potentially overestimate the strength of the findings. Additionally, these studies have limitations that readers should consider. For instance, only English-language articles were included, and the number of studies in the current review was quite limited, which could influence the final findings. Furthermore, the overall heterogeneity of the studies was significant, and we were unable to manage it using various techniques. Therefore, caution is advised when interpreting and applying these findings.

Conclusions

In this umbrella review, the pooled estimate of good self-care practice among hypertensive patients was low. Among self-care practices for hypertensive patients, the lowest adherence was observed in physical activity, whereas the highest adherence was noted in smoking cessation. In Ethiopia, participants' educational background and knowledge about hypertension were significantly linked to self-care practices among individuals living with hypertension. To improve self-care among hypertensive patients, increase efforts to promote physical activity and develop targeted educational programs. Ensure that these strategies are incorporated into public health policies and are accessible. Conduct nationwide studies to identify the determinants of physical-exercise adherence among hypertensive patients in Ethiopia as part of their self-care, and develop targeted interventions based on these findings.
