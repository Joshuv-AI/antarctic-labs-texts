The agenda-setting process for urban family physician program within Iran’s health insurance
Authors: Amirhossein Fatahpour, Hamed Tasdighi, Mostafa Amini-Rarani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and objectiveSince 2005, health policymakers in Iran have focused on the rural family physician plan to minimize unnecessary specialist referrals and enhance both efficiency and equitable access to services. In September 2021, the urban family physician plan was launched, engaging various organizations, including insurance companies. A trustee was appointed to manage the implementation of the Iranian health insurance program. This study examines how Iran Health Insurance prioritized the urban family physician program on its agenda by applying Kingdon’s agenda-setting model.Materials and methodsThis descriptive qualitative study gathered data through semi-structured interviews with 13 stakeholders in Iran’s urban family physician program, including planners, policymakers, and implementers. Purposive sampling, followed by snowball sampling, was used until data saturation was reached. Directed content analysis was applied, guided by Kingdon’s multiple streams framework.ResultsThree themes emerged: problem, policy, and political streams. The problem stream for Iran’s family physician plan included structural challenges (such as incomplete implementation, physician shortages, fragmented health data systems, poor stakeholder coordination), social-cultural barriers (including low public awareness, resistance to change), and economic issues (such as high treatment costs, inadequate insurance coverage). The policy stream proposed solutions such as electronic systems, standardized protocols, new technologies, expanded education, and improved physician working conditions. Strong government and parliamentary backing were identified as critical to addressing these challenges, emphasizing the need for sustained commitment and holistic strategies.Discussion and conclusionThe WHO, Iranian Parliament, Ministry of Health, and health insurers can serve as policy entrepreneurs to advance the family physician program within Iran’s health insurance system. The WHO can leverage global health data to emphasize Iran’s healthcare challenges, while the Ministry of Health and insurers can pinpoint issues via national research. The Parliament can facilitate public voices to gather input from citizens and experts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-23359-y.

Introduction

Health access is a fundamental right, recognized in numerous legal frameworks worldwide vital for societal well-being [1]. Governments strive to ensure equitable access to healthcare services, acknowledging their responsibility to safeguard public health. In Iran, Principle 29 of the Constitution enshrines healthcare as a basic right for all citizens [2], servig as a cornerstone of the nation’s health policy [3, 4] This principle underscores the importance of creating systems that ensure access to health services, particularly in resource-constrained settings, while promoting social equity [4].

The family physician program, a cornerstone of healthcare systems worldwide, prioritizes primary care and optimizes referrals. Originating in the mid-20th century, it has been globally adapted to enhance service quality, access, and cost control [5, 6].

Iran began adopting this model in the 1960s, introducing the rural family physician program in 2005 to enhance service equity and efficiency. Despite its success, the urban program, piloted in 2012, encountered boader implementation challenges [7–9].

Healthcare reforms in Iran frequently face political disputes, conflicting interests, and unscientific interventions. After the 2014 Health System Transformation Plan temporarily sidelined family physician efforts, the program’s expansion restarted in 2021, prioritizing a structured referral system and assigning Iran Health Insurance as the urban family physician program’s lead implementer. Through strategic purchasing and oversight, it seeks to improve healthcare access, equity, and efficiency [10–12].

This study analyzes the agenda-setting process of Iran’s urban family physician program under its health insurance system through Kingdon’s multiple streams model. It investigates how Iran Health Insurance identified and prioritized the initiative, examining factors and mechanisms that shaped its policy inclusion. The analysis aims to inform policy-making and enhance healthcare quality and access via targeted reforms.

Materials and methods

To explore the agenda-setting process for the urban family physician program within Iran Health Insurance, a qualitative descriptive approach [13] was employed. The research was conducted from May 2023 to June 2024.

The Kingdon model is widely regarded for effectively explaining the intricate dynamics of agenda-setting. It emphasizes the critical roles of timing, strategic opportunities, and the interplay between problem, policy, and political streams in shaping policy outcomes.

Participants

Participants were chosen via purposive sampling (Table 1), targeting individuals with expertise in Iran Health Insurance’s family physician program execution, ≥ 3 years of family medicine experience, and representation across local, provincial, and national levels. Snowball sampling supplemented recruitment.

Data collection

Semi-structured interviews were conducted until data saturation was reached, with data collectiona halting after 13 interviews as no new themes or insights emerged. The sample comprised 5 local, 4 provincial and 2 national-level participants, including family physicians, health insurance managers, and health management faculty, predominantly with ≥ 5 years of experience. Interview questions were designed using Kingdon’s multiple streams model, aligned with Iran Health Insurance’s urban family physician program objectives [15].

1) Problem stream: What problems prompted the implementation of the urban family physician program within Iran Health Insurance?

2) Policy stream: What solutions have been identified to address the problems related to the urban family physician program in Iran Health Insurance? 3) Political stream: What political factors/wills influenced the acceptance of the urban family physician program within Iran Health Insurance?

The questions were designed to be open-ended and follow-up in nature to facilitate deeper discussions. A general framework for the interviews was established, outlining key topics; based on participants’ responses during the interviews allowing new questions to emerge and ensuring all aspects of the urban family physician program were addressed. A final guide was created, including the purpose, a list of questions, and important notes for conducting the interviews (Appendix 1). Follow-up questions were crafted based on participants’ responses, maintaining an unbiased and comfortable atmosphere. Interviews took place in quiet, private settings (e.g., offices or preferred locations). This approach was intended to foster trust and encourage open dialogue. Active listening, empathy,, and adaptable communication styles ensured participants felt respected and at ease. Sessions began with neutral topics to ease tension, gradually transitioning to specific questions to encourage candid dialogue.

Interviews were recorded digitally, accompanied by handwritten notes, and lasted 45–70 min. Data accuracy was ensured by cross-checking notes with participants and integrating their feedback. A qualitative research expert (MAR) oversaw the process.

Analysis

A directed qualitative content analysis approach identified, classified, and coded themes within the transcribed data [16]. Each interview was systematically reviewed using Kingdon’s framework (problem, policy, political streams). after data collection, the information was deductively coded, categorized, and labeled. Themes were identified, merged, and refined to reflect recurring patterns. Sub-themes were derived to examine dimensions in depth, Codes were assigned to specific aspects and further integrated for greater precision. To enhance the validity and consistency of the themes identified during our qualitative analysis, we implemented several methodological strategies:

1) Peer Review and Debriefing: Throughout the analysis process, multiple members of the research team independently coded the interview transcripts. We then convened regular meetings to discuss the emerging codes and themes. This collaborative approach allowed us to compare interpretations, resolve discrepancies, and reach a consensus on the classification of data. The peer debriefing sessions enhanced the reliability of our findings by minimizing individual biases.

2) Audit Trail: An audit trail was meticulously maintained throughout the research process. This included detailed documentation of all data collection procedures, coding decisions, theme development, and reflective notes.

3) Reflexivity: The research team engaged in reflexive practices by continually reflecting on our assumptions, beliefs, and potential influences on the data interpretation.

4) Triangulation: We employed data triangulation by collecting perspectives from a diverse group of stakeholders involved in the urban family physician program, including policymakers, healthcare providers, and administrators. By comparing and contrasting the views from different participant categories, we were able to identify consistent patterns and verify the robustness of the themes across various contexts.

5) Use of Established Analytical Framework: We utilized Kingdon’s multiple streams model as a guiding framework for the directed content analysis. Employing this well-established theoretical model provided a structured approach to theme development and ensured consistency in how data were interpreted and categorized.

MAXQDA Analytics Pro 2020 (Release 20.0.8, VERBI GmbH Berlin) managed and organized transcribed interview data. Interviews were transcribed, converted to text, and imported into Microsoft Word. Using MAXQDA, data were systematically coded, assigning labels to text segments for categorization. Through pattern analysis, three core themes emerged, later refined into hierarchical sub-codes reflecting content interdependencies.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the trustworthiness of the study, the criteria proposed by Lincoln and Guba were applied [17, 18]. Credibility was enhanced through a 13-month process of data collection and analysis. Interview notes informed coding, and in-text quotes enhanced transferability by linking findings to external validity. Program descriptions supported broader applicability. An auditing process involving an external expert ensured dependability through cross-checking and consensus-building. Conformability was maintained by minimizing researcher biases and anchoring results in data-driven insights.

Potential biases

We identified specific biases inherent in qualitative studies and took deliberate steps to mitigate them:

1) selection bias: Given the inherent selection bias risks of purposive and snowball sampling, we strategically diversified participants across roles (policymakers, administrators, physicians, coordinators) to ensure broad stakeholder representation and reduce bias in capturing program insights.

2) Researcher Bias: Researchers’ preconceptions and perspectives could influence data interpretation, potentially skewing results. So, we engaged in reflexivity throughout the research process and we employed multiple coders during data analysis to cross-verify findings.

3) Social Desirability Bias: Participants might provide responses they perceive as favorable or acceptable rather than their true opinions. We assured participants of confidentiality and the non-evaluative nature of the study. The interviewer established rapport by creating a safe and trusting environment, emphasizing that honest and candid insights were valued over socially desirable responses.

4) Recall Bias: Participants might have difficulty accurately recalling past events or experiences relevant to the program’s implementation. We used probing questions and encouraged participants to provide specific examples and anecdotes. Where possible, we cross-referenced participant accounts with existing documents and records to validate the information provided.

Themes, subthemes and codes related to urban family Doctor program in Iran health insurance

Based on Kingdon’s multiple streams model, the factors influencing the implementation of the urban family physician program within Iran’s health insurance system were categorized into three main streams: Problem Stream, Policy Stream, and Political Stream.

Problem stream

Participants identified several issues within Iran’s health system that underscore the necessity for a family physician program. These problems are categorized into the following themes:

Structural and operational problems

a. Incomplete Implementation of the Family Physician Program and Physician Shortage.

The family physician program has not yet been fully implemented nationwide, remaining a pilot in provinces like Fars and Mazandaran. This incomplete implementation leads to inequitable accessto health and medical services across the country.

Interviewee 6: “In some regions and cities of Iran, access to medical services is limited. This issue can be problematic for individuals who require care and treatment.”

Interviewee 6: “In some regions and cities of Iran, access to medical services is limited. This issue can be problematic for individuals who require care and treatment.”

Participants highlighted that, according to international standards, Iran requires approximately 28,000 to 30,000 family physicians to adequately serve its population. While there are about 110,000 general practitioners in the country, unequal distribution and insufficient training in family medicine contribute to service gaps.

b. Unequal Distribution of Physicians and Health Services.

The disparity in physician distribution reduces the quality of health services, particularly in underserved regions. Participants noted that the lack of an effective referral system exacerbates access issues.

Interviewee 13: “Services are not categorized, and individuals visit a pulmonologist for simple cold treatment, leading to wasted time and costs, as well as ineffective specialized services.”

Interviewee 13: “Services are not categorized, and individuals visit a pulmonologist for simple cold treatment, leading to wasted time and costs, as well as ineffective specialized services.”

Interviewee 13: “Services are not categorized, and individuals visit a pulmonologist for simple cold treatment, leading to wasted time and costs, as well as ineffective specialized services.”

c. Absence of a Comprehensive and Integrated Health Information System.

Fragmented health data systems impede timely access to patient records, raising misdiagnosis risks and compromising treatment. Participants stressed that without integrated electronic records across centers, care quality declines and medical errors escalate.

d. Lack of Access to Data for Decision-Making and Planning.

Effective service allocation relies on accurate data (e.g., addresses, disease types, healthcare usage, regional needs), whose absence drives inefficiencies. Participants linked physician shortages to high workloads, low pay, and poor conditions, creating regional imbalances—underserved areas versus doctor surpluses elsewhere.

e. Instability in Policy-Making.

Policy instability was identified as a significant challenge. Frequent changes in government perspectives lead to inconsistencies in implementing the family physician program.

Participants noted that despite existing laws mandating the program, adherence is lacking:

Interviewee 4: “Each new government has different perspectives on the family physician plan, often overlooking previous programs and policies. This instability prevents sustainable implementation.”

f. Lack of Necessary Alignment among Stakeholders.

Persistent stakeholder coordination gaps especially between the Ministry of Health, insurers, and other entities undermine healthcare delivery. Participants linked service disruptions to ambiguous referral criteria and medical service definitions, compromising care quality.

A significant issue highlighted was the low level of public awareness regarding the family physician program and the referral system. Many participants noted that the general public has limited understanding of the services provided by family physicians.

This distrust could originate from limited awareness of family physicians’ responsibilities or past negative interactions. Participants emphasized public education and better stakeholder collaboration as critical steps to build trust and foster a supportive family medicine environment.

c. Resistance to Change.

Resistance to change emerged as a major challenge within the health system. Many individuals have become accustomed to directly consulting specialists when faced with health issues, a practice deeply ingrained in the medical culture.

Specialists and private-sector providers fear the program could lower patient traffic and earnings, driving reluctance to embrace family medicine practices.

Participants noted that this process not only imposes financial burdens but also causes stress and reduces patient satisfaction with medical services.

b. Lack of Financial Resources.

A central challenge identified was the financial resource deficit in health insurance and healthcare system support. Participants stressed that adequate funding is critical for quality care, yet Iran’s economic constraints and unsustainable funding mechanisms have resulted in marked shortfalls.

Interviewee 5:“The financial shortfall negatively affects both the process and quality of healthcare service delivery.”

c. Inadequate Insurance Coverage.

Inadequate insurance coverage for many therapeutic services restricts patient access and drives dissatisfaction. Participants stressed expanding coverage to ensure equitable healthcare access.

Interviewee 3:“This situation underscores the necessity of establishing a comprehensive and efficient family physician system in Iran that can better address the community’s health needs.”

They argued this system would effectively evaluate needs and gaps, pinpoint viable solutions, and improve nationwide access to quality healthcare.

Information and education solutions

Participants emphasized educational and informational strategies as vital for enhancing public understanding of the family physician program and facilitating its implementation. These solutions are categorized into the following themes:

a. Enhancing Public Awareness.

Participants highlighted the need to increase public information and awareness about family physicians and the benefits of utilizing their services.

Interviewee 4: “Increasing public information and awareness about family physicians and the benefits of utilizing their services can promote the use of these services. This can be achieved through providing information at health centers, hospitals, and other treatment facilities, as well as utilizing social media, advertising, and awareness campaigns.”

b. Conducting Workshops and Training Sessions.

Organizing training sessions and workshops for patients and their families was suggested to familiarize them with the role of family physicians and the services offered. These sessions would enable participants to understand how to collaborate effectively with family physicians during healthcare processes.

c. Developing Informational and Educational Materials.

Creating brochures, posters, and digital content—including videos and articles—that explain family physician services and guide the public on proper utilization. Such materials would serve as resources to enhance citizens’ understanding of the advantages and processes associated with family physicians.

d. Utilizing Social Media Platforms.

Designing and launching informational campaigns on various social media platforms to share useful information, positive experiences, and success stories related to family physicians. Participants believed these campaigns could help people recognize the importance of family physicians in improving public health.

e. Establishing Consultation Services.

Developing telephone or online consultation systems where individuals can ask questions and receive accurate information about family physicians. This system could include specialized consultations allowing direct communication with experts and physicians, assisting users in making informed health decisions.

f. Training for Healthcare Providers.

Conduct workshops and specialized seminars for healthcare providers to clarify the family physician program’s benefits and operational processes. These initiatives seek to enhance cross-sector collaboration and equip stakeholders with the knowledge required for effective program implementation.

g. Addressing Physicians’ Concerns.

Participants emphasized the critical need to recognize and resolve physicians’ program-related concerns, asserting that addressing their feedback is essential for improving program outcomes and building trust with management.

Financing solutions

Discussion

Policymakers and health officials must secure comprehensive and sustainable support to elevate issues onto the policy agenda. While problems and solutions exist within political contexts, broad-based backing is a critical factor in gaining policy traction. Kingdon’s multiple streams framework has proven effective in analyzing health programs, including the urban family physician program [19]. This study utilized the framework to examine Iran’s family physician program within the health insurance system, focusing on the problem, policy, and political streams. The findings demonstrate the utility of such frameworks in clarifying how health issues influence policymaking.

Iran’s healthcare system faces substantial challenges that highlight the necessity of family physicians. Structural issues—such as uneven physician distribution, insufficient training, and the lack of an integrated health information system—have resulted in inequitable access to healthcare services [20, 21]. Policy instability and poor coordination between the Ministry of Health and insurance organizations exacerbate these problems, leading to inefficiencies in service delivery. Social and cultural factors further strain resources. Limited public awareness about family physicians and the referral system, coupled with negative perceptions of primary healthcare, restrict service utilization. Many patients prefer specialists over family physicians, perceiving the latter’s services as inferior due to lack of information or past experiences. This resistance to change hinders the adoption of family medicine practices. Economic inefficiencies compound these challenges. Patients often incur high treatment costs due to long-distance travel for care, wasting time and resources. Insufficient insurance coverage for essential services exacerbates financial strain on households [22, 23].

Within Kingdon’s framework, the policy stream plays a critical role in shaping health policies through public opinion and interest group pressures [24]. To effectively implement the family physician program, specific actions are needed:

Governance Reforms: Major overhauls in education and training are essential to ensure physicians and healthcare providers fully understand and embrace the principles of family medicine. Updating curricula to emphasize primary care and community health can improve care quality and strengthen doctor-patient relationships [25].Financial Incentives: Attracting physicians to family medicine requires competitive salaries, better working conditions, and access to modern equipment. Financial incentives and special arrangements for young physicians can enhance motivation and elevate service quality [26].Information and Training Solutions: Establishing secure electronic health record systems and national data standards is vital for consistent data management. Investing in data-driven research and employing advanced technologies—such as artificial intelligence and big data analytics—can improve data collection and analysis, aiding informed decision-making and effective program implementation [27].Strategic Planning: Developing long-term strategies with clear objectives, resource allocation, and timelines can provide stability. Transparency in decision-making and active engagement with stakeholders—including physicians and patients—can build public trust. Collaboration among the Ministry of Health, insurance companies, and other organizations is crucial for achieving program goals [28].

The political stream presents significant challenges, yet it is critical for enabling policy change. Resistance from specialty medicine groups, who perceive family medicine as a threat to their established roles, influences lobbying efforts and funding decisions. This ongoing preference for acute care models over comprehensive family medicine continues to hinder reform efforts [29, 30].Government health organizations may focus on immediate cost savings rather than long-term benefits, leading to underfunded primary care initiatives. Health insurance companies also play a pivotal role. If they favor episodic care over ongoing patient-provider relationships, providers may hesitate to adopt family medicine practices. Community attitudes toward healthcare can hinder the adoption of family medicine, especially in cultures that prioritize urgent specialty care. Education and outreach are necessary to demonstrate the benefits of comprehensive, continuous care.

Political advocacy groups can support or obstruct family medicine initiatives based on their alignment with broader agendas. Changes in government, shifts in public opinion, or increased political activism can create windows of opportunity for reform. Strategic support and collaboration among stakeholders are essential to navigate the political landscape and prioritize patient-centered care [31].

International experiences highlight the importance of collaborative approaches in healthcare reform. Strengthening healthcare services through public-private partnerships can enhance quality, ensure universal coverage, and reduce costs [32]. Family physicians play a central role in managing chronic diseases, improving public health outcomes, and addressing social determinants of health. Their contributions align with the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), emphasizing the need for comprehensive primary care [33]. Research from countries with successful family physician models demonstrates improved health indicators and patient satisfaction. For instance, nations like the United Kingdom and Canada have robust primary care systems where family physicians act as gatekeepers, ensuring efficient use of resources and continuity of care.

The Vision Document for 1404 and recent budget allocations reflect a growing commitment to family medicine in Iran, presenting a crucial opportunity to address healthcare inequities. To capitalize on this momentum:

Limitations

Although this study varefully gathered and alayzed the perspectives of various stakeholders involved in the family physician program, it is subject to certain limitations. Due to the qualitative nature of the research, the findings may not be readily generalizable to other populations or settings. However, in qualitative research, the emphasis is on transferability rather than generalizability. Thus, the insights from this study may be applicable to similar contexts with comparable characteristics. Moreover, this study primiraly focused on identifying and analyzing agenda-setting challenges within the urban family physician program. To gain a more comprehensive understanding of the program, further research is warranted. Future investigations could explore other dimensions of the program through quantitative methods, such as its evaluating cost-effectiveness, assessing its implementation outcomes, and conducting comparative analyses of the program’s effectiveness across different countries. Given the inherent limitations of qualitative research, it is essential to conduct complementary studies to either validate or challenge these findings. Future researchers are encouraged to delve deeper into various aspects of the policy process surrounding the family physician program, offering evidence-based solutions to enhance or reform the current program. Insights derived from such analyses, coupled with lessons learned, could contribute significantly to the program’s improvement and its successful adaptation to diverse healthcare settings.

Conclusion

The successful implementation of the urban family physician program within Iran’s health insurance system requires coordinated efforts of key policy entrepreneurs including the World Health Organization (WHO), the Iranian Parliament, the Ministry of Health, and health insurance organizations. By aligning strategies and resources, these entities can effectively harness the opportunity to integrate the family physician program, as illuminated by Kingdon’s multiple streams framework.

The WHO can play a pivotal role by highlighting Iran’s healthcare challenges through global health indicators and sharing successful implementations from other countries. This international perspective not only underscores the urgency but also lends credibility to the initiative. Concurrently, the Ministry of Health and health insurance organizations should identify specific systemic issues via comprehensive national studies, design locally tailored solutions, and develop detailed operational plans to address the unique needs of Iran’s healthcare landscape.

The Iranian Parliament is instrumental in facilitating public discourse, by gathering input from both citizens and experts, thereby ensuring that the program reflects the populace’s needs and expectations. Legislative support is critical; by enacting necessary laws, the Parliament can provide a robust legal framework to sustain the program. Moreover, the Ministry of Health and health insurance organizations must secure political backing from the government and collaborate with other ministries to foster a cohesive policy environment. The WHO’s soft power, exercised through international recommendations, can further encourage alignment with global health standards.

For the program to succeed, coordination across all streams is imperative. Building alliances with stakeholders such as medical associations, universities, and advocacy groups strengthens can amplify support and resources. Launching public awareness campaigns will educate the community about the benefits of the family physician program, shaping cultural perceptions and minimizing resistance to change. Preparing the necessary infrastructure—including comprehensive training physicians extensively and establishing integrated health information systems is crucial to ensure readiness and efficiency. Flexibility is key; adapting the program to evolving political and social conditions will enhance its resilience and effectiveness. By remaining responsive to change, policymakers can navigate challenges and capitalize on emerging opportunities, ensuring the program’s longevity. Ultimately, this study provides valuable insights into the dynamics of health policy formulation and the intricate processes influencing the prioritization of health programs within insurance frameworks. By addressing the identified challenges and implementing the proposed solutions, policymakers can significantly enhance healthcare quality, accessibility, and equity in Iran. These efforts will contribute substantially to the overall improvement of public health standards and advance progress toward the Sustainable Development Goals (Fig. 1).
