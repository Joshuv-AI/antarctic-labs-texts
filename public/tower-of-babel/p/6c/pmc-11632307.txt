Central Message

Primary malignant EHE is not commonly seen. Here we report a case of malignant EHE originating from the sternum and invading the mediastinum and sternoclavicular joint.

The epithelioid hemangioendothelioma (EHE) is between hemangioma and angiosarcoma in pathology. This disease is common in the liver and spleen, as well as in the lung and mediastinum. Primary EHE of the sternum is rarely reported. Here we report a case of malignant EHE of the sternum and invaded mediastinum. Institutional review board approval was not required; the patient provided informed written consent for publication.

Case Report

A 72-year-old woman complained of chest pain without other discomfort for over a year that gradually worsened. A sternum tumor was found and biopsy suggested osteosarcoma.

Computed tomography showed bone destruction of the manubrium sterni and the body of the sternum. Magnetic resonance imaging showed lesions invading the sternum and a soft tissue mass entering the mediastinum. Isotope nuclide examination using 99mTc-methylene diphosphonate showed a malignant tumor in the sternum. Because it was unifocal, we performed a radical chest wall tumor resection and reconstruction on January 30, 2024.

We made a midline incision and the surgical margin started 5 cm from the tumor. The tumor exceeded the sternum, entered the left anterior superior mediastinum, adhered to the left subclavian vein, and was attached to part of left mediastinal pleura and part of left phrenic nerve. We resected the manubrium sterni, the upper two-thirds of the sternum, bilateral sternoclavicular joint, costal cartilages, bilateral anterior ribs from the first to fifth, and tissues attached to the tumors. Freezing pathology showed only the body of the sternum had residual tumors. Therefore, the remaining sternum was resected. The surgery was successful and no complication happened. After 1 week of surgery, the patient was discharged. A telephone follow-up was conducted after 3 months. The patient had no discomfort. Computed tomography showed satisfactory chest shape and internal fixation without detachment.

The postoperative pathology showed no tumor was found at the surgical margin. Histopathology showed the tumor cells were epithelioid with eosinophilic cytoplasm. The tumor destroyed the cortical bone and invaded the soft tissue adjacent to the sternum and phrenic nerve. The tumor cells were positive for CD34, ERG, and CK. The Ki67 index was 15%. Discussion

EHE is a rare vascular tumor with an epithelioid and histiocytoid appearance, originating from vascular endothelial or pre-endothelial cells. It can be seen in multiple organs, but rarely in the sternum. Malignant EHE of the sternum has not been reported before.

Computed tomography of this patient showed dissolution and destruction of the sternum and magnetic resonance imaging showed an invasive lesion with hypointense lesions on T1 weighted image and hyperintensity on T2 weighted image, as previously reported.1 The diagnosis of EHE is made based on unique histologic, immunohistochemical, and molecular characteristics.2 Given that EHE remains a rare malignancy, large randomized data are not available with regard to optimal treatment strategy. If possible, surgical resection should be performed. In patients with localized, resectable EHE, there is no evidence supporting the use of systemic therapies in the (neo) adjuvant setting.3 The surgery and resection should be carried out in centers with experience in sarcoma surgery. Our clinical center has rich experience for such cases. For this patient's huge defect of the anterior chest wall, we used 3 titanium alloy plates to horizontally connect the bilateral anterior second to fourth ribs to restore chest wall shape and ensure respiratory integrity. The advantage of titanium alloy plates is they are preformed and can be adjusted according to the defect shape and number needed during the surgery. It has been widely used in reconstruction after resection of sternum tumors in our clinical center.4

Other treatment for metastatic EHE has included cytotoxic chemotherapy (carboplatin, paclitaxel, pemetrexed, bevacizumab, and etoposide), immune therapy (thalidomide or interferon), and targeted therapies (apatinib, sorafenib, pazopanib, or sirolimus).2 There is limited evidence evaluating the role of radiotherapy alone in EHE, although EHE is considered relatively radiosensitive. Up to 90% of EHEs harbor (1; 3) (p36.3; q25) translocation, leading to the formation of WWTR1 (TAZ)-CAMTA1 (TC) fusion gene. Research identified connective tissue growth factor as a tumorigenic transcriptional target of WWTR1 (TAZ)-CAMTA1 (TC) and enhance Ras-mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling to promote tumor development.5 Our CAMTA1 gene test also been done, but unfortunately, the result was negative.

In summary, this malignant EHE of the sternum is the first reported case. At present, surgery is still the main treatment. The tumor involved the whole sternum and entered the mediastinum. After expanded resection, the chest wall was reconstructed successfully. No complications occurred after surgery. The patient recovered well and 3 months later there was internal fixation without detachment. Postoperative follow-up should be strengthened to know prognosis.
