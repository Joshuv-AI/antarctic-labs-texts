Evaluation of self-efficacy and its limiting factors among children with asthma aged 8–18 years
Authors: Raheleh Shanazari, Fatemeh Gholamzadeh, Mojgan Safari, Younes Mohammadi, Arash Khalili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAsthma is the most prevalent chronic disease in childhood, and self-efficacy is a key determinant of symptom control, treatment adherence, and prevention of exacerbations. Despite advances in pharmacological management, many children continue to experience barriers that hinder effective self-management. This study aimed to assess the level of self-efficacy and identify its limiting factors among children with asthma aged 8–18 years.MethodsThis descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 at a pediatric specialty clinic affiliated with a teaching hospital. A total of 100 children with asthma were recruited using convenience sampling. Data were collected using A demographic questionnaire, the Asthma Self-Efficacy Scale, and the Limiting Factors of Self-Efficacy Questionnaire were used to collect the data. The instruments demonstrated acceptable content validity and internal consistency. Data analysis was done with descriptive statistics, Spearman’s correlation, and multiple linear regression in SPSS version 22, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe findings indicated a moderate level of self-efficacy among children with asthma, accompanied by a relatively low-to-moderate level of perceived limiting factors. Spearman’s correlation showed a significant positive association between the frequency of asthma attacks and limiting factor scores (r = 0.197, p = 0.049), suggesting that increased exacerbations were associated with greater perceived barriers to disease management. Multiple linear regression analysis revealed that longer disease duration significantly predicted higher self-efficacy, whereas lower paternal educational level was associated with reduced self-efficacy. The most commonly reported barriers included insufficient disease-related knowledge, concerns about medication side effects, and challenges in treatment follow-up, while family support emerged as a key facilitator of self-efficacy.ConclusionChildren with asthma exhibited moderate self-efficacy in disease management, while cognitive, attitudinal, and family-related barriers undermined sustained self-care behaviors. Strengthening family-centered educational interventions, improving parental health literacy, and implementing targeted nursing programs may contribute to improving self-efficacy and reducing perceived barriers.Keywords: Asthma, Self-efficacy, Children, Limiting factors

Introduction

Asthma is the most prevalent chronic respiratory disease in childhood and adolescence, with a global prevalence of approximately 14% in this age group, and it exerts substantial impacts on academic performance, psychological well-being, and the social dimensions of children’s lives [1]. Despite the proven effectiveness of modern pharmacological therapies, for many children and adolescents asthma control remains suboptimal, with recurrent exacerbations, frequent hospitalizations, and reduced quality of life continuing to be common [2, 3]. Consequently, contemporary asthma management has progressively shifted from an exclusive emphasis on pharmacotherapy toward patient- and family-centered empowerment approaches that support daily self-management and long-term disease control [1, 3].

According to the social cognitive theory of Bandura, self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capability to perform behaviors necessary to achieve desired outcomes. In pediatric asthma, self-efficacy related to correct medication use, adherence to treatment regimens, trigger avoidance, and timely response to warning symptoms is considered a central component of effective self-management [4]. Evidence consistently shows that higher self-efficacy is associated with improved asthma control, fewer activity limitations, better quality of life, and greater adherence to inhaled medications [3, 4].

Over the past two decades, substantial evidence has demonstrated the effectiveness of self-efficacy–based education and structured support in improving asthma outcomes in children and adolescents. A systematic review by Hodgkinson and colleagues reported that interventions combining self-efficacy training with ongoing support significantly reduced hospitalizations and emergency department visits and improved quality of life among patients aged 5–18 years [3]. School-based systematic reviews have similarly shown reductions in hospital admissions, unplanned healthcare visits, and days of activity limitation following self-efficacy–focused interventions [5, 6]. Among adolescents, educational, psychological, digital health, and peer-led interventions have been associated with improvements in self-efficacy, inhaler technique, treatment adherence, symptom control, and quality of life [7, 8].

However, accumulating evidence indicates that children and adolescents face multiple barriers that may hinder the development and maintenance of asthma self-efficacy. A systematic review by Hall and colleagues identified inadequate knowledge, lifestyle factors, beliefs and attitudes, family and peer relationships, intrapersonal characteristics, and the quality of communication with healthcare professionals as key themes influencing asthma self-management in adolescents [9]. Another review across broader pediatric age groups emphasized the roles of medication beliefs, anxiety and depression, social support, and structural barriers in shaping self-efficacy and self-management behaviors [10]. Qualitative studies among school-aged children further highlight embarrassment related to inhaler use at school, inadequate school health policies, and parental uncertainty in recognizing symptom severity as major obstacles to daily asthma management [11, 12].

Importantly, these findings demonstrate that asthma self-efficacy is influenced by a complex interplay of behavioral, environmental, family-related, and psychosocial factors. Psychosocial determinants such as family health literacy, parental involvement, social support, and emotional well-being have been repeatedly identified as critical components of pediatric chronic disease self-management. Nevertheless, the relative contribution of these factors may vary across cultural and healthcare contexts, highlighting the importance of context-specific investigations [13].

The role of parents and families in children’s asthma self-efficacy is particularly crucial during late childhood and early adolescence, when dependence on parents for treatment-related decision-making and access to healthcare services remains substantial [11]. Higher parental self-efficacy in asthma management has been associated with improved parental quality of life and better asthma control in children [14]. Family-centered and nursing-led empowerment interventions, including motivational interviewing–based approaches, have demonstrated improvements in treatment adherence, parent and child self-efficacy, and reductions in parental stress [6, 15, 16]. Furthermore, growing evidence suggests that family health literacy and parental educational background may influence children’s self-management abilities and health outcomes [17].

Despite this expanding body of research, several important knowledge gaps remain. First, most existing studies have primarily focused on evaluating educational interventions rather than comprehensively assessing self-efficacy levels and perceived barriers simultaneously. Second, quantitative investigations specifically targeting the 8–18-year age group remain limited, despite this period representing a critical developmental transition from parental dependence toward progressive self-management. During this stage, children and adolescents encounter unique developmental, school-related, social, and family-related challenges that may create distinct barriers to self-efficacy [2, 9, 18]. Third, context-specific evidence from Middle Eastern and developing healthcare settings remains scarce, limiting the generalizability of existing findings to diverse populations [19].

Therefore, a comprehensive assessment of self-efficacy and its limiting factors within this transitional age group is essential for informing targeted nursing, family-centered, and community-based interventions. Accordingly, the present study aimed to assess the level of self-efficacy and its limiting factors among children aged 8–18 years with asthma attending Ekbatan Educational and Medical Center in Hamadan in 2025, in order to address existing evidence gaps and provide a foundation for the development of context-appropriate family- and community-oriented care strategies.

Methods

Study design and setting

This descriptive cross-sectional study was conducted in 2025 at the outpatient asthma clinic of Ekbatan Educational and Medical Center in Hamadan, Iran. This tertiary referral center provides specialized allergy and asthma services to children and adolescents from Hamadan province and neighboring regions.

The study aimed to evaluate asthma self-efficacy and its limiting factors at a single point in time; therefore, the design allows identification of associations rather than causal relations.

Participants and eligibility criteria

The target population consisted of children aged 8–18 years with physician-confirmed allergic asthma. Eligibility criteria included:

Inclusion criteria

Confirmed diagnosis of allergic asthma by a pediatric allergist based on clinical history, spirometry, and/or laboratory assessment, at least six months since diagnosis, current use of inhaled asthma medication, ability to understand and complete questionnaires and written informed consent from parent/guardian and assent from the child.

Exclusion criteria

Other chronic pulmonary diseases (e.g., cystic fibrosis), immunodeficiency disorders, severe cognitive impairment preventing questionnaire completion and questionnaires with > 20% missing data.

During recruitment, 137 children were screened; 31 did not meet the inclusion criteria and 6 provided incomplete questionnaires. The final analytic sample comprised 100 participants.

Sampling procedure and external validity considerations

Participants were recruited using consecutive sampling from all eligible patients attending the specialized asthma clinic during the study period.

Because the center is a tertiary referral clinic, probability-based random sampling was not feasible. However, continuous enrollment of all eligible patients minimized researcher selection bias. The potential impact of referral-center sampling on generalizability is acknowledged and addressed in the Discussion and Limitations sections.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated based on the findings of Karaarslan et al. (2023) [20], applying a 95% confidence level (Z = 1.96), a margin of error of 8.5% (d = 0.085), and an expected prevalence of 50% (p = 0.50) to ensure maximum variance. the minimum required sample was 103 participants. To ensure adequate representation across developmental age groups (8–11, 12–14, 15–18 years) and support regression analyses, the target sample size was increased to ~ 106.

Power analysis using G*Power confirmed that a sample of 100 provides 80% power to detect moderate correlations (r = 0.30) at α = 0.05.

Clinical and demographic variables

A structured demographic and clinical questionnaire collected the following data: age and sex, asthma duration, parental education and occupation, family size and birth order, parental history of asthma, number of hospitalizations, school absenteeism days, number of inhalers used and monthly asthma attack frequency.

Asthma severity and control

To improve clinical characterization, asthma severity and control were extracted from medical records based on physician classification following international asthma management guidelines. Severity was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe persistent asthma.

Study instruments

Asthma medication management and control self-efficacy scale

This researcher-developed instrument was designed following pilot testing of existing self-efficacy tools that were found to be difficult for younger children to comprehend. The scale consists of 12 items distributed across two subscales: asthma attack prevention (7 items) and asthma attack management (5 items). The items assess key self-management behaviors, including carrying inhalers, avoiding triggers, correct inhaler use, and appropriate responses to asthma symptoms. Responses are rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 to 5, yielding a total score between 12 and 60 (prevention subscale: 7–35; management subscale: 5–25), with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. Content validity was evaluated by a panel of 10 experts in pediatric nursing and allergy. Structural validity was examined using exploratory factor analysis with principal component extraction and varimax rotation, which supported a two-factor structure consistent with the theoretical domains of prevention and management. Internal consistency reliability was acceptable, with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.85 for the total scale, 0.78 for the prevention subscale, and 0.81 for the management subscale.

Self-efficacy limiting factors in asthma management questionnaire

This researcher-developed instrument was designed to identify perceived barriers to asthma self-management among children and adolescents. The questionnaire consists of 16 items organized into four domains: medication-related barriers, environmental barriers, behavioral barriers, and emotional barriers. Responses are rated on a four-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (not at all) to 4 (high), resulting in a total score range of 16 to 64, with higher scores indicating greater perceived barriers to asthma management. For interpretation, raw scores were converted into percentages of the maximum possible score: values below 50% indicate mild barriers, scores between 50% and 75% indicate moderate barriers, and scores above 75% indicate severe barriers. Content validity was confirmed by a panel of 10 experts in pediatric nursing and allergy. Structural validity was assessed using exploratory factor analysis, which supported a four-factor structure consistent with the conceptual domains. Internal consistency reliability in the pilot study demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.80. The full questionnaire is provided as Supplementary Material.

Data collection procedure

After ethics approval, eligible children were approached during clinic visits. Following consent and assent, participants completed the questionnaires independently. Clarifications were provided, when necessary, without influencing responses.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS version 22. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, medians, and frequency distributions, were calculated to summarize levels of asthma self-efficacy and perceived barriers. Statistical assumptions were examined prior to inferential analyses: normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and multicollinearity among independent variables was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with values below 5 considered acceptable. Depending on data distribution, Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were used to examine relations between study variables. Independent samples t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied to compare mean scores across demographic and clinical groups. Multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify factors associated with asthma self-efficacy. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

A total of 137 children were screened for eligibility. Thirty-one were excluded for not meeting inclusion criteria and six questionnaires were removed due to > 20% missing data. Consequently, data from 100 children with asthma were included in the final analysis.

The mean age of participants was 12.45 ± 3.05 years, and 65% were male. The mean duration of asthma was 2.25 years. Nearly half of the participants (48%) reported no school absenteeism in the previous month, whereas 26% reported more than three days of absence. In addition, 45% of the children reported no asthma attacks in the previous month, while 26% reported two or more attacks. A family history of asthma was present in 43% of households (Table 1).

Overall, the mean self-efficacy score corresponded to a moderate level, whereas perceived barriers were generally low. Asthma attacks were significantly associated with limiting factor scores, while asthma duration and paternal education were significantly associated with self-efficacy scores.

Discussion

The present study found that children with asthma demonstrated measurable self-efficacy alongside relatively low levels of perceived barriers to asthma self-management. This overall pattern is consistent with international evidence showing that many children and adolescents, despite access to effective pharmacological therapies, continue to require additional educational, behavioral, and family-centered support to effectively manage their condition [7, 21]. Previous systematic reviews have emphasized that knowledge deficits, lifestyle factors, beliefs and attitudes, family relationships, and the quality of engagement with healthcare systems are key determinants of pediatric asthma self-management [7, 10]. The barrier domains identified in the present study—including insufficient disease knowledge, medication-related concerns, difficulties in treatment follow-up, and the role of family support—closely align with these internationally reported themes [10].

A statistically significant but weak positive association was observed between perceived barriers and the frequency of asthma attacks. Although the magnitude of this association was small, its potential clinical relevance warrants consideration. One possible explanation is that recurrent asthma symptoms and perceived barriers may influence each other over time; however, the cross-sectional design does not permit conclusions regarding the direction or causality of this relationship. Previous qualitative and quantitative studies have reported similar feedback processes linking forgetfulness, negative medication beliefs, social stigma related to inhaler use, and reduced treatment adherence with increased symptom burden [5, 10, 22]. Thus, even modest correlations may reflect clinically meaningful interactions within the complex process of asthma self-management.

The observed association between longer disease duration and higher self-efficacy can be interpreted within the mastery experience framework of Bandura. Over time, repeated exposure to asthma management tasks may allow children to gain practical skills, observe positive outcomes of self-care behaviors, and integrate disease management into their daily routines and self-identity [23]. Longitudinal evidence suggests that children and families gradually develop adaptive coping strategies and more stable treatment routines as disease experience accumulates [24, 25]. One possible explanation is that children with longer disease duration may gradually acquire greater confidence in managing their condition through accumulated experience.

An important finding of this study was the association between paternal educational level and the self-efficacy of their child. This observation is consistent with family-centered models that emphasize the role of parental health literacy and socioeconomic resources in pediatric chronic disease outcomes [26, 27]. In many cultural contexts, fathers play a prominent role in healthcare decision-making, access to healthcare services, and allocation of family resources. Higher paternal education may therefore facilitate better understanding of treatment recommendations, improved communication with healthcare providers, and greater support for the self-management behaviors of their child. Evidence suggests that family health literacy and parental engagement are strongly associated with treatment adherence, asthma control, and the quality of life of children [28]. The present findings reinforce the importance of considering family-level determinants when designing pediatric asthma interventions.

The identified barrier domains also align with recurring findings from systematic reviews on asthma self-management. Limited health literacy and insufficient understanding of treatment principles have consistently been identified as major obstacles [10, 21]. Concerns about medication side effects and negative beliefs regarding inhaled corticosteroids may reduce adherence and contribute to recurrent exacerbations [10, 29]. Emotional factors such as anxiety and negative mood have likewise been shown to influence asthma self-management behaviors and perceived barriers [30]. The role of family support observed in this study further supports evidence that positive parenting and supportive home environments enhance children’s self-efficacy and adherence [27].

From a clinical perspective, the findings highlight that effective pediatric asthma care should extend beyond knowledge transfer to address beliefs, attitudes, emotional factors, and family dynamics. Multi-component interventions such as structured education, psychological approaches, and peer-led programs have demonstrated improvements in self-efficacy, quality of life, and symptom control [7, 21]. Motivational interviewing interventions targeting both adolescents and parents have also shown improvements in medication beliefs, adherence, and self-efficacy [22, 31, 32]. These findings support the integration of family-centered and psychosocial components into routine asthma care.

Several practical implications for pediatric nursing emerge. Structured and repeated educational programs for children and families should be incorporated into routine care, with particular emphasis on correcting medication misconceptions, improving inhaler technique, and strengthening self-management skills. Family-centered interventions are particularly important for families with lower educational levels. School-based and digital interventions also show promise in improving asthma outcomes and should be considered as complementary strategies [5, 7, 29].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporal or causal relationships between the study variables. Second, participants were recruited from a single tertiary referral center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of children with asthma. Children receiving care at tertiary centers may differ from those managed in primary or secondary healthcare settings with respect to disease severity, healthcare access, and socioeconomic characteristics. Accordingly, the findings may not be generalizable to all children with asthma or to other healthcare settings and geographic regions. Third, although several demographic and clinical variables were included, important psychosocial factors, such as anxiety, depression, and peer relationships, were not assessed. Fourth, the use of self-reported questionnaires may have introduced response and social desirability biases, potentially affecting the accuracy of participants’ responses. Finally, although the researcher-developed instruments demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties, further validation in larger and more diverse populations is warranted. Future multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating additional psychosocial and clinical variables are needed to confirm and extend the present findings.

Conclusion

Children with asthma demonstrated measurable self-efficacy in disease management, while cognitive, attitudinal, and access-related barriers remained common. A higher frequency of asthma attacks was associated with greater perceived barriers, and family-related characteristics, particularly paternal educational level, were associated with self-efficacy outcomes. These findings highlight the potential value of family-centered education, addressing medication beliefs, and improving follow-up care to support asthma self-management among children and adolescents. Future longitudinal and multicenter studies are needed to further clarify these associations and guide the development of effective interventions.
