Association Between Serum Adropin Levels and Cognitive Impairment in the Elderly: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Safinaz Abousamra, Mohammed M. EL Arman, Hossam Arafa Ghazi, Heba Shereif El-Sobky, E Hatata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDespite promising data from rodent models demonstrating a link between adropin deficiency and cognitive dysfunction, in-depth examinations of such an association in humans are lacking. Accordingly, the present study investigated the relationship between serum adropin levels and cognitive function in a sample of elderly individuals.MethodThis cross-sectional study included 90 elderly (≥ 65 years of age) participants from geriatric outpatient clinics at Mansoura University (Mansoura City, Egypt), who were classified with normal cognition, mild neurocognitive disorder/mild cognitive impairment, or major neurocognitive disorder/dementia according to criteria from the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Psychiatric Disorders, Fifth Edition. Assessments included a comprehensive geriatric evaluation, cognitive testing, and measurement of serum adropin levels and metabolic parameters.ResultsMultivariate regression (R2= 0.464) revealed that serum adropin (β = 3.09, p < 0.001), education ≥ 4 years (β = 1.47, p = 0.040), and total cholesterol level (β = -0.02, p = 0.004) were independently associated with Mini-Mental State Examination scores. Adropin levels ≤ 1.9 ng/mL yielded 90.2% sensitivity and 95.9% specificity for discriminating cognitive impairment (mild cognitive impairment and dementia), and ≤ 1.6 ng/mL specifically for dementia, with 83.3% sensitivity and 93.1% specificity.ConclusionsLower serum adropin levels were significantly associated with cognitive impairment in elderly individuals, with strong diagnostic discriminative ability (area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, 0.993). However, larger-scale longitudinal studies are required to establish causality and validate these findings.

Introduction

There has been an exponential increase in the elderly population worldwide, which has been accompanied by an elevated incidence of cognitive problems, including dementia. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (American Psychiatric Association, 2013), dementia (also considered to be a major neurocognitive disorder) is defined as a significant decline in ≥ 1 cognitive domain(s) that interferes with an individual’s independence in the activities of daily living (ADL). The 6 cognitive domains recognised in the DSM-5 include executive function, complex attention, language, learning and memory, social cognition, and perceptual-motor function. According to a systematic review and meta-analysis by Cao et al. (2020), the occurrence of dementia in elderly individuals nearly doubles every five years, with an overall higher prevalence among women than that among men. Among demographic factors, including sex, age, education, marital status, and occupation (Naheed et al., 2023), age remains the strongest risk factor for dementia and cognitive decline (Aggarwal et al., 2024).

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) differs from dementia, in that independent daily functioning is not severely affected, although the progression of MCI to dementia varies. A systematic review by Ward et al. (2013) found that 32% of elderly individuals with MCI developed dementia over a five-year period, whereas 10%–40% of those recovered normal cognition. Therefore, proper assessment and interventions directed toward MCI in the elderly population may help prevent dementia.

Adropin is a 76-amino acid peptide hormone encoded by the energy homeostasis-associated gene (ENHO) and is principally expressed in the brain and liver. Fasting and dietary macronutrient composition regulate adropin secretion (Kumar et al., 2008). Adropin has systemic effects on insulin sensitivity and glucose metabolism in skeletal muscle and the liver and heart (Mushala & Scott, 2021). Fujie et al. (2021) postulated that ENHO expression is correlated with the expression of genes implicated in neurodegenerative diseases, including those involved in mitochondrial function and synaptic plasticity. The cellular mechanisms underlying learning and memory in the adult brain are believed to depend on synaptic plasticity. Mitochondria play a role in regulating the complex processes of synaptic plasticity (Todorova & Blokland, 2017), and emerging evidence suggests that adropin plays a protective role in neurological disorders (Gunraj et al., 2023; Sivakumar et al., 2025). In humans, adropin expression peaks during childhood and decreases with age. Banerjee et al. (2020) demonstrated that brain adropin concentrations in rodent models deteriorate between 3 and 18 months of age. In these studies, treatment with a synthetic adropin peptide prevented this decline, and improved spatial learning and memory.

Despite evidence from animal models linking adropin to cognitive function via synaptic plasticity and mitochondrial mechanisms (Banerjee et al., 2020; Fujie et al., 2021), it remains unclear whether adropin deficiency contributes to cognitive decline in aging humans. As such, the present study aimed to address this knowledge gap with 3 objectives: to investigate the association between serum adropin levels and cognitive performance in elderly individuals; to determine whether this association remained significant after controlling for metabolic confounders, including glycaemic status and dyslipidaemia (Kumar et al., 2008; Li et al., 2016); and to evaluate the diagnostic utility of serum adropin for identifying early cognitive impairment. We hypothesised that lower adropin levels are independently associated with poorer cognitive performance.

2. Methods

2.1 Study design and population

This cross-sectional study recruited 90 elderly (≥ 65 years of age) participants from geriatric outpatient clinics at Mansoura University Hospital (Mansoura City, Egypt) between June 2024 and January 2025. Participants were recruited consecutively from among individuals attending routine geriatric assessments or presenting with cognitive concerns (as reported by patients, family members, or referring physicians). Patients and their accompanying spouses were eligible if they fulfilled the inclusion criteria and provided informed consent. Participants presented with heterogeneous cognitive status, ranging from normal cognition to clinically diagnosed dementia, enabling comparison across the cognitive spectrum. Because recruitment was clinically based, the sample may over-represent individuals with a higher comorbidity burden than community-dwelling elderly individuals.

2.2 Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included participants ≥ 65 years of age, with both men and women eligible for enrolment. Participants were excluded if they had delirium at the time of assessment, severe uncorrected hearing or visual impairments interfering with cognitive assessment, active untreated major depressive disorder (defined as Patient Health Questionnaire [PHQ]-9 score ≥ 10 or Cornell Scale for Depression in Dementia score ≥ 8), schizophrenia or other psychiatric disorders significantly affecting cognitive function, and an acute neurological event within the previous 3 months.

2.3.2 Step 2: Objective cognitive screening based on neurocognitive tests and DSM-5 descriptive criteria

All participants underwent a clinical interview with a registered geriatric physician. Participants with a history of cognitive problems were assessed using a detailed dementia sheet incorporating information from a reliable informant, including a history of short- and long-term memory impairment, executive and visuospatial dysfunction, language, attention, and behavioural problems.

Objective cognitive assessment was performed for all participants using the Arabic versions of the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) (Elokl, 2002), and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) (Saleh et al., 2019) (Khourieh, 2023). The MMSE and MoCA served complementary roles in the cognitive assessment. The MMSE was selected as the primary outcome measure because well-established age- and education-adjusted normative data exist for the Egyptian elderly population (Crum et al., 1993), enabling the objective determination of cognitive decline. Furthermore, the MMSE demonstrates adequate sensitivity for moderate to severe cognitive impairment (Perneczky et al., 2006), and its total score (0–30 points) provides a continuous variable suitable for examining the associations between biomarkers and cognitive function. The MMSE was administered to all participants regardless of education level and was selected as the dependent variable in the multivariate regression analysis.

The MoCA was included because it demonstrates superior sensitivity for detecting MCI compared with the MMSE (Nasreddine et al., 2005). The MoCA assesses executive function and visuospatial abilities more comprehensively, with these domains often impaired early in cognitive decline. To accommodate varying literacy levels, both the MoCA–Full (administered to participants with > 4 years’ formal education) and MoCA–Basic (for those with ≤ 4 years’ education; validated in Arabic by Saleh et al., 2019) were used. The MoCA primarily supports the identification of mild neurocognitive disorders in the diagnostic classification process. The MoCA has also been used to assess impairment across the required DSM-5 cognitive domains, including complex attention, executive function, learning and memory, language, perceptual-motor function, and social cognition.

2.3.3 Diagnostic classification sequence

Diagnostic classification followed a structured sequence, as follows. First, a comprehensive clinical history from participants and reliable informants documenting the onset, progression, and nature of cognitive complaints. Second, assessment of functional status using IADL and ADL scales to determine the level of independence. Third, objective cognitive testing using the MMSE and MoCA to quantify performance across cognitive domains. Fourth, synthesis of all information by the geriatric physician to determine whether DSM-5 clinical criteria were fulfilled for mild or major neurocognitive disorders.

Importantly, while the MMSE and MoCA scores informed the diagnostic classification by documenting cognitive performance, the final diagnosis was based on comprehensive DSM-5 clinical criteria rather than test scores alone. For major neurocognitive disorders (e.g., dementia), diagnosis requires documented significant cognitive decline from a previous performance level (based on informant reports and clinical assessment) coupled with a loss of independence in basic ADLs or IADLs. MMSE scores were compared with age-and education-adjusted norms (Crum et al., 1993) (table X) to support the determination of significant decline. The diagnosis of MCI requires documented, modest, cognitive decline without a substantial loss of independence. MoCA performance below validated cut-offs (≤ 25 for MoCA–Full; ≤ 21 for MoCA–Basic [per Saleh et al., 2019]) supported identification of cognitive deficits across the DSM-5 domains. Subsequently, the total MMSE scores were analysed as a continuous dependent variable to examine their associations with adropin levels. Participants were divided into three groups according to DSM-5 criteria: A (major neurocognitive disorders/dementia; B (MCI); and C (normal cognitive functions not fulfilling the DSM-5 criteria for mild or major neurocognitive disorders).

2.3.4 Step 3: Laboratory investigations

Laboratory investigations, including those for serum adropin levels, were performed on all participants. Additional laboratory investigations were performed to rule out potential confounding factors that may influence serum adropin concentrations. These tests included evaluation of glycaemic status (glycated haemoglobin A1c [HbA1c] and fasting blood glucose [FBG]), serum creatinine, fasting lipid profile, and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels.

2.3.5 Sampling

Venous blood (5 mL) was withdrawn from each participant by aseptic venipuncture after an overnight fast, with 1 mL collected in an EDTA tube for complete blood count and HbA1c analysis, while the remaining blood was allowed to clot. The tube was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The separated serum samples were divided into two aliquots. One method was applied to determine lipogram, TSH, serum creatinine, and fasting serum glucose levels. The second aliquot was stored frozen at -20°C until the adropin assay was performed using a commercially available ELISA kit (Human AD Adropin, Elabscience), with a detection range of 0.0125–0.8 ng/mL. To ensure that all samples fell within the high-sensitivity linear range of the assay and to minimise the matrix interference common in elderly serum samples, all specimens were diluted 1:10 before adropin estimation using the manufacturer-provided sample diluent. The final concentrations were calculated by multiplying the detected values from the standard curve by the dilution factor (i.e., 10). The effective quantification range was 0.125–8.0 ng/mL, within which all measured values decreased.

To minimise assay variability, all study samples (n = 90) were processed in a single batch (one plate). Therefore, an intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) of 7.5% (per internal laboratory quality control procedure) is reported. The manufacturer-indicated inter-assay precision of the kit is < 10.

2.3.6 Step 4: Data collection and statistical analysis

The collected data were coded, tabulated, and statistically analysed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), along with minimum and maximum, and were compared using analysis of variance (i.e., “ANOVA”). Qualitative information is expressed as count and percentage, and was compared using the chi-squared and Fisher’s exact tests. Post hoc tests were performed using the Bonferroni method. Correlations were analysed using the Pearson’s correlation test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to assess the performance of adropin in the diagnosis of cognitive impairment.

3. Results

3.1 Prevalence of cognitive impairment and group characteristics

Cognitive assessment yielded the following mean (± SD) scores: MMSE, 27.0 ± 3.8 (range 13–30); MoCA– Full, 25.3 ± 5.1 (range 7–30); and MoCA–Basic, 19.5 ± 5.4 (range 6–29). Based on a comprehensive DSM-5 clinical assessment, participants were classified with normal cognition (n = 49 [54.4%]), MCI (n = 23 [25.6%]), or dementia (n = 18 [20.0%]).

The cognitive groups differed significantly in key demographic characteristics (table 1). Participants with dementia were older (mean 72.3 ± 4.1 years of age) compared with those with normal cognition (67.5 ± 3.8 years) (p < 0.001). Higher education emerged as strongly protective, with 71.4% of participants with normal cognition having ≥ 4 years’ education vs. only 22.2% in the dementia group (p < 0.001). These demographic patterns informed the covariate selection for subsequent multivariate analysis.

3.2 Correlations between adropin and cognitive, functional, and metabolic variables

Serum adropin levels were significantly associated with the key demographic, functional, and cognitive metrics (table 3). Adropin concentration was negatively correlated with age (r = -0.517, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with years of education (r = 0.362, p < 0.001). Adropin levels were positively correlated with cognitive function, as shown by the total MMSE and MoCA scores. It was also positively correlated with functional independence, especially IADL (r = 0.611, p < 0.001) and ADL (r = 0.469, p < 0.001) scores. Regarding metabolic status (table 4), adropin was significantly and negatively correlated with markers of poor glycaemic control: FBG (r = -0.300, p = 0.004) and HbA1c (r = -0.306, p = 0.003). A significant and positive relationship was also detected between adropin and haemoglobin and platelet counts.

3.3 Independent correlates of cognitive score and diagnostic performance

In multivariate regression (R2= 0.464), serum adropin (β = 3.09, p < 0.001), education ≥ 4 years (β = 1.47, p = 0.040), and total cholesterol level (β = -0.02, p = 0.004) were independently associated with MMSE scores. Such a model could explain 46.4% of the variability of total MMSE score among the study participants (table 5). ROC curve analysis revealed good diagnostic discrimination (figure 1). For identifying any cognitive impairment (MCI or dementia) versus normal cognition, an adropin cut-off of ≤1.9 ng/mL yielded 90.2% sensitivity and 95.9% specificity (AUC 0.993 [95% CI 0.981–0.999]; p < 0.001). For discriminating dementia specifically from MCI or normal cognition, a lower cut-off ≤ 1.6 ng/mL yielded 83.3% sensitivity and 93.1% specificity (AUC 0.961 [95% CI 0.923–0.998]; p < 0.001) (table 6), demonstrating ability to distinguish severity levels within the cognitive impairment spectrum. Optimal cut-offs were determined using Youden’s Index (maximum sensitivity + specificity – 1).

4. Discussion

This cross-sectional study yielded three principal findings. First, lower adropin levels demonstrated a strong independent association with MMSE scores after controlling for age, education, and metabolic parameters (β=3.09, p < 0.001 in the multivariate model). Second, adropin demonstrated good discriminative ability for identifying cognitive impairment, with a cut-off of ≤1.9 ng/mL yielding 90.2% sensitivity and 95.9% specificity (AUC 0.993). Third, adropin levels were significantly correlated with functional independence measures (IADL, r = 0.611; ADL r = 0.469), suggesting its potential as an integrated marker of cognitive functional status. These findings extend animal model observations to humans and support the further investigation of adropin as a biomarker for early cognitive decline in older adults.

This study included 90 elderly participants recruited from geriatric outpatient clinics at Mansoura University. The prevalence of MCI and dementia among the participants were 25.6% and 20.0%, respectively. In a cross-sectional study involving 470 community-dwelling elderly individuals living in the Kafr El-Sheikh Governorate, Egypt, the prevalence of MCI and dementia were 37.7% and 12.5%, respectively (Tawfik et al., 2024). Sampling differences (clinic-based recruitment versus community-based assessment) and varying ascertainment methods may also have contributed to the observed variations in prevalence.

4.1 Demographics

Regarding aging and dementia (table 1), age was significantly higher in those with dementia than in normal participants (mean age 72.3 ± 4.1, 67.5 ± 3.8 years in dementia and normal cases, respectively) with no significant difference between MCI and other grades. This was consistent with a systematic review that reported a strong relationship between advanced age and dementia, as evidenced by the elevated occurrence of all-type dementia in centenarians (6592 per 10,000 cases), which was 244 times greater compared with those 50–59 years of age (27 per 10,000 cases) (Cao et al., 2020).

Additionally, higher educational levels were more prevalent among participants with normal cognitive function (71.4%) in the current study (table 1). This is consistent with findings from the study by Barcellos et al. (2025), who reported that the incidence of Alzheimer’s disease-related dementia declined in response to higher education, even in participants with genetic risk.

4.2 Comorbidities

In our study, DM was significantly more common in patients with dementia (88.9%) than that in healthy controls (55.1%) (table 2). Khaliq et al. (2025) reported that DM was an important contributor to cognitive decline according to increasing tau biomarker levels in participants with impaired glucose tolerance. Moreover, the current study demonstrated that malnutrition risk (61.1%) was higher among patients with dementia (table 2). This finding is comparable with that of a systematic review by Carey et al. (2024), who suggested an association between malnutrition and consequent development of cognitive impairment in older adults. However, the effect of malnutrition on cognitive function needs to be further studied across a broader range of age groups to determine whether this impact is independent of other confounding variables, and whether it contributes to specific micronutrient or macronutrient deficiencies. The European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (i.e., “ESPEN”) 2024 guidelines for the management of malnutrition and dehydration in patients with dementia advise regular screening for dehydration and malnutrition and the provision of oral nutritional supplements if needed (Volkert et al., 2024).

Interestingly, a high risk for falls and recurrent falls were more common (88.9% and 61.1%, respectively) in patients with dementia in our study (table 2). This finding is consistent with results reported by Zhong et al. (2025), who included 52,076 participants and concluded that participants who experienced single and recurrent falls had a greater possibility of dementia and all-cause mortality than those with no previous falls.

4.3 Correlations between adropin and demographic and clinical characteristics

There was a significant negative association between serum adropin level and age (r = -0.517, p < 0.001) (table 3), a result consistent with that in a study by Butler et al. (2012), who reported a negative correlation between adropin and aging (r = -0.263, p = 0.003). In contrast, Yang et al. (2025) reported that the adropin levels decreased in aged mouse brains, and a reduction in the number of NB-3/contactin-6, a potential adropin receptor and Notch1 ligand, was observed, although there were nonsignificant changes in peripheral tissue levels compared with those in young control mice. Whether adropin crosses the blood–brain barrier (BBB) in humans remains uncertain; however, evidence suggests potential indirect neuroprotective effects via the activation of endothelial nitric oxide synthase and the preservation of tight junctions in the BBB.

Results of the present investigation are consistent with those from a study by Aggarwal et al. (2024), who reported that aging was the most significant risk factor for dementia and cognitive decline. Moreover, our results were consistent with those from Gunraj et al., (2023), who found that adropin was positively related to markers of oxidative stress in older adults and tau-related neuropathologies and inflammation in ‘old-old’ (> 75 years of age) adults, and there is strong evidence supporting the protective effects of adropin in stroke, cardiac illness, aging, and other illnesses. Furthermore, adropin exhibited significant positive correlations with ADLs and IADLs (r = 0.469 and 0.611, respectively; p < 0.001) (table 3), indicating a higher functional status in elderly individuals with elevated adropin levels. This could be helpful because elastic band resistance training has been proposed to increase serum adropin levels, which has beneficial effects on functional performance and enhances insulin sensitivity in overweight elderly females (Azamian Jazi et al., 2022).

In the present study, cognitive function was assessed using the Arabic versions of the MMSE and MoCA. The MoCA was used to identify patients with MCI. Adropin demonstrated significant positive correlations with MMSE scores (except for stage commands, language, and naming of two objects) and MOCA scores (table 3). These findings suggest a positive relationship between serum adropin level and cognitive function in elderly individuals. This finding is consistent with that of Aggarwal et al. (2023), who suggested that a lower blood adropin concentration was significantly correlated with poorer cognitive performance, as assessed using a composite of the MMSE and animal-naming tests.

4.4 Metabolic effects of adropin

In the present study, adropin levels were significantly negatively correlated with FBG levels and HbA1c (table 4). This finding is consistent with the results of a meta-analysis and systematic review of observational studies addressing the relationship between serum adropin concentrations in adults with type 2 DM and in a control group without DM (Soltani et al., 2023). This meta-analysis revealed significantly reduced concentrations of adropin in cases with type 2 DM versus non-DM controls. Evidence from animal models supports these findings. A decline in hepatic gluconeogenic regulatory enzymes, blood glucose concentrations, and Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (i.e., “HOMA–IR”) and a significant increase in the insulin sensitivity index were observed after adropin treatment (Kuo et al., 2020). The favourable outcomes of adropin could be attributed to sensitisation of the insulin signalling pathway and increased glucose oxidation. These findings suggested that adropin enhanced insulin-mediated AKT phosphorylation and increased GLUT4 surface expression in muscle cells, thereby promoting glucose oxidation. Adropin increases the activity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex by downregulating pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase gene expression (PDH kinase-4), thereby increasing glycolysis (Gao et al., 2015). Previous studies have shown that adropin treatment increases AKT signalling and silences FoxO1 and GSK3, leading to decreased hepatic glucose production and elevated synthesis of glycogen (Gao et al., 2019).

However, this study revealed a statistically non-significant correlation between adropin levels and fasting lipid profile parameters, including low-density lipoprotein, total cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein. This result is consistent with that of Chang et al. (2018), who reported no variation in lipid profiles between Taiwanese adolescents with low and high adropin levels. In the current study, a negative correlation was observed between adropin and serum triglyceride concentration, although it was statistically non-significant. In an experimental study, researchers reported that adropin increased the total lipoprotein lipase (LPL) production in hepatocyte cultures. The capillary endothelium is rich in LPL that mediate the hydrolysis of exogenous and endogenous triacylglycerol complexes into free-fatty acids and glycerol. Therefore, adropin participates in the elimination of TAG-rich plasma lipoproteins (Lian et al., 2016).

4.5 Effects of adropin on haematological and vascular system integrity

In our study, there were significant positive correlations between adropin and both haemoglobin and platelet counts (table 4). Haemoglobin levels reflect oxygen-carrying capacity, and platelets play a vital role in haemostasis and endothelial integrity. These findings support the hypothesis that adropin enhances physiological resilience and cognitive function, particularly in elderly individuals with vascular cognitive impairment. Further studies are required to determine whether adropin directly affects erythropoiesis, megakaryopoiesis, and endothelial function, thereby contributing to cognitive benefits in elderly individuals.

4.6 Impact of different study variables on cognitive function in elderly individuals

After analysis of the included variables, only elevated adropin level and education ≥ 4 years were significant independent factors that increased MMSE score, while elevated total cholesterol level was a significant independent factor that decreased it (table 5). Such a model could explain 46.4% of the variability in total MMSE score among the study cases. This is in agreement with results reported by Sivakumar et al. (2025), who synthesised the results of observational studies investigating the role of adropin in cognitive performance and neurological illnesses. Their study reported a consistent connection between reduced adropin concentration, a range of neurological disorders, and cognitive impairment. Reduced adropin concentrations have been observed in individuals with cognitive impairment, dementia, Parkinson’s disease, bipolar disorder, and multiple sclerosis. These results highlight the potential role of adropin in the modulation of cognitive function and neurological health.

This is consistent with the findings of Guo et al., (2020), who concluded that worse cognitive function was linked to higher circulating total cholesterol levels in elderly females. In contrast, Pang et al. (2022) proposed that a higher total cholesterol level may protect against cognitive decline in highly educated elderly females without a history of stroke. This can be explained by the better nutritional status of elderly females with higher total levels.

Interestingly, adropin exhibited a significant association with early cognitive impairment (table 6, figure 1). Adropin levels ≤ 1.9 ng/mL demonstrated 90.2% sensitivity and 95.9% specificity for screening cognitive impairment (MCI and dementia), and ≤ 1.6 ng/mL specifically for dementia, with 83.3% sensitivity and 93.1% specificity. However, these cut-offs should be considered exploratory until they are validated in independent cohorts.

4.7 Clinical implications

If confirmed in larger prospective studies, serum adropin measurements could enhance early detection strategies for cognitive impairment in older adults. The high sensitivity (90.2%) at the ≤ 1.9 ng/mL cutoff suggests potential utility as a screening tool in primary care or geriatric settings, where comprehensive cognitive assessment tools may be time-consuming or require specialised training. A simple blood test combined with a brief cognitive screen could improve the identification of at-risk individuals for targeted evaluation and early intervention.

The association between adropin and metabolic parameters and functional independence suggests opportunities for developing multimodal intervention strategies. Interventions known to modulate adropin levels, including caloric restriction, exercise training, and dietary modification, warrant investigation of their potential cognitive benefits in aging populations. The strong correlation between adropin and functional independence (IADL, r = 0.611) further suggests that adropin may serve as an integrated marker of cognitive functional status, which is potentially useful for monitoring disease progression or treatment responses in clinical trials.

4.7 Limitations

This study had several limitations that warrant consideration when interpreting our findings. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Longitudinal studies are needed to establish temporal relationships and determine whether adropin levels predict the progression from normal cognition to MCI or from MCI to dementia. Second, our sample size (n = 90), while adequate for demonstrating associations, limited the statistical power to detect smaller effect sizes and perform stratified subgroup analyses. Third, the clinical recruitment may have limited the generalisability of our findings to community-dwelling older adults. Fourth, whether the measured serum adropin levels reflect brain tissue concentrations remains uncertain because the capacity of adropin to cross the BBB in humans has not been established. However, emerging evidence suggests that adropin may exert neuroprotective effects through endothelial mechanisms, independent of direct brain penetration. Finally, serum storage at -20°C— rather than the optimal -80°C—may have contributed to minor peptide degradation, although all samples were processed identically, minimising differential effects. Despite these limitations, our findings provide important preliminary evidence justifying further prospective investigations.

5. Conclusion

Adropin is a promising peptide hormone that was correlated with early cognitive impairment in a sample of elderly individuals. In our study, serum adropin levels ≤ 1.9 ng/mL and ≤ 1.6 ng/mL demonstrated a significant association with early cognitive impairment (including both MCI and dementia) and dementia only, respectively.
