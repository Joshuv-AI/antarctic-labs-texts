Effects of ultrasonic pretreatment on the fibrillation of soy protein isolate, β‑conglycinin, and glycinin: Kinetics, structure, and functional properties
Authors: Xinran Niu, Di An, Liang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study systematically investigated the effects of ultrasonic pretreatment on the fibrillation behavior of soy protein isolate (SPI), β-conglycinin (7S) and glycinin (11S). A multi-analytical approach combining Thioflavin T fluorescence spectroscopy, SDS-PAGE, dynamic light scattering, zeta potential measurement, and spectroscopic analyses was employed to monitor fibrillation kinetics and structural modifications. Ultrasonication significantly accelerated the fibrillation process by enhancing nucleation, increasing the apparent rate constant of 7S by 44% and that of SPI by 67%. SDS-PAGE analysis indicated that ultrasound treatment promoted protein degradation, generating a greater number of low-molecular-weight peptides, which may have facilitated fibril nucleation. Ultrasound treatment reduced protein particle size, increased surface charge, and promoted conformational transitions toward β-sheet-rich structures, with U-7SN showing a 20.8% rise in β-sheet content. These structural changes were correlated with enhanced functional properties, including improved solubility, emulsifying activity, and viscosity. The findings demonstrated that ultrasound promoted fibrillation by enhancing hydrophobic interactions and the formation of β-sheet structures, offering a theoretical basis for tailoring plant protein nanofibrils with targeted functionalities for food and material applications.

Introduction

Protein nanofibrils possess a non-native structure that is distinct from typical protein structures. Defined by their nanoscale thickness, micrometer-scale length, high aspect ratio, tunable flexibility, and ordered structure, amyloid-like protein nanofibrils have garnered significant research interest [1]. It has been reported that under low pH conditions, various food proteins can self-assemble into fibrous structures through long-term heating [2]. Animal proteins such as milk proteins, chicken egg white lysozyme, bovine serum albumin, and hemoglobin, as well as plant proteins such as pea protein [3], kidney bean protein [4], and pumpkin seed protein [5], can all be made into fibrils by long-term heating at temperatures higher than their denaturation temperature. Although soy protein fibrillation has been widely studied, current research has mainly focused on fibril formation conditions, structural characteristics, and functional properties, while the fundamental molecular basis of soy protein fibrillation remains insufficiently understood [6]. In particular, the key intermolecular interactions and structural evolution driving the conversion of soy protein from native globular structures into ordered fibrillar assemblies have not yet been fully clarified [7]. Driven by acoustic cavitation, wherein bubbles rapidly form, grow, and collapse, ultrasound releases intense localized energy. This facilitates protein denaturation and unfolding, thereby accelerating subsequent fibrillation [10]. Ultrasound treatment can further disintegrate large protein molecules through the mechanical forces generated by cavitation effects, promoting the generation of low-molecular-weight peptides and thereby accelerating the nucleation and growth process of nanofibrils [11]. Valued as a green technology for inducing conformational and physicochemical changes in proteins, ultrasound has attracted rising attention in the food sector, with applications ranging from promoting bioactive compound extraction to modifying ingredient functionality [12], improve protein solubility and water-holding capacity [13], and thereby modulate the functional performance of proteins in areas such as emulsion formation, foam stability, and gel network development In terms of fibril treatment, ultrasonic pretreatment affects the assembly process of peptide hydrolysates, thereby shortening the fibrillation process of oat globulin [14]. Soy protein isolate is treated with ultrasound to form fibrils, and the protein fibrils are complexed with epigallocatechin gallate to study the effects of physical interaction and chemical alterations in the structure and rheology of soy protein fibrils, and the resulting effects on their antioxidant activity [15]. However, prior research has not analyzed the structure of sonicated nanofibrils in terms of soybean protein subunits.

As the main storage protein derived from soybean seeds, soy protein possesses certain solubility, water-holding capacity, oil-binding capacity, emulsification, and foaming properties, among others [16]. Based on its exceptional nutritional profile, diverse functional attributes, and sustainable plant-based origin, it has emerged as a prominent and enduring focus of research within the global food science and nutrition community. Its core components are mainly divided into two categories based on sedimentation coefficient: the dominant β-conglycinin (7S globulin) and glycinin (11S globulin), which exhibit significant differences in amino acid composition, structural conformation, and functional performance. The 7S fraction is a trimeric glycoprotein comprising α, α', and β subunits. Both the α' and α subunits contain an extended region and a core region, whereas the β subunit possesses only a core region [17]. The 11S globulin is a hexameric protein typically composed of six subunits. Each subunit is stabilized by an intra-subunit disulfide bond connecting an acidic and a basic polypeptide [18]. 7S and 11S are the major components of SPI and possess distinct structural characteristics; their differential behavior under ultrasound can provide deeper insights into the fibrillation mechanism. Therefore, the mechanism underlying the structural changes and associated properties of soy protein could be further investigated at the molecular level of its constituent subunits.

In this study, soy protein isolate (SPI), β-conglycinin (7S), and glycinin (11S) were used as raw materials to prepare protein nanofibrils (C-SPIN, C-7SN, C-11SN), with the protein nanofibrils treated with ultrasound as controls (U-SPIN, U-7SN, U-11SN). Multiple spectroscopic techniques were employed to elucidate the differences in conformational changes and molecular interactions among different soy protein components during fibrillation under ultrasonic conditions. By analyzing the fibril formation kinetics, physicochemical properties, and microstructure of these nanofibrils, the differences in the fibril-forming ability of different soy protein components under ultrasonic conditions were obtained, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for understanding how ultrasonication influences and regulates protein nanofibril formation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Defatted soybean flour (protein content > 85%) was obtained from the Soybean Research Institute of Northeast Agricultural University (Harbin, China). Thioflavin T (ThT), 5,5′-dithio-bis-(2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), and 8-anilino-1-naphthalene-sulfonic acid (ANS) were bought from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Gel electrophoresis reagents were purchased from Boyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China). The BCA protein concentration determination kit was obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Soybean oil was purchased from Jiusan Oils and Grains Industry Group Co., Ltd. All other chemical reagents were of analytical grade.

Preparation of SPI, 7S, and 11S

The SPI extraction method was slightly modified according to Shi et al.[19]. Defatted soybean meal was dissolved in deionized water at a ratio of 1:10 (w/v) and stirred for 2 h. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 8.0 using NaOH solution (2 mol/L), stirred for 1 h and then centrifuged (8000 g, 30 min, 4 ℃) to collect the supernatant. The pH of the supernatant was adjusted to 4.5 using HCl (2 mol/L), stirred for 1 h and then centrifuged (8000 g, 30 min, 4 ℃) to collect the precipitate. The washed precipitate was resuspended, adjusted to pH 7.0 with NaOH (2 mol/L), dialyzed, and then freeze-dried.

The extraction methods of 7S and 11S were slightly modified with reference to Jia et al.[20]. Defatted soybean meal was dissolved in deionized water at a 1:15 (w/v) ratio. The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 9.0 using 2 mol/L NaOH, stirred at 25 °C for 1 h, and then centrifuged (9000 g, 4 °C, 20 min) to obtain the supernatant. For the isolation of 11S, anhydrous NaHSO3 (1.04 g/L) was added to the supernatant. The mixture was adjusted to pH 6.4 with 2 mol/L HCl, stored at 4 °C overnight, and then centrifuged (6500 g, 4 °C, 20 min) to obtain the 11S fraction. For the isolation of 7S, NaCl (14.61 g/L) was added to the remaining supernatant. The pH was adjusted to 5.4 with 2 mol/L HCl, followed by centrifugation (9000 g, 30 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant was diluted twofold with ice water, adjusted to pH 4.8, and centrifuged (6500 g, 20 min, 4 °C) to obtain the 7S fraction. Both 7S and 11S fractions were washed three times with deionized water, adjusted to pH 7.5 using 2 mol/L NaOH and dialyzed (MW: 3500 Da).

The protein contents (N × 6.25) of SPI, 7S, and 11S determined by the Dumas method were 92.32 ± 0.67%, 89.94 ± 0.07%, and 94.12 ± 0.19%, respectively, and the extraction rates were 10.12 ± 0.17%, 6.74 ± 0.46%, and 1.76 ± 0.35%, respectively.

Preparation of SPI, 7S, and 11S nanofibrils

The pH of SPI, 7S, and 11S dispersions (4%, w/v) was adjusted to 2.0 using HCl (6 and 2 mol/L), stirred at 25 °C for 2 h, and stored overnight at 4 °C. The solution was centrifuged (7500 g, 20 min, 4 °C) to collect the supernatant, and the protein concentration of the supernatant was diluted to 2% (w/v). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA method. The supernatant was treated by an ultrasonic processor (model VCX750, Sonics & Materials Inc., USA) in an ice-water bath operating at 20 kHz and equipped with a 13 mm probe. The probe was immersed (15 mm) into the sample, and the treatment was conducted in an ice-water bath at 20% amplitude for 15 min in pulsed mode (5 s on and 5 s off). The control group was the supernatant without ultrasonic pretreatment. All samples were heated at 85 °C for 0, 0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8 h. The nanofibrils prepared from SPI, 7S, and 11S proteins without ultrasonic pretreatment were named C-SPIN, C-7SN, and C-11SN, respectively. The corresponding ultrasonically treated nanofibrils were designated U-SPIN, U-7SN, and U-11SN.

Thioflavin T fluorescence assay

ThT stock solution was prepared by dissolving 8 mg of ThT to 10 mL of buffer solution (10 mmol/L, pH 7.0, 150 mmol/L NaCl) and filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane to remove undissolved solids. The stock solution was diluted 50 times with the same buffer solution to prepare the working solution. Sample (20 μL) was mixed with ThT working solution (200 μL), and incubated in the dark for 20 min. The ThT fluorescence intensity was measured using an Infinite M200 PRO (Tecan, Grdig, Austria) at an excitation wavelength of 460 nm and an emission wavelength of 490 nm.

The fibrillation kinetics were analyzed by fitting to a single-exponential equation using Origin 2021, where a is the time at 50% protein assembly and k is the apparent rate constant.

y = 1/(1 + exp(−k*(t-a))) (1).

Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE)

Non-reducing SDS-PAGE was performed on a buffer system using a 10% separating gel and a 5% stacking gel. Sample (32 μL) was mixed with loading buffer (8 μL), followed by boiling for 5 min. Sample (1 mg/mL, 10 μL) was added to each lane of the gel. When electrophoresis started, the voltage was set to 80 V, and adjusted to 120 V after the sample entered the separating gel. The gel was stained with Coomassie brilliant blue, and then decolorized with deionized water.

Particle size and zeta potential

The particle size (0.1 mg/mL) and zeta potential (1 mg/mL) of diluted samples were measured using a Zetasizer Nano ZS 90 (Malvern Instruments, UK).

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

ANS was used as a hydrophobic fluorescent probe to determine the surface hydrophobicity (H0) of the sample based on the method of Wei et al. [21], with slight modifications. The protein concentrations of the samples were diluted to 0.01, 0.02, 0.04, 0.08, and 0.16 mg/mL followed by mixing with ANS solution (8 mmol/L) and incubating in the dark for 15 min. The fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-7100, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) with an excitation wavelength of 390 nm (slit: 5 nm) and an emission wavelength of 470 nm (slit: 5 nm). The surface hydrophobicity (H0) was defined as the initial slope of the fluorescence intensity versus protein concentration (mg/mL), as determined by linear regression analysis.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

The intrinsic fluorescence spectrum was analyzed using a fluorescence spectrophotometer according to the method of Hu et al. [22]. The sample concentration was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL with deionized water (pH 2.0), with an excitation wavelength of 280 nm (slit: 5 nm) and an emission wavelength of 300–400 nm (slit: 5 nm).

Free sulfhydryl content

The determination of free sulfhydryl content was carried out according to the methods of Hayakawa & Nakai [23] and Gao et al. [24], with slight modifications. Sample (20 mg/mL, 0.2 mL) was mixed with Tris-Gly buffer (0.086 mol/L Tris, 0.09 mol/L glycine, and 0.004 mol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 8 mol/L urea, pH 8.0, 0.8 mL), and then 0.01 mL of DTNB (4 mg/mL) was added. The mixture was incubated in the dark for 15 min after vortexing and the absorbance was measured at 412 nm. The free sulfhydryl content (mmol SH/g) was calculated as follows:

SH (μmol/g) = (73.53 × A412 × D)/C (2).

Where A412 is the absorbance at 412 nm; C is the protein solution concentration (mg/mL); D is the dilution factor.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

The far-UV CD spectra (190–260 nm) and near-UV CD spectra (250–320 nm) of samples (0.05 mg/mL) were obtained using a circular dichroism spectrometer at 25 ℃ according to the method of Bahraminejad [25]. The CDNN software was used to evaluate the secondary structure content of the samples.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Freeze-dried sample (2 mg) was mixed with potassium bromide and pressed into a tablet, and the FT-IR spectra of samples were performed using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet 5700, Thermo Nicolet Corp., USA) in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1.

Transmission electron Microscopy (TEM)

Diluted samples (1 mg/mL) were dropped on a copper grid, and incubated for 1 h, and then stained with uranyl acetate for 4 min. Micrographs were acquired using a transmission electron microscope operating at an acceleration voltage of 100 kV.

Solubility

The solubility of the samples was determined according to the method of Y.-L. Wang et al. [26], with slight modifications. Freeze-dried sample (1 mg/mL) was dissolved and centrifuged (7500 g, 10 min, 4 °C) to collect the supernatant. Solubility was calculated as the percentage of soluble protein in the supernatant relative to the total protein content.

Emulsifying property determination

The emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) were determined based on the method of Wang et al. [27], with slight modifications. Diluted samples (0.001 g/mL, 6 mL) were mixed with soybean oil (2 mL), and then homogenized at 10,000 rpm/min for 2 min using a high-speed homogenizer (IKA, Germany). Samples (25 μL) were collected from the bottom of the containers, and mixed with 5 mL of SDS solution (1 mg/mL). The absorbance at 500 nm was measured using an ultraviolet spectrophotometer starting at 0 and 10 min after sample collection. The calculation formulas for EAI and ESI were as follows:(3)EAIm2/g=2×2.303×A0×Nc×θ×10000(4)ESImin=A0A0-A10×10Where, N was the dilution factor, c was the protein concentration, θ was the proportion of the oil phase (0.25), and A0 and A10 were the absorbances of the sample measured at 0 min and 10 min, respectively.

Viscosity

The viscosity of sample was evaluated at 25 °C using a TA Discovery HR-1 rotational rheometer (TA Instruments Ltd., USA) according to the method described by Tang & Wang, [4]. The apparent viscosity was measured as the shear rate increased from 0.1 s⁻1 to 100 s⁻1.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated three times, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). SPSS 27.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA) was used to determine significant differences between data (p < 0.05) through one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Duncan's multiple comparison test. The data were plotted using Origin 2021 (Origin Lab Corporation, Massachusetts, USA).

Results and discussion

Fibrillation process of soy protein isolate, β-conglycinin, and glycinin

Thioflavin T fluorescence assay

Thioflavin T was the most commonly used to monitor fibrillation due to its specific binding capability to the β-sheet-rich structure of nanofibrils [28]. Fig. 1A showed the maximum ThT fluorescence intensity of soy proteins as a function of heating time. The ThT fluorescence intensity of non-ultrasonic-treated 7S and SPI increased rapidly and then reached a plateau, while that of 11S changed little. This indicated that 7S and SPI were more likely to form β-sheet-rich fibrous structures, whereas 11S had weaker fibrillation ability. The differential behavior exhibited by 11S may be associated with its fundamental polypeptide structure, where the basic subunits inhibit or disrupt the formation and properties of fibrils derived from acidic subunits [29]. Ultrasonic treatment accelerated the kinetics of 7S and SPI, leading to a more rapid attainment of the plateau phase. The underlying reason for ultrasound-accelerated fibrillation was that ultrasonic treatment can further disintegrate large protein molecules through the mechanical forces generated by cavitation effects, thereby accelerating the nucleation and growth process of nanofibrils [11]. The apparent rate constants obtained by exponential fitting using Origin software were summarized in Table 2. Under ultrasonication, the apparent rate constants of 7S and SPI increased significantly, by 44% and 67%, respectively. Particle size analysis

The particle size governed the structural and functional properties of protein nanofibrils [30]. Fig. 1B showed the average particle sizes at heating times. Fig. 1C-E illustrated the particle size distributions of different samples during fibrillation. At 0.5 h of heating, the particle size of all samples decreased as large aggregates underwent acid-induced hydrolysis. With prolonged heating time, the particle size of all samples slightly increased, likely due to the aggregation of structural units required for fibrillation [31]. The ultrasonicated globular proteins exhibited a smaller diameter, which is consistent with the particle size distribution results. After sonication, SPI transformed from short, branched fibrils and entangled, curved fibrils into a more ordered and dispersed state [32]. Following ultrasonication, 11S protein nanofibrils shifted from a small number of long filamentous structures to shorter and finer filaments [33]. Meanwhile, 7S protein fibrils exhibited a smaller diameter and formed more worm-like aggregates after sonication [34]. In contrast to SPI, the 7S protein exhibited a more uniform and stable monomodal particle size distribution during the initial stage of heating, whereas the 11S protein often displayed a multimodal distribution throughout the heating process. At 0.5 h of heating, the particle size decreased, as large protein aggregates were hydrolyzed under acidic conditions. As heating progressed, the particle size increased slightly, likely due to the aggregation of structural units required for fibrillation [31]. For unheated SPI and 7S, the particle size distribution shifted to a unimodal pattern after ultrasonication, with a higher proportion of smaller particles. These observed reductions in particle size could be attributed to the cavitation effect induced by ultrasonication, which disrupted protein structures and altered intermolecular interactions [12].

SDS-PAGE

SDS-PAGE was used to analyze the building blocks of nanofibrils during fibrillation. Fig. 2 showed the SDS-PAGE profiles of nanofibrils formed by heating at 85 °C and pH 2.0 for different heating times. Without heating, no noticeable changes in the band patterns of SPI, 7S, or 11S were observed before and after ultrasonication, indicating that ultrasound did not affect their molecular weight distribution. With prolonged heating time, the band intensity of all samples gradually decreased, indicating that the polypeptide components in the proteins were progressively hydrolyzed into small molecular weight peptides. Upon heating for 1 h, the band at approximately 70 kDa in the U-7S protein began to degrade and disappeared completely after 2 h. In contrast, the band around 50 kDa started to degrade only after 2 h and vanished completely by 4 h. This indicated that the α-subunit was more susceptible to ultrasonic treatment than the β-subunit. [35]. At the initial stage of heating, the bands of U-SPIN and U-7SN showed a significant reduction in intensity compared to those of C-SPIN and C-7SN. This suggested that ultrasonic treatment unfolded the protein structure in advance, thereby rapidly cleaving the proteins into peptides and promoting significant aggregation within a short timeframe, which was consistent with the ThT assay results [36].Fig. 2Non-reducing SDS-PAGE results of three soy protein fractions under ultrasound (U) and without ultrasound (C) treatment.

Molecular interaction analysis

Surface hydrophobicity (H0)

As a hydrophobic fluorescent probe, ANS binds to hydrophobic regions and increases the fluorescence intensity, and was therefore used to evaluate the exposure of hydrophobic groups in the protein system [37]. Fig. 3A showed the changes in the surface hydrophobicity of the three proteins during heating with and without ultrasonication. The surface hydrophobicity of all samples exhibited a trend of initial increase followed by a decrease during heating. The initial increase could be attributed to protein hydrolysis and structural unfolding, which exposed internal hydrophobic groups. The subsequent decrease after prolonged heating was likely due to the initially exposed hydrophobic amino acids gradually aligning and becoming buried within the internal structure during fibril formation [38]. Ultrasonic treatment resulted in significantly higher surface hydrophobicity for SPI and 7S than untreated samples during the initial fibrillation stage (1 h). This indicated that ultrasonication effectively increased surface hydrophobicity, thereby enhancing the hydrophobic interactions within the nanofibrils [39]. Hu et al. Zeta potential analysis

Zeta potential is widely used to explain the electrostatic interactions between charged particles [41]. Fig. 3B showed the absolute values of zeta potential of all samples. The absolute zeta potential values of all six sample groups after 8 h of heating were higher than those at 0 h. The observed increase stemmed primarily from the fibrillation-induced exposure of positively charged amino acids, thus promoting stable nanofibril formation [[15], [42]]. The absolute value of the zeta potential increased after ultrasonic treatment. This trend could be explained by the effect of ultrasonic cavitation, which unfolded protein molecules and disrupted aggregates, leading to an increased net surface charge. The enhanced charge strengthened interparticle electrostatic repulsion, thus promoting nanofibril formation [43]. Zong et al. [44] also demonstrated that ultrasonic pretreatment exposed charged protein groups, thereby promoting fibrillation.

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy probes conformational transitions during protein folding or aggregation by monitoring fluorescence signals from Trp, Tyr, and Phe [14]. When the excitation wavelength was set at 280 nm, the spectral changes were primarily governed by Trp and Tyr residues, as their fluorescence was highly sensitive to alterations in the local polarity of their microenvironment [45]. Fig. 3C-D showed the fluorescence intensities of SPI, 7S, and 11S with and without ultrasonic treatment at 0 h (C) and 8 h (D). A redshift in λmax was observed for the all 7S fractions after heating. This indicated that the protein structure gradually unfolded which led to the transfer of tryptophan residues into a more polar solvent environment [46]. At 8 h of heating, ultrasonication increased the maximum fluorescence intensity of the proteins and their nanofibrils. This might be because ultrasonication promoted the unfolding of the protein backbone and greater exposure of specific amino acid residues, enhancing π-π stacking interactions and its fibrillation capacity [47]. Xu et al. [48] demonstrated that the increased fluorescence intensity after ultrasound was due to conformational loosening, whereby original energy dissipation pathways were disrupted and quenching from molecular packing was diminished.

Free sulfhydryl content

The fibrillation of food proteins can be reflected by measuring the content of free sulfhydryl groups, and the interaction forces during the aggregation process can be reflected by the changes in disulfide bonds [49]. Fig. 4A illustrated the variations in free sulfhydryl content of all samples during fibrillation. With the increase in heating time, the free sulfhydryl content of all samples continuously decreased. This demonstrated that during the conversion of proteins into fibrils, free sulfhydryl groups were converted into disulfide bonds. Cai et al. [50] confirmed the conversion of sulfhydryl groups to disulfide bonds during oat globulin fibrillation as well. During the fibrillation process across the six sample groups, the sonicated groups exhibited lower free sulfhydryl content than the non-sonicated groups. This phenomenon could be attributed to the high-intensity ultrasonic cavitation effect, which could generate transient free radical species. Through cross-reactions, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was generated and subsequently oxidized the free thiol groups, consequently facilitating the formation of disulfide bonds from free SH groups on cysteine residues [51]. Structural analysis

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy

Far-ultraviolet CD spectra (190–260 nm) near-ultraviolet CD spectra (250–320 nm) were used to evaluate the structural changes of samples during fibrillation (Fig. 4B and C). As shown in Fig. 4B, the presence of β-sheet secondary structure in all samples was revealed by a characteristic negative band in the 201–210 nm region. [42]. In Fig. 4C, CD features primarily arose from the aromatic side chains of the chromophores tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine, as well as disulfide bonds [53]. The signal in the 285–310 nm range was characteristic of tryptophan residues. As shown in Fig. 4C, the U-7SN sample exhibited a greater dichroic intensity in this Trp region than C-7SN, C-11SN and U-11SN. This enhanced intensity suggested a stronger fibrillogenic potential of the 7S fraction and indicated that ultrasonication could promote structural folding, dimerization, refolding, and protein reorganization during fibril formation, altering the microenvironments of aromatic amino acid residues [54]. The CDNN software was used to calculate the specific differences in secondary structure content (Table 3) [55]. After 8 h of heating, U-SPIN exhibited a decrease of 0.5% in α-helix content and an increase of 2.2% in β-sheet content; U-11SN showed a decrease of 0.5% in α-helix and an increase of 3.2% in β-sheet; while U-7SN demonstrated a more substantial decrease of 1.3% in α-helix and a significant increase of 6.0% in β-sheet content. Ultrasonic treatment facilitated the formation of a greater amount of β-sheet structures in the nanofibrils, thereby resulting in enhanced structural stability [56]. Studies by Stathopulos et al. [57] demonstrated that ultrasound-induced aggregates exhibited high β-sheet content, and proteins with native α-helical structures displayed increased β-sheet conformation within the aggregates. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

Functional group changes during protein fibrillation were analyzed by FTIR spectroscopy. The main vibrational absorptions of proteins were N–H and O–H stretching, C–H stretching [53]. The shape and position of the amide I absorption band (1700–1600 cm⁻1) and amide II (1600–1500 cm⁻1) were commonly used to characterize the secondary structure of proteins [58]. Fig. 4D showed the FTIR spectra of the six types of nanofibrils. In the amide I absorption band, ultrasound caused shifts of 4.93 cm⁻1, 5.54 cm⁻1, and 9.58 cm⁻1 for the spectra of U-SPIN, U-11SN, and U-7SN respectively, indicating that ultrasound changed the secondary structures of the three protein nanofibrils and to the characteristic stretching vibration of the peptide backbone C=O bond [59]. In the amide II absorption band, ultrasound caused shifts of 2.54 cm⁻1, 9.16 cm⁻1, and 10.42 cm⁻1 for SPI, 11S, and 7S, respectively. These shifts reflect changes in the quantity or structure of carboxyl groups (–COO), indicating the presence of intramolecular and intermolecular hydrogen bonding interactions [60]. These changes indicated that ultrasound enhanced the C-N bending vibration and also promoted molecular unfolding and protein expansion in space during nanofiber formation [61]. The 7S fraction was more susceptible to ultrasound, which induced more pronounced structural alterations during fibrillation. Also indicated that 7S had a stronger fibril-forming ability, and the self-assembly process involved not only secondary structural shifts but also more extensive structural reorganization, further corroborating earlier experimental findings.

Functional property analysis

Solubility

As a basic property of proteins, solubility affects various important functional properties of nanofibrils. Fig. 6A showed the solubility of samples. Ultrasonic treatment increased the solubility of U-SPIN, U-11SN, and U-7SN by 4.9%, 3.2%, and 4.8%, respectively, with U-7SN exhibiting the highest solubility of 80.9%. Ultrasound improved the solubility of nanofibrils because ultrasound promoted protein unfolding, induced peptide chain unfolding to expose hydrophilic groups and thereby enhanced protein-water interactions, leading to improved solubility [62]. Emulsifying properties

The emulsifying properties of nanofibrils from the three soy protein components with and without ultrasonic treatment were evaluated by emulsifying activity index (EAI) and emulsifying stability index (ESI) (Fig. 6B). EAI was defined as the ability of proteins to prevent flocculation by adsorbing and accumulating at the oil–water interface. The EAI of C-7SN group was significantly higher than C-SPIN and C-11SN. After ultrasound treatment, the EAI of the three proteins increased significantly, which could be because ultrasound treatment increased the surface area-to-volume ratio by changing the protein structure. In this case, more proteins participated in the formation of the interface layer, improving the emulsification efficiency [64]. ESI referred to the emulsion stabilization capacity, ultrasound pretreatment enhanced the ESI of nanofibrils. Ultrasound also enhanced nanofibril solubility, promoting their accelerated dispersion and adsorption at the interface, leading to the enhancement of EAI and ESI [65]. Ultrasound treatment shortened the fibrils, enhancing surface hydrophobicity facilitated faster protein adsorption at the interface, thereby forming a denser film and improving emulsifying activity. Previous research has indicated that reduced fibril length may facilitate the formation of a thicker interfacial layer, contributing to improved emulsion stability [66]. The superior emulsifying performance of U-7SN was likely attributable to its higher hydrophobicity, which facilitated more effective adsorption of oil droplets.

Rheological properties

Viscosity referred to the resistance of a fluid to flow and was related to molecular interactions. Fig. 6C showed the apparent viscosities of nanofibrils from the three soy protein components with and without ultrasonic treatment, and Fig. 6D showed the changes in shear stress. Ultrasound treatment resulted in the highest viscosity of U-7SN could stemmed primarily from the combined effects of physical forces and enhanced intermolecular entanglement generated by cavitation bubble oscillation and collapse [67]. Similar conclusions were reached by T. Li et al. [42] when investigating the viscosity of legume proteins.

Conclusion

In summary, ultrasonic pretreatment differentially enhanced the fibrillation capabilities of different soy protein components. Ultrasound treatment accelerated the conversion of proteins into low‑molecular‑weight peptides and promoted the fibrillation process by enhancing surface hydrophobicity and π‑π stacking interactions among aromatic amino acids. It also led to a significant reduction in particle size, generating abundant, short, and well‑dispersed fibrils. The 7S fraction exhibited greater sensitivity to ultrasonication, undergoing notable structural rearrangements during fibrillation and forming a higher content of β‑sheet structures. More importantly, the modified nanofibrils demonstrated superior solubility, ESI, EAI, and viscosity. These results elucidate the mechanism underlying ultrasound-assisted fibrillation and provide a conceptual framework to guide the strategic development of plant-protein nanofibrils for food and material applications.
