Bilirubin-polymer nanocarriers enable targeted farrerol delivery for glaucoma neuroprotection via Nrf2-mediated ferroptosis/apoptosis inhibition
Authors: Jing Wang, Yixian Liu, Rong Rong, Xiaobo Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Glaucoma, a leading cause of irreversible blindness, is characterized by progressive retinal ganglion cell (RGC) degeneration with limited treatment options. The natural flavonoid farrerol exhibits neuroprotective potential through antioxidative and anti-inflammatory effects, but its therapeutic application is limited by poor bioavailability and photodegradation. This study investigates farrerol's neuroprotective mechanisms and develops an optimized nanoformulation for enhanced efficacy. In vitro oxygen-glucose deprivation/reperfusion (OGD/R) and in vivo ischemia-reperfusion (IR) models demonstrate that farrerol significantly improves RGC survival and visual function. Mechanistic studies reveal that farrerol activates the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) antioxidant pathway, reduces reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and modulates key ferroptosis markers (glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) and acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4)), thereby inhibiting RGC apoptosis. To overcome delivery limitations, farrerol-loaded bilirubin nanoparticles (FB-NPs) are developed, showing enhanced stability and neuroprotective effects in glaucomatous injury models. These findings identify the Nrf2/ferroptosis/apoptosis axis as a novel therapeutic target and present an effective nanodelivery strategy for glaucoma treatment.

Introduction

Glaucoma, a leading global cause of irreversible blindness, represents a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by the progressive degeneration of retinal ganglion cell (RGCs). [1] The disease manifests clinically through distinct pathological features including optic nerve damage, visual field deficits, and characteristic optic disc changes. [2] While elevated intraocular pressure (IOP) has been well-established as a major risk factor in glaucoma pathogenesis, [[3], [4], [5]] current therapeutic strategies focusing solely on IOP reduction through pharmacological, laser, or surgical interventions demonstrate limited efficacy in halting disease progression. Notably, these conventional approaches fail to completely prevent the ongoing deterioration of visual function, and a significant proportion of patients develop glaucoma without measurable IOP elevation. [1,2,6] These clinical observations strongly suggest that non-IOP-independent mechanisms significantly contribute to RGC degeneration, necessitating the urgent need for developing neuroprotective therapies that directly target RGC survival pathways. [[7], [8], [9]]

Current evidence indicates that RGC injury and death in glaucoma result from the synergistic effects of multiple pathogenic factors, including mechanical stress, oxidative damage, mitochondrial dysfunction, and glutamate excitotoxicity. [10] The nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) plays a central role in regulating these process. [[11], [12], [13]] Under physiological conditions, Nrf2 is sequestered in the cytoplasm through its binding with Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1). Upon oxidative stress or other cellular insults, Nrf2 dissociates from Keap1, translocates into the nucleus, and binds to antioxidant response elements (AREs) to initiate the transcription of cytoprotective genes. [14,15] Growing evidence highlights Nrf2's protective role in ocular diseases. Zhao et al. [16] demonstrated that Nrf2 knockout mice develop age-related macular degeneration (AMD)- like pathological changes, including drusen-like deposits and retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) degeneration. Natural compounds like quercetin exert protective effects by activating the Nrf2/Heme Oxygenase-1 (HO-1) pathway. [17] Similarly, Wan et al. [18] reported that trimetazidine protects RGCs in glaucoma models via Nrf2-mediated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. Our previous work also further supported that Nrf2 could mitigate glutamate excitotoxicity-induced RGC death. [19] Consequently, developing neuroprotective agents that specifically target the Nrf2 signaling pathway to ameliorate RGC death represents a promising therapeutic strategy for glaucoma management.

Traditional Chinese medicinal herbs and their bioactive derivatives have demonstrated broad therapeutic potential in diverse pathological conditions. Notably, flavonoids - polyphenolic compounds with potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-angiogenic properties - show particular promise for treating neurodegenerative and ocular diseases. [[20], [21], [22]] Farrerol, a bioactive flavonoid isolated from the traditional medicinal plant Rhododendron, exhibits multiple pharmacological properties [23] including antioxidant, [24] anti-inflammatory [25] and antitumor [26] activities. Previous studies [24,27] have demonstrated that farrerol activates Nrf2-mediated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects by inducing Nrf2 nuclear translocation, thereby exhibiting potent neuroprotective properties. Additionally, studies have reported that farrerol ameliorates RPE cell death in AMD, attributable to its ability to regulate Nrf2-mediated antioxidative stress responses. [28] These results position farrerol as a promising therapeutic candidate for glaucoma. However, its clinical translation is limited by poor water solubility, low bioavailability, and photosensitivity. To overcome these limitations, we engineered a novel reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive nanotherapeutic platform (FB-NPs) through the molecular encapsulation of farrerol within a bilirubin-conjugated polymer matrix, specifically designed to preserve RGC viability in glaucomatous conditions (Scheme 1).Scheme 1((Schematic illustration of FB-NPs delivering farrerol to activate Nrf2 pathway and inhibit RGC degeneration.)).Scheme 1

Materials and methods

Preparation and characterization of FB-NPs

Firstly, the bilirubin polymer was prepared as follows: Bilirubin (50 mg, Shanghai Titan Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was added to a flask and completely dissolved in a small amount of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, D103277, Aladdin, Shanghai, China) and triethylamine (A659355, Aladdin). Subsequently, DSPE-PEG2K-NH2 (1.0 eq, Guangzhou Weihua Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, China), dissolved in chloroform (C100655, Aladdin), and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP, 0.5 eq, D109207, Aladdin) were added to the solution. The mixture was stirred until clear and then cooled in an ice bath. Dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC, 1.1 eq, D106074, Aladdin) was slowly added dropwise to the reaction mixture. After the addition, the reaction was allowed to proceed at room temperature for 2 h. The reaction mixture was concentrated under vacuum, and the crude product was precipitated using diethyl ether (D667838, Aladdin). The precipitate was dried under vacuum, redissolved in dichloromethane (D116143, Aladdin), and filtered to remove insoluble impurities. The dissolution and filtration steps were repeated twice. The filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure to yield a viscous liquid. The product was further purified by redissolving in ethanol, precipitating with diethyl ether, and washing twice with diethyl ether. Finally, the product was dried to obtain the bilirubin polymer.

Preparation of FB-NPs: Briefly, a solution of farrerol (2 mg, T6S0525, Topscience, Shanghai, China) and bilirubin polymer (20 mg) in DMSO (2 mL) were thoroughly mixed and then dripped into 20 mL of deionized water under high-speed stirring. Subsequently, the mixture was dialyzed in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 8000 Da) for 24 h. The liquid inside the dialysis bag was collected and concentrated using a 10 kDa ultrafiltration tube to obtain the final FB-NPs solution.

Cell culture and establishment of OGD/R model

The R28 retinal cell line was used to construct an in vitro model. The R28 retinal cells are retinal precursor cells derived from rat retina. [29] They possess both neuronal and glial cell characteristics and are commonly used for in vitro studies of neuronal function and neuroprotection. [30,31] R28 cells were cultured in low-glucose DMEM medium (BasalMedia, Shanghai, China) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (v/v, FBS, AUS-01S-02, CellBox, Australia) and 1 % Penicillin/Streptomycin Plus-MycoZap (v/v, ECOTOP, Guangzhou, China) in an atmosphere containing 5 % CO2 (v/v). For the OGD/R model, R28 cells were plated and grown for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with glucose-free DMEM, and the cells were incubated in a hypoxic chamber (1 % O2, v/v) for 6 h, followed by reoxygenation in low-glucose DMEM for 12 h. The control cells were maintained in low-glucose DMEM in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2.

Animal IR model and drug delivery

An in vivo IR model was established by elevating the intraocular pressure (120 mmHg) in wild-type male C57BL/6J mice (8 weeks, 20–25 g) for 60 min. A 30G needle connected to a saline reservoir was inserted into the anterior chamber of the mouse eye, and the pressure was maintained at 120 mmHg by adjusting the height of the saline reservoir. For drug delivery, farrerol and FB-NPs were administered via intravitreal injection using a Hamilton syringe equipped with a glass microneedle under a dissection microscope one day prior to IR induction. All animal procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Experimental Animal Center, Xiangya School of Medicine, Central South University. Each experimental group included at least six animals (n ≧ 6).

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, ECOTOP) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The CCK-8 assay gauges global cellular metabolic status by quantifying the activity of NAD(P)H-dependent oxidoreductases, thereby capturing the entire spectrum of cell states—healthy, stressed, apoptotic, and even early-necrotic cells—within a single measurement. R28 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells per well and cultured overnight. OGD/R modeling and subsequent drug intervention were then conducted. After a 12-h reoxygenation period, 10 μL of the CCK-8 detection reagent was added to 100 μL of culture medium in each well. The plates were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Calcein acetoxymethyl ester (Calcein AM) and propidium iodide (PI) dual staining assay

The cell death rate of R28 cells was assessed using the Calcein AM/PI dual staining assay (KGAF001, KeyGEN Biotech, Jiangsu, China). Calcein-AM/PI double staining rapidly reports viability: intact membranes let lipophilic Calcein-AM enter live cells, where esterase cleaves it to green-fluorescent Calcein AM; membrane-compromised cells admit PI, which binds DNA and glows red. R28 cells were seeded in 24-well plates, cultured for 24 h, and then subjected to OGD/R and drug treatment. The cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, G4202, Servicebio, Wuhan, China), incubated with PI and Calcein AM at 37 °C for 30 min, and imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Germany). For double-blind analysis, five random images per group were captured, and the percentage of cell death was calculated by counting PI-positive and Calcein AM-stained cells.

Annexin V-FITC/PI double staining

Apoptosis and necrosis in R28 cells following OGD/R treatment and drug intervention were analyzed using an Annexin V-FITC kit (F6012, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). After 12 h of reoxygenation, R28 cells were collected with EDTA-free trypsin (ES-8442, ECOTOP), resuspended in PBS, and stained with 5 μL Annexin V-FITC and 10 μL PI for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. The stained cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry. Cells were categorized according to the staining results of Annexin V (AV) and PI: AV−/PI− (live), AV+/PI− (early apoptosis), AV−/PI+ (necrosis), and AV+/PI+ (late apoptosis). Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Ashland, OR, USA).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

R28 cells/retinas were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, G1101, Servicebio) for 20 min. They were then washed three times with PBS for 5 min each and permeabilized with a PBS solution containing 0.1 % Triton-X-100 (T8200, Solarbio, Beijing, China) (PBST) for 10 min or 0.3 % PBST for 1 h. After blocking with 5 % bovine serum albumin (BSA, 4240GR02S, NeoFroxx, Germany) for 1 h, they were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-Nrf2 (1:100, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech, WuHan, China), anti-ACSL4 (1:200, ab155282, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-GPX4 (1:200, ab125066, Abcam),or anti-Brn3a (1:500, ab245230, Abcam) all diluted in 5 % BSA. Following three washes with PBS, the cells/tissues were incubated for 60 min with anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488 (1:100,4412, Cell Signaling Technology, Boston, USA), Actin-Tracker Green-488 (1:200, C2201S, Beyotime), or Actin-Tracker Red-594 (1:200, C2205S, Beyotime) in the presence of 5 % BSA. The cells/retinas were washed three times with PBS for 5 min, and stained with DAPI (ES-8244, ECOTOP). For immunofluorescence imaging of cells, a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8) and a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica) were used to capture at least five random images for each experimental condition. For retinal flat-mounted immunofluorescence, each retina was divided into four quadrants (sharing a central quadrant). For each quadrant, three regions were defined based on their distance from the optic nerve: central, middle, and peripheral. The density of RGCs was assessed by counting Brn3a-positive cells in these regions using Adobe Photoshop (RGCs per square millimeter). The relative RGC density in the central, middle, and peripheral regions was calculated using the control group as a reference. Statistical analysis of the percentage of Brn3a-positive cells in each group was conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

ROS production detection

The intracellular ROS levels were measured using the ROS Assay Kit (S0033S, Beyotime). After OGD/R and drug treatment, R28 cells were incubated with 10 μM DCFH-DA (diluted 1:1000 in low-glucose serum-free medium) at 37 °C in the dark for 30 min. Then, the cells were washed three times with low-glucose serum-free medium, observed, and photographed under a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). The fluorescence intensity was semi-quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software, and the relative percentage of fluorescence intensity among groups was statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software.

Histological analysis

Mice were anesthetized and sacrificed. The eyeballs were enucleated and fixed in 4 % PFA at room temperature for 24 h. After washing with PBS, the eyes were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 3 μm. The sections were then dewaxed and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), and imaged using an optical microscope (Nikon, Japan). Images were randomly captured by researchers who were blinded to the study. To assess the damage, the number of cells in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) of each section containing the optic nerve center was counted.

Flash visual-evoked potential (FVEP) detection

To evaluate visual function, FVEP were measured on the 2nd and 7th day after modeling. Mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital and underwent 15 min of dark adaptation. Electrodes were placed subcutaneously at the back (ground electrode), anterior fontanelle (cathode), and occipital bone (anode). The contralateral eye was covered with thick gauze and tin foil to prevent light interference. Flash stimulation was delivered using a multifocal electroretinography recorder (GT-2008 V-VI, Gotec, Chongqing, China). The amplitude and latency of the first positive wave were subsequently analyzed to assess visual function.

Transmission electron Microscope(TEM)

Following IR modeling and subsequent drug treatment, the retinas were harvested from the mice and immediately fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde (G105907, Aladdin) at 4 °C for 24 h. The retinas were then washed with PBS three times for 10 min each, followed by fixation with 1 % osmium tetroxide for 3 h. Afterward, the samples were rinsed thoroughly with PBS three times for 10 min each. The samples were then subjected to gradient dehydration using ethanol and acetone, embedded in epoxy resin, and sectioned into ultrathin slices of 50–60 nm. Finally, the sections were stained with 3 % uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined under an HT7700 transmission electron microscope (HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining assay

To examine the impact of IR modeling and drug intervention on RGC apoptosis in mice, a TUNEL kit (T6013, Uelandy, Suzhou, China) was utilized. For TUNEL staining of paraffin-embedded retinal sections, slides were dewaxed with xylene (X112050, Aladdin) for 20 min, twice, and then rehydrated through a descending ethanol series (100 %, 90 %, 80 %, and 70 %) for 5 min each. Following this, the TUNEL reaction solution was applied to the samples and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h according to the manufacturer's instructions. The sections were subsequently washed three times with 1 × PBS for 5 min each and stained with DAPI to visualize nuclei. Fluorescence imaging was performed using a Ni-U microscope (Nikon). The number of TUNEL-positive cells in the GCL of each retinal section was quantified.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure of the small molecule in SDF format was downloaded from PubChem based on its CAS number. The structure was then imported into ChemBio3D Ultra 14.0 for energy minimization, with the “Minimum RMS Gradient” set to 0.001, and the optimized structure was saved in MOL2 format. The optimized small molecule was subsequently imported into AutoDockTools-1.5.6 for hydrogenation, charge calculation, charge assignment, and setting of rotatable bonds, and then saved in PDBQT format.

The Nrf2 protein (PDB ID: 7O7B) was downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Water molecules and original ligands were removed from the protein structure using PyMOL 2.3.0. The protein structure was then imported into AutoDockTools (v1.5.6) for hydrogenation, charge calculation, charge assignment, and atom type designation, and saved in PDBQT format. The protein binding site was predicted using POCASA 1.1, and molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina 1.1.2. The interaction patterns of the docking results were analyzed using PyMOL 2.3.0 and LigPlot V2.2.8.

Western blot (WB) analysis

After modeling and drug intervention, proteins were extracted from R28 cells and retinas using RIPA lysis buffer (WB3100, NCM Biotech, Suzhou, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (P002, NCM Biotech). The lysates were homogenized for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The protein samples were mixed with 5 × loading buffer (WB0172, NCM Biotech) and boiled at 95 °C for 10 min. The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes.The membranes were blocked with 5 % skim milk (1172GR100, NeoFroxx) for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-NFE2L2 (Nrf2, 1:1000, 16396-1-AP, Proteintech), anti-ACSL4 (1:10000, ab155282, Abcam), anti-GPX4 (1:2000, ab125066, Abcam), anti-β-actin (1:10000, 81115-1-RR, Proteintech), anti-Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH, 1:10000, 60004-1-Ig, Proteintech), and anti-Histone H3 (1:1000, AF0863, Affinity, Jiangsu, China). After washing three times with TBS containing 0.1 % Tween 20 (BL703A, Biosharp, Hefei, China) (TBST), the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (SA00001-2 and SA00001-1, Proteintech). The membranes were then washed three times with TBST, and proteins were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) method (P102008, NCM Biotech) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Imaging was performed using a chemiluminescence imager (Bio-Rad, California, USA). Specific protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software by measuring integrated density values. β-actin, Histone H3, and GAPDH were used as internal controls.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) from a minimum of three independent experiments. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons among multiple groups, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test. For comparisons between two groups, Student's t-test was used. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Farrerol alleviates OGD/R-induced pathological damage and death in R28 cells

Several studies were performed to evaluate the protective effects and safety of farrerol in vitro (Fig. 1A). To determine whether farrerol exerts a protective effect on R28 cells in an in vitro model of glaucomatous pathological injury and to identify the optimal protective concentration, we established an OGD/R model to simulate the hypoxic and hypoglycemic pathological processes observed in glaucoma. Quantification by CCK-8 assay showed a marked 30 % reduction (p < 0.001) in overall metabolic activity following OGD/R insult compared to normoxic controls, indicating substantial cellular dysfunction. After that, R28 cell viability was assessed following treatment with varying concentrations of farrerol. Results demonstrated that farrerol concentrations ranging from 5 μM to 50 μM exhibited no significant cytotoxicity (P > 0.05). Notably, at a concentration of 20 μM, farrerol provided more effective protection at a relatively lower dose (P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B and C). Consequently, 20 μM was selected for subsequent in vitro studies. To further evaluate the protective effects of farrerol on R28 cells, Calcein AM/PI double staining was employed to visualize cell survival. The results revealed that farrerol significantly reduced OGD/R-induced R28 cell death (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1D and F). Notably, while the CCK-8 assay detected approximately 30 % cellular impairment, PI staining revealed less than 10 % terminal damaged cells (PI-positive). This discrepancy suggests that OGD/R-induced cellular injury exhibits multiphasic characteristics, involving distinct stages of cellular damage. Farrerol inhibits ferroptosis and apoptosis in R28 cells by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway

Farrerol as a natural flavonoid compound, has shown potential in treating oxidative stress. Nrf2, a transcription factor closely associated with oxidative stress, plays a significant role in farrerol-related research. [14] Previous studies also revealed that Nrf2 can mitigate R28 cell damage in glaucomatous pathological conditions and protect visual function in a glutamate excitotoxicity model. [19] Therefore, to further investigate the specific mechanisms by which farrerol exerts its protective effects in a glaucomatous pathological injury model, we first performed molecular docking. The results indicated that farrerol and Nrf2 primarily interact through hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions, with a binding energy of −6.1 kcal mol-1 which suggested a strong affinity (Fig. 1I). Additionally, ROS play a critical role in oxidative stress and mitochondrial ferroptosis. To determine whether farrerol can scavenge ROS in vitro, fluorescence microscopy revealed that farrerol effectively cleared ROS induced by OGD/R (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1J and Fig. S1A). Furthermore, Western blot analysis was used to assess Nrf2 protein levels in R28 cells after OGD/R treatment. The results demonstrated that Nrf2 expression showed no significant difference between the OGD/R group (6-h hypoxia-glucose deprivation followed by 12-h reperfusion) and the control group (P > 0.05), whereas farrerol treatment significantly upregulated Nrf2 (P < 0.01) (Fig. S3A). However, when the duration of reperfusion was extended to 18 h, a marked decrease in Nrf2 expression was observed in the OGD/R group (P < 0.001). Notably, farrerol administration still effectively elevated Nrf2 levels under these severe conditions (P < 0.01) (Fig. 1K). The time-dependent decline in Nrf2 expression suggests progressive exhaustion of endogenous antioxidant capacity. Importantly, farrerol demonstrates potent cytoprotective effects by effectively activating the Nrf2 pathway, thereby safeguarding R28 cells against OGD/R-induced injury.

Nrf2 primarily resides in the cytoplasm in resting conditions and translocates to the nucleus upon exposure to oxidative stress or other injuries to regulate the transcription of downstream target genes such as Glutathione Peroxidase 4 (GPX4), Acyl-CoA Synthetase Long-Chain Family Member 4 (ACSL4), NAD(P)H: quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), and HO-1. [14] Thus, we first performed immunofluorescence staining on R28 cells and results demonstrated that both OGD/R modeling and farrerol induced Nrf2 nuclear translocation, with farrerol showing a more pronounced promotion of Nrf2 translocation (Fig. 2A). Next, nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins were isolated and Western blot analysis was performed to detect Nrf2 expression levels in these fractions. Following 6 h of oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) and 12 h of reperfusion (OGD/R), the results indicated no significant difference in Nrf2 expression in the cytoplasmic proteins (P > 0.5) and in nuclear proteins (P > 0.05). but Nrf2 expression was significantly upregulated in the nuclear proteins following farrerol treatment (P < 0.0001) (Fig. S3B). Upon extending the reperfusion duration to 18 h, Western blot analysis revealed a significant decrease in nuclear Nrf2 expression following OGD/R injury (P < 0.01). Notably, farrerol treatment effectively counteracted this reduction, demonstrating a marked upregulation of nuclear Nrf2 levels (p < 0.0001)(Fig. 2B). These findings demonstrate that farrerol effectively enhances Nrf2 expression in R28 cells under OGD/R conditions. Moreover, immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis were conducted to determine the expression levels of Nrf2 pathway-related proteins in R28 cells after OGD/R injury. As shown in Fig. 2C, D and Fig. S1B and C, immunofluorescence results revealed that the OGD/R model significantly upregulated ACSL4 expression (P < 0.0001) and downregulated GPX4 expression (P < 0.01) in R28 cells, while farrerol treatment downregulated ACSL4 expression (P < 0.0001) and significantly restored GPX4 expression (P < 0.01). Western blot results were consistent with the immunofluorescence findings, showing that farrerol treatment reduced OGD/R-induced ACSL4 upregulation (P < 0.0001) and restored OGD/R-induced GPX4 downregulation (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2E and F). Farrerol reduces RGCs death in IR model mice in vivo

Several studies have evaluated the protective effects and safety of farrerol in mice in vivo (Fig. 3A). To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of farrerol, we first assessed its protective effects and toxicity by intravitreally injecting different concentrations of farrerol into mice one day before IR modeling. Retinas were collected from each group one day after modeling, and flat mounts were prepared to quantify RGCs using Brain-specific homeobox/POU domain protein 3A (Brn3a) staining. The results revealed that 1 mM farrerol significantly protected RGCs in IR model mice (P < 0.01, P < 0.0001, P > 0.05), whereas 5 mM farrerol exhibited toxicity to RGCs (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3C–E, F, G). Based on these findings, 1 mM farrerol was selected for subsequent in vivo experiments. Given that oxidative stress- and ferroptosis-related protein expression exhibits significant alteration at least one days after glaucomatous injury, [[32], [33], [34]] we focused on analyzing these indicators two days after IR modeling. Similarly, immunofluorescence staining of mouse retinas was performed. As shown in Fig. 4A–C, D, and E, the number of Brn3a-positive cells decreased significantly two days after IR modeling (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001), while farrerol treatment effectively reduced RGC loss in IR model mice (P < 0.01, P > 0.5, P < 0.05). To further explore the protective effects of farrerol, we evaluated its impact using H&E staining. The results demonstrated that farrerol provided partial protection against IR-induced retinal damage. The number of cells in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) of the retina decreased significantly after IR modeling (P < 0.0001), and farrerol partially restored the cell count in the GCL (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B–F and Fig. S1D). TUNEL staining was used to assess the effect of farrerol on retinal cell apoptosis. The results showed a significant increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the retina after IR modeling, particularly in the GCL and outer nuclear layer (ONL), while farrerol reduced the number of apoptotic cells (P < 0.01) (Fig. 5A–C). These findings demonstrated farrerol's protective effects at the morphological level. Furthermore, we evaluated whether farrerol could improve visual function by using flash visual-evoked potential (FVEP), which reflect the integrity of the visual pathway from the retina to the occipital cortex. The amplitude of the N1-P1 wave was significantly reduced (P < 0.0001), and the latency of the P1 wave was significantly delayed (P < 0.0001) in IR model mice compared to the control group. Pretreatment with farrerol could restore the amplitude of the N1-P1 wave (P < 0.0001) and reduce the latency of the P1 wave (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5B–D,E), indicating partial restoration of visual function. Farrerol inhibits ferroptosis and apoptosis of RGCs in IR model mice by modulating the Nrf2 signaling pathway

To further investigate whether farrerol inhibits ferroptosis and apoptosis by modulating the Nrf2 signaling pathway in vivo, as observed in vitro, we first examined the morphology of RGCs in mice using transmission electron microscope (TEM). After IR modeling, RGCs exhibited typical morphological features of ferroptosis and apoptosis, including mitochondrial swelling, disrupted and reduced mitochondrial cristae, outer membrane rupture, and nuclear pyknosis and fragmentation. In contrast, farrerol reduced mitochondrial volume and restored the integrity of mitochondrial cristae, outer membranes, and nuclei integrity (Fig. 5F and Fig. S1E). Then we analyzed the protein expression levels of Nrf2 and ferroptosis-related proteins in vivo. The results demonstrated that farrerol restored the IR-induced reduction in Nrf2 and GPX4 expression (P < 0.0001, P < 0.0001) and downregulated the IR-induced increase in ACSL4 expression (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5G, H, I, J), consistent with the in vitro findings. These results indicate that farrerol can inhibit IR-induced apoptosis and ferroptosis by regulating the Nrf2 pathway, ultimately protecting RGCs.

Preparation and characterization of FB-NPs

Our experiments demonstrated farrerol's broad therapeutic potential for ocular diseases. However, its clinical application is limited by low bioavailability via oral or topical administration, the blood-ocular barrier, and the requirement for light-protected and cold storage. To address these limitations, we developed a nanodrug system (FB-NPs) based on a bilirubin polymer loaded with farrerol. First, bilirubin was modified into a drug-loadable polymer, and was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Fig. S1F). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis revealed the bilirubin polymer had an average size of 32.09 nm and a PDI of 0.163 (Fig. S1G). FB-NPs were then constructed via the nanoprecipitation method. To confirm FB-NPs formation and evaluate its properties, we observed a distinct Tyndall effect upon laser irradiation, indicating a stable colloidal dispersion and TEM showed a uniform spherical morphology with a relative size of ∼28 nm (Fig. 6D). DLS analysis showed an average size of 31.19 nm, and a PDI of 0.339 (Fig. 6A), a zeta potential of −17.58 mV (Fig. 6B). High performance liquid chromatograph (HPLC) analysis confirmed successful farrerol loading, with both farrerol and FB-NPs exhibiting peaks at the same retention time (Fig. 6C). FB-NPs protects R28 cells from OGD/R model-induced cell death

In vitro evaluation of FB-NPs' protective effects on R28 cells was systematically conducted. Initial biocompatibility assessment using CCK-8 revealed the bilirubin polymer vehicle displayed excellent cytocompatibility at concentrations up to 0.5 mg mL−1 (p > 0.05), with significant cytotoxicity only emerging at 1 mg mL−1 (p < 0.0001) (Fig. S2A). Of particular interest, the 0.2 mg mL−1 concentration demonstrated measurable protection against OGD/R-induced cellular injury (p < 0.01) (Fig. S2B), while recapitulating the cytotoxic profile observed in normoxic conditions at higher concentrations (1 mg mL−1). We then diluted the FB-NPs, whose concentration was determined to be approximately 335.9 μM, 20- to 75-fold for subsequent in vitro cell experiments. Subsequent cellular uptake studies using FB-NPs-FITC confirmed time-dependent internalization by R28 cells (Fig. 6E). CCK-8 assays demonstrated excellent biocompatibility of FB-NPs across a wide dilution range (20-75 × , p > 0.05) (Figs. S2C and 6F). Importantly, optimal neuroprotection was achieved at 50 × dilution (containing approximately 6.78 μM farrerol), which provided superior protection at relatively low concentration (p < 0.0001) (Figs. S2D and 6G). Quantitative analysis demonstrated that FB-NPs conferred significant cytoprotection, exhibiting a 62.7 ± 6.4 % reduction in OGD/R-induced apoptosis compared to 37.9 ± 8.3 % for free farrerol (p < 0.001). Subsequent evaluation of ROS levels revealed that both the bilirubin polymer (p < 0.05) and FB-NPs (p < 0.0001) displayed potent ROS-scavenging activity. Although no statistically significant difference was observed in quantitative measurements (p > 0.05), microscopic analysis suggested a marginally enhanced protective trend for FB-NPs relative to free farrerol treatment (Figs. S2E, F, G and Figure 6H).

FB-NPs inhibits ferroptosis and apoptosis to protect RGCs in IR model mice

We conducted several studies to evaluate the protective effects and safety of FB-NPs in vivo (Fig. 7A). Immunofluorescence showed that intravitreal injection of FB-NPs-FITC resulted in its uptake by RGCs in the GCL, indicating that FB-NPs can penetrate the mouse retina (Fig. 7B). Quantitative immunofluorescence analysis of retinal sections demonstrated that FB-NPs significantly attenuated IR injury-induced RGC apoptosis (Fig. 7C–E, F, G). Regional quantification revealed that FB-NPs provided superior neuroprotection compared to free farrerol in both the peripheral region (72.75 ± 14.9 % vs 62.51 ± 10.1 %, p = 0.035) and middle region (87.42 ± 11.6 % vs 72.16 ± 8.6 %, p = 0.008). However, no statistically significant difference was observed in the central region adjacent to the optic nerve (97.3 ± 6.2 % vs 92.5 ± 8.1 %, p = 0.54). Importantly, histopathological and functional assessments demonstrated the superior therapeutic potential of FB-NPs in retinal IR injury. Quantitative analysis of H&E-stained sections revealed preservation of retinal architecture and significant restoration of GCL cellularity following FB-NPs treatment (P < 0.0001), with the nanoformulation showing enhanced protective effects compared to free farrerol as evidenced by greater statistical significance (P < 0.0001 vs P < 0.001, respectively) despite comparable effect sizes (P > 0.05 for inter-group comparison) (Fig. 7D–H and Figure S2H). This observation may be ascribed to the inherent limitations of H&E staining, which targets only retinal sections and thus cannot comprehensively reflect the total loss of retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) across the entire retinal architecture. Functional visual assessment by FVEP demonstrated that FB-NPs were more effective in rescuing N1-P1 wave amplitude compared to farrerol (P < 0.01), suggesting superior recovery of RGC function. However, for P1 latency improvement, while FB-NPs showed higher statistical significance (P < 0.001 vs P < 0.01), the difference between treatments was less pronounced (Fig. 7I, J, K). This may be attributed to the fact that the IR model induced concurrent damage to both RGCs (the primary pathology) and optic nerve structures (e.g., nerve swelling). As N1-P1 amplitude primarily reflects RGC activity, FB-NPs demonstrated clearer therapeutic advantages in this parameter, whereas P1 latency, being influenced by both neural conduction and synaptic transmission, showed relatively smaller intergroup differences due to persistent optic nerve damage in both treatment groups. Western blot analysis further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying FB-NPs' neuroprotective effects, demonstrating significant upregulation of key proteins following treatment. Specifically, FB-NPs effectively reversed IR-induced downregulation of Nrf2 (P < 0.001) and GPX4 (P < 0.0001), while markedly suppressing ACSL4 expression (P < 0.0001) compared to IR controls (Fig. 7L, M, N, O). Discussion

Glaucoma is an irreversible blinding disease marked by progressive optic nerve damage and visual field loss. While current IOP-lowering therapies remain the mainstay of clinical management, their inability to prevent ongoing neurodegeneration highlights the critical need for novel neuroprotective strategies. Therefore, future glaucoma therapies should move beyond IOP-lowering strategies alone and develop comprehensive interventions targeting RGC survival. [35] Farrerol, a flavonoid compound isolated from Rhododendron species, has emerged as a promising multimodal therapeutic agent. Beyond its well-documented anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, growing evidence suggests significant neuroprotective potential across various neurodegenerative contexts, including Parkinson's disease (PD), Alzheimer's disease (AD), and hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE). [27,36,37] However, its therapeutic efficacy in glaucomatous neurodegeneration, particularly regarding RGC protection, has remained unexplored until now.

Building on these foundations, we systematically evaluated its efficacy using well-established OGD/R and IR models that recapitulate glaucomatous pathological damage in vitro and in vivo. Our findings demonstrate that farrerol treatment can rescue R28 cell viability and inhibit apoptosis in vitro, while similarly protecting RGCs and restoring visual conduction function in vivo. Current research indicates that RGC death involves complex interactions among multiple mechanisms including neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and autophagy. [38] Persistent oxidative stress is particularly critical for RGC degeneration. During glaucomatous pathology, sustained ROS production and/or defective antioxidant systems exacerbate mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxidation, accelerating RGC death. As a pivotal nuclear transcription factor governing anti-inflammatory and antioxidant responses, Nrf2 plays a crucial role in the pathological progression of glaucoma. Current evidence suggests that under mild stress conditions, Nrf2 becomes activated to mediate compensatory redox adaptation [39]; however, its expression progressively declines with escalating injury severity, potentially reflecting antioxidant system exhaustion and exacerbated cell death [[40], [41], [42]]. Our study corroborates this dynamic pattern in glaucoma models: R28 cells exhibited either mild upregulation or stable Nrf2 expression during early-stage stress, whereas intensified injury led to Nrf2 depletion and significant downregulation. Previous studies have shown that farrerol can alleviate H2O2-induced oxidative stress by activating the Nrf2 signaling pathway. [28] Additionally, Li Y et al. demonstrated that farrerol acts as a novel Nrf2 activator to inhibit ferroptosis in a rat model of hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy. Their work showed farrerol treatment activated Nrf2 signaling, significantly reducing ROS and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels while upregulating GPX4 and Solute Carrier Family 7 Member 11 (SLC7A11) expression to mitigate ferroptosis. [27] Consistent with these findings, our results indicate that farrerol reduces ROS levels in R28 cells, promotes Nrf2 nuclear translocation, improves mitochondrial morphology, and inhibits ferroptosis by upregulating the ferroptosis marker GPX4 while suppressing ACSL4 expression. Thus, these findings establish that farrerol exerts potential protective effects against glaucomatous damage through coordinated activation of the Nrf2-mediated antioxidant response and subsequent inhibition of both oxidative stress and ferroptotic cell death pathways in retinal ganglion cells.

However, the therapeutic potential of farrerol is substantially constrained by its poor bioavailability, dose-dependent toxicity, and photosensitivity, necessitating the development of advanced delivery strategies. To address these limitations, we engineered a nanodelivery system to enhance the application of farrerol leveraging the unique advantages of nanomaterials, including high drug-loading capacity, targeted delivery potential, and enhanced biological barrier penetration - properties that have been extensively validated in biomedical applications. [[43], [44], [45]] This approach builds upon established nanotherapeutic successes, such as a nanofiber loaded with emodin for retinal ischemia-reperfusion injury and reduce neuroinflammation. [46] Additionally, a nanomaterial co-encapsulating brinzolamide and miRNA-124 has been proven dual IOP control and neuroprotection. [47] In our research, farrerol-encapsulated bilirubin polymer nanoparticles (FB-NPs) demonstrated remarkable efficacy in glaucomatous conditions, effectively scavenging ROS, potentiating Nrf2 pathway activation, and attenuating both oxidative stress and ferroptosis, resulting in significantly improved RGC survival. These findings not only validate the therapeutic advantages of nanoformulation but also provide a transformative framework for developing next-generation glaucoma therapeutics combining neuroprotection with enhanced drug delivery.

While our findings establish FB-NPs as promising neuroprotective agents, several limitations warrant consideration for future translational development. Specifically, the direct targeting of Nrf2 by farrerol was not fully validated due to the lack of interventions such as siRNA, lentiviral transfection, or specific inhibitors to modulate Nrf2 expression. Although our study confirmed the involvement of Nrf2, comprehensive analysis and functional validation of its downstream target genes (e.g., HO-1, NQO1, and GCLC) remain to be completed, which limits our understanding of the precise mechanism by which nuclear translocation of Nrf2 regulates RGC survival. Notably, FB-NPs demonstrated enhanced neuroprotective efficacy relative to free farrerol in the 7-day IR injury model. However, the chronic nature of glaucoma necessitates longer-term studies to assess sustained neuroprotection, cumulative treatment effects, and potential compensatory mechanisms that may emerge during prolonged therapy. Therefore, future studies should focus on implementing extended treatment regimens (6–12 months) in chronic glaucoma models and conducting thorough safety assessments of long-term nanoparticle exposure. These investigations will help translate the current mechanistic insights into clinically viable, sustained neuroprotective therapies for glaucoma.

Overall, this study establishes that farrerol confers retinal neuroprotection against glaucomatous injury through Nrf2-mediated amelioration of oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. Building upon this mechanistic foundation, we developed an innovative nanoformulation (FB-NPs) that overcomes the pharmacological limitations of free farrerol while enhancing its therapeutic potential. The successful integration of neuroprotective small molecules with advanced nanodelivery systems demonstrated here provides a transformative framework for developing next-generation glaucoma therapies. These findings not only advance our understanding of Nrf2-targeted neuroprotection but also offer a clinically relevant strategy to address the critical unmet need for disease-modifying treatments in glaucoma management.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that farrerol-loaded nanoparticles (FB-NPs) represent a promising dual-action therapeutic strategy for glaucoma, combining Nrf2-mediated neuroprotection against oxidative stress and ferroptosis with enhanced drug delivery capabilities, thereby addressing critical limitations of current treatment paradigms.
