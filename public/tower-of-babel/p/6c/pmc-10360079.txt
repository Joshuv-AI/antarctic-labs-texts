Activation of medial septum cholinergic neurons restores cognitive function in temporal lobe epilepsy
Authors: Junzi Chen, Yu Wang, Cong Chen, Qingyang Zhang, Shuang Wang, Yi Wang, Jiajia Fang, Ying Wang
Published: 2023

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is the most common complication in temporal lobe epilepsy (TLE) patients with hippocampal sclerosis (Jokeit et al., 2000; Keezer et al., 2016). Current anti-seizure drugs or surgeries are partially effective for epilepsy treatment, but often worsen the accompanying cognitive impairments, which lower a patient’s quality of life (GBD 2016 Epilepsy Collaborators, 2019; Kanner and Bicchi, 2022). Therefore, there is an urgent need to explore alternative therapeutic approaches and targets for the treatment of both TLE and related cognitive impairments.

The medial septum (MS) has reciprocal pathways with the hippocampus, which is a region related to both TLE development and cognitive functioning in humans (Do et al., 2016). Clinical imaging data have shown abnormal atrophy or enlargement of the MS in TLE patients with or without hippocampal sclerosis (Butler et al., 2013, 2014). Additionally, MS volume was positively correlated with recognition memory accuracy in humans (Butler et al., 2012). These clinical data indicate the MS is a key node in not only epilepsy but also memory-related circuits. Several preclinical studies have demonstrated a potential role of the MS in the treatment of both TLE and TLE-related cognitive deficits in epileptic animal models. For example, theta-frequency electrical stimulation at the MS elevated the seizure threshold (Izadi et al., 2019), inhibited epileptogenesis (Izadi et al., 2021), attenuated recurrent seizures (Wang et al., 2021) and terminated seizures (Takeuchi et al., 2021) in different epilepsy models. Impaired behavioral performance in novel object recognition (NOR) or spatial memory in epileptic rodents also was improved by septal stimulation (Izadi et al., 2019, 2021). However, the cellular mechanism for MS modulation of TLE-related cognitive deficits is not fully understood.

The MS is a complicated region where cholinergic, glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons are interwoven together and all send projections to the hippocampus (Bao et al., 2017; Wang et al., 2020; An et al., 2021). Cholinergic dysfunction in the hippocampus is particularly related to both seizures (Wang et al., 2020) and cognition (Anagnostaras et al., 2003; Cincotta et al., 2008). In the physiological condition, MS cholinergic neurons have been reported to modulate hippocampal synaptic plasticity (Gu and Yakel, 2011) and hippocampus-dependent memory (Haam et al., 2018), depending on acetylcholine (ACh) release and ACh receptors. We previously demonstrated that in the epileptic condition, an MS-hippocampal direct cholinergic circuit attenuated seizures through driving hippocampal somatostatin inhibition (Wang et al., 2020). However, the direct role of MS cholinergic neurons in epilepsy-related cognitive deficits remains unknown. Thus, in the present study, we aimed to investigate the pathological changes of the MS and cognitive deficits in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis, and identify the role of MS cholinergic neurons in seizure-related cognitive deficits in epileptic mouse models by using optogenetics and chemogenetics.

Methods

No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample size.

Information for clinical patients

AED: Antiepileptic drug; C: control; CBZ: carbamazepine; CZP: clonazepam; F: female; LAC: lacosamide; LEV: levetiracetam; LTG: lamotrigine; M: male; OXC: oxcarbazepine; P: patients; PBB: phenobarbital; TLE-LHS: temporal lobe epilepsy with left hippocampal sclerosis; TLE-RHS: temporal lobe epilepsy with right hippocampal sclerosis; TPM: topiramate; VPA: valproate

Diagnostic and inclusion criteria were previously described (Ji et al., 2018; Chen et al., 2019). Briefly, each patient was diagnosed by at least two experienced epileptologists based on detailed medical history, seizure semiology, long-term scalp video electroencephalography (EEG), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and neuropsychological assessments. All participants underwent MRI by a 3.0 Tesla Discovery MR750 scanner (GE Healthcare, Boston, MA, USA). The technical details for diffusion-tensor imaging (DTI) acquisition and analyses of MS volume and MS-hippocampal tracts were the same as in our previous study (Wang et al., 2020). In the present study, we included all patients who participated in the previous study (Wang et al., 2020), but we enrolled a greater number of patients in total and analyzed different data. Specifically, here we investigated whether TLE was accompanied by cognitive impairments in the patients, and whether it showed a relationship with MS-hippocampal tracts.

Verbal memory was evaluated by verbal paired associates and logical memory of the Wechsler Memory Scale-Revised Chinese version (WMS-RC) (Vythilingam et al., 2004; Ji et al., 2018). Nonverbal memory was evaluated by recognition and visual reproduction of the WMS-RC. Immediate recall raw score was used as the performance measure (Ji et al., 2018). The WMS-RC total raw scores were transformed to memory quotient (MQ) using normative data according to age.

Informed written consent forms for the use of imaging data were obtained before this study. All study protocols complied with the guidelines established by the National Institutes of Health and the Ethics Committee at Zhejiang University (approval No. 2014-151, approval date: October 22, 2014). A flowchart of the clinical design is shown in Additional Figure 1.

Animals

ChAT-Cre mice (Strain #:006410, RRID:IMSR_JAX:006410, 6 weeks old, n = 39) were used and genotyped according to the protocols provided by Jackson Laboratories (Bar Harbor, ME, USA). C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) (license No. SCXK (Zhe) 2021-0006). Adult male mice were housed in a 12-hour light-dark cycle environment (temperature 20–26°C, relative humidity 50–60%, five mice in each cage), with food and water provided ad libitum. The mice were randomly grouped. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics and Experimentation Committee of the Zhejiang Chinese Medical University (No. 2021-1026, approval date: October 26, 2021) and were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th ed) (National Research Council, 2011). A flowchart of the experimental design is shown in Additional Figure 2.

Stereotactic viral injection surgeries

Adult male mice (8 weeks old) were deeply anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection. Surgery was performed with a stereotaxic frame (Stoeling, Kiel, WI, USA). We used the following viruses: AAV2/9-flex-taCasp3-TEVp (1.5 × 1012 vg/mL, 0.5 μL) for ablation of MS cholinergic neurons, AAV2/9-Ef1a-DIO-hChR2-EYFP (1.58 × 1013 vg/mL, 0.5 μL) for optogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons and AAV2/8-Ef1a-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry (1.7 × 1013 vg/mL, 0.5 μL) for chemogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons. Viruses were microinjected into the MS (anteroposterior [AP] +1.0 mm, mediolateral [ML] –0.0 mm, dorsoventral [DV] –4.0 mm) (Paxinos and Franklin, 2001) of ChAT-Cre mice with a glass micropipette controlled by an injection pump at a rate of 60 nL/min. After infusion, the micropipette was left in place for 10 minutes to allow for diffusion and to prevent backflow of the virus before the pipette was slowly withdrawn. All viruses were produced by the OBiO Technology Shanghai Corp., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and had titers of > 1012 genome copies/mL. Only the mice with correct locations of viral expression were used for analysis.

Hippocampal kindling model

Electrodes (each 0.125 mm in diameter; A.M. Systems, Sequim, WA, USA) were implanted into the right ventral hippocampus (AP –2.9 mm, ML –3.1 mm, DV –3.1 mm) for kindling stimulation and EEG recording. After surgery recovery, kindling was performed as follows (Wang et al., 2017): 10 suprathreshold stimulations per day for 3 consecutive days, 1 ms square-wave pulses of 400 μA intensity with 20 Hz frequency for 2 seconds every 30 minutes. Seizure severity was classified according to the Racine scale in our previous study (Wang et al., 2020). Mice were regarded as fully kindled when they exhibited three consecutive stage 5 seizures.

Kainic acid-induced chronic seizure model

The kainic acid (KA) model was performed according to our previous study (Wang et al., 2017). Briefly, KA (0.25 μg in 0.5 μL normal saline, CSN19846, CSNpharm, Shanghai, China) was stereotaxically injected into the right dorsal hippocampus (AP –2.0 mm, ML –1.3 mm, DV –1.6 mm) of virus-injected mice and an electrode was stereotaxically implanted into the right ventral hippocampus (AP –2.9 mm, ML –3.1 mm, DV –3.1 mm) for EEG recording. Two months after KA injection, EEG was recorded by PowerLab system (AD Instruments, Bella Vista, New South Wales, Australia). Only the mice with detectable recurrent spontaneous seizures were regarded as KA-induced chronic epileptic mice.

Light stimulation and pharmacology

For in vivo optogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons, optical fibers (Inper, Hangzhou, China) were stereotactically implanted above the MS (AP +1.0 mm, ML −0.0 mm, DV −3.5 mm) 4 weeks after virus injection. Laser light was delivered through optical fibers, which were triggered by a stimulator (Inper 405). The blue light stimulation parameters were 10 ms/pulse, 20 Hz and 5 mW.

For in vivo activation of MS cholinergic neurons by chemogenetics, all mice received intraperitoneal injection of clozapine-n-oxide (CNO; 1.0 mg/kg, ab141704, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) daily for 7 days. Only the mice with the correct electrode and fiber placement were used for analysis.

Microdialysis and high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry

Microdialysis and high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS) were performed as in a previous study (Cheng et al., 2021). For extracellular ACh in hippocampus, a microdialysis probe was inserted into the dorsal hippocampus (AP –2.0 mm, ML –1.3 mm, DV –1.6 mm) through a guide cannula. Microdialysis was started by connecting the probe to a microinjection pump system (CMA, Stockholm, Sweden). The probe was continuously circulated with artificial cerebrospinal fluid solution (125 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.26 mM CaCl2, 1.18 mM MgCl2) containing 10 μM neostigmine to prevent ACh degradation at a rate of 1 μL/min. Before in vivo experiments, corrected dialysate concentrations were calculated by in vitro dialysis of a stirred artificial cerebrospinal fluid solution containing 5 nM ACh. For microdialysis in both control and KA mice, the perfusion exudate from the first 60 minutes was discarded (for system balance) and then samples were collected at least 1 hour thereafter. All of the microdialysis samples were immediately frozen at –80°C and stored until they were analyzed by HPLC-MS/MS (Agilent Technologies, Palo Alto, CA, USA).

Behavioral tests

Four weeks after virus injection, behavioral tests were performed according to previous studies (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014). Prior to training, mice were handled for 1 minute and habituated in the experimental environment for 5 minutes in the absence of objects for 2 days. Briefly, during training, the mice were placed in the presence of two identical objects and allowed to freely explore for 10 minutes. The testing procedure was conducted 30 minutes after training. For the object location memory (OLM) task, the mice were placed in the presence of the same two objects, but one object was moved to a novel place. For the NOR task, the location was the same as during training, but one object was replaced with a novel object. Both tests were performed at the base state (base), epileptic state (KA or kindled) and after optogenetic or chemogenetic manipulation of MS cholinergic neurons (KA + CNO or kindled + yellow/blue light). The exploration times spent on each of the familiar (F) object, displaced (D) object, and new (N) object were recorded manually. The discrimination index (%) was calculated as (D − F)/(D + F) × 100 or (N − F)/(N + F) × 100 for intergroup comparisons.

Fiber photometry

Fiber photometry recording was performed according to our previous study (Wang et al., 2020). Firstly, AAV2/9-hSyn-FLEX-ChrimsonR-mCherry was stereotactically injected into the MS (AP +1.0 mm, ML −0.0 mm, DV −4.0 mm) of ChAT-Cre mice to activate MS cholinergic neurons. rAAV-hSyn-ACh3.0 was stereotactically injected into the right dorsal hippocampus (AP −2.0 mm, ML −1.3 mm, DV −1.6 mm) to monitor ACh release. An optical fiber was placed into the MS for optogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons. Another optical fiber was placed into the right dorsal hippocampus to monitor the fluorescence. The fiber photometry system (Nanjing Thinkertech, Nanjing, China) used a 488 nm diode laser. The baseline was recorded for 20 seconds and then the optogenetic stimulation (20 Hz, 10 ms/pulse, 5 mW, 100 seconds) was given to activate MS cholinergic neurons. Photometry data were exported to MATLAB Mat files for further analysis. We segmented the data based on individual light stimulation trials and derived the values of fluorescence change delta F/F0 by calculating (F − F0)/F0, which were presented as heatmaps or average plots.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were sacrificed after the behavioral experiments. The brains were removed and postfixed in 4% phosphate-buffered paraformaldehyde at 4°C overnight and were cryoprotected with 30% (w/v) sucrose. Coronal 20 μm sections were obtained with a sliding freezing microtome (Leica, Wetziar, Germany). The sections were stained for ChAT (rabbit, 1:100, Millipore, Boston, MA, USA, Cat# AB143, RRID: AB_2079760) by incubating the sections with primary antibodies diluted in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) with 0.15% Triton X-100 overnight at 4°C, rinsed with PBS, and then incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488- or 594-conjugated secondary fluorescent antibody (Alexa Fluor 488, donkey anti-rabbit, 1:200, Molecular Probes, Waltham, MA, USA, Cat# 711-545-152, RRID: AB_2313584 and Alexa Fluor 594, donkey anti-rabbit, 1:200, Molecular Probes, Cat# 121413, RRID: AB_2313921) for 2 hours at room temperature (20–25°C). After 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole staining, the immunofluorescence was assessed with a laser confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Toluidine blue staining

The frozen slides were rewarmed and rinsed with PBS three times (5 minutes each time). Then, the slides were dipped in 1% toluidine blue dye solution for 20 minutes. After three rinses in water, the slides were dehydrated by quickly rinsing with 75%, 85% and 95% ethanol and anhydrous ethanol. Then, the slides were dried and cleared with xylene for 5 minutes. Finally, the slides were mounted with mounting medium (gum:xylene = 1:1). Imaging was performed with a laser confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

One-way analysis of variance followed by post hoc Tukey’s test analysis for multiple comparisons or post hoc Dunnett test analysis for multigroup comparison, unpaired/paired Student’s t-test for inter- and intra- group comparisons, respectively, and Pearson correlation analysis were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA, www.graphpad.com). No statistical methods were used to predetermine the sample sizes, or to randomize the groups. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Impaired cognitive function in TLE patients is positively correlated with the pathological changes in MS-hippocampal tracts

DTI is based on the detection of water diffusion properties and provides quantifiable information about white matter tracts (Chen et al., 2019). Representative DTI images from healthy controls, TLE-LHS patients, and TLE-RHS patients are shown in Figure 1A. DTI showed that TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis had reduced MS volume compared with healthy controls (Figure 1B, control group: 345.1 ± 7.122, TLE-LHS group: 320.9 ± 7.83, TLE-RHS group: 319.9 ± 5.119, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.0306, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.0297). TLE-LHS patients showed decreased tracts in the MS-left hippocampus (Lhip) pathway, whereas TLE-RHS patients showed unchanged tracts in either hemisphere (Figure 1C, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.0203, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.4591; Figure 1D, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.5165, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.0736).

We next tested the cognitive function of TLE patients. The neuropsychological evaluation indicated that TLE patients showed significantly impaired memory with a low MQ (Figure 1E, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.0005, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.0001), verifying that TLE is often accompanied by cognitive impairments. Particularly, the patients had a severe impairment in verbal memory, but not in nonverbal memory (Figure 1F and G, Figure 1F, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.0001, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.0037; Figure 1G, control group vs. TLE-LHS group: P = 0.9469, control group vs. TLE-RHS group: P = 0.1816). TLE-LHS patients had a more severe impairment in verbal memory than TLE-RHS patients (Figure 1F), which might be because the left temporal lobe largely controls verbal memory (Postma et al., 2020).

Further, we investigated whether the MS pathological changes were correlated with cognitive impairments in TLE patients. Pearson correlation analysis between MS volume and MQ or verbal memory, and MS-hippocampal tracts and MQ were shown in Figure 1H–J (Figure 1H: P = 0.0209, Figure 1I: P = 0.0975, Figure 1J: P = 0.0658). The above results demonstrated pathological changes of MS-hippocampal tracts and impaired memory in TLE patients, suggesting that the MS-hippocampal tracts might play an important role in the impaired memory in TLE patients.

KA-induced chronic epileptic mice show reduced ACh release in the hippocampus and cholinergic neuron loss in the MS

To verify the pathological changes of MS-hippocampus tracts in an animal model of epilepsy, we used an intra-hippocampal KA-induced epileptic mouse model, which has been reported to resemble the typical patterns of hippocampal pathology (Lévesque and Avoli, 2013) and impaired cognitive function (Wang et al., 2021). In our study, toluidine blue staining verified the pathological changes, including neuronal loss in CA1/CA3 and granule cell dispersion in the dentate gyrus, in the ipsilateral hippocampus of the KA-injected side in KA-induced chronic epileptic mice, compared with the contralateral hippocampus and that in control mice (Figure 2A).

The hippocampus, which receives cholinergic projection from the MS, is not only generally recognized as a region for encoding learning and memory, but also as one of the most common epileptogenic focuses in TLE (Bui et al., 2018). We speculated that the MS-hippocampus is a key node for the generation of cognitive impairments in TLE. We first detected the expression of MS cholinergic neurons in TLE. Immunohistochemical staining showed that there was a significant decrease in the number of MS cholinergic neurons (ChAT-positive cells) in KA-induced epileptic mice compared with control mice (Figure 2B, P = 0.0176). Because ACh in the hippocampus is mainly released from MS-hippocampus cholinergic projections, we measured the extracellular ACh level in the ipsilateral hippocampus of the KA-injected side using a microdialysis component in combination with HPLC-MS/MS. The extracellular ACh level in the hippocampus was significantly lower in KA-induced chronic epileptic mice than in control mice (Figure 2C, P = 0.0418), which suggested decreased hippocampal ACh release in TLE model mice. Next, we evaluated cognitive function in control mice and KA-induced epileptic mice by using the OLM and NOR memory tasks. KA-induced epileptic mice showed cognitive impairments with low discrimination index in both OLM and NOR (Figure 2D, P = 0.0034; Figure 2E, P = 0.0006). The above results indicate pathological changes of MS cholinergic neurons and cognitive impairments in the TLE model that were in accordance with the above clinical findings.

Selective ablation of MS cholinergic neurons results in impaired cognitive function

To further confirm the causal role of MS cholinergic neuronal loss in TLE-associated cognitive impairments (Figure 3A), a Cre-dependent adeno-associated virus (AAV-flex-taCasp3-TEVp) was microinjected into the MS of ChAT-Cre mice (referred to as ChAT::taCasp3 mice; Figure 3B) to selectively ablate MS cholinergic neurons. Immunohistochemical staining showed that 93% of MS cholinergic neurons were ablated in ChAT::taCasp3 mice, whereas those in corpus striatum, a nearby region that also is enriched in cholinergic neurons, were not affected (Figure 3C, ChAT-Cre group vs. ChAT::taCas3 group, P < 0.0001). Then, we examined learning and memory processes in ChAT-Cre mice and ChAT::taCasp3 mice using OLM and NOR memory tasks, respectively (Figure 3D). The results showed that ChAT::taCasp3 mice with ablated MS cholinergic neurons had low discrimination index in both OLM and NOR, similar to that of epileptic mice (Figure 3E1, ChAT-Cre group vs. ChAT::taCas3 group, P = 0.0018; Figure 3E2, ChAT-Cre group, ChAT::taCas3 group, P = 0.0210). The above results showed that MS cholinergic neuronal loss alone was sufficient to impair cognitive function, which mimicked the impairments of KA-induced epileptic mice. These results suggested that MS cholinergic neuron loss in epilepsy might mediate the generation of cognitive impairments in TLE.

Chemogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons reverses the impaired cognitive function in KA-induced chronic epileptic mice

Next, we investigated whether the activation of MS cholinergic neurons would reverse the impaired cognitive function in TLE models (Figure 4A). For prolonged activation of MS cholinergic neurons and to mimic the clinical treatment administration, we used chemogenetics in the KA-induced chronic TLE model. A Cre-dependent virus (AAV-EF1a-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry) was stereotactically delivered into the MS of ChAT-Cre mice (referred to as ChAT::hM3Dq mice), and MS cholinergic neurons expressing the excitatory hM3Dq designer receptor could be activated by CNO (Figure 4B). During the chronic phase of KA-induced epilepsy with spontaneous recurrent seizures, we administered CNO (intraperitoneal injection, 1 mg/kg) into ChAT::hM3Dq epileptic mice for 7 consecutive days. Compared with the base state, KA-induced chronic epileptic mice had a lower discrimination index in both OLM and NOR, and CNO administration for 7 days reversed the impaired cognitive function in the KA-induced chronic epileptic mice (Figure 4C, Figure 4C1, Base vs. KA: P = 0.0030, Base vs. KA + CNO: P = 0.7931, KA vs. KA + CNO: P = 0.0452; Figure 4C2, Base vs. KA: P = 0.0088, Base vs. KA + CNO: P = 0.9979, KA vs. KA + CNO: P = 0.0336). These results indicated that selective activation of MS cholinergic neurons reversed the cognitive impairments in the KA-induced TLE model.

Optogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons reverses the impaired cognitive function in hippocampal-kindled epileptic mice

To test whether the therapeutic effect of MS cholinergic neurons on cognitive function in TLE is a general phenomenon, we used the hippocampal-kindling TLE model (Figure 5A). Optogenetics was used to achieve selective activation of MS cholinergic neurons with high temporal precision. A Cre-dependent virus (AAV2/9-Ef1a-DIO-hChR2-EYFP) was delivered into the MS of ChAT-Cre mice, and MS cholinergic neurons expressing channelrhodopsin (ChR2) could be activated by blue light stimulation (Figure 5B). To test the effect of MS cholinergic neuronal activation on impaired cognitive function in the hippocampal-kindling TLE model, we delivered blue light (20 Hz, 10 ms/pulse, 5 mW) to stimulate ChR2-expressing MS cholinergic neurons during cognitive behavioral tests. Hippocampal-kindled epileptic mice showed deficits in both OLM and NOR, and activation of MS cholinergic neurons during cognitive behavioral tests reversed the impaired cognitive performance (Figure 5C, Figure 5C1, Base vs. Kindled + Yellow Light: P = 0.0002, Base vs. Kindled + Blue Light: P = 0.5445, Kindled + Yellow Light vs. Kindled + Blue Light: P < 0.0001; Figure 5C2, Base vs. Kindled + Yellow Light: P < 0.0001, Base vs. Kindled + Blue Light: P < 0.0001, Kindled + Yellow Light vs. Kindled + Blue Light: P < 0.0001). These results indicated that selective activation of MS cholinergic neurons reversed the cognitive impairments in multiple TLE models.

Discussion

Cognitive impairment is one of the most common complications of TLE with hippocampal sclerosis, but the underlying mechanism remains unclear (Postma et al., 2020). In the present study, we found that TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis showed memory impairments that were positively correlated with the reduced volume of MS and MS-hippocampal connectivity. In epileptic mice, we identified a crucial role of MS cholinergic neurons in the generation and treatment of seizure-related cognitive deficits, which may support the clinical translation of approaches targeting MS cholinergic neurons to both control seizures and restore associated cognitive impairments.

The TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis included in this study also demonstrated cognitive impairments, and DTI imaging showed pathological changes in MS and MS-hippocampal tracts. Atrophy of the hippocampus and interconnected pathways has been considered to reflect neuronal loss or dysfunction in the seizure focus and regions of seizure propagation (Bernhardt et al., 2009). A previous MRI study reported an increase in MS volume in TLE patients without hippocampal sclerosis (Butler et al., 2013), whereas we found a decrease in MS volume and MS-hippocampus tracts in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis in the present study. It was previously reported that memory consolidation was positively correlated with hippocampal volume in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis (Fuentemilla et al., 2013). Thus, we think that the MS may be closely associated with TLE and cognitive function. Our previous experimental studies have indicated that the MS is an efficient target for the treatment of temporal lobe seizures and accompanying memory impairments in mice (Wang et al., 2020, 2021). However, the relationship between the pathological changes of the MS and the development of seizures and cognitive dysfunction in TLE patients was still unknown. The present study is the first to report that the pathological changes of the MS and MS-hippocampus tracts are positively correlated with memory impairments in TLE patients. The MS has reciprocal pathways with the hippocampus and generates hippocampal oscillations closely related to memory function (Fisher, 2015). Thus, it is possible that atrophy of the MS and hippocampus in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis leads to the decrease of MS-hippocampal tracts, thereby impacting the memory-related oscillations and causing cognitive deficits. Of note, TLE-LHS patients had a more severe impairment in verbal memory than TLE-RHS patients. This is consistent with the traditional view that verbal deficits are usually observed in epilepsy affecting the dominant (usually left) hemisphere, whereas visual memory is typically affected by epilepsy in the nondominant (usually right) hemisphere (Postma et al., 2020). These results suggest that MS-left hippocampus tracts and MS-right hippocampus tracts target different hippocampal neuronal types and are responsible for different memory functions, which deserves further investigation.

The hippocampal network is considered to be closely involved in both seizures and seizure-related cognitive deficits. For example, dentate gyrus mossy cells have been reported to control seizures and related spatial memory deficits (Bui et al., 2018). Further, the reduction of dentate gyrus calbindin-D28k induced by chronic seizures was shown to impair cognition (You et al., 2017). However, in addition to the direct modulation of hippocampal excitatory and inhibitory neurons, noncanonical cholinergic control is also critical for hippocampal function and cognition. Cholinergic dysfunction or an ACh M1 muscarinic receptor mutant resulted in cognitive impairments (Anagnostaras et al., 2003; Ju and Tam, 2022). The generation of hippocampal theta rhythms, which contribute to memory consolidation, also requires cholinergic neurons (Huerta and Lisman, 1993; Haam and Yakel, 2017). Therefore, we attempted to measure whether the hippocampal cholinergic transmission was disrupted in chronic TLE models. The results from microdialysis showed that hippocampal ACh release was significantly reduced in the chronic epileptic mice. Because hippocampal ACh mainly originates from MS terminals, we examined the expression of MS cholinergic neurons. There was obvious apoptosis of MS cholinergic neurons in KA-induced chronic epileptic mice, which is consistent with the reduced volume of the MS in TLE patients with hippocampal sclerosis. MS cholinergic neuronal loss is inevitably accompanied by reduced MS-hippocampus tracts. Therefore, we speculate that chronic seizures result in the atrophy of MS cholinergic neurons, and in turn, the disrupted cholinergic control of the hippocampus further aggravates the seizures and related cognitive deficits.

We next explored the role of MS cholinergic neurons in the generation and treatment of seizure-related cognitive deficits. We selectively ablated MS cholinergic neurons and found that selective apoptosis of MS cholinergic neurons alone was sufficient to generate cognitive deficits that mimicked the phenotypes caused by chronic seizures. Chronic KA-induced or hippocampal-kindled epileptic mice both showed a lower discrimination index than control mice in both OLM and NOR tests, which was reversed by selective activation of MS cholinergic neurons. Notably, using optogenetics (temporal activation in the kindling model) and chemogenetics (chronic activation in the KA model), we found that although there was cholinergic neuron loss in the MS, selective activation of the remaining neurons was sufficient to improve the cognitive deficits in these models. These results suggest that MS cholinergic neurons may be a potential therapeutic target for seizure-related cognitive deficits. It would be interesting to further investigate whether activation of MS cholinergic neurons with different approaches reverses cognitive impairments in one epilepsy model with similar efficacy. In this study, we used two well-validated assays of spatial and object memory, OLM and NOR tests (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014; Yoo et al., 2021; Luo et al., 2022), which reflect hippocampus-dependent spatial memory and innate novelty preference. Although these two tasks do not directly correspond to MQ, verbal memory and nonverbal memory in humans, they do reflect temporal lobe function, which is also involved in verbal memory in humans. OLM reflects spatial memory, which relies on the hippocampus, whereas NOR involves multiple brain regions, such as insular cortex and ventromedial prefrontal cortex (Vogel-Ciernia and Wood, 2014). This suggests that MS cholinergic neurons may affect both OLM- and NOR-related brain regions.

In this study, we did not explore the neural circuit mechanism underlying the beneficial effect of MS cholinergic neurons on epilepsy-related cognitive function, which requires further investigation. We verified that optogenetic activation of MS cholinergic neurons enhanced ACh release in the hippocampus. Cholinergic signaling from the MS to the hippocampus is important for the formation of spatial memories via modulating synaptic plasticity at the cellular level or balancing gamma and theta band oscillations at the network level (Ballinger et al., 2016; Haam and Yakel, 2017). Therefore, it is possible that epileptic seizures disrupt synaptic plasticity or oscillations in the hippocampus, and that activation of MS cholinergic neurons reverses the disruption. Furthermore, in addition to cholinergic neurons, the MS also contains abundant glutamatergic and GABAergic neurons, which may play a role in cognitive function. For example, optogenetic activation of MS glutamatergic neurons drives hippocampal theta rhythms and tunes social memory stability (Huh et al., 2010; Robinson et al., 2016; Zhu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2021). However, a previous study reported that activation of MS glutamatergic neurons aggravated seizures (Wang et al., 2020), suggesting that glutamatergic neurons may not be an ideal therapeutic target for seizure-related cognitive deficits. Although MS GABAergic neurons were previously shown to modulate novel object place recognition and contextual memory retrieval through targeting hippocampal neurons (Boyce et al., 2016), activation of MS GABAergic neurons only reduced the duration of seizures (Hristova et al., 2021). Thus, the findings support MS cholinergic neurons, which were reported to effectively attenuate seizures, rather than glutamatergic or GABAergic neurons, as the potential target for the treatment of both TLE and related cognitive deficits. A limitation of the present study is that only male mice were used. We will include female mice in future investigations.

In conclusion, together with our previous study (Wang et al., 2020) showing the anti-seizure ability of MS cholinergic neurons, these findings suggest that MS cholinergic neurons are a potential target to both control seizures and restore impaired cognitive function in TLE. This may be of great therapeutic significance for the translation of MS-targeted therapeutic approaches, such as deep brain stimulation in the MS, from animal models into clinical treatments.
