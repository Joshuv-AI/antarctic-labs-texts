Extrinsic compression of the right coronary artery by a huge aortic wall aneurysm of an aortico-left ventricular tunnel: A case report
Authors: Linhao Jian, Zhixiang Zhang, Dexiang Chen, Liangqing Ge
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAortico-left ventricular tunnel (ALVT) is a rare congenital disease characterised by extracardiac channel communication between the ascending aorta and the ventricle. However, there have been no reports of ALVT compressing the coronary arteries. Here, we report the case of a 57-year-old woman who presented with unstable angina due to right coronary artery compression caused by a giant aneurysmal ALVT. Preoperative imaging failed to accurately diagnose this rare anomaly. Finally, we combined surgical exploration and a three-dimensional reconstruction technique to diagnose ALVT. After surgical resection of the aneurysm and repair of the tunnel, the patient's angina resolved. This case illustrates the importance of recognising extrinsic compression of the coronary artery as a potential cause of angina pectoris. It also highlights that ALVT with atypical anatomical features may require multiple imaging techniques and even surgical exploration to confirm the diagnosis.Keywords: Unstable angina pectoris, Aneurysm, Aortico-left ventricular tunnel, Extrinsic compression, Case report

Introduction

Aortico-left ventricular tunnel (ALVT) is an extremely rare congenital heart anomaly with an estimated incidence of approximately 0.04%–0.1 % [1]. An anatomical feature of this disease is the presence of an extracardiac channel between the ascending aorta above the sinotubular junction and the left or right ventricular cavity that may cause haemodynamic changes. The age at symptom onset and disease severity depend on the size of the tunnel and the extent of aortic regurgitation [2]. In most cases, the early surgical results proved satisfactory.

Compression of a major coronary artery by an ALVT leading to angina pectoris is even rarer. Although transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) is the primary imaging tool used to diagnose ALVT, the rate of misdiagnosis remains high [3]. A valsalva sinus rupture can also connect the aorta to any cardiac chamber with similar haemodynamic characteristics, and this is most easily confused with an ALVT.

Herein, we report the case of a 57-year-old woman who presented with angina due to compression of the right coronary artery by a large aneurysm. Preoperative imaging initially identified the structure as a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SoVA). After surgical resection of the aneurysm, the patients’ angina symptoms were relieved, and the ALVT was subsequently diagnosed by three-dimensional computed tomography reconstruction and intraoperative examination.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old woman was admitted to our hospital with the chief complaint of chest pain. The patients’ chest pain symptoms had worsened markedly one month before admission. The chest pain could be induced by walking approximately 100 m. Each attack lasted approximately 10 minutes and was gradually relieved after rest. The patient did not have any risk factors for cardiovascular disease. The patient was previously healthy and had no medical history of aortic or connective tissue disease.

On admission, her body temperature was 37.3 °C, blood pressure was 132/87 mmHg, and heart rate was 89 bpm; physical examination revealed a systolic murmur at the third left intercostal space. Blood test results, including troponin I, CK-MB, D-dimer, and BNP levels, were normal. Electrocardiography revealed a T-wave inversion in leads III and AVF. TTE revealed a bicuspid aortic valve, normal atrial and ventricular size, an ejection fraction (EF) of 67 %, a large aneurysm communicating with the sinotubular junction of the non-coronary sinus, no dilatation of the aortic root or sinus of Valsalva, and mild aortic regurgitation (Fig. 1). Coronary computed tomography angiography revealed a giant aneurysm (measuring 70 × 60 mm) to the right of the non-coronary sinus, extending anteriorly, compressing the right atrium and right coronary artery, and a suspected SoVA (Fig. 2). Coronary angiography confirmed that the proximal right coronary artery segment stenosis was caused by extrinsic compression (Fig. 3; Supplementary Video 1-2). Due to the atypical anatomical features, a definitive diagnosis could not be made preoperatively; however, it was clear that a differential diagnosis between ruptured SoVA and ALVT was required.

After a multidisciplinary discussion, the decision was made to perform surgery, and further exploration during the operation confirmed the diagnosis. Under general anaesthesia, median sternotomy was performed, a cardiopulmonary bypass was established, and the aortic aneurysm was incised. After opening the aneurysm, we found two separate ostia (measuring 5 × 6 mm) above and below the aortic valve annulus. The one located below the aortic valve annulus was connected to the left ventricular outflow tract, whereas the one located above the aortic valve annulus communicated with the sinotubular junction of the noncoronary sinus (Supplementary Fig. 1). Given the risk of aortic valve distortion, a patch material was used to repair the ostia and ensure tension-free closure. Surgical removal of the aneurysm, repair of the aortic and ventricular ostia of the ALVT with pericardial patches, and tunnel closure were performed (Supplementary Fig. 2). The diagnosis of the ALVT was confirmed through surgery, after which the patient's angina symptoms disappeared completely. Because the myocardial ischaemia was mainly due to external compression from the aneurysm, antiplatelet drugs were not prescribed. Antibiotics, nonsteroidal analgesics, and nutritional support were administered postoperatively. At the 9-month follow-up, the patient showed no symptoms of angina or heart failure. The NT-proBNP fluctuated between 70 and 120 pg/mL during the follow-up period. The follow-up electrocardiogram showed no atrioventricular block, and the TTE suggested that the aortoventricular tunnel had been perfectly repaired. There was no aortic aneurysm compression of the right coronary artery and right atrium, mild aortic regurgitation, normal atrial and ventricular size, and an EF of 68 % (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

The ALVT is an abnormal passage between the ascending aorta and the left ventricle [1]. Currently, the embryological mechanism of ALVT remains unclear and may be related to the abnormal development of endocardial processes. More than 90 % of aorto-ventricular tunnels terminate in the left ventricle, with a few cases terminating in the right ventricle [4]. The supravalvular opening is usually located above the right coronary sinus of Valsalva, bypassing the aortic valve and entering the left ventricle, resulting in an increase in the left ventricular end-diastolic volume. Hovaguimian [5] defined four distinct types of ALVT: Type I, characterised by a slit-like opening at the aortic end without aortic valve distortion; Type II, featuring a large extracardiac aortic wall aneurysm of the tunnel with or without aortic valve distortion; Type III, presenting an intracardiac aneurysm with or without right ventricular outflow tract obstruction; and type IV, a combination of types II and III. ALVT can be associated with various other cardiac anatomical abnormalities, such as aortic valve abnormalities (e.g., bicuspid aortic valve malformation), the coronary ostium located within the tunnel, or coronary ostium atresia [1,6].

Although the disease is often diagnosed in infancy or early childhood with symptoms such as heart failure and heart murmur [7], a few patients can live asymptomatically into adulthood [5]. The clinical manifestations of ALVT depend on the tunnel size and degree of aortic insufficiency. Our patient presented to the hospital with chest pain and no risk factors for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Imaging studies confirmed that extrinsic compression of the right coronary artery was the primary cause of the patient's angina pectoris. Echocardiography is considered the most important examination for the diagnosis of ALVT, and 80 % of cases can be diagnosed using ultrasonography [3]. Some anatomically atypical ALVT cases may require special imaging techniques, such as 3D CT reconstruction [8], or even intraoperative exploration [1].

It is essential to differentiate ALVT from other similar cardiac abnormalities, such as ruptured SoVA and coronary cameral fistula. A ruptured aneurysm usually presents with acute symptoms such as acute heart failure. The space-occupying effects of a SoVA adjacent to the aortic valve annulus may result in severe aortic insufficiency or left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) obstruction. In contrast to ALVT, ruptured SoVA is typically characterized by dilatation of the aortic sinus wall. Conversely, the tunnel in ALVT usually arises from the aorta above the sinus of Valsalva. The medical history and clinical presentation of our patient were not consistent with the characteristics of SoVA as described above. A coronary cameral fistula is a rare congenital variant that can occur between one or more coronary arteries and the cardiac chamber. It then passes through the myocardium and into the ventricular lumen. The aneurysmal structure in our patient originated from the aorta and was easily distinguished.

Compression of the coronary arteries by an ALVT is extremely rare. The anatomical characteristics of our patient were consistent with a type II ALVT. The prominent feature in this patient was the presence of a small tunnel combined with a giant aneurysm. The patient's angina resolved after surgical resection of the aneurysm. Therefore, surgical repair of ALVT is feasible for patients with compressive symptoms [9]. Surgery should be performed as soon as possible after ALVT is diagnosed [1,2,9], and patch closure is the most common surgical repair method [1,10]. In certain anatomical conditions, a small number of ALVT cases may be amenable to transcatheter closure techniques [11,12]. Interestingly, only one case of an asymptomatic small ALVT that healed spontaneously during close follow-up has been reported in the literature [1]. The key objectives of surgical treatment for AVLT should include: (i) complete closure of the abnormal traffic; (ii) restoration of aortic valve function; (iii) preservation of coronary circulation; and (iv) relief of ventricular outflow tract obstruction [9]. In the present case, the main feature was external compression of the coronary artery by the ALVT, whereas the aortic valve had only mild regurgitation. Therefore, surgical release of the coronary artery compression and complete closure of the abnormal blood flow channels may be the optimal treatment approach.

The main limitation of this case was that the tunnel connected to the LVOT was not detected on preoperative echocardiography. Therefore, a combination of clinical manifestations and multiple imaging examinations is necessary in cases with an atypical presentation. In a few cases, the exact diagnosis is made intraoperatively.

Conclusion

Angina due to ALVT compression of the coronary artery is extremely rare, and surgical removal of the aneurysm to repair the tunnel may be preferred. The diagnosis of ALVT can be challenging, particularly when the anatomical features are atypical. In these situations, multiple advanced imaging techniques, such as 3D CT reconstruction, may be required to accurately characterize the abnormality. In rare cases, even surgical exploration may be necessary to establish the definitive diagnosis when non-invasive imaging studies are inconclusive.
