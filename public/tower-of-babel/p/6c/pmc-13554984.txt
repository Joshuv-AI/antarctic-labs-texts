Elecsys pT181p immunoassay: Clinical performance for ruling out amyloid pathology
Authors: Craig Ritchie, Sayuri Hortsch, Clara Quijano‐Rubio, Patrick Menzel, Imke Kirste, David Caley, Anna D Burke, Christopher C Rowe, Marc Suárez‐Calvet, Dag Aarsland, Jonathan Liss, Mercè Boada, David Watson, Mark Rapp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPlasma biomarker rule‐out tests can be used early in the Alzheimer's disease (AD) diagnostic pathway to identify people with low likelihood of amyloid pathology.METHODSA care setting‐agnostic cutoff for the Elecsys® Phospho‐Tau (181P) plasma (pT181p) immunoassay was determined and validated in two independent, diverse cohorts, representative of real‐world clinical practice (N = 604; N = 787, respectively). An additional cutoff was assessed in a subset of people reflective of primary care (n = 312).RESULTSAt a care setting‐agnostic cutoff of 0.934 pg/ml, high negative predictive value (NPV) (93.8%) and acceptable positive predictive value (PPV) (46.6%) were observed against amyloid‐positron emission tomography (PET). A primary care‐specific cutoff of 0.722 pg/ml showed high NPV (97.9%) against amyloid‐PET. Amyloid positivity prevalence for each cutoff population was 22.5% and 13.1%, respectively.DISCUSSIONThe pT181p immunoassay is a high‐performance test that may substantially help improve access to amyloid pathology testing for people requiring confirmation, potentially leading to timely clinical decisions and better outcomes.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, amyloid pathology, blood‐based biomarker, cerebrospinal fluid, clinical performance, cutoff determination and validation, Elecsys immunoassay, plasma pTau181, positron emission tomography, primary care, rule out

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder and the most common cause of dementia among older adults. 1 , 2 For years, amyloid‐positron emission tomography (PET) imaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) biomarker analysis have been the only well‐validated and approved modalities for identifying amyloid pathology in routine clinical practice. 3 , 4 , 5 The levels of amyloid‐beta (Αβ) and tau in CSF, particularly tau phosphorylated at a threonine residue at position 181 (pTau181), Aβ42, and total tau, are key biomarkers for detecting amyloid pathology. 6 , 7 , 8 In AD, aggregated forms of the Aβ peptide accumulate into extracellular plaques, accelerating the formation of intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, while tau hyperphosphorylation can contribute to neurofibrillary tangle formation and neuronal dysfunction. 9

With the advent of amyloid‐targeting therapies, the confirmation of amyloid pathology has become mandatory to enable effective clinical decision‐making. 10 , 11 This has led to an increased demand for confirmatory testing using amyloid‐PET scans and CSF biomarker analysis, exposing current healthcare system bottlenecks, including the worldwide heterogeneity in AD diagnostic workup, low accessibility, long wait times, radioactive tracer exposure, and high costs associated with amyloid‐PET scans, as well as the perceived invasive nature and limited specialized personnel for CSF sample collection. 4 , 5 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Lack of awareness and stigma around the disease, inconsistent referral pathways to specialists, and subjectivity of cognitive assessments may also impede effective and timely amyloid pathology detection and confirmation. 14 , 18 , 19 Taken together, these factors substantially hinder the timely diagnosis of AD, leading to late diagnosis or underdiagnosis, which ultimately affects the management, quality of life, and outcomes of people with AD. There is therefore a clear unmet need to develop standardized triage tests that can be easily implemented early in the AD diagnostic pathway for effective identification of amyloid pathology status, which may improve access to confirmatory diagnosis and potentially lead to more timely management and better outcomes.

Testing of plasma samples is an established routine process and can be easily performed in a variety of care settings and clinical laboratories equipped with appropriate analytical platforms and trained personnel. 20 Given that plasma pTau biomarker levels progressively increase when approaching the average age of AD symptom onset, 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 plasma pTau tests have emerged as a scalable, cost‐effective, and minimally invasive tool that can be used along with other evaluations to aid in the early identification of amyloid pathology. 25 , 26 Literature findings suggest the use of plasma biomarkers as rule‐out/rule‐in triage tools, where a negative or positive test result, respectively, would indicate a lower or increased likelihood of amyloid pathology. 26 , 27 Interpretation of these test results together with other clinical information as well as imaging and/or CSF biomarkers, will aid in the early detection of the AD‐related pathological changes and potentially optimize the AD diagnostic pathway.

Recent evidence suggests that increased levels of plasma pTau181, tau phosphorylated at a threonine at position 231 (pTau231), and tau phosphorylated at a threonine at position 217 (pTau217), are strongly correlated with the density of Aβ plaques and tau tangles, and can be used to detect early AD, discriminate AD from other neurodegenerative diseases with high accuracy, and predict progression from mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia. 21 , 23 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 While plasma pTau217 has emerged as a leading plasma biomarker in the AD diagnostic pathway, 32 , 33 plasma pTau181 remains one of the most extensively studied biomarkers in the field, 30 , 31 , 34 underscoring the continued relevance of evaluating its diagnostic utility.

The Elecsys® Phospho‐Tau (181P) plasma (pT181p) immunoassay (Roche Diagnostics International Ltd, Rotkreuz, Switzerland) is the first Conformité Européenne (CE)‐certified blood‐based test developed under the in vitro diagnostic regulation as a standalone triage tool to rule out amyloid pathology related to AD, and the first plasma biomarker test approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for use in primary care. 35 , 36 Here, we report the results of the study entitled APPROACH (Amyloid Plasma Panel Research: Objective Assessment of Cutoffs and Clinical Harnessing), which aimed to prospectively determine and validate a care setting‐agnostic cutoff for the pT181p immunoassay, to be used early in the AD diagnostic pathway for ruling out amyloid pathology. Additionally, the performance of a second cutoff to rule out amyloid pathology in primary care, where a considerable proportion of people presenting are unlikely to have AD (lower pre‐test probability), 37 was evaluated in a study population subset.

METHODS

Study design and population

The prospective, multicenter, non‐interventional APPROACH study (RD006263) was conducted across 30 geographically diverse enrollment sites in Australia, Europe, and the USA. The study population was representative of clinical routine in terms of age, sex, and comorbidities, and reflected people with early signs, symptoms, or complaints of cognitive decline (subjective cognitive decline [SCD], MCI, or mild dementia).

People were considered eligible for the study if they met the following inclusion criteria: aged 55 to 80 years at the time of providing informed consent, presenting with cognitive complaints or objective memory impairment of unknown cause, and under evaluation for AD or other causes of cognitive decline; the principal investigator was uncertain that the symptoms were caused by AD; and Quick Dementia Rating System (QDRS) score between 0.5 to 12, with one box memory and recall. 38 Medical comorbidities and medications that are usually exclusion criteria from clinical trials were allowed. Further details on the enrollment criteria are provided in the Supplemental Methods. People were excluded from the study if they had: clinical diagnosis of moderate and severe dementia and/or Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score < 20; prior advanced diagnostic evaluation including amyloid‐/tau‐PET and/or CSF analysis as part of their routine medical care; presence of active delirium or encephalopathy; current or prior investigational treatment within five half‐lives or 6 months (whichever was longer) prior to enrollment and during the study; or any prior anti‐amyloid treatment in any setting. Baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the study participants were captured in electronic case report forms, and clinical diagnosis (SCD, MCI, or mild dementia) was documented by a physician during the initial visit, prior to obtaining amyloid‐PET and CSF biomarker ratio measurements. Details on plasma and CSF sample measurements as well as amyloid‐PET scans are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Study participants were enrolled between May 18, 2023, and December 17, 2024, and were reflective of individuals typically seen in primary and secondary/tertiary care settings, and specialized research centers. The care setting for each study participant was identified based on the type of site at presentation and referral history (especially when presentation occurred at research units) (Figure S1). A care setting‐agnostic cutoff for the pT181p immunoassay was determined in 604 study participants reflective of primary or secondary/tertiary care, enrolled at 12 sites in Europe and the USA, and with valid amyloid‐PET or CSF biomarker results, and valid plasma pTau181 biomarker measurements. The cutoff was established ensuring high negative predictive value (NPV) and acceptable positive predictive value (PPV), and its clinical performance was evaluated with respect to amyloid pathology status defined by amyloid‐PET visual read or the Elecsys CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio at a cutoff of 0.023. The clinical performance of the established cutoff was then independently validated in 787 participants enrolled at 18 sites in Australia, Europe, and the USA. The performance of a primary care‐specific cutoff value was additionally assessed with respect to amyloid pathology status defined by amyloid‐PET visual read status using data from a subset of the enrolled population (n = 312), comprising people reflective of primary care settings only.

Statistical analysis

Participant demographics and clinical characteristics were reported using descriptive summary statistics. The selected population had an anticipated amyloid pathology prevalence of 20% to 30% 39 and was deemed sufficient to assess and establish clinical performance metrics with an appropriate width of confidence intervals. The receiver operating characteristic–area under the curve (ROC–AUC) was evaluated, and a care setting‐agnostic cutoff with high NPV and acceptable PPV was determined to rule out amyloid pathology. 26 The established cutoff was then independently validated in concordance with amyloid pathology status as defined by amyloid‐PET visual reads (primary analysis). The performance of a primary care‐specific cutoff, optimized to rule out amyloid pathology in this population, was assessed in a subset of the validation cohort. More details are provided in the Supplemental Methods.

RESULTS

Validation of the care setting‐agnostic Elecsys pT181p immunoassay cutoff

The care setting‐agnostic pT181p immunoassay cutoff with a good rule‐out clinical performance against amyloid‐PET visual read and CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio was determined at 0.934 pg/ml (Supplemental Results; Tables S1–S3; Figures S2–S4). The clinical performance of the established cutoff for ruling out amyloid pathology in people presenting across care settings (primary and secondary/tertiary) was then validated in an independent analysis with respect to amyloid pathology status as defined by amyloid‐PET visual read or CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio measurements.

The cohort for the validation analysis comprised 787 participants (442 females [56.2%]; mean age 69.1 [standard deviation {SD} 6.53] years). Just over half of the participants (n = 419; 53.2%) were reflective of secondary/tertiary care settings and 368 (46.8%) of primary care settings. Clinically, 331 (42.1%) participants were diagnosed with SCD, 405 (51.5%) with MCI, 40 (5.1%) with mild dementia, and 11 (1.4%) had a missing diagnosis. The most common comorbidities in this population were cardiovascular disease (n = 439; 55.8%), metabolic conditions (excluding diabetes; n = 229; 29.1%), diabetes (n = 156; 19.8%), and cancer or history of cancer (n = 148; 18.8%). Kidney disease was reported in 4.1% (n = 32) of participants (Table 1).

Of the 787 enrolled participants with valid plasma pTau181 measurements, 401 (51.0%) had valid results for both amyloid‐PET imaging and CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio measurements (Figure S5). The mean time difference between blood collection and amyloid‐PET imaging was 48.6 days (range: −32.0 to 179 days), and that between blood and CSF sample collection was 25.1 days (range: −65 to 168 days). In the overall cutoff validation cohort (N = 787), the mean plasma pTau181 concentration was 0.998 pg/ml. The observed prevalence of amyloid positivity based on amyloid‐PET visual read and CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio measurements was 22.5% and 28.0%, respectively (Table 2). The agreement between pT181p immunoassay and amyloid‐PET visual read or CSF biomarker ratio results is shown in Table S4. The NPV and PPV values with respect to amyloid‐PET visual read were 93.8% and 46.6%, respectively, and overall percent agreement (OPA) was 74.8% (Table 2); the AUC for plasma pTau181 was 85.4% (Figure 2A). The NPV and PPV values with respect to the CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio were 88.8% and 55.8%, respectively, and OPA was 76.4% (Table 2); the AUC for plasma pTau181 was 81.8% (Figure 2B). In participants with valid amyloid‐PET or CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio results, a rule‐out rate of 60.4% was observed.

Assessment of the primary care‐specific Elecsys pT181p immunoassay cutoff

Triage tools have been found to improve diagnostic accuracy, especially when introduced in primary care settings where amyloid pathology prevalence is expected to be low, as shown in a study modelling the impact of the pT181p immunoassay on diagnostic performance and healthcare resource utilization in the AD diagnostic pathway. 40 Therefore, the clinical performance of a second pT181p immunoassay cutoff of 0.722 pg/ml, derived with a minimum positive percent agreement of 92% and optimized for ruling out amyloid pathology in primary care, was evaluated in a subset of participants with valid amyloid‐PET visual read results. A total of 312/650 (48.0%) participants with a mean age of 69.1 (SD 6.53) years were included in the analysis (Figure S5; Table S5).

The observed prevalence of amyloid positivity based on amyloid‐PET visual read status was 13.1% (Table 3). The agreement between pT181p immunoassay and amyloid‐PET visual read results is shown in Table S4. Using the cutoff value of 0.722 pg/ml, a rule‐out rate of 45.5% was observed. The NPV and PPV values with respect to amyloid‐PET were 97.9% and 22.4%, respectively (Table 3). Boxplots of plasma pTau181 concentration by amyloid‐PET visual read status (positive or negative) for this population subset are shown in Figure 1B. The AUC for plasma pTau181 with respect to amyloid‐PET was 83.5% (Figure 2C).

DISCUSSION

In this study, a care setting‐agnostic cutoff of 0.934 pg/ml was determined for the pT181p immunoassay, and its clinical performance was validated for ruling out amyloid pathology in people representative of primary, secondary or tertiary care, who were under evaluation for AD or other causes of cognitive decline, and presented with early cognitive decline (subjective or objective). The clinical performance of a second cutoff of 0.722 pg/ml was assessed in a population subset reflective of primary care settings only. The availability of a blood‐based biomarker test that can rule out amyloid pathology is expected to be particularly valuable in primary care, especially in geographies where underdiagnosis is more pronounced at the earliest stages, 41 and the economic burden and wait times of confirmatory testing are substantially high. 42 , 43

The performance of plasma pTau181 immunoassays has been previously explored. For example, the fully‐automated platform LUMIPULSE G600II (Fujirebio Europe, Ghent, Belgium) was associated with NPV 88.4% and PPV 74.6% for determining amyloid positivity against CSF biomarkers and based on plasma pTau181 levels in people with a diagnosis of dementia (amyloid positivity prevalence 73%), MCI (amyloid positivity prevalence 51%), or who were cognitively unimpaired (amyloid positivity prevalence 7.6%). 44 The present study is the first to determine and evaluate the clinical performance of the pT181p immunoassay with respect to amyloid‐PET and the Elecsys CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio, which are both well‐established tools for amyloid pathology detection. Good rule‐out clinical performance was observed at 0.934 pg/ml in people presenting across care settings, and was successfully validated against both reference methods, providing confidence in the results as well as comprehensive performance data across different scenarios of clinical practice. The 0.934 pg/ml cutoff was associated with a high NPV (93.8% against amyloid‐PET; 88.8% against the CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio), in line with the desired characteristics for a rule‐out test. 26 The observed PPV (46.6% against amyloid‐PET; 55.8% against the CSF pTau181/Aβ42 ratio) suggested that the risk of amyloid positivity is substantially increased in non‐ruled‐out individuals when compared with the amyloid positivity prevalence. Nevertheless, results above the pT181p immunoassay cutoff may not be consistent with a positive amyloid‐PET scan and require further investigation. A high NPV (97.9%) was also observed for the cutoff of 0.722 pg/ml assessed against amyloid‐PET in people reflective of primary care settings only.

An earlier study modelling the impact of the pT181p immunoassay on diagnostic performance and healthcare resource utilization in the AD diagnostic pathway in the USA showed that introduction of a plasma rule‐out test for amyloid pathology can improve diagnostic outcomes, especially in primary care, where low amyloid pathology prevalence is expected (10% to 23%). 40 Specifically, a 50% increase was observed in the number of true positive amyloid pathology results appropriately referred for confirmatory testing, while the number of false positive referrals decreased by 11.6% compared with standard of care. 40 A reduction in the number of healthcare visits and procedures compared with standard of care was also observed. 40 Therefore, despite the moderate‐to‐low PPV, using the pT181p immunoassay in primary care as a rule‐out test is expected to result in a high rule‐out rate, allowing the early identification of people with a high likelihood of amyloid positivity, who can be referred for confirmatory amyloid‐PET imaging or CSF biomarker analysis and directed to further AD diagnostic assessments and appropriate therapeutic interventions. 12 , 27 The test results may also help streamline the management of people with AD and lead to faster diagnosis and improved outcomes, while supporting physicians to establish a rule‐based plan for referral to specialists. 27 , 45 Conversely, identification of people with a high likelihood of being amyloid negative (ruled out) may prompt physicians to focus on the evaluation of non‐AD‐related causes of cognitive impairment, which may improve the diagnostic pathway of other conditions, while avoiding unnecessary assessments and reducing the AD‐associated patient and system burden. 26 , 27 , 46 Moreover, as reported in the revised Alzheimer's Association and International Working Group criteria, 4 , 12 evaluation of plasma pTau181 levels could aid in the detection of AD neuropathologic changes, leading to more confident clinical decision‐making, particularly in primary care or memory clinics, where people often present with non‐specific symptoms. Using this triage tool may also help identify eligible participants and/or assess treatment outcomes as surrogate endpoints in AD clinical trials, where screening is currently based on amyloid‐PET imaging or CSF biomarker analysis that are associated with low accessibility, high costs, and a high number of screen failures. 47

To enhance the effective use of plasma pTau tests in real‐world clinical practice, it is essential to validate their performance in people reflective of real‐world clinical practice that is not limited to highly preselected patients historically involved in clinical trials (e.g., Caucasian only, minimal comorbidities). Here, in order to reflect the target population for triage tests (people with early signs, symptoms, or complaints of cognitive decline), participants with unknown etiology of their cognitive impairment were enrolled, and specific enrollment criteria were considered by including people with certain QDRS and MMSE scores and excluding those with a diagnosis of moderate and severe dementia, or who had received a comprehensive assessment of AD. Additionally, study participants were aged at least 55 years, representing the population in which triage tests for amyloid pathology would add the most value. 39 The presence of comorbidities in the study population, such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, cancer, and kidney disease, further highlights consistency with clinical practice, as these conditions are commonly seen in this age range and have been associated with variations in amyloid positivity. 48 Unlike this study, previous research has mostly been conducted in highly selected cohorts, where people of different mean age (65 years or > 74 years), MCI only, or with fewer comorbidities were studied. 29 , 44 , 49 , 50 In addition, racial or ethnical diversity was lower in prior studies, with research focusing on comparing non‐Hispanic White with Black people, leading to a higher risk of selection bias. 51 , 52 Overall, to our knowledge, the present work is one of the few considering such a diverse analysis cohort, representative of real‐world populations, to evaluate the clinical performance of a plasma pTau immunoassay, and its results provide confidence on the reliability and applicability of the measurements and the immunoassay performance in ruling out amyloid positivity in real‐world clinical practice.

This study had some limitations, among which is that the assay has been designed as a rule‐out test and cannot be used as a standalone diagnostic tool for definitive confirmation of amyloid pathology and diagnosis of AD or cognitive decline. Instead, it should be used and interpreted alongside other clinical information, serving as an aid in assessing AD and other causes of cognitive decline. Notably, the prevalence of amyloid‐PET positivity in this study was relatively low (22.5%) compared with prior studies evaluating plasma biomarkers for AD. 53 , 54 , 55 This led to a moderate‐to‐low PPV (46.6% and 22.4% for the cutoff values 0.934 and 0.722 pg/ml, respectively), which implies that, while a pT181p immunoassay result equal to or below the cutoff is associated with a low probability of amyloid pathology, immunoassay results exceeding the cutoff do not confirm a high probability of amyloid pathology. As the study was designed to reflect clinical reality in routine care settings, with many participants being at the earlier stage of the AD continuum and being representative of primary care, the observed low prevalence of amyloid‐PET positivity is an important finding when considering PPV results from earlier studies 54 and is expected to represent the prevalence in populations that would benefit most from this rule‐out test. Although the number of amyloid‐negative individuals in the non‐ruled‐out population is still expected to be higher than those who are amyloid‐positive, using the pT181p test can double the percentage of people with a higher likelihood of amyloid pathology compared with the pre‐test probability, justifying further diagnostic investigation. This observation, alongside the high NPV at the established cutoffs, shows performance characteristics that are broadly consistent with those discussed for plasma triage tools, 26 , 56 and provides high confidence in the ability of the pT181p immunoassay to rule out amyloid pathology in the examined population, suggesting that the test can substantially contribute to the efficient and timely evaluation of people with early signs, symptoms, or complaints of cognitive decline.

In conclusion, the pT181p immunoassay is a high‐performance, reliable triage tool to rule out amyloid pathology in people being evaluated for AD. Implementation of the pT181p immunoassay can reduce the number of unnecessary confirmatory tests and streamline access to amyloid pathology confirmation, thus enabling a more efficient, objective, and earlier detection of amyloid pathology and consequently AD. This may subsequently help reduce the burden associated with the current standard of care, and improve the management, quality of life, and outcomes of people with AD. The test has received a CE mark to rule out amyloid pathology across care settings with a cutoff of 0.934 pg/ml, and U.S. FDA approval to rule out amyloid pathology in primary care/prior to referral to a specialist with a cutoff of 0.722 pg/ml.
