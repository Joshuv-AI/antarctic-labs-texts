Understanding Social Drivers of Health Among US Adults with Psoriasis from Different Races and Ethnicities
Authors: Victoria Barbosa, Tina Bhutani-Jacques, Valerie D Callender, Elizabeth D Bacci, Evangeline Pierce, Beth Mitchell, Julia R Correll, Nicole Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPsoriasis, a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin condition with systemic implications and substantial psychosocial burden, affects individuals across races and ethnicities. This study explored social drivers of health (SDOH), disease burden, and the impact of psoriasis across races and ethnicities.MethodsAn online, cross-sectional survey was conducted among adult US patients with psoriasis (September–December 2023). Participants were recruited through the National Psoriasis Foundation and AmeriSpeak, a national sample panel. Descriptive data were collected using patient-reported outcome measures and questions on disease knowledge, healthcare access and utilization, quality of life (QoL), and social impact. Analyses were stratified by races and ethnicities.ResultsAmong 285 participants (mean age 46.7 years; 50.9% male), 68.1% identified as white, 19.3% as Black/African American (BAA), and 20.4% as Hispanic/Latino (H/L). Overall, psoriasis severity was reported as moderate by 36.8% (white: 37.6%, BAA: 25.5%, Asian: 50.0%, and H/L: 27.6%) and severe by 10.2% (white: 8.2%, BAA: 10.9%, Asian: 20.0%, and H/L: 6.9%) of patients. Participants reported moderate QoL impact (Dermatology Life Quality Index [DLQI] mean 10.3), with high burden among H/L (16.6) and BAA (15.1) groups. During flares, 58.6% used prescription medications, with similar rates across races and ethnicities. Transportation barriers were reported by 21.4% overall, more commonly reported by H/L (44.8%) and BAA (34.5%) participants. Difficulty accessing medicine/healthcare was reported by 26.3% overall, particularly white (29.4%) participants. Overall, 29.1% delayed care owing to cultural/linguistic differences, most commonly by BAA and H/L groups. Of the healthcare services requiring partial out-of-pocket payment for treatment of psoriasis, medications were reported as the highest burden by an overall 47.0% of patients (H/L: ~55%, white and BAA ~45%). Overall, only 29.5% received financial assistance.ConclusionsIn this descriptive survey, patients perceived psoriasis as a considerable burden across races and ethnicities. Understanding diversities in patient-reported SDOH and healthcare access issues through culturally competent integrated care may potentially optimize psoriasis outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s13555-026-01851-6.Keywords: Psoriasis, Social impact, Healthcare, Race, Ethnicity

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Psoriasis affects individuals across races and ethnicities. This study explored social drivers of health (SDOH), disease burden, and impact of psoriasis across races and ethnicities. What was learned from the study? Participants identifying as Black/African American and Hispanic/Latino reported more frequent barriers to timely access, including longer wait times for appointments, transportation challenges and cultural or language differences with providers.These findings may prompt healthcare providers to consider how systemic and cultural factors shape patient experiences and access to care.While quality of life impacts were consistently high across groups, reported variations in financial burden and trust in healthcare providers highlight the need for more culturally sensitive and financially accessible care.Enhanced accessibility of digital resources for disease management is needed by integrating patient-centered, accessible support systems into clinical practice.

Introduction

Psoriasis is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory skin condition that typically presents with erythematous, scaly plaques, and is a systemic disease commonly associated with comorbidities, such as psoriatic arthritis, cardiovascular disease, obesity, metabolic syndrome, and depression [1–3]. Beyond its physical manifestations, psoriasis significantly impairs patients’ quality of life (QoL), affecting physical, social, and psychological well-being, and is frequently linked to stigma, social exclusion, discrimination, unemployment, and relationship difficulties [1, 4, 5].

Global prevalence of psoriasis ranges from 2% to 3%, with approximately 3% in the USA. Lower rates are observed in Asian and African populations, while prevalence can reach up to 11% in white populations [6, 7]. A US-based cross-sectional study found the highest prevalence among white (3.6%), followed by Asian (2.5%), Hispanic (1.9%), and Black individuals (1.5%) [8].

Management of psoriasis focuses on alleviating symptoms, improving QoL, and preventing disease progression [9]. Mild cases are typically treated with topical therapies, such as corticosteroids, vitamin D analogs, calcineurin inhibitors, and keratolytics. Moderate-to-severe cases may require phototherapy and systemic agents, including methotrexate, apremilast, acitretin, and cyclosporine. Biologic therapies, including tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) inhibitors, interleukin (IL)-12/23 inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, and IL-23 inhibitors are recommended as first-line systemic treatment options for adults with moderate-to-severe psoriasis who meet disease severity and monitoring criteria, regardless of prior treatment with conventional systemic therapies [3, 9, 10]. These therapies have demonstrated high levels of efficacy in clinical trials, with many achieving psoriasis area and severity index (PASI) 75 and PASI 90 responses and are also associated with substantial improvements in QoL and disease control, underscoring their value in achieving optimal clinical outcomes for patients with psoriasis [11]. However, access disparities persist owing to high costs, insurance restrictions, and variability in coverage, which may limit timely and equitable use of these effective treatments [9, 12].

Recent studies highlight the impact of social drivers of health (SDOH) on disease heterogeneity across racial and ethnic groups. Compared with the white population, individuals from diverse racial or ethnic backgrounds often face greater barriers to healthcare utilization, higher disease burden, poorer QoL, longer diagnostic delays, under-prescription of advanced therapies, and unequal access to healthcare [13–17]. These disparities are compounded by implicit biases, geographic barriers, limited insurance coverage, and a lack of culturally competent care [14].

Despite growing recognition of these disparities, research specifically addressing race and ethnicity-based disparities in psoriasis remains limited. Bringing awareness to healthcare access issues is crucial for understanding how diverse populations experience the condition and access healthcare, which is essential for fostering cultural humility and promoting health equity [18, 19]. The current study aimed to expand on previous research to better understand the overall burden of psoriasis, including disease knowledge and perceptions, healthcare access and utilization, impact on overall QoL, and psychosocial impacts across different racial and ethnic groups in the USA.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This study was a cross-sectional, noninterventional, anonymous web survey conducted in the USA from September 2023 to December 2023, involving participants with psoriatic disease, all of whom had skin psoriasis, with or without psoriatic arthritis. Recruitment was done through the National Psoriasis Foundation, a patient advocacy group, and AmeriSpeak, a survey vendor with a national probability sample frame covering 97% of the US population. Eligible participants were aged ≥ 18 years; able to speak, read, and write US English or US Spanish; capable of using a computer or smartphone with internet access; residing in the USA; willing to provide informed consent; and had a self-reported diagnosis of psoriasis by a medical professional.

Participants were excluded during screening if they had a self-reported diagnosis of three or more of the following conditions: atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, hidradenitis suppurativa, or alopecia areata. Participants with two of these conditions were asked to identify which condition they found most burdensome and completed the survey for that diagnosis only. Patients were also excluded if they were currently enrolled in a clinical trial for these conditions, or had any current or past diagnosis of lupus erythematosus or skin cancer.

Prior to the main survey launch, a pilot study was conducted to test the survey components and evaluate the clarity, relevance, and ease of use of the survey in a web-based format. On the basis of participant feedback from the pilot interviews, the survey was revised and implemented for the main study participants. Several data quality checks were included to ensure survey data integrity such as identification of speedsters (respondents who complete the survey in less than one-third of the median time for respondent completion) and straight-liners (respondents who consistently checked the first or last response to survey questions). Such cases were not considered completers, and their data was removed.

Survey Content

The comprehensive survey design provided a multifaceted view of participants’ health and psychosocial factors. It was a self-administered electronic survey consisting of 80–110 items, which included screening questions to assess participant eligibility and patient-reported outcome (PRO) measures (Fitzpatrick Skin Scale, Monk Skin Tone scale, European Quality of Life 5 Dimensions 5 Levels [EQ-5D-5L], Dermatology Life Quality Index [DLQI], Patient Global Impression of Severity, Neurological Disorders [Neuro-QoL] Stigma Short Form, and Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System [PROMIS] Managing Social Interactions Short Form), the Protocol for Responding to and Assessing Patients’ Assets, Risks, and Experiences (PRAPARE) tool, a subset of health care resource utilization (HCRU) questions from the All of Us Research Program, alongside de novo survey items [20–28]. A summary of the PRO measures and de novo questions can be found in Table S1.

Data Analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the sample and participants’ responses to the study questions. Analyses were stratified by racial and ethnic groups, including Black or African American (BAA), white, American Indian or Alaska Native (AI/AN), Asian, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander (NH/PI), two or more races, and Hispanic or Latino (H/L) or non-Hispanic or Latino (non-H/L). Racial and ethnic identities were asked separately, with responses not being mutually exclusive. Continuous variables were summarized using the mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range, while categorical variables were presented as frequency and percentage distribution by category.

Data analysis was performed using SAS Software, version 9.4. No data imputation was performed, and unanswered questions were coded as missing. All instruments were scored according to the developers’ guidelines, including handling missing data. Data were analyzed descriptively, and no adjustments for multiple comparisons were made.

No formal comparative statistical analyses were performed; therefore, any observed differences between study groups should be interpreted as descriptive rather than inferential.

Ethical Considerations

The study received approval from the Advarra Institutional Review Board. It was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, Good Pharmacoepidemiology Practices, and applicable laws and regulations. Participants authorized the use and disclosure of their personal health information, covering the collection and release of data related to treatment and its outcomes throughout the study period. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the study, and all recruitment procedures complied with current Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act regulations.

Results

Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Among 285 participants who enrolled and completed the survey (mean age, 46.7 years; 50.9% male), 194 (68.1%) identified as white, 55 (19.3%) as BAA, 10 (3.5%) as Asian, 5 (1.8%) as AI/AN, 3 (1.1%) as NH/PI, and 18 (6.3%) as of two or more races. In addition, 58 participants (20.4%) identified as H/L (Table 1). All results are descriptive, and data for very small subgroups (Asian and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander) are reported for descriptive completeness only and are not intended for comparative interpretation.

Quality of Life

EQ-5D-5L

Participants of different races and ethnicities showed minimal variation in health-related QoL, with an overall mean (SD) EQ-5D-5L index score of 0.7 (0.3) and an EQ-5D-5L Visual Analog Scale score of 67.8 (17.4) (Table S2). BAA and H/L participants reported slight/moderate problems in mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression (Table S3).

DLQI

The overall mean (SD) score was 10.3 (7.2), indicating a moderate impact on participants’ QoL. Most participants (92.2%) reported some effect: 24.2% small, 27.0% moderate, 28.4% very large, and 12.6% extremely large effect. Mean scores were generally consistent across racial and ethnic groups. BAA and H/L participants reported that psoriasis considerably impacted their physical and emotional well-being, daily activities (sports/leisure), work/study, interpersonal relationships, and the frequency of problems caused by skin treatments (Table S4).

Neuro-QoL Stigma Short Form, and PROMIS Self-Efficacy for Managing Chronic Conditions

The overall mean (SD) Neuro-QoL Stigma Short Form score was 17.6 (7.4), indicating a moderate level of perceived stigma among participants, with minimal variations across the groups (Table S5).

The overall mean (SD) PROMIS Self-Efficacy score was 28.9 (7.2), reflecting moderate confidence among participants in managing their psoriasis, with scores generally consistent across groups. Most participants reported being “somewhat/quite/very” confident, notably white participants (Table S6).

PRAPARE

SDOH were assessed using PRAPARE, with higher scores indicating greater social risk/vulnerability. The overall mean (SD) score was 3.6 (2.7), suggesting moderate social risk, with slight variations across the groups (H/L: 5.5 [2.8]); white 3.1 [2.6]) (Fig. 1a).

In response to the PRAPARE question on transportation barriers, 21.4% of participants reported that a lack of transportation kept them from attending medical appointments—most notably H/L (44.8%) and BAA (34.5%) (Fig. 1b). Participants were also asked whether they or their household members could not access necessities when needed in the past year. Overall, 17.2% of participants reported being unable to access food, while 26.3% could not access medicine or healthcare when needed. The inability to access medicine or healthcare was 29.4% among white, 16.4% among BAA, and 25.9% among H/L participants (Fig. 1c).

HCRU Survey Questions

The HCRU survey questions captured multiple aspects of patient–provider interactions, including respect from healthcare providers (HCPs), involvement of patients’ opinions in care, clarity of information, need for culturally congruent care, and delays in care due to cultural barriers.

Overall, 89.5% of participants reported that were treated with respect by their doctors or HCPs “always” or “most of the time.” However, 10.2% reported being treated with respect “some or none of the time” (two or more races: 16.7%, BAA: 12.7%, H/L: 12.0%, Asian: 10.0%, white: 8.7%) (Table 3). Although there were slight numerical differences across racial and ethnic groups, the variation was relatively small, suggesting that the experience of not always being treated with respect by HCPs may be a broadly shared concern rather than one limited to specific groups.

Patient responses to healthcare access and utilization survey by race and ethnicity

When asked how often their doctors or HCPs asked their opinions or beliefs on medical care or treatment, 33.0% of participants responded “always” and 25.3% “some of the time.” The proportion of patients who reported “none of the time” (10.9%) were similar across all groups (Table 3). Overall, 18.9% of participants reported that it was easy for them to understand healthcare information given by their HCPs either “some of the time” or “none of the time,” with consistent patterns across most racial and ethnic groups (Table 3).

A total of 24.9% of participants believed that it was “very important” for their doctors/HCPs to understand or be similar to them (e.g., in race, religion, or language), particularly by those from two or more races (38.9%). In contrast, 16.5% participants stated that it was “not important at all,” specifically white participants (20.1%) (Table 3). When asked how often they delayed or did not visit doctors or HCPs owing to differences in race, religion, or native language, 29.1% of participants reported “always” or “most of the time” (commonly BAA and H/L), while 52.3% reported “none of the time” (Table 3).

Preferred Support for Psoriasis Management, Healthcare Access, and Cost

Participants most commonly sought support for managing psoriasis from dermatologists (48.1%), primary care providers (40.4%), online searches (34.0%), psoriasis organizations (21.8%), and family members/online community (21.1% each). The support from online searches was commonly reported by participants of two or more races (44.4%) (Fig. 2a). Overall, when asked what influenced them to see an HCP for their psoriasis, 36.1% reported family members, 20.4% reported through previous experience or knowledge, and 18.6% reported social media. Among participants who mentioned social media (n/N = 53/285; 18.6%), TikTok was most used (98.1%), followed by Facebook (96.2%) and Twitter (90.6%); responses were not mutually exclusive (Table S7).

When asked how much they trusted their provider to treat psoriasis effectively, 44.3% of participants responded “somewhat/a little bit” (BAA: 74.6%, H/L: 72.0%, white: 33.8%); while 2.8% reported “not at all.” High (very much) trust levels varied by race and ethnicity (BAA [5.5%], H/L [14.0%], and white [24.4%]) (Fig. 2b). Medications were the most commonly paid out-of-pocket healthcare service for psoriasis in the past 6 months (47.0%) (responses not mutually exclusive). Discussion

This study, conducted among US participants with psoriasis, utilized a comprehensive survey-based analysis to explore patient perspectives on disease burden, social and QoL impacts, and healthcare access and utilization among a racially and ethnically diverse cohort. Variations in QoL, healthcare access, and utilization—such as trust in HCPs, travel and wait times, appointment availability during flares, and emergency room visits during flares—were observed among patients with psoriasis across racial and ethnic groups.

Psoriasis is often accompanied by comorbidities, such as anxiety and depression [29, 30]. In our study, a notable proportion of participants reported these conditions, with BAA and H/L participants reporting them less frequently. This finding contrasts with prior research indicating higher comorbidity rates among Latinos, possibly due to differences in healthcare access and utilization between the sample population [31]. For instance, in our sample, 35% of BAA and 28% of H/L participants were covered by Medicaid, compared with 21% of white participants, potentially reflecting issues in health care access. In addition, reluctance to disclose mental health diagnoses among BAA and H/L populations may contribute to under-reporting. Other studies support our findings, showing higher psychiatric comorbidity rates among white patients [31, 32].

Nearly half of the participants reported moderate-to-severe psoriasis, with Asian participants more frequently reporting severe psoriasis. These findings align with previous studies [33]; however, the underlying reasons for severity among certain groups are likely multifactorial. Genetic heterogeneity, including variations in alleles implicated in psoriasis pathogenesis, may contribute to differences in disease severity and presentation across populations [34]. In addition, socioeconomic factors, health literacy, social vulnerability, religious perceptions of disease and treatment, and issues, such as reduced face-to-face time and counseling with dermatologists, may all play roles [35, 36]. Collectively, these factors underscore the complex interplay between biology, environment, and healthcare systems in shaping psoriasis outcomes [37].

While most participants were satisfied with their current treatment, nearly a quarter reported dissatisfaction, highlighting unmet needs in psoriasis care [38–40]. This dissatisfaction is notable given psoriasis’s broad and often greater impact on QoL compared with other skin conditions [41]. Our study found that QoL was impaired across racial and ethnic groups, affecting daily activities, relationships, work/study, and physical, mental, social, and cognitive functioning. Although QoL impact appeared broadly similar in our sample, prior studies have identified differences in health-related QoL among racial and ethnic groups; a prior cross-sectional study reported that Black (8.3) and H/L (8.0) patients have reported higher mean DLQI scores than white patients (5.6) [34]. These findings demonstrate the pervasive impact of psoriasis on QoL and highlight the importance of understanding the different SDOH unique to racial and ethnic groups.

The lack of routine healthcare access can lead to elevated risk of psoriasis [42]. In this study, SDOH assessed via the PRAPARE tool, revealed that white participants reported few barriers to healthcare access. Over a quarter of participants reported difficulties obtaining appointments, with BAA and H/L participants reporting longer delays (over 2 months). Transportation was a barrier for nearly one-fifth, particularly among BAA and H/L groups. These findings align with previous research showing delayed medical care among Black and Hispanic patients with chronic inflammatory skin diseases (CISDs), including psoriasis, owing to transportation issues [43].

Disparities in cultural perceptions can hinder health-seeking behavior for psoriasis. Previous research has shown delays in care among Black, Asian, and Hispanic patients with CISDs [43]. In the current study, more than half of participants reported that it was important for their HCPs to understand or be similar to them in terms of race, religion, or language. The observed differences in healthcare utilization reflect variations in reporting among study participants, with BAA and H/L participants more often reporting delays or avoidance of care owing to perceived racial, linguistic, or religious differences with providers than white participants. A nationally representative study from the USA reported that patients with limited English proficiency may have difficulty interacting with the healthcare system, leading to undiagnosed psoriasis [44]. Increasing diversity in the dermatology workforce is essential as racial concordance improves patient satisfaction, treatment adherence, and trust—ultimately improving health outcomes [45–48].

Furthermore, challenges regarding health resource utilization were evident in this study. About 15% of participants had not consulted an HCP for psoriasis, while nearly half sought support from a dermatologist, and over a third relied on over-the-counter products or medications during flare-ups. This pattern was consistent across most racial and ethnic groups. Prior studies also show that Black and Asian patients were about 40% less likely than white patients to seek dermatologic care despite often having more severe disease [49, 50]. In addition, Black and other racial minority groups may have limited access to effective treatments and have been less likely than white patients to use biologic medications for psoriasis [51, 52].

Notably, some patients used online searches for support in managing psoriasis, with social media such as TikTok, Facebook, and Twitter influencing a few patients to see an HCP. These platforms can offer emotional support and patient-to-patient education, reflecting changing health information-seeking behaviors. For those with digital literacy and access, social media may complement clinical care and improve disease understanding. However, there may be substantial missed opportunities for adequately leveraging digital resources for supporting psoriasis management. In addition, for others, reliance on these platforms may signal barriers to in-person care or mistrust in healthcare systems. In addition, exposure to misinformation on social media, particularly related to medications, can complicate informed decision-making [53, 54]. These findings underscore the importance of HCPs guiding patients toward accurate, supportive digital resources.

Finally, cost was a common barrier reported in this study—over a quarter of participants skipped appointments owing to out-of-pocket costs, most commonly white participants. Affordability barriers to timely care persists across racial and ethnic groups, particularly in those with lower income or no insurance coverage [55]. Fewer than 30% of participants reported receiving financial assistance through the patient assistance program, primarily BAA and H/L participants. Prior studies indicate that Black and Hispanic patients with CISDs often experience delays in seeking care, and filling prescriptions owing to affordability challenges, compared with white patients [43]. Insurance coverage-related challenges further hinder their access to specialized care [14]. These findings highlight the need to address financial burden more broadly in efforts to improve outcomes.

This study advances current knowledge by assessing the social drivers of health reported by different racial and ethnic groups and provides real-world insights into the unique perspectives and unmet needs of diverse groups—that may influence treatment engagement, adherence, and eventually clinical outcomes. Awareness of barriers to care for psoriasis, influenced by SDOH, such as stigma, healthcare access limitations, geographic constraints, out-of-pocket costs, and cultural beliefs, can facilitate tailored management strategies [48, 56–58]. Addressing these barriers require culturally competent treatment approaches that integrate SDOH into clinical decision-making to ensure equitable outcomes [59]. For example, awareness of geographic location, transportation barriers, or insurance coverage limitations for access to specialized care (such as infusion centers) may inform modification of the management plan. Increasing provider understanding of the social and structural factors that affect patients’ experiences can help foster more culturally and socioeconomically responsive care and help reduce healthcare access issues. Future research focused on SDOH across diverse populations is crucial for optimizing psoriasis care and promoting healthcare access.

Limitations

The findings of this study should be interpreted considering the following limitations. First, the diagnosis of psoriasis was self-reported and not confirmed by a medical provider owing to the anonymous nature of the survey. Consequently, all responses are subject to self-report and recall biases, potentially affecting the accuracy of the reported information. These measures are inherently subjective; however, they are appropriate for assessing experiences central to patient-centered research. In addition, the convenience sampling method, in which participants were recruited through the National Psoriasis Foundation, limits the generalizability of the results. Only those who received and read the study advertisement were included. The small sample sizes for certain racial groups (such as AI/AN, Asian, and NH/PI) further limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader psoriasis population and reduce the ability to perform robust statistical comparisons.

This study was purely descriptive, and no variables were controlled in the analyses. While variability in sex, disease severity, and gender distribution across racial and ethnic groups was observed, these factors were not controlled for. Similarly, differences in socioeconomic status and educational attainment—known influencers of healthcare access and outcomes—were not controlled as potential confounders. Future studies may benefit from using multivariable models to adjust for these factors to better understand the drivers of healthcare access issues.

While the Neuro-QoL Stigma Short Form, and the PROMIS Managing Social Interactions Short Form are validated measures, they have not been specifically validated in psoriasis populations.

Conclusions

This descriptive cross-sectional survey showed that patients perceive psoriasis as a considerable burden that may affect QoL across racial and ethnic groups. The observed variations in social barriers and patient-reported challenges in healthcare access and utilization demonstrate the need for culturally competent and enhanced interdisciplinary care. A holistic, patient-centered approach that integrates therapeutic management with social interventions may potentially optimize psoriasis care and improve QoL.
