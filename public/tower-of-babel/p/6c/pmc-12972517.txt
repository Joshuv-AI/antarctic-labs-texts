Global research trends in hereditary angioedema, 1972–2023: Bibliometric analysis of productivity, collaboration, and thematic evolution☆
Authors: Hugo WF Mak, Hilary J Longhurst, Markus Magerl, Jonny Peter, Marc Riedl, Philip H Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare, potentially life-threatening condition. In recent years, the emergence of novel therapies has likely contributed to substantial growth in HAE research; however, a comprehensive review of the HAE research landscape remains lacking.MethodsWe conducted a systematic literature search on the Web of Science Core Collection and extracted metadata for all HAE records from 1972 to 2023. Bibliometric analysis was performed to outline trends in research productivity, participation, collaboration, focus, and global patterns in HAE research.ResultsWe analysed a total of 2443 publications. Research output increased over time (annual growth rate = 6.34%) and was correlated to countries’ Human Development Index and Gross National Income per capita (Spearman ρ > 0.40, p < 0.001). Regional analysis based on WHO classifications revealed >85% of HAE studies were led by researchers from Europe and America. However, contributions from the Western Pacific rose significantly (1972–2010: 4.6% vs 2011–2023: 8.6%, p < 0.001). International collaboration rose significantly over time, increasing from 10.9% (1972–2010) to 27.7% (2011–2023) (p < 0.001). Keyword and trend analyses indicated that research focus shifted from pathophysiology and diagnosis to patient outcomes, treatment and prophylaxis. Specifically, long-term prophylaxis emerged as a key area in recent research.ConclusionsThe volume of HAE-related publications is rapidly increasing, reflecting a transition from basic science to more clinically oriented research, particularly focusing on long-term prophylaxis. While global research efforts are becoming increasingly collaborative and diverse, participation from Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean, and South-East Asia remains limited. Greater support for these regions is essential to ensure equitable progress in HAE research and care.Keywords: Angioedema, Bibliometric, C1 inhibitor, C1–INH, Deficiency, Hereditary, Productivity, Research, Trends

Introduction

First described by Quincke and Osler respectively in 1882 and 1888, hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a genetic, potentially life-threatening disorder characterised by recurrent episodes of angioedema, and is often underdiagnosed and undertreated 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. The epidemiology of this “rare” disorder remains poorly understood, with reported prevalence estimates varying between 1 in 50,000 to 5,000,000 7, 8, 9. In most patients, angioedema results from dysregulation of the plasma contact system, due to either deficiency or dysfunction of C1 inhibitor (C1–INH). Over-activation of the plasma contact system produces excess bradykinin, which increases vascular permeability and causes swelling.10 Beyond the acute attacks of angioedema, HAE is also associated with anxiety and fear of future episodes, impaired work and school performance, and reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL) 11, 12, 13. In many regions, limited access to effective, HAE-specific therapies remains a major barrier to optimal patient management. Over the past decade, novel on-demand and prophylactic treatments have emerged, transforming HAE into a more manageable disease. These advances have not only revolutionized clinical care but also spurred significant growth in HAE-related research.

Bibliometric analysis is a methodological approach that has rapidly gained popularity across various fields of medicine in recent years 14, 15, 16, 17. Its unique capacity to process the metadata of tens of thousands of publications enables comprehensive, large-scale reviews at the disease or even discipline level that are difficult to achieve using conventional methods such as meta-analysis.18 As a result, bibliometric analysis serves as a powerful tool for synthesizing research output and mapping the development of a given field. It facilitates the identification of historical and emerging trends, key research hotspots, and persistent knowledge gaps, ultimately guiding future research directions. To our knowledge, this approach has been applied in various immunological conditions, including different inborn errors of immunity, but not yet to HAE.19,20 To address this gap, we conducted a systematic bibliometric analysis spanning a 50-year period, focusing on HAE research productivity, international participation and collaboration, as well as thematic focus and evolving trends.

Methods

We conducted a systematic search of literature on the Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC) on August 27, 2023 with the search strategy as TS = “hereditary angioedema” (Fig. 1). The database was selected for its comprehensive coverage and high-quality metadata, which are essential for conducting robust bibliometric and systematic analyses.18 Duplicate records were removed prior to screening for eligibility. Only original (articles and letters) and review articles were included; while other document types such as meeting abstracts, editorials, and corrections were excluded. Non-English publications were also excluded. The metadata of all eligible publications, including document type, title, authors and their affiliations, published journal and year, abstract and keywords, number of citations and cited references were then extracted and pooled for analysis. Country of origin of an article is defined as the country of origin of the corresponding author's affiliation. Countries are categorized according to the World Health Organisation (WHO) classification into 6 regions: Africa, Americas, Eastern Mediterranean (EM), Europe, South-East Asia (SEA) and Western Pacific (WP).21 Data on countries' Human Development Index (HDI) and Gross National Income (GNI) per capita were extracted from the United Nations Development Programme Human Development Report.22 Using reference publication year spectroscopy (RPYS), which identified a peak in citations in 2010 (with 2077 more citations than the 5-year median), we divided the dataset into 2 time periods: 1972–2010 and 2011–2023 (Supplementary Figure E1).

Literature search strategy and results

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed on R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) and IBM SPSS Statistics version 28.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY). Specifically, biblioshiny, a web-interface based on the bibliometrix and shiny packages was used for data analysis and visualisation.23 We first analysed publication trends over time and by country of origin (and WHO region). Patterns of international participation and collaboration across different time periods were also assessed. Specifically, we compared the proportion of publications originating from different WHO regions, as well as the proportion of multinational collaborations (papers involving authors from multiple countries) between the periods 1972–2010 and 2011–2023 using the Chi-square test. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between countries' HDI and GNI per capita and their respective numbers of HAE-related publications. Next, the popular keywords and trend topics in different time periods were visualised. Keywords reflecting the search criteria per se, such as “hereditary angioedema” and “HAE” were excluded. Synonyms or highly similar terms, such as “C1 inhibitor” and “C1-esterase inhibitor”, were grouped together. The lists of excluded keywords and merged synonyms can be found in Supplementary Appendix E1. Statistical significance was denoted by p-value <0.05. Ethical approval is not required for this study because it only involves published, publicly available data.

Results

Research on HAE surged in the past decade

A total of 4349 records were identified using the predefined search strategy on WOSCC with no duplicates identified (Fig. 1). Initial screening excluded 1714 records which were not original articles nor reviews, leaving 2635 records for eligibility assessment. Of these, an additional 192 non-English records were removed. Eventually, 2443 records from 684 journals, contributed by 6994 unique authors from 74 countries, were included in the final analysis.

The included publications comprised of 1740 (71.2%) full research articles, 274 (11.2%) letters and 429 (17.6%) reviews, spanning the period between 1972 and 2023. Collectively, these works received 61,432 citations (mean = 25.1 citations per article) and yielded an h-index of 108. As illustrated in Fig. 2, HAE-related publications showed a steady upward trend over time, with an annual growth rate of 6.34%.

Over 85% of HAE studies were led by researchers from Europe and America

Country of origin was successfully extracted in 95.0% (2322/2443) records, where 2301 (99.1%) were from countries with at least a high HDI (≥0.700). Notably, 41 countries with a very high HDI (≥0.800) produced 92.9% of all publications (2157/2322). Both HDI and GNI per capita were significantly associated with the number of HAE-related publications: HDI (Spearman ρ = 0.511, p < 0.001); GNI per capita (Spearman ρ = 0.462, p < 0.001).

Fig. 3 shows the trend of publications according to WHO regions. Since 2000, Europe remained the most prolific region in HAE research, followed by the Americas. Combined, these 2 regions contributed to at least 85.4% (2087/2443) of all HAE-related publications. Meanwhile, the WP demonstrated a significant increase in HAE research output (1972–2010: 4.6% vs 2011–2023: 8.6%, p < 0.001). In contrast, the remaining 3 regions (Africa, EM and SEA) collectively accounted for only 2.7% (46/1727) of HAE publications in the most recent period.

At the country level, all 10 most productive were classified as having very high HDI and were located in either Europe or the Americas, except Japan. International collaborations on a rising trend, especially after the millennium

Overall, 22.8% (556/2443) of the analysed records involved authors from at least 2 countries and were considered multinational. As shown in Fig. 4, the proportion of multinational work increased significantly over time—from 10.9% in the 1972–2010 period to 27.7% in the 2011–2023 period (p < 0.001). Concurrently, the average number of authors per paper rose from 4.23 in 1972–2010 to 6.64 in 2011–2023, with an overall mean of 5.93 authors per publication.

Research focuses shifted from pathophysiology and diagnosis to patient outcomes, treatment and prophylaxis

A total of 2649 unique keywords were retrieved from the extracted records. Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table E1 show the most frequent keywords in HAE publications of all time and in different time periods, respectively. Over the past 50 years, the top 3 most frequently cited keywords were “bradykinin” (254/2649), “icatibant” (107/2649), and “complement” (94/2649).

When analysed temporally, “bradykinin” became the most frequently mentioned keyword after the year 2000, surpassing “complement,” which gradually declined in ranking. Additionally, “quality of life” emerged as the second most frequent keyword in 2021 (26/876). Trend analysis also highlighted emerging areas of interest, including “COVID-19,” “SARS-CoV-2,” and “rare disease” (Supplementary Figure E2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive bibliometric analysis to systematically evaluate nearly all existing literature on HAE. Our findings reveal a steady expansion in HAE research over recent decades, with a marked surge in both publications and citations since 2010—coinciding with the peak identified in the RPYS that year. This suggests that research published in 2010 was highly influential and was frequently cited afterward. Notably, 2008–2010 (especially 2010) emerges as a seminal period in HAE research, likely driven by 3 landmark clinical trials evaluating nanofiltered C1-inhibitor concentrate, icatibant, and ecallantide 24, 25, 26, 27. Following the approval of these agents, there was a growing focus on disease management and HRQoL.

Over time, the focus of HAE research has shifted from acute, on-demand treatment toward long-term prophylaxis, as evidenced by the declining prominence of “icatibant” and “ecallantide,” and the rising use of terms such as “prophylaxis,” “lanadelumab,” and “berotralstat”. In recent years, new long-term prophylaxis options have proven highly effective in reducing attack frequency and improving HRQoL. However, access remains limited in many regions, including developed areas like Hong Kong and Singapore.28,29 Improving global access to long-term prophylaxis should be a key priority for the international HAE community and strategies such as strengthening patient advocacy groups, which have been associated with improved access to HAE-specific treatments, will continue to play a crucial role in achieving this goal.8,28

The term “quality of life” consistently ranked among the most frequently used keywords since 2011 and even rose to second place between 2021 and 2023. This shift reflects the development and integration of condition-specific patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), such as the Angioedema Quality of Life Questionnaire in 2012, the Angioedema Activity Score in 2013, and the Angioedema Control Test in 2020 30, 31, 32. Since their debut, patient-reported outcomes (PROs) especially HRQoL had an increasingly important role in HAE care and research, ranging from serial assessment in day-to-day practice to endpoints in clinical trials.33 However, the availability of validated PROMs remains uneven across regions. Prior to clinical application (and research use), PROMs must undergo cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation. At writing, many PROMs have limited availability beyond Europe or North America, forming barriers to cross-national collaborations, as well as more equitable patient representation in clinical studies.34 Expanding access to these instruments is essential for more inclusive, globally representative research.

An encouraging trend observed in this study was the increasing level of international collaboration in HAE research. Prior to the millennium, research activity was largely confined to North America and Europe, with collaborations limited within or between the 2 continents. Thereafter, some countries in WP (such as Japan, Korea and Thailand) began to emerge but remained fairly segregated, with little collaboration with Western countries until 2010. Since then, more WP countries became involved and gradually formed a third cluster until the COVID-19 pandemic. In the post-pandemic era, researchers appear to have now formed a single, interconnected network with the proportion of multinational authorship nearly tripled—from ∼10% in the late 20th century to over 30%. We postulate that this was driven, at least partially, by the establishment of the Angioedema Centers of Reference and Excellence (ACARE) network in 2019. However, regions such as Africa, the Eastern Mediterranean (EM), and South-East Asia (SEA) remain underrepresented. These disparities echo findings from a recent pooled analysis of HAE trials conducted in the past 2 decades, which reported persistent underrepresentation of non-European/North American sites and non-White racial groups.35 Contributing factors may include systemic challenges such as limited research funding, language barriers, and inadequate healthcare infrastructure.15 More pertinent to HAE, low awareness and underdiagnosis of the condition outside of Western countries likely contribute to the perceived lower disease burden and reduced research interest in these regions. To address these disparities, expanding the reach of global networks such as ACARE and HAE International or establishing region-specific centers of excellence may be critical.36 Such initiatives can provide much-needed infrastructure, training, and access to clinical trials for underrepresented regions.

Historically, HAE has often been considered rare outside Europe and North America, with a vast difference in prevalence in older studies, as extreme as 1 to 10 in 10,000,000 in Asia versus 1 in 50,000 in the West.37 However, more recent evidence shows that the true burden of HAE in the Asia-Pacific region is much larger than previously thought.9 Although the prevalence still varies across different Asia-Pacific localities, there are higher estimates in Singapore (0.33 per 100,000), Hong Kong (0.60 per 100,000), Australia and New Zealand (1.00 per 100,000), contrasting the regional lowest reported in India (0.02 per 100,000).8,38,39 These findings suggest that increased awareness, larger patient cohorts, and greater local investment in diagnosis and care may explain the rising research engagement observed in the WP.

Importantly, access to modern diagnostic tools and therapies still remains limited in many countries. C1–INH function testing, which has long been recommended by international guidelines for diagnosing type II HAE (accounting for 15–20% of all cases), is often only available in specialized centers or for research purposes.40 This may partly explain the apparent differences in reported prevalence between high- and low-resource settings, reinforcing the correlation between a country's socioeconomic development and its HAE research output.8,9,41 Once again, this demonstrates how active research can catalyze better patient outcomes by facilitating access to advanced diagnostics, enabling participation in clinical trials, and informing policy decision.29 At the regional level, locally generated evidence is also essential for accurately capturing disease burden and unmet needs, thereby fostering awareness among clinicians, policymakers, funders, and the public.42 Ensuring adequate and equitable representation of patients across age groups, genders, races, ethnicities, geographic regions, and socioeconomic strata is vital to producing generalizable research findings, especially in clinical trials.

This study has several limitations. As it was conducted using WOSCC as the sole data source, we may have excluded relevant studies published in regional journals not indexed by the database, potentially affecting representation from low-resource settings. While changes in journal indexing over time, such as the inclusion of more journals into the WOSCC database, may contribute to the increase in HAE literature, the historical list of indexed journals was not retrievable. Although we have tried combining synonyms for more representative and reliable results, it is possible that certain keywords of lower frequency may still be overlooked. Articles not written in English were excluded from our analysis so scholars and research output from certain non-English speaking countries or regions may be under-represented. Also, since we only used “hereditary angioedema” as the search string, our findings may not be generalised to either acquired C1–INH deficiency or HAE with normal C1–INH.

In summary, this systematic bibliometric review sheds light on our meta-knowledge of HAE, demonstrating the field's rapid development and the gradual shift in focus from pathophysiological mechanisms to patient-centred outcomes, including HRQoL and long-term prophylaxis. Although there is promising evidence of increasing diversity and international collaboration, significant gaps remain—particularly in Africa, EM, and SEA. As the global community strives toward the shared vision of a normal quality of life for all HAE patients, fostering greater collaboration and synergy remains critically important.
