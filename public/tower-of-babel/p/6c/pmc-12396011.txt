Healthy lifestyles and risk of major non-communicable chronic diseases and mortality in individuals with metabolic syndrome
Authors: Xiang Gao, Jiali Zheng, Mengdan Liu, Chong Liu, Yixin Wang, Yan Li, Weihua Chen, Xiaoguang Li, Hui Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesTo assess the associations between lifestyle factors and the risk of non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) and mortality in individuals with metabolic syndrome (MS), and to evaluate population-attributable risk (PAR) from multiple lifestyle factors.MethodsThe study included 49,891 MS individuals from the UK Biobank. Seven lifestyle factors (smoking, diet, physical activity, sedentary behavior, sleep duration, psychological stress, and social contact status) were assessed using both unweighted and weighted lifestyle scores. Cox proportional hazards model was used to analyze the associations between lifestyle factors and major NCDs (diabetes, cardiovascular disease [CVD], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [COPD], chronic kidney disease [CKD], and cancer) and all-cause mortality.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 11.0 years, 19,435 major NCDs and 3,578 deaths were recorded. Higher adherence to healthy lifestyle factors, assessed by both unweighted and weighted scores, was significantly associated with lower risks of major NCDs and all-cause mortality. Compared with participants having 0−3 healthy lifestyle factors (unweighted score), those with 6−7 factors had 28% (HR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.69−0.75) lower risk of major NCDs and 39% (HR = 0.61, 95% CI: 0.55−0.69) lower risk of mortality. Correspondingly, using the weighted lifestyle score, the very healthy group showed a 27% (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.70−0.76) lower risk of major NCDs and a 40% (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.54−0.66) lower risk of mortality compared with the very unhealthy group. The PARs associated with all 7 healthy lifestyle factors were 20.72% and 26.93% for major NCDs and all-cause mortality, respectively.ConclusionsThe adoption of multiple healthy behaviors significantly reduces the risk of NCDs and mortality in MS patients, highlighting the need for more comprehensive lifestyle interventions targeted at this group with metabolic impairments.

Introduction

In the last century, preventable non-communicable chronic diseases (NCDs) have changed from marginal killers to major causes of global mortality [1]. According to the latest statistics from the Global Burden of Disease Study, non-communicable diseases contributed 1.73 billion (95% UI 1.54–1.94) disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) in 2021 [2]. By 2050, the predominant causes of DALYs worldwide are projected to be major NCDs such as cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3]. Metabolic syndrome (MS), a cluster of clinical indicators that include glucose intolerance, insulin resistance, central obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension [4], has become a global threat [1,5,6] as a clinical precursor to various NCDs [7]. Reducing the risk of NCDs and mortality through effective MS management has become a significant public health challenge.

Lifestyle interventions have been increasingly recognized as efficient non- pharmacologic strategies for the primary and secondary prevention of MS [8,9]. There have been many studies on the relationship between lifestyles and mortality of the general population or specific populations, such as people with type 2 diabetes [10], hypertension [11], and even MS [12]. Additionally, some studies have investigating the association between lifestyles and cardiovascular disease (CVD) in the general population, with subgroup analyses conducted to assess whether these associations persist in individuals with prevalent conditions such as hypertension, diabetes, and obesity [13]. However, evidence on the relationship between lifestyles and risk of major NCDs in people with MS is lacking. Moreover, studies on lifestyle relationships with NCDs and mortality outcomes have focused on only a limited number of lifestyle factors [14,15]. It is crucial to apply the theoretical framework of lifestyle medicine to integrate emerging factors such as sleep, psychological stress, and social engagement into lifestyle scoring systems [16,17]. In addition, the extent to which the risk of NCDs and mortality can be attributed to unhealthy lifestyles in this population is uncertain.

To fill these gaps, we leveraged data from the UK Biobank to examine the associations between a combination of unhealthy lifestyle factors and the risk of NCDs and all-cause mortality, and to estimate the proportions of NCDs and all-cause mortality risk that could theoretically be prevented through lifestyle modifications in adults with MS.

Research design and methods

Study population

The study design and population of the UK Biobank have been documented earlier. [18]. In brief, the UK Biobank study started in 2006 and, until 2010, recruited more than 500,000 participants aged 40–70 years from the general population across 22 assessment centers within the United Kingdom. Participants contributed information on lifestyle and other potential health-related factors via detailed questionnaires, interviews, and physical assessments. The follow-up of patients started at enrollment until the incidence of first major NCD the date of death, or end of study as 30 November 2022, whichever came first.

A clinical consensus was utilized to identify a range of conditions representing major NCDs, including CVD, cancer, diabetes, COPD, and CKD, as the primary outcomes [19]. Participants were excluded if they did not have MS (n = 371,713), had incomplete data for MS components (n = 16,840), reported a history of any of the NCDs prior to enrollment (n = 37,321), or had missing data on exposures or covariates (n = 26,606), leaving 49,891 participants for the final analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1). All participants provided written informed consent. The UK Biobank study received approval from the North West Multi-center Research Ethics Committee. UK Biobank data are available for investigators after the acceptance of a research proposal. The present study was conducted under application number 83432 of the UK Biobank resource.

Ascertainment of MS

MS was characterized by the presence of at least three of the following conditions: central obesity, high blood pressure, dyslipidemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and hyperglycemia. These conditions were evaluated during the initial assessment through physical measurements and self-reported medication use. Definitions for each condition were as follows [4]: (a) Waist circumference (Field ID 48) was measured using a Seca 200 cm tape around the narrowest part of the torso. Central obesity was defined as ≥88 cm for women and ≥102 cm for men; (b) Systolic and diastolic pressures (Field ID 4079, 94, 4080, 93) were measured twice using IntelliSense blood pressure monitor model HEM-907XL (Omron) after a rest of at least 5 min. High blood pressure was defined as a systolic blood pressure of 130 mmHg or greater (average of two measurements), or a diastolic blood pressure of 85 mmHg or greater (average of two measurements), or as taking antihypertensive drugs [4]; (c) Low high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (Field ID 30760) was defined as <1.0 mmol/L in males, <1.3 mmol/L in females, or taking drugs treating low HDL cholesterol; (d) Hypertriglyceridemia was defined as triglyceride levels ≥ 1.7 mmol/L (Field ID 30870) or taking triglyceride-lowering drugs; (e) Hyperglycemia was identified by an hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) (Field ID 30750) level 6.0% to <6.5% or use of diabetes medications. The information of the above-mentioned drugs is listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Assessment of healthy lifestyles

We constructed a healthy lifestyle score including smoking status, diet, physical activity, sedentary behavior, length of sleep, psychological stress, and social contact status according to previous UK Biobank study and the latest proposed framework of lifestyle medicine [14], which coincided with recommendations of the World Health Organization [15]. All lifestyle factors were obtained through structured baseline touchscreen questionnaires.

In this study, a healthy lifestyle was characterized by never smoking, ideal diet, adequate physical activity, limited sedentary behavior, appropriate sleep, low psychological stress, and active social contact. The ideal diet involves sufficient intake of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, fish, dairy, and vegetable oils, and minimal consumption of refined grains, processed meats, and sugar-sweetened beverages [20]. Physical activity was adequate if participants engaged in at least 150 min per week of moderate or 75 min of vigorous activity. Sedentary behavior was considered ideal if less than or equal to 6 h daily [21]. Healthy sleep duration was set at 7−9 h per night [22]. Psychological stress, measured by the recent depressive symptom (RDS-4) scale, was deemed low with a total score less than 6. Social contact was assessed through household size, the frequency of friend or family visits, and participation in group activities; a score below 2 indicated active social contact [23]. Additional details about each healthy lifestyle factor are shown in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table S2.

Considering that the effect of alcohol consumption on health is highly controversial, this study did not consider alcohol consumption in the lifestyle scoring system. Instead it was included as a covariate. For each lifestyle factor, one point for a healthy level and 0 points for an unhealthy level were assigned. The lifestyle score was the sum of the points and ranged between 0 and 7, with higher scores indicating healthier lifestyles. In addition, we performed Cox proportional hazards modeling for each lifestyle factor with respect to incident chronic disease risk, and assigned weights to each factor based on the corresponding regression coefficients. A weighted lifestyle score was then calculated, and lifestyle categories (very unhealthy, unhealthy, healthy, and very healthy) were defined according to the quartiles of this weighted score.

Covariates

Baseline socio-demographic characteristics as well as other clinical risk factors in the UK Biobank were collected through a touchscreen questionnaire. Socio-demographic characteristics included age (divided into four groups based on quartiles); sex (females and males); self-reported race (white and others); educational attainment (college or university degree, national examination at age 16, national examination at age 17–18, and others) and employment status (paid employment, non-paid employment, pension, and unknown). A Townsend deprivation index (TDI) was derived using participants' postal codes and information from the national census output area. Participants self-reported their current alcohol consumption frequency and volume. A healthy drinking level was defined based on UK dietary guidelines as a daily intake of up to one drink for women and up to two drinks for men, excluding non-drinkers. In the UK, one standard drink is equivalent to 8 g of ethanol [15] (Supplementary Table S3).

Ascertainment of outcomes

The primary outcomes for this study were the incidence of 5 major NCDs (diabetes, CVD, CKD, COPD, and cancer), and the incidence of major NCDs was the incidence of any of the 5 NCDs, and the incidence of all-cause mortality. Since not all cancers in the general population are lifestyle-related [24], we analyzed the major cancer typesthat were previously reported to be related to lifestyles. Each resident in England, Scotland, and Wales is assigned a unique National Health Service (NHS) identification number, which was used to connect participants with their electronic health records. Patient follow-up began at enrollment and continued until the first occurrence of a major NCD, death, or the study's end date on 30 November 2022, whichever happened earliest. Diagnoses of NCDs were coded using the WHO International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision (ICD-10, Supplementary Table S4). Mortality from all causes was determined through linkage with death registries, including the NHS Information Centre for England and Wales and the Central Register for Scotland.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of participants were categorized based on lifestyle score quartiles. Continuous variables were summarized as means and standard deviations, while categorical variables were described by their counts and proportions within each quartile. Differences across quartiles were assessed using Analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. In addition, baseline characteristics between included and excluded participants were compared to assess potential selection bias.

We used Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for the risk of major NCDs and all-cause mortality among participants with MS, based on lifestyle score categories. Lifestyle scores were constructed in two ways: (1) as an unweighted sum of healthy lifestyle factors, and (2) as a weighted score in which each factor was weighted by its corresponding regression coefficient. Both lifestyle scores were categorized into quartiles according to their distributions in the study population. For the unweighted score, the 0−3 points group was used as the reference, while for the weighted score, the very unhealthy group served as the reference. Schoenfeld residual test was performed to examine proportional hazard (PH) assumption. Multivariable adjustments were made to account for age, sex, self-reported race, TDI, alcohol consumption, education and employment status. Restricted cubic splines were fitted with three knots by treating the lifestyle scores as a continuous variable via the ‘rms’ package.

To test the robustness and potential variations in different subgroups, we repeated all analyses stratified by subtype of cancers, MS components (central obesity, blood pressure, glucose, HDL cholesterol, and triglycerides), sex (males and females), self-reported race (white and non-white), age groups (<60, and ≥60), and socioeconomic status (TDI <−2.18 and TDI ≥−2.18 with −2.18 as the median).

We also conducted sensitivity analyses. First, we constructed a lifestyle score by summing each traditional lifestyle score (including smoking status, diet, physical activity, and sedentary behavior). Second, we excluded subjects who died throughout the study to assess the possible effects of death on the results. At last, we used multiple imputation to impute all missing independent variables to test the influence of missing variables.

To quantify the influence of lifestyle risk factors on the incidence of major NCDs and all-cause mortality, we determined the population attributable risk (PAR) percentage using a Cox proportional hazards model [25,26]. Imagining a scenario where no participants had specific risk factors, we discerned the risk discrepancy between this hypothetical setting and actual observations. Our assessment encompassed PAR for individual factors, such as smoking, diet, physical activity, sedentary behavior, sleep patterns, psychological stress, social contact status, and their combinations. In calculating the PAR for cumulative combinations of lifestyle factors, we used the frequency with which the lifestyle factors were investigated in the relevant literature (those with higher frequency were cumulated first), and combined them in the order of smoking + diet + physical activity + sedentary behavior + sleep patterns + psychological stress + social contact status. For precision in our PAR calculations, we employed bootstrap resampling, analyzing both singular and combined risk contributors. The analysis leveraged R's survival and boot packages.

The association between healthy lifestyles and risk of major NCDs and all-cause mortality in MS patients

During a median follow-up of 11.0 years, 19,435 cases of major NCDs and 3,578 cases of all-cause mortality were identified. Adherence to healthy lifestyle factors was significantly associated with reduced risks of major NCDs and all-cause mortality (Fig. 1, and Supplementary Table S6). Among the lifestyle factors, the top three most significant protective factors for the incidence of major NCDs were no smoking (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.80−0.85), less stress (HR = 0.83, 95% CI: 0.78−0.88), and adequate sleep (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.87−0.92). When the weighted lifestyle score was used (Fig. 2B), similar associations were observed. Compared with the very unhealthy group, participants classified as unhealthy, healthy, and very healthy groups had 11% (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.85−0.92), 21% (HR = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.76−0.83), and 27% (HR = 0.73, 95% CI: 0.70−0.76) lower risks of major NCDs, respectively. For all-cause mortality, the corresponding risk reductions were 11% (HR = 0.89, 95% CI: 0.81−0.97), 31% (HR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.63−0.75), and 40% (HR = 0.60, 95% CI: 0.54−0.66) for the unhealthy, healthy, and very healthy groups, respectively.

Regarding specific diseases, both scoring approaches showed similar patterns. The weighted lifestyle analysis showed the lowest HR for COPD (HR = 0.17, 95% CI: 0.14–0.20) in the very healthy group. Significant risk reductions were also observed for diabetes (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.59–0.69), CKD (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.57–0.73) and CVD (HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.64–0.73). In contrast, associations for cancer were less pronounced, with significant reductions only observed in the healthy and very healthy group. To explore these findings further, we conducted more detailed analyses for cancer subtypes and cause-specific mortality, presented in Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S7, respectively. Only bladder cancer, lung cancer, esophageal cancer, cancer mortality, and COPD mortality showed statistically significant associations with lifestyle.

Subgroup analysis showed that the relationship between lifestyle factors and the risk of NCDs and all-cause mortality were consistent across age (Supplementary Table S8), sex (Supplementary Table S9), ethnicity (Supplementary Table S10), and socioeconomic status subgroups (Supplementary Table S11). Sensitivity analysis shows that the results were not substantially changed after using traditional lifestyle scores (Supplementary Table S12), using data from participants who excluded deaths (Supplementary Table S13), or using multiple interpolation to fill in missing values for the independent variables (Supplementary Table S14). The association between healthy lifestyle scores and the risk of major NCDs, diabetes, CVD, COPD, and CKD, but not cancer displayed distinct dose-response relationships among the MS population (Supplementary Fig. S3).

We further explored the associations between lifestyle and the risk of NCDs across different MS component risk factors (Supplementary Figure S4). Except for cancer, a healthier lifestyle demonstrated protective effects against major NCDs, diabetes, CVD, COPD, CKD, and all-cause mortality.

In multivariable-adjusted analyses assessing PAR of each unhealthy lifestyle factors on various NCDs, distinct patterns emerged (Fig. 3, and Supplementary Tables S15-S21). Smoking contributed the largest PAR for major NCDs (9.98%), followed by unhealthy diet (5.22%), and short or long sleep duration (3.06%). For all-cause mortality, smoking (18.25%) and physical inactivity (5.12%) were the leading contributors. For COPD, the PAR was highest at 74.24%. Dietary factors (PAR = 12.80%) and smoking (PAR = 6.32%) were most influential for CKD. For CVD and diabetes, smoking and unhealthy diet were also the two leading contributors, with PARs of 10.34% and 7.78% for CVD, and 8.04% and 7.63% for diabetes, respectively.

Discussion

In this cohort study of individuals with MS from the UK Biobank, adherence to an integrated healthy lifestyle pattern was significantly associated with reduced risks of major NCDs and all-cause mortality. We found that approximately 21% of major NCD events and 27% of all-cause mortality could potentially be prevented if participants optimally adhered to seven lifestyle factors, even among individuals with MS at baseline.

Comparison with other studies

Adherence to healthy lifestyle is certainly an effective strategy to lower risk of NCDs in MS population. Our findings are generally consistent with those of previous studies in the general population [[27], [28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. For example, the Framingham Heart Study revealed that achieving high levels of physical activity, having a normal weight, and never smoking were associated with a lower risk of cardiovascular disease, and a greater number of years lived free of cardiovascular disease [29]. In both the Diabetes Prevention Program trial and the Finnish Diabetes Prevention Study, which were conducted among individuals at high risk of diabetes, lifestyle interventions significantly improved CVD risk profiles and reduced diabetes incidence [[33], [34], [35]]. Another study, which also used UKB data, reported that in the general population, participants with the highest lifestyle score had a 70% lower risk of developing CKD than those with lifestyle scores of less than zero [36]. In summary, our study provides new evidence that a higher healthy lifestyle score is associated with a lower risk of major NCDs such as diabetes, CVD, COPD, and CKD in a MS population.

Additionally, the association between a healthy lifestyle and mortality has been studied in people with MS. Niu et al., focusing specifically on individuals with MS, confirmed that a healthy lifestyle can significantly lower the risk of all-cause mortality in this population [12]. Our results showed that a healthy lifestyle is associated with a lower risk of mortality in a MS population, similar to previous studies.

We also estimated the PARs to quantify the proportion of major NCDs and mortality potentially preventable by modifying unhealthy lifestyle factors. We found that the PAR for major NCDs increased with the number of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors eliminated, reaching the highest value for COPD (74%), substantially higher than for other NCDs. This high proportion likely reflects the established roles of smoking [37], unhealthy diet [38], and sedentary behavior [39] in COPD pathogenesis. These findings underscore the substantial impact that comprehensive lifestyle modification could have on disease prevention in individuals with MS.

Potential mechanisms

The observed associations between healthy lifestyles and reduced risk of NCDs and mortality in MS patients may reflect systemic physiological benefits induced by lifestyle modifications. For individuals with MS, these lifestyle modulations may confer amplified protective effects by rectifying inherent metabolic imbalances [40,41]. Regular physical activity and healthy dietary patterns can improve glucose metabolism, lipid profiles, and reduce systemic inflammation, thereby decreasing the risk of diabetes and CVD [42,43]. Abstinence from smoking reduces chronic inflammation and tissue damage, consequently lowering susceptibility to COPD and certain cancers [24]. Additionally, optimal nutritional intake could mitigate renal oxidative stress, potentially slowing CKD progression [36]. Nevertheless, the interactions and potential synergistic effects among multiple lifestyle factors in reducing NCD risk remain unclear and warrant further investigation.

Public health impact

MS patients, given their heightened vulnerabilities, stand as a sentinel population for NCDs for whom interventions can yield significant benefits. Our study is among the first to systematically examine the associations between an integrated lifestyle score-including both traditional and emerging lifestyle factors-and the incidence of major NCDs and all-cause mortality in a large MS cohort. The findings demonstrate that comprehensive lifestyle modifications are associated with substantially lower risks of NCDs and mortality, even in individuals already diagnosed with MS. These results support the integration of multifaceted lifestyle interventions into clinical practice and public health strategies, potentially reducing both disease burden and related healthcare costs in this vulnerable population.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths. First, it was based on a large, well-characterized prospective cohort of 49,891 individuals with MS from the UK Biobank, ensuring high statistical power and generalizability. Second, the comprehensive lifestyle score used in this study integrates both traditional (smoking, diet, physical activity, sedentary behavior) and emerging lifestyle factors (sleep duration, psychological stress, social contact), providing a more holistic assessment of lifestyle impact on major NCDs risk than previous studies. Third, the study applies both unweighted and weighted lifestyle scores, as well as estimation of PARs, enabling understanding of the potential population-level benefits of healthy lifestyle adherence.

This study also has several limitations. First, lifestyle factors were assessed at baseline only, without considering changes over time, which might lead to potential misclassification bias and underestimation of associations. Second, residual confounding from unmeasured or incompletely measured variables (such as genetic factors, and medication use) may persist despite extensive covariate adjustments. Third, the observational nature of our study precludes establishing causality. Lastly, the UK Biobank cohort primarily consists of individuals of European ancestry and may not be fully representative of diverse populations, potentially limiting the generalizability of our results to other ethnic or demographic groups.

Conclusions

Our study based on a large cohort indicates that adherence to a healthy lifestyle pattern (integrating smoking status, diet, physical activity, sedentary behavior, sleep pattern, psychological stress, and social contact status) is associated with a substantially lower risk of incident major NCDs and death in MS participants. These findings underscore the necessity for comprehensive lifestyle interventions as a key strategy in managing and preventing NCDs in this high-risk population diagnosed with MS.
