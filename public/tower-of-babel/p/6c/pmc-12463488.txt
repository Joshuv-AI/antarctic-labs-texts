A Case of Mild OSA Exacerbated by Alcohol Use
Authors: Sahan Chandrasekara, Eli Dabscheck, Mary Spiteri, Renel Cuesta, Teanau Roebuck, Matthew T Naughton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is highly prevalent and exacerbated by alcohol consumption. With an increasing uptake of unsupervised home‐based sleep studies for diagnosis, alcohol use can significantly confound results and impact clinical management. We present the case of a 65 year‐old male referred for OSA evaluation. An initial home sleep study, performed in the setting of significant alcohol intake, demonstrated moderate OSA (AHI 22.1) with predominantly supine sleep (99% of total sleep time). A subsequent alcohol‐free in‐lab study revealed only mild OSA (7.5) with predominantly non‐supine sleep (41.9% of total sleep time supine). This marked change reflects the effect of alcohol on OSA severity and sleep architecture. Our case emphasises the importance of documenting alcohol use prior to and during sleep studies and the incorporation of alcohol reduction in OSA management for selected patients.Keywords: alcohol, CPAP, OSA, sleep, sleep apnoea

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) is a prevalent condition with significant morbidity. Alcohol consumption, which is prevalent amongst Australian residents, is a well‐established modifiable factor known to increase OSA severity [1, 2, 3]. With the increasing use of home‐based sleep studies for OSA diagnosis, alcohol use may significantly confound findings, complicating the clinical application of study results.

Case Report

A 65 year‐old male with a history of persistent atrial fibrillation was referred by our cardiology department to our sleep outpatient service for evaluation of possible obstructive sleep apnoea. A Level 2 home‐based sleep study was organised prior to review based upon an elevated STOP‐BANG score.

With regard to his sleep history, he reported 8–8.5 h in bed per night, with a sleep latency of up to 1 h and consolidated sleep overnight. He reported that he usually slept non‐supine. He was known to snore but had never had any witnessed apnoeas. He reported occasional unrefreshing sleep but no daytime somnolence and no regular daytime naps. An Epworth Sleepiness Scale score was 4.

He worked full time in digital printing. Alcohol intake was significant—up to 5 standard drinks per day, with 3–4 alcohol‐free days per week.

On examination, his weight was 88 kg and height was 182 cm (BMI 26.6). His Mallampati score was 2 and his nasal patency was normal. Pulse rate was regular, highly suggestive of sinus rhythm. Heart sounds were dual with no murmurs. There were no signs of liver failure.

Review of his unattended home (Level 2) sleep study revealed a technically adequate study, with 6 h 30 min of captured sleep (sleep efficiency 79.3%). Findings were consistent with moderate obstructive sleep apnoea, with an AHI of 22.1 events per hour (supine AHI—21.9, non‐supine AHI—45) and 1.6% sleep time spent with SpO2 < 90%. However, he had consumed five standard drinks on the day of the study. Notably, his mean heart rate was 64 sinus bpm and 99% of sleep was supine. His weight was 87 kg at the time.

Three months later, an in‐laboratory attended (Level 1) sleep study was performed, with no alcohol use on the day of the study. Five hours 19.5 min of sleep was captured (sleep efficiency 73.2%). This study revealed only mild obstructive sleep apnoea, with an AHI of 7.5 (supine AHI—11.6, non‐supine AHI—4.5) and zero time spent with SpO2 < 90%. Only 42% of sleep was supine. The mean heart rate was also lower at 59 bpm. Weight was measured at 89 kg. Sleep study results are summarised in Table 1 and Figure 1.

Based on the above results, a management plan was formulated. This stipulated side sleeping where able, and minimisation of alcohol intake. Use of continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) or a mandibular advancement splint (MAS) was not felt to be necessary at this stage.

Discussion

We present a case of dramatic and reversible increase in OSA severity driven by alcohol consumption. It has previously been demonstrated that even moderate alcohol intake can increase AHI and heart rate in patients with OSA [2].

Home based sleep studies have been widely adopted into clinical practice and have been endorsed for use in patients with a high pre‐test probability of OSA [4]. During a home‐based study, there is no direct supervision of substance use including alcohol.

In our patient, there was a significant association between AHI and alcohol use. This was clearly demonstrated by a significantly greater AHI (22.1 vs. 7.5), more hypoxemia (1.6% vs. 0.0% total sleep time (TST) with SpO2 < 90%), and more tachycardia (64 vs. 59 bpm) with alcohol despite greater weight (89 vs. 87 kg), although we note he spent more time supine with alcohol than without (99% to 42% TST). Alcohol increases airway collapsibility and potentiates respiratory events through several mechanisms, including central nervous system depression, reduction of upper airway dilator tone, and lowering of the arousal threshold [1].

Alcohol reduction has been endorsed in national guidelines as an important component in OSA management [5], however, no data is available as to the application of this approach in real‐world clinical settings. In our patient, re‐evaluation of OSA severity in the absence of alcohol allowed a management plan focusing on lifestyle modification (alcohol minimisation and positional therapy) rather than device‐based therapy.

As this is a single case report, generalisability may be limited. However, given the established body of literature on alcohol and sleep, we believe this case serves as a valuable example of the significance of alcohol in OSA severity.

In the context of a profound burden of both alcohol use and OSA in the community, our case highlights the importance of documenting alcohol use prior to and during home sleep studies. Furthermore, it reinforces the critical role of alcohol reduction in the OSA management in selected patients.
