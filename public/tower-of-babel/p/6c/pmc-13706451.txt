Early Detection of Mortality Risk in Preterm Neonates Using a Random Forest Classifier and Tree-Based Pipeline Optimization Tool Automated Machine Learning
Authors: Vahid Monfared, Ladan Haghighi, Afkham Hashemi, Neda Farzizadeh, Zahra Najmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To develop and validate a machine learning–based model for early prediction of in-hospital mortality among preterm neonates using routinely available perinatal and neonatal clinical variables.Methods:This retrospective single-center cohort study included 1612 live-born preterm neonates delivered between 26 and 37 weeks of gestation. Sixteen clinical maternal, perinatal, and neonatal variables were collected, including APGAR scores (appearance, pulse, grimace, activity, respiration) , need for cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), gestational age, birth weight, fetal presentation, in vitro fertilization, and obstetric history. Data preprocessing included anomaly handling, multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE), one-hot encoding/standardization, and class imbalance management using adaptive synthetic sampling and cost-sensitive learning applied to the training dataset. Model selection compared multiple classifiers, with the final model being a Random Forest identified through the Tree-Based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT) automated machine learning (AutoML) pipeline. Model performance on a held-out test set was assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-score, likelihood ratios, and confusion matrix analysis. Feature importance was assessed using Chi-square/Cramér’s V, logistic regression coefficients, and model-based importance from Random Forest, gradient-boosted trees, and XGBoost. SHapley Additive exPlanations were used to determine the direction and magnitude of feature effects. Overfitting was mitigated through cost-sensitive learning, and consistency between training and test performance indicated minimal overfitting.Results:The model achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.99, accuracy of 0.98, sensitivity of 0.98, specificity of 0.99, PPV of 0.99, NPV of 0.96, and F1-score of 0.988. The most influential predictors of in-hospital mortality were need for CPR, 1-minute APGAR score, gestational age < 28 weeks, 5-minute APGAR score, birth weight < 1 kg, and fetal anomalies.Conclusion:A Random Forest–based AutoML model can accurately predict in-hospital mortality among preterm neonates using routinely available clinical variables. This data-driven tool may support earlier risk stratification, informed clinical decision-making, and improved allocation of neonatal intensive care resources in maternal-fetal and perinatal care settings.

Introduction

Each year, approximately 15 million neonates are born preterm, defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation.1 In 2023, > 380,000 preterm births were reported,2 and in 2021, preterm low-birth-weight neonates accounted for 14.8% of neonatal deaths in the United States (US) alone.2 Preterm birth is associated with numerous risk factors, including prior preterm delivery, shortened cervical length, extremes of maternal age and pre-pregnancy body mass index, low socioeconomic status, tobacco use, genetic polymorphisms, and infection.3

Preterm neonates are particularly vulnerable to numerous short- and long-term morbidities, including respiratory distress syndrome, infection, seizures, intraventricular hemorrhage, hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy, necrotizing enterocolitis, feeding difficulties, periventricular leukomalacia, cerebral palsy, and visual and auditory impairments.4 Mortality rates vary substantially by gestational age, ranging from 10% to 70%.5,6 Globally, an estimated 52 million children < 5 years of age are projected to die between 2019 and 2030, with neonates accounting for half of these deaths.7 Beyond morbidity and mortality, prematurity imposes a substantial economic burden. In the US, the estimated societal cost of prematurity was $26.3 billion annually as of 2005.2 The average annual cost per neonate delivered at 24 weeks of gestation was $603,778—nearly 100-fold higher than that in full-term neonates.8

Early identification of neonates at high risk of mortality is critical for improving survival and reducing morbidity by enabling timely intervention.7 Counseling regarding prognosis, resuscitation techniques, and anticipated neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) course should ideally begin before delivery when a very preterm birth is anticipated.9,10 Clinical decision-making in the NICU is complex, and artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) approaches offer the potential to enhance neonatal care by providing early risk stratification.7 ML algorithms can analyze large, complex datasets to identify patterns and risk factors, supporting targeted and preventive clinical interventions.111213

Several studies have explored ML-based mortality prediction in preterm neonates. Shu et al.2 developed ML models for very-low-birth-weight neonates, achieving an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC) of 0.74 for mortality prediction. Li et al.14 reported improved survival prediction for extremely preterm neonates (23–29 weeks) with an AUC-ROC of 0.91, although the study lacked external validation and raised concerns regarding algorithmic bias. Lee et al.15 developed a Random Forest model that outperformed logistic regression and the Clinical Risk Index for Babies-II (CRIB-II), achieving a per-neonate AUC-ROC of 0.93; however, its limitations included an arbitrary 6-h prediction window and ethical concerns regarding the inclusion of race as a predictor. Despite promising results, the broader applicability of AI-based models remains constrained by single-center designs, retrospective methodologies, limited population diversity, low accuracy, and insufficient external validation.

The present study seeks to address these gaps by developing and evaluating an AI-driven predictive model for early, accurate assessment of mortality risk in preterm neonates. By leveraging comprehensive clinical data and advanced ML techniques, this work aims to provide a reliable tool to support timely prognosis and inform delivery room and NICU decision-making (see Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study included 1612 live-born preterm neonates (26–37 weeks’ gestation) delivered at Shahid Akbar Abadi Hospital, Tehran, Iran. Data were extracted from hospital records and included fetal–neonatal, maternal, and pregnancy characteristics; antenatal medications; delivery details; neonatal care; postnatal outcomes; and hospital stay duration. All predictor variables were ascertained at or immediately after birth (within the first 5 min of life), before the outcome of survival status at hospital discharge, which occurred days to weeks later. The need for cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and APGAR (appearance, pulse, grimace, activity, respiration) scores are standard components of neonatal illness-severity indices (CRIB-II, score for neonatal acute physiology II (SNAP-II), score for neonatal acute physiology with perinatal extension II (SNAPPE-II)) and do not represent temporal data leakage (see Fig. 1).

Eligible infants were live-born preterm neonates (< 37 weeks) with at least one documented prenatal examination before 20 weeks’ gestation to ensure accurate dating. Exclusion criteria included neonates born to diabetic or hypertensive mothers, those with lethal congenital anomalies, absence of prenatal steroid exposure, or receipt of antenatal magnesium sulfate. Gestational age was determined by last menstrual period or, when unavailable or inconsistent, first-trimester ultrasonography. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines (Table 1).

Predictor variables were selected based on established clinical relevance to neonatal mortality and consistent availability in hospital records. Although factors such as chorioamnionitis, premature rupture of membranes, blood lactate, and arterial pH are clinically relevant, they were not routinely documented and were therefore unavailable for inclusion.

ML models

Multiple supervised ML algorithms were evaluated to predict neonatal survival at hospital discharge, including logistic regression, Random Forest, gradient-boosted trees, and XGBoost. Logistic regression was used as a baseline statistical classifier for binary outcomes, modeling the log-odds of mortality as a function of predictor variables using maximum likelihood estimation. The logistic regression model is:

log(p1−p)=β0+β1X1+β2X2+…+βnXn (1)

In which p is the probability of neonatal death or survival. The parameter p1−p is the odds ratio. β0, β1, … βn are the regression coefficients that represent the relationship between the independent variables and the log-odds of the dependent variable. The coefficients are estimated using maximum likelihood estimation. Once the model is fitted, the coefficients can be used to estimate the probability that a given observation falls into the “1:dead” category by transforming the log-odds back into a probability with the logistic function:

p=11+e−(β0+β1X1+β2X2+…+βnXn) (2)

This probability can then be used to classify observations based on a chosen cutoff value, typically 0.5. If p is greater than the cutoff, the observation is predicted to be in the “1” category, otherwise, it is predicted to be in the “0” category.

Following model comparison, the Random Forest Classifier was selected as the best-performing model. The Tree-Based Pipeline Optimization Tool (TPOT), an automated ML (AutoML) framework, was employed solely to identify the optimal pipeline and hyperparameters. All data preprocessing, model training, and evaluation were subsequently performed independently in Python. Fig. 1 illustrates the research workflow and methodological pipeline.

TPOT was configured with the ‘TPOT sparse’ setting and optimized for F1-score over multiple generations, with early stopping. The hyperparameter search space included bootstrap sampling, splitting criterion (Gini/entropy), maximum features, minimum samples per leaf and split, and number of estimators. The optimal pipeline selected by TPOT was a Random Forest classifier with the following configuration:

RandomForestClassifier(bootstrap = False, criterion = entropy, max_features = 0.5, min_samples_leaf = 1, min_samples_split = 16, n_estimators = 100).

Data preprocessing

All preprocessing was performed before model training. Key steps included:

1. Data cleaning: detection and correction of outliers, imputation of missing values, removal of duplicate records, and verification of dataset integrity.

2. Feature encoding: all predictors were converted into binary indicators (0/1) to accommodate both categorical and scalar variables. All 16 candidate predictors were retained. Random Forest classifiers perform embedded feature selection through impurity-based splitting, making them robust to weak predictors without explicit prior feature elimination. Feature relevance was independently confirmed using Chi-squared tests, Cramér’s V, logistic regression coefficients, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, all of which yielded a consistent ranking of influential predictors. Twelve features were inherently categorical. Gestational age was dichotomized at < 28 weeks (extreme prematurity, World Health Organization (WHO) classification). Birth weight was dichotomized at < 1000 g (WHO classification of extremely low birth weight), and APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min were dichotomized at ≤ 3, the clinically established threshold for neonatal depression.

3. Class imbalance handling: the target label (“STATUS_dead_alive”) was imbalanced (alive ≈ 80%; dead ≈ 20%). Adaptive synthetic sampling (ADASYN) was applied to the training data only, combined with cost-sensitive learning to mitigate bias toward the majority class.

4. Anomaly detection: Applied before binarization to remove extreme or erroneous scalar values without distorting categorical variables.

5. Missing-value imputation: missing values were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations (MICE), applied exclusively to predictor variables to preserve independence from the outcome. Missing data occurred in gestational age (< 28 weeks; 80 cases, 5.0%), birth weight (< 1000 g; 84 cases, 5.2%), and maternal education (149 cases, 9.3%). All remaining variables were complete, and no values were missing after imputation.

To maintain real-world representativeness, class-balancing techniques were not applied to the test dataset. TPOT employed five-fold stratified cross-validation on the ADASYN-augmented training data to mitigate overfitting to synthetic samples, as evidenced by consistent performance on both training and test sets.

Model training and evaluation

The dataset was randomly split into 80% training and 20% testing sets. TPOT used five-fold stratified cross-validation during pipeline optimization to identify optimal hyperparameters and further limit overfitting beyond held-out testing. Penalty functions were incorporated to improve metrics and avoid overfitting. Multiple models were trained on both the original and ADASYN-balanced training datasets. Evaluation was performed on the untouched test set using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC, with a classification threshold of 0.5. Feature contributions were assessed using impurity-based importance from the Random Forest model and coefficients from a logistic regression model fitted to the binarized dataset.

All analyses were performed in Python (v3.11) using scikit-learn, TPOT, and supporting libraries.

Because the study was a retrospective analysis of fully de-identified records, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the ethics committee.

Results

This study reports findings from ML, AI, and statistical analyses conducted in Python to identify predictors of in-hospital mortality among preterm neonates. The outcome variable was consistently coded as 1 = dead (positive class) and 0 = alive across all analyses. Study design and reporting adhered to the TRIPOD guidelines (Table 1).

Feature association and correlation analysis

Relationships among predictors and their associations with neonatal survival status (“STATUS_dead_alive”) were examined using a correlation heatmap (Fig. 2). The heatmaps offer a graphical representation of inter-feature relationships and feature–outcome associations, enabling assessment of relative importance without class balancing. Missing values were addressed before analysis to ensure valid correlation estimates.

As shown in Fig. 2, the features most strongly associated with neonatal mortality were the need for CPR, 1-minute APGAR score, gestational age < 28 weeks, birth weight < 1 kg, and 5-minute APGAR score. In contrast, primigravidity, fetal sex, extreme maternal age (< 20 or ≥ 40 years), number of live children, and delivery method demonstrated weaker associations with mortality status. The correlation heatmap (Fig. 2) demonstrated no substantial multicollinearity among predictors, with inter-feature chi-square values markedly lower than feature-to-outcome associations.

Logistic regression-based feature importance

Logistic regression was used to assess feature importance by estimating coefficients for each predictor, indicating both the direction (positive or negative) and magnitude of the association with the binary outcome variable. A positive coefficient indicates an increased probability of the positive class, while a negative coefficient indicates the opposite. Larger absolute coefficient values signify a stronger influence on the outcome. Analyzing these coefficients helps identify which features are most influential in predicting the binary outcome, aiding feature selection and understanding their contribution to the model’s predictions (Supplementary Fig. 1, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137). The outcome variable was consistently coded as 1 = dead and 0 = alive.

The model intercept was highly significant, indicating baseline differences in the odds of the outcome when predictors were absent. Need for CPR, APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min, and gestational age < 28 weeks showed statistically significantly higher adjusted odds of neonatal death. In contrast, variables such as singleton pregnancy, extreme maternal age, and fetal sex were not statistically significant (P > 0.05), suggesting limited independent predictive value in this model. Lack of statistical significance does not preclude an effect but may reflect limited power or a small effect size. Delivery method and repeat cesarean section demonstrated inverse coefficient directions but did not reach statistical significance.

Classification performance and ROC curve analysis

For model evaluation, the outcome variable was coded as 1 = dead (positive class) and 0 = alive. On the independent test dataset, the confusion matrix showed 215 true positives (TP), 1 false positive (FP), 4 false negatives (FN), and 102 true negatives. While overall classification was strong, the presence of four FN classes—instances where the AI/ML model incorrectly predicted the survival for neonates who died or were at high risk—represents the most clinically important error. These results underscore the importance of continued model optimization with a specific focus on reducing FN rates (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137).

To further assess discriminatory performance, a ROC curve was generated (Fig. 6). The ROC curve illustrates the balance between the true positive rate (TPR) and the false positive rate (FPR). Essentially, it involves calculating and plotting TPR and FPR for various thresholds on a single chart. Classifiers with curves that approach the upper-left corner of the plot demonstrate superior performance, reflecting high sensitivity and low FP rates. In this study, the ROC curve indicates a strong overall discriminative ability of the model.

AUC-ROC for the test and train datasets. AUC-ROC: Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve.

The area under the red curve is nearly equal to 1, indicating that the model exhibits good accuracy (Fig. 6). The near-identical AUC-ROC values for the training and test datasets (both 0.99) indicate minimal overfitting and strong generalizability. TPOT pipeline selection was based on internal five-fold cross-validation beyond the held-out test set. The AUC-ROC reflects discrimination across all thresholds and is compatible with a small number of misclassifications at any single operating point.

At the classification threshold of 0.5, the model produced 4 FNs and 1 FP across 322 test samples. FN cases—where survival was predicted but death occurred—represent the most clinically consequential error and were specifically targeted through cost-sensitive learning and threshold optimization. Despite this, overall performance remained strong, with an accuracy of 0.98, a sensitivity of 0.98, a specificity of 0.99, a precision (PPV) of 0.99, a negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.96, and an F1-score of 0.988, indicating balanced performance under class imbalance (Table 3 and 4). As summarized in Table 4, the proposed model reduced both false negatives (4 vs. 17) and false positives (1 vs. 3) compared with the prior study,18 indicating improved overall discriminative performance and, in particular, a substantial reduction in missed diagnoses.

The model’s high sensitivity demonstrates its ability to correctly identify nearly all high-risk neonates, while its high specificity minimizes false alarms. Likelihood ratios further support excellent discriminative capacity. Collectively, these metrics confirm that the model reliably distinguishes between survival and mortality outcomes and outperforms previously published approaches.16

To further validate robustness, feature importance was compared across multiple classifiers, including logistic regression, gradient-boosted trees, and XGBoost (Supplementary Fig. 3 and 4, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137). All models consistently identified the need for CPR as the most influential predictor, with in vitro fertilization (IVF) conception showing the least impact. The TPOT-optimized Random Forest classifier (input matrix, bootstrap = False, criterion = entropy, max features = 0.5, min samples leaf = 1, min samples split = 16, n estimators = 100) outperformed all alternatives, justifying its selection as the final model (Supplementary Fig. 5, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137).

SHAP-based feature impact analysis

The SHAP method was used to quantify both the magnitude and direction of feature effects (Supplementary Fig. 6 and 7, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137). SHAP analysis identified the need for CPR as the most influential factor in predicting neonatal survival, followed by the 1-minute APGAR score, gestational age < 28 weeks, fetal anomalies, and the 5-minute APGAR score. These findings align with established clinical knowledge and highlight key targets for early intervention. (Supplementary Fig. 6, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137).

The SHAP level-wise analysis demonstrated that Need_for_CPR_yes has a strong positive impact on the probability of death, whereas Need_for_CPR_no exerts a protective effect (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 7, http://links.lww.com/MFM/A137). By providing transparent, clinically interpretable explanations, SHAP enhances trust in model predictions and supports integration into neonatal decision-making.

SHAP summary bar chart illustrating the direction and magnitude of important feature levels influencing the prediction of neonatal mortality. APGAR: Appearance, pulse, grimace, activity, respiration; CPR: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation: CS: Cesarean section: IVF: In vitro fertilization; SHAP: Shapley additive explanations.

Another key determinant is the 1-minute APGAR score. The level APGAR_Minute_1_yes (indicating a low APGAR score) showed a positive SHAP value, suggesting an increased predicted risk of mortality. Conversely, APGAR_Minute_1_no (normal APGAR score) exerted a negative SHAP effect, indicating a protective association with survival.

Gestational age also plays a critical role. Gestational_Age_28_Weeks_yes, representing extreme prematurity, was positively associated with predicted neonatal mortality, whereas Gestational_Age_28_Weeks_no (more advanced gestation) lowers this risk. Similarly, Fetal_Anomaly_yes was associated with a higher predicted probability of death, while Fetal_Anomaly_no was associated with a lower predicted probability of death, highlighting the impact of congenital conditions on outcomes.

Low birth weight (Birth_Weight_1_kg_yes) was associated with increased mortality risk, whereas normal or higher birth weight (Birth_Weight_1_kg_no) was associated with a protective effect. History of child death shows that previous loss (History_of_Child_Death_yes) was associated with a higher predicted mortality risk, whereas History_of_Child_Death_no was associated with a lower risk, suggesting that this variable contributes to the model’s mortality prediction.

Use of IVF (IVF_yes) was associated with slightly higher predicted mortality, whereas IVF_no had little influence. For repeat cesarean section, Repeat_CS_yes was associated with reduced mortality, potentially due to proactive obstetric management, whereas Repeat_CS_no had minimal effect.

Non-cephalic presentation (Presentation_Non_Cephalic_yes) was associated with a higher mortality risk, while Presentation_Non_Cephalic_no was protective. Furthermore, fetal gender appeared to be influential: Fetal_Gender_yes (likely representing male infants) was associated with a slightly higher risk, and Fetal_Gender_no (female) with a reduced risk—consistent with known biological vulnerabilities.

Some other features, such as Delivery_Method, Live_Children, First_Gravidity, Single_Pregnancy, and Maternal_Age_Under20_Above40_Years, made relatively minor contributions, but their directions align with clinical expectations. For example, Delivery_Method_no, Live_Children_no, and First_Gravidity_no show small protective effects, while Single_Pregnancy_yes has a nearly neutral contribution.

Overall, Need_for_CPR_yes, APGAR_Minute_1_yes, and Fetal_Anomaly_yes were the most critical positive contributors to mortality risk, while Need_for_CPR_no and APGAR_Minute_1_no were strongly associated with survival.

Discussion

Main findings

This study identified the need for CPR, APGAR scores at 1 and 5 min, gestational age < 28 weeks, and birth weight of approximately 1 kg as the most significant predictors of neonatal survival, based on chi-square analysis, Cramér’s V, and logistic regression. In contrast, maternal age extremes, fetal sex, gravidity, and delivery method demonstrated relatively weak independent effects. The optimized Random Forest model achieved exceptional performance metrics: 98% accuracy, 98% sensitivity, 99% specificity, 99% PPV, 96% NPV, 0.99 AUC-ROC, and an F1 score of 0.988, underscoring its robustness and clinical potential for early risk stratification.

Feature-level explainability confirmed Need_for_CPR_yes, APGAR_Minute_1_yes, and Fetal_Anomaly_yes as the strongest contributors to mortality risk, whereas Need_for_CPR_no and APGAR_Minute_1_no were strongly associated with survival. Although internal cross-validation and held-out test performance were consistent, external validation and bootstrapped CIs for AUC-ROC were not computed and should be pursued in future studies.

The observed protective association of cesarean delivery and repeat cesarean section likely reflects planned delivery in closely monitored pregnancies, enabling optimized timing and immediate availability of neonatal resuscitation resources. The slightly increased mortality risk among male neonates aligns with the well-documented male sex disadvantage in neonatal outcomes, attributed to slower pulmonary maturation, higher susceptibility to respiratory distress syndrome, and greater inflammatory vulnerability compared to female neonates.

Among secondary features, variables such as IVF conception, maternal age extremes, and gravidity showed relatively minor contributions to the model, suggesting they may function as weak predictors or interact with stronger predictors rather than as independent risk factors. Singleton pregnancy had a near-neutral contribution, likely because multiple gestation was uncommon in this cohort. Overall, predictive performance was driven primarily by immediate postnatal clinical indicators, rather than maternal or obstetric history.

The strong model performance is consistent with the pronounced univariate separation between outcome groups for top predictors; for example, CPR was required in 83.7% of deceased neonates versus 5.4% of survivors, providing a strong discriminative foundation for the classifier.

Comparison with prior work

Established NICU scoring systems, such as CRIB I and II, SNAP-II, and SNAPPE-II, integrate early physiologic and perinatal data to assess illness severity.17,18 However, their predictive accuracy declines in extremely low-birth-weight neonates. Despite using data from geographically distinct populations (Europe versus North America), the CRIB model demonstrated relatively strong performance (mean AUC-ROC ≈ 0.88), particularly for very low-birth-weight infants.19 Similarly, our model incorporates gestational age and birth weight as significant risk factors,20,21 while also reinforcing the continued prognostic value of APGAR scores, almost 50 years after their introduction.222324 We included 1-minute and 5-minute APGAR scores in our prediction model; they were among the most important features affecting neonatal survival. Compared to preterm very-low-birth-weight neonates who did not require CPR, those who experienced it in the delivery room and NICU had a higher chance of experiencing negative consequences.25,26 In our study, the need for CPR had the highest positive coefficient and was statistically significant, suggesting a strong association with the likelihood of the outcome.

Previous ML studies have reported more modest performance. Shu et al.2 developed ML models using 47 maternal and neonatal features collected within the first 2 weeks of life from 3341 neonates in a single institution. Their tree-based models, including XGBoost and Random Forest, achieved an AUC-ROC of 0.74 for mortality prediction but had limitations, including a single-center dataset, a retrospective design, overfitting, and the inclusion of outcomes occurring before the prediction window.2 Gates et al.27 constructed an XGBoost model to predict invasive mechanical ventilation or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) failure in 20,363 very preterm neonates across 27 NICUs, achieving strong test performance (AUC-ROC 0.90, F1-score 0.88, area under the precision-recall curve 0.94). Limitations included broad definitions of CPAP failure, the omission of critical perinatal variables, and variability in clinical practices, which limited generalizability. Additionally, SHAP explanations revealed predictive patterns but lacked causal confirmation.27 Lee et al.15 used a Random Forest model on 275 neonates from a single NICU to predict mortality, outperforming logistic regression and CRIB-II with an AUC-ROC of 0.93. Constraints included a limited sample size, single-center data, reliance on vital signs only, and ethical concerns regarding the use of race as a predictor. Hamilton et al.28 analyzed 1039 preterm deliveries < 32 weeks from multiple US hospitals using a hybrid logistic regression and CART model based on five pre-birth features. The model yielded an AUC-ROC of 0.853, 81% sensitivity, and 76% specificity; however, it was limited by a potential lack of generalizability, simplified variable groupings, and a small number of morbidity cases. In this study, our optimized Random Forest model outperformed prior work, achieving 98% accuracy, 98% sensitivity, 99% specificity, and an AUC-ROC of 0.99. Its key advantage is rapid, accurate risk classification using easily accessible clinical data, potentially supporting faster clinical decisions and improved neonatal survival.

Preterm births impose a substantial financial burden on NICUs, especially for neonates born between 23 and 30 weeks of pregnancy. Among a cohort of 12,660 neonates (2005–2017), the most significant incremental expenses were associated with intestinal obstruction ($94,738; 95% CI: $70,093–$117,294), ventriculoperitoneal shunt surgeries ($86,457; 95% CI: $60,774–$111,552), and chronic lung disease ($77,498; 95% CI: $74,937–$80,013).29 The average yearly cost for a preterm neonate born at 24 weeks was $603,778, which is 100 times more than the average yearly cost for a full-term neonate (2008–2019).8

AI-driven predictive models that accurately assess the mortality risk of preterm neonates in the NICU hold substantial therapeutic and economic importance. From a clinical perspective, these models can aid in the early identification of high-risk neonates, enabling swift interventions and the optimal allocation of critical care resources, such as specialized personnel, ventilators, and expensive medications. From an economic perspective, accurate forecasts help hospital administrators reduce NICU costs by preventing long-term complications. An additional strength of this work is the presentation of feature-level directional impact on the outcome using SHAP analysis.

The high model performance likely reflects the strong discriminative power of the top predictors, particularly the need for CPR and APGAR scores, which have well-established and robust associations with neonatal mortality. The Cramér’s V values exceeding 0.5 for these variables indicate very strong univariate associations with the outcome, providing a solid foundation for accurate multivariate prediction.

Several methodological factors may account for the superior performance of our model compared to prior studies. Compared to Shu et al.2 (AUC 0.74), our approach incorporated automated hyperparameter optimization via TPOT and addressed class imbalance by combining ADASYN oversampling with cost-sensitive learning, whereas their study acknowledged overfitting concerns. Lee et al.15 (AUC 0.93) used a substantially smaller cohort (n = 275) from a single NICU and relied solely on vital signs, whereas our model leveraged 16 perinatal features from 1612 neonates. Hamilton et al.28 (AUC 0.853) employed a hybrid model using only five pre-birth features, which inherently limits its discriminative capacity compared to our broader feature set, which includes immediate postnatal indicators. Li et al.14 (AUC 0.91) focused on extremely preterm neonates (23–29 weeks) and acknowledged algorithmic bias, whereas our model covers a wider gestational age range (26–37 weeks).

Strengths and limitations

The study’s cohort of 1612 preterm neonates (26–37 weeks) over 12 years enhances the statistical power and generalizability. Logistic regression, Chi-squared analysis, and Random Forest enabled precise predictions with an accuracy of 98%, a sensitivity of 98%, a specificity of 99%, an AUC-ROC of 0.99, and an F1-score of 0.988, outperforming previous studies. Key clinical variables, such as the need for CPR, APGAR scores, gestational age, and birth weight, were identified. Limitations include the retrospective design, which prevents causal inference, and prediction only at birth, excluding post-discharge risks. Future work should integrate predictive modeling into electronic medical systems to improve NICU care, reduce costs, and aid family decisions.

Furthermore, a formal sensitivity analysis comparing MICE-imputed results with the complete-case analysis was not performed, which represents a limitation of this study. However, the overall proportion of missing data was low (< 6% for all predictor variables), reducing the likelihood that imputation meaningfully affected the conclusions. Development of separate prenatal-only and early postnatal prediction models could further clarify the timing and clinical utility of prediction at different stages of care.

The binary transformation of continuous variables (gestational age, birth weight, APGAR scores) entails some loss of granularity. Retaining these as continuous predictors could be explored in future models, though the selected thresholds correspond to internationally recognized clinical cutoffs with established prognostic value.

Formal calibration assessment (calibration curve, Brier score) and clinical utility analysis (decision curve analysis, net clinical benefit) were not performed and should be addressed in future validation studies.

The exclusion of neonates born to mothers with diabetes, hypertension, or without antenatal corticosteroid exposure was intended to reduce confounding from maternal comorbidities with independent effects on neonatal outcomes. However, these populations represent a substantial proportion of preterm births in clinical practice, and their exclusion limits the model’s generalizability to real-world NICU settings. Future multicenter studies should include these high-risk subgroups to develop a more universally applicable prediction tool.

Stratified analysis of model performance by gestational age subgroups (extreme prematurity < 28 weeks, very preterm 28–32 weeks, and moderate-to-late preterm 32–37 weeks) was not performed. Given the relatively small number of extremely preterm neonates in our cohort, subgroup-specific performance estimates may be unstable and should be evaluated in larger, multicenter datasets.

Conclusion

This study integrated AI and statistical methods to develop a robust predictive model for assessing mortality risk in preterm neonates immediately after birth. Key methodological strengths included anomaly detection, cost-sensitive learning (weighted labels), class-imbalance correction using ADASYN, and one-hot encoding during preprocessing. AutoML (TPOT) was used to optimize model parameters to achieve maximum accuracy and high F1 scores. Outputs were carefully monitored and feedback incorporated to minimize errors and enhance performance.

Two major outcomes were achieved. First, a highly accurate predictive model was developed with a threshold controller to minimize FPs and FNs, critical for survival analysis. Second, feature importance analysis identified the most influential factors affecting neonatal outcomes. The top six predictors were need for CPR, 1-minute APGAR score, gestational age < 28 weeks, 5-minute APGAR score, birth weight < 1 kg, and fetal anomalies. Less influential factors included IVF, delivery method, maternal age, and fetal sex. Most features were positively correlated with survival, whereas delivery method and repeat cesarean section were inversely correlated. SHAP analysis visualized the positive or negative impact of feature levels on outcomes. Future work should focus on external validation and the development of deployable clinical tools, such as a mobile or web-based application, to facilitate real-world implementation.
