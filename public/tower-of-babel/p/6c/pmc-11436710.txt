Exploring copper metabolism-induced cell death in gastric cancer: a single-cell RNA sequencing study and prognostic model development
Authors: Yi Chen, Yunmei Liao, Lang Huang, Zhibin Luo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) is the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally. Despite advancements in treatment, the overall 5-year survival rate remains below 30%, particularly in advanced stages. Copper metabolism, vital for various cellular processes, has been linked to cancer progression, but its role in GC, especially at the single-cell level, is not well understood.ObjectiveThis study aims to investigate copper metabolism in GC by integrating single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data and developing a prognostic model based on copper metabolism-related gene (CMRG) expression. The study explores how copper metabolism affects the tumor microenvironment and identifies potential therapeutic targets.MethodsscRNA-seq data from gastric cancer and normal tissues were analyzed using the Seurat package. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) were used for dimensionality reduction and clustering. Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) was employed for T cell subpopulation analysis. A high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (HdWGCNA) identified key molecular features. LASSO regression and Random Survival Forest (RSF) techniques were used to create and validate a prognostic model. Survival analysis, immune microenvironment assessment, and drug sensitivity analysis were conducted.ResultsSixteen cell clusters and nine distinct cell types were identified, with T cells showing significant roles in cell communication. The NMF analysis of CD8 +T cells revealed five copper metabolism-related subtypes. The prognostic model based on nine CMRGs indicated significant survival differences between high- and low-risk groups. High-risk patients showed shorter survival times, increased immune cell infiltration, and altered immune responses. Drug sensitivity analysis suggested higher efficacy of certain drugs in high-CMRG patients.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Copper metabolism, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Copper metabolism-induced cell death, Immune microenvironment

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths globally and is the fifth most common cancer. In 2020, there were over 1 million confirmed cases of gastric cancer, with approximately 769,000 deaths [1, 2]. Gastric cancer generally has a poor prognosis, with an overall 5-year survival rate of less than 30%, even after surgical treatment [3, 4], particularly for patients with tumor, lymph node, and metastasis (TNM) stage T3 and T4 [5]. Based on different molecular subtypes identified in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), gastric cancer is now considered to have specific genomic abnormalities, which open avenues for targeted therapies [6]. In this context, further research is essential to understand the mechanisms underlying gastric cancer development, which is crucial for designing effective strategies for targeted preventive medicine. However, previous studies that used tissue transcriptomics based on large sample sizes to explore the molecular characteristics of GC and precancerous stages have masked the features of distinct cell populations. Additionally, studies that rely on purifying cell populations using predetermined markers have been limited to only a few specific cell types, making it difficult to fully distinguish between cell types and to detect rare cell populations or cells with specific states [7–11]. The heterogeneity caused by diverse tumor cell subgroups and a complex microenvironment has always been a significant challenge in diagnosis and treatment [12].

The relationship between cancer and copper metabolism remains an active area of research in human malignancies [13]. Abnormal copper metabolism, which refers to copper-mediated cell death or a specific type of copper toxicity due to disruptions in intracellular copper metabolism, represents a unique pathway of cell death. Copper is an essential trace element that coordinates various cellular processes, from energy production to antioxidant defense and angiogenesis [14]. This process involves mechanisms such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and disruptions in metal ion homeostasis. Mechanistically, copper-induced cell death, also known as “cuproptosis,” is driven by the binding of copper to fatty acylated enzymes in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which leads to protein aggregation and proteotoxic stress, ultimately causing cell death. Numerous previous studies have provided substantial evidence linking metabolic dysregulation with clinical outcomes and treatment responses in various cancer types, suggesting that copper metabolism could offer new targeted strategies for cancer therapy [15, 16].

Recently, single-cell RNA sequencing has enabled the specific analysis of cell populations within the highly complex tumor microenvironment at a single-cell level, revealing previously uncharacterized molecular complexities [17]. Compared to bulk analysis, single-cell analysis can more accurately identify rare gene mutations and molecular features within tumors, potentially aiding in the design of optimal therapeutic approaches to prevent tumor recurrence [18]. In a recent study, researchers systematically mapped the intricate single-cell landscape of gastric cancer, addressing both intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity [19]. However, to date, there have been no reports on single-cell sequencing studies investigating copper metabolism-mediated programmed cell death in gastric cancer. Recent studies have shown that aberrant regulation of copper metabolism is closely associated with malignant progression in several cancer types, including gastric cancer. Intracellular accumulation of copper ions may induce oxidative stress through the activation of certain signaling pathways, which in turn triggers cell death, especially in the copper-dependent programmed cell death pathway. Understanding the relationship between copper metabolism and cell death, especially the mechanism of action in the tumor microenvironment, may provide new breakthroughs in cancer therapy. Therefore, through single-cell gene expression studies, we reveal extensive changes in cell numbers, transcriptional states, and cell–cell interactions within the GC tumor microenvironment under copper metabolism pathways. By leveraging publicly available high-quality scRNA-seq data, we conducted an in-depth exploration of copper metabolism features across various cell subpopulations in gastric cancer. These findings have important clinical implications for the treatment of gastric cancer. First, abnormalities in copper metabolic pathways can be used as new biomarkers for earlier diagnosis of gastric cancer and prediction of patient prognosis. Second, targeted therapies against copper metabolism are becoming a hot research direction in the field of cancer treatment. By regulating the accumulation of copper ions in tumor cells or reducing the activity of copper-dependent enzymes, it may be effective to inhibit the growth and induce the death of cancer cells. This therapeutic strategy targeting copper metabolism is not only expected to improve the effectiveness of existing gastric cancer therapies, but may also contribute to reducing the risk of recurrence by reducing the viability of cancer cells. Our study provides novel insights into the copper metabolism characteristics of malignant and non-malignant cells in gastric cancer, offering valuable clues for predicting patient prognosis.

Methods and materials

Data acquisition and processing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data were obtained from the GSE184198 dataset in the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), including single-cell samples from one gastric cancer patient (GSM5580154) and one healthy individual (GSM4729232). Additionally, gene expression profiles of 375 gastric cancer patients and 32 normal individuals were obtained from the TCGA database, and gene expression profiles of 483 gastric cancer patients were obtained from the GSE84437 dataset in the GEO database. The obtained gene expression profile data were batch-effect corrected using the “limma” and “sva” R packages. Samples obtained from the TCGA database were used for training, while the GSE84437 dataset was used as the validation set. Copper metabolism related genes from molecular characteristics (MSigDB) 7.5.1 download version of the database [20].

Single-cell data processing

Single-cell RNA sequencing data of gastric cancer (GC) and normal tissues (NT) were divided into multiple cell types. We performed quality control on the data, filtering cells with fewer than 3000 feature genes (nFeature_RNA). Next, the correlation between the number of feature genes and total RNA counts was analyzed using scatter plots, and data standardization and normalization were conducted based on cell identity (GC or NT). Batch effects were corrected, and data from different samples were integrated using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and the Harmony algorithm, selecting highly variable genes as features. Cell clustering analysis was performed using t-SNE and UMAP dimensionality reduction techniques. Cell types for each cluster were identified and labeled based on the expression heatmap of immune and stromal cell marker genes. A “CellChat” object was instantiated for cell communication analysis using the “createCellChat” function.

T-cell NMF subgroup analysis

Further non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) analysis was performed on the obtained T-cell data to identify copper metabolism-related gene expression subgroups. First, raw data were read and processed, including the distribution of feature gene numbers (nFeature_RNA) and total RNA counts (nCount_RNA), and the correlation between them. T-cell GC and NT cells were standardized and normalized, and significantly differentially expressed genes were identified by analyzing the variance and mean distribution of gene expression. PCA and the Harmony algorithm were used for data integration and dimensionality reduction to ensure data consistency between different samples. Then, the NMF method was applied to T-cell data to extract feature genes, followed by UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering analysis to obtain metabolic-related gene expression subgroups.

Pseudotime and communication analysis of T-cell subgroups

Pseudotime and communication analysis was performed on the obtained T-cell subgroups with copper metabolism-related gene expression. A single-cell dataset (mycds) was created using the “newCellDataSet” function from the “Monocle” package, and size factors and dispersion were estimated. High-variance genes were selected for downstream analysis based on the dispersion table. Pseudotime heatmaps were plotted using the “plot_pseudotime_heatmap” function from the “Monocle” package to show the expression changes of key genes over pseudotime. Additionally, cell communication analysis was performed using the “CellChat” R package, identifying overexpressed genes and ligand-receptor pairs with the “identifyOverExpressedGenes” and “identifyOverExpressedInteractions” functions. Communication probability calculations, network construction, and centrality calculations were conducted, and ligand and receptor projections into the protein interaction network were further analyzed using the “projectData” function.

Modular and network analysis of T-cell scRNA-seq data using “hdWGCNA”

“hdWGCNA” and “Seurat” methods were used to perform modular and network analysis on T-cell scRNA-seq data to identify core genes. Single-cell data from previous analyses were used for subsequent “hdWGCNA” analysis. Genes expressed in at least 5% of cells were filtered based on predetermined thresholds. Soft-threshold power was calculated using the “hdWGCNA” package to determine appropriate co-expression network construction parameters, selecting 7 as the final soft-threshold power. After constructing the co-expression network, dendrograms and heatmaps were plotted to show correlations between modules. Batch effects were corrected using the “harmony” package, and module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated. Finally, the UCell method was used to calculate expression scores for each module's characteristic genes to identify target core genes.

Enrichment analysis

Thirty significantly expressed genes from brown, blue, and turquoise modules were identified as core genes. PPI networks were constructed, and enrichment plots were generated using the gprofiler website (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/). We also performed mutation analysis on the CMRGs. Using the "maftools" package in R, we analyzed tumor mutation data from the TCGA database. We extracted a list of predicted genes and set significance levels at 0.01 and 0.05. We conducted pairwise comparisons of gene mutation types (wild-type and mutant) and plotted box plots to show differences in risk scores between different gene mutation types. Statistical markers were added to indicate the levels of significance.

Ten machine learning algorithms for model selection and survival validation

Model genes were obtained through 101 models combined with 10 machine learning algorithms and subjected to survival analysis in training and validation sets [21–23]. A rigorous comparison of 101 different models generated by 10 machine learning algorithms was conducted in training and validation sets. These machine learning algorithms included Random Survival Forest (RSF) [24], Elastic Net (Enet), Lasso, Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, Cox’s partial least squares regression (plsRcox), Supervised Principal Components (SuperPC), Generalized Boosted Regression Models (GBM), and Survival Support Vector Machine (survival-SVM) [25]. Notably, in the Lasso+RSF model, only 9 genes were required to achieve satisfactory results. Therefore, a risk score was constructed based on these 9 meticulously selected CMRGs (copper metabolism related genes). The risk score for each patient was calculated using the following formula: Risk Score = ExpressionmRNA1 × CoefmRNA1 + ExpressionmRNA2 × CoefmRNA2 + … + ExpressionmRNAn × CoefmRNAn. Risk scores for all ccRCC patients were systematically calculated, and the median was established for the training set using the R package survminer. This allowed us to categorize ccRCC patients into low-risk and high-risk groups and plot survival curves. Principal component analysis (PCA) was also performed using the R package “limma”.

Nomogram construction and independent prognostic analysis

Data processing and survival analysis were conducted using the survival, regplot, and rms packages in the R software environment [26, 27]. Survival analysis was performed using the Cox proportional hazards model, and a nomogram was generated to visualize the model's predictive ability [28–30]. The nomogram was used to calculate the predicted risk score for each sample. Calibration curves for 1, 3, and 5 years were plotted to assess the model’s predictive accuracy, showing the concordance between predicted and observed values [31, 32]. ROC curves were also plotted to compare the performance of different clinical features with the nomogram. Significant variables (p < 0.05) were included in a multivariate Cox regression model to further analyze their independent prognostic value. Results of univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were tabulated, and corresponding forest plots were drawn to visually display the impact of each variable on survival.

Enrichment analysis

A comprehensive enrichment analysis supported by the “GSVA” R package [33, 34] was conducted using the “c2.cp.kegg.v7.4.” dataset for gene set variation analysis. To determine statistically significant subgroup differences, an adjusted p < 0.05 was used. The Metascape website (https://metascape.org/) was used for the enrichment analysis of 7 CMRGs.

Immune infiltration analysis

We selected samples with p-values below the specified threshold (pFilter < 0.05) and performed some data cleaning to explore the relationship between immune cell composition and tumor risk. This segment of code is used for performing Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) and single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) [17]. Using GSVA and ssGSEA methods, we calculated scores for each gene set in the samples, which were standardized to range between 0 and 1 to investigate the significance of model gene expression in immune function [35]. We converted risk groups into two levels and conducted pairwise comparisons to determine differences in TIDE scores between different risk groups, analyzing the levels of immune escape in high-risk and low-risk groups.

Drug sensitivity analysis

We used the “proprophetic” R package to evaluate the treatment response of high-risk and low-risk patient groups. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for each gastric cancer patient was determined using the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) (https://www.cancerrxgene.org/).

Single-gene survival analysis

Survival curves were plotted using the “Kaplan–Meier” method, and the log-rank test was used to compare survival differences between high-expression and low-expression groups. First, survival curves were calculated using the “survfit” function. Then, the log-rank test for survival differences was performed using the “survdiff” function to calculate p-values.

Molecular docking

To assess the binding energy and interaction patterns between candidate drugs/small molecules and their targets, we used AutodockVina 1.2.2, a computational protein–ligand docking software [36]. Interaction information between F5 and disease-related drugs was obtained from the CTD website (https://ctdbase.org/), the molecular structure of F5 from the PCSB PDB website (https://www.rcsb.org/), and the 3D molecular model of Tretinoin from PubChem (https://www.rcsb.org/). Protein and ligand files were prepared, converting all protein and molecule files to PDBQT format, removing all water molecules, and adding polar hydrogen atoms. The grid box was centered to cover each protein's domain and allow free molecule movement. The interface pocket was set as a 30 Å × 30 Å × 30 Å cubic pocket with a grid spacing of 0.05 nm. Molecular docking studies were visualized using Autodock Vina 1.2.2 (http://autodock.scripps.edu/).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with the use of R software, versions 4.3.1 and 4.1.3. Data from the GEO database and TCGA database, and carry on quality control and batch effect correction, to ensure the reliability and consistency of data. Subsequently, various techniques were applied to dimensionality reduction and visualization, including principal component analysis (PCA), tSNE and UMAP, to better understand the structure and characteristics of the data. Different algorithms such as NMF and hdWGCNA were used for cell subtype analysis, modularity and network analysis, feature selection and model construction, and the prognostic efficacy of the models was verified. In the statistical analysis, we set p-values and false discovery rate (FDR) q-values less than 0.05 to determine statistically significant results. These methods provide comprehensive data processing and analysis support for our study, and contribute to a deeper understanding of the role of copper metabolism in the occurrence and development of gastric cancer, its impact on prognosis and potential therapeutic targets.

Data processing and quality control

The entire workflow of our study is detailed in Fig. 1. We obtained samples from gastric cancer patients and normal tissues from the 10 × scRNA-seq dataset GSE184198. The single-cell data was processed using the Seurat package. To ensure data quality and the rigor of our analysis, we first conducted meticulous quality control, quantifying several quality metrics, including assessing the number of feature genes per cell, UMI counts, and the percentage of mitochondrial and hemoglobin gene expression, thereby eliminating interference from senescent cells and red blood cells (Fig. 2A–E). After stringent quality control and data normalization, PCA and UMAP analysis identified 16 distinct clusters (Fig. 2F). During the annotation of these 16 cell clusters, cell types were determined by plotting marker gene expression (Fig. 2G, H), leading to the identification of nine different cell types (Fig. 2I). We then performed cell–cell communication analysis using the “CellChat” package. The communication results indicated that T cells were significantly involved in interactions with various cell types, showing active intercellular communication (Fig. 2J).

T-cell NMF decomposition

Given the active role of T cells in cell communication, we conducted an in-depth analysis of T cells. We extracted the T cell population for PCA-based dimensionality reduction and clustering (Fig. 3A, B). This analysis identified 13 clusters of T cells (Fig. 3C, D), which were further categorized into five cell types based on the expression of marker genes in each cluster (Fig. 3E, F). Non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) is a matrix decomposition method that operates under the constraint that all elements in the output matrix are non-negative. Compared to principal component analysis (PCA), NMF has a natural advantage in analyzing tumor cell heterogeneity. Gene expression data is usually of very high dimensionality and may contain information about thousands of genes. NMF can simplify the data and extract a small number of major features by mapping these high-dimensional data into a low-dimensional space. Decomposition of scRNA-seq data by NMF allows identification of principal components and features of cellular expression, and categorization of cells into subpopulations according to expression features. This is useful for parsing complex cell populations, especially in tissues with extremely high heterogeneity (e.g., tumors or the immune system).We applied NMF to CD8 +T cells, replacing PCA with NMF results for dimensionality reduction and clustering (Fig. 3G). To identify the potential role of CD8 +T cells in copper metabolism, we defined NMF cell subpopulations based on copper metabolism marker genes, resulting in the following subpopulations: FKBP4 +CD8 +T, TFRC+CD8 +T, RNF7 +CD8 +T, COX17 +CD8 +T, and Non-Aggre-CD8 +T (Fig. 3H, I).

HdWGCNA identification of signature genes

We employed high-dimensional weighted gene co-expression network analysis (HdWGCNA) to identify key molecular features of primary T cells. During the construction of the co-expression network, we observed that when the scale-free topology fit index reached 0.85, the soft-threshold power β was set to 7 (Fig. 5A), constructing an unweighted network for primary macrophage cells to achieve optimal connectivity. We identified eight gene modules, with correlations among the eight modules displayed (Fig. 5B–D). Subsequently, we assessed the module scores of the five T cell subpopulations and found that the yellow, black, and green modules were highly activated in the aβ cell group (Fig. 5E). Thus, we consider the yellow, black, and green modules to be associated with primary T cells. Next, we extracted the copper ion signature genes from these modules for enrichment analysis (Fig. 6). The enrichment results showed that these genes are highly related to the immune function of T cells, highlighting the critical role of copper ions in T cell immunity.

Integrated machine learning for prognostic model construction

To identify the most critical copper metabolism-related genes, we utilized an integrated machine learning approach, using TCGA as the training set and gastric cancer data from GEO as the validation set to analyze the gene screening efficacy of different models. Copper metabolism-related genes identified in HdWGCNA were input into the machine learning-based integration program to identify the optimal copper metabolism genes with the highest accuracy and stability. Combining Lasso regression and random survival forest (RSF), we obtained the final copper metabolism gene set that performed best across both datasets (Fig. 7A), comprising nine copper metabolism-related mRNAs. Gastric cancer patients were divided into high-CMRGs and low-CMRGs groups based on the median score of CMRGs. Figure 7B and C show significant differences between the high-CMRGs and low-CMRGs groups. Additionally, in all datasets, gastric cancer patients with high CMRGs had reduced survival time (Fig. 7D, E). Overall, the results indicate that CMRGs demonstrate stable performance across multiple datasets.

Drug sensitivity analysis

The study of drug sensitivity differences between high-CMRGs and low-CMRGs score cohorts revealed potential strategies for gastric cancer treatment. We evaluated the significant differences in IC50 values of 32 drugs. This implies that the risk score can serve as a predictive benchmark for identifying drug sensitivity and informing clinical drug decision-making. Notably, several drugs had lower IC50 values in the high-CMRGs score group, indicating stronger therapeutic effects in treating high-risk gastric cancer patients. These findings suggest the potential utility of the risk score in drug selection and dose optimization for breast cancer treatment. However, it is important to note that further validation is required to confirm these results and assess the clinical applicability of the risk score in predicting drug sensitivity (Fig. 12A–L). Combining the results of individual gene survival analysis and somatic mutation frequency, we found that the F5 gene plays a significant role in gastric cancer development. We conducted molecular docking analysis to explore drugs interacting with the F5 gene, with results showing that retinoic acid has favorable docking results (Fig. 14). Discussion

Cancer has long been a focal point of research in the life sciences and medical fields. Over the past few decades, cancer incidence and mortality rates have been rising rapidly. In fact, there is a significant association between copper in the human body and cancer, with numerous medical studies indicating that serum copper levels in cancer patients and tumor tissues can be higher or lower (mostly higher) compared to normal individuals [15]. Previous studies have shown that serum copper levels are elevated in cancer patients compared to healthy individuals, and these levels decrease to normal when the tumor is removed [37]. Consistent with prior research, our study also observed an upregulation of copper metabolism-related genes in gastric cancer patients compared to normal individuals.

Copper plays a critical role in enzymatic reactions, mitochondrial function, and antioxidant defense. Regulating copper homeostasis is crucial to prevent copper deficiency and excess [38]. Copper transporters and chaperones, such as ATP7A and ATP7B, are essential in maintaining copper homeostasis by facilitating copper uptake, transport, and excretion across various tissues. Abnormalities in copper metabolism can lead to copper deficiency or overload [39]. Numerous studies have shown that the accumulation of copper ions can cause cytotoxicity, where increased intracellular copper concentrations can induce a specific cytotoxic effect. Copper directly binds to fatty acylated components in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle [40]. This leads to the aggregation and imbalance of these proteins, blocking the TCA cycle, inducing proteotoxic stress, and ultimately causing cell death [41]. In our study, we constructed a prognostic model incorporating nine copper metabolism-related genes, which significantly correlated with poor prognosis in cohorts with higher copper metabolism risk scores compared to those with lower scores. This suggests that elevated copper ion levels indeed influence the progression of gastric cancer.

To investigate the specific mechanisms by which copper ions affect gastric cancer, we performed various enrichment analyses to reveal possible reactive processes. In patients with high copper metabolism risk scores, we observed enhanced interactions with muscle, vasculature, and extracellular matrix. This reflects copper’s role not only as a component in maintaining cellular functions but also as a central hub in cellular signaling pathways involved in cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis. Through these pathways, copper enhances the invasiveness and metastatic potential of cancer cells by activating metabolic and proliferative enzymes [42, 43]. Studies have shown that copper promotes angiogenesis and tumor growth by participating in the activity of several angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietins. Copper can bind to VEGF and promote its dimerization, which is essential for VEGF binding to its receptor and initiating the signaling pathway that promotes angiogenesis. Therefore, blocking copper-dependent angiogenesis is a potential strategy for inhibiting tumor growth [44, 45]. Additionally, the deconvolution results of immune cells showed that a high copper metabolism risk score led to more immune cell infiltration, indicating that copper metabolism also affects the immune response in patients [46]. Through integrated machine learning, we have, for the first time, constructed a prognostic model comprising nine copper metabolism genes associated with tumor metastasis, coagulation, and metal ion accumulation, providing new directions for future research on copper metabolism-induced cell death. This could accelerate studies on copper toxicity inhibitors and immunotherapies for immune modulation in cancer treatment. Although the development of copper-targeting agents for cancer therapy is still in its early stages, our promising bioinformatics results suggest that this approach has the potential to revolutionize cancer treatment. Further research is needed to explore the therapeutic efficacy and safety of these drugs.

Despite the important findings regarding the association between copper metabolism and cancer, our study has some limitations. We lack comprehensive single-cell sequencing data for gastric cancer with large sample sizes. In addition, the clinical heterogeneity of gastric cancer patients with significant differences in genes, metabolic pathways, and immune responses among patients makes it difficult to comprehensively assess the generalized role of copper metabolism in different patients with a single dataset. Although we have revealed some interesting copper metabolism-related features by single-cell RNA sequencing, further experimental evidence is needed to support its potential for clinical application and generalizability, and a multicenter clinical cohort can provide samples from different geographic regions and populations, which will be more conducive to the external validation of the results of the study [47, 48].These factors limit the further development of our research. In conclusion, our findings may provide new insights into the study of the tumor microenvironment in gastric cancer mediated by copper metabolism, contributing to research on metal ion-mediated cell death pathways [45, 49].

Conclusion

This study is the first to systematically map the single-cell characteristics of copper metabolism-related genes in gastric cancer and to propose a prognostic model based on copper metabolism genes. The research findings indicate that copper metabolism plays a critical role in the progression of gastric cancer, influencing not only the invasiveness and metastatic potential of cancer cells but also being closely related to the patient's immune response. Through various bioinformatics analyses, the study identified several key genes associated with copper metabolism and confirmed their high expression levels in gastric cancer patients. Despite revealing the importance of copper metabolism in gastric cancer, the study has certain limitations due to the lack of large-scale validation and support from multicenter clinical cohorts. Nevertheless, these findings offer new perspectives for future cancer treatments, particularly in the areas of copper metabolism-related targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Future research should further validate the functions of these genes and explore their potential applications in clinical treatments.
