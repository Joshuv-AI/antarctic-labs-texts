Electrospun CS/PCL/HA nanofiber membranes incorporating bimetallic La/Cu-MOF for Ni(ii) adsorption: structure–property relationships, mechanism, and optimization process
Authors: Mashael A. Alghamdi, Ahlem Guesmi, Mohamed G. El-Desouky, Ashraf A. El-Bindary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study describes the production of a new multifunctional membrane made of electrospun nanofiber (LCMCPH) composed of chitosan (CS), polycaprolactone (PCL), and hyaluronic acid (HA) with a bimetallic lanthanum/copper metal–organic framework (La/Cu-MOF) for actual elimination of Ni(ii) ions from water. The LCMCPH nanofiber membrane was prepared through electrospinning and then thoroughly characterized by means of XRD, SEM, BET, FT-IR, EDX, and advanced 2D correlation spectroscopy. It confirmed that the MOF had been successfully introduced into the polymeric matrix without losing its fibrous structure in the mesoporous region. Batch adsorption tests indicated high uptake capacity under optimized conditions up to 354.59 mg g−1. The adsorption process was optimized systematically with the Box–Behnken design (BBD) and response surface methodology (RSM), where interaction time and adsorbent dose were found to be the two most significant factors. The quadratic model developed was validated statistically through ANOVA with R2 = 0.9669, showing a very good fit between predicted and experimental results. Isotherm studies indicated that adsorption is best fitted by the Langmuir model, which implies monolayer adsorption by a maximum capacity of 345.08 mg g−1; kinetic studies revealed that the process follows a pseudo-second-order model, meaning chemisorption is the predominant mechanism. Thermodynamic limits indicated that adsorption is endothermic and spontaneous in nature. Further mechanistic studies revealed that adsorption takes place via coordination, electrostatic interaction, and pore diffusion. The optimized conditions (pH ≈ 6, interaction time ≈ 100 min, dose ≈ 0.02 g) gave rise to a high attractiveness value (0.992), thus confirming the efficiency of the optimization approach. Good selectivity and performance in real water matrices highlight its potentiality as a promising scalable adsorbent for wastewater treatment applications.

Introduction

Heavy metal ions in water resources are increasingly becoming a global environmental concern because of their toxicity, persistence, and non-biodegradability. Nickel(ii) is one of the most hazardous contaminants frequently reported due to its wide use in industries like electroplating, battery manufacturing, mining, and metallurgy. Excessive exposure to Ni(ii) can cause serious health problems, including dermatitis, respiratory disorders, nephrotoxicity, and even cancer. Therefore, developing an efficient, sustainable, and cost-effective method for removing Ni(ii) from water systems has great importance.1 Chemical precipitation, membrane filtration, ion exchange, and electrochemical techniques are traditional procedures that have been extensively used for the elimination of heavy metals from wastewater. These techniques are not efficient due to their limitations such as high operational costs, sludge generation, incomplete removal, and secondary pollution.2,3 Recently adsorption has developed as one of the most preferred methods because of its simplicity, high efficiency, and flexibility. The performance of an adsorption system greatly depends on the characteristics of the adsorbent material in terms of surface area, porosity and functional groups.4–7

Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are considered highly advanced adsorbent materials due to their high surface areas with tunable pore structures and diverse functionalities. Bimetallic MOFs possess better physicochemical properties compared to monometallic ones due to synergistic effects between different metal centers.8,9 Lanthanum (La) and copper (Cu) active sites co-doping in a single structure MOF may provide multiple active sites, thus improve stability and enhance performance toward adsorption of heavy metal ions.10 However, powdery nature usually limits the practical application of MOFs due to difficulties in separation and recovery.11 This challenge can be addressed by integrating MOFs into polymeric matrices as an effective strategy.12

Electrospun nanofiber membranes have attracted much attention because they possess a high surface area-to-volume relation with an consistent porous structure that is easily tailored morphologically.13,14 Chitosan (CS), polycaprolactone (PCL), and hyaluronic acid (HA) are very promising polymers since they are biocompatible with functional groups that can stabilize the nanofibrous structure.15,16 Chitosan is a natural polymer containing hydroxyl and amino groups; thus, it can coordinate with metal ions or even provide electrostatic interaction for binding. Polycaprolactone will give mechanical strength to keep the structure stable, while hyaluronic acid will add more functional groups and improve hydrophilicity. The mixture of these polymers with MOFs leads to hybrid materials having better stability, adsorption capacity, and reusability.17

The Box–Behnken design (BBD) optimization of adsorption conditions has numerous advantages in enhancing the process efficiency and reliability. BBD, as a response surface methodology, allows for systematic assessment of combined and interactive belongings of some variables similar pH, adsorbent quantity, communication time, and initial metal concentration with relatively fewer experimental runs compared to full factorial designs. This approach reduces experimental cost and time as well as resource consumption without sacrificing statistical reliability.18 One major strength of BBD is that it generates a quadratic model that describes the system behavior accurately and finds optimal operating conditions through three-dimensional response surface plots and contour analysis.19 Also, BBD does not require extreme experimental conditions which is very good for adsorption systems because such conditions sometimes lead to instability or degradation of materials.20 The statistical validation model through ANOVA, ensures the significance and adequacy of the developed model that can predict adsorption performance precisely. Additionally, BBD helps in identifying synergistic or antagonistic interactions between variables giving more insight into the adsorption mechanism. In all aspects, the application of Box–Behnken design improves process optimization significantly enhances adsorption efficiency and supports robust scalable water treatment systems development.2,3

Herein is presented an innovative and integrated methodology for heavy metal removal by the design of a multifunctional electrospun nanofiber membrane containing a bimetallic La/Cu-MOF within a CS/PCL/HA polymeric matrix. The principal novelty resides in the synergistic combination of a bimetallic MOF with tri-component biopolymer-based nanofibrous structure, which has not been reported previously for Ni(ii) adsorption. Unlike conventional MOF powders, immobilization of La/Cu-MOF into an electrospun membrane shall overcome separation and recovery limitations while maintaining high accessibility of active sites. Further, advanced characterization techniques including 2D correlation FT-IR spectroscopy are uniquely integrated to clarify the adsorption mechanism at molecular level in this study. Another major innovation is the application of BBD coupled with RSM to systematically optimize adsorption parameters and establish a statistically validated predictive model. This work also provides comprehensive mechanistic insight combining coordination, electrostatic interaction, and diffusion processes within one system. In addition, the developed membrane shows high adsorption capacity together with excellent selectivity in the presence of competing ions as well as good performance in real water matrices; hence, it can be practically applied. Altogether, this study presents a new category of MOF-based nanofiber composites having improved functionality and bridging material design with mechanistic understanding and process optimization for advanced applications in water treatment.

Resources and apparatuses

Synthesis of an adsorbent

Synthesis of La/Cu-MOF

The bimetallic La/Cu metal organic framework (La/Cu-MOF) was synthesized by a solvothermal method using lanthanum nitrate hexahydrate, copper nitrate trihydrate and 2-aminoterephthalic acid as the organic linker. The ligand solution was prepared by dissolving 0.181 g (1 mmol) of 2-aminoterephthalic acid in 12 mL of N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under ultrasonic agitation for 15 min to yield a clear solution. Separately, a metal precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.94 g (0.5 mmol) of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O and 0.22 g (0.5 mmol) of La(NO3)3·6H2O in 10 mL deionized water with continuous stirring. The two solutions were then mixed and the pH adjusted to ca. 5.0 with dilute nitric acid to give favorable conditions for the coordination. The mixture obtained was stirred for 30 min, then transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, and heated at 120 °C for 24 h under solvothermal conditions. The resulting precipitate was cooled to room temperature, filtered under vacuum, washed thoroughly several times with DMF and ethanol to remove any unreacted species and residual solvents, and then dried at 70 °C for 12 h.21–23

Synthesis of La/Cu-MOF/CS-PCL-HA nanofiber membrane (LCMCPH)

A 2% chitosan (CS) solution was made with 95% acetic acid, a 1% hyaluronic acid (HA) solution (w/v) was made in deionized water, and a 15% polycaprolactone (PCL) solution (w/v) was made in 90% acetic acid.24 Nanofiber frameworks were formed by means of the electrospinning procedure on a composite solution of PCL, CS, and HA in the volume ratio of 7 : 2 : 1.25 The polymer solution utilized contained 5% (w/w) La/Cu-MOF at a concentration of 15% (w/v) in 90% acetic acid; this resulted in a solution that was then put into a glass syringe by a 20-gauge blunt-tipped needle attached to it which was connected to a syringe pump positioned at the flow rate of 1.2 mL h−1. For electrospinning, this blunt-tip needle received an approximate voltage of about 27 kV from high voltage power supply located at about fifteen centimeters from grounded collecting plate (Fig. 1).

Elimination and batch studies of removal of Ni(ii) using LCMCPH nanofiber membranes

The adsorption characteristics of the LCMCPH nanofiber membranes were optimized by a systematic study on the effects of pH, dosage, original concentration of Ni(ii), interaction time, and temperature. For the batch adsorption experiment involving Ni(ii), standard solutions with Ni(ii) were diluted to prepare 25 mL samples.26 The adsorption isotherm was carried out at concentrations reaching from 20 to 800 mg L−1 for an interaction period of 100 min at 25 °C with a sample volume equal to 25 mL, pH 6, and adsorbent mass 0.02 g. The result of interaction time was studied using a concentration of 400 mg L−1 over the times ranging from 5 to 100 min while keeping the temperature, pH, volume, and adsorbent amount constant. The temperature effect was studied at a concentration of 300 mg L−1 and contact time of 100 min over the temperature range of 20 to 45 °C while keeping volume, pH, and adsorbent amount constant (Table S3). The desired pH level in the experiment was maintained by adjusting it with 0.1 N HCl or NaOH as necessary. The collected data were used to study isotherm and kinetic properties as well as thermal aspects in adsorption processes. Flame atomic absorption spectrometer measurement for Ni(ii) in solutions before and after adsorption was carried out. Average sorption capacities for each sample were based on three independent runs; therefore, absorbed quantities for Ni(ii), qe, and removal percentage (% RE) were calculated by eqn (1) and (2).12

The processes being studied are further improved by the use of these models. Results and discussion

Characterization of LCMCPH nanofiber membranes

X-ray diffraction patterns (XRD)

The diffraction pattern for the pristine La/Cu-MOF (green curve) shows several sharp and intense peaks at low 2θ values (≈8–20°), confirming its highly crystalline nature.34 These characteristic peaks are due to the well-ordered coordination framework constructed from La3+ and Cu2+ ions with the 2-aminoterephthalic acid linker. Distinct reflections demonstrate that a bimetallic MOF has been successfully formed, which possesses a periodic porous structure. The CS/PCL/HA composite (brown curve) shows a broad diffraction peak centered around 2θ ≈ 20–25°, typical of semi-crystalline polymers. This broad halo is attributed to the amorphous nature of CS and HA and semi-crystalline domains of PCL. The absence of sharp peaks indicates that an amorphous structure predominates in the polymeric matrix. When La/Cu-MOF was introduced into the CS/PCL/HA matrix (red curve), the XRD pattern indicated some features from both components; that is, it retained a broad peak characteristic of polymers with additional weak diffraction peaks corresponding to MOF. These new peaks are present but not very intense because they are partially masked by the background from the amorphous polymer and due to their uniform dispersion in the polymer matrix. The outcome affirms that La/Cu-MOF was successfully embedded without compromising its crystalline structure. The composite with Ni(ii) loaded on it (as seen in the blue curve) shows apparent variations in the intensity of some peaks and slight shifts in their places. The intensification of certain peaks, along with their sharpening, indicates either improved crystallinity or structural rearrangement subsequent to the incorporation of Ni(ii). Also, new diffraction features appearing or broadening of existing peaks can be attributed to interactions between species of Ni(ii) and functional groups belonging to both the MOF and polymer matrix. This proves that immobilization of Ni(ii) onto LCMCPH nanofibers has taken place, probably through coordination or surface adsorption mechanisms (Fig. 2(a)).

N2 adsorption/desorption isotherm

The N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms of LCMCPH and Ni@LCMCPH nanofiber membranes can be confidential as type IV isotherms with H3-type hysteresis loops rendering to IUPAC classification, indicating the presence of a mesoporous structure with slit-shaped pores typically arising from the aggregation of fibrous materials (Fig. 2(b)). At low relative pressure, a gradual increase in nitrogen uptake is observed that resembles to monolayer-multilayer adsorption; pronounced adsorption at intermediate relative pressures is attributed to capillary condensation in mesopores. The absence of a limiting adsorption plateau at high comparative pressures further supports the open mesoporous channels.33 The LCMCPH membrane has a higher adsorption capacity than Ni@LCMCPH across the entire pressure range, which implies that there is an accessible surface area and porosity decrease after the incorporation of Ni(ii). This is also supported by BET analysis (Table S5), wherein the specific surface area goes down from 94.16 to 51.11 m2 g−1, together with a decline in increasing pore volume from 0.62 to 0.48 cm3 g−1; this clearly indicates that partial pore filling and blockage have been induced by Ni(ii) species. The distribution of pore radii in Fig. 2(c) shows that LCMCPH has a very clear mesopore structure, with most pores being about 2.84 nm wide. On the other hand, Ni@LCMCPH has most of its pores much narrower at around 1.88 nm and not as many of them, which means that after adding metal the pores became smaller and less accessible. Likewise, the profiles for pore sizes in Fig. 2(d) show that LCMCPH has a wider and larger distribution of volumes in its pores; however, Ni@LCMCPH displays a smaller volume and one that is moved slightly to the right, which means there were changes to the structure and a decrease in how full of pores it is. The same can be said about the results in Table S6. The main pore radius and average particle radius both decrease after Ni(ii) is incorporated, which means that Ni(ii) species were successfully introduced into the porous framework and partially occupied the pore channels. Even though the mesoporous architecture was retained, it was found that textural properties of this nanofiber membrane changed dramatically with the addition of Ni(ii), indicated by decreased surface area, pore volume, and pore size. These parameters are generally known to affect adsorption behavior and mass transfer characteristics.

FT-IR

FT-IR spectra of LCMCPH and Ni@LCMCPH nanofiber membranes reveal successful functional group incorporation and interaction among polymeric matrix, La/Cu-MOF, and Ni(ii) species (Fig. 2(e)). A broad absorption band appears in the region of 3200–3500 cm−1 due to overlapping O–H stretching vibrations from CS, HA, and amino-functionalized MOF linker N–H stretching; this indicates that there are strong hydrogen bonds in the composite structure. The peaks at about 2920 and 2850 cm−1 are due to the asymmetric and symmetric C–H stretching vibrations of the aliphatic chains from PCL. In addition, bands between 1400–1600 cm−1 may be allocated to aromatic CC stretching of the MOF organic linker. Peaks below 1000 cm−1 relate to C–O–C and metal–oxygen (M–O) vibrations confirming coordination within the framework of the MOF. With the addition of Ni(ii), bands are noticeably shifted and slightly intensified, especially those in the O–H/N–H as well as CO regions.35 This means there are strong interactions and possible coordination among Ni(ii) ions and useful groups like amino and carboxyl moieties. The synchronous 2D correlation spectrum has strong auto-peaks along the diagonal which reflect significant variations in the intensity of functional groups; pronounced cross-peaks suggest simultaneous or cooperative changes among hydroxyl, carbonyl, and aromatic groups during the incorporation of Ni (Fig. 2(f)). In summary, these FT-IR and 2D correlation analyses together confirm that Ni incorporation does not change the basic chemical structure of the composite but brings about significant interactions and coordination in the polymer–MOF network. Such changes modify local chemical environments and can improve functional performance in materials.

SEM analysis

The surface morphology and elemental distribution of the nanofiber membranes were detected by SEM and elemental mapping. The SEM images of the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane show a highly interconnected, randomly oriented fibrous network with smooth and continuous fibers, which indicates that electrospinning was successful and that CS, PCL, HA, and La/Cu-MOF components were blended uniformly. The fibers have relatively uniform diameters in the nanoscale range (approximately 70 to 90 nm) without any visible bead formation; this reflects good solution homogeneity as well as stable spinning conditions (Fig. 3(a)). An architecture with porosity created by interwoven fibers offers a large surface area and thus aids in mass transfer during adsorption processes. Elemental mapping analysis proves that the constituent elements are homogeneously distributed; these include C, N, and O from the polymer matrix plus La and Cu from the MOF structure (Fig. 3(b)). The weight percentages are around 39% for C, 14% for N, 37% for O, 5% for La, and 4% for Cu. This indicates a successful process of bimetallic MOF incorporation into a nanofiber matrix without any phase segregation. After Ni(ii) was added to it, SEM images of Ni@LCMCPH show mostly preserved fibrous morphology with some increase in fiber diameter approximately between 90–140 nm and newly developed small surface protrusions or roughness possibly due to deposition or coordination of Ni species on fiber surface which indicates that there has been an interaction between the functional groups of this composite and Ni ions (Fig. 3(c)). Also, elemental mapping of Ni@LCMCPH proves the presence and uniform distribution of Ni across the membrane as well as C, N, O, La, and Cu at compositions about 42% (C), 12% (N), 36% (O), 4% (La), 4% (Cu) and 2% (Ni) (Fig. 3(d)). The homogeneously dispersed Ni verifies that it has been introduced without any aggregation which is very important for maximizing active adsorption sites. The SEM results along with mapping confirm that after loading with nickel metal the nanofibrous structure not change while introducing new morphological changes by nickel metal as well as new active sites to improve membrane functional properties.36

EDX analysis

The elemental composition and successful incorporation of metal species within the nanofiber membranes were further confirmed by EDX analysis.36 The spectrum of LCMCPH shows the presence of C (37.87%), N (15.64%), and O (38.54%) which are attributed to the polymeric components (CS, PCL, and HA), along with distinct signals of La (7.63%) and Cu (6.32%) confirming the effective incorporation of the La/Cu-MOF within the nanofiber matrix (Fig. 4(a)). The uniform appearance of these elemental peaks without any impurity signals indicates high purity and effective blending of the composite components. The EDX spectrum of Ni@LCMCPH upon Ni(ii) incorporation shows the presence of an additional Ni signal with a content of 3.12% which offers direct suggestion for the effective loading of Ni species onto the membrane (Fig. 4(b)). Slight changes in elemental percentages are observed at C (34.08%), N (16.4%), and O (37.6%) that remain dominant, while La (4.33%) and Cu (4.47%) decrease compared to LCMCPH; this may suggest partial surface coverage or redistribution of active sites after Ni(ii) incorporation since these elements have lower values after metal inclusion into the framework. The well-defined characteristic peaks for Ni(ii), La, and Cu confirm that the bimetallic MOF structure is maintained upon further functionalization with Ni(ii). Also, no new unexpected peaks appear which would mean that no impurities or secondary phases came in during the modification process.36

Zero point of charge

The pHzpc of the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane was obtained by using the pH meaning method (Fig. 5(a)). This is important because it helps to understand how surface charge behavior affects Ni(ii) adsorption.37 In this method, a series of 0.01 N KNO3 solutions with initial pH values between 2 and 10 were organized and adjusted to the desired pH with dilute HCl or NaOH. A specific amount of nanofiber membrane was then added to individually solution, which was stirred continuously for 24 h to reach equilibrium.38 The difference (ΔpH = pHfinal − pHinitial) was plotted against the initial pH after the final pH readings were recorded. Since there is no net surface charge there, the pHzpc is identified at the point where ΔpH equals zero. As can be seen from the results, the value of pHzpc for this membrane LCMCPH is 4.53; hence it can be said that when the surface is more than 4.53 it becomes negatively charged due to deprotonation and below this value due to protonation of functional groups like –NH2, –OH, –COOH makes it positively charged. This change in surface charge is very important for understanding adsorption behavior. Under acidic conditions (pH < 4.53), there will be electrostatic repulsion among the positively charged surface and Ni2+ ions, which decreases adsorption capacity, while at pH values greater than pHzpc, negatively charged sites are created that attract strongly (e.g., –COO− and –O−) and also coordinate with Ni(ii) ions. The adsorption process is therefore enhanced greatly under slightly acidic to near-neutral conditions (around pH 6), where both mechanisms of attraction via electrostatic forces and chelation work together effectively for removing Ni(ii) confirming that the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane is suitable for cationic heavy metal adsorption in aqueous systems.

Batch experimentations

Impact of pH

The pH of the solution significantly impacts how well Ni(ii) is adsorbed on LCMCPH. This was tested in a systematic study over a pH series from 2 to 8, as shown in Fig. 5(b). Results clearly indicate that adsorption capacity (qe) is very much dependent on the solution pH; it went up steadily from 142 mg g−1 at pH 2 to reach its highest value of 280 mg g−1 at pH 6, then slightly decreased with further increases in pH. This tendency can be directly related to the earlier established zero charge pH (pHzpc) value of 4.53. At any pH below 4.53, the surface of the LCMCPH membrane is mostly positively charged because functional groups such as –NH2 and –OH are protonated. This creates strong electrostatic repulsion toward positively charged Ni2+ ions and significant competition with excess H+ ions for available active sites. Hence, low adsorption efficiency results from this situation.39 After this, as pH rises past pHzpc, the surface slowly becomes negatively charged due to deprotonation of –COOH and –OH groups that form new binding sites like –COO− and –O−. These new sites allow for better attraction by electrostatics plus coordination interaction with Ni(ii) ions meaning more adsorption happens here; it reaches a peak at pH six where both mechanisms work together best for uptake attraction by electrostatics plus complexation synergistically helping achieve maximum uptake is at its optimum level. After pH 6, a small fall in adsorption capacity could be due to an initial metal hydroxide formation (for example; precipitation competition between adsorption and precipitation processes Ni(OH)2) which reduces effective interaction between active places on the membrane and adsorbate ions. The general conclusion from these results is that operating at pH values above the pHzpc (4.53) favors Ni(ii) adsorption, with pH 6 being optimum for achieving maximum removal efficiency.

Impact of dose

Using the LCMCPH, the adsorbent dosage effect on Ni(ii) removal was investigated in the range of 0.02–0.5 g, as shown in Fig. 5(c). The findings revealed that there is a clear inverse relationship between adsorption capacity (qe) and adsorbent dose while removal efficacy (%) increases directly. The adsorption capacity has reduced substantially from 250 mg g−1 at a dose of 0.02 g to 14.9 mg g−1 at 0.5 g, whereas the removal efficiency increased from 66.67 to 97.6% over this range; this behavior relates to an increase in available active sites with adsorbent quantity, which improves overall elimination efficiency due to greater surface area and more binding sites for Ni(ii) ions. However, the decrease in qe at higher doses is mainly due to the unsaturation of adsorption places and possible aggregation or overlap of nanofiber structures, which reduces the effective surface area and limits the accessibility of active sites per unit mass. Moreover, at lower doses, a higher concentration incline between Ni(ii) ions and accessible adsorption places favors more efficient utilization of active sites resulting in higher adsorption capacity; therefore, although higher adsorbent doses favor maximum removal efficiency an optimal balance among adsorption capacity and efficiency must be considered with lower doses providing higher capacity but higher doses ensuring near-complete elimination of Ni(ii) from solutions.40

Impact of initial Ni(ii) concentration

The performance of the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane regarding adsorption was influenced by initial Ni(ii) concentrations over a broad range (20–800 mg L−1); this is depicted in Fig. 5(d). Results show that adsorption capacity (qe) increases with initial metal ion concentration, from 27.45 mg g−1 at 20 mg L−1 to a maximum of 347.55 mg g−1 at 800 mg L−1 an increase more than significant. Higher concentration gradients mean better diffusion of Ni(ii) into the porous structure of nanofibers which means improved uptake adsorption. On the other hand, the removal efficacy (%) decreases from 98.6% at low concentration to 35.74% at the highest concentration; it has an opposite trend because of practically saturation of active adsorption sites where a fixed number of available binding sites cannot accommodate all increasing numbers of Ni(ii) ions in solution. Most complete is removal at lower concentrations due to abundance of active sites relative to metal ions and less complete is removal at higher concentrations because competition among Ni(ii) ions intensifies even though adsorption capacity is greater. The findings highlighted the requirement for balanced system conditions in accordance with particular application needs in wastewater treatment, since high starting concentrations increase adsorption capacity but optimal removal efficiency arises at lower concentrations.41

Impact of interaction time

The impact of interaction time on the uptake of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH was studied in a time range between 5 and 100 min; results are exposed in Fig. 5(e). It is evident that adsorption capacity (qe) increased very fast at first, from 83.81 mg g−1 at 5 min to 235.51 mg g−1 at 25 min, then continued slowly until it reached equilibrium around 90–100 min with a maximum value of 354.59 mg g−1. This fast uptake observed in early stages may be due to abundant active places readily obtainable on the external surface of nanofiber membranes and high concentration gradients between Ni(ii) ions in solution and adsorbent surface which means very fast mass transfer and surface adsorption. As time progresses, the rate of adsorption slows down because active sites are progressively occupied and diffusion resistance increases since Ni(ii) ions start powerful into inlet pores and inner structure of nanofibrous network; this stage is largely measured by intraparticle diffusion and pore filling mechanisms. Equilibrium is eventually reached when rates for adsorption equal desorption with no further significant increase in adsorption capacity being observed.42 The relatively short equilibrium time (∼90 min) also indicates high efficiency and fast kinetics of the LCMCPH, which can be attributed to its high surface area, interconnected porous structure, and presence of multiple functional groups facilitating strong interactions with Ni(ii). It confirms that there are two mechanisms involved in this process: one is rapid surface adsorption while the other one is diffusion-controlled mechanism that takes long time to occur.

Impact of temperature

The impact of temperature on the adsorption of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH was evaluated over a temperature variety of 20–45 °C as illustrated in Fig. 5(f). The results show clearly that the capacity of adsorption (qe) increases progressively with cumulative temperature from 195.68 mg g−1 at 20 °C to 366.2 mg g−1 at 45 °C. This trend indicates that the process is endothermic; higher temperatures enhance interaction between Ni(ii) ions and active sites of adsorbent. The improvement in adsorption capacity can be attributed to several factors, namely increased mobility of Ni(ii) in solution, reduced viscosity of the medium, and enhanced diffusion metal ions into the interior pores of nanofiber membrane. Also, high temperatures may favor activation more binding sites and stronger coordination interactions between practical groups such as –OH, –NH2, and –COOH with Ni(ii) ions in composite structure. Increasing temperature also assists overcoming mass transfer resistance for deeper ion penetration into the porous network. These observations confirm that an adsorption process is thermodynamically favorable at higher temperatures and likely administrated by chemisorption mechanisms including strong connections between Ni(ii) ions and the functionalized nanofiber membrane. This essentially means that there is a good association among temperature and adsorption capacity which underlines how efficient the LCMCPH system can be at elevated temperatures and its practicability in real wastewater treatment processes.43

Adsorption isotherm

The adsorption isotherm of Ni(ii) on the LCMCPH was studied with non-linear isotherm models as shown in Fig. 6(a) and further analyzed by linearized Langmuir as shown in Fig. 6(b). The data fitted well to the Langmuir isotherm model, which suggests that adsorption predominantly arises through monolayer coverage on a homogenous surface with a limited number of identical active places. This assumption holds true since functional groups and metal centers are uniformly distributed within the nanofiber membrane (Tables S7 and S8). The Langmuir parameters derived from linear fitting (Table S9) indicated maximum adsorption capacity (qm) equal to 345.08 mg g−1, and a Langmuir constant (KL) value of 0.063 L mg−1, which reflects strong empathy among Ni(ii) ions and adsorbent surface. Further, the dimensionless factor of separation (RL) was calculated to be 0.068; this value lies in the variety of (0 < RL < 1) thus confirming that adsorption is highly favorable under these conditions. The good match between experimental data and modeled results plus the high qm value also support very good performance for adsorption by LCMCPH nanofiber membrane. These results can be interpreted as indicating that Ni(ii) adsorption takes place concluded a monolayer chemisorption mechanism involving strong interaction among Ni(ii) ions and active functional groups of composite material.44

The adsorption equilibrium of Ni(ii) was further assessed on the LCMCPH using the Freundlich isotherm model, which defines adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces and is summarized in Table S9. The Freundlich constants indicated n = 7.45 and KF = 155.07 (mg g−1) (L mg−1)1/n, suggesting that adsorption is extremely promising. An n value greater than 1 means that the adsorption of Ni(ii) is promising with high intensity and a relatively large KF value indicates strong adsorption capacity for this membrane.45 The applicability of the Freundlich model implies heterogeneous surface sites having different energies for adsorption, which matches well with complex structures of composite materials containing several functional groups (–NH2, –OH, –COOH) and bimetallic MOF active centers. It also indicates multilayer adsorption because Ni(ii) ions will be adsorbed not only at primary active sites but also over the already deposited layers through secondary interactions. This goes hand in hand with results from the Langmuir model where monolayer adsorption was dominant but surface heterogeneity plus multilayer adsorption played some role in total Ni(ii) uptake onto the LCMCPH (Fig. 6(a)).

The isotherm model of Dubinin–Radushkevich was further functional as an attempt to clarify the adsorption mechanism of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH. This model has been traditionally used for understanding the nature of an adsorption process, whether it proceeds through physical or chemical interactions. The D–R parameters summarized in Table S9 provided a theoretical saturation capacity (QDR) equal to 316.23 mg g−1 and a constant (KDR) of 7.5 × 10−6 mol2 kJ−2. More notably, the mean adsorption energy (Ea) was determined to be 36.84 kJ mol−1, which is much higher than the threshold amount of 8 kJ mol−1 that distinguishes chemisorption from physisorption. This high energy value indicates very clearly that the Ni(ii) adsorption on the LCMCPH is controlled primarily by chemisorption mechanisms since such behavior would require strong chemical bonds between Ni(ii) ions and active useful groups in adsorbent such as amino (–NH2), hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and coordination interactions with metal centers (La and Cu) within MOF structure. These are supported by FT-IR results and 2D correlation spectroscopy which showed significant shifts in functional groups due to binding of Ni(ii).46

The isotherm model of Temkin was also used, which describes the Ni(ii) adsorption onto the LCMCPH under conditions where indirect adsorbate–adsorbent interactions take place and adsorption heat varies with surface coverage. The parameters of the Temkin obtained (Table S9) are a Temkin constant (KT) equal to 316.18 L mol−1 and a heat of adsorption parameter (bT) equal to 106.99 J mol−1. A relatively high value for KT means that there is strong binding affinity between Ni(ii) and the active sites of the nanofiber membrane. Also, the magnitude of bT indicates that the heat of adsorption decreases linearly by growing surface reporting; this condition applies to adsorbent–adsorbate interactions involving heterogeneous surfaces.47 It describes how when Ni(ii) ions fill up available active sites, interaction energy slowly reduces due to site heterogeneity and possibly repulsive forces between adsorbed species. The applicability of the Temkin model supports strong chemical interactions and further confirms that the adsorption method is not purely ideal but influenced by connections between adsorbed ions and the composite surface. This is in good agreement with results from Langmuir, Freundlich, and Dubinin–Radushkevich models which together indicate that adsorption of Ni(ii) on LCMCPH nanofiber membrane involves a mix of strong binding interactions, surface heterogeneity, and energy variation during an adsorption process.

The Jossens isotherm model also explained the adsorption equilibrium of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH. This model is commonly used to represent adsorption systems characterized by heterogeneous surface properties and energetic non-uniformity. The Jossens parameters obtained (Table S9) included a constant K equal to 175.53 and an exponent J equal to 8.14, which means that there is a strong contact among Ni(ii) ions and the surface of adsorbent.47 The relatively high value of K indicates high adsorption affinity and capacity of the LCMCPH toward Ni(ii). The exponent J further elaborates on how the energies of adsorption are distributed as well as surface heterogeneity; when J takes on a significant value, it suggests that ideal homogeneous adsorption behavior does not take place since such behavior would show smaller values for this parameter indicating that adsorption occurs over a variety of active sites by different binding energies. This observation is in line with the composite nature of the LCMCPH membrane, which contains multiple functional groups (–NH2, –OH, –COOH) and bimetallic MOF centers that contribute to diverse adsorption interactions. Further support for complex adsorption mechanisms involving both surface heterogeneity and strong adsorbate–adsorbent interactions comes from the fact that this model fits better than others, as seen in the Freundlich and Temkin models. The Jossens isotherm thus helps confirm that the process controlling Ni(ii) uptake by an LCMCPH nanofiber membrane relates to multisite interaction dynamics with varying energy distributions across its surface.

The isotherm model of Toth was used to define more accurately the adsorption performance of Ni(ii) on the LCMCPH, especially in cases of surface heterogeneity as well as non-ideal Langmuir behavior at both low and high concentrations. The parameters from the Toth isotherm are listed in Table S9; these include a maximum capacity of adsorption (qm) of 1008.36 mg g−1, a Toth equilibrium constant (KT) equal to 0.116 L mg−1, and a heterogeneity parameter (t) with a value of 0.66. Since t does not equal unity (t ≠ 1), this clearly indicates that the adsorption system has significant surface heterogeneity, confirming the presence of energetically non-uniform active sites within the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane. A relatively high value for qm predicted by the Toth model would be an indication of very strong adsorption potential and hence capability for accommodation by material in large numbers for Ni(ii) particularly at higher concentrations.47 Also, KT indicates favorable interactions among Ni(ii) ions and adsorbent surfaces. Applicability of Toth's model is supportive evidence that binding energies are not ideally homogeneous but rather are distributed due to multiple functional groups and bimetallic MOF active sites existing together. These results were consistent with those from Freundlich and Jossens models further emphasizing that adsorption of Ni(ii) on LCMCPH nanofiber membrane occurs via a heterogeneous multi-site interaction mechanism characterized by strong affinity and high adsorption capacity.

In summary, fitting adsorption isotherms data to the Langmuir, Freundlich, Dubinin–Radushkevich (D–R), Temkin, Jossens, and Toth models provides a comprehension of Ni(ii) adsorption onto LCMCPH. The best fit was obtained with the Langmuir model suggesting that monolayer adsorption occurs on a relatively uniform distribution of active sites which has very high maximum capacity (qm = 345.08 mg g−1) and favorable factor of separation (RL = 0.068). The applicability of the Freundlich, Jossens, and Toth models clearly indicates that the surface of the adsorbent is heterogeneous due to multiple useful groups coexisting with bimetallic MOF active centers providing a wide range of adsorption energies; this is further validated by high values for KF and n > 1 as well as t ≠ 1 in Toth heterogeneity parameter. The Temkin model indicated that heat decreases linearly with coverage suggesting strong interaction between adsorbate and adsorbent as well as variation in energy during the process. More importantly, Dubinin–Radushkevich confirmed that adsorption is mainly controlled by chemisorption through a very high mean adsorption energy indicating strong chemical bonding via coordination and electron sharing between Ni(ii) ions with useful groups like –NH2, –OH and –COOH and also metal centers inside MOF structure. Together these models describe an elaborate phenomenon wherein Ni(ii) gets adsorbed on the LCMCPH through monolayer coverage at primary active sites coupled with multilayer heterogeneity phenomena and strong chemical binding leading to high efficiency and capacity suitable for advanced applications in wastewater treatment.

Adsorption kinetics

The pseudo-first-order kinetic model was used to assess the kinetics of Ni(ii) uptake by the LCMCPH in order to ascertain adsorption rates and their reliance on site accessibility. The rate constant (K1), calculated at 0.042 × 10−2 min−1, indicated a moderate adsorption rate during early stages of the process.48 The pseudo-first-order model assumes that site occupation rates are directly proportional to unoccupied sites, typical for physisorption processes. Although it provides insight into initial adsorption behavior, this model cannot fully describe later stages where stronger interactions predominate; hence, it will not be sufficient for complete description of the entire adsorption mechanism. The low value of K1 indicates that while rapid adsorption may happen initially due to an abundance of available surface sites, the overall process cannot be described in terms of simple physical adsorption. This observation when compared with other kinetic and isotherm models indicates that the Ni(ii) adsorption on LCMCPH involves more complex mechanisms beyond what could be assumed by the pseudo-first-order model (Tables S7 and S8).

The adsorption kinetics of Ni(ii) on LCMCPH were further fitted to the pseudo-second-order kinetic model, as exposed in Fig. 6(c) (non-linear form) and Fig. 6(d) (linear form), with the relevant parameters summarized in Table S10. The method of adsorption is primarily regulated by chemisorption mechanisms, as this model demonstrated a good fit to experimental data. The rate constant (K2) was intended as 9.85 × 10−5 g mg−1 min−1 and the equilibrium of adsorption capacity (qe) was found to be 344.8 mg g−1, very close to the experimentally obtained value; thus, this strong correlation confirms how reliable is a pseudo-second-order model in describing behavior related to adsorption. The pseudo-second-order model, in contrast to the pseudo-first-order model, takes into account that the rate-limiting step entails coordination via metal centers inside the MOF structure as well as chemical interactions like electron sharing or exchange between Ni(ii) ions and active functional groups on adsorbent sites like –OH, –NH2, and –COOH groups. The good agreement between calculated and experimental (qe) values with this fitting indicates that chemisorption is indeed the mechanism dominating this process. These results were consistent with those from FT-IR analysis and Dubinin–Radushkevich isotherm model results confirmed strong chemical bonding involvement plus complexation interactions in adsorbing Ni(ii) on LCMCPH.49

The mechanism of how Ni(ii) is taken up by the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane was studied further using the model of intraparticle diffusion.50 This model aids in determining whether internal particle processes are significantly influencing the rate of adsorption. The parameters for intraparticle diffusion parameters (Table S10) showed a diffusion rate constant (Ki) equal to 40.05 mg g−1 min1/2 and a parameter for boundary layer thickness (X) equal to 32.44 mg g−1. Since Ki has a relatively high value, it means that this process contributes much to the overall mechanism of adsorption. However, due to the non-zero intercept (X ≠ 0), it indicates that intraparticle diffusion cannot be the only rate-limiting step; instead, this process goes through multiple stages where adsorption begins with fast external surface adsorption or film diffusion followed by slow intraparticle diffusion of Ni(ii) into the inlet pores of nanofiber membranes until equilibrium is reached. There is a large boundary layer effect as shown by intercept value indicating surface resistance during initial stages of adsorption. These findings further confirm that while intraparticle diffusion plays a significant part in the adsorption process, it collaborates with other mechanisms such as chemisorption and surface adsorption. This supports multi-step adsorption behavior which was seen earlier in kinetic and isotherm analyses.

The adsorption activity of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH was further described using the Elovich kinetic model, especially for systems including heterogeneous substrates and chemisorption processes.51 The Elovich parameters in Table S10 gave an original adsorption rate constant (α) of 0.056 mg g−1 min−1 and a constant of desorption (β) of 91.86 g mg−1. The relatively high value of α indicated a rapid initial adsorption rate, which could be credited to the abundance of available active places on the surface of the nanofiber membrane during early stages of the adsorption method. At the same time, large β values reflected decreasing adsorption rates with time due to progressive occupation of active sites and increased surface coverage. Applicability to the Elovich model would mean that such an adsorption process takes place on highly heterogeneous surfaces with widely distributed activation energies, which is exactly what we expect from a composite structure like that of our LCMCPH membrane containing multiple functional groups and bimetallic MOF active centers. Further support for this model comes from predominant chemisorption involving strong interactions such as electron sharing otherwise exchange between Ni(ii) ions and useful groups (–NH2, –OH, –COOH), plus coordination with metal sites; these findings are in good agreement with those derived from a pseudo-second-order kinetic model besides Dubinin–Radushkevich isotherm results.

The kinetics of Ni(ii) adsorption onto the LCMCPH were comprehensively assessed using pseudo-first-order, pseudo-second-order, intraparticle diffusion, and Elovich models. This modeling helped clarify the adsorption mechanism. The pseudo-second-order model provided the best description of the experimental data, with a calculated adsorption capacity (qe) close to the experimental value confirming that adsorption is largely controlled by chemisorption involving strong interactions such as coordination and electron sharing between Ni(ii) ions and useful groups (–OH, –NH2, –COOH) plus MOF metal centers.50 The pseudo-first-order model was not very applicable, which means physical adsorption cannot describe the system adequately. The intraparticle diffusion model showed pore diffusion plays a significant character in the adsorption procedure but confirmed that it is not the only rate-limiting step through its non-zero intercept; film diffusion also has an important part to play. In addition, the Elovich model confirmed a highly heterogeneous surface and further emphasized that the nature of this process is chemisorption since there is variation in activation energy among different adsorption sites. All this resources that Ni(ii) adsorption onto LCMCPH takes place by a multi-step mechanism where quick surface adsorption happens first followed by intraparticle diffusion then finally characterized by heterogeneous chemisorption resulting in high adsorption efficiency with fast kinetics suitable for practical water treatment requests (Fig. 6(d)).

Diffusion process

The process of intraparticle diffusion was studied by using the multi-linear Weber–Morris plot as shown in Fig. 6(e); details are provided in Table S11. The plot has three linear portions, which means that the adsorption happens through a multi-stage mechanism rather than one step controlled by diffusion. The first stage has the highest diffusion rate constant (Ki = 50.66 mg g−1 min1/2), indicating rapid external surface adsorption or film diffusion wherever Ni(ii) ions rapidly move from bulk solution to membrane surface due to high concentration gradient and many available active sites. The second one with a lower constant diffusion rate (Ki = 36.82 mg g−1 min1/2) represents gradual intraparticle diffusion where Ni(ii) ions enter into inlet pores and fibrous network of composite material, making adsorption rate controlled by diffusion resistance within porous structure. The third one shows the lowest constant diffusion rate (Ki = 15.82 mg g−1 min1/2), which is an equilibrium phase where the process slows down significantly because of saturation of active sites and reduced driving force. This further demonstrates that intraparticle diffusion is not the only rate-limiting step and that surface contacts and film diffusion also play important roles because no linear plots traveled through the origin. Overall, this behavior shows that a combination of surface chemisorption, intra-particle diffusion, as well as external mass transfer controls the overall kinetics of Ni(ii) adsorption onto the LCMCPH.50

The mechanism of diffusion for the adsorption of Ni(ii) ions onto the LCMCPH, as revealed in Fig. 6(f), consists of a number of mass transfer and interaction processes that happen one after another: external diffusion, internal (intraparticle) diffusion besides adsorption at active sites. First, Ni(ii) ions travel from the main solution to the outside surface of the nanofiber membrane through film diffusion (which is also known as external diffusion). This takes place because there is a concentration gradient between the solution and adsorbent surface. This step happens very quickly and it is reflected in kinetic studies by an initial stage. Then, these ions will go inside its porous structure and connected fibrous network via intraparticle diffusion where resistance within pores starts controlling the rate of adsorption. At this stage, mesopores and microchannels are pathways for Ni(ii) ions to get to internal active sites. Lastly, immobilization by adsorption happens on active sites involving strong interactions such as coordination/chelation with functional groups (–NH2, –OH, –COOH) and La and Cu metal centers within MOF structure. These interactions are mostly chemisorptive in nature according to kinetic and isotherm analyses; therefore, the overall adsorption process will be defined by mass transfer plus surface reaction steps without any one mechanism being exclusively responsible for control over this process. The high adsorption efficiency plus fast kinetics observed here can be explained by such multi-step diffusion pathway through the previously discussed multi-stage intraparticle diffusion model.50

Thermodynamics

The thermodynamics of Ni(ii) adsorption onto the LCMCPH were discussed through Van't Hoff and Arrhenius analyses as shown in Fig. 7(a–c), with relevant parameters provided in Table S12. The Van't Hoff diagram (ln Kcvs. 1/T) presented in Fig. 7(a) manifested a linear trend from which the thermal enthalpy change could be computed as 96.63 kJ mol−1 and entropy change as 335.55 J mol−1 K−1; these values confirmed an endothermic process since more adsorption capacity was observed at higher temperatures experimentally. A large positive entropy change indicates that randomness increases at solid-solution interfaces during adsorption, which means enhanced disorder due to the movement of water molecules besides the formation of stable Ni(ii)-adsorbent complexes.52 The Gibbs free energy values vary (ΔG°) at diverse temperatures (293–318 K) are negative, as indicated in Fig. 7(c), with a range from −1.68 to −10.07 kJ mol−1 and becoming more negative with increasing temperature.53 This pattern provides more evidence that the adsorption process is spontaneous and that it converts more thermodynamically advantageous as the temperature increases. Besides, the Arrhenius plot (ln K2vs. 1/T) in Fig. 7(b) gave an activation energy (Ea) of 42.44 kJ mol−1 which implies that the adsorption method is measured by chemisorption rather than physisorption. The thermodynamic parameters show that Ni(ii) adsorption on LCMCPH is a spontaneous and endothermic process with increased randomness and mainly governed by chemical interactions, which further supports the conclusions reached from kinetic and isotherm studies.54

Mechanism of interaction

Different interactions among the active places of the La/Cu-MOF structure and the functional groups of the polymeric matrix (CS, PCL, and HA) govern the process of trapping Ni(ii) on the LCMCPH. Coordination/chelation interactions are dominant where stable complexes were formed between Ni(ii) ions and electron-donating groups like –NH2, –OH, and –COOH groups in CS, HA, and the MOF linker as confirmed by shifts in FT-IR bands N–H/O–H, CO and amides. Also, metal–ligand interactions between Ni(ii) ions with oxygen atoms in the MOF structure lead to forming Ni–O bonds that are indicative of strong binding affinity. The presence of La and Cu centers enhances adsorption through possible synergistic effects where these metal sites facilitate electrostatic attraction as well as being secondary coordination centers. Besides, under optimal pH conditions, electrostatic interactions play a significant role since the partially deprotonated functional groups on the membrane surface will attract positively charged Ni(ii) ions.55 The porous nanofibrous structure evidenced by SEM and BET analyses provides abundant accessible active sites for rapid diffusion of Ni(ii) ions into the inside structure; however, partial pore filling and blockage after adsorption indicate that these species occupy internal pores as well as surface sites. It is conceivable that surface complexation and ion-exchange mechanisms take place whereby Ni(ii) replaces protons or other loosely held cations in the functional groups of the composite. Therefore, it would be most appropriate to characterize this adsorption process as a multi-mechanism system involving coordination, electrostatic attraction, hydrogen bonding, and pore diffusion which results in efficient and stable immobilization of Ni(ii) on LCMCPH (Fig. 6(f)).

Effect of interfering ions

The coexistence of ions and its impact on the adsorption presentation of Ni(ii) onto the LCMCPH was studied under optimized conditions. An original Ni(ii) concentration of 400 mg L−1, a fixed concentration of 50 mg L−1 for each competing ion, an adsorbent dose of 0.8 g L−1, and solution volume maintained at 50 mL were used while keeping optimized pH and interaction time. In the absence of competing ions, the adsorption capability achieved was 276.8 mg g−1 confirming that high intrinsic adsorption efficacy is related to the property of the material LCMCPH nanofiber membrane.56 The presence of monovalent cations K+ and Na+ reduced the adsorption capacity moderately to 255.1 and 218.6 mg g−1, individually proving weak competition for active sites. Divalent cations especially Mg2+ and Ca2+ brought about more significant reductions in adsorption capacities (248.4, and 226.9 mg g−1, individually) because of stronger electrostatic competition and possible site blocking effects. Among anions tested, NO3− gave the least interference with relatively high adsorption capacity (232.6 mg g−1). Cl−, PO43−, and SO42− were moderate interferers having adsorption capacities of 256.2, 248.6, and 170 mg g−1, individually. In general, these results indicate that although competing ions decrease Ni(ii) adsorption to different extents, the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane still has a significant adsorption capacity which emphasizes its high selectivity toward Ni(ii) as well as its practical applicability for treating complex wastewater matrices (Fig. 8).

Effect of real sample

Under optimized experimental conditions (Ni(ii) = 300 mg L−1, adsorbent quantity = 0.8 mg L−1, volume of solution = 50 mL, optimal pH was 6 and interaction time at 100 min), the elimination efficiency of Ni(ii) by the LCMCPH nanofiber membrane was tested in various water matrices. It achieved ≈96.2% in deionized water. The removal efficiency slightly dropped to ≈91.4 with tap water as a matrix, due to background ions like Ca2+, Mg2+, Cl−, and HCO3−. In industrial wastewater having high ionic strength and competing species, it further decreased to about 84%. This reduction in uptake still indicates that LCMCPH nanofiber membrane has very high selectivity for Ni(ii) and resistance against matrix interferences. It proves that adsorption mechanism will work well in real water systems and that material can be used for practical wastewater treatment applications.57

Reusability

The adsorption experiment was performed using a 50 mL aliquot of Ni(ii) solution with an original concentration of 200 mg L−1 and 0.02 g of the LCMCPH under controlled settings for 45 min at pH 6. After adsorption, the mixture was filtered to get Ni(ii)-saturated adsorbent, which then reacted with 50 mL of 0.05 mol per L EDTA solution and stirred for one hour at constant temperature (303 K) (Fig. 9(a)). To increase the final product's purity, the LCMCPH were removed from the resulting solution by centrifugation and then cleaned with distilled water. The LCMCPH were dried at 338 K to prepare them for further possible use in adsorption cycles. Adsorption and regeneration experiments were conducted systematically in five different cycles. For a sorbent material to be practically applicable on a commercial scale, it should possess very good reactivation ability. The uptake capacities measured during the five sequences of sorption–desorption–sorption cycles are displayed in Fig. 9(a) as 97.2, 95.4, 94.1, 91.6, and 88.7% of the initially assigned value; this indicates an excellent reactivation ability of the material. The decrease in adsorption capacity was probably owing to a reduction in the number of available active places together with changes in the exact geometric configuration of the material with increasing cycle numbers which emphasizes that there is great potential for practical recycling use of these beads. Also, the comparison of the XRD patterns presented in Fig. S1 gives more evidence regarding the LCMCPH's structural stability before and after regeneration. This study demonstrates that the adsorbent retains its structure even after undergoing wide usage during five series.58

ANOVA

The quadratic model developed for Ni(ii) adsorption on the LCMCPH is statistically significant and reliable, according to the ANOVA in Table 2. This is indicated by a high F-value (22.75) and a very low P-value (0.0002), which means that the model fits the experimental data well. Out of all the factors tested, connection time (B) was originate to be the most critical variable with an extremely high F-value (107.78) and P-value (<0.0001), followed by adsorbent dose (C), which also had a significant effect (F = 34.09, P = 0.0006). The pH factor (A) was statistically insignificant as indicated by its P-value of 0.7300, suggesting that it has relatively less influence in this range of study.59 The communication terms such as AB, AC, and BC have P-values larger than 0.05; hence their combined effects are not significant, meaning that each variable act independently most of the time. On the other hand, quadratic terms A2 and B2 are statistically noteworthy at P < 0.05 level indicating curvature in response surface hence confirming that quadratic model is suitable for describing system behavior while C2 remains insignificant. A high number of determination R2 = 0.9669 and adjusted R2 = 0.9244, which show strong agreement between experimental and predicted values along with a sufficient precision value of 16.26, further corroborate the model's adequacy. The externally studentized residuals' normal probability plot in Fig. 11(a) shows that the residuals are roughly normally distributed because the points are near the straight reference line with just slight variations. This alignment indicates fulfillment of an assumption necessary for validity in ANOVA and regression analysis: normality. The absence of significant curvature or systematic deviation from this line indicates that major outliers or skewness do not affect the model. Slight dispersion of points is observed near the central region but remains within acceptable limits and does not indicate any serious violation of statistical assumptions. Furthermore, symmetric distribution of residuals around zero confirms that model errors are random and unbiased. Overall, this plot validates adequacy for developing a quadratic model and confirms reliability along with soundness statistically regarding regression results describing adsorption process.64

Conclusion

This study described a novel multi-functional electrospun nanofiber membrane (LCMCPH) that effectively removes Ni(ii) ions from aqueous solutions. It is based on a CS/PCL/HA polymeric matrix loaded with a bimetallic La/Cu-MOF. Full characterization proved the successful loading of the La/Cu-MOF into the nanofibrous structure, which has high surface area, many active sites, and a well-defined mesoporous structure. The adsorption performance reached an ultimate capacity of about ∼354 mg g−1, showing the efficiency of this designed composite. The most influential parameters were revealed to be contact time and adsorbent dose through process optimization with BBD and RSM at their optimal values: pH ≈ 6, connection time ≈ 100 min, and low adsorbent dosage (∼0.02 g), achieving high desirability (∼0.992). Monolayer adsorption, mostly chemisorption, occurred because it best fit into Langmuir isotherm adsorption kinetics that followed pseudo-second-order kinetics. Adsorption thermodynamics proved that the process was endothermic and spontaneous, whereas mechanistic studies indicated that adsorption took place via coordination interaction, electrostatic attraction as well as pore diffusion within the composite structure. Besides that, this membrane performed well in competitive ions and real water matrices proving its selectivity plus practical use; in totality this study presents an attractive scalable efficient MOF-based nanofiber adsorbent with large prospects for advanced wastewater treatment together with heavy metal purification.
