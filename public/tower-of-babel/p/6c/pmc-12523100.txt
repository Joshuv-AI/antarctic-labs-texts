Comparison of hemodynamics and respiratory function between ciprofol-remifentanil and propofol-remifentanil in obese patients undergoing gastrointestinal endoscopic anesthesia: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chunfang Jian, Minghua Zhang, Xiaochun Zhong, Xihua Li, Hangxiang Fu, Ying Chen, Yi Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo assess the safety of ciprofol for painless gastroenteroscopy in obese patients by comparing the effects of ciprofol-remifentanil and propofol-remifentanil on hemodynamic parameters, respiratory function, and other adverse reactions.MethodsA total of 70 obese patients who underwent painless gastrointestinal endoscopy at Longyan First Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Longyan, China) from September 2023 to February 2024 were enrolled. They were randomly assigned into the propofol group (Group A) and the ciprofol group (Group B) by a random number table. Group A received propofol combined with remifentanil anesthesia during the endoscopy, while Group B received ciprofol combined with remifentanil anesthesia. The systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) levels were compared between the two groups at pre-anesthesia (T0), 1-min post-anesthesia (T1), 3-min (T2), 6-min (T3), 12-min (T4), and 5-min post-recovery (T5). Arrhythmias, hypotension, tachycardia, bradycardia, and the use of vasoactive drugs were documented. Additionally, the incidence and average severity of hypoxemia, the average duration of hypoxemia, and the average minimum SpO2 level were recorded. The occurrence rates of jaw thrust, mask ventilation, nasopharyngeal airway insertion, tracheal intubation, and other adverse reactions were recorded.ResultsCompared with Group A, Group B exhibited significantly higher blood pressure levels at T1 and T2, as well as higher SpO₂ levels at T1, T2, and T3 (P < 0.05). The incidence of hypotension was lower in Group B (P < 0.05), and no significant differences were identified in arrhythmias, tachycardia, bradycardia, or the use of vasoactive drugs between the two groups. Group B also demonstrated a lower incidence of hypoxemia, reduced average severity, shorter duration, and a higher minimum SpO₂ level compared with Group A (P < 0.05). Additionally, Group B required fewer jaw thrust interventions (P < 0.05) and had a significantly lower incidence of injection pain (P < 0.05). No significant differences were found in the incidence of other adverse reactions between the two groups.ConclusionThe ciprofol-remifentanil combination used in painless gastroenteroscopy in obese patients resulted in more stable hemodynamics, significantly reduced respiratory depression, and less injection pain.Trial registrationThe trial was registered in the China Clinical Trials Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300075619, Date of registration: 11/09/2023).Keywords: Ciprofol, Obesity, Gastrointestinal endoscopy, Hemodynamics, Respiratory function, Adverse reactions

Background

Gastrointestinal endoscopy is one of the most accurate and direct methods for screening and diagnosing gastrointestinal diseases, especially early gastrointestinal tumors [1, 2]. Painless gastrointestinal endoscopy has been widely applied in clinical practice. Obese patients typically require higher doses of anesthetic drugs during induction due to their increased body mass. This results in an elevated risk of hypotension and other anesthesia-related challenges, including limited neck extension, pharyngeal fat accumulation, difficulty in maintaining an open airway, and significant fat deposition in the chest and abdomen which contributes to reduced lung and chest compliance [3]. Additionally, numerous obese patients suffer from obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and other airway obstruction issues, resulting in chronic hypoxia, reduced oxygen reserves, and an increased risk of respiratory complications, such as respiratory depression, apnea, airway obstruction, and hypoxemia, especially during the administration of anesthetics and analgesics [4].

Propofol is the most commonly used intravenous anesthetic drug for outpatient gastroenteroscopy, which is characterized by fast onset of action, rapid awakening, and high clearance [5], while it is accompanied by significant dose-dependent hypotension [6], respiratory depression [7], and injection-site pain [8, 9]. Ciprofol is a novel 2,6-disubstituted phenol derivative independently developed in China, which is 4–5 times more potent than propofol. Studies have reported that the efficacy of ciprofol is comparable to that of propofol, while the incidence of injection pain with ciprofol is significantly lower [10–13]. Additionally, the incidence of hypotension and respiratory depression is reduced with ciprofol [14, 15]. As a result, ciprofol can be used as an intravenous anesthetic for painless gastrointestinal endoscopy and anesthesia induction, providing enhanced safety. However, studies have primarily concentrated on the general population, and the effects of ciprofol on hemodynamics, respiratory function, and other adverse reactions in obese patients, due to their unique anatomy and physiology, remain poorly understood.

Remifentanil has a rapid onset of action and a short elimination half-life [16], with synergistic effects when combined with other intravenous anesthetics. Compared with other long-acting opioids, it has a relatively mild impact on hemodynamics, and respiratory depression is easier to recover from and control. This enables precise regulation of the depth of analgesia and reduces the risk of postoperative respiratory depression. Although the use of remifentanil in painless gastroenteroscopy has been less well studied, this prospective randomized controlled trial aimed to compare the effects of propofol-remifentanil and ciprofol-remifentanil on hemodynamics and respiratory function.

This study employed a prospective randomized controlled trial to compare the effects of propofol-remifentanil and ciprofol-remifentanil on hemodynamics, respiratory function, and adverse reactions. The findings provide valuable insights into managing anesthesia during painless gastrointestinal endoscopy, particularly in obese patients.

Methods

Research ethics

This was a prospective, single-blind, parallel randomized controlled study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Longyan First Hospital of Fujian Medical University (Longyan, China; Approval No. LYREC2023-K051-01) and registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (Registration No. ChiCTR2300075619). This study adheres to the applicable Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians.

Participants

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 70 patients who underwent painless gastrointestinal endoscopy at Longyan First Affiliated Hospital of Fujian Medical University between September 2023 and February 2024 were selected. All patients had a body mass index (BMI) of 28 kg/m2 or higher and were randomly assigned to two groups of 35 patients each using a random number table. Group A received propofol, and Group B received ciprofol.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients meeting the indications for painless gastrointestinal endoscopy; written informed consent obtained; age ≥ 18 years and BMI ≥ 28 kg/m2; American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification of I to III; no contraindications for nasal intubation; clear consciousness prior to the examination. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with gastric emptying disorders; upper gastrointestinal obstruction; coagulopathy; malignant tumors; severe cardiovascular diseases; severe respiratory diseases or significant organ dysfunction; severe OSA or a history of difficult tracheal intubation; a history of allergy to propofol, ciprofol, or remifentanil; drug abuse; alcoholism; long-term use of antidepressants, analgesics, or psychiatric medications; cognitive dysfunction; severe liver or kidney dysfunction; pregnancy, breastfeeding, or planning pregnancy; nasal deformities, rhinitis, epistaxis, or a history of nasal surgery; upper respiratory infections; or other anesthesia contraindications.

Anesthesia

All patients fasted for at least 8 h and refrained from drinking for 2 h preoperatively. Pre-anesthesia assessments were conducted for each patient before gastrointestinal endoscopy, including measurements of height, weight, age, gender, ASA classification, and BMI. Upon entering the procedure room, continuous monitoring was initiated, including electrocardiogram (ECG), heart rate (HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2). An intravenous Line was established, and a slow infusion of 5% glucose solution was administered. Patients were positioned on their left side, and oxygen was delivered via mask at a rate of 6 L/min until SpO2 exceeded 98%. Remifentanil was then administered slowly at 0.15 μg/kg, followed by either Group A: propofol 1 mg/kg, or Group B: ciprofol 0.2 mg/kg, and each drug was given over at least 30 s. During induction, patients were intermittently aroused, and if the eyelash reflex had not disappeared within 2 min, additional doses of propofol (0.5 mg/kg for Group A) or ciprofol (0.2 mg/kg for Group B) were administered, with a minimum 1-min interval between doses, until adequate sedation was achieved. The depth of sedation was assessed using alertness and sedation scores, as described previously [17]. The scoring criteria were as follows: 5 points for a rapid response to normal voice; 4 points for a sluggish response; 3 points for eye-opening to loud or repeated calling; 2 points for mild shoulder or head response; and 1 point for a response to trapezius squeeze. Successful general anesthesia induction was defined as an alertness and sedation score of ≤ 1 point, without the use of alternative anesthetic agents. During the procedure, the anesthesiologist determined whether additional drugs were necessary based on the patient’s behavior, such as movement, eye-opening, or speaking.

All procedures were performed by experienced anesthesiologists and attending physicians in the endoscopy room. If SpO2 dropped below 95%, the anesthesiologist would perform jaw thrust and airway management. In the case of hypoxemia (SpO2 < 90%), mask ventilation with pure oxygen was initiated, and bag-valve-mask ventilation was applied if necessary. For severe hypoxemia (SpO2 < 85%), a lubricated nasopharyngeal airway, coated with mineral oil, was inserted into the nasal cavity. If bradycardia (HR < 50 beats/min) occurred, intravenous atropine (0.5 mg) was administered. In the event of hypotension (defined as a decrease in SBP by more than 30% from baseline or a systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg), dopamine (2 mg) or metaraminol (1 mg) was given, depending on the HR. Continuous monitoring of blood pressure and HR would guide the need for additional vasoactive drugs. Before the patient regained consciousness, the nasopharyngeal airway was removed. If nausea or vomiting occurred after awakening, dexamethasone 5 mg was administered intravenously. After the examination, patients were allowed to leave the post-anesthesia care unit when their steward awakening score was ≥ 5 [18]. Anesthesia administration was performed by a senior anesthesiologist, while data recording was conducted by another anesthesiologist.

Outcomes and data collection

The primary outcomes of this study were the incidence of hypotension and hypoxemia. Secondary outcomes included blood pressure, HR, and SpO2 at various time points, cardiovascular adverse events, interventions for respiratory adverse events, the dosage of vasoactive drugs administered, and other adverse reactions, such as laryngospasm, bronchospasm, nausea, vomiting, cough, epistaxis, sore throat, dizziness, allergic reactions, intraoperative awareness, and injection site pain.

Data were obtained by independent investigators who underwent evaluation training for all outcomes. These investigators were blinded to the objectives and design of study during the data collection phase.

Other adverse reactions

Intraoperative awareness: The incidence of intraoperative awareness was assessed in both patient groups using the Bryce questionnaire method [19, 20], administered 30 min and one week after the examination. What do you remember happening between these two events? Did you dream? What was the worst thing you felt during the procedure? If patients reported any memories of the surgery, further inquiries were made about the content of their awareness, including sensations (auditory, visual, and pain), motor function, and mental state. Communication with the supervising anesthesiologist, surgeon, and operating room nurse was carried out to verify the accuracy of the patient’s account. Statistical analysis

In the pilot experiments, 20 eligible obese patients who had undergone painless gastroenteroscopy were randomly assigned to Group A (propofol group, n = 10) and Group B (ciprofol group, n = 10). The analysis concentrated on SBP 1 min after anesthesia (T1) and the mean duration of hypoxemia. At T1, SBP was 105.77 ± 12.00 mmHg in Group A and 119.57 ± 13.31 mmHg in Group B. The mean duration of hypoxemia was 7.71 ± 7.50 s in Group A and 1.21 ± 2.25 s in Group B (P < 0.05). The SBP at T1 was significantly higher in Group B than that in Group A (P < 0.05), while the mean hypoxemia duration in Group B was significantly shorter than that in Group A (P < 0.05).

PASS 21.0 software was used to calculate the sample size. The Student's t-test was applied, with an equal group allocation ratio. The hemodynamic index suggested a minimum sample size of 24 patients per group, while the respiratory index indicated a minimum of 28 patients per group. To account for a 20% dropout rate, 35 patients were recruited per group, resulting in a total of 70 patients. Data analysis was performed using SPSS 23.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Normally distributed quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation ( ± s), and non-normally distributed data were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used for making comparisons at different time points, while categorical data were analyzed using the χ2 test or the Fisher's exact test. Ordinal data were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

This randomized clinical trial demonstrates that ciprofol-remifentanil provides superior hemodynamic stability and respiratory safety compared with propofol-remifentanil during painless gastroenteroscopy in obese patients. These outcomes address key challenges faced by obese patients, involving hypotension and respiratory depression.

Ciprofol demonstrated superior hemodynamic stability during the induction phase (T1-T2), maintaining higher SBP/DBP levels compared with propofol, aligning with previous studies [21, 22]. This may be attributed to reduced myocardial inhibition and vasodilation, which is crucial for obese patients who are highly sensitive to anesthetic-induced circulatory compromise due to cardiovascular overload and metabolic disorders [23]. Notably, while the ciprofol group showed numerically lower dopamine (1 vs. 4, P = 0.356) and metaraminol (0 vs. 4) requirements, the overall use of vasoactive drugs did not reach statistical significance (P > 0.05). This discrepancy may be due to the study's limited sample size (n = 35 per group), which lowered statistical power for secondary outcomes. Additionally, our cohort contained insufficient numbers of patients with Class II/III obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2), who may have significant drug requirements, recognizing as a target population for future studies. Importantly, ciprofol exhibited cardiovascular safety similar to propofol, with no significant difference in HR fluctuations or adverse events, including arrhythmias, hypertension, and bradycardia/tachycardia. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [24]. These findings collectively suggested that ciprofol could present hemodynamic advantages without increasing the risk of cardiovascular adverse events, highlighting its favorable circulatory safety profile.

Ciprofol demonstrated superior respiratory safety in obese endoscopy patients, as evidenced by a 31.5% reduction in airway interventions (P < 0.05) and attenuated hypoxemia severity versus propofol. This advantage originates from its pharmacodynamic specificity: it exhibits a 4–fivefold higher affinity for GABA receptors compared with propofol [25], enabling equivalent sedation efficacy at lower plasma concentrations. This reduces depression of the brainstem respiratory centers, in contrast to propofol, inducing respiratory rhythm disorders through direct medullary inhibition [26]. For obese patients, the benefit was particularly evident in reduced jaw thrust requirements (P < 0.05), a key indicator since they are prone to airway collapse due to fat accumulation [27]. While observed efficacy advantage was less remarkable than the 54.7% decrease recorded in non-obese individuals [28], this discrepancy can be attributed to protocol differences. The remifentanil-based regimen, causing shorter-acting respiratory depression compared with alfentanil [28], may partially offset the advantages of ciprofol, and obesity-induced μ-opioid receptor upregulation may exacerbate the respiratory effects of remifentanil. Clinically, Group B demonstrated superior oxygenation, with significantly higher SpO₂ levels at T1-T3 (P < 0.05) (Table 2), a 3.43% greater nadir SpO₂ (P < 0.05) (Table 4), and a 30.1% reduction in the incidence of hypoxemia (P < 0.05) (Table 4). These improvements persisted despite comparable mask ventilation requirements (Table 3), suggesting that ciprofol could specifically mitigate obesity-related anatomical obstruction rather than generalized respiratory depression. The preserved oxygenation capacity, being crucial in obesity-prone hypoventilation, was further evidenced by shorter hypoxemia duration, indicating faster recovery from airway events, emerging as an essential safety feature for outpatient endoscopy.

Ciprofol demonstrated superior tolerability with markedly reduced injection pain incidence (0% vs 20%, P < 0.05). This can be attributed to its unique formulation properties. In contrast to propofol, inducing pain through aqueous free-drug concentrations that directly stimulate vascular Aδ fibers [29], higher lipid solubility [30] and 4–5 times greater potency [25] of ciprofol collectively minimize vascular irritation while maintaining anesthetic efficacy.

The intraoperative awareness rate was similar between the two groups (2.86% vs. 0%, P > 0.05), which aligns closely with baseline data for gastrointestinal endoscopy (1.67%) [31]. However, due to the inability to objectively monitor anesthesia depth using bispectral index (BIS), these results should be interpreted with caution. This methodological limitation highlights the necessity of future validation using depth-of-anesthesia monitoring technologies. Importantly, no allergic reactions were identified in either group (0% vs. 0%), providing preliminary safety reassurance for expanding clinical use of ciprofol. Nonetheless, given its recent market approval in 2020, continued pharmacovigilance is essential. These findings highlight the urgent need for dose–response characterization and standardized depth monitoring to establish evidence-based sedation guidelines for obesity.

This study proved ciprofol's potential advantages for obese patients. However, it had three key limitations. Firstly, the single-center design and modest sample size (n = 70) might limit generalizability of the findings, particularly for higher-risk subgroups, such as those with Class II/III obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) and severe OSA. Secondly, while participants and data collectors were blinded, blinding could not be maintained for the administering anesthesiologists due to the distinct dosing regimens (0.2–0.4 mg/kg ciprofol vs. 1–2 mg/kg propofol), which were necessary for patient safety, introducing potential performance bias. Finally, the lack of objective depth-of-anesthesia monitoring (e.g., BIS) might restrict pharmacodynamic characterization, especially during the hemodynamically unstable induction phase. To address these Limitations, future multicenter trials should incorporate the following improvements: 1) Enrollment of high-risk cohorts with Class II/III obesity (BMI ≥ 35 kg/m2) and severe OSA (AHI ≥ 30); 2) Development of BMI-stratified dosing protocols to enable complete blinding through equivalent-dose formulations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, the findings demonstrate that ciprofol-remifentanil is a clinically valuable option for obese patients requiring endoscopic sedation, potentially reducing injection pain, hypotension, and respiratory suppression.
