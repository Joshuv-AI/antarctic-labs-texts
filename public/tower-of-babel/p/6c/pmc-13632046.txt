Illness perception of breast cancer among patients receiving treatment in a university teaching hospital, South-west Nigeria: a qualitative phenomenological study
Authors: Deborah Tolulope Esan, Oluwatomigbekele Dorcas Onabanjo, Nasirat Omolara Rasaq, Carlos Guillermo Ramos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreast cancer remains a significant public health challenge in Nigeria, with perceptions of the illness influencing coping, treatment adherence, and quality of life. Drawing on Leventhal’s Illness Perception Model, this study explored how women diagnosed with breast cancer construct meaning from their experiences, while situating these findings within existing qualitative evidence from Sub-Saharan Africa and addressing a relative gap in phenomenological and theory-informed studies in Nigeria.MethodsA qualitative phenomenological design was employed, involving in-depth interviews with 20 breast cancer patients aged 33–76 years at Bowen University Teaching Hospital (BUTH), Ogbomoso, Oyo State, Nigeria. Data were collected between August and October 2025. Participants included married, widowed, divorced, and single women, with diagnoses ranging from stage I to IV. Data were collected via semi-structured interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed using thematic analysis to identify recurring patterns and lived experiences.ResultsFour main themes emerged: (1) Illness as a spiritual test or act of God, fostering resilience through faith; (2) Attribution to stress, lifestyle, and modern living, linked to socio-economic pressures; (3) Perception as hereditary or biologically predetermined, heightening familial concerns; and (4) Initial fatalism as a death sentence, evolving into a manageable condition with medical and emotional support. Perceptions shifted from fear to empowerment, shaped by treatment, faith, and social networks.ConclusionIllness perceptions among Nigerian breast cancer patients are multifaceted, blending spiritual, lifestyle, and biomedical elements, and evolve with lived experiences. These findings underscore the need for culturally sensitive, patient-centered interventions to enhance coping and promote early detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16527-9.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women worldwide, with an estimated 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths reported globally [1]. In Nigeria, breast cancer accounts for approximately one-quarter of all cancers among women and remains a major contributor to cancer-related mortality [2]. Although incidence appears to be increasing, this trend is more accurately attributed to improved detection and demographic transitions, while delayed diagnosis, limited screening, and socio-cultural factors primarily contribute to late-stage presentation and poorer survival outcomes rather than increased incidence [3–5].

Survival outcomes for breast cancer vary significantly between high-income countries and Sub-Saharan Africa. While five-year survival rates exceed 80–90% in high-income settings, studies from Nigeria report substantially lower survival, largely due to late presentation and limited access to timely and comprehensive treatment [6, 7].

Breast cancer profoundly affects multiple dimensions of women’s lives, including physical, psychological, social, and spiritual well-being. Beyond clinical outcomes, the way individuals perceive their illness plays a critical role in shaping their emotional responses, coping strategies, treatment adherence, and overall quality of life [8, 9]. Evidence suggests that breast cancer is often perceived as a life-threatening condition associated with disruptions in body image, sexuality, and social roles, particularly in sociocultural contexts where the breast symbolizes femininity, motherhood, and identity [10, 11].

Understanding patients’ perceptions of breast cancer is therefore an essential component of patient-centered care. Illness perceptions influence health-seeking behaviour, engagement with treatment, and adaptation to illness, making them a critical focus in nursing and psychosocial oncology [12, 13]. Existing literature on illness perceptions in breast cancer has predominantly focused on Western populations, highlighting themes such as fear of recurrence, stigma, and biomedical explanations. However, in African contexts, including Nigeria, illness perceptions are often shaped by a complex interplay of spiritual beliefs, cultural norms, and socio-economic realities [14, 15]. Studies across Sub-Saharan Africa indicate that patients may interpret cancer as a spiritual test, divine punishment, or socially mediated condition, with faith playing a central role in coping [16]. While qualitative studies from countries such as Ghana, Uganda, and South Africa have explored illness perceptions within diverse African contexts, these studies often vary in methodological approach and theoretical application. In Nigeria specifically, there remains a relative paucity of phenomenological and theory-informed qualitative research that explicitly applies structured frameworks such as the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM-SR), particularly in semi-urban settings and with attention to how illness perceptions evolve over time [17]. Furthermore, few studies have explored how these perceptions evolve over time among patients receiving treatment in semi-urban healthcare settings. This gap constrains the development of culturally responsive interventions tailored to local contexts.

This study adopts a qualitative phenomenological approach to explore the lived experiences of breast cancer patients and how they construct meaning around their illness. Leventhal’s framework is used as a sensitizing lens to support interpretation while preserving participants’ subjective narratives [18].

The primary aim of this study is to explore illness perceptions among breast cancer patients attending Bowen University Teaching Hospital (BUTH), Ogbomoso, Oyo State, Nigeria. Specific objectives include: (1) identifying common themes in how patients perceive the causes, consequences, and controllability of breast cancer; (2) examining the role of spiritual, lifestyle, and hereditary factors in shaping these perceptions; and (3) understanding how perceptions evolve over time with treatment and support. The findings may contribute to context-specific insights from a semi-urban Nigerian setting, demonstrating the application of the CSM-SR framework, and highlighting the temporal evolution of illness perceptions among patients undergoing treatment, thereby informing culturally sensitive, patient-centered interventions. This study is situated within a semi-urban Nigerian context, where cultural beliefs, healthcare access, and socio-economic factors intersect to shape illness experiences. By addressing this gap, the study highlights the importance of integrating biomedical care with psychosocial and spiritual support in breast cancer management.

Theoretical framework

This study is underpinned by Leventhal’s Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM-SR), also referred to as the Illness Perception Model a widely used cognitive-behavioral framework that explains how individuals interpret and respond to illness [18]. The model posits that individuals develop “common-sense” representations of illness, which guide coping behaviours and influence health outcomes. These representations are shaped by personal experiences, cultural beliefs, social context, and interactions with healthcare systems.

The CSM-SR conceptualizes illness perceptions across several interrelated dimensions:

Illness Identity – the symptoms and labels individuals associate with the illness.Cause – beliefs about the factors responsible for the illness, including biomedical, psychosocial, and spiritual explanations.Timeline – perceptions regarding the duration and course of the illness (acute, chronic, or cyclical).Consequences – beliefs about the physical, emotional, and social impact of the illness.Controllability/Cure – perceptions of whether the illness can be managed or cured through personal actions, medical treatment, or external forces.Illness Coherence – the extent to which individuals feel they understand their illness.Emotional Representations – the emotional responses elicited by the illness, such as fear, anxiety, distress, or hope, which play a central role in shaping coping behaviours.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative phenomenological design focused on exploring the lived experiences and illness perceptions of breast cancer patients. Phenomenology, as a qualitative research tradition, seeks to understand the essence of participants’ subjective experiences, allowing for an in-depth examination of how individuals make meaning of their illness [19]. Data were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006), which is compatible with phenomenological inquiry when focused on participant meaning-making. Leventhal’s Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM-SR) was used as a theoretical sensitizing framework to inform interview questions and initial interpretation, rather than as a prescriptive coding template. This approach allowed participants’ narratives to guide theme development while situating findings within an established theoretical model [18]. This design was chosen to capture the nuanced, contextual narratives of breast cancer patients in a Nigerian setting, where cultural and spiritual factors significantly influence health experiences.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Bowen University Teaching Hospital (BUTH), Ogbomoso, South-west Nigeria. BUTH, formerly known as Baptist Medical Centre, was established in 1907 and upgraded to a teaching hospital in 2009. This 400-bed hospital serves over 50,000 outpatients and 10,000 in-patients annually. It is located in Ogbomoso, a semi-urban city in Oyo State, BUTH caters to a diverse population with both traditional and modern lifestyles. The hospital is strategically positioned along the Ogbomoso-Ilorin Road, providing prime healthcare services to urban and rural dwellers in Ogbomoso and surrounding towns. As a privately-owned tertiary healthcare facility, BUTH is managed by the Nigerian Baptist Convention and affiliated with Bowen University, Iwo. The hospital is renowned for delivering quality healthcare and training healthcare professionals, including nurses, doctors, and allied health workers. Data collection occurred in private consultation rooms within the oncology unit to ensure participant comfort and confidentiality.

Participants and sampling

Purposive sampling was used to recruit 20 women diagnosed with breast cancer, ensuring a diverse representation based on age, marital status, cancer stage, and treatment duration. Inclusion criteria included: (1) confirmed diagnosis of breast cancer by a physician; (2) age 18 years or older; (3) ability to communicate in English or Yoruba (with translation assistance); and (4) willingness to participate voluntarily. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe cognitive impairment or terminal illness precluding informed consent or interview participation; and (2) refusal to provide consent. The sample size of 20 was determined by data saturation, defined as the point at which no new themes or insights emerged from subsequent interviews. This sample size is consistent with phenomenological studies in health research, which typically range from 6 to 25 participants, prioritizing depth of experiential data over statistical generalization. Furthermore, during the data collection period (August–October 2025), approximately 30 eligible patients attended the oncology unit; recruitment continued until thematic redundancy was achieved at participant 20. This sample size is consistent with phenomenological research, which prioritizes depth of understanding over statistical generalization.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews conducted between August and October 2025 by the second author, who is a registered nurse under the supervision of the first author, who is a professor of nursing with experience in oncology. Interviews lasted 45–60 min each and were guided by an interview schedule based on IPM dimensions, including open-ended questions such as: “How did you first perceive your breast cancer diagnosis?” “What do you believe caused your illness?” and “How has your understanding of the disease changed over time?” Probes were used to explore spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, and biomedical perceptions. Interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim. Field notes were taken to capture non-verbal cues and contextual details. Translation from Yoruba to English was performed by a bilingual research assistant, with back-translation for accuracy. Data collection ceased at saturation, ensuring comprehensive coverage of experiences.

To enhance transparency, an outline of the semi-structured interview guide based on the dimensions of Leventhal’s Illness Perception Model (IPM) is provided below: (1) Illness identity – perceptions of symptoms and diagnosis; (2) Cause – beliefs about spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, or biomedical causes; (3) Timeline – perceptions about the duration and progression of the illness; (4) Consequences – perceived physical, emotional, and social impacts; and (5) Controllability/Cure – beliefs regarding treatment, faith, and recovery. This structured guide ensured comprehensive exploration of illness perceptions while allowing flexibility for participants lived experiences.

Methodological orientation and analytic process

To address the methodological coherence of the study, we explicitly outline how phenomenology, thematic analysis, and the CSM-SR framework were integrated. First, the phenomenological orientation guided the overall research stance, emphasizing the exploration of participants’ lived experiences and the meaning they attribute to their illness. Second, reflexive thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2006) was selected as the analytic method because it allows for flexible, iterative identification of patterns across narratives while remaining grounded in participant data. Third, the CSM-SR was employed as a theoretical sensitizing framework—used to inform the development of the interview guide and to provide an initial interpretive lens, but not to impose a priori coding categories. The analytic process proceeded as follows: (1) familiarization through repeated reading of transcripts; (2) inductive open coding of participant narratives without reference to the CSM-SR; (3) initial theme development based on emergent patterns; (4) reflexive discussions among the research team to examine how CSM-SR dimensions (e.g., cause, timeline, consequences, controllability, emotional representations) might help organize and interpret the emergent themes without forcing data into pre-existing boxes; and (5) final theme refinement. Reflexivity journals were maintained throughout to document how the researchers’ backgrounds (nursing, oncology experience) and theoretical knowledge influenced interpretation, helping to avoid excessive framework imposition.

Data analysis

Reflexive thematic analysis, as outlined by Braun and Clarke, was employed to identify patterns and themes in the data [20]. Transcripts were read multiple times for familiarization, followed by inductive coding, theme development, and iterative refinement as previously described. NVivo software was used to organize and manage the data.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from BUTH’s Research Ethics Committee (Protocol Number: BUTH/REC-26837). Informed consent was secured from all participants, including consent for audio recording and anonymized data use. Confidentiality was upheld by assigning pseudonyms (e.g., R1-R20) and storing data securely. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw at any time without repercussions. No incentives were provided to avoid coercion. The study adhered to the Helsinki Declaration, ensuring sensitivity to participants’ emotional well-being, with referrals to counseling services if distress arose.

Result

This qualitative phenomenological study explored illness perceptions among 20 breast cancer patients aged 33 to 76 years at a tertiary hospital in Ogbomoso, Oyo State, Nigeria. Participants represented a range of life stages, with most being married (n = 15), followed by widowed (n = 3), divorced (n = 1), and single (n = 1). Half (n = 10) were diagnosed with advanced stages (III or IV), while seven had stage II breast cancer, and three had stage I. Thematic analysis of in-depth interviews revealed four overarching themes and one evolving perception, illustrating how patients’ lived experiences shaped their understanding of breast cancer as intertwined spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, and biomedical phenomena. Initial perceptions were often fatalistic, but prolonged engagement with treatment and support fostered resilience and reframing.

Theme 1: illness as a spiritual test or act of god (IPM dimension: cause and controllability)

Many participants viewed breast cancer as a divine trial or purposeful act, integrating faith into coping and meaning-making. This perception mitigated fear and promoted emotional resilience, with participants seeing the illness as an opportunity for spiritual growth rather than punishment. For instance, one woman stated, “I believe this illness is a test from God to strengthen my faith and patience, and whenever fear comes, I just pray and ask God for mercy and healing” (R1). Another reflected, “I believe my illness is a way God is testing my faith, not a punishment, but something meant to make me stronger and depend on Him” (R17). Even amid uncertainty, faith anchored hope, as a widow noted, “The worst that can happen is death and I have made peace with God already, so I just want to enjoy my grandchildren” (R2). A younger participant echoed this sentiment, emphasizing holistic healing: “Faith has been my anchor, because healing is not only about the body but also the mind and spirit. My pastor prays with me every week, and that gives me strength to go for my chemotherapy” (R12). This spiritual lens helped women endure physical changes and maintain optimism. Notably, for some participants, faith did not replace biomedical treatment but complemented it: “I pray, but I also take my drugs. God uses the doctors to heal” (R8).

Theme 2: illness attributed to stress, lifestyle, and modern living (IPM dimension: cause)

Participants often linked breast cancer to everyday stressors, overwork, poor diets, and modern lifestyle changes, reflecting socio-economic realities. This attribution fostered personal accountability and regret, prompting lifestyle adjustments. One participant explained, “I think my cancer came from stress at work because I overworked myself for years without rest” (R6), while another connected it to hormonal imbalances from long hours: “I believe stress and hormonal imbalance caused mine because I used to sew day and night and barely slept” (R14). Dietary factors were prominent, with concerns about processed foods: “The kind of oil and food we eat nowadays is not natural anymore, and I strongly believe it plays a big role in sickness like this” (R4). A businesswoman added, “Skipping meals, poor eating habits, and stress from business contributed to my illness” (R18). Another participant stated, “I think it is because I worked too hard as a farmer, carrying heavy loads. My body just broke down” (R20). These perceptions highlighted how patients contextualized illness within broader societal shifts, often leading to renewed health commitments.

Theme 3: illness as hereditary or biologically predetermined (ipm dimension: cause and consequences)

Some women perceived breast cancer as genetically inherited, especially those with family histories, intensifying fears for relatives while encouraging vigilance. This view coexisted with spiritual beliefs, blending biomedical and faith-based explanations. A participant asserted, “I strongly believe my cancer is hereditary because my mother had the same condition” (R10), and a younger woman recalled, “My aunt died from it years ago, so when I was diagnosed, I thought my world was ending” (R12). Even without confirmed history, genetics were acknowledged: “Cancer can be caused by both lifestyle and genetics, and some things are beyond human control” (R7). A farmer integrated faith: “Since my aunt had cancer too, I believe it may be hereditary, but I still trust that God knows best” (R20). Another participant expressed fear for her daughters: “I look at my daughters and I worry. I pray it does not happen to them. I have told them to check their breasts always” (R5). This perception underscored intergenerational concerns and acceptance.

Theme 4: illness initially perceived as a death sentence or source of fear (ipm dimension: consequences and timeline)

At diagnosis, breast cancer evoked terror and fatalism, shaped by cultural narratives and limited awareness, leading to emotional distress. Over time, however, perceptions evolved through support and treatment. One woman described initial shock: “When I first heard breast cancer, my heart sank because I thought cancer was a death sentence” (R11). Another noted age-related surprise: “I used to think it was an old woman’s disease, so I was terrified and couldn’t believe it was happening to me” (R12). Fear persisted in quiet moments: “Sometimes at night I feel afraid when I start thinking deeply about the future and my family” (R1). Yet, reframing occurred: “Cancer is not a death sentence, and with proper care one can still live well” (R3), illustrating dynamic perceptions.

Unlike Theme 1, where faith primarily functioned as a meaning-making and coping resource, fear in this theme reflects initial illness appraisal and perceived severity before coping mechanisms were fully activated. Over time, however, faith and social support contributed to reframing these fears.

Contradictory and coexisting beliefs

While the themes above are presented separately for analytical clarity, it is important to note that participants frequently held multiple, sometimes contradictory, beliefs simultaneously. For example, R10, who strongly endorsed hereditary causation (“I strongly believe my cancer is hereditary because my mother had the same condition”), also stated in the same interview: “But ultimately, nothing happens without God’s knowledge. If He says I will live, I will live.” Similarly, R4, who robustly rejected spiritual causation in favor of lifestyle factors (“The kind of oil and food we eat nowadays is not natural anymore… I don’t think God gives people cancer”), nevertheless attended church services regularly and prayed before each chemotherapy session. These coexisting explanations were not experienced as contradictory by participants but rather as layered and context-dependent, with different explanatory models activated in different settings (clinical versus home or spiritual environments).

Evolving perception: illness as a manageable condition requiring medical and emotional support (IPM dimension: controllability and timeline)

With treatment progression, many reframed breast cancer as controllable, emphasizing early detection, adherence, and support. This shift promoted active coping and trust in healthcare. Participants stressed screening: “Early detection is very important, people should not ignore signs but go for screening” (R3), and redefined life: “Breast cancer doesn’t mean a woman’s life has ended, we just need compassion, encouragement, and timely medical attention” (R11). Adherence was key: “I follow my treatment plan and take my medicine as prescribed because that is what keeps me going” (R1). Emotional support was vital: “Love and encouragement help recovery more than medicine alone” (R18). Another participant reflected on her transformation: “At first, I could not even say the word ‘cancer.’ Now, I tell other women in my church to go for check-ups. I have accepted it and I am managing it” (R9). This perception reflected adaptation, resilience, and a move from catastrophe to meaningful living.

Overall, these themes reveal breast cancer as a multifaceted experience, evolving from fear to empowerment through spiritual, social, and medical lenses, profoundly influencing coping and well-being.

Discussion

This qualitative phenomenological study explored illness perceptions among breast cancer patients in a tertiary hospital in Ogbomoso, Nigeria, revealing how women construct meaning around their diagnosis within a socio-cultural context shaped by spirituality, economic realities, and evolving biomedical engagement. Drawing on Leventhal’s Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation (CSM-SR), the findings extend beyond descriptive categorization by demonstrating how cognitive and emotional representations of illness dynamically interact over time, shifting from initial fear and fatalism to adaptive coping and perceived controllability.

Importantly, this study contributes to the literature by illustrating how multiple explanatory models spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, and biomedical coexist rather than compete, forming a layered and context-specific illness representation. As shown in the contradictory beliefs subsection, participants fluidly moved between explanatory frameworks depending on context, suggesting that illness representations are not static cognitive structures but are dynamically negotiated in response to social, clinical, and spiritual environments. This integrative pattern is less explicitly articulated in prior Sub-Saharan African studies, which often emphasize singular dominant explanatory beliefs.

This contribution also extends recent qualitative evidence from Nigeria reported by Asuquo et al. [21], which primarily explored women’s experiences across the breast cancer care continuum, including financial hardship, psychosocial distress, coping strategies, and supportive care needs. While both studies highlight the importance of spirituality, family support, and engagement with healthcare services, our study advances the literature by specifically examining how illness perceptions evolve over time and how spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, and biomedical explanations are dynamically integrated within patients’ cognitive and emotional representations of illness using the Common-Sense Model of Self-Regulation. This theoretical perspective provides additional insight into the processes through which women reinterpret their illness throughout diagnosis and treatment.

Spiritual and faith-based perceptions

The perception of breast cancer as a spiritual test or divine act reflects a deeply embedded cultural framework through which patients interpret illness [16, 22]. While this aligns with studies across Sub-Saharan Africa, our findings suggest a more nuanced role of faith not merely as a passive explanatory belief, but as an active coping resource that reshapes emotional representations (fear, anxiety) into hope and acceptance [23].

Within the CSM-SR framework, this reflects a transformation in both “cause” and “emotional representation” dimensions, where spiritual beliefs simultaneously explain illness and regulate emotional distress [24]. Crucially, participants did not view faith as an alternative to medical treatment but as complementary, consistent with R8’s statement: “I pray, but I also take my drugs. God uses the doctors to heal.” These findings challenge assumptions that spirituality necessarily impedes biomedical engagement.

Comparatively, studies in Uganda often report more rigid fatalistic interpretations that delay care-seeking, whereas South African studies demonstrate a gradual shift toward biomedical alignment with structured psychosocial support [25, 26]. In contrast, this study highlights a hybrid model in the Nigerian context, where faith and biomedical engagement are not mutually exclusive but interact synergistically over time [27].

This finding extends current understanding within the Nigerian context by showing that faith does not inherently hinder care but can facilitate resilience and treatment adherence when appropriately integrated into care delivery [28, 29].

Lifestyle and stress-related attributions

Participants’ attribution of breast cancer to stress, overwork, and modern lifestyle reflects an attempt to construct causal explanations within familiar socio-economic realities. This illustrates how patients draw on “available narratives” within their environment to make illness intelligible, even when these explanations are only partially aligned with biomedical evidence [30, 31].

Within the CSM-SR, this aligns with the “cause” dimension, but our findings further show that such attributions carry emotional consequences, particularly guilt and self-blame, which may negatively affect psychological well-being [24]. For example, R6 expressed regret: “I overworked myself for years without rest. If only I had rested more.” Healthcare providers should be alert to these self-blame narratives, which may compound distress.

Compared to other Sub-Saharan African studies that emphasize supernatural causation, the prominence of lifestyle explanations in this study suggests a transitional epistemological space, where biomedical awareness is emerging but remains intertwined with socio-economic interpretations. This highlights the need for context-sensitive health education that corrects misconceptions without dismissing patients’ lived realities, particularly in settings where structural factors such as poverty and work demands limit individual agency [32, 33].

These interpretations do not occur in isolation but interact with other belief systems, including hereditary and spiritual explanations, shaping a multidimensional understanding of illness [34].

Hereditary and biological perceptions

The perception of breast cancer as hereditary reflects increasing awareness of biomedical explanations, particularly among patients with family histories [35]. However, rather than replacing spiritual beliefs, genetic explanations coexisted with faith-based interpretations, illustrating a pluralistic understanding of causation [36, 37]. This coexistence is significant because it demonstrates that biomedical knowledge is not simply adopted but is integrated into existing belief systems, consistent with interpretive phenomenological perspectives on meaning-making [38–40].

Compared to findings from Uganda, where hereditary beliefs are often associated with inevitability and helplessness, participants in this study demonstrated a more agentic response, combining vigilance with faith and medical engagement [41, 42]. R5’s concern for her daughters (“I look at my daughters and I worry”) illustrates how hereditary perceptions translate into protective behaviors, including encouraging breast self-examination among family members. This suggests that increasing genetic awareness in Nigeria may support proactive health behaviors, provided it is accompanied by appropriate counseling to mitigate anxiety and fatalism [43].

Initial fatalism and fear

Initial perceptions of breast cancer as a “death sentence” reflect deeply rooted cultural narratives of cancer as incurable [44]. Within the CSM-SR, this corresponds to heightened “consequence” and “emotional representation” dimensions, characterized by fear, uncertainty, and perceived loss of control [24]. However, a key contribution of this study lies in demonstrating that these perceptions are not static but evolve significantly with treatment experience, social support, and counseling [45]. The shift from “I thought cancer was a death sentence” (R11) to “Cancer is not a death sentence, and with proper care one can still live well” (R3) within the same individual over the course of treatment illustrates this dynamic transformation. While studies in Uganda report more persistent fatalism, and South African studies document gradual shifts with structured interventions, our findings highlight a relatively rapid reappraisal process among participants actively engaged in care [46]. This underscores the importance of early psychosocial intervention at the point of diagnosis, where illness perceptions are most vulnerable to negative framing [47].

The dynamic interplay of cognitive and emotional representations

A distinctive contribution of this study is the explicit mapping of the shift from initial fatalism to perceived manageability onto the CSM-SR dimensions. At diagnosis, participants exhibited high threat appraisal across “consequences” (belief that cancer would end life or severely disable), “timeline” (perception of acute, rapid decline), and “emotional representations” (terror, hopelessness). With treatment engagement, social support, and faith-based coping, participants reconfigured their representations: “controllability” strengthened (belief that treatment and adherence matter), “timeline” shifted from acute to chronic, and “emotional representations” transformed from fear to hope and agency. This dynamic reconfiguration aligns with the CSM-SR’s proposition that illness representations are not fixed but are updated through ongoing experience and coping feedback loops [24].

Evolving to a manageable condition

Over time, participants reframed breast cancer as a manageable condition, emphasizing adherence, early detection, and emotional support. This shift reflects a reconfiguration of “timeline” and “controllability” dimensions within the CSM-SR, where illness moves from an acute catastrophic event to a chronic but manageable condition [24]. Notably, this transition was not solely driven by medical treatment but was co-constructed through social support, faith, and increased understanding of the disease [48]. Compared to other Sub-Saharan African contexts where fatalism may persist, this finding suggests that sustained engagement with healthcare systems can significantly reshape illness perceptions, even in resource-constrained settings [49].

This dynamic transformation represents a key contribution of the study, highlighting the potential for targeted interventions to shift perceptions toward resilience and agency. Unlike previous qualitative work that has largely focused on supportive care experiences and health system challenges among women living with breast cancer in Nigeria [21], the present study provides a theory-informed understanding of how illness perceptions are continually reconstructed throughout the treatment journey. By demonstrating that multiple explanatory beliefs coexist and change over time rather than being mutually exclusive, the findings contribute a nuanced perspective that may inform future psychosocial and patient education interventions in comparable settings. This study contributes to existing literature by demonstrating how illness perceptions among Nigerian breast cancer patients are not singular or static, but are dynamically constructed through the interaction of spiritual, socio-economic, and biomedical belief systems over time [14, 50].

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strengths lie in its ability to provide an in-depth understanding of the complex, dynamic nature of illness perceptions among Nigerian breast cancer patients. The phenomenological approach allowed for a nuanced exploration of the participants’ experiences, highlighting the interplay between spiritual, lifestyle, and biomedical elements. The explicit integration of the CSM-SR as a sensitizing framework, while preserving inductive openness, strengthens the theoretical grounding of the analysis. The identification of contradictory and coexisting beliefs adds analytical depth beyond descriptive reporting.

However, limitations involve the small, hospital-based sample, potentially limiting generalizability to non-tertiary settings or to patients who do not seek treatment in formal healthcare facilities. Self-reported data may include recall bias, and the study’s qualitative nature precludes causal inferences. The study’s small sample size and hospital-based setting may not capture diverse perspectives or experiences. Future studies could use mixed methods for broader applicability. Additionally, the study excluded individuals who did not seek treatment or who refused treatment, which may have led to underrepresentation of more negative or fatalistic illness perceptions. This could result in a partial view of illness perceptions, particularly among individuals with limited engagement with formal healthcare systems.

To address these limitations, future studies could employ mixed-methods designs, combining qualitative insights with quantitative data to enhance generalizability and applicability. Larger, more diverse samples could provide a broader understanding of illness perceptions across different populations and settings. Longitudinal studies could also explore changes in illness perceptions over time, providing insights into the dynamic nature of these perceptions.

Conclusion

This study provides valuable insights into the complex and dynamic nature of illness perceptions among Nigerian breast cancer patients. The findings highlight the shift from initial fatalism to a more manageable view of breast cancer, facilitated by medical adherence, emotional support, and early detection. The study demonstrates that spiritual, lifestyle, hereditary, and biomedical explanations coexist in a layered, context-dependent manner, and that illness perceptions evolve significantly over the treatment trajectory. These findings reinforce the importance of integrating psychosocial, cultural, and biomedical approaches in breast cancer care within Nigerian and similar Sub-Saharan African contexts. The study’s findings have significant implications for practice and policy, emphasizing the need for multidisciplinary, patient-centred care that integrates biomedical treatment with psychosocial support, culturally sensitive patient education, and appropriate spiritual care alongside community-based awareness campaigns. By addressing the psychological, social, and cultural aspects of breast cancer, healthcare providers can improve patient outcomes and quality of life.
