Decade of Risk: Predicting Ten-year Cardiovascular Disease Events (Stroke/myocardial Infarction) in Hypertensive Patients at a Tertiary Care Institute in Jharkhand
Authors: Sudip Bhattacharya, Manuj Kumar Sarkar, Manish Goyal, Vanisree Ramanathan, Abha Kumari, Soumi Kundu, Alok Singh, G. Jahnavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) disproportionately impact low- and middle-income countries. In India, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) lead in mortality and disability, driven by tobacco use, poor diet, and inactivity. Large-scale prevention remains limited.Aim:This study aims to identify CVD risk factors and estimate the 10-year risk of stroke or myocardial infarction (MI) among hypertensive patients attending the Medicine outpatient department (OPD) at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Deoghar, India.Method:This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Medicine OPD of AIIMS Deoghar, Jharkhand, from February to June 2024, including 365 hypertensive patients aged ≥30 years selected through purposive sampling. Eligibility criteria and recruitment procedures were predefined. Data were collected using a pre-tested structured questionnaire covering sociodemographic, clinical, and behavioral factors. Blood pressure was measured using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer under standard conditions, and laboratory investigations (random blood sugar [RBS] and lipid profile) were performed as per hospital protocols. The primary outcome was the 10-year CVD risk estimated using QRISK 3, with relevant variables systematically recorded. The 10-year CVD risk was estimated using the QRISK3, a validated risk prediction tool originally developed in the United Kingdom. It calculates individual risk of developing CVD (including MI and stroke) based on multiple variables such as age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio, and comorbid conditions (e.g., diabetes, chronic kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, and rheumatoid arthritis). The algorithm provides an absolute 10-year risk percentage to guide cardiovascular risk stratification and preventive decision-making. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± SD and categorical variables as proportions. Associations were assessed using Chi-square and Pearson’s correlation. A P value < 0.05 was considered significant. Bias was minimized through standardized measurements and predefined criteria. Ethical approval was obtained from Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) and Institutional Review Committee (IRC), AIIMS Deoghar, and written informed consent was taken from all participants.Results:The study included 57.8% male and 42.2% female participants. Smoking prevalence was high (88.8%), and 22.7% of participants had diabetes. Uncontrolled hypertension was present in 21.6%, with 20.8% receiving treatment. The mean 10-year QRISK3 score was 11.67 (SD = 11.16), with a relative risk mean of 1.53 (SD = 1.01). The mean QRISK3 Healthy Heart Age was 58.68 years (SD = 12.44), indicating a substantial deviation from optimal cardiovascular health. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between age, systolic blood pressure, and RBS with CVD risk scores.Conclusion:Among hypertensive patients attending a tertiary-care center in Jharkhand, the mean 10-year QRISK3-estimated cardiovascular event risk was 11.67%, indicating a moderate-risk profile. Age, systolic blood pressure, glycemic status, and lipid abnormalities were the major contributors to risk. These findings highlight the need for routine cardiovascular risk assessment, optimal blood pressure control, smoking cessation, and improved diabetes management to reduce future stroke and MI risk.

INTRODUCTION

The growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs) is a major global health concern, with low-income countries disproportionately affected.[1] In South-East Asia, NCDs account for over half of all deaths, and in India, cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are projected to become the leading cause of mortality and disability. Despite global efforts to combat NCDs, large-scale community-based interventions remain limited in India.[2]

NCDs are the leading cause of mortality globally, accounting for approximately 74% of all deaths worldwide, with over 43 million deaths reported annually.[3] WHO estimates a 15% increase in NCD deaths between 2010 and 2020, reaching 44 million annually. In India, CVDs account for nearly 25% of all deaths, with a higher mortality rate than the global average.[4] Behavioral risk factors like tobacco use, poor diet, and inactivity contribute to most CVD cases, yet many deaths remain preventable.[5]

Epidemiological research has been instrumental in identifying NCD risk factors and evaluating screening, diagnosis, and treatment methods. While traditional studies assess disease risk through relative measures, predicting individual risk remains complex. Models like the Framingham Risk Score,[6] QRISK3,[7] and WHO/ISH risk charts[8] estimate absolute disease risk, aiding in personalized treatment planning.

The Ottawa Charter advocates for a settings-based approach to health promotion, emphasizing localized interventions. Implementing CVD prevention at All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Deoghar, aligns with this principle. Tertiary care hospitals offer a strategic setting for surveillance and preventive measures, highlighting the need for baseline data to guide effective CVD risk reduction initiatives.

METHODOLOGY

This cross-sectional study was conducted in the Medicine OPD of AIIMS, Deoghar, India, from February 2024 to June 2024, with a sample of 365 hypertensive patients selected through purposive sampling. The study included a mixed cohort of participants, comprising both hospital employees (e.g., doctors, nurses, and support staff) and non-employee patients attending the Medicine OPD. As no prior local data on mean QRISK3 scores among hypertensive patients were available, a pragmatic sample size of 365 participants was recruited during the study period to ensure adequate precision for estimation and subgroup analyses. A purposive sampling strategy was adopted to specifically recruit hypertensive patients fulfilling predefined eligibility criteria and possessing all variables required for QRISK3 estimation. Although recruitment occurred among attendees of a tertiary-care OPD, participant selection was based on the availability of complete risk-factor data rather than simple convenience. The study aimed to estimate the 10-year CVD risk using QRISK3. QRISK3 is a CVD risk prediction model designed for use in the UK population. It estimates the 10-year risk of developing CVD based on multiple demographic, clinical, and lifestyle variables. The model incorporates traditional risk factors such as age, sex, smoking status, systolic blood pressure, total cholesterol/high-density lipoprotein (HDL) ratio, and medical history (e.g., diabetes, atrial fibrillation, chronic kidney disease). Additionally, QRISK3 accounts for social determinants of health, including the Townsend deprivation score, which reflects socioeconomic status. It has been validated in large cohorts and is recommended for clinical use to guide preventive interventions.[9]

Data were collected from registered hypertensive patients undergoing routine investigations such as random blood sugar (RBS) and lipid profile testing. We recorded diabetes status based on patient history and available clinical records. In the absence of HbA1c values, we used random blood glucose measurements as a surrogate indicator of glycemic status. Sociodemographic variables, including age, gender, and occupation, along with medical history related to diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption, rheumatoid arthritis, chronic kidney disease, and rhythm abnormalities, were recorded. We defined “smoker” as current tobacco use at the time of assessment, based on self-report. We did not classify participants by smoking intensity (e.g., pack-years) or distinguish between former and never smokers. Blood pressure and RBS levels were measured using a mercury sphygmomanometer and a glucometer, respectively, while lipid profiles were assessed, and newly diagnosed cases were referred for further evaluation. We measured blood pressure using a calibrated mercury sphygmomanometer following standard guidelines. We recorded two readings at an interval of at least 5 min after ensuring the participant had rested in a seated position for at least 5 min. We supported the arm at heart level and used an appropriately sized cuff. We took measurements in the right arm for all participants unless contraindicated. We recorded the average of the two readings for analysis. We regularly calibrated the device as per institutional protocol to ensure accuracy. We measured lipid profiles under fasting conditions as per routine clinical practice. We analyzed serum total cholesterol, HDL, and LDL levels using standard enzymatic methods in the central laboratory of AIIMS Deoghar. We conducted all assays using automated analyzers following manufacturer protocols. The laboratory participates in internal quality control procedures and external quality assurance programs, and ensuring accuracy of test results. The inclusion criteria encompassed patients aged over 30 years, willing to participate, diagnosed or newly identified hypertensive patients, and those undergoing routine hypertension investigations, while exclusions applied to patients below 30 years, seriously ill individuals, and those with hypertensive complications. Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS (version 22), with nominal data expressed as proportions and continuous variables presented as mean and standard deviation. Categorical variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. To minimize bias, we implemented clearly defined inclusion and exclusion criteria, utilized the standardized and validated QRISK3 risk prediction model, and followed a systematic and objective data collection process. This study follows the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational research to ensure transparency and completeness.[10]

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) and Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) at AIIMS, Deoghar, with permissions from the Departments of Community Medicine and Internal Medicine, and informed written consent was secured from all participants.

RESULTS

All participants in the study were hypertensive by design. Among them 21.6% had uncontrolled blood pressure at the time of assessment, while 20.8% were on antihypertensive treatment. Participants were categorized based on occupational groups rather than institutional roles. A proportion of participants were hospital employees (doctors and nurses), while the majority belonged to non-healthcare occupational categories (“others”), representing the general patient population. Among the 365 participants, 57.8% were male and 42.2% were female. Regarding education levels, 53.2% had completed primary education, while 19.2% had reached the middle school level, 20.0% had secondary education, 4.9% were graduates, and 2.7% had postgraduate qualifications, with no participants having postdoctoral education. In terms of staff categories, 17.8% were doctors, 12.3% were nurses, 1.1% were HA/SA, and 68.7% belonged to other categories [Table 1].

A high proportion of participants (88.8%) reported current smoking status. Although non-smokers demonstrated higher mean QRISK®3 scores compared to smokers. The apparently higher QRISK3 scores among non-smokers disappeared after age adjustment, suggesting confounding by age. A family history of angina or heart attack in a first-degree relative under 60 years of age was reported by 3.0% of participants. A history of rhythm abnormality or atrial fibrillation was noted in 0.8%, while 2.2% had rheumatoid arthritis and 3.0% reported chronic kidney disease. Hypertension was present in 21.6% of the participants (uncontrolled), with 20.8% receiving treatment for high blood pressure.

Diabetes was reported by 22.7% of participants, with 12.2% on statin therapy. Aspirin use was low, with only 2.8% of respondents reporting usage. The use of atypical antipsychotic medication was noted in 0.6% of participants, while none reported regular steroid tablet use, severe mental illness, or treatment for erectile dysfunction. Only 0.3% of respondents reported experiencing migraines.

The mean 10-year QRISK® score of 11.67 (SD = 11.16) places the study population in the moderate-risk category (10–20%), indicating a considerable proportion of participants at elevated cardiovascular risk. In comparison, the mean risk score for a healthy individual with similar demographic characteristics was 8.55 (SD = 8.38) that falls within the low-risk category (<10%), suggesting a higher-than-expected risk burden in the study population.

The mean relative risk of 1.53 (SD = 1.01) indicates a moderate increase in cardiovascular risk (>1.5) compared to healthy counterparts. Similarly, the mean QRISK® Healthy Heart Age (58.68 ± 12.44 years) exceeded the participants’ chronological mean age, reflecting borderline to accelerated cardiovascular ageing.

Furthermore, the mean lifetime atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk of 33.56% (SD = 16.55) falls within the high-risk category (>20%), while the optimal ASCVD risk (1.90 ± 2.13) remains in the low-risk range (<7.5%) [Table 2].

Correlation analysis using Karl Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) demonstrated that age had a strong and statistically significant positive correlation with the 10-year QRISK®3 score (r = 0.767, P < 0.001), the healthy comparator score (r = 0.906, P < 0.001), QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age (r = 0.893, P < 0.001), and Optimal ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.776, P < 0.001), indicating a consistent increase in cardiovascular risk with advancing age. In contrast, relative risk showed a weak but significant negative correlation with age (r = −0.188, P = 0.001). Lifetime ASCVD Risk% did not show a significant association with age (r = −0.020, P = 0.787).

Among blood pressure parameters, systolic blood pressure demonstrated significant positive correlations with the 10-year QRISK®3 score (r = 0.343), relative risk (r = 0.362), QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age (r = 0.338), and Lifetime ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.460), all P < 0.001, highlighting its strong association with cardiovascular risk. Diastolic blood pressure showed significant positive correlations only with Lifetime ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.347, P < 0.001) and Optimal ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.173, P = 0.020), while associations with other parameters were not statistically significant.

Anthropometric variables showed limited associations. Height demonstrated weak, but significant positive correlations with the 10-year QRISK®3 score (r = 0.180, P = 0.001), the healthy comparator score (r = 0.125, P = 0.022), and Optimal ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.181, P = 0.016). In contrast, weight and BMI were not significantly associated with most cardiovascular risk parameters.

RBS showed significant positive correlations with the 10-year QRISK®3 score (r = 0.237, P < 0.001), relative risk (r = 0.185, P = 0.001), and QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age (r = 0.205, P < 0.001), indicating the influence of glycemic status on cardiovascular risk.

Among lipid parameters, total cholesterol (r = 0.550, P < 0.001) and LDL (r = 0.421, P < 0.001) showed strong positive correlations with Lifetime ASCVD Risk%, while HDL also showed a modest but significant positive correlation (r = 0.188, P = 0.012). Additionally, the cholesterol/HDL ratio demonstrated significant positive associations with relative risk (r = 0.126, P = 0.022) and Lifetime ASCVD Risk% (r = 0.291, P < 0.001). [Table 3]

Males had significant mean scores across all risk assessment parameters compared to females, except for relative risk that did show a significant difference between the male and female. Among educational levels, no significant differences were observed in any of the risk scores.

Smoking status influenced several risk scores, with non-smokers showing significantly higher 10-year QRISK®3 scores, relative risk, and QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age. However, no significant differences were found in cardiovascular risk scores based on a history of angina. Patients with a history of rhythm abnormalities or atrial fibrillation had significantly higher 10-year QRISK®3 scores and QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age. Conversely, rheumatoid arthritis was not associated with significant differences in risk scores. Chronic kidney disease was linked to significantly higher values in all risk parameters except for the score of a healthy person with the same age, sex, and ethnicity, which remained unaffected.

Hypertension and blood pressure treatment were associated with significantly higher risk scores across all parameters except Optimal ASCVD Risk%. Diabetes also resulted in significantly higher 10-year QRISK®3 scores, relative risk, and QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age. Patients on statins had significantly higher risk scores except for the score of a healthy person with the same age, sex, and ethnicity and Optimal ASCVD Risk%. Those taking aspirin showed significantly higher 10-year QRISK®3 scores and QRISK®3 Healthy Heart Age. Patients on atypical antipsychotic medication had a significantly higher 10-year QRISK®3 score, whereas no significant differences were observed in any risk score between individuals with or without migraines.

Relative risk exhibited a significant positive correlation with age, systolic blood pressure, and RBS, though no significant relationship was found with the cholesterol/HDL ratio.

Optimal ASCVD Risk% also showed a significant positive association with age and height, but not with diastolic blood pressure. A one-unit increase in age and height led to increases of 0.249 and 0.066 units in Optimal ASCVD Risk%, respectively, while the effect of diastolic blood pressure was not statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The study provides a comprehensive analysis of cardiovascular risk factors among a diverse cohort of participants, offering valuable insights into the relationships between demographic, clinical, and lifestyle variables. However, several key observations and limitations must be critically examined. The study population exhibits a high prevalence of smoking (88.8%) that is an alarming finding given its well-established association with CVD. This underscores the need for targeted smoking cessation interventions within the study group. Similar findings were revealed from a study which concluded that Current smoking significantly elevates the risk of nearly all CVD subtypes, at least doubling the likelihood of conditions such as acute myocardial infarction (AMI), cerebrovascular disease, and heart failure.[11] The significant proportion of participants with uncontrolled hypertension (21.6%) and diabetes (22.7%) further highlights the substantial burden of CVD risk factors within this population. The relatively low usage of aspirin (2.8%) and statin therapy (12.2%) raises concerns about the adequacy of preventive measures among high-risk individuals. A similar finding was observed in USA, despite a significant rise in aspirin use among patients with coronary artery disease, its utilization remains insufficient.[12] Given the known benefits of these medications in reducing cardiovascular events, further investigation into barriers to their use is warranted.

The results indicate significant differences in cardiovascular risk scores based on demographic and clinical variables. Males exhibited higher mean scores across all risk parameters except for relative risk, reflecting a potential gender disparity in CVD risk. A similar finding was observed in a study. The study highlights the variability among recalibrated models using different datasets from the same country.[13] Despite this, the lack of significant differences in risk scores across educational levels suggests that other factors, such as occupational and lifestyle characteristics, may play a more prominent role in influencing cardiovascular risk. A substantial gap between observed and optimal risk underscores the need for targeted preventive interventions in this population.[14]

The correlation analysis provides robust evidence of the impact of traditional CVD risk factors on cardiovascular prediction models. The strong positive correlations between age and multiple risk prediction parameters are consistent with the well-established notion that aging is a primary determinant of CVD risk.[1516] The positive association between systolic blood pressure and QRISK3 estimates observed in our study is consistent with evidence demonstrating that even modest increases in systolic blood pressure substantially increase future cardiovascular and renal risk. Wan et al. reported a graded increase in cardiovascular outcomes across higher blood pressure categories among hypertensive patients, while Clark et al. demonstrated that blood pressure-related vascular risk remains a strong predictor of adverse cardiovascular outcomes.[15161718] The observed association between height and QRISK3 scores is expected because height forms part of the QRISK3 algorithm and therefore should not be interpreted as an independent epidemiological finding. The association between RBS levels and increased risk scores further supports the importance of glycemic control in cardiovascular prevention.[1920] The observed high prevalence of smoking in this cohort (88.8%) is considerably higher than that reported in most hypertensive outpatient populations and may reflect selection patterns, reporting bias, or clustering of high-risk individuals in a tertiary-care setting. Given that smoking is a major component of the QRISK3 algorithm, this distribution may have contributed to elevated cardiovascular risk estimates. Interestingly, non-smokers demonstrated higher mean QRISK3 scores than smokers. This apparently paradoxical finding is likely attributable to residual confounding, particularly by age, which is a dominant determinant of QRISK3-derived risk. Older participants were more likely to fall into the non-smoking category, thereby inflating their calculated cardiovascular risk despite the absence of current tobacco use. Future studies incorporating age-adjusted analyses or multivariable modelling may better elucidate the independent contribution of smoking to cardiovascular risk.

The interpretation of QRISK3 scores in the present study warrants caution. QRISK3 was originally developed and validated in United Kingdom populations and incorporates the Townsend deprivation index, a socioeconomic measure that lacks a validated equivalent in the Indian setting. Given the disproportionately high burden of premature CVDs among South Asians, QRISK3 may underestimate lifetime cardiovascular risk in younger individuals while potentially overestimating risk in some older adults. Alternative risk prediction tools, including the WHO cardiovascular risk charts and recalibrated Framingham models, may provide complementary risk assessments, and future validation studies are needed to determine the most appropriate cardiovascular risk prediction model for Indian populations.[8]

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. Important determinants of cardiovascular risk, including dietary habits, physical activity, psychosocial stress, and detailed socioeconomic indicators, were not assessed and may have influenced the observed associations. Random blood glucose was used because HbA1c values were unavailable in routine records. Compared with HbA1c, random blood glucose is subject to greater biological variability and may misclassify both diabetic and non-diabetic individuals. Because diabetes status contributes directly to QRISK3 estimation, under-recognition of diabetes could underestimate cardiovascular risk, whereas transient hyperglycemia may overestimate risk. Another important limitation is the use of the QRISK3 algorithm, which was developed and validated in UK populations and may not be fully generalizable to the Indian population because of differences in genetic, environmental, and epidemiological characteristics. Furthermore, QRISK3 incorporates the Townsend deprivation score, for which no validated Indian equivalent exists, potentially affecting risk calibration. Smoking status was self-reported and recorded only as current use without quantification of smoking intensity or duration, introducing the possibility of misclassification bias. Finally, the use of non-probability sampling from a tertiary care outpatient setting may have introduced selection bias and limits the external validity and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the reported QRISK3 estimates should be interpreted with appropriate caution and validated in larger, population-based Indian cohorts.

CONCLUSION

Among hypertensive patients attending a tertiary-care center in Jharkhand, the mean 10-year QRISK3-estimated cardiovascular event risk was 11.67%, placing the cohort in the moderate-risk category. Age, systolic blood pressure, glycemic status, and lipid abnormalities were the principal determinants of elevated risk. The substantial difference between observed and healthy-comparator risk scores suggests a considerable burden of potentially preventable CVD. Routine cardiovascular risk stratification, aggressive blood pressure control, smoking cessation interventions, and improved diabetes management should be integrated into outpatient hypertension care to reduce future stroke and MI risk.
