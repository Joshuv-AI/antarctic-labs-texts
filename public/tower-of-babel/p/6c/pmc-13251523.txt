Pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions: a systematic review
Authors: Jennifer J Park, Bryan B Chen, Marc N Potenza
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn response to the increasing prevalence of online behavioral addictions, researchers have investigated various treatment approaches, including pharmacotherapies. This systematic review aimed to summarize pharmacotherapeutic studies of online behavioral addictions.MethodsPubMed, MEDLINE, and Embase were searched to identify randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental studies of pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions. Studies were excluded if they only targeted offline behavioral addictions. Study findings were summarized, and the Effective Public Health Practice Project Quality Assessment Tool was used for quality assessment.ResultsThis systematic review included 20 studies (1016 participants). Bupropion, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), and opioid receptor antagonists were most frequently studied and showed preliminary promise in reducing symptom severity. Many pharmacotherapeutic studies addressed co-occurring concerns, such as depression.ConclusionsPharmacotherapies for online behavioral addictions appear promising, but the evidence base was limited by small samples, few randomized controlled trials, and short follow-up periods. Given preliminary support for psychotherapy + pharmacotherapy for problematic gaming, future research may evaluate the efficacy of combined treatment for a broader range of online behavioral addictions. Future research should also investigate pharmacotherapies for emerging problematic online behaviors, such as AI-based sexual engagement and problematic cryptocurrency trading.Keywords: Compulsive sexual behavior disorder, gambling, internet addiction disorder, medication, pharmacotherapy, social media, treatment, video games

Introduction

Online behavioral addictions are not a formal category of disorders but may broadly encompass different forms of problematic behaviors that occur online, including problematic online gaming, online gambling, use of social media, and use of pornography. In 2019, the World Health Organization included “gaming disorder, predominantly online” and “gambling disorder, predominantly online” in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11) [1]. According to the ICD-11, gaming disorder and gambling disorder are characterized by impaired control over gaming/gambling, prioritization of gaming/gambling over other activities and interests in life, and continued or escalating gaming/gambling despite negative consequences, as well as functional impairment [1]. The official recognition of these disorders has facilitated increased research into prevalence, prevention, and treatment [2,3]. Although emerging online behavioral addictions (e.g., problematic use of social media) have not been formally recognized in diagnostic nomenclature, their increasing prevalence has reflected a need for further research to improve public health [4].

Around the world, it is estimated that around 17% of individuals experience social media addiction, 14% experience internet addiction [5], and 3–6% experience gaming disorder [5,6]. Online behavioral addictions have been associated with harms that map across neurobiological, social, academic, occupational, and health domains. In children, adolescents, and adults, online behavioral addictions have been associated with differences in functional connectivity (e.g., in cortico-striatal circuits) and brain structure [7,8]. Online behavioral addictions, particularly gaming disorder, have also been associated with depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, alexithymia, sleep concerns, social isolation, and substance use disorders (SUDs) [9–14]. Thus, there has been a growing need to develop and evaluate effective treatment approaches for online behavioral addictions.

Thus far, treatments for online behavioral addictions have been primarily psychological, with some examination of pharmacotherapies [15]. A recent systematic review found support for the efficacy of specific treatment approaches including cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), exercise, and group counseling [15]. However, it also found that existing studies that support this efficacy had notable limitations, including small sample sizes and high heterogeneity of results across studies. To our knowledge, no review has specifically investigated the use of pharmacotherapies for online behavioral addictions, but reviews exist for individual disorders that fall under the umbrella of online behavioral addictions. For example, in 2023, a systematic review of pharmacological treatments specifically targeting gaming disorder found preliminary support for the efficacy of a broad range of medications for symptom reduction, with the most commonly used medications being bupropion, methylphenidate, and various selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors [16]. Furthermore, while there have been several reviews on gaming disorder treatment in general [17–20], there has been a notable lack of treatment-focused reviews on other types of online behavioral addictions. Given these gaps in the literature, the present systematic review aimed to provide an overview of existing studies investigating pharmacotherapies for online behavioral addictions.

Methods

Due to the heterogeneity of the available literature, a systematic review was conducted over a quantitative meta-analysis. Included studies differed in the specific online behaviors, the pharmacological interventions, outcome measures, and follow-up frequency and duration, preventing meaningful quantitative comparison. The present systematic review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21]. This systematic review has been registered on PROSPERO (CRD420261291612, available from https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420261291612).

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that met the following criteria: (i) included a pharmacotherapeutic intervention for online behavioral addictions (the intervention could also be combined with other forms of treatment, such as psychotherapy); (ii) the intervention was intended to reduce the severity, duration, or frequency of an addictive online behavior (the intervention could also simultaneously target co-occurring disorders, such as depression); (iii) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or quasi-experimental studies; (iv) reported a post-intervention outcome on severity, duration, and/or frequency of an addictive online behavior. The following exclusion criteria were used for this systematic review: (i) only targeted offline behavioral addictions (e.g., land-based gambling); (ii) the article was not available in English, (iii) gray literature, and (iv) case studies.

Search strategy

Three databases (PubMed, MEDLINE, and Embase) were searched in August 2025, with an updated search conducted in January 2026. The following search strategy was used: ((disorder* OR problem* OR excessive OR pathological OR compulsive OR addict*) AND (gaming OR “online gambling” OR “internet gambling” OR pornography OR cybersex OR “social media” OR “social networking” OR “online shopping” OR “online buying” OR “internet”)) AND (medication OR medicine OR pharmacotherap* OR pharmacolog* OR drug*). We also conducted a manual search (e.g., searching the reference lists in relevant reviews) to identify additional studies for screening.

Screening

Two researchers used the inclusion/exclusion criteria detailed in Section 2.1. to independently conduct title and abstract screening on Rayyan. The same researchers then independently conducted full-text screening.

Data extraction

Using a structured spreadsheet, the following data were independently extracted from the included studies by two researchers: sample size, participant demographics (i.e., age and sex), study design, inclusion criteria specific to online behavioral addictions, number of participants with an online behavioral addiction, assessment points and measures, intervention components (including any information on targeting co-occurring concerns), intervention duration, comparison conditions, and outcomes related to changes in severity, duration, and frequency of addictive online behaviors.

Quality assessment

Two researchers used the Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) Quality Assessment Tool for Quantitative Studies [22] to independently assess selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection methods, and study attrition in included studies. Each study received a global rating of strong (no “weak” ratings), moderate (one “weak” rating), or weak (two or more “weak” ratings).

Results

Search results and study characteristics

Figure 1 (PRISMA flow diagram) shows that 20 studies were included in this systematic review. The studies included a total of 1016 participants, with sample sizes ranging from 12 to 126. Of the participants, 970 (95.5% on average, range = 37.9% to 100%) were male, with 13 studies including only male participants.

PRISMA flow diagram of article selection.

Of the 20 included studies, seven were RCTs, nine were pre-post studies, two included both a pre-post open-label phase and a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase, one was a non-randomized controlled trial (randomization was not reported), and one study was a non-RCT, naturalistic study. The studies were mostly conducted in Asia (n = 12), followed by the United States (n = 4), and Europe (n = 4). A synthesis of included studies is provided below and in Table 1.

Problematic online gaming

Of the included studies, 10 investigated pharmacological treatments for problematic online gaming. Five studies used bupropion treatment, with all interventions administering 150 mg/day, increased to 300 mg/day after one week. One pre-post study focused on bupropion alone and found significant reductions in both duration and severity of online gaming in the treatment group [23]. An RCT assessed the efficacy of bupropion with weekly education sessions on healthy internet use [26]. Results showed a greater reduction in duration and severity in the bupropion + education group compared to the placebo + education group after eight weeks of treatment, with no change in either group at the twelve-week follow-up. Another RCT compared bupropion with CBT against bupropion with weekly 10-minute interviews to monitor progress [30]. While both groups showed a decrease in symptom severity, combining bupropion with CBT significantly improved outcomes after eight weeks of treatment. However, there were no significant changes in either group at the twelve-week follow-up. Two RCTs compared bupropion with escitalopram (10 mg/day, increased to 20 mg/day after one week), yielding mixed results. Specifically, one study provided pharmacological treatment with education on online gaming and found no difference between intervention with bupropion and escitalopram [24]. On the other hand, another study focused only on pharmacological treatment and found bupropion to yield a significantly greater reduction in severity of internet gaming disorder (IGD) compared to escitalopram [28].

Broadly, the efficacy of selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) as a drug class was investigated by four pre-post studies. Of these studies, three reported average daily doses of escitalopram at 15.8 ± 9.2 mg, fluoxetine at 50.0 ± 9.2 mg, or paroxetine at 30.0 ± 14.1 mg [25,27,29]. These three studies were conducted at the same medical center by the same research group and used similar treatment protocols. It is possible that participants overlapped between studies. The remaining study did not report SSRI dosages [31]. Each of the four studies found SSRIs to yield a significant reduction in severity. Lastly, one RCT focused on problematic online gaming in participants with co-occurring attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) [32]. The study compared the efficacy of a non-stimulant ADHD medication (atomoxetine) to a stimulant (methylphenidate) for reducing IGD severity. Results showed that there were no significant differences between the treatments.

Problematic online gambling

Four studies have investigated pharmacological interventions for problematic online gambling. One focused exclusively on online gambling, while the others focused on both online and offline gambling. One RCT administered one intranasal naloxone spray (4 mg) as needed in response to gambling urges, up to four times a day, in combination with psychosocial support [34]. No significant differences were found between naloxone and placebo groups post-treatment. A pre-post study assessed the efficacy of naltrexone (50 mg; once daily) with weekly CBT sessions for problematic online gambling and found significant reduction in severity after treatment [33]. Another pre-post study investigated memantine for problem gambling [35]. Of the 29 participants, two reported internet gambling as their primary gambling modality. Results showed a significant reduction in hours spent gambling and severity of problem gambling, though results specific to online gambling were not shown. Lastly, a study with an open-label phase (pre-post) and a double-blind, placebo-controlled phase (RCT) investigated N-acetyl cysteine (NAC) as a pharmacologic treatment for problem gambling [37]. Similar to the previous study, the study population focused on problem gambling in general, with three participants reporting internet sports betting as their primary gambling modality. In the open-label phase, 600 mg/day was given for two weeks, increasing to 1200 mg/day for two weeks, then 1800 mg/day for the remainder of the study (eight weeks total), with dosages increasing only if there were no clinical improvements. Significant reductions in problem-gambling severity were found. In the double-blind placebo-controlled phase, a portion of participants were switched to a placebo group. The reductions in problem-gambling severity found in the open-label phase were not sustained in the second phase among participants receiving placebo. Again, no results specific to online gambling were shown.

Problematic online gambling and gaming (co-occurring)

One pre-post study examined bupropion for both IGD and internet-based gambling disorder [36]. Participants were given 150 mg/day bupropion for one week, increasing to 300 mg/day based on tolerability and efficacy. Significant reductions were found in both disorders after twelve weeks of treatment, and no differences were found between groups.

Problematic usage of the internet (PUI)

Two studies investigated treatment of PUI. Escitalopram was investigated in a study with an open-label, pre-post phase in which all participants with “impulsive-compulsive internet usage disorder” were given 10 mg/day, and increased to 20 mg/day after one week [38]. The study found a significant reduction in both duration of internet use and PUI severity following ten weeks of treatment. Then, participants were divided into a treatment group that would continue escitalopram, while placebo was given to the other group. No significant differences in duration of internet use or PUI severity were found between groups in this double-blind-discontinuation phase. Another naturalistic study examined the efficacy of clonazepam + various SSRIs on treating internet addiction with co-occurring obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), finding significant reductions in internet-addiction severity and OCD severity [39].

PUI and problematic online gaming

One non-RCT assessed methylphenidate in participants with co-occurring ADHD and problematic online gaming and/or PUI [40]. Participants were given methylphenidate or treatment-as-usual. The study found no significant difference in severity of problematic gaming or PUI between groups post-treatment, with both groups showing significant reduction in symptoms.

Compulsive online sexual behavior

Two studies investigated pharmacotherapy for compulsive sexual behavior (CSB). A pre-post study assessed naltrexone as a treatment for CSB [41]. Six participants out of twenty specifically reported engagement in cybersex. More participants reported behaviors with possible online components, such as pornography use. While the study found significant reductions in CSB severity post-treatment and at follow-up, results specific to online CSB were not reported. An RCT investigated citalopram, where participants were given 20 mg/day for two weeks with increments of 20 mg every 14 days based on efficacy and tolerability [42]. A maximum dose of 60 mg/day was given. All participants were asked about internet usage for sexual purposes, including hours of internet use for sexual purposes and hours of pornography use (which may occur on the internet). While there were no significant differences in CSB severity or hours of internet use for sexual purposes post-treatment between the citalopram group and placebo group, the citalopram group experienced significantly greater reductions in weekly duration of pornography use.

Quality assessment

As shown in Table 1, most studies (n = 15, 75%) scored “moderate” on the EPHPP tool. Three studies scored “weak” (15%) and two studies scored “strong” (10%).

Discussion

Given the promising nature of pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions and the importance of expanding the currently limited options of empirically supported treatments [43,44], more methodologically rigorous, large-scale clinical trials are needed to establish pharmacological guidelines for online behavioral addictions and recommendations around optimal dosing schedules. Future guidelines could be tailored to specific subtypes of online behavioral addictions, as different behaviors (e.g., online gaming versus online gambling) may involve distinct neurobiological, neurocognitive, and psychosocial mechanisms [45,46]. Currently, no medications have sufficient evidence to support clinical recommendations or have been approved by regulatory agencies for the treatment of online behavioral addictions. This may reflect the relatively recent recognition of online conditions such as gaming disorder (and the lack of specific recognition of problematic use of social media and other emerging addictions) in diagnostic classification systems [1,47] as well as the early stages of pharmacological research in this field, rather than a lack of pharmacotherapeutic potential. Compared with previous reviews of pharmacotherapy for SUDs (e.g., a systematic review of alcohol use disorder identified 118 randomized clinical trials) [48], the present systematic review identified a relatively small number of treatment studies for online behavioral addictions, which highlights the early and emerging nature of this field. To advance evidence in this field, future trials may be guided by the methodological limitations identified in the present systematic review, including small sample sizes and limited longer-term follow-up assessments, control groups, and randomization.

In current clinical practice, pharmacological approaches for online behavioral addictions would be off-label and may be transdiagnostic in the sense that co-occurring concerns could also be targeted (for which indications with regulatory approval could be employed). Such approaches align with findings from this systematic review, which identified that nearly half of the pharmacotherapeutic studies considered co-occurring concerns, including depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD. These studies delivered bupropion (for co-occurring major depressive disorder or depressive symptoms), various types of SSRIs (for co-occurring depressive/anxiety disorders and OCD), atomoxetine (for co-occurring ADHD), or methylphenidate (for co-occurring ADHD), with promising short-term results in reducing severity of gaming disorder and internet addiction and co-occurring concerns. Although online behavioral addictions frequently co-occur with these psychiatric conditions [49–51], it is important to note that they represent distinct clinical concerns capable of manifesting independently rather than as symptoms or consequences of these other disorders [52,53]. However, transdiagnostic approaches to pharmacotherapeutic and psychological treatment may improve the efficiency and cost-effectiveness of treatment delivery and uptake by addressing shared underlying mechanisms (e.g., impulsivity, emotion dysregulation, and reward sensitivity) [54–56].

Across the included studies, the pharmacotherapies that showed promise in reducing the severity of online behavioral addictions or the duration of addictive online activity may be conceptually interpreted through neurobiological models of addictions (e.g., altered reward processing, seemingly habitual/automatic responses to addiction-related cues, and impaired executive control), which have previously been captured in the I-PACE model of behavioral addictions [57–59]. For example, the relatively consistent findings for bupropion (antidepressant) in problematic online gaming may reflect modulation of dopaminergic and noradrenergic signaling within mesocortolimbic pathways implicated in reward sensitivity and cognitive control [60–63], potentially improving top-down regulation of gaming-related urges. Considering previous research reporting altered serotonergic functioning in individuals with behavioral addictions [64], SSRIs may influence problematic online behaviors through serotonergic modulation of frontostriatal circuits involved in impulse control and response inhibition [65]. Additionally, opioid antagonists that have shown promise for problematic online gambling, including naltrexone, may attenuate reward salience and reward system connectivity by reducing endogenous opioid signaling within the ventral striatum [66]. Although these mechanisms may provide a framework linking medication classes to addiction-relevant neurobiological models, the current evidence base for pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions remains preliminary. Future pharmacotherapy trials may benefit from explicitly integrating neurobiological models and considering biomarkers/neuromarkers to help clarify treatment targets in online behavioral addictions. For example, several neuromarkers for severities of problematic gaming and problematic use of social media in youth have been reported in studies using advanced machine learning approaches, including increased or decreased functional connectivity between sensorimotor, salience, frontoparietal, medial frontal, and default mode networks [67,68]. These neuromarkers may inform the development of personalized interventions involving pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions or potentially be used to assign patients to specific medications in the future.

Combined therapy for online behavioral addictions appears to be understudied, with our systematic review only identifying five studies on problematic online gambling/gaming that delivered bupropion or opioid receptor antagonists with CBT (n = 2), education (n = 2), or psychosocial support such as motivational interviewing (n = 1). CBT is the most extensively studied and well-supported treatment for online behavioral addictions, particularly problematic gaming [17,69], yet evidence for its integration with pharmacotherapy remains in an early stage. A recent meta-analysis of studies investigating gaming disorder suggested that a combination of psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy may yield a greater therapeutic response than psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy alone [70]. This advantage may reflect distinct but synergistic contributions of each treatment modality. Psychotherapy may provide skills for self-management, and medications may alter biochemical pathways in manners that may facilitate greater engagement in psychotherapy and enhance individuals’ capacity to apply these skills [71,72]. Psychotherapy may also help to increase medication adherence for some individuals [72] and reduce feelings of medication dependency for others [71], further reflecting the potentially complementary nature of combined treatment. Thus, more research should be conducted to investigate the effectiveness and appropriateness of combined treatments for various online behavioral addictions. Investigating combined intervention strategies that have demonstrated efficacy for other behavioral addictions may represent a feasible and timely approach. For example, topiramate was superior to placebo when combined with a cognitive intervention among people with gambling disorder [73]. Additionally, among individuals with co-occurring tobacco use and gambling disorders, the nutraceutical NAC was superior to placebo when combined with smoking cessation and imaginal desensitization targeting gambling behaviors in promoting a more rapid reduction in tobacco smoking and a more sustained improvement in problem-gambling severity [74]. These and other approaches could potentially help people with online gambling problems and other internet-related behavioral addictions. As data accumulate over time, algorithms for pharmacotherapies and combined pharmacological and behavioral therapies may be developed, as has been done for gambling disorder [62,75]. Additionally, potential roles for noninvasive neuromodulation could be explored in conjunction with pharmacological and behavioral therapies as some forms have demonstrated preliminary efficacy for treating online behavioral addictions like gaming disorder [76]. Furthermore, no studies have investigated combined pharmacotherapy and internet-delivered psychotherapy. Future trials might investigate the effectiveness of such approaches, considering the increased accessibility, scalability, and potentially reduced stigma associated with online interventions [43,77–79].

Pharmacotherapeutic research for online behavioral addictions has focused primarily on problematic gaming, mirroring trends in the broader literature on online behavioral addictions. The present systematic review identified only one study specifically targeting problematic online gambling despite meaningful distinctions between online and offline engagement. Notably, there is a growing need to address increased engagement in problematic online sports betting and newer forms of online gambling such as cryptocurrency trading, particularly among youth [80–82]. Similar limitations were found for online CSB, where existing pharmacotherapy studies recruited mixed samples that included both online and offline presentations. Future studies should evaluate medication effects in a larger number of participants engaging specifically in online sexual behaviors, such as online pornography use and other emerging behaviors (e.g., AI-generated sexual content and sexual relationships/exchanges involving AI chatbots and websites like OnlyFans).

Age and sex should also be considered when interpreting current pharmacotherapeutic research. Although online behavioral addictions such as problematic gaming and problematic use of social media show higher prevalence among adolescents [4,6], we found that most pharmacotherapy studies were conducted in adults. This may be due to clinical, developmental, and ethical concerns surrounding medication use in children and youth [17]. Large longitudinal studies (e.g., the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) study) that collect information on various forms of PUI, medications, and mental health conditions may provide some insight into how pharmacotherapies relate to online behavioral addictions in real-world settings, and such information could further guide RCTs. Furthermore, 96% of participants included in the present systematic review were male, likely reflecting both the focus of included studies on problematic gaming and the gender-skewed epidemiology of gaming disorder [83]. More pharmacotherapeutic research is needed for emerging behavioral addictions, particularly for problematic use of social media, which shows higher prevalence among females [83] yet has no pharmacotherapy studies to date. Similarly, compulsive buying/shopping shows a female predominance and similarly has not been investigated with respect to pharmacotherapies for online treatments [54]. Future studies may examine potential sex differences in treatment response across different online behavioral addictions.

Most studies identified in this systematic review used self-report measures to assess behavioral addiction symptoms, and there appeared to be notable variability in the specific scales used across studies examining the same behavioral addiction. Progress in pharmacotherapeutic research may be constrained by the absence of standardized screening tools for different subtypes of online behavioral addictions [84,85]. While there has been significant progress in gaming disorder (e.g., a WHO-led project currently being undertaken to develop culturally informed instruments [86]), there is a need for further coordinated advancement in assessment and treatment research for other online behavioral addictions, although ICD-11-based instruments have been developed and validated in several jurisdictions [87–89]. Through such efforts, a broad range of online behavioral addictions may be identified and effectively treated across developmental stages.

The heterogeneous effects observed across pharmacotherapies for online behavioral addictions may reflect broader neurobiological constraints that also appear evident in pharmacological treatments for SUDs. Many of the medications examined in the present review, including antidepressants, SSRIs, and opioid receptor antagonists, have shown mixed or modest efficacy across several SUDs [48,90]. Given that SUDs and behavioral addictions involve some overlapping neurobiological mechanisms that are implicated in reward, impulsivity, and compulsivity [91], future pharmacological research may therefore benefit from more mechanism-informed approaches that target specific addiction-related processes. Such strategies may also be strengthened by personalized treatment approaches that account for individual differences in neurobiological profiles, symptom presentations, and comorbid concerns, which may influence treatment response.

Limitations

Several limitations of this systematic review should be considered. First, the restriction to English-language publications may have resulted in the exclusion of potentially relevant non-English studies. Second, the inclusion of only peer-reviewed, published articles may have led to the omission of relevant evidence from the grey literature. Finally, heterogeneity in the definitions and criteria used to characterize specific online behavioral addictions (e.g., PUI/internet addiction) across studies may have limited the comparability of findings.

Conclusions

This systematic review identified 20 studies evaluating the efficacy of pharmacotherapy for online behavioral addictions, including problematic online gaming, online gambling, usage of the internet, and online sexual behaviors. Half of the identified studies targeted problematic online gaming (n = 10) and primarily included adult and male participants. Nearly half of the studies addressed online behavioral addictions with commonly co-occurring concerns, including depression, anxiety, ADHD, and OCD. The most frequently investigated medications were bupropion, various types of SSRIs (escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, citalopram), and opioid receptor antagonists (naloxone and naltrexone). Overall, these medications demonstrated potential in reducing severity of online behavioral addictions and duration of problematic engagement. However, conclusions are limited by the small number of RCTs, lack of long-term follow-up, small sample sizes, heterogeneity of participants (e.g., those with and without co-occurring concerns), and mixed findings across addictive behaviors and medications. Thus, the current evidence base appears to be insufficient to directly support clinical recommendations.
