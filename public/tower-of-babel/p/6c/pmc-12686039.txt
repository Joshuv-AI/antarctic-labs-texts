Insulin Quantification Through Electrochemical and Optical Aptasensors: A Review
Authors: Jia Qi Chong, Muhammad Faiz Md Shakhih, Bhuwaneswaran Vijayam, Citartan Marimuthu, Congo Tak Shing Ching, Asnida Abdul Wahab
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetes mellitus is a serious global health issue, impacting hundreds of millions of people. Current management of diabetes focuses on achieving optimal glycemic control through consistent glucose monitoring. While insulin is crucial for regulating blood glucose levels, its role extends beyond insulin therapy and is often underutilized. Estimating insulin resistance is vital for early diagnosis of type 2 diabetes and improving insulin dosing accuracy. Fasting insulin measurements facilitate the assessment of insulin resistance using the homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), which correlates well with the gold standard research methods. Despite its potential, the current clinical practices face limitations such as prolonged detection times, centralization, and high costs, which hinder routine monitoring. There is an urgent need for a reliable, portable, and cost-effective real-time insulin detection device. Aptasensors, widely developed across various industries, offer a promising solution. These sensors promise fast, affordable, and simple measurement while maintaining high sensitivity. This review explores recent developments in insulin-binding aptamers and aptasensors, emphasizing optical and electrochemical biosensors. Their analytical performance, including specificity, sensitivity, and detection limits is discussed. Addressing the limitations of current biosensors, such as sensitivity, selectivity, and response time, is essential for advancing affordable and reliable insulin monitoring.Keywords: Insulin, Aptasensor, Optical sensor, Electrochemical sensor, Diabetes

Introduction

Insulin is a 51-amino-acid-long peptide hormone, synthesized and stored by the pancreatic islet of Langerhans. In pancreatic β-cells, the precursor of insulin, a 110 amino acid polypeptide composed of signal peptide, B chain, connecting peptide (c-peptide), and A chain, is translated (Fig. 1a). Post-translational modification (e.g., cleavage and folding) occurs, converting the precursor from preproinsulin to proinsulin, and lastly mature insulin consisting of A and B chains connected via disulfide bridges (Fig. 1b) [1, 2]. Insulin was discovered to play a vital role in various metabolic pathways in the body, primarily glucose homeostasis, via several mechanisms of action targeting different tissues. Insulin mediates glycogen synthesis and lipogenesis while inhibiting gluconeogenesis in the liver. In adipose tissues, otherwise, it is responsible for lipogenesis and reduces lipolysis. On the contrary, insulin promotes protein synthesis and glycogen storage in skeletal muscles [3, 4]. Insulin acts differently under various conditions (e.g., fasting and ketotic states) to maintain euglycemia [5]. Dysregulation of insulin results in several health conditions, which include diabetes mellitus (DM) [6]. DM is a chronic health condition related to defective insulin synthesis, which is mainly categorized into two types, type 1 (T1DM) and type 2 (T2DM), based on pathogenesis (Fig. 1c) [7]. Due to the high prevalence of onset at childhood ages, T1DM is also known as juvenile diabetes [8].

(a) Amino acid sequence of preproinsulin, (b) cleavage of preproinsulin to insulin, and (c) pathogenesis of type 1 and type 2 diabetes [7].

The root cause of T1DM is associated with an autoimmune attack of immune cells towards pancreatic β-cells, as they are mistreated as an invader [9]. Consequently, β-cells with responsibility for insulin production are destroyed and resulting in failure of insulin synthesis, the condition of absolute insulin deficiency. Genetic and environmental factors could predispose an individual to T1DM. As T1DM is irreversible, life-time insulin replacement therapy is required to control glucose levels [10].

On the other hand, T2DM often begins with insulin resistance, the condition of relative insulin deficiency [7]. Such a condition triggers compensatory production of insulin in response to insulin reduction, attempting to maintain a normal glucose range. However, excessive workload on β-cells would cause a decline in β-cells mass and function, which eventually leads to the onset of diabetes due to β-cells dysfunction [11]. The underlying factors of T2DM are unclear and prone to be multifactorial. Several possible risk factors of T2DM are glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, and glucolipotoxicity. Long-term exposure to extreme levels of glucose, free fatty acids, and both contributes to the β-cells dysfunction, which is proven as a critical pathogenic mechanism of T2DM [12, 13]

DM rapidly emerged among the populations worldwide, leading to serious health consequences. According to the International Diabetes Federation [14], the global burden of DM has reached a total of 537 million affected adults in 2021, while an increase of 100 million cases is estimated in less than ten years. Underdiagnosis is common in DM, with approximately 239.7 million people unaware of their conditions [15]. DM ranked as the eighth leading cause of mortality in 2019, from the fourteenth in 1990 [16]. In 2021, approximately 6.7 million deaths were attributed to DM-associated diseases, with a person dying of DM every five seconds [14]. A study by Ling et al. [17] has been conducted to assess DM-associated deaths worldwide from 2000 to 2016. From this study, vascular complications derived from DM are concluded as the main contributors to DM-related mortality, including nephropathy, cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, etc. [17]. The steadily rising trend of DM mortality globally throughout the years could indirectly reflect the insufficiency of current diabetes intervention, and points out the need for more effective intervention strategies [18].

In current clinical practice, the goal for DM therapy focuses on maintaining optimal glycemic control, preventing DM complications for a better prognosis. Good glycemic control is measured by several parameters, such as fasting and postprandial glucose concentration of below 7.0 mmol/L and 8.5 mmol/L, respectively, and HbA1c of less than or equal to 7.0%. These parameters vary depending on the patient’s demographic and health status [19, 20]. However, achieving ideal glycemic control remains a significant challenge. Multiple studies reported poor glucose control among diabetic patients across different geographical regions [21–24]. These studies reflect the insufficiency of DM management that fully relies on glucose monitoring as implemented in current clinical practices. The development of glucose biosensors is undeniably a game changer in DM intervention and opens the door for point-of-care (POC) glucose monitoring, easing the lives of DM patients [25]. Nonetheless, several issues remain unsolved before efficient DM management can be achieved. While real-time continuous glucose monitoring has helped in improving glycemic control by lowering glycated hemoglobin levels, it still falls short in preventing hypoglycemia. This is supported by the high prevalence of severe hypoglycemia, particularly associated with insulin therapy [26]. In addition, suboptimal glycemic control remains high among patients, especially associated with insulin treatment [27–30]. Routine insulin monitoring could be a breakthrough, as evaluating glucose concentration alone is clearly insufficient to estimate proper insulin dosing. According to the current state of the art, insulin dose is decided via premeal glucose level and meal size (carbohydrate contents), where adjustments of insulin injection units are made with the changes in these parameters as recommended [20, 31]. However, rather than indirect prediction, direct assessment of blood insulin is expected to be more efficient [32]. Taking account of both glucose and insulin concentration in the estimation of insulin dosage is believed to enhance dosing accuracy, thus realizing tight glycemic control [30, 33].

In addition, assessing fasting insulin concentration permits evaluation of insulin resistance, a critical factor of type 2 DM (T2DM). A mathematical approach, as shown in equation (1), namely homeostasis model assessment insulin resistance (HOMA-IR), is prominently utilized for predicting insulin resistance and insulin sensitivity [34].

The gold standard for assessing insulin resistance refers to the hyperinsulinemic euglycemic clamp (HEC), introduced by DeFronzo et al. [35], via examining whole-body glucose uptake [35–37]. However, due to its complexity and cost-ineffectiveness, it is confined to research settings and is impractical for daily practice. Therefore, HOMA-IR is widely accepted as an alternative to the gold standard methods with strong correlation [38–42].

Early diagnosis is crucial and often relates to better clinical outcomes, where immediate intervention is made possible. However, early detection of DM is barely feasible via glucose monitoring as practiced currently. In the early stage of DM, abnormalities of glucose concentration are not detectable due to the compensatory insulin secretion overwhelming that of insulin resistance. Only after the compensatory phase, hyperglycemia started to manifest and bring along associated symptoms [43]. Therefore, adopting routine monitoring of insulin concentration could enable detection of DM at the first stage, before the onset of symptoms. Abnormal fasting insulin concentration levels, either lower or higher, could predict T1DM and T2DM accordingly [44–46].

Insulin is still underutilized in the clinical setting, as the only prominent role of insulin is limited to insulin replacement therapy, despite its potential implications as forementioned. Impelled by the significance of insulin quantification, we aim to provide an overview of the current strategies to quantify insulin and their drawbacks. Our core highlight is the promising potential of the aptamers as the molecular recognition elements (MREs) in the sensing of insulin, ranging from electrochemical to optical-based biosensors. We also offer our perspectives on addressing the current bottlenecks that cripple the progress of these sensors towards commercialization.

Fundamentals of Biosensors

Biosensors are composed of several components, basically including biosensing elements, a transducer, and a signal processor. In principle, the biorecognition element is responsible for recognizing a specific analyte through a biological event (e.g., antigen-antibody interaction, aptamer-target interaction, enzyme-substrate interaction, etc.) [47]. Once the biorecognition event occurs, the resulting signal is converted by a transducer into a readable form [48]. Finally, a signal processor interprets and displays the data for analysis.

Biorecognition Elements

A biorecognition element is defined as any molecule capable of recognizing a specific target of interest from the sample matrix while remaining unresponsive to other nonspecific interference. Some of the commonly used biosensing elements include antibodies, aptamers, enzymes, etc. While for this article, the focus would be on the aptamers, which would be further discussed in the following section.

Aptamers

Aptamers are single-stranded nucleic acids, either ssDNA or RNA, capable of folding into unique three-dimensional (3D) conformation, responsible for their high affinity and selectivity against a wide variety of target molecules (Fig.2A) [49]. Aptamers form diverse structures, including Guanine-quadruplex (G-quadruplex), hairpin loop, internal loop, and pseudo-knot, as illustrated in Fig. 2a–e with different aptamers [50–54]. Aptamers can be very specific as they can distinguish very closely related molecules that differ even by a single functional group [55, 56].

(a) Aptamer folds into 3D structures for target binding [57]. Secondary structure of aptamers: (a) pseudoknot; (b) G-quadruplex; (c) three-way junction (three stems converge at a junction); (d) internal loop; and (e) hairpin loop [58–62].

In most cases, aptamers are selected through an in vitro process known as systemic evolution of ligands by exponential enrichment (SELEX), which it first coined in 1990 by Tuerk and Gold [63]. Sequences (one or a few) with the highest affinity towards a specific target are selected through SELEX as depicted in Fig. 3, starting from an initial random oligonucleotide library and undergoing multiple iterative rounds of selection, partitioning, and amplification [64].

SELEX process [64].

Aptamers Versus Antibodies for Biosensing

Antibodies have been used as biorecognition elements in clinical diagnostics (e.g., tumor markers, biomarkers, pathogens, etc.) for decades [65–67]. However, aptamers gradually gain attention over antibodies due to their advantages surpassing those of antibodies. Antibodies are typically produced via in vivo procedures (involving animal studies), where the targets provoke an immune response in laboratory animals, inducing the synthesis of antibodies as a natural immune defense mechanism [68]. As a result, targets for antibodies are limited to immunogens. On the other hand, aptamers are produced fully in vitro through SELEX and themselves have low immunogenicity. Aptamers can be designed against a vast variety of targets, including proteins, ions, whole cells, small molecules, including metal ions, and toxins. In addition, aptamers recognize small molecules due to their much smaller size, which allows accessibility to the epitopes on the surface of molecules, unimpacted by steric hindrance as compared to antibodies [69, 70]. Due to their size as well, it permits a greater number of immobilized aptamers per unit area, which indirectly contributes to the sensor’s sensitivity [71]. On the other hand, antibodies are known for their susceptibility towards temperature and pH changes, resulting in permanent loss of function, and thus limiting shelf life. Comparatively, aptamers are stable and have reversible denaturation properties [72–74]. A comparison between antibodies and aptamers is provided in Table 1 [75–79].

Insulin-Binding Aptamers as a Promising MRE for Insulin Sensing

Several DNA aptamers have been discovered against insulin (Table 2). Unlike common aptamers, ILPR2 is not selected through SELEX and is also known as a ‘natural aptamer’. The insulin-linked polymorphic region (ILPR) consists of the sequences 5’-ACAGGGGTGTGGGG-3’, a segment located upstream of the noncoding region of the insulin promoter gene, where polymorphism occurs concerning the number of repeats. Two or more tandem repeats are noticed for the ability to form an intramolecular G-quadruplex structure [80]. The statement is proven by Connor et al. [81], at which ILPR2 composed of two ILPR repeats, captures insulin via interaction with G-quadruplex.

Three aptamers are selected against insulin by Yoshida et al. via SELEX, from the initial library, namely IGA1, IGA2, and IGA3, which comprise guanine-rich sequences [82]. Through further assay, IGA3 is defined as the aptamer with the highest binding ability towards insulin, while ILPR2 exhibits the poorest binding affinity. An in silico study using molecular dynamics simulation supports the conclusion drawn by Yoshida et al. [82] as the insulin-IGA3 complex was found to be stable with high binding energy. Mulyani et al. [83] claimed that guanine nucleotides greatly contribute to the interaction between the aptamer and the target insulin, suggesting that the aptamer forms a G-quadruplex structure. Due to its specificity towards insulin, IGA3 is extensively exploited for the development of biosensors for insulin detection.

Interestingly, despite numerous previous studies focused on guanine-rich aptamers as the G-quadruplex structure is believed crucial for insulin binding, Wu et al. [84] found that a cytosine (C) rich DNA aptamer exhibits stronger interaction towards insulin, with rapid kinetics. Wu et al. [84] synthesized a few oligonucleotides, including cIGA3, which is complementary to IGA3, the poly-C (C20, C30), poly-G, poly-T, and poly-A DNA with repeated bases [84]. Among all oligonucleotides tested against free insulin, only C-rich and G-rich oligonucleotide binds the insulin, as manifested by fluorescence reading. Remarkably, the C-rich oligonucleotides exhibit more intense fluorescence than IGA3. The study concluded that both IGA3 and C-rich aptamer to be non-specific against insulin, which is also contradicting other studies on IGA3 for few decades.

Transducers

According to different transducers, biosensors are classified into optical, electrochemical, and mass-based biosensors. In the field of medical diagnostics, electrochemical and optical biosensors are most widely employed for the detection of pathogens (e.g., bacteria and viruses), biomolecules (e.g., glucose and urea), cancer markers (e.g., alpha-fetoprotein), etc. [85–90].

Electrochemical Biosensors

The five main classes of electrochemical biosensors are amperometric, voltammetric, potentiometric, conductometric, and impedimetric biosensors. Regardless of classes, all types of electrochemical transducers rely on charge transfer occurring at the electrode-analyte interface. Normally, an electrochemical cell can be either a two or three-electrode system, composed of a working and reference electrode, with an extra counter electrode in the latter (Figure 4).

Schematic illustration of (a) amperometric/voltammetric; (b) potentiometric; (c) conductometric; (d) impedimetric biosensors [91]; and (e) organic electrochemical transistor (OECT) [92].

In principle, both amperometric and voltammetric biosensors achieve quantitative determination of analytes through measuring the difference in current arising from the redox reaction (oxidation or reduction) from biochemical events occurring at the working electrode once potential is applied, in comparison to the reference electrode. In amperometric biosensors, the potential applied is kept constant. On the other hand, current is measured over a range of potential in a voltammetric approach.

Potentiometric methods involve measuring potential differences resulting from the analyte-electrode surface interaction, which is proportional to the analyte’s concentration. As no current is applied, the potential detected is responsive to the ion accumulated at the ion-selective working electrode. Conductometric technique wise, alteration in electrical conductivity of the solution is assessed, as conductivity is affected by the concentration of ions. Instead of conductivity, impedance (resistance) is measured in impedimetric biosensors, where electron transfer could be affected by the binding of the target to the electrode surface [91, 93, 94].

Organic electrochemical transistor (OECT) is not a typical electrochemical transducer but is gaining attention in biosensing. Generally, OECT converts biochemical signals into electrical signals by modulating the conductivity of an organic channel in an electrolyte, connected to three components: the gate electrode, source, and drain. As gate voltage is applied, ions from the electrolyte penetrate the channel, causing alteration of charge carrier density in the channel via electrochemical doping or de-doping. Consequently, the change the conductivity, which directly affects the drain current [95, 96].

Optical Biosensors

According to the transduction approaches, several types of optical biosensors are available, as depicted in Figure 5.

Optical biosensors with different transducing mechanisms, including (a) colorimetric; (b) surface plasmon resonance (SPR); (c) liquid-crystal; (d) fluorescence; and (e) surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS) [97] [98].

In a fluorescence/luminescence-based biosensor, the analyte of interest or the biosensing element is tagged with a fluorescent label (fluorophore), at which fluorescence will be generated once a biorecognition event occurs. The fluorescence intensity is proportional to the concentration of analytes. SYBR green is an example of a fluorescence tag [99, 100].

Colorimetric biosensors employ simple and fast methods where the detection of the target analyte is observable through the naked eye. Through spectrophotometers or integration of cameras, quantification can be achieved. Several dyes can be used in this case. For instance, acridine orange switches to yellow once the virus is detected [101, 102].

Other than that, SPR techniques detect changes in refractive index resulting from the changes in electromagnetic resonance of the evanescent wave as the target analyte is present at the sensing surface (metal). In principle, as incident light strikes a metal at a particular angle (SPR angle), the photon interacts with electrons in the metal, leading to surface plasmons (oscillation of electrons). However, as the target binds to the bioreceptor at the metal surface, the refractive index is affected, which shifts the angle, leading to an alteration in reflected light [103, 104].

Surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS) acts as an optical transducer by amplifying Raman signals of target molecules adsorbed onto nanostructured metal surfaces, typically gold or silver. This enhancement arises from localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR), where incident light causes collective oscillations of electrons in the metal nanoparticles, generating intense electromagnetic fields near the surface. When target molecules interact with these fields, their Raman scattering is significantly intensified, producing a unique spectral [105, 106].

Liquid crystals (LCs) act as optical transducers by changing their molecular orientation in response to external stimuli, such as target binding. These orientation shifts alter the birefringence and light transmission properties of the LC layer, producing visible changes that can be optically monitored via spectroscopic imaging [107, 108].

Recent Advances in Insulin Aptasensors Development

Incorporating insulin aptamers into insulin biosensors engenders diagnostic platforms that can be used for insulin quantification. Scientists developed different types of insulin biosensors, ranging from electrochemical to optical biosensors. In this section, recent insulin aptasensors (since 2019) were screened and reviewed as follows (Figure 6).

Electrochemical-Based Insulin Aptasensors

Electrochemical sensors are sensors that quantify the presence of a target based on the electrical signal measurement, which is derived from the interaction between the bioreceptor immobilized on the surface of an electrode and its corresponding target molecules. They offer a convenient, easy, and real-time measurement of a target molecule while retaining high sensitivity. Insulin aptamers have been widely incorporated into electrochemical sensing platforms, resulting in the development of electrochemical insulin biosensors. This section includes all types of electrochemical-based insulin aptasensors to the best of our knowledge. It is worth noting that no organic transistor-based insulin aptasensors have been reported beyond the year 2019.

Redox-Reporter-Conjugation-Based Approach

Redox reporters can be conjugated to aptamers to transduce the aptamer conformational change in the presence of that target into electrical currents that can be measured to indicate the presence of a target. In one study, Wu et al. [110] demonstrated the applicability of their aptasensor for real-time insulin monitoring. Using their sensor, as much as 90% signal changes were obtained within 60 s after sample addition. The time required for the detection of 20 nM and 20 μM of insulin is similar, indirectly reflecting its rapid electron transfer regardless of sample concentration. Nonetheless, even though 90% of the signal is saturated within seconds, roughly 20 minutes is required for complete saturation. The fast association rate of the aptamer and the target insulin enables rapid measurement of the target insulin. In addition, the researchers also investigated the influence of redox probe appendage to the aptamer for insulin biosensing. They have derived different aptamers that were functionalized via different strategies. In the first strategy, Methylene blue (MB) label was conjugated at the extremity of the aptamer while in another strategy, a similar label was incorporated within the aptamer. It was discovered that the former was able to achieve better signal production as compared to the latter. Appending an aptamer at the 5’ or 3’-end of the aptamer permits a much more efficient electron transfer that translates into a higher signal. Moreover, the MB groups appended at the extremities of the aptamers are not blocked by the aptamer-target complex formation, which allows a free flow of electrons detectable by the electrochemical sensor. On the other hand, incorporating MB within the aptamer is potentially blocked by the aptamer-target complex, thus stifling the transfer of electrons, resulting in comparatively lower signal production. The limit of detection (LOD) and linear range of the biosensor are 200 nM and 0.2–20 μM, respectively. Moreover, the sensor showed a high specificity against the target insulin as compared to the non-target molecules such as glucagon and somatostatin. One of the exclusive steps applied for enhancing biosensor performance involves treatment with 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), which disrupts the formation of interstrand G-quartets but enhances intrastrand G-quadruplex.

Signal-On and Off Approach

In the signal-on and off approach, two different modes were created. In the signal-on mode, the flow of electrons from the redox reporter onto the electrode surface is promoted, which indicates the presence of the target. In the signal-off mode, the movement of the electron to the electrode surface is thwarted, which is indicative of the absence of the target. The flow state of the electron is dictated by the conformational change of the aptamer in the presence and absence of the target. Zhao et al. [110] created a dual-signaling electrochemical biosensor using a signal-on and signal-off approach. In this approach, two different probes were designed, a signal off probe, which is the IGA3 aptamer modified with MB, and a signal on probe, which is a sequence complementary to the IGA3 aptamer appended with Ferrocene In the presence of the target insulin, hybridization occurs between the aptamer and the complementary sequence, linking the signal off and on probes together as complex structure. Within the complex, Ferrocene (Fc) is located away from the electrode surface, generating a low initial signal as electron transfer is disabled. In contrast, MB having close contacts to the electrode surface triggers intense electrochemical signals from the signal-off probe by default. In the presence of the target insulin, the specific binding between the target and the aptamers induces the conformational change of the former. As a result, the complementary sequence was detached from the MB-tagged IGA3 aptamer. Bending of ssDNA permits Fc and the electrode surface to be a closer proximity, for redox reaction. Therefore, dual signaling via signal-on and signal-off probes is detected concurrently, with an amplification in the former while declination in the latter, proportional to the insulin concentration. The aptasensor is highly selective towards insulin alone as compared to target molecules, thrombin, HSA, and human IgG. The sensor was also evaluated using insulin spiked into human serum and compared with a commercial ELISA kit as a reference. Remarkably, the sensor’s recoveries are comparable to those of ELISA, ranging from 93.8% to 110.2%.

Zhou et al. [111] adopted a similar concept as Zhao et al. [110] in their sensor design, employing a signal-on and signal-off approach. However, instead of using MB to label the aptamer, anthraquinone (AQ) was used. As for the signal-off probe, MB was incorporated into the complementary ssDNA. In the absence of insulin, the hybridization of the two probes inhibits the Guanine (G)-quadruplex formation, causing a double-stranded conformation that causes the MB to move closer to the electrode surface and AQ facing the other dimension. In the presence of the target insulin, the dissociation of the complementary ssDNA occurs and causes the formation of a G-quadruplex structure, which brings AQ nearer to the electrode surface. At the same time, the G-quartet structures of the aptamer allow specific interaction with the target insulin. The concomitant attenuation of the MB signal and enhancement of the AQ signal can be used to determine their ratio (IAQ/IMB), which reflects the insulin level. Implementing dual signal approaches augments reliability, minimizes the influences of interfering agents, and increases the specificity of the sensor. The distinct value of signal ratio for insulin and other interfering agents tested (e.g., L-ascorbic acid, thrombin, L-cystine, and IgG) indicates high selectiveness of the aptasensor towards insulin. Moreover, a relatively stable signal ratio of 83% is preserved after four weeks’ storage, with recoveries ranging from 86.5% to 107%.

Another signal-off insulin aptasensor was reported by Sahin et al. [112]. The SPCE was modified with gold for thiol-mediated immobilization of 5’-thiolated aptamer onto the SPCE. MB was labeled at the other end of the aptamer, signal-off probe. In short, upon insulin binding, the conformational switch of the aptamer resulted in increased distance between the MB tag of the aptamer from the electrode interface, reducing electron transfer, and mitigating the electrical signal generated. Via square wave voltammetry, the LOD and linear detection range of this aptasensor are 18.5 pM and 25–150 pM, accordingly. An interference assay was conducted against streptavidin, uric acid, glucose, and thrombin, mixed with insulin and insulin alone. From the assay, most of the interfering agents caused negligible impact, except for uric acid, which generated about a two-fold signal higher than insulin. After 10 days of storage in the fridge, the aptasensor maintained more than 90% (92%) signal compared to the first day. The real sample study was not included.

Paper-Based Electrochemical Sensor

Paper-based electrochemical sensors use paper as the platform to house electrodes to perform electrochemical sensing. The main advantage of a paper-based biosensor for insulin quantification is the affordability and the need for a label-free approach. Niroula et al. [113] developed a paper-based biosensor for insulin quantification. The graphite working electrode is coated with carboxyl functionalized graphene (graphene–COOH), which forms covalent bonding with amine-modified aptamer (crosslinking mediated by carbodiimide bond) for stable immobilization. Using the paper-based aptasensor, insulin was able to be detected in 10× diluted samples with the limit of detection down to 1.5 pM, achieving a linear range of detection from 5 to 500 pM. This sensor was also evaluated against other sensing strategies using an ELISA kit, horse-radish peroxidase (HRP) labeled immunosensor. Upon comparison, the sensor achieved a similar detection limit to the other sensors. In the future, the authors can analyze the specificity, stability, and recovery of the sensor.

Exonuclease-Based Electrochemical Sensing

Exonuclease enzyme catalyzes the removal of one nucleotide at a time from the 5’ or 3’ of a nucleic acid. The usage of an exonuclease able to degrade free or unbound nucleic acids, which can enhance the specificity and sensitivity of a sensor. In the study by Liu et al. [114], two types of insulin aptasensors were developed, using a glassy carbon electrode (GCE) and laser-scribed graphene electrode (LSGE), both immobilized with insulin aptamers. In this unique sensor, Exonuclease I was added for the hydrolytic cleavage of the unbound aptamer from the electrode after the addition of the target insulin, which is captured by the electron-immobilized aptamers. Next, the gold nanoparticles linked-aptamer (AuNPs-Apt) were added, recognizing the aptamer-bound insulin on the surface of the electrodes, constructing a sandwich configuration. Finally, MB was added, which was intercalated into the guanine bases of aptamers, permitting electrochemical detection via current changes. The authors have compared both these sensors and have found out that LSGE achieved a lower detection limit than that of GSE, with the values of 22.7 fM and 9.8 fM, respectively, with identical dynamic range. However, GSE is claimed to be more specific to insulin than LGSE. A mixture consisting of 1 μM of glucose, uric acid, lysozyme, and L-cysteine obtained 24.3% of the signal as compared to the signal received from 0.1 μM insulin (100%). The differences in the specificity could be attributed to the high porosity of LGSE, pores of which can be filled up by non-target molecules via physical absorption that translates into electrical signals. The sensor also has high stability, as the stability is maintained even after 20 days of storage under 4 °C, with a signal reduction of approximately 10% which is reasonable.

Impedimetric-Based Biosensor

The electrochemical impedance technique was recruited in the development of insulin aptasensors as reported by Abazar and Noorbakhsh [115]. A glassy carbon working electrode was modified with carbon quantum dots mixed with chitosan, followed by incubation with glutaraldehyde, forming GC/CQD-Chit/GLA. Covalent immobilization (crosslinking) of amino-functionalized aptamer was performed next, generating GC/CQD-Chit/GLA/Apt. Changes in resistance were examined with various concentrations of insulin. The sensor achieved a sensitivity and detection limit of 80.07 Ω/nM and 106.8 pM, respectively, dynamic range of 0.5–10 nM. Stability wise, 96% response remained after a week of storage under 4 °C. Good selectivity towards insulin compared to L-cysteine, ascorbic acid, uric acid, and BSA. Insulin spiked serum was prepared for recovery assay by sensors and a commercial immunoassay, revealing similar recoveries, which are 96.8%–105.6% and 98.5–101.9% respectively.

Microneedle Array-Based Wearable Sensor

The main flow for insulin biosensors is based on in vitro detection, while Dervisevic et al. [116] constructed a wearable sensor for in vivo insulin detection, meant for point-of-care (POC) diagnosis. With the microneedle array (MNA) integrated, transdermal insulin detection is made possible from the interstitial fluid (ISF). This biosensor offers advantages for POC testing as it eliminates the need for sample pretreatment (e.g., serum extraction), which is compulsory in most of the developed insulin biosensors. This could be related to the reduced matrix effect in the interstitial filtration (large proteins in blood are filtered) [95]. In addition, the sensitivity of the sensor is enhanced by gold deposition on the microneedle to improve conductivity and provide a larger surface for aptamer immobilization through carbodiimide coupling (EDC/NHS coupling). The developed sensor is proven capable of extracting and quantifying insulin concentration in ISF, for both in vitro and in vivo procedures. The sensor’s analytical performance is assessed via in vitro electrochemical analysis, with insulin-spiked artificial ISF. Sensitivity, linear range, and detection limit of the sensors are 65 Ω/nM, 0.1–4 nM, and 10 pM respectively. Selectivity is tested against insulin, BSA, IGF-1, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (ErbB2), TKN buffer, and artificial ISF (contains NaCl, KCl, HEPES, glucose, sucrose, MgSO4, CaCl2, and NaH2PO4). The sensor is selective towards insulin, indicated by negligible signal obtained from those interferences. On the other hand, in vivo testing is performed on mice, successfully detecting the changes of insulin concentration induced by administration of glucose. However, it is worth noting that interstitial insulin level is generally lower than insulin level in arterial plasma, suggested with approximately 50% reduction [117]. Furthermore, the issue with delayed transportation of insulin transcapillary to reach the interstitial space needed to be further investigated [118].

Cathodic Photoelectrochemical Sensor

Based on its name, a photoelectrochemical sensor integrates both electrochemical and optical biosensing techniques, leveraging the advantages of each approach. Cathodic photoelectrochemical cells are highly resistant to interference, beneficial for target detection within a complex matrix. In the study of Shang et al. [119], an aptasensor for insulin quantification is designed through such an approach. Amorphous molybdenum sulfide (a-MoSx) and gold nanoparticles were fabricated on the surface of indium tin oxide (ITO) glass sheet in sequence, forming ITO/a-MoSx/Au photocathode. Through Au–S bond, the thiol-labeled aptamer was immobilized onto ITO/a-MoSx/Au. A photosensitizer is otherwise constructed with thionine@MoS2 QDs as a signal amplifier, conjugated to the terminal end of the aptamer via EDC/NHS activation. In insulin presence, the insulin-aptamer complex activates the sensitization of thionine@MoS2, amplifying photocurrent. A dose response curve is obtained, with a broad linear range from 0.1 pg/mL to 100 ng/mL and an ultralow LOD of 28 fg/mL. Interferences tested are negligible, including BSA, HRP (horseradish peroxidase), Gox (glucose oxidase), Lys, Hemin, Proinsulin, and C-peptide. The spike test performed with recoveries of 98.4–101.9% and RSD ranges from 3.3 to 4.8%. Stability reported to be stable after two weeks under 4 °C, but the signal percentage is not specified.

Wireless Smartphone-Integrated Sensor

Liu et al. [120] reported a non-invasive aptasensor adopting modified screen-printed carbon electrodes (SPCEs) into a biochip, for wireless (via Bluetooth), simultaneous detection of glucose and insulin using a smartphone. Their strategy involves the integration of two SPCEs, each modified for the detection of specific targets (glucose/insulin). Into a biochip. As the sample loaded into the biochip, biorecognition occurs at the two sensing interfaces (SPCEs) concurrently, and the electrochemical signal generated was detected using a multi-channel potentiostat, where the processed data was directly displayed in a smartphone application connected to the biochip. The SPCEs were modified consecutively with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs), 3’ end methylene blue tagged, 5’ thiol labeled glucose and/or insulin aptamers via gold-thiol immobilization, and electrode surface blocking (preventing non-specific binding) with thiolated polyethylene glycol (PEG). Evaluation of the sensor’s performance solely focuses on insulin sensing rather than glucose sensing. The reaction time for this aptasensor is 30 min. It achieved LOD of 45 pM, but the linear range is relatively narrow from 0.05 to 15 nM. The selectivity of these aptasensors towards insulin was studied, and as the low concentration of insulin was tested, the electrical signal dropped to 55%. When tested with non-specific interferences, human immunoglobulin (IgG), bovine serum albumin (BSA), interferon-γ, and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), the signal remained high (>97%). Selectivity was further validated by mixing insulin with each interference, where the signal dropped to 49–51%, similar to the response generated by insulin alone. Additionally, the stability of the aptasensor was tested, with a decent result of 86.72% preserved after 30 days of storage at 4 °C. Lastly, as mentioned, this aptasensor emphasizes non-invasive detection targeting saliva. Thus, insulin-spiked preprocessed saliva was measured with this aptasensor. The recovery rate is between 92.0 to 98.8% with RSD of 1.52 to 3.11%.

Several important aspects, such as nanoparticles used and aptamer immobilization strategies, are also included. It is seen that sensitivity and response time are rarely reported, albeit their significance in determining the sensor’s performance (Table 3). Further investigation is required to assess the impact of interference concentration and serum matrix effect [123]. Optical Aptasensors

Optical aptasensors are sensors that rely on the aptamer-target complex formation that impart changes in the optical properties in order to detect the presence of the corresponding target. They can provide rapid, facile, sensitive, and specific measurements. Compared to that of electrochemical biosensors, optical sensors demonstrate higher LOD due to the much higher sensitivity of the optical-based measurement as compared to the electrical signal-based measurement. Likewise, insulin aptamers have been widely applied in optical sensors.

Chemiluminescence-Based Sensor

Sun et al. developed an optical insulin biosensor based on chemiluminescence (CL) [124]. The construction of the sensor involves the synthesis of aptamer functionalized nanosilica graphene oxide aerogel (IGA3/SiO2@GOAG) hybridized with the complementary oligonucleotide conjugated to gold nanoparticles (ssDNA-AuNPs). Aptamer IGA3 was adsorbed onto the SiO2@GOAG nanocomposites via non-covalent interaction (e.g., hydrogen bond and π–π stacking). In the presence of the target, the aptamer is dissociated from its complementary oligonucleotides. As a result, the ssDNA-AuNPs are released to catalyze the reaction of luminol-H2O2, resulting in the production of CL signal, proportional to the insulin concentrations. The initial CL intensity is negligible, which eliminates the concern of background signals. Remarkably, detection can be completed within 55 s upon introduction of the sample. A linear detection of insulin is achieved within the ranges of 7.5 pM–5.0 nM, with the LOD of 1.6 pM. Recoveries of standard insulin lie within 98.0%–100.6%, with a relative standard deviation (RSD) of 1.84%–2.82%. Results obtained from the aptasensor are roughly equivalent to those of HPLC. Specificity analysis was performed against insulin and other non-target molecules such as adrenaline (Adr), BSA, thrombin (THR), cysteine (Cys), homocysteine (Hcy), Fe3+, Ca2+, CO32-, and SO42-. The sensor is highly specific to insulin compared with other interferences. Moreover, the sensor performance reduces by 8.1% after a week of storage at 4 °C; the storage period should be further extended to assess for long-term stability.

Fluorescence-Based Sensor

As another type of optical sensor, a fluorescence-based sensor is dependent on the fluorescence emission by the fluorophore to inform the presence of the target. Employing an aptamer in a fluorescence-based sensor enables a rapid, cheap, and sensitive approach to sensing insulin. In one study, Muhammad et al. proposed a dual emissive aptasensor based on fluorescent nanoparticles for concurrent insulin and glucose measurement [125]. Designation of probes involves conjugation of aptamers to the fluorescent emitting nanoparticles: carbon dots (CD) and quantum dots (QD), via carbodiimide (EDC/NHS) reaction, followed by fluorescence quenching via adsorption onto a nano-graphite surface, a fluorophore-quencher. Insulin aptamers were conjugated to CD while glucose aptamers were conjugated to quantum dots QD through amide bonding. In the absence of the target, the aptamers remain attached to the surface of the nano-graphite via π − π stacking, which quenches the fluorescence. In the presence of the target, the aptamers are desorbed from the surface of the nano-graphite due to the conformational change of the aptamer. As a result, the fluorescence emission from both the CD and QD is restored. The fluorescence intensity can be measured at the emission wavelengths of 450 nm and 585 nm, which represent CD (insulin) and QD (glucose), respectively. The sensor demonstrated the intensity to be in a linear relationship with the insulin concentration, ranging from 0.2 to 2 nM, with a LOD of 0.018 nM. Specificity assay proved that the sensor is only selective against insulin and glucose compared to other molecules such as ions (e.g., NH4+, Zn2+, Na+, K+, Mg2+, Cl−, HCO3−, Cu2+, Fe3+, Ca2+), urea, albumin, and uric acid. Human serum spiked insulin assay discloses the recoveries of the sensors to fall within 95.0–101.0%, with the highest RSD of 4.84%. Interestingly, the accuracy of the sensor is comparable to the gold standard protocols practiced in hospital laboratories.

CDs are also widely utilized in sensing applications as they are highly soluble in water, involve a low cost of production, excellent stability with high resistance to photobleaching. In another study, an insulin aptamer, IGA3, was used in concert with the aptasensor implementing CD, in particular a red emission CD (R-CD) with lower cytotoxicity, proposed by He et al. [126]. In this sensor, QD was conjugated to the aptamer to form R-CD/IGA3 functionalized aptamer. Fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) is the basis of this sensor, as the absence or presence of the target insulin can change this FRET that impacts the energy transfer from an excited fluorescence donor (R-CD/IGA3) to an acceptor (graphene oxide) involving dipole-dipole interaction, which is confined to a close proximity between 10 and 100 Å [127]. Similar to the previous sensor by Muhammad et al., in the absence of the target insulin, the R-CD/IGA3 probe remains immobilized onto the surface of graphene oxide (GO) [125]. The quenching property of the GO quenches the fluorescence from the QD. However, in the presence of the target insulin, the R-CD/IGA3 probe undergoes a conformational change, which releases it and causes the emission of fluorescence. Therefore, the infra-red fluorescence, which is detected at the emission wavelength of 625 nm, corresponds to the insulin concentration. The biosensor offers LOD of 1.1 nM, whereby the linearity falls to between 1.3 and 150 nM. The spike recovery of insulin in fetal bovine serum was 97.6–101.3%, with the lowest RSD of 2.1%. The sensor is highly specific against the target insulin with negligible binding against Fe3+, Ca2+, K+, Mg2+, CO32−, SO42−.

Another FRET-based biosensor for the sensing of insulin was designed by Zeng et al. [128], employing a fluorophore, namely quaternized tetraphenylethene salt (TPE-Z), a type of aggregation-induced emission luminogen well known for extreme fluorescence emission once in the aggregated state [129]. IGA3 conjugated TPE-Z and GO serve as fluorescence probe and quencher, respectively. Similar working principle as aforementioned in He et al. [126], except the replacement of CD with TPE-Z in this case [126]. The LOD achieved was 0.44 pM across a linear range of 1.0 pM–1.0 µM. The specificity of the sensor was tested against BSA, HSA, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), alpha fetoprotein (AFP), prostate specific antigen (PSA), and a mixture of all, which are present at a concentration 10 times higher than that of insulin. The sensor still shows a high selectivity against insulin as compared to other molecules. The spiked serum test reveals recoveries of 96.1 − 104.3% with the RSD of below 5.2%.

In another sensing application, Mandani et al. [130] describe an insulin aptasensor that adopts surface energy transfer (SET) between a dipole and surface (typically a metal surface), also known as donor and acceptor. SET is highly distance-dependent and only triggered within a distance of approximately 10 nm [131]. In this case, indium phosphide/zinc sulfide QD (InP/ZnS-QD) conjugated to an aptamer via EDC/NHS crosslinking acts as the donor while silver (Ag) nanoplate serves as the fluorescence quencher or acceptor. One advantage of SET as compared to FRET is that the former is able to overcome the distance limit faced by the latter, as in this system, which utilizes a non-immobilized aptamer. In the absence of insulin, the aptamer-QD remains attached to the Ag surface, resulting in fluorescence quenching. However, with the addition of the target insulin, the aptamer is desorbed from the surface to bind the target insulin, which results in fluorescence emission. As the excitation light (370 nm) irradiates the system, insulin-bound aptamer-QD emits fluorescence depending on its concentration, detected at the wavelength of 410 nm. The sensor achieved LOD, linear range, recoveries, and stability of the biosensors of 0.5 pM, 0.001–5000 nM, 95–101.7% (RSD < 7.4%), 90% after 10 days, respectively. No significant contrast is reported upon comparison with HPLC and aptasensor analysis of human plasma samples. The sensor is highly specific against the target insulin with minimal signal production with other interfering agents, including HSA, BSA, Cys, alanine (Ala), glycine (Gly), hemoglobin (Hb), Lyz, ascorbic acid, Fe3+, and Ca2+present at 100 times higher concentrations.

Reflectance-Based Measurement

Apart from CL and fluorescence measurements, a simple reflectance-based measurement can also be employed for insulin sensing. One such sensor was developed by Taib et al. [132], where color changes were identified in the presence and absence of insulin. In this sensor, the insulin aptamer was immobilized to the amine-modified porous silica microparticles via glutaraldehyde, crosslinking through imine bond (C=N) formation. The IGA3 aptamers were also tagged through π − π interaction with Nickel (II) Salphen, which acts as a yellow color label. In the presence of insulin, discoloration from yellow to brownish orange happens, detectable at 656 nm. The binding between the aptamer and the target insulin plateau can be achieved within 40 min of reaction, for the measurement of 50 μIU mL−1 insulin. Even though 40 min is acceptable as a rapid test according to the World Health Organization’s criteria, it is relatively long as compared to glucose biosensors, where results are acquired within seconds [133, 134]. The sensor’s performance declines roughly 15% on the sixth day of storage and is retained until day 24 and proceed with a gradual loss of up to 30% on the fiftieth day. Noteworthy, the biosensors can be stored at room temperature. The relatively long-term stability is claimed to be attributed to the strong imine interaction between IGA3 and glutaraldehyde, whilst degradation of the aptamer serves as a factor influencing its analytical performance. Even though the highest reflectance response is obtained with insulin, other biomolecules, including BSA, ascorbic acid, C-reactive peptide, D + glucose monohydrate, and uric acid, demonstrate a relative intensity of 36–72% relative to that of insulin, in ascending order. Spike test using bovine pancreas insulin reveals recoveries of 100.8–107.1%. The performance of the sensor was compared with that of ELISA, and both show agreement with each other in terms of performance.

Surface Plasmon Resonance-Based Sensor Array

A sensor array was developed by Singh [135], which achieved simultaneous detection of four samples, integrated with a microfluidic technique. The aptasensor is based on the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) technique, where a camera is employed to detect the changes in refractive index as analytes bind to aptamers immobilized on the sensor surface. A gold chip is used, functionalized with a self-assembled layer created from cysteamine crosslinking with PANAM dendrimer through glutaraldehyde. Carboxylated quantum dot (QD) was then bound to the dendrimer, allowing immobilization of amino-modified aptamer onto the QD, followed by BSA as a blocking agent. Calibration curves for the estimation of insulin concentration are created via a set of standards with known concentrations. Two-fold diluted serum samples were tested with the sensor, obtaining a low LOD of 800 fM, linear within the range of 0.8–250 pM. Results from the sensor and ELISA are also relatable to one another when tested on a diabetic patient’s serum. Nonetheless, selectivity, recovery, and stability assays are not conducted.

Liquid Crystals-Based Sensor

Chen et al. [136] constructed a label-free liquid crystals (LCs)-based biosensor for insulin detection that operates at an aqueous-LC interface. In their work, the glass surface was modified with dimethyloctadecyl[3-(trimethoxysilyl)propyl]ammonium chloride (DMOAP) and 3-glycidylpropoxytrimethoxysilane (GPTMS). The amino-terminated IGA3 aptamers were immobilized onto the glass via crosslinking with GPTMS. Nematic LCs, namely 4-cyano-4′-pentylbiphenyl (5CB). They were anchored homeotropically (perpendicular) on the glass through the alignment agent, DMOAP. The principle of biorecognition relies on the optical response of LCs to surface-induced alignment changes. In the absence of insulin, the alignment of LCs remains in place, resulting in a dark optical image as light is prevented from passing through the cross-polarizers. The dark-to-bright transition occurs as the immobilized aptamer selectively binds insulin, which disrupts the LCs’ alignment in localized regions, yielding bright areas under polarized light. Quantitative detection was achievable by analyzing morphological differences (e.g., brightness) of polarized optical microscopic images. Specificity of the sensor was evaluated against IGF-I, IGF-II, IGFBP3, and GH. All the interferences only manage to generate 20% of the intensity generated by insulin detection. To assess the sensor’s storage stability, the aptamer-conjugated glass and GPTMS/DMOAP modified were stored at 4 °C and dry conditions (room temperature) accordingly. After 15 days of storage, the sensor preserved a significant signal (> 90% of the initial signal). Intra and inter-batch variation of the sensors was also evaluated, generating good reproducibility with RSD lower than 10%. Moreover, insulin spiked diluted urine was tested, obtaining recovery ranging from 93.4 to 113.18% with RSD of 5.9–14.1%.

Roles of Nanomaterials in Aptasensors

Signal amplification is often needed in the construction of biosensors, specifically for the detection of analytes with low concentration. In this case, nanomaterials are widely utilized to overcome limitations (e.g., high detection limit and low sensitivity) of the unmodified biosensing surface (e.g., electrode) and thus enhance their performance [138]. Nanomaterials are defined as materials with at least one dimension in the nanoscale (1–100 nm) [139]. Due to their small size, they provide a larger surface area compared to bulk materials of similar mass [140]. The improved surface area allows for immobilization of aptamers in greater numbers, which in turn improves sensitivity via signal amplification resulting from efficient aptamer-target binding events [141].

Other than that, metal-based nanoparticles could provide excellent conductivity, which facilitates rapid electron transfer. AuNPs are well-known nanoparticles employed in aptasensor construction, particularly in the field of electrochemical aptasensors. Owing to its biocompatibility, inertness, excellent electrical conductivity, and high surface area-to-volume ratio, AuNPs are widely utilized, particularly for the development of electrochemical biosensors [142, 143]. Several studies employed AuNPs for the development of electrochemical insulin aptasensors, achieving excellent LODs at which the lowest down to femtomolar [110, 114, 116]. A huge difference is observed between the LOD of the insulin aptasensor constructed with unmodified gold electrodes and AuNPs-modified electrodes. The former achieves picomolar to femtomolar detection while the lowest LOD achievable for the latter is only at the nanomolar level [109–111, 114, 116]. This indirectly proved the nanosize effect of nanoparticles compared to bulk materials, which could enhance the biosensor’s performance. Due to its popularity, the protocol for AuNPs synthesis is also well-explored [144]. Another advantage of AuNPs-modified surface is the facile immobilization of thiol-labeled aptamer and gold/gold nanoparticles modified surface, for the spontaneous formation of well-oriented self-assembled monolayer. As shown in Table 3, the thiol-mediated immobilization approach is utilized in almost all cases where the insulin aptasensors involve the usage of gold/AuNPs modified electrodes [109–111, 114, 119]. A major drawback to be concerned with AuNPs is the tendency to aggregate with increased salt concentration (exposed to biological samples) and the relatively high cost [145].

The nanoscale quantum dots (QDs), typically within 2–10 nm, exhibit unique optical properties due to quantum confinement effects [146, 147]. The QDs exhibit wide-ranging absorption and emission spectra (ultraviolet to visible light) dependent on their size. Generally, the bandgap for electron-hole pair excitation increases as the size of QDs reduces. Their tunable optical properties make them extensively used in optical biosensing for specific applications [148, 149]. Several studies have utilized the fabrication of quantum dots for the development of insulin aptasensors based on optical biosensing [115, 125, 126, 130, 135].

Despite the excellent optical properties of QDs, the metallic QDs are highly toxic, which raises concerns about potential health and environmental hazards [150–152]. Structural modification of QDs by overcoating the core QDs with a protective shell (e.g., Zinc Sulfide (ZnS)), creating core-shell quantum dots as a workable solution. Such modification reduces the toxicity associated with metallic QDs (mitigates the exposure of heavy metal), at the same time enhancing photostability and fluorescence intensity [153, 154]. Among the metallic QDs, Cadmium Selenide/Zinc Sulfide (CdSe/ZnS) is most extensively studied, while InP/ZnS is advantageous because they are less toxic. Based on our observation, QDs (CdSe/ZnS QDs) modified aptasensor shows the best LOD (800 fM) compared to others, ranging from 1.1 nM to 22.7 fM. This could be attributed to the high surface area to volume ratio and excellent quantum yield of CdSe/ZnS QDs.

Carbon-based QDs, which are also known as carbon quantum dots (CQDs), are a safer alternative to metallic QDs (negligible toxicity), with lower cost [155, 156]. Red emissive carbon quantum dots (R-CDs) are emerging in the field of optical biosensing owing to several benefits as compared to other carbon dots (e.g., blue emissive carbon dots). Firstly, autofluorescence is minimized in the red/near infrared wavelengths as biological molecules predominantly emit at shorter wavelengths [157]. Thus, increases signal-to-noise ratio, making it ideal for ultrasensitive biosensing, particularly in complex biological samples. An R-CDs-based insulin aptasensor developed by He et al. [126] demonstrated good anti-interference performance against several ions when tested alongside insulin. However, its performance in complex matrices has not been investigated [126]. Other than that, R-CDs offer excellent photostability as they are capable of maintaining consistent fluorescence intensity even when subjected to prolonged light exposure [158, 159]. The most significant challenges with the usage of R-CDs are the sophisticated and unstable synthesis techniques, which could cause performance and poor quantum yield at times [160–162].

Compared with other QDs, amorphous molybdenum sulfide (a-MoSx) could be a better option in photoelectrochemical (PEC) biosensing. A-MoSx is highly photocatalytic owing to its ability for visible light absorption and the abundance of active sites (unsaturated sulfur). Nonetheless, one critical downside of a-MoSx is its poor electron transfer capability [163, 164]. In PEC biosensing, efficient photoelectric conversion is of utmost importance. Doping with conductive metal (e.g., gold nanoparticles) could resolve the issue. AuNPs, other than electrochemical properties (efficient charge transfer), also possess plasmonic properties, capable of enhancing light absorption [142, 165]. The wide linear range (spanning nanomolar to picomolar concentrations) obtained by an a-MoSx/Au modified PEC aptasensor for insulin detection could suggest that the synergistic effects of MoSx/Au nanocomposites contribute to strong photoelectrochemical properties [119].

Techniques for Aptamer Immobilization

A key step in the construction of aptasensors is the immobilization of aptamers onto solid surfaces (e.g., electrode). Meticulous selection of immobilization techniques is vital to mitigate the sensor’s performance. Several strategies are applied in aptamer immobilization, mainly categorized into physical and chemical methods (Figure 7).

(a) Physical adsorption; (b, f) chemisorption on gold; (c, d) covalent attachment through EDC/NHS coupling; and (e) glutaraldehyde coupling [166, 167].

Physical adsorption involves aptamer attachment onto the electrode surface with non-covalent bonds, e.g., electrostatic interaction and Van der Waals forces [168]. For instance, Sun et al. [124] constructed an insulin aptasensor by immobilizing the insulin-targeting aptamer, namely, IGA3, onto SiO2@GOAG surface via physisorption through hydrogen bond and π − π stacking. Several major concerns with physical adsorption are the potential of aptamer detachment off the surface due to weak interactions and difficulty in controlling the aptamer orientation. However, this approach is simple, cost effective, and versatile (applicable for a wide range of materials and aptamer). In addition, as it does not involve chemical reaction, the structure of aptamer can be fully preserved [169].

Chemical immobilizations are more commonly used in the field of aptasensor development, through thiol-mediated chemisorption or covalent crosslinking [170]. Thiolated aptamers are spontaneously attached onto a gold/gold nanoparticle fabricated surface covalently, causing the formation of a uniform, well-oriented monolayer, known as a self-assembled monolayer (SAM) [171]. The interaction between the sulfur group and gold (Au–S) is a strong bond with covalent and ionic character, approximately 50 kcal/mol [172, 173]. Although thiol-metal SAM is relatively facile compared to other chemical approaches, the need for noble metal, particularly gold, increases the cost of production. Thiol-mediated SAM requires aptamer modification with a thiol group. Other than that, the Au–S bond is susceptible to oxidation, which may limit its shelf-life [174]. The thiol-gold SAM approach is preferred for electrochemical-based insulin aptasensing, indicated by their uses in several studies [109–111, 114, 116, 119]. This could be related to the fact that highly conductive gold electrodes offer additional advantages for electrochemical detection.

Crosslinking is performed with chemical crosslinkers, either EDC/NHS or glutaraldehyde. Referring to Table 4, EDC/NHS coupling is most used among optical-based insulin aptasensors [125, 126, 135]. EDC/NHS crosslinking is a two-step process, involving carboxyl group activation by EDC, forming a highly reactive intermediate, and subsequently stabilization of NHS, forming NHS ester. Lastly, the primary amine (strong nucleophile) attacks the NHS ester, displaces NHS, and forms an amide bond with the carboxyl group [173]. EDC/NHS coupling involves cumbersome procedures. The intermediates formed during the process are susceptible to hydrolysis, which could cause reduced coupling efficiency (fewer bond formations) [175]. Moreover, the functionalization of aptamer or transducer surface with an amine group and/or carboxylic group is mandatory. Covalent amide bond (C–N) generated has approximately 70 kcal/mol, crosslinking involves multiple covalent bonds, which could offer potential for long-term stability [176, 177]. On the other hand, imine bond (C=N) is formed from the dehydration condensation between aldehyde and amine via glutaraldehyde-mediated crosslinking. The imine bond has a bond energy of 147 kcal/mol, way stronger than that of amide and Au–S bond [178]. Despite its high bond energy, it is unstable and is highly susceptible to hydrolysis. Other than that, the cytotoxicity of glutaraldehyde is of serious concern for its usage [179, 180].

The long-term stability of aptasensors is largely dependent on the aptamer immobilization technique implemented. Theoretically, the stability of aptasensors should indeed follow this ascending order: physisorption < thiol-gold mediated SAM < EDC/NHS crosslinking < glutaraldehyde mediated crosslinking according to the bond strength. As expected, an insulin aptasensor employing the glutaraldehyde-mediated crosslinking approach managed to retain relatively good stability, with approximately 70% signal after 50 days of storage, under room temperature [132]. However, based on Tables 3 and 4, the stability of the insulin aptasensors does not exactly follow this trend. As reported by Zhao et al. [110], the stability of their insulin aptasensor utilizes thiol-mediated aptamer immobilization, is approximately 90% after a week of storage in the fridge. Surprisingly, Sun et al. [124] achieve similar stability by implementing a physisorption approach. Furthermore, a scenario is observed where even employing the same approach, stability could appear to have huge variations. For example, both studies employ the same immobilization technique (thiol-mediated immobilization), and both insulin aptasensors exhibit approximately 10% signal loss. However, in one study, this occurs after one week of storage, while in the other, it is observed after 20 days [110, 114]. In short, even though immobilization techniques play a role in determining the sensor’s stability, it is not solely dependent on the approach of aptamer immobilization but also on other factors (e.g., nanoparticles). Other than that, longer storage could be more informative for the assessment of the sensor’s stability.

Mechanism of Aptasensing

Aptasensing relies on the ability of aptamers to recognize and bind specific targets through conformational changes in their three-dimensional structure. Upon target binding, the aptamer typically rearranges into or stabilizes a specific shape (e.g., G-quadruplex, hairpin), which is then transduced into a measurable signal. There are typically two types of aptamers, folding and unfolding aptamers. From the term itself, folding aptamer refers to an aptamer that folds upon induced to the target, while the latter works the opposite way. Aptasensors are broadly classified into label-free and label-based approaches [181, 182].

Label-based aptasensors involve the use of tagged aptamers, in which the target binding leads to alteration of the label's position or environment. For electrochemical and optical aptasensors, different labels are utilized due to their different transducing mechanisms. Redox active labels (e.g., methylene blue, anthraquinone, and ferrocene) are commonly conjugated to the aptamer ends for electrochemical-based aptasensors, as their measurement relies on changes in the electron transfer at the electrode surface upon target binding [109–111]. On the other hand, fluorophores (e.g., quantum dots and carbon dots) and quenchers (e.g., graphene oxide and silver) are typically used with optical-based aptasensors, which detect the changes in fluorescence intensity that arise from target-induced conformational change [125, 130, 135]. For the label-based approach, the signal is generated from the label, which serves as an approach for signal amplification. Thus, sensitivity is often higher with these types of aptasensors. On the contrary, conjugation of the label could also alter the structure of the aptamer, impacting the binding affinity towards its specific target [183]. In addition, the need for aptamer modification with the label added complexity to the construction and would increase the cost of production. Stability of the aptasensors could be significantly affected by the degradation or detachment of the label [184].

A label-free approach is a simpler method to directly detect the changes (e.g., absorbance, refractive index, impedance, etc.) upon target biorecognition without the need for labeling. In electrochemical-based aptasensors, soluble redox mediators (e.g., ferricyanide (Fe(CN)6]3−/4−) and ruthenium complexes (Ru(NH3)6]3+)) are added as an external redox probe (in the detection buffer) to generate a signal based on the changes in electron transfer efficiency resulting from the target-induced conformational changes [113, 115]. A label-free aptasensor is much lower in cost as labeling is not required, eliminating the need for expensive reagents and extra processing steps. The aptamer preserves its original affinity and selectivity without being disrupted by the tag. However, it often exhibits lower sensitivity as compared to the label-based aptasensors. Othar than that, label-free based aptasensors could generate immediate signal changes upon target interaction, offering advantages for real-time monitoring [90, 182].

Several strategies are employed for label-free and/or label-based approaches for target detection, which are to be further discussed in the following subsections. Figure 8 illustrates each strategy.

Signal On/Off Strategy

The signal ‘on’ or ‘off’ approach is widely employed in aptasensor development, mainly involving label-based sensors. In an electrochemical setting, it relies on the distance between the aptamer terminal label and the electrode surface. The electron transfer is enhanced, as the redox label is closer to the electrode, and vice versa. In an optical setting, it depends on the distance between the fluorescence tag and quencher at each end of the aptamer. Fluorescence is quenched by the quencher as the two are close to each other. The distance is affected by the conformational switch upon target binding. Whether it is a signal ‘on’ or ‘off’ sensor depends on the aptamer binding mechanism, whether it folds or unfolds upon binding. Several insulin aptasensors regardless electrochemical or optical-based employed this strategy [110, 119, 125, 126, 128, 130, 132, 135]. An example of an electrochemical-based signal-off insulin aptasensor was developed by Sahin et al. [112], In which insulin-specific aptamers were labeled with methylene blue (MB) at the 3′ end. Initially, the MB tag was positioned close to the electrode surface, enabling efficient electron transfer. Upon target binding, conformational changes in the aptamer displaced the MB tag away from the electrode, resulting in a proportional decrease in current due to reduced electron transfer. Optical aptasensors adopting such a strategy are described in the study of Mandani and the team [130]. In this study, quantum dots (InP/ZnS-QDs) attached to the aptamers act as the fluorescent donors, while the silver nanoplates (Ag-NP) are the acceptors or quenchers. The aptasensor utilized surface energy transfer (SET), a distance-dependent, non-radiative energy transfer from the QD to the metal surface. Without insulin, the aptamer remains extended, keeping the QD far from the Ag-NP, and fluorescence is retained. Upon insulin binding, the aptamer folds, bringing the QD within ~ 10 nm of the Ag-NP, triggering SET and quenching the fluorescence.

Target-Induced Strand Displacement Strategy

Aptasensor based on strand displacement strategy involves the use of an aptamer and an additional ssDNA strand that is partially or fully complementary to it (cDNA). In principle, the cDNA hybridizes with the ssDNA aptamer, forming a stable duplex (unfolded, linear aptamer). While the presence of the target induces the dissociation of the duplex, triggering the target-induced conformational changes. Consequently, the cDNA is released, displaced by the target due to affinity. Normally, strand displacement strategies are applied to a label-based approach. Throughout the stand displacement, signals are produced through the various mechanisms by the label. In the case of Zhou et al. [111], the capturing aptamer and cDNA are labeled with different redox labels: methylene blue (MB) and anthraquinone (AQ) at different ends. MB is tagged at the 3’ end of the cDNA while AQ is conjugated at the 5’ end of the aptamer. The detection mechanism of these aptasensors depends on the changes of current signal ratio generated from each redox label throughout the strand displacement (with or without target), provided that electron transfer is enhanced with close contact (redox label to electron surface) and vice versa. Another chemiluminescence-based insulin aptasensor adopting a strand displacement strategy is described [124]. The cDNA is conjugated with gold nanoparticles (AuNPs). As strand displacement occurs, the release of cDNA-AuNPs into the detection buffers catalyzes the luminol-H2O2, generating high chemiluminescence intensity.

Surface-Blocking Strategy

Surface blocking strategy is the simplest strategy among the three. This strategy is mainly for electrochemical aptasensors, applicable for both label-based and label-free aptasensing. The core sensing mechanism of this approach is based on the target-induced steric hindrance due to target binding to the immobilized aptamer, which obstructs the electron transfer at the electrode interface. Several electrochemical-based aptasensors adopted these strategies for insulin measurement [109, 113–116]. As reported by Wu et al. [109], The MB-labeled IGA3 aptamer undergoes conformational changes upon insulin presence, causing interference with the electron transfer rate, leading to current reduce with increasing insulin concentration. On the other hand, Abazar and Noorbakhsh [115] adopted a label-free aptamer in Fe(CN)63−/4−(redox mediator). This study proved the steric hindrance effect from aptamer-insulin complex formation on the electrode surface, resulting in electron transfer blockage as charge transfer resistance increases with insulin concentration.

Aptasensors as a Potential Alternative to the Current Gold Standard Insulin Assay

In the current clinical setting, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) is most widely utilized for insulin detection [185]. ELISA is dependent on the usage of antibodies that are specific to their target. For the quantification of insulin, ELISA is most used in the format of sandwich assay, whereby two antibodies that are specific against the target insulin are used, enabling them to bind to distinct epitopes and used in pairs. The primary antibody captures the target while the secondary antibody, tagged with the reporter group (e.g., biotin), binds to another epitope on the surface of the target insulin. After the addition of the substrate to the enzyme that catalyzes the chemical reaction, the colored product formed is proportional to the amount of insulin, which can be measured through optical density (OD) [186].

Although ELISA is the gold standard, it exhibits several drawbacks which make One setback associated with the usage of immunoassay is the inability to distinguish insulin from its analogs and other insulin-related molecules (e.g., proinsulin) due to the lack of sensitivity of antibodies, potentially leading to cross-reactivity [187]. Apart from that, immunoassays impede normalization of insulin assays due to their high cost, which ranges from approximately $300–600 per kit [188–190]. Moreover, immunoassays are also time-consuming, which usually takes hours, involve complex protocol demanding operation by well-trained personnel and centralized instruments (e.g., microplate reader) for assay [32, 33, 191]. Therefore, these assays are confined to the laboratory setting and are not suitable for real-time or point-of-care (POC) insulin monitoring. One major issue also concerns the inherent drawbacks of antibodies, which are very expensive, have a large batch-to-batch variation, are temperature-sensitive, and are not feasible for modification.

A forementioned, routine insulin monitoring has the potential to significantly enhance diabetes management. However, the current gold standard (ELISA) lacks the capacity for real-time or near-real-time detection, limiting its usefulness for frequent monitoring. This underscores the need for alternative diagnostic platforms that are better suited for routine use. As discussed in the previous section, aptamer-based biosensors (aptasensors) offer a promising solution that provides portable, affordability, timely measurements, simple operations with considerable sensitivity and specificity.

Addressing the Gaps Towards Commercialization of Insulin Biosensors

Over decades of development, with thousands of publications on aptasensing, very few of them have made their path towards commercialization. The main hurdle to this is the long-term storage stability criteria. A minimum of six months of storage is recommended, where none of the abovementioned aptasensors reach the standard [192]. Signal degradation with storage is commonly seen on the insulin aptasensors, which limit their shelf-life to less than a month in most cases. This could be ascribed to phase separation of the aptamer and blocking agent (e.g., polyethylene glycol). Consequently, the increased local aptamer density causes ‘aptamer crowding’, making insulin inaccessible to aptamers [193]. To address this issue, instead of a single thiol group, anchoring an aptamer with multiple thiol groups could serve as a workable solution to prevent phase separation [194]. However, caution must be exercised so that these multiple thiol groups do not impact the ‘standing configuration’ of the aptamer for optimal binding with the target insulin. Another impediment is the long response time required for the abovementioned aptamer measurement, which can be up to 40 min. The time delay of biosensors is a critical barrier for insulin determination, where real-time results provide the most valuable information for immediate actions. Careful decision making on nanoparticles, immobilization techniques, and optimization of the experimental and reaction conditions could significantly affect the binding kinetics and charge transfer efficiency. Selectivity and sensitivity are also key features to be discussed. Several potential interfering agents are revealed to cause signal interruption to a varying extent [112, 132]. In addition, sensitivity remains an issue for POC detection of insulin in human whole blood due to the combined effect of its low concentration and matrix effect [195, 196]. Therefore, generating more potent aptamers is of necessity, providing better options with higher binding affinity towards insulin.

Commercializing aptasensors for clinical or POCT applications heavily hinges on several critical factors: user affordability, scalability, convenience, and flexibility. With the ease of large-scale production within a short duration, without batch-to-batch variation at low cost, aptamers could satisfy all the criteria. Compared to antibodies, aptamer offers plenty of advantages, which solely chemical synthesis involves, without the need for in vivo procedures, highly resistant to environmental degradation (pH and temperature) [75–78]. This further enhances the affordability by reducing unnecessary cost and loss due to product spoilage, as aptamer offers a long shelf life and is chemically stable. Noteworthy, the cost of production for aptamers is at least 10-fold lower than antibodies at the same weight [78, 79]. In terms of scalability, aptasensors are highly compatible with mass-production techniques such as screen printing and inkjet deposition. This enables high-throughput fabrication of disposable biosensors, ensuring consistent quality and supply [197, 198]. Other than that, flexibility is another key advantage in which diverse chemical modification of aptamer can be facile (e.g., labeling). Furthermore, the construction of aptasensors is highly flexible as aptamers are easily integrated into various platforms, including electrochemical, optical, and microfluidic systems. Miniaturization of aptasensors is also possibly achieved by integrating a variety of nanomaterials. Aptasensors can be designed for detection in various biological samples (e.g., blood, saliva, urine, etc.) according to needs [114]. Convenience of use can be achieved as aptasensors could be developed in wearable form, smartphone-integrated, etc. assists in adaptation to daily life. [32, 199–201].

In addition to the technical challenges surrounding the insulin biosensors (e.g., stability, sensitivity, and specificity), translating these technologies into clinical practice presents another layer of complexity. Current diabetes management continues to rely on glucose monitoring, a well-established and globally standardized approach [19, 202]. Although the integration of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems has improved outcomes, suboptimal glycemic control remains a common issue [203]. This reflects the limitations of using glucose levels as an indirect marker for insulin dosing. Despite the potential of insulin biosensors to provide more accurate and personalized therapy, the widespread availability and familiarity of existing technologies reduce the urgency for their adoption. Furthermore, commercialization efforts are hindered by the need for extensive clinical validation, user education, and the redesign of clinical workflows. To support this transition, the establishment of standardized clinical benchmarks for insulin assays is also essential. Continued efforts from expert groups, such as the Insulin Standardization Workgroup, play a crucial role in enabling regulatory acceptance and broader clinical integration [204].

Conclusion

This review paper highlighted the significant potential of insulin biosensors in diabetes management, while also underscoring the lack of effective tools as a major barrier for the implementation of insulin routine monitoring. Aptasensors are nominated as a highly potent candidate for achieving insulin detection, particularly attributed to their advantages for real-time and POC testing. Therefore, the recent insulin-sensing aptasensors are reviewed and discussed. This review concludes that, although aptasensors show great promise, a desired insulin aptasensor with excellent overall performance is yet to be realized. Several gaps and challenges are addressed to achieve the final goal of commercialization.
