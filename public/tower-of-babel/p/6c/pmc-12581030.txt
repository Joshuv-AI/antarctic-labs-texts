Smartphone-based patient self-assessment of neuropathy grade: a validation study of the neuropathy tracker alignment with clinical tests
Authors: Astrid Emilie Kongsmark Madsen, Maria Joy Normann Haverberg, Jakob Eyvind Bardram, Martin Ballegaard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsRepeated evaluation for grading of symptoms and signs in distal symmetrical peripheral neuropathy (DSPN) is a core aspect of patient care and inflicts a burden on both the patient and the healthcare system. We designed the smartphone-based Neuropathy Tracker to enable patients to document symptoms and perform a guided self-examination of signs of neuropathy by themselves. We investigate the alignment and classification strength of this grading with grading performed by professionals.MethodsWe included 21 patients with DSPN and 18 controls. In a test-retest paradigm, the patients used the Neuropathy Tracker, and they had examinations by clinical professionals to grade their neuropathy using the Utah Early Neuropathy Scale (UENS) and the Total Neuropathy Score – Clinical Version (TNSc). Further, they had quantitative examinations of Temperature Thresholds and Nerve Conduction Studies for amplitudes of Sural Sensory Nerve Action Potentials.ResultsIn a Bland-Altman analysis of differences between repeated tests, we found similar 95% confidence limits between Neuropathy Tracker and UENS, while TNSc had fewer wide limits. The comparison between the Neuropathy Tracker and UENS showed no bias in test score differences. Classification with receiver operating curve analysis showed Neuropathy Tracker to be slightly inferior to UENS and TNSc to classify neuropathy, but still with excellent ability, with area under curve values of 0.903±0.053.ConclusionsPatient-administered neuropathy grading using the Neuropathy Tracker could add to the clinical evaluation in DSPN without an in-person visit to the outpatient clinic.

Introduction

Distal symmetrical peripheral neuropathy (DSPN) is a common disorder of the peripheral nervous system1 with an impact on disability and quality of life,2 especially if involving pain.3 The diagnosis and classification of the involved nerve fibres enable both the search for aetiological factors and possible treatments and prognostication. This effort is relying on a detailed clinical investigation of symptoms and signs of sensory, motor and autonomic functional deficits to characterise and localise the neurological dysfunction to the peripheral or central nervous system. After a clinical diagnosis of DSPN, laboratory tests could confirm physiopathology for classification of the nerve pathology and detect aetiology (eg, diabetes). This diagnostic stratification forms the basis of international recommendations for both research and clinical practice.4 5

In clinical practice, any patient will need to have an evaluation by a professional experienced in the treatment of patients to confirm that symptoms are caused by diabetic neuropathy rather than by alternative causes. This investigation will also look for signs of asymmetric and possibly inflammatory neuropathy needing further evaluation for the correct treatment.6

In a resource-deprived healthcare system, all patients need evaluation at the relevant level of specialisation and without unnecessary contact.

This study focuses on health technology tools to help in optimising the clinical course for the patient. A purely symptom-based diagnosis of painful neuropathy does not have sufficient diagnostic power,7 and objective signs of dysfunction could improve the evaluation before a clinical visit. The Neuropathy Tracker is a smartphone-based patient reported outcome tool, guiding the patient to perform self-evaluation of signs of neuropathy.4

The aim of the study is to document that the Neuropathy Tracker grading method of patient’s self-examination of neuropathy grade could replace clinical re-evaluations using established grading methods based on a clinical examination. We explore the reliability of the tool in comparison to clinical evaluation performed by the professional.

Methods and materials

We recruited patients from the Neuromuscular Outpatient Clinic. Patients had symptoms and signs diagnostic of neuropathy4 and no alternative explanations for those. We excluded patients with central nervous system disorders, skin conditions in the legs or who did not have the ability to perform a self-investigation or operate a smartphone device.

Control subjects were recruited among people accompanying patients. They were included if they had no symptoms or signs of neuropathy.

After completing an informed consent, the study participants had an interview to disclose neuropathy history, comorbidities and the use of medication. During the session, previous neurotoxic treatments and results of recent laboratory tests were assessed through access to the electronic patient record.

The patients completed the Neuropathy Tracker procedure as described in detail previously.4 They completed a Doleur Neuropathique-4 (DN4) questionnaire8 9 on neuropathic pain together with a visual analogue scale (0–100) to quantify pain level. Symptoms from autonomic nervous system disorders were recorded by using a Danish version of COMPASS-31.10 11 Patients completed a Danish language version of PROMIS Global Health (v1.2) for structured information on the functional impact of neuropathy.

Participants were asked to complete the Neuropathy Tracker self-evaluation of symptoms and signs of neuropathy, as described in detail previously in detail. They were handed a smartphone with the software pre-installed, and they performed the procedure without supervision in a separate room but with the possibility of calling for assistance. Patients were blinded to the results of other tests performed by the professionals and the professionals were blinded to the results of the patient evaluation.

The grading section of the Neuropathy Tracker guides the user through a systematic self-evaluation for a zero to 44 score grading of non-painful neuropathy symptoms in the legs (0–4) and signs from a pinprick sensation test using a needle (0–24) and a vibration sensation test using the smartphone vibration (0–6), both on successive levels from the toes to the knee on both legs.4 Finally, allodynia and hyperalgesia from touch or pinprick, respectively, are graded (0–4), as well as position sense (0–2) and maximal force on extension of both great toes (0–4).4 We collected qualitative feedback from the patient experience using the app for later improvements to the design.

Trained professionals completed a clinical neuropathy evaluation using the UENS12 and the 7-Item Total Neuropathy Score – Clinical Version (TNSc) (table 1).13

Clinical neuropathy scale composition

Composition of neuropathy scales used in the study. Total Neuropathy Score – Clinical version (TNSc) and to a lesser degree Neuropathy Tracker grades symptoms as well as findings from clinical examinations. The grade within each item represents both severity and distribution of dysfunction. Utah Early Neuropathy Scale (UENS) and Neuropathy Tracker grades allodynia and hyperaesthesia on examination while TNSc grades this as a symptom.

The TNSc grades symptoms and signs on a zero to 28 scale with 0–4 scores on three areas of symptoms (sensory, motor and autonomic) and four areas of clinical investigation (pin sensibility, vibration sensibility, motor strength and deep tendon reflexes).

The UENS grades neuropathy using a zero to 32 scale based on motor strength (0–4) on great toe extension (0–2 on both feet), pin sensation (0–24) from 0 to 2 levels of pinprick sensation from six segments on both legs, signs of allodynia in feet or toes on both legs (0–2), vibration sense (0–4) and position sense (0–4) on great toes with grading from normal over reduced to absent and ultimately examination of deep tendon Reflexes at the ankle (0–4) on both legs.

To have external validation from non-clinical studies, participants completed the Quantitative Sensory Threshold (QST) determination for temperature on both feet. Both were conducted according to the procedures of the German Network for the Study of Neuropathic Pain and compared with published reference values.14

Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS) were completed in all participants. For this study, we calculated mean peak-to-peak Sensory Nerve Action Potential (SNAP) amplitude from both sural nerves, and we used unpublished national reference values for comparison.

Both the Neuropathy Tracker and clinical evaluations using the UENS and TNSc were repeated at the end of the visit or within a week.

All neuropathy scores were reported as sum scores and sub-scores. Sub-scores were grouped according to examined fibre type into large fibre score (touch, vibration, reflexes and muscle force) and Small Fibre Score (Allodynia, Hyperalgesia and Pinprick). The sub-scores are reported because the Neuropathy Tracker and UENS are focused more on the small fibre signs (28 of 44 and 26 of 42 points) compared with TNSc (8 of 28 points) dominating early neuropathy.

We analysed alignment within the tests by calculating differences in a test-retest paradigm of sum scores and subscores, as well as by examining side differences between the two legs from the Neuropathy Tracker tests and the UENS tests.

The alignment between the tests was examined by calculating differences between the Neuropathy Tracker, UENS and TNSc sumscores and subscores.

The descriptive analysis of differences in sum scores showed no skewness or kurtosis and did not show a significant difference from normal distribution. We used a 1-sample t-test (analysis of variance) to calculate the bias and 95% Limits of Agreement. We then tested for proportional bias using a linear regression analysis with differences as the dependent variable and the mean of absolute values as the independent variable.

The correlation between total scores was analysed by a Pearson product-moment test.

To test the diagnostic properties, we analysed the receiver operating characteristics (ROC) of the Total Scores of Neuropathy Tracker, UENS and TNSc. We examined the effect of changing the state parameter from a clinical neuropathy diagnosis to abnormal laboratory tests using QST thresholds or SNAP amplitudes beyond the −2< z-score ≤ 2 range for the corresponding age group. We reported the area under the curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% limits for each neuropathy score tool. The quality of ROC analysis is summarised with both the Gini Index and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov (K-S) metrics. From this, we propose a cut-off value for each measure.

Ethics statement

The study was conducted according to Helsinki guidelines with all participants being informed before consenting in a written statement. The Regional Ethics Committee was consulted (EMN-2023–01676). They decided the study to be accepted by the Institutional Regulatory Body (EMN-2020–13003). After completion of the study, the data set was anonymised.

Results

We recruited 21 patients (10 male, 11 female) with DSPN and 18 control subjects (eight male, 10 female) for the study. All participants participated in two tests using Neuropathy Tracker, and 13 patients (65%) and 10 controls (53%) were able to complete a second clinical evaluation of UENS and TNSc. Control individuals were significantly younger compared with patients (51.5±17.3 y vs 70.2±8.7; p<0.001), but they had the same BMI (28.7±4.9 vs 27.1±5.7; p=0.78) as we focused on having groups with the same living conditions.

Neuropathy burden in the study showed a moderate load of symptoms and signs with a wide distribution of neuropathy scores. On inclusion, patients had a moderately severe Neuropathy Tracker grade of 14.76±7.22 (mean±SD) (range 0–28) compared with controls 3.94±4.33 (range 0–15). Using the UENS, those values were 16.48±7.22 (range 2–30) and 1.22±1.63 (range 0–5) and for TNSc, 9.67±3.51 (range 2–15) and 0.44±0.92 (range 0–3).

Looking at the relative influence of large fibre sub-scores on the total sum, we found Neuropathy Tracker large fibre points contributed only 27.4% (85 of 310) of the summated points in patients and 33.8% (24 of 71) in controls. Using UENS, we found a higher contribution from large fibre sub-scores, contributing 50.6% (175 of 346) of points in patients and 77.3% (17 of 22) in controls, while TNSc large fibre scores accounted for 57.6% (117 of 203) of points in patients and 75.0% (6 of 8) in controls. As we designed sub-scores to include all point-giving items, we conclude that the small fibre score points are correspondingly more dominant among patients using the Neuropathy Tracker relative to the UENS and TNSc.

Twelve patients (57.2%) reported neuropathic symptoms with the DN4 (median 4, range 0–6) in contrast to only three controls (16.7%) (median 0, range 0–3). They reported autonomic symptoms with Compass-31 scores not significantly higher (χ2 test: p=0.08) with patients (median 14.5, range 0–45) compared with controls (median 9.0, range 0–22). The impact of their condition, measured by PROMIS, showed significantly higher scores (χ2 test: p=0.012) among patients (median 5.0, range 0–8) compared with controls (median 2.0, range 0–8).

The alignment of sum scores and sub-scores on test-retest studies showed no significant bias in differences between test scores within Neuropathy Tracker, UENS or TNSc (figure 1 and online supplemental table 1). The limits of agreement had similar ranges encompassing 95% of differences with Neuropathy Tracker and UENS for both sum scores and sub-scores, with the SD slightly larger using the Neuropathy Tracker. The TNSc had smaller ranges of differences between the lower possible absolute score ranges at hand. The absolute test sum scores correlated between repeated tests at a high level with Pearson R values for Neuropathy Tracker at 0.920 (95% CI 0.851 to 0.957), UENS at 0.915 (95% CI 0.805 to 0.965) and TNSc at 0.963 (95% CI 0.911 to 0.985) (online supplemental table 1).

Between legs, both the Neuropathy Tracker and the UENS scores had no significant bias and similar ranges of differences (online supplemental table 1).

Comparing the Neuropathy Tracker with UENS, we found no significant bias in differences between sum scores and small fibre sub-scores. The 95% CIs of differences showed large ranges (−10.77–11.43 and −6.53–8.37 points) with both scoring tools. We found significant bias of score differences between NT and TNSc and between UENS and TNSc at a comparable level (4.36 and 4.03). Absolute sum scores of Neuropathy Tracker and UENS correlated reasonably well (fitted correlation: UENS score=0.39 + TN score × 0.93 with Pearson R of 0.800 (95% CI 0.648 to 0.970) with a reduced value looking at patients alone (Pearson R 0.703; 95% CI 0.389 to 0.870). Between Neuropathy Tracker and TNSc and between UENS and TNSc, the correlation was also acceptable with Pearson R at 0.663 (95% CI 0.440 to 0.810) and 0.849 (95% CI 0.729 to 0.919), respectively (for more detail, please refer to online supplemental table 2). Fitted curves reflected the bias found above (NT score=4.3 + TNSc score × 1.01; UENS score=1.4 + TNSc score × 1.49).

Analysing data exclusively from the patient group, we found the same results with no significant bias or differences between NT and UENS scores, but significant and larger biases between NT-TNSc and UENS-TNSc differences (5.1 and 6.8 points), suggesting that a study to establish a transformation would be needed to compare scores in the clinical setting.

Looking at the impact of absolute neuropathy grades, we found no proportional bias, suggesting larger differences with higher total scores between NT and UENS (p=0.146) in the entire cohort or in neuropathy patients in isolation (p=0.999). Differences between TN and TNSc and between UENS and TNSc had a significant proportional bias, though, looking at both the entire cohort (p=0.001 and p<0.001) and within the neuropathy group (p=0.002 and p<0.001).

Overall, the differences between test results from NT and UENS were less prominent in the small fibre sub-scores regarding both bias and proportional bias (online supplemental table 1). Comparing both NT and UENS to TNSc, small fibre sub-scores reflected substantial bias and proportional bias.

The ability to discriminate between healthy subjects and patients was analysed by their ROC (table 2). Using their inclusion classification as analysis state, UENS and TNSc showed outstanding discriminatory ability with AUC values of 0.988±0.14 and 0.995±0.007 (AUC±SE), respectively. compared with this, the Neuropathy Tracker had less but still excellent ability with an AUC of 0.903±0.053.

An alternative diagnostic confirmation of neuropathy often involves quantitative assessment of sensory thresholds or Nerve Conduction Studies. We found ROC AUC values from the three neuropathy grading tools (Neuropathy Tracker, UENS, TNSc) low, but except for WDT, all did act as reasonable classifiers with 95% CI being above the 50% line of no effect (table 1).

Discussion

We aimed to investigate the validity of patient self-evaluation of a clinical neuropathy grade compared with a grading performed by a trained professional. Using smartphone-based grading was well accepted by the patient, and the interaction with the patient on interpretation of instructions was collected for later redesign of the app.

We found that the Neuropathy Tracker scores had 95% confidence limits for differences between test-retest values comparable to those found with UENS, on which we designed the patient-performed score. The comparison between the two tests also showed no significant bias overall and no proportional bias with changes in the level of neuropathy scores, suggesting that we could use patient-reported scores in parallel with evaluations performed by the professionals. This conclusion covers both the total neuropathy grading scores and subscores of the small fibre test and, to a lesser extent, the large fibre function test.

Comparing Neuropathy Tracker and UENS to the TNSc neuropathy grading, we did find a narrower confidence range in the TNSc evaluation, examining both test-retest and intertest differences. This result is at least partially explained by the TNSc having a lower range of scores (0–32) compared with the Neuropathy Tracker (0–44) and the UENS (0–38). We also found a significant bias between the test score results, with lower TNSc scores explained by the different scale compositions and a heteroscedastic distribution, giving a proportional bias with higher differences for high neuropathy scores. As a result, we cannot support using the Neuropathy Tracker as a supplementary test to follow patients otherwise examined with the TNSc tool without conversion of the scores.

Translation of the neuropathy scores into a severity grade has implications for both clinical studies and clinical practice. With the TNSc, severity could be described as grade 1 (scores 1–7), grade 2 (scores 8–14), grade 3 (scores 15–21) and grade 4 (scores >21).15 Using the same structure, Neuropathy Tracker grades would be grade 1 (scores 5–11), grade 2 (scores 12–18), grade 3 (scores 19–25) and grade 4 (scores>25). This translation of neuropathy grading needs to be confirmed in a larger dataset of neuropathy patients of different aetiologies.

Due to the scale designs, signs of small fibre dysfunction dominated Neuropathy Tracker and UENS scores. TNSc reflects large fibre dysfunction from vibration sensitivity, muscle strength and tendon reflexes to a higher degree.

We found signs of pinprick scored differently by the patient and the professional evaluator with the Neuropathy Tracker scores dominated by pinprick score points to a much higher degree than found in UENS, built with a comparable design. This suggests that self-evaluation brings out more severe findings in the small fibre domain than investigations by the professional. Whether this is a result of a different sensory stimulus by the different examiners, a result of a different experience of a sensory stimulus elicited by another person or a result of communicating the experience to an external examiner is unclear.

The variability of clinical neuropathy grading was examined in the Rochester Diabetes Study,16 and using ICC analysis of test-retest results, they found a high correlation between both sensory and motor function evaluation and a somewhat lower correlation between assessment of tendon reflexes. This suggests that a grading tool including reflexes is more variable. We did not find this when comparing the differences of TNSc sum scores. With a low number of individuals repeating TNSc tests, we did not have sufficient power to conclude on this, though. We could support the finding of a high validity and correlation between sensory examinations both within and between test tools.

We investigated the ability of the Neuropathy Tracker and the two clinical and four laboratory tests to discriminate between subjects in the neuropathy and the reference groups. We did find that all tests reporting results from a clinical examination performed better than the laboratory-based measurements. We did find smaller differences in AUC characteristics between the three non-laboratory tests but could not compare these differences to prefer any of those tests.

The lower discriminatory ability for the laboratory tests could be partially a result of the inclusion criteria being based on the clinical information of history, symptoms and signs from the referring doctor.

We could add that the value of a laboratory support to a diagnosis is not restricted to the presence of pathology but also includes valuable information on the involvement of specific nerve fibre functions. We use Qualitative Sensory Thresholds and Nerve Conduction Studies to classify the neuropathy and to support the clinical effort to find the aetiology and guide the prognosis.

The strength of our study is the systematic use of a test-retest paradigm to bring out differences, and not relying on correlation studies only. Furthermore, with the inclusion of both UENS and TNSc, we can discuss to what degree a clinical tool could compare to a self-evaluation.
