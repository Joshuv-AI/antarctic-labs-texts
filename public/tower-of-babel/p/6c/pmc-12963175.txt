Symptoms of depression and anxiety are early predictors of multi-domain disability progression in progressive MS
Authors: Romy A. M. Klein Kranenbarg, Katelijn M. Blok, Yifan van Hasselt, Sylvia Melisse, Nabil Asahaad, Christiaan de Brabander, Franka Claes, Ilse Hoppenbrouwers, Naghmeh Jafari, Luuk van Rooij, Johnny Samijn, Joost Smolders, Beatrijs Wokke, Janet de Beukelaar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn multiple sclerosis (MS), depression and anxiety are common, yet their relation to disease progression is unclear. We investigated whether Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) symptoms predict neurological disability progression.MethodsIn our prospective cohort of people with primary progressive MS (PPMS), depressive and anxiety symptoms were assessed using HADS. Disability progression at 1 and 2 years was assessed using three predefined endpoints: (1) Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), (2) a three-variable composite endpoint (EDSS, Timed 25-Foot Walk (T25FW), or Arm Function in Multiple Sclerosis Questionnaire (AMSQ)), and (3) a five-variable composite endpoint (EDSS, T25FW, AMSQ, Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT), or Patient-Determined Disease Steps (PDDS)). Logistic regression modelled whether HADS scores predicted disability progression. Boruta feature selection identified the most informative HADS items.ResultsOne hundred eleven adults with PPMS completed the HADS (all ≥ 1-year follow-up; 62 ≥ 2 years). Progression occurred predominantly on individual domains. At 1 year, higher total HADS scores predicted progression on the five-variable composite endpoint (OR 3.10, [95% CI 0.99–9.75], p = 0.05; AUC 0.59, [95% CI 0.47–0.70]). At 2 years, the depression sub-score consistently predicted progression on EDSS (OR 8.88, [95% CI 1.12–70.66], p = 0.04), and the five-variable composite endpoint (OR 9.46, [95% CI 1.50–59.52], p = 0.02). Boruta feature selection identified HADS depression-associated items 6 and 8 as best predictors; together achieving similar predictive accuracy compared to the full HADS questionnaire.ConclusionHigher HADS scores, particularly depressive symptoms, predicted disability progression. These findings indicate prognostic value of patient-reported outcomes in predicting disease worsening and their potential use in guiding personalised treatment in progressive MS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00415-026-13701-8.

Introduction

Depression and anxiety are amongst the most prevalent and impactful neuropsychiatric symptoms in multiple sclerosis (MS), with clinically significant symptoms affecting roughly one-third of patients, and lifetime risk of a depressive disorder approaching 50% [1–4]. They occur more frequently than in the general population or other chronic diseases, and often arise early, sometimes even before MS diagnosis [5–7]. Whether depression should be considered a core symptom of MS rather than a comorbidity remains a topic of ongoing debate [8, 9]. Recognition and treatment of depression and anxiety are important, given their profound impact on quality of life and functioning [10].

Emerging evidence suggests that depression and anxiety in MS are not merely reactive to disability or disease burden. Structural and functional brain alterations distinguish MS patients with depression or anxiety from those without and from healthy controls [11]. There is growing interest in shared immune-mediated mechanisms linking MS and mood disorders, and recent genetic data demonstrate that higher cumulative genetic burden for depression is associated with increased MS disease activity and confirmed disability worsening, suggesting overlapping pathways for both depression and anxiety and disease progression in MS [12–14].

Identifying reliable early predictors of progression in MS remains a critical unmet need, especially with emerging novel therapies targeting progression [15]. We hypothesise that elevated depression and anxiety scores may serve not only as risk factors for disability progression, but also as accessible indicators to identify people with MS at increased risk for subsequent neurological disability worsening, and thus potential candidates for progression-targeted therapies. Previous studies on mood symptoms and disability progression in MS have been inconsistent, likely due to heterogeneous study cohorts combining different MS disease courses and exhibiting wide variation in baseline disability and inflammatory activity [16–19].

To address this knowledge gap, our study focuses on a well-defined, homogeneous cohort of prospectively-followed people with progressive MS—specifically those with a clinical diagnosis of primary progressive disease course of MS (PPMS). We aimed to clarify whether symptoms of depression and anxiety predict subsequent neurological disability progression. In addition, we explored the importance of individual HADS items to prediction of progression, and whether a subset of key HADS items—rather than the full scale—could achieve similar predictive accuracy.

Methods

Study population and inclusion criteria

Adults with a diagnosis of PPMS were recruited into the Dutch multicentre SPIN-P cohort (‘Study to Predict Inflammation and Neurodegeneration in PPMS’); all fulfilled the 2024 McDonald criteria [20, 21]. Participants attend annual visits for clinical assessments and complete questionnaires, including the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). Individual HADS items were classified as depression- or anxiety-related, as specified in the original HADS questionnaire [22]. For this analysis, we included participants with a HADS completed within 1 month of a clinical visit and at least one follow-up visit at least 10 months later, enabling assessment of disease progression whilst accommodating variability in scheduling annual visits. When multiple HADS were available, the earliest was selected to maximise follow-up. Data were extracted from the SPIN-P database on June 11, 2025.

Clinical and patient-reported outcome measures

Annual clinical assessments used in this analysis included the Expanded Disability Status Scale (EDSS), Timed 25-Foot Walk (T25FW), and Symbol Digit Modalities Test (SDMT). Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) comprised the HADS (assessing symptoms of depression and anxiety), the Arm Function in Multiple Sclerosis Questionnaire (AMSQ), and the Patient-Determined Disease Steps (PDDS; assessing walking ability).

Definition of progression outcomes

Baseline was defined as the first clinical visit with HADS completed within 1 month and at least one follow-up visit ≥ 10 months later. The first eligible follow-up visit assessed progression (“progression at 1 year after HADS”), and participants with an additional follow-up visit were also evaluated over a longer timeframe (“progression at 2 years after HADS”).

If data were unavailable for a progression measure, that outcome was considered non-progressive.

Minimally clinically important differences defined progression: EDSS increase of ≥ 1.0 if baseline EDSS ≤ 5.5 or ≥ 0.5 points if > 5.5 [23]; AMSQ ≥ 18-point increase [24]; T25FW ≥ 20% increase or inability to complete the test at follow-up despite being able to perform it at baseline [25, 26]; SDMT ≥ 8 point decrease [27]; PDDS ≥ 1 point increase [28].

Confirmed EDSS progression, assessed in participants with additional follow-up available to provide additional context on the persistence of progression, was defined as persistence at the subsequent visit 1 year later.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were performed in R (v.4.5.1). Baseline characteristics of participants with and without progression were summarised using gtsummary (v.2.2.0) and compared using Fisher’s exact or Kruskal–Wallis test. To evaluate potential effects of data completeness, characteristics were also compared amongst non-progressive participants with limited versus complete progression data (two–three versus four-five available progression variables). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) were calculated with R’s default continuous method; when interpolation yielded non-observed values, the discrete quantile method was applied. Differences in baseline HADS scores were assessed using negative binomial generalised linear models (MASS, v.7.3.65), with progression as predictor and HADS score as outcome, as preliminary descriptive analyses, followed by post hoc Tukey’s test. Sensitivity analyses were performed including only participants with complete data on all five progression measures, and by excluding the AMSQ from the composite progression endpoints, given its limited availability.

To investigate whether HADS scores predicted 1-year and 2-year disease progression, we fitted logistic regression models. HADS scores were log-transformed (+ 0.1), and baseline age was included as a covariate. All candidate baseline parameters (sex, disease duration (time since first symptoms), baseline EDSS and baseline age) were considered for the model. Ultimately, only baseline age was included as a covariate because of its known effect on disease progression [29]. Others were excluded due to the lack of clinical implication in relevance. In addition, given the limited numbers of events, we restricted to a smaller set of co-variables to avoid model overfitting. Model assumptions were checked using packages: car v.3.1.3, DHARMA v.0.4.7 and forecast v.8.24.0. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated.

Considering the sample size and variations in our dataset, we employed a bootstrapping-nested k-fold cross-validation approach. Model performance was estimated in the inner loop using k-fold cross-validation (caret, v.7.0–1), bootstrap resampling in the outer loop assessed the distribution of the cross-validation estimates (boot, v.1.3–31.3). We performed 100 bootstrap iterations and within each bootstrap sample of the dataset, we performed fivefold cross-validation. This resulted in a distribution of area under the curve (AUC) values. From this distribution, we calculated the mean AUC, generated the average receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (pROC v.1.18.5) across the fivefold, and estimated confidence intervals for the average ROC curve, which were used for final visualisation. Statistical differences between model AUCs were assessed using one-way ANOVA and post hoc Tukey’s test.

To identify the most influential HADS questions, Boruta feature selection (Boruta, v.8.0.0) was applied using a random forest classifier over 50 bootstrap iterations on 2-year progression data for the five-variable composite endpoint, chosen for its highest discriminative performance and coverage of all progression domains. A new random seed was set for each iteration. Feature importance distributions and selection frequencies were summarised for visualisation. Logistic regression models were then refitted using only Boruta-identified HADS items to assess predictive performance.

Results

Study population and follow-up

In the SPIN-P study, 187 participants with a diagnosis of PPMS completed 411 HADS questionnaires. Of these, 111 participants met the inclusion criteria for primary analysis. A subset of 62 participants completed 2-year follow-up (Fig. 1). The median interval between baseline visit and HADS completion was 6 days [IQR 2–14]. The first follow-up visit ≥ 10 months later, occurred after 378 days [IQR 365–399], and in the 2-year subset the second after 763 days [IQR 734–804]. Overlap between progression domains

Progression was largely confined to individual outcomes, with limited overlap across domains (Fig. 2). At 1 year, for the three-variable composite endpoint, 11 participants (31.4%) progressed on EDSS only, 9 (25.7%) on T25FW only and 8 (22.9%) on AMSQ only, whilst only 2 participants (5.7%) progressed on all three outcomes (Fig. 2A). For the five-variable composite endpoint, progression was similarly mostly limited to individual domains, with no participants progressing on all five outcomes (Fig. 2B). Progression rates and baseline characteristics

At 1 year (n = 111), progression occurred in 17 participants (15.3%) on EDSS, 35 (31.5%) on the EDSS/T25FW/AMSQ composite endpoint and 53 (47.7%) on the five-variable composite endpoint (EDSS/T25FW/AMSQ/SDMT/PDDS). Amongst participants with EDSS progression at 1 year, 10 of 17 had additional follow-up; all showed confirmed progression. At 2 years (n = 62), progression rates were 19 (30.6%) for EDSS, 28 (45.2%) for the EDSS/T25FW/AMSQ composite and 32 (51.6%) for the five-variable composite endpoint. Amongst EDSS progressors at 2 years, 4 had additional follow-up, all confirming progression. Baseline characteristics (Table 1) did not differ by progression status, except that at 1 year, participants with EDSS progression were younger (median 55 [IQR 47–60] vs. 62 years [IQR 55–66], p = 0.014). Sex, disease duration, baseline EDSS and use of psychotropic medication (PTM) or immunomodulatory treatment (IMT) were not significantly different. Amongst non-progressive participants, those with fewer available progression variables (two-three vs. four-five) had higher median baseline EDSS at both 1 and 2 years, and at 1 year, more frequent PTM use. No differences were observed in baseline HADS, sex, age, disease duration or IMT use (Supplementary Table S2). Baseline HADS and progression outcomes at 1-year follow-up

At 1 year, participants with progression on the five-variable composite endpoint (n = 53) had higher baseline total HADS scores (median 10 [IQR 6–14] vs. 7 [IQR 4–11], p = 0.04), whilst anxiety (median 4.0 [IQR 2.0–7.0], vs. 2.0 [IQR 1.0–5.0], p = 0.05) and depression scores (median 5 [IQR 3–8] vs. 4 [IQR 2–7], p = 0.12) did not differ significantly (Fig. 2G, M, S, respectively). For EDSS progression, higher baseline HADS scores were observed but non-significant (median total HADS 11 [IQR 7–13] vs. 7 [IQR 5–13], p = 0.26, anxiety 5 [IQR 2–7] vs. 3 [IQR 1–7], p = 0.21, and depression 5 [IQR 4–8] vs. 4 [IQR 3–7], p = 0.53; Fig. 2E, K, Q, respectively). Similarly, for the EDSS/T25FW/AMSQ composite endpoint, medians were higher in progressive participants, but non-significant (median total HADS 10 [IQR 7–14] vs. 7 [IQR 4–13], p = 0.12, anxiety 4 [IQR 2–7] vs. 2 [IQR 1–6], p = 0.13 and depression 5 [IQR 3–8] vs. 4 [IQR 2–7], p = 0.28; Fig. 2F, L, R, respectively). Sensitivity analyses including only participants without missing data or excluding AMSQ showed similar trends (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Boxplots for HADS scores by progression on T25FW, AMSQ, SDMT and PDDS (Supplementary Fig. S3) show consistent but non-significant trends towards higher scores in progressors.

Baseline HADS and progression outcomes at 2-year follow-up

At 2 years, participants with progression on the five-variable composite endpoint (n = 32), had higher baseline total HADS (median 11 [IQR 7–18] vs. 7 [IQR 5–10], p = 0.01) and depression scores (median 7 [IQR 4–9] vs. 3 [IQR 2–6], p = 0.01), with non-significant differences in anxiety (median 4 [IQR 2–8] vs. 3 [IQR 1–5], p = 0.06; Fig. 2J, V, P, respectively). For EDSS progression, baseline total HADS was higher (median 11 [IQR 7–18] vs. 8 [IQR 4–11], p = 0.02), whilst anxiety and depression scores showed non-significant trends (HADS anxiety: median 5 [IQR 2–10] vs. 3 [IQR 1–6], p = 0.05; HADS depression: median 7 [IQR 4–9] vs. 4 [IQR 2–8], p = 0.06; Fig. 2H, N, T, respectively). For the three-variable composite endpoint, total HADS was again higher in progressors (median 11 [IQR 7–18], vs. 8 [IQR 5–11], p = 0.03), whilst anxiety and depression scores showed non-significant trends (HADS anxiety: median 5 [IQR 1–8] vs. 3 [IQR 1–5], p = 0.07; HADS depression: median 7 [IQR 4–9] vs. 4 [IQR 2–7], p = 0.06); Fig. 2I, O, U, respectively). Sensitivity analyses including only participants without missing data or excluding AMSQ showed similar trends (Supplementary Figs. S1 and S2). Discussion

To identify people with MS at high risk of progression, we aimed to clarify whether (certain) symptoms of depression and anxiety predict subsequent neurological disability progression.

In a well-characterised cohort of people with a diagnosis of PPMS, we found that symptoms of depression and anxiety measured by the HADS predicted subsequent neurological disability progression across clinician-assessed and patient-reported outcomes. This association was driven predominantly by depressive symptoms: at 1 year, only total HADS predicted progression, whilst at 2 years, both total and depression scores did. Boruta feature analyses showed predictive accuracy was maintained using just two depression items (HADS questions 6: “I feel cheerful”, and 8: “I feel as if I am slowed down” [22]), supporting the dominant role of depressive symptoms. HADS item 8 emerged as the most consistently important predictor, and item 6 contributed additional discriminative information. Beyond their impact on quality of life and functioning [10], depressive and anxiety symptoms may, thus, also serve as predictors of disease progression, facilitating early identification of patients at risk and guiding treatment in the era of emerging progression-targeted therapies.

Depression and anxiety symptoms were measured using the HADS, which we chose specifically because it is a brief, well-validated screening instrument widely used in MS populations, capturing both depression and anxiety with minimal respondent burden. Both mood symptoms were common in our cohort, consistent with prior reports in MS [1–4]. Early recognition and management are warranted irrespective of disability worsening. Participants who later progressed generally had higher baseline HADS scores than non-progressors, with more pronounced differences at 2 years. Baseline total HADS scores were consistently higher in progressors across all endpoints, and depression—but not anxiety—scores were significantly higher in progressors on the five-variable composite endpoint, reflecting that longer follow-up better captures progression events.

Previous studies on depression, anxiety and disability progression in MS have reported inconsistent findings, likely due to heterogeneous cohorts combining relapsing–remitting MS (RRMS), SPMS and PPMS, with variable inflammatory status and baseline disability [16–19]. In contrast, our clinically homogeneous PPMS cohort with long-standing disease (median disease duration 10–12 years) minimised confounding by inflammatory activity, baseline disability and mood symptoms driven by a recent diagnosis, though it should also be noted that generalisability to earlier progressive MS stages or less disabled populations may be limited. The only baseline difference was age: participants with EDSS progression at 1 year were slightly younger. Although older age at PPMS onset is generally associated with faster disability accumulation [30], EDSS progression was more easily captured in younger participants, likely reflecting a ceiling effect at higher EDSS scores, and more persistent inflammatory disease activity in younger patients [31, 32]. However, chance variation or regression to the mean in this small subgroup (n = 17) cannot be ruled out. After adjusting for age, baseline total HADS scores—particularly depressive symptoms—predicted subsequent disability progression, supporting their potential as early indicators of progression in PPMS.

The mechanisms underlying the association between depressive and anxiety symptoms and subsequent disability progression remain to be fully elucidated. Potential shared immune-mediated and genetic pathways between MS and mood disorders, and brain alterations distinguishing MS patients with depression or anxiety from those without, suggest that depressive and anxiety disorders may reflect chronic compartmentalised inflammation [11–14]. Current mood symptoms, as measured by HADS, could then possibly represent an expression of a shared underlying disease process. However, alternative explanations should be considered. First, reverse causation is possible, whereby early subclinical progression could manifest as mood changes. Second, confounding by unmeasured factors could affect both mood and neurodegeneration.

Most studies on progression independent of relapse activity (PIRA; disability progression not directly related to clinical relapses) or progression independent of MRI activity (PIRMA; progression not directly related to new focal inflammatory lesions) have assessed a single domain, typically EDSS [33–35], capturing only part of heterogeneous disability accrual in progressive MS. We hypothesise that progression across different functional domains reflects heterogeneous manifestations of the same underlying disease process, which may present differently depending on lesion location and load or individual functional reserve. In our study, multiple progression endpoints allowed us to assess disability accrual across clinician-assessed and patient-reported outcomes. Progression usually occurred in a single domain but never across all domains; only ~ 15% of progressive participants had progression solely on the EDSS at both 1 and 2 years, highlighting the heterogeneous manifestation of disability progression. The limited overlap between domains likely reflects the relatively short follow-up rather than fundamentally distinct mechanisms of progression which could be argued to differentially relate to HADS. Importantly, this multidimensional approach also enabled a more specific definition of non-progression, by identifying participants who remained stable across all assessed outcomes. We, therefore, extended the established three-variable composite endpoint (EDSS, T25FW, 9HPT) [23, 36] to a five-variable composite endpoint including SDMT and PDDS. The HADS depression score was consistently associated with progression across all three evaluated progression endpoints at 2 years. Discriminative performance was highest for the five-variable composite endpoint, although absolute differences in AUC compared with EDSS and the three-variable composite endpoint were small and of uncertain clinical relevance, indicating limited added discriminative value beyond EDSS alone. Overall, HADS scores showed modest discriminative ability and are not sufficient enough as a standalone tool for prediction of disability progression. Instead, HADS scores, particularly depressive symptoms, may provide complimentary prognostic information when considered alongside other clinical, imaging or biomarker measures. Modest discrimination may reflect the relatively small sample size and limited follow-up duration, rather than absence of a true predictive signal, and the observed AUC increases from 1-year to 2-year progression suggest that longer term follow-up could improve predictive accuracy. At the same time, the strengthening of associations with longer follow-up and more comprehensive composite endpoints may also partly reflect increased sensitivity to detect progression events through broader outcome definitions. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted cautiously, particularly given the exploratory use of multiple progression definitions. PROMs capture aspects of disability not fully reflected by EDSS and may, therefore, contribute to a more comprehensive assessment of progression, even though their additional predictive value remains modest in the current dataset. Besides, PROMs such as AMSQ correlate with performance-based measures like 9-HPT [37], yet are faster and more feasible for routine use, enhancing patient-centred care, though implementation remains limited [38]. Boruta analyses showed predictive accuracy was preserved using only two HADS items (6 and 8), comparable with previous work indicating a single question can detect depression in MS [39]. External validation is required before such abbreviated assessments could be recommended for clinical use.

Our study has several strengths. First, it was conducted in a clinically homogeneous PPMS cohort, limiting confounding by inflammatory status and baseline disability. Second, by examining multiple progression domains, we demonstrate that disability accrual is often confined to a single domain and rarely captured by EDSS alone, underscoring the limitations of a single measure. Third, the prospective design with standardised assessments reduces recall bias, whilst broad inclusion of all adults with a diagnosis of PPMS minimised selection bias. Fourth, we assessed clinical feasibility by identifying the most informative HADS items using Boruta feature selection, and evaluating predictive performance of models via bootstrapped cross-validation. Together, these approaches support the clinical relevance, statistical robustness and generalisability of our findings.

This study has some limitations. First, only a subset of participants could be included due to incomplete HADS data and limited follow-up, reducing analysis power. Amongst included participants, incomplete progression data—mainly due to functional limitations such as limited walking ability—could have led to misclassification as non-progressive in 10 out of 111 participants at 1 year, and 4 out of 62 participants at 2 years. Comparisons between non-progressors with fewer versus more complete progression data showed only higher EDSS in the former group, reflecting greater baseline disability, and reducing the likelihood of detecting further progression. Importantly, baseline HADS scores did not differ between these groups. Sensitivity analyses including only participants with complete progression data showed similar results, suggesting that incomplete data did not drive the observed associations. These patterns reflect the realities of prospective studies in people with advanced disability and highlight challenges for both research and future clinical practice. Moving forward, as therapies targeting PIRA/PIRMA become available, regular EDSS assessments in routine care will gain greater relevance, as these measurements can directly inform treatment decisions. Furthermore, although limited sample size and follow-up duration reduced predictive power, the consistency of findings across multiple analyses supports the reliability of the observed patterns. In addition, although HADS assessments were generally completed within a few days of the baseline visit, minor variability in timing and the use of the earliest available HADS when multiple assessments existed may have introduced some heterogeneity in exposure measurement. Fourth, we relied on HADS rather than structured neuropsychiatric interviews, the diagnostic gold standard for mood disorders. HADS is a screening instrument, and elevated scores indicate depressive or anxiety symptoms rather than confirmed psychiatric diagnoses. As such, HADS captures a spectrum of symptom severity, including sub-syndromal or transient symptoms, which may differ from clinically diagnosed mood disorders. Importantly, our aim was to investigate whether symptoms of depression and anxiety—rather than diagnosed mood disorders—were associated with subsequent disability progression. Furthermore, although depression and anxiety in MS may reflect more than reactive responses to disability [11–14], this study cannot establish causality with chronic compartmentalised inflammation. Nonetheless, these symptoms remain accessible biomarkers for identifying individuals at risk of future disability progression. Moreover, although our aim was to identify the most optimal and least amount of HADS questions for progression prediction, the three HADS measures (total, anxiety, depression) and three progression endpoint measurements are not independent but associated with each other. This association can limit the interpretability of model comparisons especially given the limited number of events in this study, underscoring the need for larger, independent replications to confirm the results.

In conclusion, brief assessment of depressive and anxiety symptoms using the HADS may provide prognostic information for identifying people with progressive MS at higher risk of disability progression. Whilst these findings support the potential use of HADS for risk stratification and prioritisation of monitoring, their application to guide treatment decisions requires further investigation in independent cohorts. Regardless of their prognostic value, addressing depressive and anxiety symptoms is clinically important for patient well-being and quality of life.
