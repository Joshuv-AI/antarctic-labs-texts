Implementation of a High-Sensitivity Troponin Assay and Its Association With Resource Utilization in Patients With Suspected Acute Coronary Syndrome
Authors: Radhika K Neicheril, Yosef Manla, Daniel Chacon, Kaylee Sarna, Alice Sonnino, Jared Piotrkowski, Kevin Perry, David Snipelisky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo examine the association between the implementation of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) and downstream resource utilization in patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) with suspected acute coronary syndrome (ACS).Patients and MethodsWe analyzed 461 ED encounters of patients with suspected ACS between January 1, 2022 and December 12, 2022. The final propensity score–matched analysis included 300 encounters in which the conventional cardiac troponin (c-cTn) assay (n=150) or hs-cTn assay (n=150) was used. Groups were compared for downstream resource utilization, including cardiology consults, cardiovascular imaging, disposition of care, ED length of stay, and 30-day outcomes, using appropriate statistical testing.ResultsCompared with the c-cTn group, the hs-cTn group had a lower rate of requiring cardiology consults (6 [4%] vs 45 [30%]; P<.001) and reduced downstream utilization of echocardiograms (10 [6.7%] vs 59 [39.3%]; P<.001), nuclear stress tests (6 [4%] vs 27 [18%] P<.001), and cardiac catheterization (2 [1.3%] vs 27 [18%]; P<.001). Significantly fewer patients were referred to observation (17 [11.3%] vs 96 [64%]; P<.001) or admitted (7 [4.7%] vs 42 [28%]; P<.001) in the hs-cTn assay group. In addition, the median ED length of stay was significantly shorter in the hs-cTn group (4 vs 28 hours; P<.001). The 30-day outcomes, including readmission for ACS (0 [0%] vs 0 [0%]; P>.99) or mortality (0 [0%] vs 1 [0.7%]; P>.99) rates, were comparable.ConclusionImplementing hs-cTn in the ED can significantly reduce downstream testing and hospital resource utilization. Wider adoption of hs-cTn assays could present an opportunity to optimize care of patients with suspected ACS.

A substantial proportion of patients presenting to the hospital with symptoms suggestive of acute coronary syndrome (ACS) are ultimately classified as low to intermediate risk.1 Nevertheless, this population frequently undergoes costly and potentially unnecessary cardiac testing, including invasive procedures. The introduction of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) assays has enhanced the evaluation of chest pain in the emergency department (ED), with several studies supporting the superior diagnostic accuracy of the newer-generation hs-cTn assays.2 Although it was originally developed to improve diagnostic accuracy for myocardial infarction (MI) through rapid and precise detection of troponin changes, hs-cTn assays have since gained utility in identifying myocardial injury even in the absence of infarction.3

Despite their growing adoption internationally, hs-cTn assays remain underused in the United States, and most US hospitals continue to use less-sensitive assays.4,5 Recently reported findings from the National Cardiovascular Data Registry Chest Pain-MI Registry found that among 550 participating hospitals, the use of hs-cTn assays increased from 3.3% in 2019 to only 32.6% in 2021.4

Additionally, real-world data on the impact of hs-cTn implementation on downstream resource utilization in patients presenting with ACS remain limited,4,6, 7, 8 with published studies showing variability across institutions. Moreover, concerns exist on how the increased sensitivity of hs-cTn may lead to unnecessary admissions and follow-up testing.6

Therefore, in this single-center experience from the United States of patients presenting to the ED with suspected ACS, we aimed to examine the association between hs-cTn implementation and downstream resource utilization, including cardiology consultations, cardiac imaging, invasive coronary angiography, and length of stay. Furthermore, we evaluated both immediate outcomes during the initial ED visit and subsequent outcomes at 30 days postdischarge.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Population

We retrospectively analyzed ED encounters involving patients evaluated for suspected ACS between January 1, 2022 and December 12, 2022 at a single tertiary care center (Cleveland Clinic Florida); 461 encounters were included. Encounters were stratified based on assay used: conventional cardiac troponin (c-cTn; n=232; fourth generation; Roche Diagnostics; lower reportable range, 0.010; 99th percentile, <0.010 ng/mL; 10% coefficient of variation [CV], 0.030) and hs-cTn (n=229; Roche Diagnostics; lower reportable range, 6 ng/L; 99th percentile, 12 ng/L; combined sex reference interval from TRAPID-AMI study; 10% CV, 11). The assay used was based on the physician’s choice. Data on patient demographic characteristics, clinical characteristics, laboratory and imaging findings, HEART scores, resource utilization metrics, and clinical outcomes were extracted through a structured chart review. The institutional review board approved this study with a waiver of informed consent based on its retrospective study design.

Patients evaluated with the c-cTn assay underwent testing at baseline, 6 hours, and 12 hours, with results guiding physician decisions regarding further testing and admission. For the hs-cTn group, a single order in the electronic medical record automatically generated blood draws at 0, 1, and (if indicated) 3 hours as per the following rapid rule-out decision algorithm:

•If the initial hs-cTn was ≥52 ng/L, patients were directly admitted to inpatient.

If the initial hs-cTn was ≥52 ng/L, patients were directly admitted to inpatient.

•If the initial (h0) hs-cTn was <12 ng/L, testing was repeated at 1 hour: if the 0-1 hour change (Δ) was <3 ng/L, the 3-hour sample was not triggered, and MI was ruled out; a Δ of 3-9 ng/L prompted a 3-hour sample; and a Δ of ≥10 ng/L led to an inpatient admission.

If the initial (h0) hs-cTn was <12 ng/L, testing was repeated at 1 hour: if the 0-1 hour change (Δ) was <3 ng/L, the 3-hour sample was not triggered, and MI was ruled out; a Δ of 3-9 ng/L prompted a 3-hour sample; and a Δ of ≥10 ng/L led to an inpatient admission.

•If the initial hs-cTn was 12-51 ng/L, a 1-hour retest was obtained, and management depended on Δ values: Δ < 10 ng/L → obtain a 3-hour sample; Δ ≥ 10 ng/L → admit as inpatient.

If the initial hs-cTn was 12-51 ng/L, a 1-hour retest was obtained, and management depended on Δ values: Δ < 10 ng/L → obtain a 3-hour sample; Δ ≥ 10 ng/L → admit as inpatient.

•For all patients who proceeded to 3 hours, decisions were based on the 0-3-hour Δ values: Δ < 6 ng/L → rule-out; Δ = 6-9 ng/L → admit to observation as appropriate; and Δ ≥ 10 ng/L → admit as inpatient.

For all patients who proceeded to 3 hours, decisions were based on the 0-3-hour Δ values: Δ < 6 ng/L → rule-out; Δ = 6-9 ng/L → admit to observation as appropriate; and Δ ≥ 10 ng/L → admit as inpatient.

The need for subsequent diagnostic testing or consultation was ultimately determined at the discretion of the treating physician, guided by c-cTn/hs-cTn results and the overall clinical context.

Propensity Score Matching

To account for differences in baseline patient characteristics 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with 0.1 SMD was conducted for age; a medical history of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia; a history of coronary artery disease (CAD), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), MI, or stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA); and tobacco use. The groups comprised 150 patients each after PSM, and SMD values were provided for matching characteristics.

Clinical Outcomes

After PSM, a comparative between-groups analysis was conducted for downstream resource utilization, including cardiology consultations and cardiovascular diagnostic testing (transthoracic echocardiography, nuclear stress testing, coronary computed tomography angiography, and invasive coronary angiography). Additional end points included disposition from the ED (direct discharge from ED, admission to observation, or inpatient hospitalization) and the overall length of stay of the patient. Thirty-day clinical outcomes, including all-cause mortality and readmission for ACS, were also assessed.

Statistical Analyses

Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages and were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Continuous variables were presented as medians (interquartile range [IQR], Q1, Q3) and compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. P values of <.05 were determined to be statistically significant in this analysis. SAS 9.4 (SAS) and R 4.4.1 (R Foundation) were used to analyze the data.

Results

Clinical Characteristics of the Study Cohorts Before Propensity Matching

A total of 461 ED encounters were included in the analysis, with a median patient age of 66 years and 226 (49%) women. Comorbid conditions were common, including diabetes mellitus in 136 patients (29.5%), hypertension in 275 (59.7%), and dyslipidemia in 222 (48.2%). A history of CAD, PCI, MI, stroke, or TIA was present in 139 encounters (30.2%), whereas 81 (17.6%) encounters featured patients with a family history of CAD or congestive heart failure. The median estimated glomerular filtration rate was 79.0 mL/min/1.73 m2 (IQR, 60.0-95.0 mL/min/1.73 m2), and the median HEART score was 4.0 (IQR, 3.0-5.0) (Table 1). Patients evaluated with hs-cTn assays were younger (median age, 61 vs 70 years; P<.001) and had a lower burden of cardiovascular comorbidities than those assessed with c-cTn assays. Specifically, lower rates of diabetes (50 [21.8%] vs 86 [37.1%]; P<.001), hypertension (109 [47.6%] vs 166 [71.6%]; P<.001), and dyslipidemia (99 [43.2%] vs 123 [53%]; P=.04) were observed. Additionally, the hs-cTn cohort had a significantly lower prevalence of established cardiovascular disease, including prior CAD, PCI, MI, stroke, or TIA (33 [14.4%] vs 106 [45.7%]; P<.001) (Table 1).

Clinical Characteristics and Outcomes in the Propensity Score–Matched c-cTn and hs-cTn Groups

After 1:1 PSM was conducted for age; a medical history of diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia; a history of CAD, PCI, MI, stroke, or TIA; and tobacco use, 300 patients were included in the analysis. The median patient age was 65.5 years. Half of the patients (n=150) were women. No significant differences were observed in baseline characteristics after PSM (Table 2). Median HEART scores were comparable between-groups (4 [IQR, 3-5] vs 4 [IQR, 3-5]; P=.18). The use of hs-cTn assays was associated with a markedly lower rate of cardiology consultation (6 [4%] vs 45 [30%]; P<.001) and a higher likelihood of excluding a cardiac etiology for chest pain (140 [93.3%] vs 119 [79.3%]; P<.001) than with the use of c-cTn assays; final cardiac diagnoses are presented in Figure 1. Correspondingly, downstream diagnostic testing was significantly reduced in the hs-cTn group, including transthoracic echocardiography (10 [6.7%] vs 59 [39.3%]; P<.001), nuclear stress testing (6 [4%] vs 27 [18%]; P<.001), and invasive coronary angiography (2 [1.3%] vs 27 [18%]; P<.001) (Figure 2). Patients in the hs-cTn group were less frequently admitted to observation status (17 [11.3%] vs 96 [64%]; P<.001) or inpatient care (7 [4.7%] vs 42 [28%]; P<.001) and experienced significantly shorter ED length of stay (median, 4 vs 28 hours; P<.001) (Table 3). No significant differences between-groups were observed in 30-day clinical outcomes, including rates of ACS-related readmission (0 [0%] vs 0 [0%]; P>.99) or all-cause mortality (0 [0%] vs 1 [0.7%]; P>.99).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective study of propensity score–matched cohorts, implementing hs-cTn assay in the ED was associated with a significant reduction in downstream resource utilization among patients evaluated for suspected ACS. Compared with c-cTn testing, hs-cTn testing was linked to decreased cardiology consultations, reduced cardiovascular imaging, fewer hospital admissions, and shorter ED length of stay. These findings support the use of hs-cTn and show that its higher sensitivity does not lead to increased testing or admissions due to concerns about false-positive results.

Chest pain is the second most common reason for ED visits in the United States, accounting for over 6.5 million encounters annually.9,10 Accurate and timely triage of chest pain is critical for not only identifying patients with life-threatening conditions but also avoiding unnecessary admissions or testing. Cardiac troponin assays are used in approximately 1 in 5 ED visits, underscoring their central role in ACS evaluation.11 The latest generation of troponin assays, hs-cTn, offers improved analytical performance, detecting troponin levels in at least 50% of healthy individuals with a CV of ≤10% at the 99th percentile. These characteristics enhance the diagnostic accuracy of hs-cTn compared with that of earlier-generation assays.12

Only 5.1% of ED patients with chest pain in the United States will have an ACS and more than half will ultimately be found to have a noncardiac cause.9 In our study, 9.6% of patients were diagnosed with ACS, and 86.3% had noncardiac etiologies of chest pain. The higher than expected ACS rate may reflect the higher cardiovascular risk profile in our patient population. Notably, after PSM accounting for baseline differences in comorbidities, hs-cTn implementation was associated with a 17.6% greater ability to rule-out cardiac causes of chest pain.

Implementing hs-cTn assays was associated with more than 77% reductions in cardiology consultations and downstream testing, including echocardiography, stress testing, and coronary angiography, as well as shorter median ED length of stay. Furthermore, it resulted in a higher rate of direct discharges from the ED, with an 82.3% reduction in referrals to observation and an 83.2% reduction in-hospital readmission rates. Our results align with previously published data supporting the clinical and operational benefits of hs-cTn assays. Ganguli et al,13 in a study of approximately 108,000 ED visits, observed that after hs-cTn implementation, there was an increase in troponin and ECG testing but fewer cardiac coronary computed tomography angiography, stress tests, and PCI. They were also less likely to undergo a cardiology consultation and had shorter ED visits and fewer hospitalizations.13 Similarly, Singer et al8 reported significant reductions in ED length of stay after transitioning to hs-cTn testing at a large academic center. McCarthy et al,4 in their study of participating hospitals in the National Cardiovascular Data Registry Chest Pain-MI Registry from January 2019 through September 2021, examined the associations between hs-cTn use, use of in-hospital diagnostic imaging, and patient outcomes. The authors concluded that hs-cTn assay was associated with a modestly shorter length of stay but a greater use of echocardiography for non–ST-segment elevation ACS and less-invasive angiography among low-risk patients.4

In contrast, Koirala et al7 reported in their single-center study evaluating the transition from c-cTn to hs-cTn that implementation of hs-cTn was associated with an increase in ED length of stay and no substantial change in cardiac testing, cardiology consultation, or ACS diagnoses, among adult ED patients presenting with chest pain. Furthermore, Ford et al6 also reported that transition to hs-cTn was associated with increased ED length of stay and no substantial change in cardiac testing, cardiology consultation, or ACS diagnoses. These differences may reflect variability in institutional protocols, patient demographic characteristics, and local workflows. Our data help to support the utility of hs-cTn in preventing overutilization of such and therefore supports its use.

Finally, we did not observe any significant differences in 30-day ACS readmission rates or 30-day all-cause mortality between the 2 groups. This could be attributed to the small sample size and low event rates. Larger, prospective multicenter studies using standardized protocols are needed to validate the safety, effectiveness, and impact of hs-cTn on patient outcomes and health care utilization. Future research should also assess its cost-effectiveness across diverse health care settings to inform broader adoption.

This study has several important limitations. First, it has a retrospective design and a small sample size, which could introduce potential selection and confounding biases; in an effort to balance confounding variables, propensity-matched scoring was used. Second, the study was conducted at a single-center, and findings may reflect institutional protocols and patient populations that limit generalizability to other health care settings. Third, mid-term outcomes and resource utilization were not evaluated. Finally, the physician’s choice between the 2 assays and differences in measurement intervals may have introduced bias to the study.

Conclusion

The implementation of the hs-cTn assay in the ED was associated with a significant reduction in downstream diagnostic testing and overall hospital resource utilization. These findings suggest that broader adoption of hs-cTn assays may offer a valuable strategy to enhance the efficiency of care for patients presenting with suspected ACS.
