To the Editor:

We read with great interest the comprehensive review by Randerath et al. [1] on nocturnal hypercapnia in obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA) and obesity hypoventilation syndrome (OHS). The authors elegantly highlighted that hypercapnia is not merely a mirror of hypoxia, but an active pathophysiological process influencing ventilatory drive, cerebral blood flow, autonomic activity and renal function. We wish to extend this important framework by proposing that nocturnal hypercapnia may be viewed not only as a respiratory end-point, but also as a measurable signal embedded within a broader sleep–circadian–hypothalamic network.

Recurrent upper-airway obstruction, intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation and arousal – processes that coexist with nocturnal carbon dioxide (CO2) fluctuations in OSA – may also disturb central circadian regulation. The suprachiasmatic nucleus of the hypothalamus coordinates hormonal rhythmicity across the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal, hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal and hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axes. Disruption of sleep continuity can therefore have consequences beyond ventilation. A recent hypothesis proposed that OSA, benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and other sleep-disrupting conditions may induce an acquired, sleep-disruption-related hypogonadism through chronic sleep fragmentation and circadian dysregulation [2]. This concept is supported by experimental evidence showing that even short-term sleep restriction in young healthy men can reduce daytime testosterone levels by 10–15%, suggesting that the chronic sleep fragmentation observed in OSA may exert analogous effects [3]. In male patients with severe obesity, decompensated OSA has also been associated with lower total testosterone, while successful continuous positive airway pressure therapy was followed by increased testosterone concentrations in a small longitudinal subset (n=14), independently of body mass index (BMI) change [4].

A particularly relevant mechanism linking OSA to this broader network is the atrial natriuretic peptide (ANP)–antidiuretic hormone (ADH; also known as arginine vasopressin, AVP)–aquaporin vicious cycle. OSA-induced negative intrathoracic pressure and intermittent hypoxia can promote atrial distension and ANP release. Elevated ANP may antagonise ADH-driven renal water reabsorption and reduce aquaporin expression in renal collecting ducts, thereby contributing to nocturnal polyuria and potential refractoriness to AVP supplementation [5]. Nocturnal polyuria, in turn, worsens sleep fragmentation, which may further impair hypothalamic circadian regulation. This self-reinforcing loop provides a clinically plausible bridge between respiratory events, renal water handling, nocturia and endocrine dysregulation.

The same sleep–circadian disruption may also intersect with autonomic regulation. Circadian dysregulation and sleep fragmentation have been proposed to contribute to autonomic nervous system imbalance, overactive bladder symptoms and nocturnal polyuria in men with BPH, with OSA acting as a potential amplifier of this cycle [6]. This is clinically important because autonomic instability, altered blood pressure patterns, nocturia and metabolic dysfunction frequently coexist in patients with OSA/OHS, yet they are often managed across separate specialties.

Downstream of hypothalamic and endocrine disruption, mitochondrial dysfunction may represent a convergent biological pathway linking OSA-related sleep disturbance to metabolic disease. Sleep deprivation and circadian disruption can perturb mitochondrial homeostasis, including mitochondrial morphology and abundance, respiratory-chain activity, ATP production, membrane potential and reactive oxygen species (ROS)/redox balance [7]. In men, testosterone levels correlate positively with insulin sensitivity, maximal oxygen uptake and skeletal-muscle oxidative phosphorylation gene expression, suggesting a link between androgen status, mitochondrial function and insulin resistance, although this association is attenuated after adjustment for BMI [8]. In patients with type 2 diabetes, low testosterone has also been associated with reduced mitochondrial membrane potential, increased mitochondrial ROS production and impaired antioxidant defences, although in vitro evidence is mixed regarding the direct causal effect of testosterone on mitochondrial parameters [9]. More broadly, mitochondrial dysfunction is increasingly recognised as a shared mechanism in metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease and neurodegenerative disorders [10].

From a clinical perspective, this complementary framework does not diminish the importance of CO2 measurement or positive airway pressure (PAP) therapy. Rather, it suggests that PAP therapy, while essential, may not fully reverse downstream endocrine and metabolic consequences in all patients, particularly when obesity, nocturnal polyuria, circadian misalignment and autonomic dysregulation persist. Future studies in OSA/OHS could therefore benefit from integrating nocturnal transcutaneous CO2 monitoring with markers of circadian and hypothalamic function, including sleep fragmentation indices, nocturnal urine production, ANP or copeptin, testosterone and gonadotropins, autonomic measures such as heart rate variability, and metabolic or mitochondrial biomarkers.

In conclusion, the CO2-focused framework proposed by Randerath et al. [1] provides a crucial foundation for understanding nocturnal hypercapnia in OSA and OHS. We propose that this framework can be further enriched by positioning nocturnal hypercapnia within a broader sleep–circadian–hypothalamic network involving endocrine dysregulation, nocturnal polyuria, autonomic imbalance and impaired mitochondrial homeostasis. Such an integrated perspective may help refine phenotyping and guide more comprehensive management strategies for patients with OSA/OHS and metabolic complications.
