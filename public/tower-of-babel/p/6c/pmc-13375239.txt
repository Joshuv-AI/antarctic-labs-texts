Roll-to-roll compatible in situ self-assembly of silver nanostructures on hybrid knitted textile for EMI shielding
Authors: Hamed Mohammadi Mofarah, Hyung Woo Choi, Mehr Khalid Rahmani, Jihane Karib, Ghassan E. Jabbour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A roll-to-roll compatible reactive blade coating self-assembly method has been developed for synthesizing silver nanorods (AgNRs), silver nanocubes (AgNCs), and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) directly on a hybrid knitted fabric made from copper filaments and cotton fibers. The three resulting conductive fabrics, functionalized with AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs, respectively, were evaluated for electromagnetic interference (EMI) shielding effectiveness (SE) across the X-band frequency range (8.2–12.4 GHz). The EMI SE of the fabric increased with additional layers of nanosilver particles. The application of (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) improved hydrophobicity and washing stability while reducing the water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) and air permeability within acceptable levels. Under the present coating conditions, the AgNR-coated fabric exhibited the highest electrical conductivity and EMI SE, achieving a shielding level of 52.9 dB within the X-band frequency range. The results show that in situ silver nanostructure growth combined with post-treatment surface functionalization provides a practical route toward multifunctional EMI-shielding textiles with improved durability.

Introduction

The widespread use of electronic devices and communication networks has made reducing electromagnetic pollution crucial.1,2 High-performance EMI shielding materials are increasingly in demand for applications in communication systems, national defense, and industrial equipment.3,4 Conducting porous materials offer a lightweight alternative to traditional shielding methods. Their porous structure enhances EMI SE by allowing absorption and reflection of electromagnetic waves (EMWs). This results in improved overall shielding performance, making them suitable for modern applications where both weight and efficiency are critical factors.5,6 Porous textiles, particularly knitted fabrics, are ideal for developing cost-effective EMI shielding materials. Techniques such as blade coating,7–9 dip-coating,10–12 electroless plating,13,14 vacuum filtration,15 and vacuum-spraying,16,17 have been used to enhance textile properties by incorporating nanoparticles. Increased conductivity improves EMI shielding efficiency,18,19 but excessive reflection from impedance mismatch may also lead to secondary electromagnetic pollution. Therefore, the design of high-efficiency EMI shielding textiles should consider both reflection and absorption, with the preferred balance depending on the conductivity, architecture, and end-use requirements of the material.20,21

In improving the conductivity of shielding materials, various conductive agents are used, such as AgNPs,22 nanowires (AgNWs),17,23–25 carbon nanotubes,26–28 graphene,29,30 and MXene.17,31 One-dimensional (1D) metal nanorods (NRs) and nanowires (NWs) are particularly effective due to their high conductivity and aspect ratio. Among these, AgNRs and AgNWs are highly promising. De et al. documented the synthesis of hydrogels incorporating AgNRs, achieving EMI SE values of up to 53.63 dB by varying the thickness of the constructs.32 Zhao et al. developed a carbon aerogel (CA)/tin dioxide (SnO2) nanorods (NRs) composite to measure its EMI SE. The CA/SnO2 NRs composite exhibited an EMI SE of 36.6 dB at the X-band frequency, with a material density of 0.33 g cm−3.33 Hong et al. coated a network of AgNWs, synthesized via the polyol method, onto a highly stretchable PET fabric. This fabric achieved a conductivity of 175 S m−1 and an EMI SE of 37 dB.34 To ensure the practical use of functional textiles in challenging environments, it is crucial that the structural integrity and performance of functional cotton fabrics remain stable. One effective method for enhancing durability and extending the lifespan of multifunctional textiles is through the application of hydrophobic surfaces.35,36 This reduces dirt accumulation, decreases washing frequency, and prevents liquid penetration into textile matrices, which can degrade active materials. Various techniques have been documented for imparting hydrophobicity to cotton textiles, thereby enhancing their sustainability and performance. Hydrophobic modification of cotton fabrics typically involves a two-step process known as the lotus leaf effect. First, nanostructures are created on the fabric surface. Second, a low-surface-energy agent, such as an amino silane compound,37 is deposited onto the roughened surface. This dual-step process is essential for achieving hydrophobicity. Consequently, significant research efforts have focused on synthesizing hydrophobic composites by sequentially applying nanoparticle-induced surface roughening followed by amino silane functionalization procedures.38,39

The objective of this research was to develop a multifunctional hybrid-knitted fabric that combines EMI shielding with improved resistance to washing. The base material was a flexible hybrid knitted fabric featuring a Full Milano knit pattern that incorporates a copper core with a diameter of 0.08 mm.40,41 The key contribution of this study lies in its innovative use of a reactive blade coating technique for the in situ synthesis of AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs directly on the fabric surface, rather than preparing the nanostructures separately42 and depositing them afterwards. This technique not only simplifies the production process but also offers a potentially scalable roll-to-roll manufacturing path, making it a cost-effective solution for producing conductive fabrics with applications in EMI shielding. A second contribution here is the use of APTES treatment to improve laundering stability through hydrophobic surface modification, while still preserving moisture and air transport properties. Together, these results establish a practical route for engineering durable multi-functional EMI-shielding textiles.

Experimental

Materials

Silver nitrate (AgNO3), silver perchlorate (AgClO4), ethylene glycol (EG), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP, MW ∼40 000 g mol−1), sodium borohydride (NaBH4), sodium chloride (NaCl), and (3-aminopropyl)triethoxysilane (APTES) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Hydrochloric acid (HCl, 32%) and ethanol (EtOH) were sourced from Fisher, and single-edge stainless steel blades (industrial grade) from Personna. The substrate used in this study was a hybrid knitted cotton fabric fabricated with core-sheath copper/cotton yarn. A 0.08 mm diameter copper core, and cotton fibers as sheath, featuring a Full Milano knit pattern with a gauge of 12, as described in our previous work.40

Synthesizing AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs on hybrid knitted fabric

In this work, a reactive blade coating technique was used for the in situ self-assembly of AgNRs, AgNCs, AgNPs on the surface of hybrid knitted cotton fabric. Fig. 1a depicts the optical microscope image of hybrid knitted fabric. Fig. 1b shows the schematic of the coating process. Initially, reducer ink was applied to the pre-cleaned fabric sample using the blade coating technique, followed by the application of silver ink on top of reducer ink. The blade was positioned at a 50° angle relative to the fabric during application, the coating speed was set to 1 mm s−1, and the coated fabric was subsequently heated in an oven at 75 °C to facilitate the reaction.

(a) Optical microscope image of hybrid knitted fabric with Full Milano knit pattern. (b) Schematic of roll-to-roll compatible in situ reactive self-assembly synthesis of silver nanostructures and EMI SE test set-up. (c) Schematic illustration of electromagnetic wave interaction with an EMI shielding material.

For the synthesis of AgNRs, a solution containing 0.34 g of PVP (MW 40 000 g mol−1) and 50 mg of NaCl was prepared in 20 ml of ethylene glycol (EG) under stirring at 600 rpm at room temperature to serve as the first ink. The second ink was made by dissolving 0.11 g of AgNO3 in 5 ml of EG with stirring at the same conditions. For AgNCs, the first ink was a mixture of 25 ml of EG and 5 ml of hydrochloric acid (HCl) stirred at 600 rpm at room temperature. The second ink for AgNCs consisted of 0.24 g of AgNO3 and 0.88 g of PVP (MW 40 000 g mol−1) dissolved in 30 ml of EG, also stirred at 600 rpm. For AgNPs, the first ink was prepared with 75 mg of NaBH4 in 20 ml of ethanol (EtOH), applied first to the pre-cleaned fabric. This was followed by applying the second ink on top of the first ink, containing 100 mg of silver perchlorate (AgClO4) in 15 ml of isopropyl alcohol (IPA). The treated fabric was then placed in an oven to promote the formation of AgNPs. The formation of Ag nanostructures on the hybrid knitted fabric is governed by the reduction of Ag+ ions to metallic Ag0, followed by nucleation and growth on the textile surface. For AgNP synthesis, AgClO4 serves as the Ag+ precursor, while NaBH4 acts as a strong reducing agent. The rapid reduction of Ag+ by NaBH4 promotes fast Ag0 nucleation, resulting in the formation of small Ag nanoparticles directly on the fabric surface. This reaction can be simplified as: 8Ag+ + BH4− + 3H2O → 8Ag0 + H3BO3 + 7H+. In contrast, the AgNR and AgNC syntheses follow a polyol-based reduction pathway, where AgNO3 provides Ag+ ions and ethylene glycol acts as both the solvent and a mild reducing agent under thermal treatment. Since the oxidation of ethylene glycol may proceed through different intermediate species, this process can be represented schematically as: Ag+ + EG → Ag0 + oxidized EG species. The final morphology of the Ag structures is controlled by the balance between Ag+ reduction kinetics, nucleation density, chloride-mediated growth regulation, and PVP-assisted surface stabilization. PVP acts as a capping and stabilizing agent by coordinating to Ag surfaces through its carbonyl groups, while Cl− ions supplied by NaCl or HCl regulate the concentration of free Ag+ through AgCl formation or silver chloride complexation. In the AgNR system, the reaction environment favors anisotropic growth, leading to rod-like Ag structures. For AgNC synthesis, the higher PVP content and chloride-containing acidic environment promote facet-selective stabilization and controlled cubic growth. Therefore, the different Ag morphologies observed on the fabric surface originate from differences in precursor chemistry, reduction rate, chloride-assisted growth control, and PVP-mediated facet stabilization. The procedures were repeated to apply single, double, and triple layers of AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs onto the hybrid knitted cotton fabric. A single layer involved applying a reducer-coated film followed by a precursor layer on top of it, with the nanoparticle layer forming after heat treatment.

Instrumentation and characterization

The chemical structure of the pristine and coated fabrics was analyzed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet 6700 FTIR spectrometer equipped with an ATR accessory in attenuated total reflectance mode over the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm−1. The crystalline structure and phase composition of the coated samples were examined using a Bruker D8 Endeavor X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 10–80°. The surface morphology and coating distribution were investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Gemini SEM 500), and the elemental composition was analyzed using the energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) system attached to the SEM. Electrical properties, including sheet resistance and apparent electrical conductivity, were measured using an Ossila four-point probe setup under ambient conditions. The EMI SE was evaluated in the X-band frequency range of 8.2–12.4 GHz using a Rohde & Schwarz vector network analyzer connected to a rectangular waveguide system. The scattering parameters were recorded and used to calculate the total shielding effectiveness and its reflection and absorption contributions.

Washing cycle

The fabrics coated with AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs were subjected to an accelerated agitation washing test to evaluate coating retention and EMI SE stability under intensified washing-related stress. The samples were immersed in 200 ml of a Triton X-100 aqueous solution (surfactant : deionized water = 1 : 100, v/v) in a cylindrical glass container and agitated at 400 rpm using magnetic stirring under free-floating conditions. Two washing durations, 9 min and 90 min, were applied as separate accelerated conditions. After washing, the fabrics were thoroughly rinsed with deionized water and air-dried under ambient conditions before EMI SE testing.

This method was used as a controlled comparative durability assessment and is not intended to replace standardized textile laundering protocols or provide direct cycle-to-cycle equivalence with ISO or AATCC methods. Commonly used laundering protocols, such as ISO 6330 and AATCC TM61, were developed for general textile washing or colorfastness assessment rather than for conductive or EMI-shielding textiles. Therefore, for functional conductive fabrics, washing exposure should be evaluated together with electrical or electromagnetic performance measurements before and after washing. In the present study, all samples were exposed to the same water/surfactant environment, hydrodynamic shear, fiber-to-fiber contact, and mechanical agitation, allowing their relative washing stability to be compared under reproducible laboratory conditions.

Coating APTES

A blend of ethanol and deionized water in a 100 : 10 weight ratio was prepared, to which 10 wt% APTES was added. The pH was adjusted to 4.5 using acetic acid, followed by 15 minutes of stirring to initiate silane hydrolysis.43 The solution was aged 24 hours at room temperature to complete the hydrolysis reaction.43,44 The fabric samples were immersed in this solution for one hour and then dried at 120 °C for two hours.45 This treatment aimed to impart hydrophobic properties to the fabric, enhancing its resistance to laundering and overall durability.

Water vapor transmission rate (WVTR)

The water vapor transmission rate was assessed using the JIS L 1099 B2 method, which measures fabric permeability. The setup included a glass cup containing a potassium acetate solution (300 g/100 ml in DI water) covered with a PTFE film to create a vapor-permeable environment.46 The fabric sample, cut to 10 cm × 10 cm, was secured between two PTFE films. This assembly was inverted and floated in a water tank maintained at 23 °C. The mass of the test assembly was recorded before and after 15 minutes submersion to determine the rate of water vapor transmission.41,46 This method was conducted under controlled conditions at 22 ± 2 °C with airflow. Air permeability measurement

The air permeability of fabric samples was evaluated under controlled conditions (22 ± 2 °C, 65% RH) using the ASTM D737 standard. This method involved a 20 cm2 test area with a consistent 100 Pa pressure differential applied across the fabric. The resulting air permeability was measured in cm3 s−1 cm−2. The fabric samples were securely clamped onto a hollow disc with at least a 1 cm extension on all sides, ensuring no air escaped from the cut edges.41 This procedure followed the guidelines set by Adamu et al. and Mhreen Ijaz et al.47,48

All experimental measurements were repeated five times for each sample condition. The results are reported as mean ± standard deviation, with error bars in the relevant figures indicating the spread of the measurements and the reproducibility of the observed trends. Because this study focuses on comparative changes in morphology, electrical properties, EMI shielding performance, washing stability, WVTR, and air permeability as a function of coating architecture, the results were interpreted using descriptive statistics. No additional mean-separation test was applied, and no statistical-significance claim is made.

Cyclic bending test

The mechanical stability of the conductive Ag-coated hybrid knitted fabric was evaluated using a finite-radius cyclic bending test, as illustrated in Fig. 2a. Fabric specimens with three layers of Ag coating were bent around a fixed cylindrical rod with a diameter of 6 mm, corresponding to an approximate bending radius of 3 mm, and then released to their original flat configuration. The specimen followed an approximately semicircular path around the rod, therefore, the deformation was defined by the finite rod radius rather than by a sharp zero-radius fold. The bending-release process was repeated for 1000 cycles. After bending, the samples were visually examined, and changes in sheet resistance, apparent electrical conductivity, and X-band EMI SE were measured to assess retention of electrical and shielding performance. Samples with one and two Ag coating layers were not subjected to the cyclic bending evaluation.

Schematic illustrations of the durability evaluation procedures: (a) finite-radius cyclic bending test, in which the fabric sample was repeatedly bent around a 6 mm diameter cylindrical rod for 1000 cycles, and (b) humid–thermal exposure test, in which the fabric sample was exposed to a sealed humid atmosphere at 60 °C for 72 h without direct contact with liquid water.

Environmental aging test

The environmental stability of the Ag-coated hybrid knitted fabrics was assessed under an accelerated humid–thermal exposure condition. Samples with three layers of Ag coating were exclusively subjected to this evaluation. The specimens were placed within a clean glass beaker, which was subsequently situated inside a larger sealed glass container filled with de-ionized water. Care was taken to prevent direct contact between the samples and the liquid water. The sealed container was maintained at 60 °C for 72 hours to create a humid–thermal environment. Since the relative humidity was not directly regulated or measured, this procedure is described as a humid–thermal exposure test rather than a controlled aging protocol at 75% RH. Following exposure, the samples were removed, allowed to dry and stabilize under ambient laboratory conditions, and then characterized. Measurements of sheet resistance, apparent electrical conductivity, and X-band EMI SE were conducted and compared to those of unaged samples in order to evaluate the retention of electrical and shielding performance subsequent to humid–thermal exposure.

Results and discussion

Characterizations, morphology, and conductivity of coated hybrid knitted fabric

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis identified characteristic peaks of the cellulosic structure in cotton, including bands at 3322–3334 cm−1 (O–H stretching), 2890–2947 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1141–1167 cm−1 (C–O stretching), and 1017–1044 cm−1 (C–O–C stretching).49–51 These peaks correspond to functional groups like alcohols, esters, ethers, and alkanes. The peak at 1022 cm−1 is attributed to ether functional groups, while 1159 cm−1 indicates C–O stretching bonds. The C–H stretching vibrations at 2890 cm−1 and O–H stretching at 3328 cm−1 are characteristic of alkanes and alcohols, respectively. The FTIR analysis showed no new peaks or significant shifts in existing peaks, suggesting that the silver nanostructures are physically, rather than chemically, bonded to the cotton fibers, preserving the original cellulosic structure. This indicates that the surface of the cotton yarn, containing various organic functional groups, did not undergo chemical changes during silver nanostructure coating. In addition, the intensified peak in the 1000–1100 cm−1 region can be attributed to overlapping Si–O–Si and Si–O–C vibrations, indicating hydrolysis/condensation of APTES and possible covalent anchoring to cellulose (Fig. 3a).

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis further confirmed the formation of silver nanostructures. The XRD patterns for the hybrid knitted fabric, shown in Fig. 3b, display distinct peaks at (111), (200), (220), and (311), indicative of the face-centered cubic (FCC) crystal structure of silver.52 These peaks are characteristic of the crystalline nature of silver, providing solid evidence of the successful deposition of silver nanostructures on the cotton substrate. In the uncoated hybrid knitted fabric, the first four peaks correspond to the cellulosic structure of cotton, while the last three peaks are attributed to the FCC structure of the copper filaments embedded within the fabric.

The energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) spectra in Fig. 3c confirm the successful formation of these silver nanostructures on the hybrid knitted fabric. Furthermore, scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the uncoated and coated fabric, are shown in Fig. 3d–g, reveal the growth of AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs. The in situ growth of silver nanostructures on the fabric substrate precluded conventional purification or morphology selective separation processes. Consequently, while the synthesis conditions favored the formation of AgNRs and AgNCs, the coexistence of a limited population of spherical AgNPs was unavoidable. Such mixed morphologies are inherent to in situ growth on porous textile substrates, where local variations in nucleation and growth kinetics can lead to the simultaneous formation of multiple silver nanostructure geometries.

The surface electrical properties of the coated fabrics were evaluated using a four-point probe method. For each sample, measurements were collected from multiple locations and averaged to minimize the influence of local surface variation. The sheet resistance was recorded in Ω sq−1. The corresponding apparent electrical conductivity was calculated using σ = 1/t(Rst), where Rs is the sheet resistance, t is the thickness, and σ is the conductivity expressed in S m−1. The term apparent conductivity is used because the samples are porous coated textiles rather than dense, uniform conductive films. The obtained values were used to compare the effect of Ag nanostructure morphology and coating-layer number on the electrical performance of the hybrid knitted fabrics. Experimental data revealed that substrates coated with AgNRs exhibited higher apparent electrical conductivity compared to those treated with AgNCs, which in turn showed better conductivity than those modified with AgNPs (Fig. 4). The AgNRs demonstrated superior apparent electrical conductivity, aligning with previous research findings.53 SEM analysis (Fig. 3g) indicated a random configuration of AgNRs with increased physical contact, contributing to enhanced conductivity as the number of coated layers increased. Fig. 4 illustrates the variations in conductivity and sheet resistance among samples with single, double, and triple layers of AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs, respectively. In all cases, increasing the number of coating layers reduced the sheet resistance, indicating the formation of progressively more connected conductive pathways on the textile surface.

Increasing the number of coating layers is expected to increase the effective coating thickness and promote the formation of a denser and more continuous conductive network on the textile surface. With successive deposition cycles, conductive fillers progressively cover the fiber surfaces, fill gaps between neighboring fibers and yarns, and improve interparticle/interlayer contact. This thicker and better-connected coating structure facilitates electron transport, thereby increasing the apparent electrical conductivity of the coated fabric while reducing its sheet resistance. The improved conductivity enhances impedance mismatch at the air-material interface, leading to stronger reflection of incident electromagnetic waves. In addition, the increased coating thickness and interconnected conductive pathways provide longer propagation routes for electromagnetic waves within the material, promoting ohmic loss, interfacial polarization, multiple scattering, and energy dissipation. Therefore, multilayer coating architectures can improve EMI SE through the combined effects of increased coating thickness, enhanced conductivity, reduced sheet resistance, and improved electromagnetic wave attenuation.

Among the three morphologies, the AgNR-coated fabrics showed the lowest sheet resistance and the highest apparent electrical performance under the present processing conditions. This trend is consistent with the SEM observations, which suggest that the elongated AgNRs establish more effective interparticle contacts and a more continuous conductive network than the AgNC and AgNP coatings. However, because the three Ag morphologies were produced using different precursor/reducing systems, the actual Ag loading may differ among the samples. Therefore, the electrical differences should be interpreted as the combined result of nanostructure geometry, coating coverage, conductive-network continuity, interparticle contact, and possible differences in deposited Ag amount.

EMI SE mechanism

When an EMW impinges on a shielding material, several attenuation mechanisms act together. A portion of the incident energy is first lost by reflection at the interface owing to the impedance contrast between air and the shield. The field that penetrates the material undergoes absorption, where electromagnetic energy is dissipated as heat through conductive and magnetic losses. Only a small portion continues through the barrier as transmittance. In porous or multilayered structures, the EMW also experience multiple reflections, which extend its propagation path and can enhance the overall absorption. However, for thin, highly conductive silver nanostructure coatings are highly conductive and form very thin layers, the contribution of multiple reflections at X-band frequencies is negligible, particularly when the absorption exceeds 10 dB or the total shielding effectiveness is greater than 15 dB.40,54Fig. 1c shows the schematic of electromagnetic shielding mechanism. In silver-nanostructure-coated fabrics, reflection appears to be a major contributor to EMI SE, as shown in Fig. 5, although absorption also contributes to the overall attenuation. Silver's high electrical conductivity provides a dense population of free electrons that interact strongly with incident electromagnetic waves. When these waves encounter the conductive surface, the oscillating electric field induces surface currents, promoting strong reflection and reduced transmission. Thus, for the present fabrics in the X-band, the shielding response appears to be largely reflection-dominant, even though absorption-dominant shielding may be preferable in some applications to reduce secondary electromagnetic pollution.

Effect of randomly oriented AgNRs on EMI SE

AgNRs showed the highest EMI SE among the tested nanosilver morphologies. AgNRs exhibit high apparent electrical conductivity, which facilitates efficient electron transport and a dense electron population on the nanorod surface. This increased surface area and electron density contribute to the enhanced EMI SE observed in AgNRs. The distinct morphological characteristics of AgNRs, including their high aspect ratio and random orientation, are likely factors that enhance their EMI SE performance.57,58 AgNRs can form a three-dimensional conductive framework, maintaining high conductivity and effective EMI shielding even in the presence of gaps between nanorods.57 Previous studies suggest that the random dispersion of silver within composite materials can support effective EMI SE, which is consistent with the strong performance here for the AgNR-coated fabrics.59,60Fig. 5a–c illustrates the EMI SE of hybrid knitted fabrics coated with randomly oriented AgNRs. The results indicate a clear trend: as the number of AgNR coating layers increases from one to three, the EMI SE of the samples also increases. Several factors contribute to the increased EMI SE of hybrid knitted fabrics coated with additional layers of AgNRs. The primary advantage of AgNRs lies in their inherently high electrical conductivity, which facilitates efficient electron movement across the fabric surface. Additional AgNR layers enhance this conductivity by creating a denser network of continuous conductive pathways, which likely improves charge transport and enhances EMI SE. In addition, the high aspect ratio of AgNRs increases the available surface area, which may promote stronger interaction with incident EMWs and contribute to improved shielding through reflection and attenuation.

Effect of randomly oriented AgNCs on EMI SE

AgNCs exhibit anisotropic structural features associated with their crystallographic facets.61 These features may influence how AgNC-based coatings interact with incident EMWs. However, the dominant experimentally supported driver in the present study is the formation of a more continuous conductive network with increasing coating layers. The intricate conductive network formed by randomly oriented AgNCs provides efficient pathways for dissipating electromagnetic energy, primarily through improved electrical connectivity that lowers resistance and enhances the SER and SEA contributions, significantly enhancing the overall EMI shielding capability.59Fig. 5a–c presents the EMI SE results for both plain hybrid knitted fabric and fabric coated with AgNCs. The data indicate that increasing the number of AgNC layers leads to a corresponding rise in the EMI SE of the samples. This improvement is primarily due to the increased presence of AgNCs on the fabric surface, which enhances apparent electrical conductivity and, consequently, the overall EMI SE.

By adding more layers of AgNCs, the surface area and complexity of the conductive network within the fabric increase significantly. This network is crucial for enabling efficient electron flow, which is essential for achieving high EMI SE. The increased coating thickness from the additional layers improves surface coverage and network continuity, thereby strengthening the interactions with EMWs and improving absorption and reflection. Moreover, the random arrangement of AgNCs in multilayer coatings may contribute to the enhanced EMI SE of the fabrics. Rather than relying on a single idealized orientation, each additional layer of AgNCs, applied without uniform stacking, introduces various orientations that increase the density and diversity of electrical conduction paths, which is consistent with the measured increase in shielding performance with layer number. Furthermore, the heterogeneous multilayer coating promotes broader surface coverage and more continuous conductive pathways, which may improve the dissipation, absorption, and reflection of EMWs. The random arrangement of AgNCs may enhance interactions between neighbouring particles, thereby contributing to the observed increase in shielding efficacy.

Effect of randomly oriented AgNPs on EMI SE

With each successive application of AgNP coatings on the hybrid fabric, there is a notable increase in apparent electrical conductivity, as depicted in Fig. 4. This increase is critical for enhancing the fabric's shielding capabilities against electromagnetic radiation within the X-band frequency range. The underlying mechanism for this observation is the progressive densification of the nanoparticle arrangement with additional coatings. This densification creates a more interconnected conductive network, facilitating improved electron flow, which is crucial for achieving higher conductivity. The closer proximity of nanoparticles in successive layers reduces interparticle junction resistance and increases network connectivity, which is consistent with the measured conductivity increase and the corresponding increase in EMI SE. Fig. 5a–c depicts the EMI SE results for both plain hybrid knitted fabric and fabric coated with AgNPs. Additionally, the application of more AgNP layers increases the coverage over the fabric fibers and expands the total surface area. This increase in surface area allows for more AgNPs to interact with EMWs, which contributes to the increase in EMI SE. The improved coverage also likely produces a more uniform and effective barrier against electromagnetic interference, thereby contributing to the enhanced shielding performance.

Comparison between EMI SE of AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs

The data presented above indicate that the EMI SE of the hybrid knitted fabrics in the X-band region depends strongly on the conductive coating architecture and the type of Ag nanostructure formed on the fabric surface. It is important to consider that nanoscale Ag structures are much smaller than the wavelength of X-band radiation, which ranges from approximately 25 to 37.5 mm. Therefore, their influence on shielding is not due to geometric blocking of the incident wave, but rather to their effect on surface conductivity, local field interactions, conductive-network formation, reflection, and electromagnetic attenuation.

As illustrated in Fig. 5a–c, the AgNR-coated fabrics consistently exhibited higher EMI SE than the AgNC- and AgNP-coated fabrics for the same number of coating cycles. This trend is consistent with the lower sheet resistance measured for the AgNR-coated fabrics and suggests the formation of a more effective conductive network. The improved performance of AgNR-coated fabrics is likely related to the elongated morphology of AgNRs, which can promote interparticle contact, charge-carrier transport, and conductive pathway formation more effectively than lower-aspect-ratio particles.57,58,62–65 The extended geometry of AgNRs may also increase the probability of forming percolated pathways across the fiber and yarn surfaces, thereby reducing resistance and improving electromagnetic attenuation. In addition, the anisotropic geometry and random arrangement of AgNRs within the coating may provide additional conductive, scattering, and attenuation pathways, contributing to the observed shielding performance.52,53

However, this comparison should be interpreted with appropriate consideration of the different chemistries used to generate AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs. Although all samples were prepared using the same substrate, blade angle, coating speed, drying temperature, sample dimensions, and number of coating cycles, the precursor/reducing systems were necessarily different in order to obtain distinct Ag morphologies. As a result, the actual Ag mass loading may differ among the three coated fabrics. SEM/EDS and XRD confirm the formation, crystallinity, and surface distribution of Ag-rich metallic nanostructures, but they do not provide direct whole-sample quantification of Ag mass loading. Therefore, the superior EMI SE of the AgNR-coated fabrics should not be attributed solely to morphology. Rather, the observed differences are best understood as the combined result of nanostructure geometry, coating coverage, conductive-network continuity, interparticle contact, sheet resistance, and possible differences in deposited Ag amount. Within this limitation, the lower sheet resistance of the AgNR-coated fabrics supports the interpretation that their elongated geometry promotes more effective percolated conductive pathways than the lower-aspect-ratio AgNC and AgNP coatings under the present processing conditions.

Washing effect on EMI SE of nanosilver-coated samples

To evaluate washing-related stability, hybrid knitted fabrics coated with three layers of AgNRs, AgNCs, or AgNPs were subjected to the accelerated agitation washing test described in Section 2.4. The washing sequence was performed over 9-minute and 90-minute cycles using magnetic agitation set at 400 rpm, with Triton X-100 as a surfactant. Fig. 5d shows the EMI SE of the specimens before and after accelerated washing. The decline in EMI SE was modest after the 9-minute wash but became more substantial after the 90-minute wash. These accelerated washing conditions were selected to impose stronger water/surfactant exposure and agitation than simple immersion or gentle handling, while maintaining identical conditions for all samples. After a 9-min wash cycle, the hybrid knitted fabric coated with 3 layers of AgNRs exhibited a decrease of 1.50 dB in EMI SE. In comparison, the EMI SE reduction for the hybrid knitted fabric coated with 3 layers of AgNCs was 1.80 dB, while the fabric coated with three layers of AgNPs showed a reduction of 1.40 dB. After the 90-min washing condition, the EMI SE values decreased substantially relative to those of unwashed fabrics. Washing cycles typically lower the EMI SE because some of the silver nanostructures are dislodged or gradually worn away from the fabric surface. This loss of conductive material breaks up the continuous metallic network, raising the fabric's electrical resistance and, in turn, reducing its ability to block electromagnetic radiation. To mitigate this attenuation and enhance laundering stability, the fabric was functionalized with a hydrophobic coating, as discussed in the following section.

Hydrophobicity effect on EMI SE and washing durability

To impart hydrophobic characteristics, the coated hybrid fabric underwent a treatment regimen utilizing APTES. The samples consisted of hybrid knitted fabrics with distinct nanosilver coatings: three layers of AgNRs on the first set, three layers of AgNCs on the second set, and three layers of AgNPs on the third set. After treatment, the hydrophobic fabrics were subjected to the same accelerated agitation washing conditions for 9 min and 90 min, as described in Section 2.4. Notably, the measured EMI SE values post-washing showed only minor variation relative to the unwashed samples, as indicated in Fig. 5e.

This outcome suggests that APTES treatment improves washing stability under the accelerated agitation conditions used here by forming a hydrophobic surface layer that likely reduces direct exposure of the silver coating to water and surfactant during washing. This protective effect may help preserve the conductive network and maintain EMI shielding performance after washing cycles. However, the durability requirements of EMI-shielding textiles depend on the intended service environment. For wearable or repeatedly deformable systems, cyclic bending, stretching, abrasion, and repeated wear are important failure modes. In contrast, for low-strain shielding layers, liners, wraps, laminate inserts, curtains, or enclosure applications, large repeated stretching may not be the dominant mechanical stress. Therefore, the present results should be interpreted as evidence of improved laundering stability under the tested conditions, rather than complete validation of mechanical durability for all use cases. Future work should include application-specific mechanical and environmental durability tests, with sheet resistance and EMI SE measured before and after deformation or aging. To assess the hydrophobic properties of the treated samples, the contact angle between the fabric surface and a laundering solution was measured. As expected, the contact angle of the laundering solution on the APTES-coated fabrics exceeded 120°, indicating excellent hydrophobicity of the treated fabrics. The contact angle between the laundering solution and the various samples is shown in Fig. 7b–d.

Humid–thermal aging effect on electrical stability and EMI SE

The impact of humid–thermal exposure on the electrical stability of Ag-coated hybrid knitted fabrics was assessed following a 72-hour exposure at 60 °C within a sealed humid environment, without direct contact with liquid water. As shown in Fig. 6a, after humid–thermal aging, the untreated (with APTES) AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated samples exhibited reductions in apparent electrical conductivity of 20.51%, 19.97%, and 19.49%, respectively, compared with their corresponding unaged reference samples. Similarly, compared with their corresponding unaged reference samples, the untreated (with APTES) AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics showed increases in sheet resistance of 25.01%, 24.74%, and 25.12%, respectively, after humid–thermal aging. This degradation aligns with increased junction resistance within the conductive network. Moisture and elevated temperature may facilitate interfacial alterations, including potential partial oxidation at Ag–Ag, Ag–Cu, and Cu–Cu junctions; however, since post-aging chemical analysis was not conducted, oxidation is discussed herein as a plausible mechanism rather than definitive evidence. This electrical degradation was further reflected in the EMI shielding performance, where all silver nanostructure-coated fabrics untreated (with APTES) treatment showed an approximately 4 dB reduction in X-band EMI SE after humid–thermal exposure relative to their corresponding reference samples, as shown in Fig. 6b.

(b) EMI SE, including total shielding effectiveness (SETotal), reflection loss (SER), and absorption loss (SEA), after humid–thermal exposure. (c) EMI SE after cyclic bending. Conversely, the APTES-treated samples exhibited greater stability under the same humid–thermal exposure conditions. As shown in Fig. 6a, the apparent electrical conductivity decreased by only 6.92%, 6.95%, and 6.99% for the AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics, respectively, compared with their corresponding reference samples. Consistently, the sheet resistance increased by 8.98%, 8.87%, and 9.01% for the AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics, respectively. This improved electrical stability was also reflected in the EMI shielding performance, where all APTES-treated silver nanostructure-coated fabrics showed only an approximately 1 dB reduction in X-band EMI SE relative to their corresponding reference samples, as shown in Fig. 6b. This enhanced retention is consistent with the APTES-treated surface diminishing moisture access to the conductive Ag/Cu network. These findings support the beneficial role of APTES under the short-term humid–thermal conditions evaluated herein; however, longer-term aging studies under controlled relative humidity, followed by chemical analysis, are necessary to comprehensively assess environmental durability.

Cyclic bending effect on electrical stability and EMI SE

The influence of repeated finite-radius bending on the electrical stability of the Ag-coated hybrid knitted fabrics was evaluated after 1000 bending-release cycles. As shown in Fig. 6a, the samples without APTES treatment exhibited decreases in apparent electrical conductivity of 14.87%, 14.88%, and 14.83% for the AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics, respectively, compared with their corresponding reference samples. Consistently, the sheet resistance increased by 18.75%, 18.95%, and 18.48% for the AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics, respectively. This change suggests partial disturbance of the conductive pathways within the Ag-coated Cu/cotton knitted structure. During bending, localized tensile and compressive stresses may generate small microcracks in the Ag nanostructured coating, weaken some Ag–Ag and Ag–Cu contact points, or reduce the number of effective conductive junctions across the fabric surface. These changes increase contact resistance between neighbouring conductive domains, leading to lower apparent conductivity and higher sheet resistance. The decrease was limited under the tested condition, which may be related to the copper-containing yarn and the interconnected Full Milano knitted architecture, both of which can help maintain electron-transport pathways after repeated finite-radius bending. This retention of electrical performance was further reflected in the EMI shielding results, where all silver nanostructure-coated fabrics without APTES treatment exhibited an approximately 1.5 dB reduction in X-band EMI SE after bending relative to their corresponding unbent reference samples, as shown in Fig. 6c.

For the APTES-treated silver nanostructure-coated fabrics, the apparent electrical conductivity decreased by 12.18%, 12.65%, and 12.03% for the AgNP-, AgNC-, and AgNR- coated fabrics, respectively, after 1000 bending-release cycles compared with their corresponding unbent reference samples, as shown in Fig. 6a. The corresponding sheet resistance increases were 14.84%, 14.53%, and 13.74%, respectively. Moreover, the X-band EMI SE of all APTES-treated silver nanostructure-coated fabrics decreased by only approximately 1 dB after bending, as shown in Fig. 6c. The slightly improved retention of the APTES-treated fabric is consistent with partial stabilization of the coated fiber surface and reduced disruption of the conductive pathway during bending. These results indicate that the conductive network retained much of its electrical and shielding functionality under the specific finite-radius cyclic bending condition used here. They should not be interpreted as complete validation of mechanical durability under sharp folding, stretching, abrasion, tape stripping, or other untested deformation modes.

Water vapor transmission rate (WVTR) and air permeability of samples

The Water Vapor Transmission Rate (WVTR) evaluation was conducted on hybrid knitted fabrics treated with three layers of silver nanostructures AgNRs, AgNCs, or AgNPs) and subjected to APTES treatment. The performance of these samples was compared to their respective untreated versions to assess the impact of the APTES treatment. Additionally, the WVTR of both an uncoated (plain) hybrid knitted fabric and plain samples treated solely with APTES was measured to evaluate how the introduction of hydrophobic characteristics affects breathability. The results showed that the plain fabric exhibited a high breathability rating of 23 320 g.m−2·24h−1, classified under JIS L 1099 B2 as highly breathable. The breathability of the hybrid knitted fabric, without a silver nanostructure coating but treated exclusively with APTES, measured at 14 820 g.m−2·24h−1, indicating moderate breathability. For the hybrid knitted fabric with 3 layers AgNPs coating, breathability was recorded at 22 337 g.m−2·24h−1, which decreased to 13 963 g.m−2·24h−1 after APTES treatment. The hybrid knitted fabric with three layers AgNCs coating exhibited breathability values of 22 327 g.m−2·24h−1 and 13 958 g.m−2·24h−1 for untreated and APTES-treated samples, respectively. Lastly, the hybrid knitted fabric with three layers AgNRs coating registered breathability values of 22 299 g.m−2·24h−1 and 13 932 g.m−2·24h−1 for untreated and APTES-treated variants, respectively. The breathability (WVTR) of the samples is graphically depicted in Fig. 7a. According to JIS L 1099 B2 guidelines, all APTES-treated samples demonstrated moderate breathability, while those without APTES treatment maintained high breathability. The introduction of hydrophobicity reduced breathability from a high to a moderate level. This reduction was achieved while maintaining strong EMI shielding performance. These results highlight a practical tradeoff in which improved washing durability and hydrophobicity are achieved while maintaining moderate breathability.

The results from Fig. 7a indicate that the plain fabric, without APTES treatment, exhibited the highest air permeability, while the fabric treated with APTES and coated with three layers of AgNPs demonstrated the lowest air permeability. APTES-coated samples consistently showed lower air permeability compared to their uncoated counterparts. However, the reduction in air permeability across the samples was not substantial. These findings reveal that surface treatments and nanomaterial coatings markedly influence breathability, while their effect on air permeability is comparatively minor. The exceptional breathability and air permeability of the plain fabric are attributed to the absence of coatings, which would otherwise restrict air and moisture flow. The application of APTES results in a noticeable reduction in breathability, with a relatively minor decrease in air permeability. This reduction can be attributed to the formation of a continuous coating through APTES treatment, which partially obstructs the pores between the yarns, thereby impeding the movement of moisture vapor and air. The chemical properties of APTES are particularly relevant when applied to silver nanomaterial coatings on cotton fabrics. While nanoparticles partially reduce the natural porosity of the cotton fabric, they do not completely block moisture penetration due to their size and the uneven coating of fibers. However, the APTES layer effectively seals these residual channels and repels water due to its hydrophobic nature. Consequently, this causes a noticeable decrease in the WVTR for APTES-treated samples, as indicated by the data. It is evident that the addition of the APTES layer significantly enhances the hydrophobic barrier of the nanosilver coatings, with minimal impact on air permeability.

Conclusions

This study reports a flexible hybrid knitted fabric composed of cotton fibers and 0.08 mm copper filaments for X-band EMI shielding. The uncoated hybrid fabric provided an EMI SE of 22 dB, mainly due to the embedded copper network. A reactive blade-coating process was then used to grow AgNRs, AgNCs, and AgNPs directly on the textile surface through sequential application of reducing and Ag-precursor inks followed by heat treatment. The process is compatible with scalable continuous coating approaches, including roll-to-roll manufacturing.

Under the present coating conditions, the AgNR-coated fabrics showed the highest EMI SE, reaching 52.9 dB in the X-band. Increasing the number of Ag coating cycles also improved EMI SE, consistent with the formation of denser and more conductive surface networks. However, it should be noted that the three Ag nanostructures were synthesized using different precursor/reducing systems to enable the formation of distinct morphologies. Although identical blade-coating parameters, substrate dimensions, coating speed, blade angle, drying temperature, and number of coating cycles were used for all samples, the resulting Ag loading may differ among AgNR-, AgNC-, and AgNP-coated fabrics. Therefore, the differences in EMI shielding effectiveness should not be attributed solely to morphology. Instead, the observed performance variation is considered to arise from the combined effects of nanostructure geometry, coating coverage, conductive-network formation, interparticle contact, sheet resistance, and possible differences in Ag loading. SEM/EDS and XRD confirm the presence, crystallinity, and surface distribution of Ag-rich metallic nanostructures, but these techniques do not provide direct whole-sample quantification of Ag mass loading. Consequently, the differences in EMI SE and electrical conductivity should not be assigned solely to nanostructure morphology. They are more appropriately interpreted as the combined effect of nanostructure geometry, coating coverage, conductive-network continuity, interparticle connectivity, sheet resistance, and possible differences in deposited Ag amount. Future work should quantify Ag content using methods such as ICP-OES, TGA, calibrated XRF, or controlled gravimetric analysis and should correlate Ag areal loading with conductivity, EMI SE, durability, and cost-performance efficiency.

APTES treatment improved laundering stability while largely preserving EMI SE under the washing conditions used in this study. The treatment reduced water vapor transmission relative to the untreated fabrics, but the coated textiles still retained substantial air permeability. These results show that hydrophobic post-treatment can improve washing resistance while maintaining useful transport properties. At the same time, the durability claims should be limited to the conditions evaluated here. Additional testing is needed to fully evaluate the mechanical and environmental durability of the coated textiles under service-relevant conditions. Future work should quantify Ag loading using ICP-OES, TGA, calibrated XRF, or carefully controlled gravimetric analysis, and should relate EMI SE and electrical performance to Ag areal loading, coating thickness, and sheet resistance. Additional durability testing should be selected according to the intended application. For wearable or repeatedly deformable uses, stretching, abrasion, repeated laundering, and wear testing would be important. For low-strain shielding layers, liners, wraps, laminate inserts, curtains, or enclosure applications, handling, flexing, adhesion, abrasion, and oxidation assessments may be more relevant. In all cases, sheet resistance and EMI SE should be monitored before and after testing to evaluate the stability of the Ag/Cu conductive network.
