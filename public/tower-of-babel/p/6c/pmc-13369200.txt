Paraoxonases as metabolic and signaling modulators: prospects for therapeutic and preventive implications
Authors: Sepand Tehrani Fateh, Sahand Tehrani Fateh, Seyed Ali Ziai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParaoxonases (PONs) are a family of three isozymes, PON1, PON2, and PON3, with lactonase and esterase enzymatic activities. These enzymes have been implicated in the pathophysiology of numerous disorders, including cancer, atherosclerosis, liver diseases, neurodegenerative conditions, and toxicities. Specifically, alterations in PONs, such as changes in gene expression, mRNA levels, protein abundance, and enzymatic activity, have been associated with a range of pathological conditions. PONs are primarily linked to these disorders through their antioxidant and detoxifying functions, although additional, yet unidentified, mechanisms may also contribute. A growing body of evidence indicates that modulating PON levels or activity of PONs may confer therapeutic benefit in the prevention, management, and treatment of certain diseases. Promising strategies include enzyme replacement therapy, drug repurposing, and genetic engineering techniques aimed at restoring or enhancing PON function. Moreover, PONs’ level and activity can be influenced by life-style factors and the microbiome, offering additional avenues for intervention. In this review, we propose that the modulation of PONs holds therapeutic and preventive potential, and we discuss the current and emerging strategies by which this may be achieved.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Diseases, Molecular biology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Paraoxonases (PONs) are a family of mammalian enzymes comprising three isozymes, PON1, PON2, and PON3, with diverse enzymatic activities, including paraoxonase, arylesterase, peroxidase, lactonase-lactonization functions1. PON1 was the first member of this family to be identified, initially recognized for its ability to hydrolyze organophosphate compounds2. PONs are considered promiscuous enzymes, and their physiologically relevant substrates remain incompletely characterized; however, lactones appear to be their primary natural substrates3,4.

PON1 and PON3 are predominantly extracellular enzymes that are secreted by hepatic cells and circulate in the bloodstream where they are associated with high-density lipoproteins (HDLs)5,6, whereas PON2 is ubiquitously expressed across various tissues and exerts its antioxidant effects at the cellular level7. All members of the PON family exhibit antioxidant properties, which contribute to their anti-inflammatory effects. Beyond their classical antioxidant roles, PONs are increasingly recognized as modulators of cellular signaling, metabolic homeostasis, and intercellular communication.

These enzymes play a crucial role in various diseases, including atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, cancers, hepatic and renal disorders, and organophosphate poisoning8. PONs level and activity are further shaped by a diverse array of pharmacological, environmental, and genetic influences. Genetic variants can profoundly alter enzyme expression or function and contribute to inter-individual differences in disease susceptibility and progression. At the same time, dynamic extrinsic factors such as drugs, lifestyle and microbiome composition further modulate PON regulation across physiological and pathological contexts.

Despite this extensive body of evidence, more than 6000 publications on PONs are indexed in PubMed, and the growing recognition of the therapeutic potential of modulating PONs in both acute and chronic disorders, the translational landscape of PON research remains underdeveloped, with no PON-targeted therapeutic having successfully reached clinical practice.

This gap may be originated from the multifaceted nature of PON biology, including their diverse roles in metabolic and signaling pathways, limited tissue and disease specificity, and multilayered regulation. A critical synthesis of recent advances, emerging therapeutic approaches, and promising modulators is therefore essential to evaluate PONs not only as an antioxidant and metabolic enzymes but also as actionable signaling nodes with translational promise, ultimately guiding future efforts toward effective therapeutic development.

Paraoxonases in health and disease

Understanding the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of PONs in human physiology and how they position themselves at the intersection of inflammatory and lipid-related disorders has garnered increasing attention. PON1 and PON3 are predominantly synthesized in the liver and circulate bound to HDL, where they contribute to HDL’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions5,6. PON2, by contrast, is an intracellular enzyme localized to cellular membranes, most notably the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum, where it exerts antioxidant effects at the cellular level7.

Thus, all enzymes in the PON family possess antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions, although they operate in different compartments and to varying extents. PON1 uniquely hydrolyzes organophosphates and also hydrolyzes homocysteine thiolactone and lipid peroxides, thereby protecting lipoproteins and cell membranes from oxidative damage (Fig. 1). Oxidative stress induces the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which oxidize LDL9. Oxidized LDL (oxLDL) could trigger Toll-like receptor signaling pathways, particularly TLR4 in macrophages, leading to the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, interleukins, and additional ROS10. PON1, together with other HDL-associated components, converts oxLDL back to LDL11. PON1 also exerts inhibitory effects on CCL2, which is a chemokine involved in promoting the recruitment and differentiation of monocytes into macrophages12–14. Other member of this family, associated with mitochondria, PON2 helps maintain coenzyme Q10 in its reduced state, thereby limiting the formation of ROS that can arise from the unstable intermediate ubisemiquinone15. In the endoplasmic reticulum, PON2 further reduces caspase activation mediated by the unfolded protein response and suppresses apoptosis by downregulating the pro-apoptotic transcription factor CHOP16. PON3 is expressed in a wide range of tissues and exists both as an HDL-associated enzyme circulating in the blood after hepatic synthesis and as a mitochondrial membrane–associated protein3. In mitochondria, PON3 has been shown to interact with coenzyme Q10 and is proposed to limit the accumulation of the unstable intermediate ubisemiquinone, thereby reducing mitochondrial superoxide production and attenuating oxidative stress17. Additionally, PON3 may help protect LDL from oxidative modification, similar to PON118.

The function of PONs related to lipid metabolism and oxidative pathways makes them particularly relevant to disease pathogenesis. Reduced PONs activity is consistently linked to pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory states and has a documented association with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), metabolic disease and diabetes, infectious disease, and cancer, all conditions in which oxidative–inflammatory mechanisms play key pathogenic roles.

In ASCVD, PON1 is essential for the ability of HDL to protect LDL and outer cell membranes from oxidative modification. PON1, by reducing the burden of oxLDL, decreases macrophage uptake of oxLDL, limits foam cell formation, and protects against oxLDL-induced oxidative stress and apoptosis. PON2 exerts anti-atherogenic effects primarily by limiting mitochondrial ROS production19. PON2 is not directly associated with lipoproteins; however, it can inhibit LDL oxidation and enhance HDL’s antioxidant properties and cholesterol efflux capacity20. As discussed above, both PON2 and PON3 localize to the mitochondrial membranes, where they interact with ubisemiquinone and prevent ubisemiquinone-mediated superoxide generation and apoptosis, thereby providing protection against atherosclerosis17.

In metabolic diseases and diabetes, PON1 deficiency promotes insulin resistance through oxidative stress–driven signaling pathways, whereas exogenous PON1 has been shown to protect pancreatic β-cells. Clinically, low serum PON1 activity is frequently observed in individuals with metabolic syndrome and type 2 diabetes and is associated with increased markers of oxidative stress and adverse cardiometabolic profiles21. More recently, PON2 has been implicated in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis. In-vitro modeling of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease indicates that PON2 supports lipid metabolism, limits oxidative stress, and that pharmacological PON2 activation can enhance autophagy, improve mitochondrial function, and alleviate steatohepatitis-like features22.

The interplay between inflammation, oxidative stress, and carcinogenesis has increasingly implicated PONs in cancer biology. Reduced PON1 activity is associated with pro-oxidative and pro-inflammatory states, and some PON1 genetic variants have been linked to cancer susceptibility in specific populations. The antioxidant and anti-apoptotic functions of PON2 suggest a role in promoting cancer cell survival and resistance to chemotherapy. PON2 appears to be protective in normal tissues by reducing oxidative damage, but when overexpressed in cancer cells, it promotes tumor growth by making the cells less susceptible to oxidative and ER stress23. PON3, though less well characterized, may contribute to cellular protection through its HDL-associated antioxidant activity or its mitochondrial functions. The role of PON3 seems to be dependent on tumor type, stage, and the level of oxidative stress. In early carcinogenesis, PON3’s antioxidant activity may be protective24, whereas in established tumors, the same activity can favor survival and therapy resistance25.

PON enzyme replacement therapy

Purified or recombinant PON, administered via various routes, provides rapid therapeutic benefits

PON replacement or enzyme therapy, through the administration of exogenous PON via various routes such as intravenous, intramuscular26, or intraperitoneal26 injection, topical formulation, and intranasal27 has been proposed as a strategy to harness the therapeutic potential of PON (Fig. 2). Enzyme therapy represents a promising approach for certain diseases and pathological conditions due to its effectiveness, specificity, and rapid onset of action. In acute intoxications or other rapidly developing conditions, immediate therapeutic intervention is critical. Therefore, strategies aimed at inducing endogenous PON expression may be suboptimal, as the processes of transcription, translation, and protein secretion take time to reach therapeutically relevant serum levels. Additionally, some individuals exhibit inherently low or dysfunctional PON activity, limiting the effectiveness of strategies that rely on upregulating endogenous PON. In such cases, administration of exogenous PON may provide a more efficient therapeutic effect compared to approaches targeting expression or activation of an impaired enzyme. PON for enzyme therapy can be either purified from animal or human donors28,29 or produced using recombinant technology in expression systems such as E. coli26. The administration of PON, however, may be limited by challenges such as immunogenicity, rapid clearance, enzyme instability, and unintended interactions. Additionally, the high versatility of PON’s enzymatic reactions may lead to off-target effects. The application of recombinant protein technology and nanotechnology offers key advantages, including reduced cost, scalability, targeted therapy, and the ability to engineer proteins through directed evolution for enhanced stability, activity, and specificity.

Externally administered PON rapidly attenuates acute toxicity

Given the diverse physiological functions of the PON enzyme family, including roles in detoxification and lipid metabolism, diseases with relevant underlying etiologies may benefit from PON-based enzyme therapy. Organophosphate intoxication, which remains prevalent in certain regions of the world30–34, continues to be a major public health concern due to its high morbidity and mortality rates35. Neurological complications are the most commonly reported outcomes of organophosphate poisoning36,37, however, other severe manifestations such as acute pancreatitis and pancreatic necrosis38,39, cardiac complications40,41, and gastrointestinal manifestations42–44 have also been observed. Immediate intervention following organophosphate exposure is critical in preventing life-threatening consequences.

PONs effectively hydrolyze organophosphates, offering a promising therapeutic approach to mitigate their toxicity45. Administration of purified exogenous PONs via intravenous or intraperitoneal injection has been shown to confer significant protection against chlorpyrifos toxicity when given both prior to and following exposure, with effects lasting up to 24 h. This suggests their potential as adjunctive treatments for organophosphate poisoning, particularly in tissues such as the brain and diaphragm, where damage contributes most significantly to morbidity46,47. Recombinant PONs expressed in E. coli and administered via intraperitoneal or intramuscular routes have shown comparable protective effects26.

Compared to other candidate enzymes, such as bacterial phosphotriesterases, PON1 exhibits several superior properties for treating organophosphate toxicity. While PON1 shows comparable catalytic efficiency against agents like sarin and soman, it does not elicit an immunogenic response upon injection, allowing for improved circulation. Additionally, PON1 operates optimally at physiological temperatures and across a broad pH range, making it a more suitable therapeutic candidate48.

Externally administered PON improves lipid profile

Atherosclerosis and dysregulation of HDL and LDL levels have been widely studied as disease models for evaluating the therapeutic potential of PON in lipid metabolism disorders, owing to substantial evidence supporting PON’s involvement in their pathophysiology49. PON has been demonstrated to protect LDL from oxidative modification, hydrolyze lipid peroxides in ex-vivo samples from human carotid and coronary atheromatous lesions, and reduce monocyte transmigration—key processes involved in the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis50,51. Based on these pathophysiological mechanisms, administration of PON has been proposed as a potential therapeutic strategy to mitigate atherosclerosis and lipoprotein imbalance by decreasing the burden of oxidized lipids52.

At the organ level, administration of PON1 has been shown to increase arylesterase and lactonase activities in the serum, liver, kidney, and heart of mice following injection53,54. Consistent with these findings, intraperitoneal injection of recombinant PON1 has been reported to significantly enhance macrophage- and HDL-associated PON1 arylesterase and lactonase activities. This is accompanied by an increased capacity of HDL to promote cholesterol efflux, inhibition of macrophage-mediated LDL oxidation, and suppression of monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation—all suggesting the potential anti-atherogenic effects of exogenously administered PON153,54. Furthermore, intraperitoneal injection of oxidized LDL–PON1-treated macrophages resulted in a marked increase in the fecal excretion of macrophage-derived cholesterol, providing additional evidence for the role of PON1 in enhancing reverse cholesterol transport55.

However, despite this promising evidence, the precise mechanisms by which PONs influence lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis remain incompletely understood. The complexity of their regulation, combined with the interplay of genetic and environmental factors, presents significant challenges in translating these findings into effective therapies. Moreover, the necessity for long-term administration to sustain therapeutic benefits raises practical concerns regarding treatment cost, patient adherence, and safety. These unresolved issues underscore the need for further basic and clinical research to clarify the full spectrum of PONs’ roles in lipid homeostasis and to critically evaluate their true potential as therapeutic agents.

Externally administered PON shows potential in suppressing infections

Enzyme therapy with PON may also have therapeutic potential in the management of certain infections. Quorum sensing, particularly lactone-based autoinducers such as N-acyl homoserine lactones (AHLs), plays a critical role in the regulation of virulence factors during bacterial infections56. AHL-mediated quorum-sensing systems have been identified in pathogens such as P. aeruginosa57, Burkholderia cepacia58, Aeromonas hydrophila59, and Klebsiella pneumoniae60. These signaling molecules enable bacteria to coordinate gene expression, including virulence, once a critical population density is reached. Consequently, disrupting AHL signaling has been shown to prevent or attenuate infections61–63.

PON enzymes, with their inherent lactonase activity, efficiently hydrolyze AHLs, thereby interfering with bacterial communication and virulence27. Compared to bacterial lactonases, PONs may offer advantages due to their lower immunogenicity when administered locally or systemically. The case for PON enzyme therapy is further strengthened by evidence that certain AHLs, such as 3OC12 produced by P. aeruginosa, can inhibit endogenous PON activity, compromising host innate defense. Therefore, exogenous administration of PON may not only suppress bacterial virulence by degrading AHLs but also restore impaired host immunity by replenishing PON activity. Using PON as a therapeutic approach for infections, however, is still in its infancy, with no direct evidence supporting its applicability. This represents a promising avenue for future research, particularly given the rising challenge of microbial resistance.

Protein engineering and nanocarriers enhance PON enzyme therapeutic efficacy

Although numerous studies have employed purified or recombinant PONs as standalone therapeutic agents, recent efforts have increasingly focused on enhancing the pharmacological and biochemical properties of PONs to improve their therapeutic efficacy. Some approaches, such as directed evolution, aim to optimize enzymatic properties including substrate specificity, catalytic efficiency, and stability53,64. Others focus on developing advanced delivery systems to improve PON stability, circulation time, and targeted delivery27. For instance, encapsulation of PONs in liposomes has been shown to prolong their half-life and enhance enzymatic activity65. Similarly, immobilization of PONs onto nanomaterials—such as magnetic nanoparticles—can improve both stability and enzymatic performance, while also enabling targeted delivery through the application of an external magnetic field66.

Despite advancements in enhancing circulation and targeting capabilities, the efficacy of PONs and their accessibility to certain tissues remains limited due to suboptimal pharmacokinetic properties or the presence of biological barriers such as the blood-brain barrier and the blood-retinal barrier. In this regard, fusing recombinant human PON1 to the third domain of human serum albumin (HSA) markedly improved its therapeutic profile. This HSA–PON1 construct retains full catalytic activity yet exhibits a dramatically prolonged circulating half-life and provides dose-dependent prophylactic protection against paraoxon exposure in rats, including delayed symptom onset, preserved cholinesterase activity across tissues, and prevention of mortality67.

Given the critical role of PONs in protecting against organophosphate-induced neurotoxicity and their involvement in both acute and chronic neurological disorders, strategies to improve PON delivery to the brain, or to other organs protected by similar barriers, are of significant therapeutic interest. One proposed approach involves engineering PONs as fusion proteins with monoclonal antibodies targeting the human insulin receptor, which has been shown to facilitate transcytosis across the blood-brain barrier68. However, such modifications may alter the enzymatic activity or stability of PONs, and further studies are warranted to preserve or enhance their functional integrity. Overall, protein engineering strategies aimed at improving PON activity, solubility, and stability, along with the development of novel carriers for targeted delivery, hold promise for increasing the therapeutic efficacy of PON-based enzyme therapies.

Pharmacological agents affecting PON level and activity

Previously approved drugs alter PON status at multiple molecular levels

Drug repurposing offers a strategic approach to utilize approved or investigational drugs beyond their original therapeutic indications. This method is associated with a lower risk of failure due to established safety profiles and can accelerate the development of effective therapies through more cost-efficient pathways69. Several approved drugs—originally indicated for conditions such as hypertension, infections, and dyslipidemia—have shown potential to modulate the status of PON enzymes. These drugs typically exert their effects through two primary mechanisms: directly influencing the enzymatic activity of PON or altering its expression (Fig. 3). In addition, less characterized mechanisms may be involved, including post-transcriptional or post-translational modifications, or indirect effects via systemic metabolic changes that impact PON regulation. While such effects on PON activity or expression may have initially been viewed as off-target, they nonetheless highlight opportunities for therapeutic repurposing aimed at modulating PON levels and function. However, because most evidence derives from enzyme- and cell-level studies, their clinical relevance remains unproven, and current findings should be regarded as only an early foundation for identifying potential pharmacologic modulators of PON.

PON level can be pharmacologically modulated

Drugs can also modulate PON status by affecting its gene expression. For example, resveratrol significantly enhances PON1 enzyme activity compared to placebo, likely by regulating gene expression through binding to sequences similar to the estrogen response element-2 in the PON1 promoter region70. Additionally, resveratrol acts as a potent agonist of PPARγ71,72 and as a ligand for aryl hydrocarbon receptors (AhRs)73 both of which increase PON1 gene expression and activity upon activation. Other PON inducers include pioglitazone and curcumin, which upregulate PON2 expression and are noted as potent PPAR agonists74,75. Ticagrelor has also been reported to induce PON expression, though whether it directly influences PON transcription remains unclear76. Conversely, valproic acid has been shown to reduce PON2 mRNA and protein levels in a dose-dependent manner in glioblastoma multiform cells, via a mechanism that has yet to be characterized77.

The effect of anti-hyperlipidemic drugs, including fibrates and statins, on PON status has garnered significant interest in recent years, given PONs’ role in lipid metabolism and their function as indicators of metabolic health. Treatment of HuH7 cells with fenofibric acid induces a moderate but significant increase (30–40%) in PON1 mRNA levels78. This upregulation of PON1 mRNA correlates with increased serum PON1 activity, as demonstrated in meta-analyses of clinical studies. Notably, the type of fibrate used is important: gemfibrozil and fenofibrate produce greater increases in serum PON1 activity compared to bezafibrate, suggesting a structure-activity relationship79.

Treatment of HuH7 cells with statins such as pravastatin, simvastatin, or fluvastatin results in a 30–50% reduction in PON1 mRNA levels; these inhibitory effects are fully reversed by co-treatment with mevalonate78. Supporting this, fluvastatin reduces plasma and liver PON1 activity in rats80. However, contrasting findings exist: simvastatin was found to upregulate PON1 promoter activity in HepG2 cells and to increase serum PON1 activity and concentration in patients81. Meta-analyses of clinical data suggest that statin therapy significantly elevates PON1 paraoxonase and arylesterase activities, although it does not significantly alter PON1 protein concentration82. These conflicting results across cell lines, animal models, and human studies indicate that further research is required to clarify how statins modulate PON. Moreover, a structure-activity association may exist for statins, as meta-analytic stratification by statin type shows a significant effect for simvastatin, a borderline significant effect for atorvastatin, and no significant effect for rosuvastatin on PON1 activity82.

Extending the metabolic perspective, incretin-based therapies add further nuance to PON regulation. Although GLP-1 receptor and DPP-4 pathways intersect with metabolic and oxidative stress signaling, their effects on PON1 appear variable rather than uniformly inductive. In fructose-fed rats, exenatide lowered PON1 arylesterase activity, plasma levels, and hepatic protein without altering mRNA, suggesting modulation at a post-transcriptional metabolic level, whereas sitagliptin showed no significant effect. Together, these findings imply that regulation of metabolic pathways does not necessarily converge on PON biology83. Although mechanistically possible, the therapeutic implications of modulating PONs expression, as suggested here, remain insufficiently validated in clinical settings and warrant further investigation.

Pharmacological interference with PONs’ enzymatic functions enables targeted therapeutics

Modulation of PON enzymatic activity is among the most extensively studied mechanisms to regulate PON status. Both inhibition and induction of PON activity may be therapeutically beneficial, depending on the underlying disease etiology. Inhibition of enzymatic activity can occur via competitive or noncompetitive mechanisms, depending on how a drug interacts with the amino acid residues of PON enzymes84. The chemical structure of the drug plays a crucial role in determining its mode of inhibition. For example, gadolinium-based contrast agents such as gadoteric acid, gadopentetic acid, gadoxetate disodium, and gadodiamide have all been shown to inhibit PON activity, but their mechanisms differ according to their molecular structure. Specifically, gadoxetate disodium and gadodiamide act as competitive inhibitors, whereas gadoteric acid and gadopentetic acid exhibit noncompetitive inhibition85. Similar structure-dependent inhibitory effects have been observed with other drug classes, including calcium-channel blockers86, imidazolium and benzimidazolium salts87, proton-pump inhibitors88, carbamates89, as well as antibacterial and antifungal agents90,91.

In addition to the overall molecular structure, structure-activity relationship studies within specific chemical families have shown that variations in functional groups and their electrochemical properties significantly influence the inhibitory activity of compounds against PON enzymes. Quinones, for example, are potent inhibitors of PON1; however, their selectivity and inhibitory potency depend on the nature of substituents on the aromatic ring. Quinones bearing free chloro and hydroxyl groups exhibit particularly strong inhibition of PON184. Similarly, in sulfonamides, the presence and position of functional groups such as chloride, bromide, and amine on the thiophene ring are critical determinants of their inhibitory effects92.

In the case of indazoles, variations in binding affinity related to fluoride, chloride, and bromide substituents on the 1H-indazole molecule correspond to distinct inhibitory activities93. This pattern extends to other compounds such as midodrine, where free amine and hydroxyl groups contribute to its inhibitory potential94. Moreover, the presence of a chlorine atom in molecules like moxifloxacin hydrochloride and lornoxicam, as well as fluoride groups in certain serotonin reuptake inhibitors, is associated with strong PON1 inhibition95–97.

Conversely, in imidazolium coumarin derivatives, methylation at position 2 reduces inhibitory activity, likely due to the absence of an acidic imidazolium proton98. Additionally, coumarin-substituted benzimidazole-based Ag(I), Ru(II), and Pd(II) N-heterocyclic carbene complexes exhibit weaker PON1 inhibition compared to their corresponding benzimidazolium salts99. These findings suggest that differences in the electrochemical properties of molecules—mediated by the electronegativity of substituents—play a key role in determining their interactions with the PON active site and thus their inhibitory potency.

Notably, some drugs have been shown to selectively affect the lactonase or arylesterase activities of PON. For example, both nevirapine and its metabolite 12-hydroxy-nevirapine increase paraoxonase and lactonase activities, whereas only 12-hydroxy-nevirapine enhances arylesterase activity100. A similar pattern is observed with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs. Diclofenac sodium and tenoxicam significantly reduce lactonase activity, but only tenoxicam decreases arylesterase activity of PON101. These findings, along with the variable inhibitory potency of different drugs on PON, suggest a broad range of potential to selectively modulate specific enzymatic activities of PON depending on the therapeutic application.

In contrast to most drugs that inhibit PON activity, some have been found to enhance its enzymatic function, albeit through less well-characterized mechanisms. For instance, glycation of PON1 reduces its activity, while metformin increases PON activity, likely due to its anti-glycemic effects75. Metformin does not directly affect PON enzymatic activity but rather reduces blood glucose levels, which in turn may decrease PON1 glycation and restore its activity. Although the impact of other anti-glycemic agents on PON status remains unexplored, it is plausible that they might similarly improve PON activity unless they exert stronger inhibitory effects via direct or indirect pathways. Additionally, increased intake of vitamins C and E, as well as treatment with orlistat, a lipoprotein lipase inhibitor, have been linked to enhanced PON1 activity102.

Recent work has also shown that targeted activation of PON enzymes, beyond indirect metabolic modulation, may represent a potential therapeutic strategy. In a high-throughput screen of nearly 1000 marketed drugs, dantrolene emerged as a potent small-molecule activator of PON1, enhancing its catalytic efficiency and improving HDL-associated antioxidant capacity linked to immunosuppressive and atheroprotective effects103. Similarly, Vutiglabridin, a recently identified PON2 agonist, enhances PON2 activity, leading to reduced mitochondrial ROS and improved mitochondrial function. These effects translate into activation of autophagy and decreased hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammation in nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, as well as PON2-dependent neuroprotection in Parkinson’s disease through attenuation of glial activation and preservation of dopaminergic neurons22,104,105.

Although various pharmacological agents can alter PON status, most available evidence comes from in-vitro or enzyme-level assays, leaving the clinical relevance of these observations uncertain. Furthermore, because the PON family comprises three subtypes with distinct tissue distributions and biological roles, subtype-specific evaluation of drug responses is needed before therapeutic applications can be credibly proposed.

Genetic engineering and gene therapy of PON

Genetic heterogeneity of PONs as a basis for functional modulation

The PON gene cluster, including PON1, PON2, and PON3, is located on chromosome 7q21.3-22.1 in humans. These genes share extensive structural homology, which may be due to a tandem duplication of a common evolutionary precursor45,106. Various polymorphisms exist in the gene structure and promoter regions of all members of the PON family, which determine the level and activity of these enzymes107–109.

Different studies have found associations between various SNPs, PON1 activity, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular diseases in diverse ethnic groups110–119. Many other disorders, their clinical course, and outcomes, including cancers (e.g., prostate, lung, and breast cancers), type 2 diabetes mellitus, aggressive periodontitis, idiopathic inflammatory myopathies, sickle cell disease, and different neurological disorders (e.g., autism, Parkinson’s disease, schizophrenia), have been shown to be associated with genetic polymorphisms in the PON1 gene8,120–122. Genetic polymorphisms in PON2 and PON3 are less investigated compared to PON1; however, some SNPs have been found to be associated with various disorders. Associations between PON2 genetic polymorphisms and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, and cardiovascular diseases have been studied, although the results remain controversial123–128. PON2 exerts its function at the cellular level; therefore, modulating this enzyme may have broader applications alongside PON1. In the case of PON3, six functionally active SNPs have been identified in its promoter region, and they have been shown to influence circulating PON3 levels129. Although the association between PON3 SNPs and diseases, especially cardiovascular diseases, has been investigated, and some SNPs have also been found in the coding region of PON3, there is no consensus on the impact of these SNPs on disease susceptibility130.

The pivotal role of PONs in various diseases and the influence of genetic and epigenetic factors on their concentrations underscore the importance of genetic manipulation and modulation of the PON enzyme family for developing therapeutic strategies. Many studies have investigated genetic modification of PON genes to further elucidate their roles in the pathogenesis of diseases, particularly atherosclerosis, metabolic disorders, organophosphate intoxication, and cancer. Although the clinical implications have not yet been directly addressed, similar techniques could be applied for therapeutic purposes. Based on the existing evidence, various approaches, including gene delivery, RNA interference, and overexpression or knocking out the genes, as well as gene editing techniques, could be utilized to harness PONs’ therapeutic potential in different diseases (Fig. 4).

Atherosclerosis and lipid disorders

Genetic engineering of PON enzymes represents a promising therapeutic strategy. PON1 activity is associated with the development of atherosclerosis and insulin resistance and has been reported as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular diseases131,132. Overexpression of human PON1 in mice deficient in both leptin and LDL receptor, which are susceptible to atherosclerosis, significantly inhibited atherosclerosis, reduced total plaque volume, and decreased the amount of oxidized LDL and macrophages in the plaques. However, expression of human PON1 in these mice had no effect on plasma total cholesterol, triglycerides, or HDL distribution profiles133. Additionally, expression of human PON3 in apolipoprotein E knockout mice increased antioxidant capacity, enhanced macrophage cholesterol efflux, and reduced lipid hydroperoxide levels134. Co-expression of PON1 and PON3 in apolipoprotein E knockout mice reduced atherosclerotic plaque area, although they exerted their antiatherogenic effects through distinct mechanisms. While co-expression showed synergistic effects in promoting macrophage cholesterol efflux, no such synergism was observed in reducing atherosclerotic lesion size135. Ex vivo engineering of cells with PONs followed by transplantation into the liver represents another strategy to achieve the beneficial effects of PONs136.

While PON genes can be manipulated directly, viral vectors have been utilized to deliver PON genes, successfully inducing their therapeutic effects. It has been demonstrated that intravenous injection of an adenovirus containing human PON2 into apolipoprotein E knockout mice suppressed the progression of atheroma formation and reduced oxidative stress, although PON2 was not detectable in their serum. The serum of these mice contained lower levels of lipid hydroperoxides, exhibited enhanced macrophage cholesterol efflux, and LDL from these mice was less susceptible to oxidation. These results indicate that an increase in PON2, an intracellular enzyme, may modulate the oxidative stress of plasma components134. Additionally, certain cells, such as endothelial progenitor cells, can be engineered via adenoviruses containing PON genes and subsequently transplanted into target tissues. Interestingly, this method demonstrated superior therapeutic effects compared to administration of adenoviruses alone. Sendai virus has also been utilized as a vector to deliver the PON1 gene locally to atherosclerotic lesions, as local expression of PON1 may be more effective. This local overexpression inhibited LOX-1 expression in the intima, media, and smooth muscle cells of the artery. LOX-1 is a mediator and biomarker of plaque formation, induced by oxidized LDL in atherosclerotic lesions. Oxidative stress in PON1-treated arteries was also decreased. This suggests that local expression of PON1 may prevent oxidized LDL formation, thereby inhibiting macrophage accumulation, intimal thickening, and atherosclerotic plaque formation in the vascular lumen137. Nanocarriers such as liposomes have also proven beneficial in delivering PON genes, enhancing their therapeutic effects. Transfection of rat epicardial fat with liposome-encapsulated recombinant PON1 gene has been shown to suppress atherosclerosis by increasing PON1 gene expression in peripheral arterial vascular tissue, leading to reduced total cholesterol, LDL levels, and the coronary artery intima/media thickness ratio138.

Although carriers and vectors have been extensively utilized in PON gene therapy, intravenous injection of plasmid DNA containing PON1 also possesses therapeutic benefits. This approach results in increased serum PON1 levels and superoxide dismutase activity, decreased total cholesterol, LDL, total triglycerides, and hepatic lipid accumulation139.

Cancer

The PON enzyme family has been implicated in cancer pathogenesis through various, yet not fully elucidated, mechanisms. For example, studies suggest that PON1 plays a critical role in regulating the immune system, thereby influencing cancer development140. Additionally, oxidative stress is a key contributor to cancer pathogenesis, and PON enzymes may affect tumor progression by modulating the cellular redox state. While oxidative stress is a major driver of mutagenesis, it can also serve as a therapeutic tool by inducing apoptosis in cancer cells.

Radiotherapy and chemotherapy remain standard treatments for various cancers, primarily by increasing ROS levels that induce DNA damage and apoptosis in cancer cells. However, some cancers develop mechanisms to evade this ROS-induced apoptosis by upregulating enzymes that mitigate oxidative stress. Notably, overexpression of PON2, an enzyme with antioxidant activity, has been reported in several tumor types141–144. Given PONs’ ability to counteract oxidative stress, modulating their expression through genetic engineering has been investigated as a potential cancer treatment strategy across multiple malignancies, including melanoma145, bladder cancer23,146, oral squamous cell carcinomas141, gastric cancer143, ovarian cancer144, and hepatocellular carcinoma24.

Knockdown of PON2 or silencing using RNA interference in cancer cells such as melanoma, bladder, gastric carcinoma, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, and oral squamous cell carcinomas diminishes malignant features such as proliferation, invasion, and migration, and enhances sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents and radiotherapy23,141,143,145–147. In contrast, PON2 overexpression in a mouse xenograft model of ovarian cancer prevented tumor formation and suppressed proliferation of ID8 ovarian cancer cells by downregulating IGF-1 expression144. Similarly, PON3 overexpression in a mouse model of hepatocellular carcinoma reduced tumor growth compared to controls24.

Overall, silencing PON2 appears therapeutically beneficial in most cancers studied, except ovarian cancer, where its overexpression inhibits tumorigenesis. PON2 acts at the cellular level to protect cancer cells from oxidative stress, potentially reducing the efficacy of ROS-inducing therapies like chemotherapy and radiotherapy. Knockdown of PON2 decreases cell viability and enhances treatment susceptibility. Conversely, PON1 and PON3 primarily exert antioxidant effects in serum, with their downregulation linked to hepatocellular carcinoma24. These enzymes may help inhibit tumor development by reducing oxidative DNA damage.

Toxicity

Genetic variations in PON1 can influence individual susceptibility to toxicity. One of the important variations in PON1 gene is the p.(Q192R) polymorphism, which is located in the active site of the enzyme and affects the catalytic activity of the enzyme toward different substrates. The 192Q allozyme is more efficient in hydrolyzing diazoxon and nerve agents (sarin and soman), whereas the 192R allozyme is more efficient in hydrolyzing paraoxon and chlorpyrifos-oxon6. The Q192R polymorphism also affects PON1 activity on lactone structures; for instance, 192Q has higher efficiency in hydrolyzing δ-valerolactone and 2-coumaranone, while 192R has higher efficiency in hydrolyzing thiolactone108,148. Recombinant adenoviruses encoding the two human PON1 isoforms (192R and 192Q) have been constructed and administered intravenously to mice to evaluate their efficacy against organophosphate poisoning. Both isoforms increased serum arylesterase activity, but only the 192R isoform elevated paraoxonase activity as anticipated. Importantly, both isozymes prevented the inactivation of brain acetylcholinesterase following chlorpyrifos exposure149. Similarly, gene therapy using liver-specific adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) delivering the engineered PON1-IF11 gene conferred protection against multiple lethal doses of G-series nerve agents—including tabun, sarin, cyclosarin, and soman—in mice. PON1-IF1164,150,151 is a modified PON1 variant with enhanced organophosphate hydrolysis efficiency that provides asymptomatic protection lasting several months post-administration. These findings suggest that PON1 gene therapy may serve as an effective prophylactic treatment against G-series nerve agents in humans152. Furthermore, intravenous injection of naked plasmid DNA encoding PON1 increases serum enzyme levels, providing protection against organophosphate poisoning by extending survival time and reducing mortality153. Overall, overexpression of PON1 via gene therapy shows promise as both a prophylactic and therapeutic approach to mitigate the harmful health effects of organophosphate exposure.

Oxidative stress

The liver, as the main site of metabolic activity in the body, metabolizes various compounds that produce ROS. Although the liver is equipped with specialized defense mechanisms against ROS, it remains susceptible to oxidative stress. Since PONs have antioxidant properties, the protective effects of gene therapy with PONs in liver injury have garnered significant attention. In this context, adenovirus-mediated delivery of PON1 and PON3 genes has demonstrated protective effects against carbon tetrachloride-induced liver injury, an established experimental model of severe toxic liver damage. The extent of hepatic oxidative stress and subsequent liver injury was reduced by enhanced expression of PON1 or PON3154. The effects of PON transfection are also reflected in liver function biomarkers, such as alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, and malondialdehyde, as well as histological assessments showing prevention of hepatocyte necrosis, hemorrhage, vacuolar change, and hydropic degeneration155.

Lifestyle factors

Physical activity variably modulates PON levels and activity

The interplay between physical activity and PON enzymes has garnered interest, primarily due to the shared connection with oxidative stress. Nonetheless, studies investigating this relationship have yielded mixed results. Some findings indicate that PON1 activity significantly declines after exercise, possibly as a consequence of increased oxidative stress and free radical production. This post-exercise reduction in enzymatic activity appears to be attenuated by alpha-tocopherol supplementation, likely through the mitigation of lipid peroxidation156. Similarly, while moderate exercise does not appear to affect PON1 expression, acute bouts of exercise have been shown to downregulate its expression157.

Conversely, regular physical activity has been associated with elevated PON1 activity. For instance, adolescent athletes with consistent exercise routines exhibit higher PON1 activity levels compared to sedentary controls, despite showing increased markers of oxidative stress such as total oxidative status, oxidative stress index, and lipid hydroperoxides158. Moreover, genetic factors may modulate the response, as studies indicate that improvements in PON1 activity following aerobic exercise depend on the individual’s PON1-192 phenotype159.

Based on these findings, it can be hypothesized that regular physical activity may enhance PON status and yield beneficial outcomes, whereas single episodes of acute exercise may have the opposite effect. This hypothesis is partly supported by data showing that wrestlers have higher basal PON1 activity than sedentary controls, with no significant difference in activity observed immediately after exercise160. However, this view is challenged by a meta-analysis that concluded both acute and chronic exercise have negligible and non-significant effects on PON1 concentration and activity overall161.

Beyond PON1, PON3 also appears to be responsive to physical activity. Training increases both PON3 mRNA and plasma protein levels. In contrast, acute exercise reduces PON3 protein levels by 30% and enzymatic activity by 40% in trained individuals, while having no effect in sedentary controls157.

Variability in study design and measured parameters poses a major challenge in determining how physical activity influences PON status and in assessing whether such modulation could translate into clinically meaningful effects. Future studies should standardize physical activity, such as frequency, intensity, duration, and caloric expenditure, and account for plasma volume shifts. Additionally, genetic polymorphisms, PON isoform-specific activities, and the choice of assay substrates must be clearly addressed. Rigorous studies are needed to confirm current findings and uncover the underlying mechanisms.

Dietary modulation of PON level and activity

Diet is closely linked to the status of PON enzymes through its energy content and composition. However, delineating its precise effects on PON activity and expression remains inherently challenging due to the multitude of contributing factors. The impact of caloric intake on the status of PON1 appears to be complex and somewhat contradictory. Caloric restriction has been reported to reduce serum PON1 levels and enzymatic activity, despite having no significant effect on PON1 mRNA expression. This discrepancy suggests that post-transcriptional mechanisms may be involved. One proposed explanation is the decreased stability of HDL–PON1 complexes or a reduction in circulating HDL particles, which are essential for the transport and stabilization of PON1 in the bloodstream. In contrast, caloric restriction has been shown to increase paraoxonase activity in the liver, while leaving hepatic arylesterase activity unchanged162.

Although investigated in a limited number of cell lines and animal models, hyperglycemic conditions have been consistently associated with a reduction in the enzymatic activity of both PON1 and PON2163,164. This decline is likely attributable to nonenzymatic glycation of the proteins, which impairs their function. Interestingly, despite the reduced enzymatic activity, high glucose concentrations have been shown to induce transactivation of the PON1 gene in hepatocytes, suggesting a compensatory transcriptional response165. The effect of high-fat diets on PON1 activity, however, has been variable166,167.

Beyond caloric content, the nutritional composition of the diet directly influences PON status. In particular, the chemical structure of dietary fatty acids plays a significant role in modulating PON activity. For instance, replacing dietary saturated fats with trans fats has been shown to reduce serum PON activity168. Additionally, certain fatty acids are differentially associated with PON1 activity. Myristic acid (a saturated fat) and gadoleic acid (a monounsaturated fat) are positively correlated with PON1 activity, whereas arachidonic acid and eicosapentaenoic acid (both polyunsaturated fats) are negatively associated169.

Polyphenols are widely investigated dietary compounds affecting the status of PONs170. Broadly classified as flavonoids or non-flavonoids, they represent a diverse class of bioactive molecules typically characterized by at least one aromatic ring substituted with hydroxyl, phenolic, carbonyl, or other functional groups171–173. The presence of hydroxyl groups on flavonoid rings, as in flavones and flavonols such as luteolin, quercetin, kaempferol, and apigenin, enhances binding affinity to recombinant PON1, whereas conjugation via glucuronidation or sulfation reduces this interaction by masking hydroxyl groups.

Pomegranate juice is the most studied polyphenol-rich food in both animal and human research174. It has been shown to increase PON1 and PON2 expression levels175,176, as well as enhance paraoxonase, arylesterase, and lactonase activities of PON1177. Notably, pomegranate juice also promotes the binding of PON1 to HDL, thereby improving the enzyme’s stability and function177. Furthermore, it has been reported to restore PON1 activity suppressed by a high-fat diet, suggesting its potential therapeutic role in oxidative stress-related metabolic disturbances175. Quercetin and resveratrol, two of the most studied polyphenols found in pomegranate juice and various berries, have been shown to increase both PON1 expression and activity178. Quercetin also upregulates PON2 protein levels, an effect with potential therapeutic relevance in obese asthmatics179. Despite these effects, other studies report no observable effect. This variability may stem from differences in experimental design, dosage and duration of supplementation, as well as characteristics of the study populations174.

Certain transcription factors, receptors, and signaling pathways are known to mediate the effects of polyphenols on PONs. The role of PPARs as transcriptional regulators of PON1 and PON2 expression has been well established176,180. Additionally, PON1 gene expression can be upregulated by both synthetic and natural dietary polyphenols via AhR-related pathways, suggesting a possible ligand-receptor interaction between polyphenols and AhR181. Other potential regulatory mechanisms involve the sterol regulatory element-binding protein-2 through its interaction with specificity protein 1 (Sp1)174 and NF-κB signaling pathway118. Beyond gene expression regulation, polyphenols may directly influence PON enzymatic activity. For instance, the isoflavan glabridin has been shown to bind to PONs, inducing structural changes that significantly enhance the enzyme’s ability to hydrolyze oxidized LDL-associated cholesteryl ester hydroperoxides182.

Although individual dietary components can induce or inhibit PONs, their combined effects within whole diets are far more complex due to interactions among nutrients, variability in intake, and substantial inter-individual differences in metabolism, genetics, and lifestyle. As a result, dietary findings, while informative for preventive or complementary strategies, remain far from clinical applicability. For such approaches, broader dietary patterns rather than isolated compounds should guide future investigations, with careful consideration of individual variability.

Microbiome

Microbiome composition modulates host metabolic components, including PONs

The microbiome plays an active role in human health and disease183. It shapes multiple aspects of host physiology and, in particular, influences metabolic pathways relevant to PON biology. The microbiome can be modulated through dietary interventions184, fecal microbiota transplantation185, antibiotics186, as well as the use of prebiotics and probiotics187 to achieve desired clinical outcomes188–190.

Multi-omic studies further support interactions between the microbiome and host metabolic proteins. In one analysis, 41 of 263 unique blood proteins were found to be significantly associated with Shannon diversity, a measure of taxonomic richness and evenness. Among the most strongly associated analytes was the LDL receptor. Other lipid markers such as triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol have also been linked to microbiome composition (Fig. 5). PON3 also appeared in the initial analysis but did not remain statistically significant after adjustment for sex, age, and BMI, and no other paraoxonase family members showed notable associations191,192. At the microbial taxa level, fourteen unique taxa, including the lipid-lowering species Akkermansia muciniphila, the butyrate-producing bacterium Roseburia hominis, and the hydrogen-consuming archaeon Methanobrevibacter smithii, have shown significant associations with PON3 levels193.

Despite the potential associations, evidence causally linking microbiome modulation to PON biology for clinical benefits remains scarce. In the only randomized controlled trial assessing this relationship to date, synbiotic supplementation in patients with chronic heart failure did not alter circulating PON1 levels, despite modest improvements in lipid parameters and inflammatory markers. This highlights the gap between correlative multi-omic associations and demonstrable modulation of PON enzymes in vivo, underscoring the need for well-controlled clinical studies specifically designed to evaluate microbiome–PON interactions194.

Microbiome-derived metabolites modulate PON status

One key mechanism by which the microbiome could influence human physiology is through the production of bioactive compounds. 63 metabolic pathways have been associated with PON3, and 97% of these pathways are significantly linked to more than one PON3-associated microbial species, typically in a consistent direction. Among the most strongly associated pathways are L-methionine biosynthesis and thiamine biosynthesis193. It should be noted that functional redundancy in microbial metabolism masks specific species’ roles, limiting precise targeting of PON-associated pathways. In addition to the normal microbiome, colonization by pathogenic bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus can also interfere with PON activity195.

Quorum-sensing molecules are among the most influential microbial products affecting PON status. PON enzymes, particularly PON2, are recognized as lactonases that protect against the effects of lactone-based quorum-sensing molecules. These signaling molecules, in turn, regulate PON expression and activity at multiple levels196–198. N-(3-oxododecanoyl)-L-homoserine lactone (3OC12) from P. aeruginosa, downregulates PON2 mRNA and protein expression and reduces its hydrolytic activity, at least in part by increasing intracellular Ca²⁺ levels199. Additionally, 3OC12 may influence PON levels by modulating the activity of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs), which are known to regulate PON gene expression200. Notably, PON2 activity is diminished more rapidly and extensively than protein levels, suggesting that 3OC12 also exerts post-translational effects that inhibit PON2’s enzymatic function199.

Trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), also derived from the microbiome, influences PON status. TMAO is primarily produced through a two-step process: first, the gut microbiota convert dietary choline and L-carnitine into trimethylamine (TMA); then, TMA is oxidized to TMAO in the liver. Specific bacterial gene clusters within the microbiome are responsible for TMA production. For example, the choline utilization (Cut) gene cluster—identified in the sulfate-reducing bacteria Desulfovibrio desulfuricans and Desulfovibrio alaskensis G20—catalyzes the anaerobic conversion of choline to TMA. The key genes in this cluster, CutC and CutD, encode choline TMA-lyase and its activating protein, respectively.

Another microbial pathway converts L-carnitine to TMA via a two-subunit oxidoreductase encoded by CntA and CntB, found in genera such as Acinetobacter and Serratia. A second carnitine-derived pathway involves the intermediate γ-butyrobetaine, which is produced in the ileum and further converted to TMA in the cecum and colon. Genes CntA/CntB and YeaW/YeaX—encoding enzymes with oxygenase and oxidoreductase activity that exhibit substrate promiscuity for γ-butyrobetaine, L-carnitine, choline, and betaine—are present in various gut microbiota, including Gammaproteobacteria (Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Citrobacter, Providencia, Shigella), Betaproteobacteria (Achromobacter), Firmicutes (Sporosarcina), and Actinobacteria.

In a cohort of patients with heart failure, plasma TMAO levels were found to be weakly but significantly inversely correlated with serum arylesterase activity201. Although a direct causal relationship between TMAO levels and PON1 activity has yet to be fully elucidated, TMAO is known to play roles in cholesterol and sterol metabolism, as well as in protein stabilization, which may indirectly influence PON1 function. Additionally, TMAO has been implicated in the pathogenesis of several diseases, including atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, renal dysfunction, diabetes, and cancer, potentially through mechanisms that also affect PON activity.

Despite evidence suggesting that the microbiome can influence PON status, translating microbiome–PON relationships into therapeutic strategies remains premature. Not only is the causal nature of this relationship unestablished, but microbiome-based interventions also face inherent challenges, including diet as a major confounding factor, substantial inter-individual variability in microbiome composition, and the risk of off-target effects.

Future perspective and the path toward clinical translation

Therapeutic strategies targeting enzymatic processes have a well-established history of clinical success, often achieved by either inhibiting or enhancing specific enzyme activities. Many widely-used medications—including statins, antibiotics, and enzyme replacement therapies—exert their effects through such modulation. Despite PONs serving as critical hubs in regulating redox balance, inflammation, and metabolite signaling, and accumulating evidence linking their dysregulation to a broad spectrum of diseases, they have yet to receive comparable attention as therapeutic targets. Additionally, lifestyle factors such as diet, exercise, and microbiome composition have been shown to influence PON expression and activity, suggesting non-pharmacological avenues for modulation.

Given the multifactorial etiology of many PON-associated diseases, effective therapies may involve a combination of approaches: enzyme replacement, drug repurposing targeting PON pathways, genetic manipulation, lifestyle interventions and microbiome modulation. Depending on the disease context, PON modulation may yield prevention, symptom control, or, in rare cases where enzyme deficiency is causal, potential cures. For acute conditions like toxicities, transient enzyme therapy may suffice, whereas chronic diseases such as atherosclerosis or cancer may require sustained multifaceted strategies.

Importantly, combinatorial or tissue-specific modulation of PONs’ enzymatic activities or expression levels could fine-tune therapeutic outcomes. For example, enhancing arylesterase activity may benefit organophosphate poisoning, while increased lactonase activity might be preferable in infectious contexts. Such differential regulation requires further mechanistic insights and targeted delivery methods.

Despite promising indications, the clinical feasibility of PON-based therapies remains contingent on rigorous etiological evidence demonstrating causal roles of PONs in disease pathogenesis, alongside the development of precise, safe, and effective modulation techniques. Current challenges include limited etiological studies, technological constraints in enzyme delivery and gene editing, and the complexity of PON-related pathways.

Additionally, several challenges and potential risks must be acknowledged when targeting PONs therapeutically. Due to their high genetic polymorphism, PON enzymes exhibit significant inter-individual variability in expression levels and enzymatic activity, complicating the prediction of treatment responses and potentially limiting the broad applicability of any single therapeutic strategy. Furthermore, PONs participate in multiple physiological functions and interact with diverse metabolic and signaling pathways, making it difficult to isolate and precisely direct their therapeutic effects through a single mechanism. This pleiotropy increases the risk of disrupting normal homeostasis, which could trigger a range of adverse outcomes, worsen existing disease processes, or even cause new pathologies. Therefore, the complexity of PON regulation and function demands carefully balanced therapeutic interventions.

Future research should prioritize elucidating the mechanistic underpinnings of PON involvement in disease, validating causative links, and conducting well-designed clinical trials and meta-analyses. Technological advancements—such as nanocarrier systems for enzyme stabilization, improved vectors for gene therapy, and integration of in-silico, in-vitro, and in-vivo models enhanced by machine learning—hold promise for overcoming existing hurdles. These innovations could enable personalized and efficient PON-targeted therapies with minimal adverse effects.

In summary, modulation of PON enzymes represents a compelling therapeutic strategy with the potential to impact a broad spectrum of acute and chronic diseases. With ongoing research and technological progress, PON-centered interventions may emerge as vital components of future precision medicine.
