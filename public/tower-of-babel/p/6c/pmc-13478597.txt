BMI–adiposity discrepancy across weight categories in primary school children: a prospective school-based cohort study
Authors: Shu-Feng Hsu, Yu-San Liao, Ching-Chi Chi, Jrhau Lung, Li-Wen Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The BMI-for-age z score (BAZ) is widely used to assess adiposity in children; however, whether standardized BMI consistently reflects body fatness across the BMI spectrum remains uncertain. In this prospective school-based cohort study in Chiayi, Taiwan (October 2014–May 2025), we enrolled 3,293 primary school children aged 6–12 years with up to 12 repeated body composition measurements per participant (17,339 total observations) using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). BAZ was strongly correlated with BIA-derived percent body fat (PBF) at baseline (Pearson r = 0.895; P < 0.001). However, discrepancy magnitude differed significantly across BMI categories (Kruskal–Wallis P < 0.001), with the smallest discrepancy in normal-weight children and greater discrepancies at both extremes. Longitudinal mixed-effects models showed that age (β = 0.367% points per year; 95%CI, 0.325–0.409; P < 0.001) and female sex (β = 2.608% points; 95%CI, 2.364–2.852; P < 0.001) were independently associated with higher PBF after adjustment for BAZ (β = 6.058; 95%CI, 5.994–6.122; P < 0.001), based on the primary model with random intercepts and random slope for age. These findings indicate that BAZ captures population-level adiposity variation, but its agreement with individual body fatness varies across the BMI spectrum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56628-w.

Introduction

Childhood is characterized by rapid and nonlinear physiological development accompanied by dynamic changes in both body size and body composition. In the two-compartment model of body composition, total body weight represents the sum of fat mass and fat-free mass,1,2 and age-related weight gain may result from varying proportions of these compartments across developmental stages. Consequently, when tracking growth trajectories or evaluating the effects of long-term interventions, an indicator that allows comparisons across ages and developmental stages is essential to distinguish true changes in adiposity from normal growth-related variation.

Body mass index (BMI) remains the most widely used surrogate measure of adiposity in clinical and epidemiologic settings. In pediatric populations, however, absolute BMI values are inherently age- and sex dependent, necessitating interpretation via standardized growth references such as the 2000 CDC BMI-for-Age Growth Charts3 and the 2007 WHO Growth Reference.4 Expressing BMI as a BMI-for-Age z score (BAZ) facilitates comparison relative to a reference population and allows longitudinal tracking across childhood. Nevertheless, the BAZ represents weight-for-height relative to population norms rather than measured body adiposity and does not distinguish between fat mass and fat-free mass. These compartments vary by age, sex, pubertal status, ethnicity, and individual growth patterns.5,6 Therefore, even after standardization, whether the BAZ consistently reflects adiposity across developmental stages and across the BMI spectrum remains uncertain.7–9.

Overweight and obesity are defined by the World Health Organization as “abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that presents a risk to health”.10 Although obesity is fundamentally a condition of excess body fat, the most common marker used to assess body fat is BMI—a measure of excess body weight, not body fat itself, and therefore only an indirect proxy for adiposity.11 A key limitation of BMI is that the association between BMI and body fat varies with age, sex, and ethnicity: at any given BMI, older individuals carry more fat mass than younger individuals, and females carry more fat mass than males.12 These biological sources of variation mean that BMI-based weight classifications may systematically misrepresent true adiposity at the individual level, underscoring the clinical importance of accurate adiposity assessment beyond BMI-based classification.

Body composition can be assessed via techniques such as skinfold thickness, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA), dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), air displacement plethysmography (ADP), and cross-sectional imaging.13 Among these methods, BIA is noninvasive, portable, and well suited for large-scale field studies in pediatric populations.14,15 Importantly, fat mass and fat-free mass carry potentially distinct cardiometabolic implications — higher fat mass index has been associated with less favorable metabolic risk profiles in young children, whereas greater fat-free mass may attenuate selected cardiometabolic markers.16 By providing estimates of percent body fat (PBF), BIA offers an opportunity to empirically evaluate the extent to which standardized BMI corresponds to measured adiposity.

In this school-based prospective cohort of primary school children, we examined both cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between the BAZ and BIA-derived PBF. In addition to assessing overall correlation, we quantified BMI–adiposity discrepancy via a residual-based approach and examined whether the magnitude of discrepancies differed across BAZ categories and by sex. By characterizing both the concordance and deviation between the standardized BMI and measured adiposity, this study aimed to delineate the interpretative boundaries of the BAZ as an age-standardized indicator of adiposity during childhood.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective, school-based open cohort study was conducted between October 2014 and May 2025 in Chiayi County, southwestern Taiwan, encompassing multiple successive IRB-approved protocols. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Chang Gung Medical Foundation (IRB No. 201401027A3, 201700932A3, 201802057B0, 201901889A3, and 202200380A3) and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all the participating children and their parents or legal guardians prior to enrollment. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

The study population comprised an opportunistic sample of primary school children from 11 publicly funded schools that agreed to participate; school participation was voluntary and no random sampling was performed. Ethnicity was not formally recorded at enrollment; the cohort reflects the demographic diversity of the local school-age population in Chiayi County, which includes children of various backgrounds as well as children of new immigrants primarily from Southeast Asian countries. Healthy children aged 6–12 years (school grades 1–6) were recruited after providing written assent and parental or guardian informed consent, and were followed longitudinally from enrollment until graduation. Body composition assessments were scheduled once per academic semester. Attendance at individual assessments varied because participation at each visit was voluntary, and some children were absent on the measurement day due to illness or other reasons. Missed observations were excluded and not imputed; the linear mixed-effects model accommodates this unbalanced follow-up under a missing-at-random assumption. Consequently, the number of measurements per participant ranged from 1 to 12 (total observations: 17,339).

Children were excluded if they had metallic implants incompatible with BIA, major chronic systemic diseases, long-term medication use (> 3 months), pregnancy, or conditions that precluded cooperation with study procedures. The exclusion criteria were identical across all cohorts.

Anthropometric assessment

All assessments were performed in the morning at each school site, approximately 2 h after breakfast, by trained research assistants via standardized procedures as previously described.17 Height and weight were measured once per visit, with measurements recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm and 0.1 kg, respectively. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. BAZ values were derived via the 2007 WHO Growth Reference via the WHO AnthroPlus software.4 Children were classified as underweight (BAZ < − 2), normal weight (BAZ − 2 to 1), overweight (BAZ > 1 to 2), or obese (BAZ > 2).

Bioelectrical impedance analysis

Body composition was assessed using a portable bioelectrical impedance analyzer (InBody 230; Biospace Corp., Seoul, Korea), which employs an 8-electrode, hand-to-foot configuration operating at frequencies of 20 kHz and 100 kHz to provide segmental body composition estimates across five body segments (right arm, left arm, trunk, right leg, and left leg). The validity of this device for estimating body composition in Taiwanese children aged 7–12 years has been established by our group against dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry as the reference method, demonstrating strong correlations for fat-free mass, fat mass, and PBF, although the device underestimated fat mass and percent body fat relative to dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry in both sexes.17.

The device was deployed during scheduled school visits, enabling standardized assessment within the school environment across repeated measurement waves. Fat-related parameters—including body fat mass, trunk fat mass, PBF, and trunk percentage fat—as well as lean-related parameters—including fat-free mass and skeletal muscle mass—were obtained for analysis. BIA and anthropometric assessments were performed on the same day under consistent procedural conditions. BIA-derived PBF was used as a practical field measure of adiposity to evaluate the agreement between standardized BMI and measured body fatness; it is acknowledged that BIA does not constitute a criterion standard for body composition assessment, and that estimates may be influenced by hydration status and device-specific prediction algorithms.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized using each child’s first available measurement (n = 3,293). Longitudinal analyses incorporated all 17,339 observations using linear mixed-effects models. Three competing model structures were evaluated using likelihood ratio tests: a random-intercepts-only model (M1), a model adding a random slope for age (M2), and a model adding a random slope for BAZ (M3) as a sensitivity check. M2 fitted significantly better than M1 (χ² = 3,208.2, df = 2, P < 0.001; ΔBIC = 3,189) and was adopted as the primary model. Fixed-effect estimates were consistent in direction and magnitude across all three specifications. The cross-sectional BAZ–PBF correlation was assessed via Pearson and Spearman coefficients. BMI–adiposity discrepancy was defined as the absolute residual from linear regression of PBF on the BAZ at baseline; differences across weight-status categories were tested with the Kruskal–Wallis test. Sensitivity analyses using restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were performed to assess robustness to nonlinearity in the BAZ–PBF relationship. Knots were placed at BAZ = − 2, 1, and 2 for the 3-knot model (corresponding to the WHO-defined underweight, overweight, and obesity boundaries) and at BAZ = − 2, 0, 1, 2, and 3 for the 5-knot model. Results

Baseline characteristics of the study participants

The baseline characteristics of the 3,293 primary school children (1,647 boys and 1,646 girls) are summarized in Table 2. According to the BAZ category, 1.8% were underweight, 60.6% were normal weight, 18.2% were overweight, and 19.3% were obese. Boys were taller (130.4 vs. 129.1 cm; P = 0.005) and heavier (32.6 vs. 29.9 kg; P < 0.001) than girls and had higher BMIs (18.6 vs. 17.4 kg/m²; P < 0.001) and BAZs (0.93 vs. 0.41; P < 0.001). No significant difference in PBF was observed between boys and girls at baseline (25.8% vs. 25.3%; P = 0.121), consistent with the known sex convergence in absolute body fatness before puberty. Despite similar fat percentages, boys had significantly greater absolute body fat mass, trunk BFM, fat-free mass, and skeletal muscle mass than girls did.

Cross-sectional association between BMI z score and percent body fat

At baseline, the BAZ was highly correlated with BIA-derived PBF (Pearson r = 0.895; 95% CI, 0.888–0.902; P < 0.001; Spearman ρ = 0.889; P < 0.001). BIA-derived PBF differed substantially across BAZ categories (Fig. 1). The median PBF was 14.5% (IQR, 11.7%–16.0%) in the underweight group, 19.9% (IQR, 16.9%–23.2%) in the normal-weight group, 30.7% (IQR, 27.7%–33.6%) in the overweight group, and 39.1% (IQR, 35.6%–42.9%) in the obesity group.

Despite the strong overall correlation, considerable overlap in PBF distributions was observed across adjacent BAZ categories. This interindividual variability within BMI-based classifications provided the empirical basis for quantifying the BMI–adiposity discrepancy using a residual-based approach.

Discrepancy of BMI–adiposity across weight-status categories

The BMI–adiposity discrepancy was operationally defined as the absolute value of the residuals from a linear regression model relating the BAZ to PBF. Using this residual-based measure, the magnitude of discrepancy varied significantly across BAZ categories at baseline (Fig. 2, Kruskal–Wallis test, P < 0.001). The smallest discrepancy was observed in children with normal weight (median absolute residual, 2.49; IQR, 1.13–4.17; n = 1,996), whereas substantially greater discrepancies were observed in those with underweight (median, 4.74; IQR, 3.12–7.30; n = 60), overweight (median, 2.89; IQR, 1.35–4.59; n = 600), and obese (median, 3.10; IQR, 1.55–5.26; n = 637) weights. Sex-stratified analyses demonstrated consistent patterns in both boys and girls (Supplementary Figure S1). The distributions of the absolute BMI–adiposity discrepancy stratified by sex and BAZ category are presented in Supplementary Table S1, confirming that the pattern of increasing discrepancy at the extremes of the BMI distribution was present in both sexes.

Longitudinal association between BMI z score and percent body fat

The results from the longitudinal linear mixed-effects models are presented in Table 3. The primary model (M2: random intercepts plus random slope for age) demonstrated significantly better fit than the random-intercepts-only model (M1: χ² = 3,208.2, df = 2, P < 0.001; ΔBIC = 3,189), confirming that children differed not only in baseline PBF but also in individual rates of age-related adiposity change. In M2, PBF was positively associated with BAZ (β = 6.058% points per unit BAZ; 95% CI, 5.994–6.122; P < 0.001). After adjustment for BAZ: older age (β = 0.367% points per year; 95% CI, 0.325–0.409; P < 0.001) and female sex (β = 2.608% points; 95% CI, 2.364–2.852; P < 0.001) were independently associated with higher PBF. Fixed-effect estimates were consistent across all three model specifications (M1, M2, M3), supporting the robustness of these associations. Effect estimates were similar in direction and magnitude to those from baseline cross-sectional analyses, indicating consistency of the BAZ–adiposity association across repeated measurements.

Residual diagnostics and model assumptions

Residual diagnostics based on baseline data (Supplementary Figure S2) revealed increasing dispersion of residuals across the BAZ range, indicating heteroscedasticity and potential nonlinearity in the association between the BAZ and PBF. These patterns are consistent with the observed amplification of BMI–adiposity discrepancy at the extremes of the weight distribution and support the use of distributional (rank-based) tests for comparing discrepancy across categories. Sensitivity analyses using RCS models with knots placed at the WHO BAZ classification boundaries showed modest improvement in fit (R² = 0.809 and 0.825 for the 3- and 5-knot models, respectively, vs. 0.802 for the linear model). The Kruskal–Wallis test remained highly significant under all specifications (all P < 0.001), confirming robustness to nonlinearity; discrepancy in the underweight group was attenuated under spline models (Supplementary Figure S3).

Misclassification analysis

Misclassification analysis revealed that 16.9% of BAZ-normal-weight children were underfat by DXA-equivalent PBF criteria. Among BAZ-overweight children, 86.8% had normal PBF. Among BAZ-obese children, 22.9% had normal PBF and 76.9% were overfat or obese by PBF. Among BAZ-underweight children, 70.0% were concordantly underfat. Full results are presented in Table 1; sex- and age-stratified patterns are in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Discussion

In this large and school-based cohort with repeated body composition measurements spanning up to six years of primary school, BAZ was strongly associated with BIA-derived PBF at the population level but demonstrated variable agreement at the individual level, with greater discrepancy observed toward the extremes of the BMI distribution. These findings indicate that while the BAZ adequately captures overall patterns of adiposity for population-level surveillance and screening, its precision as an individual-level estimator of body fatness is attenuated outside the normal-weight range. The considerable overlap in PBF distributions across BAZ categories further underscores the limitations of weight-status classifications on the basis of BMI alone during a developmental period characterized by dynamic and asynchronous changes in fat and fat-free mass.

Absolute BMI is not suitable for comparing adiposity across the age range of 6–12 years examined in this study. Both the 2000 CDC BMI-for-Age Growth Charts and the 2007 WHO Growth Reference demonstrate that BMI-for-age curves shift progressively upward over these years, reflecting normal somatic growth. Accordingly, a constant absolute BMI does not indicate stable relative weight status within age- and sex-specific reference distributions, as children naturally gain height and weight during this period. Interpretation on the basis solely of absolute BMI may therefore conflate physiological growth with genuine changes in adiposity. In this context, BAZ—which standardizes BMI relative to age and sex—is more appropriate than absolute BMI when comparing weight status across children at different ages.

Age-related differences in adiposity persisted even after standardization by BAZ. In the primary longitudinal model (M2), PBF increased by 0.367% points per year, independent of BAZ and sex. Thus, at the same BAZ and within the same sex, older children exhibited greater adiposity. While this age-related increase is consistent with known physiological changes including the adiposity rebound and pubertal fat redistribution, it may also reflect secular trends in dietary patterns, physical activity, and lifestyle factors over the study period (2014–2025). This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that children with identical BAZs may have substantially different levels of PBF.20,21 Together, these observations indicate that although the BAZ standardizes relative weight status for age, it does not fully capture age-related variation in body composition. The persistence of an independent age effect supports the need to evaluate the BMI–adiposity discrepancy beyond age-standardized BMI, particularly in longitudinal studies spanning pubertal development.

Sex-related differences in adiposity were also apparent after adjustment for age and BAZ. Girls had a 2.608% point greater percentage of body fat than boys did, which is consistent with established physiological differences in body composition during childhood, whereby girls generally maintain a greater proportion of body fat and lower fat-free mass than boys do.22,23 Notably, this difference persisted after adjustment for BAZ, indicating that standardized BMI does not fully account for sex-specific variation in adiposity. Consequently, even at the same age and BAZ, body composition differs systematically between boys and girls, reinforcing the limitation of BMI-based metrics as surrogates for adiposity across sexes.

These findings have practical implications for the use of BIA in pediatric settings. The misclassification analysis quantified the extent of BMI–adiposity discordance: 86.8% of BAZ-overweight and 22.9% of BAZ-obese children had normal DXA-equivalent PBF, while 16.9% of BAZ-normal-weight children were underfat — findings that underscore the limited precision of BMI-based classification at the individual level. Our results support the selective incorporation of body composition assessment as a complementary tool in contexts where more precise estimation of adiposity is clinically relevant. Potential applications include children at the extremes of the BAZ distribution, those with discordant clinical features (e.g., metabolic abnormalities despite a normal BMI classification or normal metabolic profiles despite high BMI), and settings in which longitudinal monitoring of body composition is a primary objective. At the population level, routine BIA for all children may not be feasible or necessary; however, targeted application in specific subgroups could enhance risk stratification and improve the interpretability of BMI-based classifications without substantially increasing the clinical or logistical burden.

The residual diagnostics indicated heteroscedasticity and potential nonlinearity in the BAZ–PBF relationship—increasing dispersion of residuals at higher BMI z scores—consistent with published reports that the BMI–adiposity relationship becomes less precise as weight status increases. These patterns support the use of rank-based statistical tests for comparing discrepancies across categories and suggest that linear models may underestimate the complexity of the BAZ–adiposity relationship, particularly in children with obesity.

Strengths include the large school-based cohort with up to 12 repeated measurements per child, enabling both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, and the use of a residual-based framework to quantify BMI–adiposity discrepancy as a continuous measure rather than categorical misclassification. The findings were consistent across sex-stratified and mixed-effects models, supporting robustness.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, adiposity was assessed using BIA rather than a criterion multicompartment model,24 and BIA prediction equations may be less accurate at adiposity extremes.17 Future validation against a four-compartment model in a subset of this cohort would strengthen data interpretation. Second, the open cohort design resulted in variable follow-up length across participants, and if attendance was systematically related to body composition or health status, residual selection bias cannot be excluded. Third, the cohort was derived from a single region in Taiwan, and ethnicity was not formally recorded; as Asian youth carry greater DXA-measured PBF than White youth at equivalent BMI,25 standard BMI cutoffs may underestimate adiposity in this cohort, and an Asian-specific PBF reference was unavailable. Fourth, potential clustering by school, measurement wave, and examiner team were not fully modelled, although a school fixed-effect sensitivity analysis confirmed negligible impact on all estimates (maximum Δβ = 0.4%); examiner identity was not recorded, though standardized training was applied throughout. Finally, pubertal status was not assessed, and residual confounding from developmental stage cannot be excluded.

Conclusion

The residual-based discrepancy analysis showed that the smallest median discrepancy was observed in children with normal weight, with greater discrepancies at both extremes of the BAZ distribution. Sensitivity analyses using restricted cubic spline models confirmed that this pattern was robust to model specification, though the magnitude of discrepancy at the underweight extreme was attenuated in nonlinear models, suggesting partial contributions from model misspecification in that subgroup. Age and sex independently predicted PBF after adjustment for BAZ, indicating that standardized BMI does not fully capture these sources of variation in body composition. BAZ remains a practical tool for population-level screening, but the selective use of BIA may improve adiposity estimation in children at the extremes of the weight distribution or in clinically ambiguous cases. It should be noted, however, that BIA prediction equations may themselves be less accurate at adiposity extremes, and findings should be interpreted accordingly.
