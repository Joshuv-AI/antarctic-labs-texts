Comparative Analysis of Gene Expression Analysis Methods for RNA in Situ Hybridization Images
Authors: Valeria Ariotta, Eros Azzalini, Vincenzo Canzonieri, Sampsa Hautaniemi, Serena Bonin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene expression analysis is pivotal in cancer research and clinical practice. Although traditional methods lack spatial context, RNA in situ hybridization (RNA-ISH) is a powerful technique that retains spatial tissue information. Here, RNAscope score, RT–droplet digital PCR, and automated QuantISH and QuPath were used for quantifying RNA-ISH expression values from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded samples. The methods were compared using high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma samples, focusing on CCNE1, WFDC2, and PPIB genes. The findings demonstrate good concordance between automated methods and RNAscope, with RT–droplet digital PCR showing less concordance. Additionally, QuantISH exhibits robust performance, even for low-expressed genes like CCNE1, showcasing its modular design and enhancing accessibility as a viable alternative for gene expression analysis.

Reliably estimating gene expression values is of paramount importance in cancer research and various clinical tasks, including diagnostic and prognostic tasks.1, 2, 3, 4 Over the past decades, the abundance of mRNA molecules has been detected using RT-PCR, microarrays, and sequencing.5, 6, 7 Although these technologies resulted in several advances,8, 9, 10, 11 they measure gene expression from bulk or single cells without considering the spatial context. In contrast, in situ hybridization (ISH) technologies, such as the RNAscope assay12, 13, 14, 15 and spatial transcriptomics,16,17 are capable of quantifying gene expression in single cells in their spatial context.18, 19, 20 The spatial-preserving transcriptomics technologies not only offer an enhanced alternative for conventional biomarker detection but also represent a substantial transformation in research disciplines, such as oncology and neuroscience.15,21, 22, 23

In the analysis of RNA-ISH data, diverse approaches exist, including semiquantitative assessment on the standard microscope, where human involvement remains essential for result interpretation, to automated image analysis that allows the streamlining of the analytic process, offering a standardized and efficient pathway to quantify and characterize RNA expression patterns. In particular, digital image analysis can be performed through dedicated software, such as QuPath,24 or fully automated pipelines, such as QuantISH,20 SMART-Q,25 and dotdotdot.26

Herein, the expression levels of the oncogene CCNE1, the diagnostic biomarker WFDC2, and the housekeeping gene (PPIB) were analyzed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples from patients with ovarian high-grade serous carcinoma (HGSC). HGSC is the most prevalent subtype of tubo-ovarian cancer and is known for its low 5-year survival rate of only 40%.27 CCNE1 amplification, occurring in 15% to 20% of HGSC cases,20,28,29 has been linked to survival in patients with HGSC.20,30,31 WFDC2 is a well-established ovarian cancer biomarker32, 33, 34 and is associated with survival in patients with HGSC.35 PPIB was selected as a control gene, and it is already used in several ovarian cancer studies by RNAscope assay and RT-PCR analysis.36,37 The expressions of these genes were assessed using RT–droplet digital PCR (RT-ddPCR) and on matched tissue microarray (TMA) slides by RNAscope assay, analyzing RNA signal both digitally (QuantISH and QuPath pipelines) and in a semiquantitative manner (RNAscope score).

We conduct a comparative analysis of the four methods used for gene expression assessment to investigate the reliability, concordance, and limitations of the approaches. The justification for selecting these four methods is as follows: RNAscope and RT-ddPCR represent widely recognized probe-based techniques commonly used in gene expression analysis; and QuantISH and the QuPath pipeline constitute, to our knowledge, the only two available open-source pipelines for RNA-ISH analysis.

Notably, this study presents a novel comparison, as these four methods have not been previously evaluated collectively in the literature, despite the existence of a few studies aimed at comparing RNA expression quantification methods. In particular, the research conducted by Morley-Bunker et al38 compared two commercial methods, SpotStudio (Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Inc., Newark, CA) and Leica Biosystems (Buffalo Grove, IL) Aperio ImageScope software, with two open-source methods, ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD; https://imagej.net/ij)39 and WEKA.40 Another study by Holzer et al41 compared three commercial software solutions, HALO (Indica Labs, Corrales, NM), CellMap (Flagship Biosciences, Westminster, CO), and Developer XD (Definiens, Cambridge, MA), with a freely available one, Aperio ImageScope software. Both articles demonstrated promising results in RNA expression quantification using software-based approaches, aligning with our findings.

Materials and Methods

FFPE Samples and TMAs

FFPE tissue blocks were obtained from the National Cancer Institute of Aviano (Pordenone, Italy). The samples were peritoneal metastases obtained from surgical debulking of women diagnosed with stage III and IV HGSC, already characterized and assessed for tumor purity in a previous study.42,43 Multiple sections (10 μm thick) were cut from each tissue block for subsequent RT-ddPCR experiments. The blocks were then used to construct five TMAs, as previously described.42,43 The tissue microarrays were used for the RNAscope hybridization assay.

RNAscope Assay and Analysis

The in situ detection of PPIB, CCNE1, and WFDC2 was performed using the manual RNAscope 2.5 HD Detection Kit-Brown from Advanced Cell Diagnostics, following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, slides (4 μm thick) were cut and dried at 60°C for 1 hour. Tissue sections were subsequently deparaffinized with xylene (2 × 5 minutes) and absolute ethanol (2 × 1 minute) at room temperature, followed by a 10-minute incubation with hydrogen peroxide. Epitope was retrieved by exposing the slides to RNAscope 1× Target Retrieval Reagent (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) for 15 minutes at 98°C. Tissues were then permeabilized using RNAscope protease plus (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) for 30 minutes at 40°C, followed by a 2-hour incubation with specific PPIB, CCNE1, and WFDC2 RNA probes (Advanced Cell Diagnostics) at 40°C. After signal amplification, colorimetric detection was performed using diaminobenzidine for 10 minutes at room temperature. Subsequently, slides were counterstained with hematoxylin and mounted with EcoMount solution (BioCare Medical, Pacheco, CA). The total expression level, represented by the number of estimated dots per cell in tumor tissue across the entire spot, was assessed using a semiquantitative scoring method based on the Advanced Cell Diagnostics scoring criteria (RNAscope score), as outlined in the RNAscope data analysis guideline (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). Briefly, the mRNA signal was graded as score 0 (no staining or <1 dot per 10 cancer cells), score 1 (1 to 3 dots per cancer cell), score 2 (4 to 9 dots per cancer cell, with none or few dot clusters), score 3 (10 to 15 dots per cancer cell and/or <10% of dots are in clusters), or score 4 (>15 dots per cancer cell and/or >10% of dots are in clusters). Representative images of RNAscope scoring for CCNE1, PPIB, and WFDC2 are reported in Supplemental Figure S1.

Imaging and Image Analysis

Tissue microarray slides were scanned at ×40 original magnification using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic 250 FLASH II digital slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) at the Genome Biology Unit supported by the Helsinki Institute of Life Science and the Faculty of Medicine, University of Helsinki (Helsinki, Finland), and Biocenter Finland. Subsequently, the scanned TMAs were subjected to automated RNA expression analysis using QuantISH and QuPath. For the analysis, tissue spots from patients who had received neoadjuvant chemotherapy and exhibited either no tumor cells or had missing/damaged cores have been excluded. The final data set comprised peritoneal metastatic tissues from 55 patients diagnosed with HGSC between 2007 and 2017. The FFPE material included slices cut from five tissue microarray blocks (n = 5 TMAs), each containing an average of two cores per patient (range, 1 to 3; each core measuring 1.2 mm), for a total of 359 spots.

QuantISH

The analysis with QuantISH adheres to the methods outlined in the article by Jamalzadeh et al.20 In summary, the process involves three main steps: preprocessing, cell segmentation and classification, and RNA signal quantification.

A few modifications were implemented in the cell segmentation and classification stages to adapt the analysis to the used data set. A deep learning approach named nucleAIzer44 was used for cell segmentation, representing a more advanced method than CellProfiler used in the original pipeline. This method was applied to each cleaned spot after preprocessing.

For cell classification, despite following the framework presented in the article by Jamalzadeh et al,20 it was necessary to retrain the supervised quadratic classifier used for cell classification. This retraining involved using new annotations from 580 cells, from which three features (area, mean intensity, and eccentricity) were extracted and used as input features for training purposes. The identified cell types include cancer, stroma, immune cells, and artifacts.

Ultimately, the RNA signal was determined as the average expression of cancer cells exclusively in each spot, with consideration given to the cell area as a weight. This approach enhances reliability in quantification by assigning greater significance to larger cells, which have a more pronounced impact on expression, while minimizing the influence of smaller cells and reducing the potential impact of artifacts left after classification. The expression values are represented in intensity units within the range of [0,1], where 0 signifies absence and 1 represents saturation of chromogenic signals.

QuPath

Various steps were executed using QuPath software version 0.4.3. Initially, the cores of each TMA were identified using the TMA dearrayer function. Subsequently, RNAscope images underwent analysis following the guidelines outlined in the manufacturer's technical note, QuPath analysis guideline (Advanced Cell Diagnostics). This involved adjusting stain vectors and subcellular detection parameters. To identify cells within the cores, annotations from 42 regions of interest, each measuring 200 × 200 pixels, were chosen to represent TMA spots. These annotations were then used as training images. A random tree classifier was trained to detect both cancer and noncancer cells. The trained classifier was subsequently applied to analyze all TMA slides. Following the classification into cancer and noncancer cells, RNA signals were identified in each core using the QuPath Subcellular Detection function. Subsequently, the quantification of the number of cancer cells, the estimated number of signal dots, and their mean intensity was extracted from the QuPath measurements table using Python version 2.7.8 and higher (https://www.phython.org) for further comparative analyses. The QuPath script used for the analysis is presented in the Supplemental Code S1.

RT–Droplet Digital PCR

Total RNA was extracted from a tissue section (10 μm thick) using the Maxwell RSC RNA FFPE kit on the dedicated instrument (Promega, Madison, WI), and 300 ng of the extracted RNA was reverse transcribed to cDNA, following previously described protocols.45 The expression levels of CCNE1, PPIB, and WFDC2 were subsequently determined using digital droplet PCR using the TaqMan assay.

The correct annealing temperature for each amplicon was established through a thermal gradient. The reaction was performed in a final volume of 20 μL, comprising 1× ddPCR Supermix for Probes (no dUTP; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA; catalog number 1863024), 17 ng cDNA, 18 pmol of each primer, and 5 pmol of the specific probe. A no-template sample served as a negative control in each run. The PCRs were partitioned into droplets using a QX200 droplet generator (Bio-Rad) and transferred to a 96-well PCR plate. End point PCR was conducted in a thermal cycler (iCycler; Bio-Rad) under the following conditions: enzyme activation at 95°C for 10 minutes; 40 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 seconds and annealing at 56°C for 1 minute at a ramp rate of 1°C/second; and enzyme inactivation at 98°C for 10 minutes, followed by signal stabilization at 4°C.

The fluorescence of each droplet was quantified using a QX200 droplet reader (Bio-Rad), and the absolute concentration of the target sequence was calculated as copies/μL. The primer sequences can be found in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.2.1 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and R version 4.3.0 (R Project for Statistical Computing; https://www.r-project.org). (R codes are provided in Supplemental Code S2.) For patients with multiple spots, the average expression was calculated. The distribution of the data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test to determine whether it was parametric or nonparametric. Differences between the two groups were compared using unpaired t-tests or U-tests for independent samples, Wilcoxon paired tests for matched pairs and Spearman method were used for the correlation between QuPath and QuantISH. Differences among three or more groups were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test for independent samples or the Friedman test for matched samples. For each method, gene expression levels were normalized concerning the housekeeping gene PPIB following the procedure described in gene expression data analysis guidelines of nSolver software version 4.0 (NanoString Technologies, Seattle, WA). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The expression levels of individual genes for each patient were evaluated using different methods: in situ visual assessment by RNAscope (Advanced Cell Diagnostics score), in situ digital assessment by QuantISH (intensity units) and QuPath pipelines (number of estimated signal dots), and RT-ddPCR analysis on bulk tissue (copies/μL). The results for each method are summarized in Table 2. On comparison, considering the average values, all methods produced a similar order of gene expression, with WFDC2 having the highest expression, CCNE1 having the lowest expression, and PPIB having in between expression (Friedman test; P < 0.0001). Gene pairwise comparisons returned significant P values in all methods (Wilcoxon paired test; P < 0.0001). Furthermore, the correlation of the cancer cell proportion between QuantISH and QuPath was also calculated (Supplemental Figure S2). For each gene, both methods achieved a significant correlation of between 64% and 78% (ρ = 2.2 × 10−12 for all genes). However, a few outliers are present in each graph, represented by poor-quality spots. These outliers include faded spots with no signals, which are typically closest to the origin of the axes in the graphs, and overexpressed spots, where the signal is dark and clustered, making it difficult to detect cell borders. Additionally, the WFDC2 correlation was lower compared with PPIB and CCNE1. This lower correlation can be attributed to the overall poorer quality of the TMAs used for WFDC2 analysis, which had more frequent issues with spot quality and clarity.

Accuracy of Gene Expression Assessment in Comparison to Visual Scoring

The concordance between the RNAscope visual scoring system and the respective expression values quantified by QuantISH, QuPath, and RT-ddPCR for CCNE1, PPIB, and WFDC2 genes was assessed (Figure 1). The RNAscope scoring system, known for its histologic assessment, served as a benchmark for the comparative analysis, providing a standardized basis for evaluating the performance of different methods in distinguishing between these distinct scoring categories. The frequencies of RNAscope scores for each gene are represented in Supplemental Figure S3.

Box plot representing the concordance between visual estimation by RNAscope score and gene expression level quantified by QuantISH (A), QuPath (B), and RT–droplet digital PCR (RT-ddPCR; C) for CCNE1 (red dots), PPIB (green dots), and WFDC2 (blue dots).

Overall, all methods were able to distinguish between any two scoring bins with high statistical significance (U-test; P < 0.0001). However, the mean difference between scores 0 and 1 was less pronounced for QuPath (P = 0.01) and RT-ddPCR (P = 0.02) than QuantISH. Additionally, for RT-ddPCR, the mean difference between scores 3 and 4 was less pronounced (P = 0.005).

Intermethod Correlations

There was a high and statistically significant correlation among all methods (Figure 2A). In particular, QuantISH and QuPath demonstrated a noteworthy alignment across all methods (mean ρ = 0.91 for QuantISH and 0.90 for QuPath), exhibiting a strong correlation with RNAscope visual assessment (ρ = 0.94 for QuantISH and 0.90 for QuPath). Conversely, RT-ddPCR showed slightly lower but still notably high correlation coefficients among the different methods (mean ρ = 0.85).

On examining individual genes more closely, varying correlations among methods (Figure 2B) were observed. The mean intermethod correlations for CCNE1, PPIB, and WFDC2 were ρ = 0.67, 0.82, and 0.76, respectively. The CCNE1 gene, which has low expression levels, showed the lowest correlation values compared with PPIB and WFDC2, which have moderate and high expression levels, respectively. QuantISH maintained high correlation values with the RNAscope score for all genes analyzed (mean ρ = 0.88; range, 0.82 to 0.91), whereas QuPath and RT-ddPCR exhibited gene-specific variations [mean ρ = 0.80 (range, 0.60 to 0.90) and mean ρ = 0.67 (range, 0.62 to 0.71), respectively]. QuPath exhibited lower concordance for the CCNE1 gene, whereas RT-ddPCR exhibited lower concordance for WFDC2. Notably, RT-ddPCR consistently yielded lower correlation values than the other methods.

The housekeeping gene PPIB exhibited higher correlation values across all the methods (Figure 2B). Particularly striking was the pronounced drop-off in the PPIB expression levels observed in RT-ddPCR (r = –0.69) compared with the in situ hybridization methods (mean ρ = −0.44; range, −0.37 to −0.50), suggesting that the gene expression values based on RT-ddPCR are more sensitive to sample age due to nucleic acid degradation.

Intermethod Correlations according to Gene Expression Level

The variation in the level of agreement between QuPath, QuantISH, and RT-ddPCR was then analyzed within each RNAscope score as determined by visual assessment (Figure 3). Considering all genes, QuPath and QuantISH showed a high degree of agreement in the detection of cases without significant signal (n = 10, score 0; ρ = 0.84), whereas an increase in correlation values was observed when moving from low to high expression levels, as follows: score 1 (n = 22, ρ = 0.44), score 2 (n = 62, ρ = 0.57), score 3 (n = 44, ρ = 0.72), and 4 (n = 27, ρ = 0.71). On the contrary, RT-ddPCR showed higher concordance with both QuantISH and QuPath for scores 2 to 3 (medium level of expression), whereas for scores 0 to 1 (low expression) and score 4 (high expression), the correlation values were low.

Concordance Between RNAscope Scoring Detected by Visual Estimation and Qupath Analysis

To evaluate the concordance between the RNAscope visual scoring system and QuPath directly, the RNAscope scoring groups were generated on the basis of the estimated number of dots in QuPath. For all genes, the Bland-Altman analysis resulted in a bias of −0.036 with a 95% limit of agreement ranging from −1.37 to 1.30 (Figure 4A). The pairwise comparison of visual and digital scoring showed 41% discordant cases (Figure 4B); the highest discordance rates were detected for score 1 versus 2 (40%), followed by scores 3 versus 4 (33%), 2 versus 3 (12%), and 0 versus 1 (10%). Notably, the discordant cases were mainly represented in QuPath software by the estimated dots between 0 and 6 and, to a lesser extent, by the estimated dots >15 (Figure 4C). No significant difference in the proportion of concordant and discordant samples was observed when looking at individual genes (χ2 test; P = 0.1) (Figure 4D). However, CCNE1 had the highest discordance rate (51%), followed by WFDC2 (40%) and PPIB (31%).

Intermethod Correlations: Normalized Data

The expression levels of CCNE1 and WFDC2 were normalized against those of PPIB to account for sample degradation. Subsequently, normalized data were used to conduct a Spearman correlation analysis to retest intermethod agreement. Overall, all methods displayed a good level of correlation (Figure 5A). QuantISH and QuPath exhibited the highest intermethod agreement (mean ρ = 0.85 and 0.84, respectively), followed by RT-ddPCR and RNAscope score (mean ρ = 0.81 and 0.78, respectively). Both in situ automated methods and RT-ddPCR showed a good to medium level of agreement with the RNAscope score (ρ = 0.84 for QuantISH, ρ = 0.76 for QuPath, and ρ = 0.74 for RT-ddPCR).

Both CCNE1 and WFDC2 displayed a medium level of concordance between methods [mean ρ = 0.50 (CCNE1) and 0.69 (WFDC2)] (Figure 5B). However, QuantISH showed a higher correlation with the RNAscope score for the low-expressed gene CCNE1 (ρ = 0.51) compared with QuPath and RT-ddPCR (ρ = 0.36 and 0.39, respectively). At high expression levels of WFDC2, both in situ digital methods performed significantly better than RT-ddPCR (ρ = 0.77 for QuantISH and 0.70 for QuPath, compared with 0.46 for RT-ddPCR).

Discussion

A comparative analysis of four methods for gene expression assessment was conducted. Three methods involved quantifying RNA expression in TMA slides using visual assessment (RNAscope score) and automated image analysis (QuantISH and QuPath pipelines). The fourth method involved absolute target quantification using RT-ddPCR on matched bulk tissue. Specifically, this study aimed to highlight the practical utility of fully automated digital image analysis, demonstrating its potential as a viable alternative to the traditional RNAscope score approach and evaluating its concordance with a gold standard, such as RT-ddPCR.

All methods used in this study were suitable for analyzing CCNE1, PPIB, and WFDC2 gene expression. QuantISH and QuPath showed a good and remarkably high correlation with the RNAscope visual score. However, some differences were observed when examining specific genes. Both methods demonstrated good agreement with the RNAscope score in detecting targets with medium to high expression levels, such as PPIB and WFDC2. However, for weakly expressed genes, like CCNE1, QuantISH outperformed QuPath significantly. This variation may be attributed to differences in signal detection: signal intensity per cell area in QuantISH versus the number of estimated dots in QuPath. In particular, QuPath detects the number of transcripts in each cell but does not consider the intensity and size of each dot, which reflects the number of Z-probes bound to the target molecule. This can be problematic in degraded samples with few dots and low signal intensity. Accordingly, the results indicate that the correlation between QuantISH and QuPath improved as the expression levels increased (from score 1 to 4). Additionally, the direct comparison between QuPath-derived scores and visually estimated RNAscope scores revealed only moderate agreement, highlighting discrepancies in the detection of targets with very low (0 to 6) and very high (>15) numbers of dots. The difficulty for pathologists to accurately estimate the number of dots in the context of tissue heterogeneity and signal variation may contribute to this issue. Accordingly, QuPath may overestimate the number of RNA dots within formed clusters, particularly when dealing with highly expressed targets.

Another crucial point that emerged from the study is the difference between in situ hybridization and RT-ddPCR in terms of gene expression results. In agreement with Tran et al,46 both automated and semiquantitative data obtained from RNAscope showed a significant correlation with RT-ddPCR. However, RT-ddPCR was performed on bulk tissue without discrimination between stromal and cancer cells in the final quantification. Furthermore, a deeper analysis revealed that the correlation values varied depending on the level of expression and the type of gene analyzed. There was good concordance between RT-ddPCR and in situ methods for targets with medium expression levels, such as PPIB. Nevertheless, correlations were poorer for low and high expression levels (CCNE1 and WFDC2) even after normalization. The underestimation of the number of copies in some WFDC2 samples at extremely high expression may be due to the intrinsic dynamic range of ddPCR, which results from more than one cDNA template in each droplet. As for CCNE1, the poor correlation may be attributed to a combination of sample degradation, low target expression, and the Monte Carlo effect, limiting the quantification of low-amount transcripts. Sample age and the consequent nucleic acid degradation are common issues in gene expression analysis from FFPE tissues and have been reported for many methods, including RNAscope and RT-ddPCR. It is important to consider these factors when interpreting results.22,47,48 In this sense, the cohort is suitable for comparing the accuracy of different methods because it includes FFPE tissue blocks with different ages and varying levels of nucleic acid degradation. As shown by the results on the housekeeping gene PPIB, RT-ddPCR analysis appears to be more susceptible to age block than in situ hybridization methods. This is because additional factors other than spontaneous nucleic acid degradation due to the fixation procedure can exacerbate target depletion. These factors include RNA extraction, reverse transcription efficiency, and the presence of PCR inhibitors.49 Overall, these results suggest that the RNAscope assay should be preferred over RT-ddPCR for accurate gene expression analysis and that orthogonal validation of RNA biomarkers requires careful selection of samples with good nucleic acid quality, especially when dealing with low-expressed targets.

Digital image analysis can be a valid method to quantify the RNAscope signal beyond the semiquantitative evaluation by visual assessment. Manual scoring is time-consuming and requires at least two pathologists to validate the results12; moreover, tissue heterogeneity and variability of the RNA signal may lead to inaccurate estimates. In this study, even after accounting for samples' degradation, both the QuantISH and QuPath approaches showed good overall agreement with the RNAscope score. In particular, QuantISH showed robust correlation results across different genes and performed well with CCNE1 despite its relatively low and subtle gene expression. QuantISH also proved to be a more flexible and modular approach than QuPath. Although both analyses required computational resources, the modular nature and adaptability of QuantISH made it more accessible and less prone to error, unlike QuPath, which was resource constrained and lacked flexibility, resulting in multiple crashes. The RNA hybridization signal was analyzed on the QuPath software following the guidelines suggested by the RNAscope manufacturers; however, other pipelines can be used on the same program to achieve the same goal.

Although this investigation has demonstrated the comparability of these approaches, it is important to note some limitations. First, the quality of some spots in the TMAs was suboptimal, displaying variations in spot thickness, folded tissue, and degradation. However, both QuantISH and QuPath performed well despite these challenges. Second, the current analysis did not account for the distribution of RNA expression across different areas within the spots, and these factors are acknowledged as limitations. Last, although only cases with high tumor purity were included in the study, stromal tissue may account for a small proportion of the cDNA copies detected by RT-ddPCR on bulk tissue. Moreover, the selected TMA spots may not recapitulate the transcriptional heterogeneity in both cancer and stromal cells on bulk tissue samples.

In summary, the comparative assessment of various methods for quantifying gene expression in RNA-ISH TMAs has underlined the utility of fully digital techniques. These findings demonstrate that the automated approaches can be an alternative approach for gene expression analysis, particularly highlighting QuantISH as the preferred option because of its modular and flexible software design. The potential clinical implications of adopting fully automated digital methods are promising, offering cost-effective, robust, and accessible tools. Notably, the demonstrated reliability of these automated approaches, even under suboptimal TMA conditions, suggests their prospective application in disease stratification, prognosis prediction, and tailoring personalized treatment strategies across various malignancies.
