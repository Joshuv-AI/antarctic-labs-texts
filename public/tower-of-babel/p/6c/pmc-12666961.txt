Introduction

The number of patients requiring cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) and anticoagulation therapy has been increasing annually owing to the aging population and the growing complexity of antithrombotic therapy for cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. In the short term, after pacemaker implantation, infection and hematoma in the device pocket are common causes of pocket masses.1 Chronic expanding hematoma (CEH) occurring after permanent pacemaker implantation is very rare.2,3 The optimal treatment strategy for CEH in the pacemaker pocket has not been well clarified. We reported a rare case of pacemaker pocket CEH that was successfully treated with complete surgical removal and insertion of a closed drainage tube.

Case report

A 74-year-old man with swelling of the pacemaker pocket was admitted to our hospital. He had undergone pacemaker implantation for sick sinus syndrome with heart failure 10 years before presentation. The patient underwent percutaneous coronary intervention and radiofrequency catheter ablation twice to treat the paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (AF) and prevent AF recurrence. Despite this, AF recurred. Moreover, coronary artery disease with asymptomatic ischemia, as revealed by myocardial perfusion scintigraphy, persisted, and the patient was prescribed amiodarone, aspirin, and warfarin for treatment. The patient had no history of overt trauma. On admission, the swollen pocket was soft and nontender with no signs of infection. Laboratory inflammatory marker levels were within the normal limits (Figure 1). Ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) revealed a mass beneath the pacemaker in the pocket (Figures 1B, 1C, and 2A). The CT performed 7 years ago, which was conducted before catheter ablation, showed swelling at the pacemaker pocket. Metal artifact–reduced CT showed a well-defined mass with heterogeneous density and areas of higher attenuation than the muscle, suggesting that it might be a mix of a hematoma and recent hemorrhagic activity. CT scans obtained over time for other clinical reasons were available, and the time course revealed a slow progression of the mass (Figure 3). Given the pocket swelling and generator battery depletion, we planned the surgical removal of the mass and generator exchange in collaboration with the plastic surgery team. After preoperative discussion with plastic surgeons, we aimed for en bloc resection and techniques to minimize dead space. Baseline 12-lead electrocardiography revealed a sinus rhythm, and a pacemaker check showed 0% AF burden for 6 months. Aspirin was stopped 1 week before the procedure, warfarin was stopped 3 days before the procedure, and the prothrombin time-to-international normalized ratio on the day before surgery was 1.26. Preoperative imaging and long-term clinical history did not indicate the presence of malignant lesions. However, we planned to perform intraoperative frozen-section pathology if unexpected findings were observed. Intraoperatively, the gross appearance of the mass was consistent with that of a hematoma, and no features suggestive of malignancy were observed; thus, frozen sections were not required. The mass in the pacemaker pocket was entirely removed, the generator was exchanged, and the lead parameters remained unchanged. Finally, the closed drainage tube (J-VAC Closed Wound Drainage System, 10F, Ethicon, Inc) was inserted beneath the new generator to reduce hematoma formation. Postoperatively, we found that the excised mass was encapsulated and contained an organized hematoma (Figure 4A). Histology revealed no malignancy or infection, but showed features of partial organization, recanalization, granulation tissue, and hemosiderin deposition, consistent with CEH (Figure 4B, 4C, 4D, and 4E). The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and intravenous antibiotics were administered for 1 week. On the first postoperative day, approximately 60 mL of blood was drawn through a drainage tube. Because there was no bleeding or hematoma progression, the drainage tube was removed 3 days after the procedure. The patient was discharged postoperatively on the eighth day. Aspirin was resumed 1 month later. Four months after discharge, AF was detected via remote monitoring of the pacemaker, and warfarin was readministered. Although switching from warfarin to a direct oral anticoagulant was considered, warfarin was selected because of its low cost. Discontinuation of aspirin was also discussed, but was resumed because of residual ischemia and the patient’s strong preference. Twelve months after discharge, the mass in the pacemaker pocket had not recurred; however, further follow-up is required.

This case highlights that CEH is a rare but important cause of device pocket masses. The slow progressive time course of CEH in the pacemaker pocket over 10 years is unique. CEH should be considered a possible cause of the mass in the pacemaker pocket. Effective management of CEH at the pacemaker pocket may require close collaboration between cardiology and plastic surgery, and postoperative insertion of a drainage tube for a few days may be a good therapeutic option for CEH.

Discussion

We report a rare case of CEH in the pacemaker pocket, successfully treated through complete excision of the mass and placement of a drainage tube, achieved via a collaborative approach between cardiologists and plastic surgeons.

First, Reid et al4 reported a hematoma growing over a month, referred to as CEH. To date, only 2 cases of CEH in device pockets have been reported.2,3 CEH has been reported to be associated with various implanted devices, including breast implants and orthopedic prostheses.5,6 These reports suggest that chronic irritation or minor repeated trauma may contribute to its development. CEH is a rare disease, and the mechanism of CEH is not well understood.7 Pathologically, it consists of a dense fibrous capsule containing fresh and organized blood components.8 The pathogenesis of CEH is thought to involve repeated episodes of microbleeding within a preexisting hematoma, accompanied by low-grade inflammation and progressive fibrous encapsulation that impairs resorption. This leads to a gradual increase over time. CEH is a slowly progressive mass that can persist for more than a month and, in some cases, even more than 10 years. Slow-growing soft tissue masses in the pacemaker pocket can be difficult to diagnose in older patients, especially those on long-term anticoagulation therapy, owing to challenges with magnetic resonance imaging and CT imaging. Differential diagnoses include CEH, malignancy (soft tissue sarcoma), and chronic abscesses. Although not performed in this case, owing to limitations of the health insurance system and the patient’s renal failure, imaging modalities such as metal artifact reduction mode contrast-enhanced CT and positron emission tomography–CT may be useful in differentiating CEH from other entities and could be considered in future cases.7 Recognition of CEH is critical because, unlike malignant lesions, it is benign and can mimic aggressive disease both clinically and radiologically. Our case underscores the need for clinicians to consider CEH in the differential diagnosis of enlarging pacemaker pocket masses, particularly in patients with prolonged anticoagulant use, to guide appropriate management and avoid unnecessary and extensive interventions.

In general, the ideal treatment for CEH is complete removal of the hematoma, including the capsule, to reduce the risk of recurrence.9 Based on preoperative meetings with the plastic surgery team, we planned the procedure using 2 key strategies to minimize the risk of hematoma recurrence: aiming for complete en bloc excision of the mass and implementing meticulous techniques to eliminate any residual dead space. Complete excision is often challenging owing to the proximity of the leads. In the present case, collaboration with plastic surgeons enabled safe and complete removal without lead damage. Another critical factor is bleeding risk management. In addition, multiple studies have demonstrated that pocket hematomas are strongly associated with an increased risk of subsequent device-related infections.10 In patients with CIEDs, remote monitoring enables close observation of the arrhythmia burden. In our patient, warfarin was safely withheld for 4 months owing to zero AF burden and was later restarted when AF recurred. Previous studies have shown that closed drainage tubes reduce the risk of hematoma after CIED implantation in patients on antithrombotic therapy without increasing the risk.11 Collectively, these data support that proactive prevention of hematoma formation—and when appropriate, drainage combined with antibiotic coverage—was justified to reduce both recurrence and infection risks in our patient. In our case, temporary drainage combined with intravenous antibiotics may have contributed to a favorable outcome. This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary collaboration and postoperative drainage in managing CEH of pacemaker pockets.

Limitations

This study was limited by its relatively short follow-up period of 12 months. Given the chronic and recurrent nature of CEH, long-term observation is necessary to fully evaluate the risk of recurrence, especially in the context of delayed resumption of anticoagulant therapy. Careful follow-up is warranted to monitor late complications and recurrence.

Conclusion

Here, we reported a rare but clinically significant case of CEH of the pacemaker pocket, which should be considered in the differential diagnosis of gradually enlarging device pocket masses.
