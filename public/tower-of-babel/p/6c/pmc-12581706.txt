Investigating the impacts of future oil prices on the achievement of 1.5°C climate goal
Authors: Hao Chen, Kai Gong, Renhao Wang, Cen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryReducing greenhouse gas emissions from global oil sector is essential for meeting the 1.5°C goal. Existing studies largely adopt top-down approaches, overlooking heterogeneity in oilfield carbon intensity and economic feasibility. We integrate an economic production and life cycle emission model of 4,382 oilfields to assess how future oil prices affect alignment with the climate goals. The results show that the average break-even price was 22.9 $/bbl in 2019, well below the projected future oil prices, indicating substantial profitability. The oil sector is expected to emit 386.7 Gt CO2 cumulatively by 2050, exceeding the 1.5°C goal carbon budget (50% chance) by 6.2%. If only oil sector emissions are considered, achieving the 1.5°C goal (50% chance) would require average oil price to remain below 57.4 $/bbl. These findings highlight the urgent need for coordinated strategies to reconcile the oil sector’s future production with the imperative climate goals.Subject areas: Earth sciences, Energy resources, Energy policy, Economics

Introduction

The global oil sector, which is at the forefront of energy transition and net-zero pathways, is the second-largest greenhouse gas (GHG) emitter.1 The critical role of the oil sector calls for greater attention to analyze its future trends in production and GHG emissions,2,3 particularly in light of the consensus reached at the 28th annual United Nations Climate Meeting (COP28) on the transition away from fossil fuels. The economic recovery after COVID-19, strategic behavior of the Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries, and geopolitical events have kept world crude oil prices fluctuating at high levels, providing ample economic incentives for future growth in oil production and GHG emissions within the industry. Following the urgent 1.5°C limit, predicting and controlling oil-related GHG emissions under potentially high future prices is critical for keeping the Paris Agreement target on track.

The relationship between oil-related GHG emissions and the attainment of climate goals has gained extensive academic interest. Previous studies show a significant conflict between current oil reserves and climate goals. The potential GHG emissions associated with existing oil reserves are an obvious obstacle to achieving the 1.5°C climate goal.4,5,6,7,8 Current extraction rates by oil producers have also not aligned with the necessary climate goal trajectory.9,10,11,12 Hence, stringent limitations must be imposed on future oil extraction to achieve the climate goals outlined in the Paris Agreement.13 Under the 2°C goal (50% chance), at least one-third of oil reserves must remain untapped before 2050.14 To achieve the 1.5°C goal (50% chance), 58% of oil reserves should be kept underground by 2050,15 and oil production needs to decrease by at least 62% compared with the levels of 2020.16

For oil investors, the expectation of future oil prices is a key factor influencing their production behavior.17 If future oil prices remain high, they are more prone to produce oil, which would lead to a contradiction between oil sector emissions and remaining carbon budgets (RCBs) for climate goal. Most prevalent studies adopt integrated assessment models or energy system models to analyze the impact of oil prices on oil production and GHG emissions from a top-down perspective.18,19,20,21 They assume that oil demand is affected by economy, population, etc., and then use the marginal cost curve of oil to clear the market. However, these approaches offer limited insight into the micro heterogeneity of different oilfields, particularly in terms of economic feasibility and carbon intensity (CI). Meanwhile, oil investors are more sensitive to oil prices than to oil demand, and their decisions are often based on an assessment of the net present value (NPV) of projects at future oil prices.17,22 Lastly, some previous supply-side analyses of oil production only juxtapose oil prices with costs23,24,25 but often neglect the impact of national oil fiscal regimes on oilfield profitability. This exclusion can distort the estimation of oil production and its associated impact on climate goals.

Here, we analyze how future oil prices will affect the 1.5°C climate goal based on 4,382 existing global oilfields (accounting for 98.3% of global oil production in 2019).26 First, we calculate the breakeven prices (BEPs) of global oilfields to analyze their economic feasibility by considering national oil fiscal regimes. Then, we build a dynamic oilfield NPV model to simulate global oil production from 2020 through 2050 by screening positive NPV projects under forecasted future oil prices. Second, we employ a life cycle CI estimation model of oilfields to analyze GHG emissions from wells to wheels for global oilfields, which is an integration of the Oil Production Greenhouse Gas Emissions Estimator (OPGEE 3.0), Petroleum Refinery Life Cycle Inventory Model (PRELIM 1.6), and Oil Products Emissions Module (OPEM 1.1). Third, we integrate the simulated oil production and estimated life cycle CI of all oilfields to forecast the oil-related CO2 emissions and assess whether they are compatible with the climate goal. Our results show that, under future oil price pathways, existing oilfields retain strong economic incentives to continue production. Consequently, cumulative CO2 emissions from the global oil sector alone could severely undermine the 1.5°C and 2°C climate goals. This suggests that merely halting future oil exploration and development is insufficient to align with the Paris Agreement. A timely and coordinated phase-out of existing oilfields is therefore critical. This study quantifies the climate implications of continued oil production under various oil price scenarios and evaluates the extent to which existing oilfields are compatible with climate goals.

Results

Global oilfield breakeven price

Considering national oil fiscal regimes, we compute the BEPs for global oilfields in 2019 to represent their economic feasibility,27,28 as shown in Figure 1. There are three major national oil fiscal regimes for oil investors in conducting the oil investment business29,30 (see Figure S3), including concession, production sharing contract (PSC), and service contract (SC). Concession and PSC are the most prevalent globally, accounting for 74% and 20% of total global oil production, respectively (see Figure S4); SC occurs in Ecuador and Iran. In 2019, the production-weighted average BEP for global oilfields is 22.9 $/bbl (ranging from 0.6 $ to 149.0 $/bbl), which is far below contemporaneous oil price. Regionally, oilfields with lower BEPs are predominantly clustered in resource-rich areas such as the Middle East, Russia, and the United States. Conversely, fields with higher BEPs are commonly found in China and Southeast Asia. The significant differences in the BEPs between countries imply considerable disparities in the economic pressure for the global oil sector’s transition to mitigate climate change. Countries with lower BEPs often possess stronger economic incentives to delay the transition process, and their higher oil reserves exacerbate this impact.

The BEPs of oilfields are intricately linked to their geographical locations, oil fiscal regimes, and oil classifications (see Figure 2). Geographically, the distribution of BEPs in onshore oilfields spans a wide range yet encompasses nearly the entire range of low BEPs. Shallow offshore fields have demonstrated high economic viability, with BEPs under 15 $/bbl. In contrast, deep-water and ultra-deep-water fields generally show higher BEP spectra. From the perspective of fiscal regimes, fields with lower BEPs typically operate under concessions, whereas those with higher BEPs mainly fall under PSC. Regarding oil types, medium oil (60.8% of production) forms the backbone of the global supply, predominantly populating the lower BEP range. Recognizing the BEP differences among various oilfield types can provide targeted and detailed guidance for cost-effective strategies for global oil transition.

By examining the differences in height between the triangular scatter points and bar chart, we can calculate the underestimation ratio of BEPs when the oil fiscal regime is not considered. Overall, the average underestimation ratio of global oilfields is 32.9%. Regarding oil fiscal regimes, fields have the lowest average underestimation ratio (18.9%) under concession, while fields have the highest at 137.5% under SC. Notably, onshore and medium oilfields, which are key contributors to global production, also show an average underestimation ratio exceeding 33%. Moreover, the economic ranking of oil production feasibility across countries may also be reversed when oil fiscal regimes are taken into account. For instance, Oman oilfields (BEP = 18.9 $/bbl) appear more economically viable than Colombia oilfields (BEP = 27.2 $/bbl) when national oil fiscal regimes are not considered. However, once fiscal regimes are incorporated specifically, a PSC in Oman and a concession in Colombia, the economic ranking is reversed, with Oman’s BEP rising to 42.5 $/bbl and Colombia’s to 32.6 $/bbl. Consequently, a mere comparison of oil prices and field production costs is inadequate for a precise assessment of the economic viability of oilfield production,23,31 which can lead to biases in forecasts of future oil production in the sector.

Global oil production under different future oil price pathways

The forecast of future oil prices is crucial to the profitability of oilfield projects, which in turn influences the production decisions of oil investors. To enhance the credibility, representativeness, and diversity of future oil price forecasting, we gather 111 projected oil price pathways for the period from 2022 through 2050 from various sources (detailed in Table S1), as shown in Figure 3. Most long-term oil price projections suggest a stabilization within the 50 $–80 $/bbl range, following an initial period of decline. The average oil price is projected to be around 75 $/bbl by 2030 and 81 $/bbl by 2050. 91 paths (over 80%) of the forecasted pathways have an average price above 60 $/bbl during the period, providing substantial economic incentives for oil investors to persist in production when compared with BEPs. This will challenge the deep decarbonization goals outlined in the Paris Agreement for energy systems.

An economic production model was developed to simulate the production of oilfields by calculating the NPVs under forecasted oil prices (details are presented in the STAR Methods). Crucial factors, such as production costs and carbon tax, exhibit potentially varying trends over time, leading to a certain level of uncertainty regarding the future economic feasibility of oilfields. Therefore, a Monte Carlo simulation approach was employed to conduct 10,000 pathway predictions of the annual global oil production from 2020 through 2050 (details are presented in the STAR Methods). As shown in Figure 4, all the simulated global oil production paths show an inverted U-shape that peaks around 2029 and then declines. The projected global annual oil production for 2050 ranges between 0 and 38.5 million barrels per day (MMbpd), with a median value of 31.2 MMbpd. These simulations highlight the substantial impact of oil prices on production behavior, and some low-price pathways lead to the cessation of global annual oil production as early as 2035.

Global oilfield life cycle CI

The life cycle CI of oilfields from the well to the wheel was estimated using an integration of existing open-source models (detailed in the STAR Methods), as depicted in Figure 5. The life cycle CI of global oilfields reveals significant heterogeneity across different regions. The production-weighted average life cycle CI of oilfields was 528.8 kg CO2e/bbl in 2019, ranging from 438.8 to 1,269.0 kg CO2e/bbl. Geographically, European oilfields typically exhibit lower life cycle CI, whereas Central and west Asian regions host many high-emission fields. Therefore, future emission reduction efforts in the oil sector should prioritize large-scale oilfields with higher life cycle CI, such as Canada’s oil sand fields, to achieve greater efficiency in reducing emissions.

Cumulative CO2 emissions from the oil sector and their climatic impacts

The future GHG emissions of existing global oilfields can be projected by integrating simulated oil production and the estimated life cycle CI (see Figures S16 and S19). To ensure consistency with the definition of RCBs, we need to convert the GHG emissions into CO2 emissions (details are presented in the STAR Methods). Figure 7 displays the distribution of the projected cumulative CO2 emissions of the oil sector and its components for key years from 2020 through 2050. During this period, global oil fields maintain sufficient economic incentives for continued production under most oil price pathways, which leads to increased cumulative oil output. Therefore, the cumulative life cycle CO2 emissions of the oil sector showed a significant rising trend, with the median values increasing from 173.7 Gt CO2 in 2030 to 386.7 Gt CO2 in 2050. To assess the influence of oil CO2 emissions on the feasibility of meeting Paris Agreement, we compared the estimated cumulative oil-related CO2 emissions with the 1.5°C and 2°C goal RCBs39 (calculated from the beginning of 2020, see details in STAR Methods). The RCB represents the remaining total CO2 emissions allowable while limiting global warming to a certain climate goal. Our results reveal that, by 2050, the median cumulative CO2 emissions from the oil sector are projected to exceed 6.2% of the RCB (364 Gt CO2) for the 1.5°C goal (50% chance) and consume 50.1% of the RCB (772 Gt CO2) for the 2°C goal (83% chance). Moreover, over 67.8% of the simulated oil production paths have cumulative emissions exceeding the RCB of the 1.5°C goal (50% chance) by 2050, indicating that the oil sector alone is enough to jeopardize the 1.5°C climate goal under the projected future oil prices. Therefore, merely halting the exploration of new oilfields will not be sufficient to meet the goals of the Paris Agreement, and strategies for phasing out existing oilfields are essential.

We also explored the relationship between the average oil prices from 2020 through 2050 and the probability of achieving the 1.5°C goal from the perspective of the oil sector; see Figure 8. Due to data limitations and the complex substitution effects between sectors, it is difficult to include other sectors in a bottom-up modeling framework. Therefore, we focus exclusively on the oil sector CO2 emissions in this study. The cumulative CO2 emissions increased nonlinearly with the average oil price, but the growth rate slowed when the average oil price was above 70 $/bbl. This is because most existing oilfields can be profitable in production at an average oil price above 70 $/bbl, despite multiple cost uncertainties. As expected, rising average oil prices render production from high-cost oil fields economically viable, increasing total oil output and consequently reducing the likelihood of achieving the 1.5°C goal. For instance, an increase in the average oil price from 40 $ to 60 $/bbl results in an additional 116.8 Gt CO2 from the oil sector, reducing the probability of meeting the 1.5°C goal from 62% to 52%. Considering the CO2 emissions from the oil sector only, the average oil price from 2020 through 2050 should remain below 57.4 $/bbl to maintain global warming within 1.5°C with a 50% chance. If all sectors are taken into account, achieving the 1.5°C goal becomes infeasible, and achieving the 2°C goal (83% chance) would require the average oil price to remain below 67.2 $/bbl. However, given the recent trend of high oil prices, exacerbated by geopolitical events, the oil sector is challenged to reconcile its future production with its imperative climate goals.

Discussion

Evaluating future GHG emissions from the global oil sector is crucial for adhering to climate goals. Diverging from previous macro-level demand-side analyses, this study utilizes a field-level production and emission accounting model to analyze the impact of oil price on oil sector’s alignment with the 1.5°C goal. The bottom-up approach and findings presented in this study can complement long-term climate and integrated assessment models by better capturing the heterogeneity of global oilfields. Our findings indicate that future elevated oil prices will ensure sufficient profits for the majority of oilfields, while rendering the 1.5°C goal nearly unattainable even when only considering emissions from the global oil sector. Consequently, to maintain alignment between the global oil sector CO2 emissions and 1.5°C RCB, the focus should not only be on reducing the development of new oilfields but also on gradually instituting a phase-out transition strategy for existing oilfields.

However, the oil sector faces multidimensional challenges during future transitions. Economies that are heavily reliant on oil revenue face stark pressure from economic development, such as Libya, where oil revenue accounts for more than half of its gross domestic product.40 Meanwhile, the oil sector, responsible for employing approximately 7.6 million people worldwide by 2023,38 presents a critical quandary in addressing potential unemployment issues arising from the transition. The inherent economic entrenchment in the oil sector could result in mass-stranded assets owing to substantial future production reductions and vast existing related infrastructure.41 Considering the significant position of the oil sector within the national economic supply chain, such economic losses and stranded assets could potentially be amplified.42 Moreover, geopolitical tensions and an increase in extreme weather events significantly disrupt future oil markets, adding to the uncertainty surrounding the oil sector’s future transition. Under the negative influence of constrained economic development and stranded assets, oil-exporting countries face difficulties in providing support for advancing the oil sector transition, particularly given the public-good nature of climate change and the potential problem of free-riding. The COP28 effectively illustrated the challenges faced by the oil sector, where intense debates over the gradual phase-out of fossil fuels have failed to reach a consensus. Therefore, an orderly, equitable, and fair emission-reduction strategy that balances the interests of all stakeholders is necessary for the oil sector.43

Limitations of the study

Although our study yields critical insights, it has several limitations. First, our bottom-up analysis of the impact of future oil prices on global oil sector CO2 emissions was conducted from the perspective of a single sector. Future studies could consider the effects from other sectors to provide a more comprehensive assessment, if detailed data from other sectors are available. Second, our model treats each oilfield as an independent decision maker in simulating future oil production, neglecting the impacts of market structure and strategic behavior among different producers. Future studies could incorporate these market characteristics into the model to better reflect real-world production behaviors. Addressing these limitations will enhance overall understanding of the role of the oil sector in achieving climate goals.

Method details

Data source and processing

Oilfield data

The oilfield data used in this study were sourced from Wood Mackenzie.44 This database encompasses a wide range of information, including production, cost, geographical location, and reservoir characteristics. There are three types of oilfields in the database, which consist of parent oilfields, child oilfields, and standalone oilfields. Parent oilfields are first developed in a resource area, providing infrastructure and knowledge for nearby child oilfields (child oilfields can be considered finer subdivisions of parent oilfields). The standalone oilfield is an independent oil reservoir, developed and managed separately, and equipped with unique infrastructure and logistics.

To simulate the impacts of future global oil production from 2020 through 2050 on the achievement of climate goals, it is essential to consider not only the oilfields that are already in production in 2019 (latest data we can obtain), but also those that have been explored and will produce in the future study period (see Figure S1). Following the classification method for oilfields outlined by Masnadi et al.,31 we initially filter out 4382 oilfields that were expected to produce oil between 2019 and 2050. Among these fields, there are 1,464 parent oilfields (aggregated from 6,657 child oilfields) and 2,918 standalone oilfields. The total oil production in these fields accounted for 98.3% of the global production in 2019.26 They also exhibit good representativeness at the national level, with an average coverage of 93.3% (see Figure S2).

Future oil price pathways

We collected data on 111 oil price pathways from various sources, including government agencies, international organizations, oil companies, consulting firms, banks, and academic papers (see Table S1). Categorized by source, there were 47 from government agencies, 5 from international organizations, 8 from oil companies, 29 from consulting firms, 7 from banks, and 15 from academic papers. All oil price pathways were adjusted to the 2020 real price.47 Among all oil price forecasts, 76 price pathways have an ending year by 2050, whereas the remaining pathways have ending years no earlier than 2028. To standardize all pathways to the same time horizon from 2020 through 2050, we calculated the average annual oil price growth rate (0.27%) of the 76 pathways for the period from 2030 through 2050, and then used it to linearly extrapolate oil price predictions for the remaining pathways.

National oil fiscal regimes

They form the legal basis for revenue allocation between governments and oil investors.48,49 Global oil fiscal regimes consist of two main types: the concession system and the Contract System30,49(as illustrated in Figure S3). Under the Concession system, oil investors are granted ownership of oilfield resources and pay royalties to the government for use of the mining area. Under the Contract system, the government retains ownership of oilfield resources, and oil investors can obtain a share of oil production or sales revenues through contractual agreements. The Contract system is further divided into SC and Production PSC, with the key distinction being whether oil investors receive cash or crude oil49,50 (details illustrated in Figures S5–S7). Considering the royalty tax and income tax rates, oil fiscal parameters48 across different countries exhibit significant differences (as illustrated in Figures S8–S10), affecting substantially varied revenue distributions, even within the same oil fiscal regime.

Global oilfields economic production model

Based on the widely used NPVs criteria in the investment and production decisions, we developed a global oil economic production model based on a dynamic calculation of NPVs for all oilfields.17,22,23 Specifically, the model includes: (1) deriving the annual production profile curve of oilfields, (2) calculating the NPVs of oilfields, and (3) simulating the economic production curve of oilfields.

The oilfield profile curve is derived from annual production forecasts for every oilfield from 2020 through 2050, sourced from the Wood Mackenzie.44 The annual production forecast of them is mainly determined by the physical characteristics such as reserves and location of oilfields (as illustrated in Figure S11), while the market factors account for a relatively small proportion.

To calculate the NPVs for oilfields, we first incorporated the revenue reallocation under different fiscal regimes into the traditional net profit estimations. Moreover, we added a carbon tax into the calculation of net profits to simulate the impact of future supply side climate policies. This is because the trend toward carbon pricing seems to be prevalent under increasingly stringent climate policies,51,52 which can be evidenced by recent developments in Canada to establish a carbon market for upstream oilfield production.53 For the 530 oilfields lacking cost data (12.1% of the total oilfields), we used the average cost in their corresponding countries (calculated by the oilfields with cost data) to fill in the missing information.

Then, based on the projected oil price pathways, we assume the oil investors are perfectly foresighted and calculate the NPV in year t of each oilfield for its future production.

Under the assumption of perfect foresight among oil investors, the discounted total profit from future production yields the NPV for that period. For each oilfield, if the NPV for the current year exceeds zero, it indicates that oil production is profitable, and the oilfield will produce according to the forecasted output. Otherwise, they do not produce oil and postpone it to future production.

By iterating each year, we can simulate the production decisions for all oilfields during the study period under a given oil price pathway. Note that every year an oilfield makes a production decision, and its future oilfield profile curve needs to be updated. By aggregating the economic production curves of all oilfields, we can derive the global oil economic production curve, which represents the total economically viable production of global oilfields under a given oil price pathway.

Life cycle carbon intensity estimation model of oilfields

The life-cycle GHG emissions of the oil industry comprise upstream (exploration, development, and production), midstream (processing, refining, and transportation), and downstream emissions (consumption). The upstream CI was estimated using the OPGEE v3.0c, the midstream CI was calculated using the PRELIM 1.6, and the downstream CI was assessed using the OPEM 1.1. All three GHG emission calculators are open-source, and use Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) 100-year global warming potential (AR5/GWP100) factors. The detailed explanations are described below.

OPGEE, developed by Stanford University, is a model54 designed to assess greenhouse gas emissions from the production and transportation of crude oil. The system boundary extends from the wellhead to the refinery gate. Further details and guidelines can be found in ref.55. PRELIM, a model developed by the University of Calgary,45 was employed to estimate the greenhouse gas emissions associated with different types of crude oil and various refining processes. The system boundary encompasses the period from the refinery gate to the refinery exit. Specific usage instructions can be found in ref.56. OPEM, developed by the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, is a model used to assess greenhouse gas emissions resulting from the end-use of oil products.46 Its system boundary extends from the refinery exit to final consumption.

Monte Carlo uncertainty analysis

Because our simulation of the impact of global oil emissions on climate goals is long term, it is necessary to consider the time-varying effects of key variables (production cost and life cycle CI) from 2020 through 2050. To address this, we obtained the annual change rates of four key uncertainty factors from the literature, including the annual change rate in production cost, carbon tax, upstream CI and midstream CI, assuming constant oil fiscal parameters (Table S3). For life cycle CI, upstream CI and midstream CI are found to change with time.35,57 For the downstream CI, we rely on the default assumption of OPEM 1.1, and set them as constant, assuming complete utilization and 100% combustion of oil products. The specific settings of the uncertainty factors are as follows:

The first uncertain factor is the annual change rate of oilfield costs, we assume a triangular distribution58 with a maximum value of 6.7%, a minimum value of −0.7%, and a mode value of 2.2%. The second uncertainty factor is the annual rate of carbon tax increases. Based on the findings of Barrage and Nordhaus,59 we set the baseline carbon tax in 2020 at 6 $/t CO2, and assumed a triangular distribution60 with a maximum value of 16.6%, a minimum value of 1%, and a mode value of 10%. The third uncertain factor is the annual change rate of the upstream CI, and a triangular distribution61 was assumed, with a maximum value of 5.5%, a minimum value of -1.5%, and a mode value of 3.0%. The fourth uncertain factor is the annual change rate of the midstream CI, a triangular distribution61 is also assumed for it, with a maximum value of -0.5%, a minimum value of -2.5%, and a mode value of -1.5%.

After identifying the types of uncertainty distributions and their parameters for the above key variables, we employed Latin Hypercube Sampling for Monte Carlo simulation,62 ultimately generating 10,000 simulated trajectories of global oil production and associated GHG emissions.

Quantifying the climate impact of oil sector

Integrating the life-cycle CI with the economic production of all oilfields under different oil price pathways, we used the Monte Carlo approach to simulate the GHG emission pathway of the global oil sector from 2020 through 2050 (see Figure S16). For future oilfields that lacked actual production data, the average life-cycle CI in their corresponding countries was used as the fill-in value. Quantifying the climate impacts of oil sector on the probability of achieving climate goals is complex, nonlinear,4 and involves multiple uncertainties.63 Apart from using a climate model, we developed an approach to approximate the impacts of oil sector emissions on the probability of achieving climate goals.

Sourced from the latest estimation results published on Nature Climate Change,39 Table S4 presents the RCBs from 2023 onward under 1.5°C and 2°C climate goals with different probability levels, as well as historical global CO2 emissions from 2020 to 2022. To ensure consistency with the study period, the effective RCBs from 2020 onward is calculated as the sum of the RCBs from 2023 and cumulative CO2 emissions from 2020 to 2022 over the entire period. As shown in Figures S17 and S18, the points were then fitted using a cubic function. Because the R2 of the estimated function is over 0.999, the fitted function can effectively analyze the impacts of cumulative CO2 emissions on the probability of achieving climate goals.

Since RCBs are defined in terms of cumulative CO2 emissions, we needed to convert the oil sector’s cumulative GHG emissions into cumulative CO2 emissions to ensure consistency. Based on the existing literature, we adopt the CO2 shares in total GHG emissions for upstream (65%),64 midstream (95.6%),35 and downstream (99.5%)36 segments. We then apply these coefficients to the corresponding upstream, midstream, and downstream GHG emissions of the oil sector to obtain the cumulative CO2 emissions. For the 2°C goal, we first calculated the share of oil sector GHG emissions in global total GHG emissions. Based on compiled data from 310 scenarios—including both global GHG emission pathways and oil consumption data—from the IPCC AR6 “C3” category16 (defined as achieving the 2°C goal), as shown in in Figure S20, we converted oil consumption into millions of barrels4 and multiplied it by our estimated average life cycle CI of 528.8 kg CO2e/bbl to calculate, for each scenario, the cumulative GHG emissions from the oil sector over 2020–2050. This allowed us to determine the share of oil sector emissions in global total CO2 emissions over 2020–2050 across scenarios, which averaged 36.8% (ranging from 16.8% to 62.3%). Then we analyzed the impact of future average oil price changes on the probability of achieving the 2°C goal. Using the average share, we converted our estimated cumulative CO2 emissions from the oil sector under different oil price pathways into global total CO2 emissions, resulting in a range of 234.8–1183.2 Gt CO2.

This simple approach is useful for two reasons. On the one hand, it is easy to use and can avoid the requirement of many additional parameters. For example, climate models require a comprehensive global GHG emissions pathway, which not only requires the oil sector’s GHG emission pathway from this study but also assumptions on the emission pathways from other sectors.65 However, the interactions among different sectors are complex, and such assumptions can introduce additional biases.66 On the other hand, our approach yields highly accurate fitting results, and the cumulative CO2 emissions from the oil sector of our study (range from 86.4 to 435.4 Gt CO2) all fall within the 1.5°C goal fitting interval (range from -49 to 955 Gt CO2), avoiding the inaccuracies associated with out-of-sample fitting. Ultimately, the probability of achieving the climate goal under a specific amount of oil sector cumulative CO2 emissions can be obtained by calculating the estimated quadratic function value.
