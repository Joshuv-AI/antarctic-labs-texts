Study on the Compatibility of Acrylic Acid-acrylamide-diethyldiallyl Ammonium Chloride Copolymer with Cement Slurry in CO2 Environment
Authors: She Chao-yi, Ma Yong, Guo Jian-hua, Liu Sen, Wang Yao, Xia Lian-bin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This paper investigates the structure and aggregation stability of acrylic acid-acrylamide-diethyldiallylammonium chloride (FA367), followed by an investigation into the impact of acidic CO2 environments on the interaction between FA367 and cement slurry. The results showed that FA367 formed stable flocculation in cement slurry filtrate, which was not affected by acid gas CO2. With an FA367 concentration of 0.6%, the fluidity of the cement slurry measures 18.1 cm, but this fluidity is lost upon introducing CO2. At 90 °C, the compressive strength after 1, 3, and 7 days is 10.23, 77.48, and 62.97%, respectively, compared to pure cement. However, with the addition of CO2, the compressive strength increases to 27.03, 99.59, and 75.47%, respectively. The interaction between FA367 and cement slurry impedes cement hydration without altering hydration product composition. Instead, it fosters needle-like aggregations between hydration products, affecting the cement stone’s compact structure. Upon CO2 introduction, these needle-like structures diminish, resulting in an overall more intact cement stone structure.

Introduction

Drilling fluids play a crucial role in ensuring the safety and efficiency of drilling operations.1 During the construction of ultradeep well cementing, the cement slurry often comes into contact with drilling fluid, forming flocculation structures that are challenging to break down. This leads to reduced fluidity, shortened thickening time of the cement slurry, and a significant decrease in strength and interface cementation strength.2−5 In oil and gas well exploitation, along with the inherent incompatibility between the physical and chemical properties of cement slurry and drilling fluid, contribute to this deterioration. Additionally, the presence of CO2 and other acidic gases further exacerbates the compatibility issues between cement slurry and drilling fluid.6−9 In the field cementing operation of the Penglai gas field, the interaction between the cement slurry and drilling fluid with acid gas CO2 not only intensifies pollution and compromises construction safety but also extends drilling cycles and escalates drilling costs.6−9

FA367 exhibits strong inhibition and excellent slurry performance, effectively addressing the challenge of poor drilling fluid stability. Its primary functions include inhibiting dispersion, enhancing drilling fluid viscosity, and reducing filtration loss. As one of the commonly used organic tackifiers in drilling fluid, FA367 is chosen in this study to investigate the pollution mechanism of cement slurry under CO2 environments. Currently, extensive research has been conducted both domestically and internationally on the compatibility of drilling fluid and cement paste,2,10−14 with many discussions focusing on their contact pollution mechanisms,2,14,15 However, there is limited research on the contact pollution mechanism of these substances in acidic CO2 mediums. Therefore, it is imperative to study the influence of acid gas CO2 on the pollution of cement slurry and drilling fluid, particularly focusing on the mechanism of contact pollution. This research aims to provide a theoretical foundation for establishing antipollution cement slurry systems and spacer technical reserves, meeting the complex operational requirements of deep and ultradeep wells in the Penglai gas area. It seeks to ensure the safety of cementing operations in these wells and the long-term sealing ability of cement rings.

In this study, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy was initially employed to analyze acrylic acid-acrylamide-diethyldiallylammonium chloride (FA367). Subsequently, utilizing the single-factor analysis method, the impact of FA367 on the engineering performance of cement slurry and its filtrate was examined in the presence or absence of CO2. The influence of acidic CO2 medium on the contact pollution between FA367 and cement slurry was investigated using X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, thermogravimetric analysis, and scanning electron microscopy (SEM).

Experimental Part

Experimental Materials and Instruments

The experiment utilizes G-grade oil well cement supplied by Jiahua Special Cement Co., Ltd. The cement filtrate is obtained from G-grade Jiahua oil well cement through pressure filtration using a high-temperature and high-pressure water loss meter. The production method of the cement filtrate is illustrated in Figure 1 below. Oil well cement additives water loss control agent (G33S) and dispersant (USZ) are provided by Weihui Chemical Co., Ltd. Additionally, the acrylate-acrylamide-diethyldiallyl ammonium chloride copolymer (FA367) is supplied by the Drilling Fluid Technical Service Company of CNPC Chuanqing Drilling Engineering Co., Ltd.

Test and Analysis Methods

Infrared Spectrum Analysis

The copolymer FA367 was analyzed using a Nicolet 6700 infrared spectrometer, with a spectral analysis range from 4000 to 500 cm–1. In this experiment, the halide potassium bromide (KBr) tablet method was employed to prepare the sample. Specifically, the FA367 sample was mixed and ground with potassium bromide in a 1:100 ratio. The copolymer’s structure was characterized using infrared spectrometry to determine the composition of the sample.

Analysis of Basic Properties of Cement

According to API standard16 issued by the American Petroleum Institute, cement paste samples both with and without FA367 were prepared. The influence of FA367 on the fluidity, rheology, and compressive strength of cement paste in the presence and absence of CO2 was investigated using single-factor analysis.

To elucidate the pollution mechanism of FA367 on cement slurry under or absence of acidic CO2 gas conditions, FA367 was incrementally added to both the cement slurry filtrate and the filtrate exposed to CO2 gas. Subsequently, the aggregation stability of the introduced FA367 was compared between the two scenarios.

Characterization of Mechanical Properties

Following the Chinese standard GB/T 19139–2012, a pressure testing machine (TYE-300B, Wuxi Jianyi Instrument Machinery Co., Ltd.) was utilized in accordance with the compressive strength testing procedure. The load was continuously applied until the cement sample fractured, and the compressive strength of the cement sample was evaluated at various curing durations. 71.7 kN/min ± 7.2 kN/min for specimens with anticipated strengths surpassing 3.5 MPa and 17.9 kN/min ± 1.8 kN/min for specimens with expected strengths equal to or less than 3.5 MPa. Pressure values were recorded for samples measuring Φ25 × 25 mm, with three samples tested per group, and the reported values represent the averages.

XRD Analysis

An XRD analyzer (DX-2007B, Dandong Haoyuan Instrument Co., Ltd.) was employed to analyze the phase composition of the cement sample. The sample was dried at 50 °C for 72 h and then ground into powder. CuKα incident radiation was utilized, spanning from 5 to 80°, with a scanning rate of 0.04°/s. The tube voltage and current were set at 40 kV and 30 mA, respectively.

Thermogravimetric Analysis

A TGA/SDTA85/e instrument from Mettler Toledo, Switzerland, was utilized to analyze the composition of cement samples under the protection of a nitrogen atmosphere at a flow rate of 50 mL/min. The sample was ground into powder after drying. The heating rate was set at 10 °C/min, with the test temperature ranging from 40 to 1000 °C.

SEM Analysis

The hydration of cement at a specific time point was terminated by rapid cooling with liquid nitrogen, while the morphology of the cement slurry at specific time point was preserved through vacuum drying. Following drying, the cement stone was fashioned into a thin sheet, and gold was deposited on the sample surface to enhance electrical conductivity before scanning. Subsequently, a scanning electron microscope (ZEISS EVO MA15, Carl Zeiss AG, Germany) was employed to observe the process of microstructure change in the cement slurry. Different acceleration voltages ranging from 7 to 20 kV were selected, and the samples were scanned at different magnifications.

Results and Discussion

Infrared Spectral Analysis

In order to study the molecular structure of FA367, infrared tests were carried out, and the results are shown in Figure 2. The wide peak at 3428 cm–1 is attributed to the N–H asymmetric stretching vibration absorption peak at secondary amide-CONH2, and overlapped with the N–H symmetric stretching vibration absorption peak at 3263 cm–1. At 2924 and 2854 cm–1 are the asymmetric stretching vibration absorption peaks of −CH3 and −CH2 bonded to N+ in quaternary ammonium cationic structure groups. The characteristic absorption peak of the amide I band is at 1661 cm–1. The part of the peak at 1622 cm–1 overlaps with the strong peak of the amide I band, which is the asymmetric stretching vibration of the carboxylic acid conjugate system. The symmetric stretching vibration absorption peak of the carboxylate conjugate system appears at 1406 cm–1. 1456 cm–1 is the stretching vibration peak of C–N bond. The region between 1400 and 400 cm–1 represented the low-frequency bending vibration region of the compound’s molecular structure. Based on these peaks, it can be inferred that FA367 is a copolymer consisting of acrylic acid-acrylamide-diethyldiallyl ammonium chloride.17−22

Effect of Cement Slurry Performance

The Influence of Cement Slurry Foundation Properties

The basic formula of cement slurry in this experiment comprises 100% G grade oil well cement, 1% water loss reducer G33S, and 0.5% dispersant USZ, 44% water-cement ratio. Table 1 illustrates the increase in FA367 addition from 0 to 0.6%. It was observed that FA367 had no effect on the density of the cement slurry. However, it did lead to the thickening of the cement slurry and a reduction in fluidity. The fluidity decreased with the increasing addition of FA367. Initially, the flow of A0 cement slurry was satisfactory. However, as FA367 concentration increased, the cement slurry gradually thickened. When the addition reached 0.6%, the flow decreased to 18.1 cm, which is close to the safety requirements for cementing operations (where the flow of cement slurry should not be less than 18 cm).3 This is attributed to the hydrophilic groups in FA367 forming hydrogen bonds with the Si–O on the surface of the cement particles, adsorbing fine cement particles, and causing the cement slurry to aggregate. Consequently, the movement resistance of the particles increases, resulting in reduced fluidity.2,15,23

Aggregation Stability Test in Cement Slurry Filtrate

The composition of cement slurry is complex.24,25 To elucidate the reaction between FA367 and cement slurry filtrate, FA367 was prepared into a solution and added to the cement slurry filtrate with or without CO2, and its state was observed. As depicted in Figure 3a1–a3, the FA367 solution appeared as transparent floc gel. Upon addition to the cement slurry filtrate, it remained suspended above the filtrate, insoluble and stratified, without dissipating over time. This insolubility is attributed to the poor chemical compatibility of metal cations with carboxylate (−COO–) groups, resulting in a product structure insoluble in water,15,26 as shown in Figure 4. In Figure 3b1–b5, before CO2 injection, the cement slurry filtrate exhibited a light green and clear appearance. With CO2 injection, the cement slurry filtrate gradually became cloudy and eventually presented a milky yellow turbidity. This turbidity arises from the precipitation reaction between metal cations (mainly Ca2+) present in the cement slurry filtrate and CO2 dissolved in water, combined with the original light green color of the cement slurry filtrate. Following the addition of FA367 solution, b5 displayed a state similar to a3, indicating that the acidic gas CO2 does not disrupt the interaction between the cement slurry filtrate and FA367 solution.

Effect of Cement Properties

Compressive Strength of Cement

To investigate whether FA367 affects the development of compressive strength of cement stone in the presence of acid gas CO2, cement stone samples with varying dosages were cured at 90 °C for 1, 3, and 7 days, respectively, to test their compressive strength. It can be seen from Figure 5a that the strength of A0.6 cement stone on days 1, 3, and 7 is the lowest. They are 1.86 ± 0.09, 15.86 ± 0.79 and 18.26 ± 0.91 MPa respectively, which are 10.23, 77.48 and 62.97% of A0 cement stone. As can be seen from Figure 5b, the strength of A0.6-CO2 cement stone at 1, 3, and 7 days is the lowest in the environment with CO2 input. They are 5.1 ± 0.26, 26.95 ± 1.35 and 27.94 ± 1.40 MPa, respectively, which are 27.03, 99.59 and 75.47% of A0-CO2 cement stone. From the data, it is evident that FA367 hampers the hydration process of cement, with a more pronounced hindrance effect observed with higher FA367 dosages, particularly within the initial 24 h of hydration. This is attributed to the hydrophilic groups in FA367 forming hydrogen bonds with Si–O on the surface of cement particles, hindering water-cement particle contact and reducing the hydration reaction rate. Simultaneously, the carboxylate group reacts chemically with numerous cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+, Fe3+) produced by cement hydration (as shown in Figure 4), disrupting the development of the cement stone microstructure and resulting in reduced strength.2,15,23,26

Compressive strength of cement stone.

Comparing Figure 5a,b, it is evident that the compressive strength of all samples increased after CO2 injection. However, the growth rate of compressive strength for cement containing FA367 in a CO2 environment is higher than that of A0 cement. The compressive strength growth rate of A0 cement stone is 3.80, 32.19, and 27.66%, and the strength growth rate of A0.2 cement stone is 115.98, 67.04, and 42.82%, respectively. The strength growth rates of A0.4 cement were 238.73, 66.35 and 75.08%, respectively. The strength growth rates of A0.6 cement were 174.19, 69.92 and 53.01%, respectively. These findings indicate that CO2 attenuates the negative effect of FA367 on cement strength. This is attributed to the fact that the strong alkali environment resulting from early cement hydration converts the amide group in FA367 into carboxylate,15,27 while the acidic gas CO2 reduces the alkalinity of the environment.

In conclusion, within the first 24 h of cement hydration, FA367 significantly hinders the cement hydration process, with the obstruction effect becoming more pronounced with higher dosages. However, CO2 mitigates the negative impact of FA367 on cement strength.

Cement Phase Composition

Figure 6 depicts the XRD pattern of pure cement and FA367 cement samples after curing at 90 °C for 3 days, both before and after CO2 injection. These patterns reflect the changes in the main hydration products.28 As can be seen from the figure, the composition of the hydration product remained unchanged after the addition of FA367, with the main product being Ca(OH)2.14,29 Cement samples containing FA367 (A0.6, A0.6-CO2) had lower calcium hydroxide peaks than pure cement samples (A0, A0-CO2), regardless of whether CO2 was injected. This can be attributed to the hydrophilic groups in FA367 being adsorbed on the surface of the cement particles, thereby hindering the hydration reaction. This hindrance is manifested as a decrease in the diffraction strength of the hydration product Ca(OH)2.

XRD pattern of cement stone.

The emergence of calcite peaks in A0-CO2 and A0.6-CO2 samples indicates that the CO2 introduced reacts with some hydration products. Interestingly, the peak value of calcium hydroxide increased in A0-CO2 compared to the A0 sample, and a similar effect was observed in A0.6-CO2 compared to the A0.6 sample. This suggests that the introduction of CO2 into the cement slurry can mitigate the negative effects of FA367. Notably, the results of XRD analysis align with those of the compressive strength test.

Cement Phase Content

Figure 7 displays the TG test results of pure cement and FA367 cement samples after curing at 90 °C for 3 days, both before and after CO2 injection. It is evident from the figure that the thermal decomposition temperature of the samples remains consistent, indicating that FA367 does not affect the hydration products of the cement. The thermal decomposition process can be divided into three temperature ranges: 40–300 °C, 400–500 °C, and above 540 °C. In the first range, the main components include calcium aluminate hydrate, calcium sulfoaluminate hydrate (Afm), C–S–H gel, ettringite (Aft), and the loss of bound water. The second temperature range is the decomposition temperature of Ca(OH)2, while the third range represents the decomposition temperature of CaCO3.30−33 Cement mass loss rate is calculated according to formula 1 as shown below.1

From Figure 7, it is evident that the total hydration product content of A0 is 24.15% higher than that of A0.6, while the total hydration product content of A0-CO2 is 20.6% higher than that of A0.6-CO2. This indicates that the presence of FA367 inhibits the hydration reaction process of cement. Furthermore, after the introduction of CO2, the total hydration product content of A0.6-CO2 increased by 25.14% compared to A0.6. This suggests that the introduction of CO2 into the cement slurry weakens the inhibition effect of FA367. Importantly, this finding is consistent with the XRD results.

Analysis of Cement Micromorphology

The scanning electron microscope test results are shown in Figure 8. When observing pure cement at 15 min, its interior displays flaky and irregularity granular mineral particles. By the 1-h, numerous aggregated small particles surround the flake minerals. By the 2-h, the flaky mineral particles begin to connect, indicative of continuous cement particle dissolution. After 3 days of curing, the pure cement samples exhibit a dense cement structure, with observable C–S–H gel and Ca(OH)2. In contrast, the morphology of the sample mixed with FA367 at 1 min resembles that of pure cement at 15 min, both representing the initial stages of cement hydration. By 2 min, numerous small needle-like substances adsorbed on the surface of mineral particles, indicating hydrogen bond adsorption between the hydrophilic group in FA367 and the oxygen on the cement particle surface. By 10 min, the mineral content in the cement decreases while the hydration products increase. The product surfaces become adsorbed with small needle-like substances, and cement particle dissolution continues. After 3 days, alongside C–S–H gel and Ca(OH)2, many needle-like structure products are observed in FA367-doped samples. These needle-like structure products aggregate between hydration products, compromising the overall structural integrity of the cement and resulting in decreased mechanical properties, as shown in Figure 5.

Micromorphologies of samples at different times.

After the introduction of CO2, notable changes were observed in the cement samples. Cured for 3 days, compared to the A0 sample, the A0-CO2 sample exhibited a significantly rougher surface, accompanied by increased formation of hydration products such as C–S–H gel. This indicates more thorough hydration of cement particles. Similarly, compared to the A0.6 sample, the A0.6-CO2 sample displayed a reduction in needle-like structures, suggesting a more complete overall structure of the cement. Macroscopically, the strength of the cement stone is higher, as shown in Figure 5.

Pollution Mechanism Analysis

According to the infrared test results, FA367 is identified as a copolymer consisting of acrylate, acrylamide, and diethyldiallyl ammonium chloride. FA367 contains hydrophilic groups such as −CONH2, −COOH. These hydrophilic groups have the capability to form hydrogen bonds with Si–O on the surface of cement particles. As a result, they adsorb onto the surface of cement particles, impeding the interaction between water and cement particles. This hindrance reduces the rate of hydration reaction, disrupts the development of the microstructure of cement stone, and consequently leads to a reduction in strength.2,23 The strong alkaline environment generated by the early hydration of cement triggers the transformation of the amide group in FA367 into carboxylate.15,27 When the carboxylate groups contact with a large number of cations (Ca2+, Mg2+, Al3+, Fe3+) produced by cement hydration, chemical reactions will occur.15,26 This results in the formation of a water-insoluble product structure on the surface of cement particles, as depicted in Figure 4. These structures adsorb small cement particles, causing the cement slurry to aggregate and leading to thickening, flocculation, and poor fluidity of the slurry,2,15,23 as shown in Figure 9.

Schematic diagram of FA367 flocculated adsorption cement.

After CO2 is injected into the cement slurry, OH– in the cement slurry is consumed to produce CO32–. Subsequently, CO32– reacts with Ca2+ in the cement slurry to produce CaCO3. This reaction results in an increase in the consistency of the cement slurry and a significant reduction in fluidity, as demonstrated in Table 2. Simultaneously, the acid gas CO2 diminishes the alkalinity of the cement hydration environment and weakens the negative impact of FA367 on the cement slurry.

Additionally, at 90 °C, the compressive strength of samples cured for 1, 3, and 7 days was observed to be 10.23, 77.48, and 62.97% of that of pure cement, respectively.
