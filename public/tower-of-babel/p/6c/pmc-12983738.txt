Which factors mediate the effect of childhood socioeconomic disadvantage on mental health in young adulthood? A protocol for a target trial emulation using linked administrative data from New South Wales, Australia
Authors: Lucy Leigh, Madeleine Hinwood, Christopher Oldmeadow, Jamie Bryant, Todd Heard, Kristy Fakes, Breanne Hobden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionEarly-life socioeconomic status (SES) increases the risk of poor mental health outcomes in adulthood. However, the mechanistic pathways underlying this relationship remain poorly understood. While addressing socioeconomic inequalities remains a long-term goal, identifying specific mediating pathways could reveal more immediate opportunities for effective interventions.Methods and analysisUsing linked administrative data from New South Wales, Australia (1990–2022), we will emulate a target trial examining the relationship between SES at birth and acute mental health admissions and presentations between the ages of 18 and 25 years. An interventional mediation analysis will be used to examine the effect of multiple mediating pathways, including education, housing stability, child protection contact, parental incarceration and justice system involvement, on the outcome. Introduction

There is a clear social gradient in mental health that begins in early life, with childhood socioeconomic status (SES) consistently associated with long-term mental health outcomes.1,5 Children born into environments of socioeconomic disadvantage are strongly predisposed to develop mental health problems in young adulthood and beyond.5 This association is thought to be mediated through various developmental pathways and social exposures during critical periods of child and adolescent development.6 For instance, children in low SES environments are more likely to experience certain adverse events such as family stress, limited access to education, interactions with justice systems and exposure to significant harm,7 all of which can impact neurological development and psychological well-being.8

Despite strong evidence for these developmental trajectories, the prevention and care of mental health disorders has historically focused on medical and psychological models applied later in life.9 A social model of mental health recognises that while individual-level determinants are important, mental health outcomes are fundamentally shaped by societal conditions and structural inequalities.10 This model suggests that many factors on the pathway between early-life SES and adult mental health could be suitable targets for intervention.11 While eliminating socioeconomic disparities remains a crucial long-term goal, identifying and understanding the mechanisms through which SES affects mental health, such as educational opportunities, housing stability or exposure to adversity, may help identify where interventions might be most effective in addressing disadvantage and poor mental health outcomes.11

In social epidemiology, identifying causal pathways is crucial not merely for understanding mechanisms, but for revealing specific points where interventions could modify the impact of early-life disadvantage on later outcomes.12 However, disentangling these pathways presents significant methodological challenges.13 Unlike clinical interventions, the complex social exposures of interest are rarely subject to randomised trials,14 15 particularly when examining life-course effects of early socioeconomic disadvantage on mental health outcomes, where multiple mediating factors may operate simultaneously over long periods.

Modern causal inference methods have fundamentally transformed our ability to make causal statements from non-randomised data. The formal counterfactual framework pioneered by Rubin and Pearl provides a rigorous mathematical foundation for causal claims when specific assumptions are transparently stated and tested.16 To address these challenges for our research question, we propose using an interventional mediation framework nested within a target trial approach.13 17 While socioeconomic exposures cannot be explored using traditional clinical trials, the target trial framework offers an approach for causal inference using observational data.18 A target trial is the ideal randomised trial that would be performed to answer the causal question of interest, which is then emulated in an observational dataset.19 Embedding interventional mediation analysis within the target trial framework enables estimation of the potential impact of hypothetical interventions on specific mediating factors, even when using observational data where such interventions have not been—and perhaps cannot be—directly tested.20 This framework will therefore allow examination of the extent to which different social factors mediate the relationship between early-life SES and mental health outcomes, thereby identifying which factors if modified, would have the greatest impact on reducing mental health inequities attributable to socioeconomic disadvantage.

Recent advances in causal mediation analysis, particularly the development of interventional (in)direct effects, allow us to partition the total effect of SES into direct and indirect effects operating through specific pathways of interest in complex, multifactorial settings.21 22 This approach is particularly valuable for complex social exposures where multiple mechanisms may be at play. By estimating these effects within a target trial framework, we can provide more reliable evidence about which intervention targets might be most effective in reducing mental health inequalities. This approach provides a rigorous foundation for identifying and evaluating potential interventions, even when working with observational data. This work seeks to provide policy-relevant evidence about the relative importance of different causal pathways and guide decision-making for targeted intervention efforts. This study will thus employ the target trial framework to estimate the effect of SES at birth on mental health hospital admissions and Emergency Department (ED) presentations (referred to as ‘mental health presentations’ for the remainder of this paper) in young adulthood, examining several proposed interventional targets through which this effect may operate. This outcome was chosen to identify targets for policy intervention that could prevent the most severe mental health crises among socioeconomically disadvantaged young people. Severe mental health disorders, indexed via hospitalisation or emergency presentation, likely represent mental health crises that have not been adequately addressed within community settings.23 24 Exacerbated mental health crises may be due to a culmination of insufficient early intervention and support that disproportionately affects those from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds.

Aims

To describe the development of a target trial protocol for a causal mediation analysis, which aims to build on prior research detailing associations between SES and poor mental health in young adulthood. Specifically, we aim to partition the total effect of SES into so-called ‘interventional’ effects operating via selected exposures of interest.

Methods

Data source

A linked Australian dataset, the New South Wales (NSW) Department of Communities and Justice (DCJ) Human Services Dataset (HSDS). The HSDS includes data on children and young people born on or after 1 January 1990 (primary cohort), and their parents, guardians and carers (secondary cohort). The dataset links state and national datasets including child protection, justice, housing, health, education, parental risk indicators, mental health indicators, government services and alcohol and other drug use. The dataset is anonymised, and data confidentiality is maintained through multiple safeguards: access is restricted to approved researchers through secure computing environments, and all data handling follows institutional and data custodian security protocols. Personal identifiers are not accessible to the research team, and all outputs are subject to disclosure review before release. Designing this target trial within the context of an existing data source enabled us to factor characteristics and limitations of the dataset into the study design. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the custodians of each linked dataset. No consent was sought from participants for the use of anonymised administrative data.

Target trial framework

Developing the causal question of interest

The broad scientific question for this project is to investigate which, if any, of the proposed social exposures available in the linked dataset have the greatest potential to reduce poor mental health outcomes by mitigating socioeconomic inequalities between exposure groups through relevant interventions or policies. To transform this broad inquiry into a specific hypothesis-driven causal question answerable via a target trial design, we operationalised the exposure, mediators and outcomes based on available information about these administrative data sources. Final variable specifications may require minor refinement following data access, cleaning and variable transformation, but all substantive analytical decisions are prespecified in this protocol.

Target population

This target trial would occur in the population of all children born in NSW (January 1990 through June 2003). This represents the broader group to which we wish to generalise our findings, extending beyond the specific birth cohort included in our dataset. This framework enables inferences to be drawn about the average causal effects of SES on mental health in young adulthood over calendar time in comparable Australian contexts, while acknowledging the effects may vary across time periods and substantially different social, policy and economic contexts.

Outcome

The primary outcome of interest is severe mental health outcomes in young adulthood, defined here as mental health presentations requiring acute or tertiary care. This outcome will be captured by the total number of mental health-related presentations occurring between the ages of 18 and 25 years. This includes both emergency department presentations and hospital admissions where mental health is the primary reason for seeking care. These acute care events represent significant mental health crises that not only impose substantial costs on healthcare systems but also reflect profound personal suffering, and the disproportionate burden of these severe outcomes among individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds represents a critical health inequity.

Primary exposure: SES at birth

The primary exposure of interest is SES at birth. Previous studies have used variables including education, income, wealth and occupation to construct indices of SES.25 An index of SES will be constructed using the following variables for the current study, measured at the time of birth or closest recorded prior:

Parental education: Measured as the highest level achieved (primary, secondary or tertiary), assumed to be time-invariant.

Household income: If available, categorised into quintiles.

Parental occupation: If available, classified using standard occupational categories.26

While these components are correlated, each captures distinct pathways through which socioeconomic position may influence mental health outcomes. A composite SES score will be created by multiplying selected components (eg, education and wealth) to create an indicator under the assumption that each factor has a unique impact on defining SES that is not exchangeable. This approach will be empirically driven; however, it will also consider the pragmatics of data completeness, quality and distribution to reduce potential data biases. Alternative approaches may be assessed in sensitivity analyses. For components with substantial missing data, we will employ appropriate missing data techniques27 and conduct sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of our findings across different SES measurement approaches.

Mediators

The identification and selection of mediators to include in the final analysis involved multiple steps, combining expert knowledge, literature review and data considerations. An expert panel consisting of health behaviour researchers, statisticians, mental health researchers, practising psychologists, a social worker and young mental health service consumers were convened to identify potential mediators of the effect of SES on mental health presentations in young adulthood.

Identified mediators were then categorised into those potentially observable in our administrative dataset, including contact with social services or education data, and those which are unobservable, such as experiences of discrimination or social isolation. Consensus on which of the available mediators to prioritise for exploration in the study was achieved through iterative discussion across multiple meetings (n=4), convened between 16 May 2024 and 9 February 2026. In the first meeting, panel members independently reviewed the mediators identified in the available administrative datasets and proposed additional candidates for exploration based on clinical experience and consumer perspectives. The second meeting involved discussion of mechanisms to prioritise mediators. Data availability was raised as an important factor in prioritisation. A third meeting was held once data availability had been assessed to determine which mediators could reasonably be retained. Following a final meeting to enable discussion of the final available variables, a rank order survey will be administered to the expert panel to prioritise the list of mediators. This will then be applied to data analysis. Final rankings reflected group consensus after these discussions.

The availability and consistency of data for each potential mediator will be assessed when the data are obtained. Variables reported over the entire follow-up period of interest for our analysis will be retained.

Initially, the five mediators with highest priority and sufficient coverage will be included in the mediation model, which is a methodological decision focusing on a constrained number of mediators for feasibility (many mediators can lead to data spareness, and create challenges with model convergence due to increasingly complex model structures) and interpretability. We recognise that this process necessarily excludes other potentially important mediators. Omitting relevant mediators can bias estimates of both direct and indirect effects, including the effect of an unmeasured mediator being incorrectly attributed to either the indirect effect of measured mediators or absorbed into the direct effect of SES. Our interpretation of mediation results will therefore rely on the assumption that omitted mediators do not strongly confound or interact with the pathways being estimated. To address this limitation, and to gain a deeper understanding of factors and mechanisms which might contribute to the association between SES and mental health outcomes, we will integrate findings from a complementary qualitative study as part of a larger mixed-methods project. This approach will help identify additional mechanisms and inform future iterations of the mediation model, with the overall aim of developing relevant and effective solutions.

Defining time zero

Birth was considered the most appropriate point to begin follow-up time (ie, ‘time zero’), for several key reasons. First, this approach establishes a clear temporal sequence where exposure precedes mediators which themselves precede outcome assessment, strengthening causal inferences. Second, birth represents the initial socioeconomic conditions to which individuals are exposed, that are known to be critical determinants of later health outcomes. While socioeconomic position may change throughout childhood, using birth as the index time point allows us to directly examine how the conditions people are born into shape their long-term mental health.

Identification of potential confounders associated with both SES at birth and mental health ED presentations and hospital admissions

This target trial aims to emulate random assignment to high, middle or low SES by assuming exchangeability conditional on relevant covariates. This approach attempts to adjust for real-world factors that produce imbalance in mental health outcomes across different SES groups. When considering confounders of the relationship between SES at birth and ED mental health presentations and hospital admissions in young adulthood, we included factors that could both influence the SES level a child is born into, and which may affect mental health outcomes through pathways independent of SES. A directed acyclic graph (DAG) will be used to identify potential confounding variables of the exposure outcome, the mediator-outcome and the exposure-mediator relationships. Examples of these confounding variables identified in collaboration with the expert panel include:

Parental characteristics: parental age, parent health/mental health status, parental education, birth order and migration history/effects of racism or discrimination.

Parental characteristics: parental age, parent health/mental health status, parental education, birth order and migration history/effects of racism or discrimination.

Geographic and environmental factors: regional/area characteristics at birth, proximity to healthcare services.

Geographic and environmental factors: regional/area characteristics at birth, proximity to healthcare services.

Societal factors: period effects (eg, policy changes, programmes or initiatives designed to address SES disparities and/or improve health outcomes, such as the Brighter Futures or Youth Hope programmes).34

Societal factors: period effects (eg, policy changes, programmes or initiatives designed to address SES disparities and/or improve health outcomes, such as the Brighter Futures or Youth Hope programmes).34

This strategy focuses on confounders at baseline (those which precede or coincide with time of birth) and avoids selecting for mediators. To quantify the potential impact of unmeasured confounders, several complementary approaches will be employed. (1) A quantitative bias analysis, including E-value calculations, will be conducted to assess how strong unmeasured confounding would need to be to explain away any observed effects. (2) Undertaking negative control analyses, examining outcomes that should not be affected by SES but would be subject to similar confounding patterns. (3) Implement a series of sensitivity analyses examining the robustness of the findings to different assumptions about unmeasured confounding, measurement error in our SES index, missing data and model specification. This comprehensive approach to sensitivity analysis will provide transparency and bounds around the potential impact of unmeasured confounding variables on our conclusions.

Target trial emulation protocol

Following the structure outlined in Hernan and Robins (2016), we specified each component for the target trial specification, and its analogue conducted in our observational dataset (online supplemental table 1).

Eligibility criteria

The eligible study population is those born in NSW between 1 January 1997 and 30 June 2003. The end date was selected to ensure adequate follow-up time in young adulthood, allowing for at least 6 months of outcome assessment at age 18 years in the final year of available data (2021). Including all births during this period reflects the population-wide relevance of our research question and acknowledges the impact of SES across all members of the population.

Additional criteria relating to data quality and availability are required for reliable effect estimation in observational data. This will require valid linkage across relevant administrative datasets, sufficient data to enable the exposure (SES) to be defined, and the presence of key covariates required for baseline confounder adjustment.

Treatment strategies and hypothetical interventions

In the pragmatic target trial, the primary exposure of interest would be assignment to low versus high SES at birth (from this point on, we refer to the low vs high comparison only for ease, however, all methods described will also be applied to the low vs middle SES comparison). While this could not be randomised in practice, conceptualising it within the context of a hypothetical target trial enables clarification of the causal effects we wish to estimate. In emulating this trial, we will determine SES using a composite index derived from available administrative data indicators including parental education, occupation and area-level measures. The overall aim of the study is to estimate the effect of a set of hypothetical intervention(s) that would shift the distribution of key mediating factors among children born in low SES to match those observed among children born in high SES. This approach is appropriate for ‘ill-defined’ mediation effects, where we lack well-defined mediator interventions (such as a specific treatment or programme) in our data. Instead, we have indicators of social disadvantage (such as homelessness, educational disengagement) that could be targets for a future hypothetical intervention.

Selection of mediators for inclusion in the models was as described above. The distributions of mediators will be estimated from observed data, comparing patterns between high and low SES groups to define our hypothetical interventions. This approach allows us to estimate the potential impact of interventions that would achieve similar distributions of these social factors across SES groups, while acknowledging the complexity of implementing such changes in practice.

Follow-up period

The follow-up period of interest is young adulthood, defined as the years young people are aged 18–25 years inclusive, a critical period for the emergence of mental health conditions. The delay between time zero and outcome assessment allows for the accumulation of exposure to mediating factors that may be on the causal pathway from SES at birth to mental health problems in young adulthood. In this target trial emulation, the same time zero and follow-up periods will be retained while addressing practical considerations in the administrative dataset. The study follow-up period will be based on data availability; however, we will allow for those born in later years to have shorter follow-up periods. To handle variable follow-up time, we will require a minimum of 6 months of follow-up for inclusion, and use the logarithm of follow-up time as an offset in the analysis to account for different durations. Follow-up will be censored at age 25 years, interstate migration or death, whichever comes first, with inverse probability of treatment weighting planned to adjust for the impact of the latter two as competing events.

Assignment procedures

In the hypothetical target trial, participants would be randomly assigned to either low or high SES at birth. In this trial emulation, SES will be determined at birth, and we will assume exchangeability conditional on the set of baseline covariates detailed in the Identification of potential confounders associated with both SES at birth and mental health ED presentations and hospital admissions section. The validity of this assumption will be evaluated through sensitivity analyses described in our confounding assessment framework.

In the target trial emulation, these estimands will be identically defined and estimated conditional on measured confounders.

Unlike natural effects which are typically obtained in mediation analyses, interventional effects correspond more closely to potential real-world interventions and require fewer untestable assumptions. Interventional effects are also ‘policy relevant’—they involve the idea of implementing a policy that would shift the mediator distribution to the extent to which it is affected by the exposure. By estimating these effects in multiple mediators simultaneously, we aim to identify the most impactful pathways through which SES influences early adult mental health, and thus inform targeted interventions that could be deployed to mitigate inequalities in mental health. Further, this approach allows us to account for potential interactions between SES and the chosen mediators, providing a more nuanced understanding of these complex relationships.

Statistical methodology

The causal mediation approaches described by Lindmark et al36 and Micali et al37 will be adapted for this study to accommodate a count outcome, to investigate the reduction in the inequality (disparity) in mental health presentations and admissions in young adulthood that could be achieved by intervening on different mediators. We aim to estimate the following effects in two stages:

Stage 1: outcome model specification

The outcome model will be specified using Poisson regression (or other count model as appropriate) for the number of mental health presentations, incorporating adjustment for measured confounders and exposure-mediator interactions. The inclusion of exposure-mediator terms in the outcome model is necessary to allow for the possibility that the effect of each mediator on the outcome may differ by the level of the exposure variable (SES at birth).37 38 If present, exposure-mediator interactions modify the interpretation of the indirect effects and are thus required for the correct identification of interventional direct and indirect effects. The interaction terms will be specified for each mediator based on prior knowledge and theoretical considerations. If there is no strong rationale for a particular interaction, we will assess possible effect modification using interaction tests and visualisation during preliminary model-building, with the final model retaining interaction terms that are theoretically justified or empirically supported to improve the model fit and interpretation of effects. The model will use a flexible specification that allows for interdependence between mediators, with the logarithm of the follow-up time included as an offset term, and robust standard errors. Inclusion of the offset term explicitly accounts for differing follow-up durations, allowing maximisation of available data while appropriately adjusting for heterogeneous follow-up times. This will allow us to quantify the following estimand:

Adjusted total effect: This quantifies the disparity in mental health admissions and presentations; defined as the rate-ratio (adjusted for baseline confounders) of mental health admissions and presentations among children born to low SES status compared with those born to a high SES status.

To balance interpretability, statistical stability and feasibility within a g-computation framework, the primary analysis will include a constrained set of five mediators, as described above. This is a pragmatic decision representing the trade-off between capturing key mechanisms and maintaining estimability. Although a larger set of potential mediators is available in the data, simultaneously including all of them in one model may lead to unstable estimates and increased sensitivity to modelling assumptions. We recognise that excluding additional mediators may result in part of their effect being absorbed into the estimated direct effect of SES or into the joint indirect effect of included mediators. Accordingly, mediation estimates will be interpreted as reflecting the contribution of the selected mediator set rather than all possible pathways. Where feasible and informative, selected secondary analyses will explore the robustness of conclusions to alternative mediator specifications, guided by empirical data patterns and interpretability.

Handling intercurrent events and missing data

Missing data in the observational emulation of the target trial is conceptualised as being analogous to incomplete measurement or loss to follow-up in a pragmatic clinical trial. Our approach aims to preserve, as far as possible, the estimands defined in the target trial protocol.

Two main intercurrent events are anticipated to occur, in which individuals are removed from the administrative dataset: interstate moves and deaths. For participants who move interstate, inverse probability weighting will be used to account for differential observation time. Because socioeconomic differences in survival represent a competing risk that may bias total and mediated effects, mortality patterns will be examined across SES strata, with sensitivity analyses (eg, inverse probability weighting for survival) undertaken where the possibility of selective mortality is observed.

Missing data are expected across SES components, confounders, mediators and outcomes. In most cases, it will not be possible to distinguish between missing mediator or outcome data and a non-event (eg, a lack of year 10 result could either be due to missing data, or a true lack of attainment). Empirical simulation studies of interventional mediation analyses demonstrate the largest bias when intermediate confounders, mediators and outcomes all contribute to missingness, a plausible configuration in our setting, and that no single method is uniformly unbiased under complex missingness.27 Accordingly, we will not default to multiple imputation, but will use missingness-directed DAGs (m-DAGs) to characterise hypothesised missingness mechanisms and assess whether target estimands are recoverable from observed data alone.13 39

Where estimands are recoverable, complete case analysis will be used only where recoverability conditions indicate it to be unbiased and auxiliary variables are unavailable. Otherwise, we will employ methods aligned with our g-computation framework, including inverse probability weighting for missingness, substantive-model-compatible multiple imputation or hybrid approaches. For multiple imputation, fully conditional specification will be used with compatibility enforced with respect to the outcome model, including all variables specified in the g-computation models and relevant interaction terms. Where appropriate, auxiliary variables predictive of missingness or partially observed values will be incorporated, guided by DAGs to avoid conditioning on colliders.

Where estimands are not recoverable from observed data, analyses will proceed using the most principled available approaches but interpreted as providing bounds on plausible effect sizes rather than definitive estimates. Interpretation will prioritise consistency of conclusions across methods and sensitivity analyses, rather than reliance on any single estimator.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess robustness across four key domains: unmeasured confounding, missing data mechanisms, measurement error and model specification. To examine unmeasured confounding, E-values will be calculated for total and mediated effects to quantify the minimum strength of association an unmeasured confounder would require to explain observed findings, benchmarked against associations observed for measured confounders. Negative control outcomes not plausibly affected by SES will also be examined.

Sensitivity to missing data assumptions will be guided by the m-DAG assessment. For variables where informative missingness is considered plausible, targeted sensitivity analyses will be undertaken to examine departures from missing-at-random assumptions, including delta-adjusted analyses across plausible ranges informed by expert input and empirical evidence. Where informative, additional approaches such as pattern-mixture contrasts or tipping-point analyses will be used to assess the deviation required to materially alter conclusions.

Measurement error in SES index components and the composite index will be assessed using quantitative bias analysis, considering the likely direction and magnitude of misclassification. Sensitivity to model specification will be examined via alternative operationalisations of key variables and interaction terms.

Implementation

The analysis will be implemented using R statistical software. G-computation will be performed using the ‘gfoRmula’ package, with bootstrapping (1000 replicates) for CI estimation. Mediator distributions will be estimated empirically from the high SES group, and interventional effects will be calculated using standardisation methods. Results will be presented as rate ratios with 95% CIs, and key sensitivity analysis results will be displayed graphically.

Discussion

We have detailed the specification of a target trial and its emulation in an observational dataset, the aim of which is to evaluate potential policy interventions for reducing the impact of SES at birth on young adulthood mental health. This protocol addresses several key challenges in advancing our understanding of the causal pathways underlying the social determinants of health. First, by employing the target trial framework, we make explicit the causal questions we aim to answer, and the assumptions and data required for valid inference. Second, our use of interventional mediation effects allows us to examine multiple pathways simultaneously without requiring strong assumptions about mediator ordering or independence. Finally, this method does not require that the interventions themselves be present in the data; rather, we can evaluate the potential impact that future interventions can have on causal pathways between intervention and outcome.

The approach detailed here has several strengths. By using administrative data, we can examine real-world patterns of service use at a population level over an extended period of time. The interventional effects framework provides policy-relevant estimates that identify which mediators, if modified, would have the greatest impact on reducing disparities in severe mental health disorders in young adulthood. While this approach could inform where to target potential interventions (ie, which social determinants would yield the greatest benefits if addressed), it does not specify which interventions would be most effective. These findings rather inform the prioritisation of targets for interventions, even in the context of complex social exposures that cannot be directly manipulated (eg, through randomised trials). Further research would be required to determine the most effective specific interventions for each identified target.

We acknowledge that this approach may also have some important limitations. The use of administrative data, while providing comprehensive coverage, cannot capture all important social and environmental factors. For instance, many potential mediating factors were identified by the expert panel that do not exist in linked administrative data (such as participation in sporting associations or social clubs, experience of bullying at school). The complex nature of social disadvantage means that our selected mediators likely represent only a subset of relevant pathways. Additionally, the administrative dataset only includes those enrolled in public schools, which excludes approximately 37.8%40 of NSW children in Catholic and independent schools. While this limits generalisability to the full NSW birth cohort, public schools are broadly representative of the general population in Australia. However, a limitation of the study is that findings may not be applicable to the highest SES groups.

The main study outcome requires acute or tertiary care for a mental health episode to target those with exacerbated mental health issues that are potentially not being appropriately managed. However, the authors acknowledge that access to primary care may itself be a mediator in the pathway between childhood disadvantage and mental health outcomes. Improvements in SES or identified mediators could enhance engagement with primary care services, potentially preventing progression to crisis-level care. Nevertheless, the absence of primary care data in available registries is a limitation of this study.

Causal mediation analysis relies on strong assumptions, including adequate control of confounding for relationships between the exposure, mediators and the outcome. Although a rich set of covariates are available in the dataset, residual and unmeasured confounding variables cannot be ruled out. In addition, missing data or incomplete capture of some events in administrative data may limit the identification of some estimands. To address these challenges, we have developed a comprehensive sensitivity analysis strategy to explore the robustness of findings to plausible departures from confounding and missingness assumptions, which will allow transparent interpretation of the strengths and limitations of inference.

Despite these limitations, this protocol outlines a rigorous approach to understanding how early-life socioeconomic disadvantage affects mental health outcomes in young adulthood. Our findings will contribute to the evidence base for developing and implementing health and social policies aimed at addressing the mental health consequences of socioeconomic inequalities. By identifying the most impactful mediating pathways, our findings could help prioritise interventions and inform policies aimed at reducing mental health inequities. Future research could continue to extend on this work by incorporating additional data sources, examining other relevant outcomes or investigating specific subpopulations.
