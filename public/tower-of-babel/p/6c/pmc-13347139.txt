Correlations and Timeliness of Respiratory Syncytial Virus Surveillance Indicators—United States, 2018–2024
Authors: Erin L. Anderson, Erica Billig Rose, Amber Winn, Sophia Chkonia, Heidi Moline, Ariana Toepfer, Monica E. Patton, Christopher A. Taylor, Aaron Kite‐Powell, Stephanie Dietz, Michael Sheppard, Jourdan DeVies, Benjamin J. Silk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major cause of hospitalizations in young children and older adults in the United States. Monitoring seasonal RSV activity through national surveillance systems and understanding the relationships among RSV surveillance indicators can help ensure timely administration of prevention products and improve preparedness among healthcare systems and public health programs.MethodsWe analyzed RSV data across six surveillance years from the National Respiratory and Enteric Virus Surveillance System (NREVSS), National Syndromic Surveillance Program (NSSP), commercial laboratories, New Vaccine Surveillance Network (NVSN), and Respiratory Syncytial Virus Hospitalization Surveillance Network (RSV‐NET). We characterized RSV seasonality and compared pediatric and all‐age surveillance indicators (percent positivity, percentage of ED visits, and hospitalization rates) at key points during the RSV season. We then estimated cross‐correlations and lags between each system and NREVSS before, during, and after the COVID‐19 pandemic.ResultsFive RSV seasons occurred between 2018 and 2024. Pediatric indicators were greater than all‐age indicators at NREVSS season onset (range: 1.4–6.3 times greater), peak (1.3–5.8), and offset (1.1–4.7). Nationally, indicators were highly correlated across systems (ρ > 0.92) during the prepandemic and postdisruption periods, with minimal lags (0–1 weeks). However, correlations (ρ = 0.48–0.99) and lags (0–6 weeks) varied across regions and time windows during these periods.ConclusionsPediatric surveillance indicators provide the earliest indications of RSV season onset. CDC systems that provide complementary data on RSV activity and associated outcomes are highly correlated at the national and regional levels but may not capture local trends that are important for optimizing prevention.

Background

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a common cause of lower respiratory tract infections in young children and older adults. Between October 1, 2024, and September 27, 2025, an estimated 3.6 million to 6.8 million outpatient visits, 190,000–370,000 hospitalizations, and 11,000–24,000 deaths were associated with RSV infections in the United States [1]. Since 2023, several RSV prevention products have become newly available for populations at high risk for severe RSV disease. For older adults, a single dose of any FDA‐approved RSV vaccine is recommended for all adults aged ≥ 75 years and for adults aged 50–74 years who are at increased risk for severe RSV disease [2, 3]. Either maternal vaccination or a long‐acting RSV monoclonal antibody (nirsevimab or clesrovimab, licensed in 2023 and 2025, respectively) is recommended to prevent RSV–associated lower respiratory tract infections in infants < 8 months of age entering or born during their first RSV season [4, 5, 6]. Because protection against RSV wanes over time, administration before the start of an RSV season is recommended to achieve optimal benefit and cost‐effectiveness [2, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Therefore, a detailed understanding of the correlations and timeliness of RSV surveillance indicators is needed to ensure the timely administration of prevention products, improve public health preparedness, and measure the impact of prevention products on RSV epidemiology, seasonality, and burden in the United States.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) characterizes RSV seasonality using the percentage of positive tests reported to the National Respiratory and Enteric Virus Surveillance System (NREVSS), a passive, laboratory‐based surveillance network that monitors viral activity [8, 9]. Since 2017, CDC has used a percent positivity threshold to define RSV season onset as the first of 2 consecutive weeks when the weekly percentage of positive RSV tests exceeds 3% [9]. RSV circulation follows a distinct, seasonal pattern in the United States, with activity typically beginning in the fall, peaking in December or January, and ending in the spring [8, 9, 10]. However, the timing of RSV epidemics varies across geographic regions, typically starting earliest in Florida and latest in the northwestern United States [11]. In 2020, RSV seasonality was disrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic and the typical season did not occur [12, 13]. The 2021–22 RSV season began much earlier and extended longer than prepandemic seasons, beginning in late spring and ending in winter. In 2022–23, RSV activity started to return to prepandemic seasonality patterns [13].

CDC monitors RSV activity and RSV‐associated outcomes using data from emergency department (ED) electronic health records, testing data from commercial laboratories, and RSV‐associated acute respiratory illness (ARI) and hospitalization data from sentinel networks. However, the correlations between RSV surveillance indicators across systems have not previously been evaluated. In this study, we described the populations, healthcare settings, and geography covered by five surveillance systems that monitor RSV activity and illness in the United States. Using data from NREVSS, we characterized national and regional RSV seasonality using a 3% positivity threshold. We then compared RSV indicators from each system (e.g., the percent of tests positive for RSV, the percent of RSV‐related ED visits, and RSV‐associated hospitalization rates) at key points during the RSV season. We also compared pediatric RSV indicators from each system to data from pediatric‐specific facilities reporting to NREVSS. To evaluate national and regional correlations of surveillance indicators across systems, we estimated the cross‐correlations and lags of indicators from each system compared to NREVSS before, during, and after RSV circulation was disrupted by the COVID‐19 pandemic.

Methods

Data Sources

We analyzed RSV surveillance data from five systems with distinct approaches, catchment areas, and indicators (Table 1). In NREVSS, participating clinical, public health, and commercial laboratories voluntarily report the weekly total numbers of RSV tests performed and positive RSV detections, which are used to calculate national and regional percent positivity. Results are reported each epidemiologic week as defined by CDC's Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report [14]. NREVSS surveillance years are 12‐month periods defined based on troughs in RSV circulation [13]. In this analysis, NREVSS data were limited to laboratories that reported RSV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing results for at least 30 weeks of the NREVSS surveillance year and an average of ≥ 10 positive PCR tests per week. The 2018–19, 2019–20, and 2023–24 surveillance years began in early July (epidemiologic week 27) and ended the following year in late June (week 26). Because the typical winter RSV epidemic in 2020–2021 did not occur and the 2021–22 epidemic began in the spring, the 2021–22 and 2022–23 surveillance years were defined as early March (week 9) to late February (week 8) of the following year [13]. Data from early July 2020 (week 27) to late February 2021 (week 8) and early March 2023 (week 9) to late June 2023 (week 26) were excluded from this analysis due to low RSV activity.

The National Syndromic Surveillance Program (NSSP) collects electronic healthcare data from laboratories, urgent and ambulatory care centers, EDs, and inpatient care settings to monitor a wide variety of health conditions. NSSP is a collaboration among CDC and state, local, and tribal health departments, healthcare facilities, and academic and private sector partners to receive near real‐time data from more than 7,200 health care facilities and 83% of US EDs [15]. Weekly percentages of RSV‐associated visits among all ED visits were calculated using discharge diagnosis codes among all patients and pediatric patients (< 18 years old) only. Commercial laboratory testing data were also obtained from NSSP and included PCR test results from two large commercial laboratories. Test results were aggregated by epidemiologic week based on test result date. Patient age was calculated in years by comparing the patient's birthdate and the test collection date. Geographic regions were assigned using the location of the provider or facility.

The New Vaccine Surveillance Network (NVSN) is an active, prospective, population‐based surveillance system that conducts surveillance for medically attended pediatric ARI in seven pediatric health systems. Data are collected through parent interviews, medical chart reviews, and systematic, multipathogen specimen testing for children < 18 years of age who present to an outpatient, urgent care, ED, or hospital setting with ARI. For this analysis, NVSN data were limited to patients who received inpatient or ED care. RSV PCR test results were aggregated by epidemiologic week using the patient's screening date.

The Respiratory Syncytial Virus Hospitalization Surveillance Network (RSV‐NET) is an active, population‐based surveillance system that monitors RSV‐associated hospitalizations in children and adults. Demographic and clinical data are collected for patients with positive, laboratory‐confirmed RSV test results within 14 days before or during hospitalization. Although the catchment area has changed over time, RSV‐NET captured RSV‐associated hospitalizations in 58 counties in 12 states covering approximately 27 million persons (about 8% of the US population) during the 2023–24 surveillance year [16]. Prior to October 2020, RSV‐NET conducted prospective surveillance only during the typical RSV season (defined as October to April). Year‐round surveillance began in October 2020 [16]. Unadjusted, weekly hospitalization rates were calculated using US Census population estimates for each surveillance area.

Weekly RSV surveillance indicator measures were calculated for each system. For systems that capture laboratory results (i.e., NREVSS, NVSN, and commercial laboratories), indicators included all‐age and pediatric‐specific (< 18 years old) RSV percent positivity, defined as the number of positive tests divided by the total number of tests administered and multiplied by 100. Because NREVSS does not capture patient‐specific age data, percent positivity was calculated among pediatric healthcare facilities that report to NREVSS. For systems that capture hospital‐based indicators, indicators included all‐age and pediatric‐specific percentages of RSV‐associated ED visits among all ED visits (NSSP) and RSV‐associated hospital admission rates per 100,000 population (RSV‐NET).

Indicators were assessed nationally using data from all sites and by US Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) region using data from sites within each region. Because RSV in Alaska, Florida, and Hawaii often circulates earlier or for a longer duration compared to the rest of the US, these states were excluded from regional analyses but included in national analyses when those states' data were represented in the surveillance system [10]. RSV circulation in Florida was assessed separately; data volume was not sufficient to assess seasonality in Alaska, Hawaii, or the US territories.

Data Analysis

We compared RSV surveillance indicator values from each system at the onset, peak, and offset of the RSV season as defined by NREVSS. RSV seasonality was characterized using a 3% test positivity threshold applied to NREVSS data, as previously described [9, 13]. Seasons were defined as periods with percent positivity ≥ 3% for at least 6 consecutive weeks. Season onset and offset were defined as the first and last of 2 consecutive weeks, respectively, when RSV percent positivity was ≥ 3%. The peak was the week with the highest percent positivity; if multiple peaks were observed within the same season, the earlier week was selected. Season duration was defined as the inclusive number of weeks between season onset and offset. In instances when percent positivity decreased below 3% for > 1 week before increasing to greater than 3% for > 2 weeks, we defined season onset using the later week and season offset using the earlier week.

We also estimated the cross‐correlations and lags between each system and NREVSS nationally and by HHS region during pre‐COVID‐19‐pandemic (2018–19 and 2019–20 NREVSS surveillance years), pandemic‐disrupted (2021–22), and postdisruption (2022–23 and 2023–24) periods in three steps. First, to allow for comparisons across surveillance systems, indicators were smoothed using a 3‐week centered moving average and normalized to the maximum of their respective prepandemic values. Second, we calculated Spearman's rank correlation coefficients (Spearman's ρ) using lags from −6 to +6 weeks over a 26‐week moving window. Third, correlations were averaged across windows; the lag that produced the highest mean correlation was selected as the overall lag for that period. Negative values (leads) corresponded to indicators that preceded NREVSS percent positivity, and positive values (lags) corresponded to indicators that followed NREVSS percent positivity. Pediatric indicators were compared to percent positivity from pediatric‐specific NREVSS facilities only. Analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4, and R statistical computing software (Version 4.4.1). This activity was reviewed by the CDC, deemed not research, and was conducted consistent with applicable federal law and CDC policy (45 C.F.R. part 46.102(l) (2), 21 C.F.R. part 56; 42 U.S.C. §241(d); 5 U.S.C. §552a; 44 U.S.C. §3501 et seq.).

Results

Seasonality

Five US RSV epidemics (seasons) occurred between July 1, 2018, and June 29, 2024 (Table 2). In 2020, RSV activity was disrupted due to the COVID‐19 pandemic and did not reach the 3% epidemic threshold in NREVSS (i.e., no 2020–21 season was defined). During 2023–24, the average number of laboratories reporting to NREVSS each week was 2.0 times greater compared to 2018–19, and the average number of weekly test results increased fourfold from 15,797 in 2018–19 to 65,981 in 2023–24 (Table 2).

Using the 3% epidemic threshold, national prepandemic RSV seasons typically began in October, peaked in December, and ended in March or April, with a duration of 23–28 weeks (Table 2). During 2021–22, the national RSV epidemic began 20–21 weeks earlier than prepandemic seasons and lasted 33 weeks, with a season onset in late May, peak in August, and offset in January. The 2022–23 RSV season lasted 22 weeks, beginning in late August, peaking in November, and ending in January. In 2023–24, the national RSV season closely aligned with prepandemic seasonality patterns, lasting 22 weeks from October to March, with a peak in November. Between 2018 and 2024, RSV season onsets began earliest in the southern United States (Table S1). NREVSS season onsets in Florida occurred an average of 5.8 weeks earlier (range: 2–10 weeks) than the rest of HHS Region 4 (Alabama, Georgia, Kentucky, Mississippi, North Carolina, South Carolina, and Tennessee), 6.8 weeks earlier (range: 0–11 weeks) than HHS Region 6 (Arkansas, Louisiana, New Mexico, Oklahoma, and Texas), and 11.6 weeks earlier (range: 9–16 weeks) than national RSV seasons.

Across all surveillance years, pediatric indicator values were higher than their respective all‐age indicators at RSV season onset, peak, and offset (Figure 1). These differences were most pronounced at season onset and in systems utilizing hospital‐based indicators. Compared to all NREVSS facilities, percent positivity from pediatric facilities reporting to NREVSS was 1.8–3.0 times greater at season onset, 1.4–2.2 times greater at season peak, and 1.1–2 times greater at season offset (Tables 3, 4, 5). Similarly, among pediatric patients, commercial laboratory percent positivity was 1.4–1.7 times higher at season onset, 1.3–1.6 times higher at season peak, and 1.2–2.0 times higher at season offset compared to percent positivity from all patients. The percentage of RSV‐related ED visits reported to NSSP was 5.3–6.3 times higher among pediatric patients than patients of all ages at season onset, 3.8–5.8 times higher at season peak, and 3.4–4.7 times higher at season offset. Within RSV‐NET, hospital admission rates among pediatric patients were 3.4–3.7 times higher at NREVSS season onset, 2.9–4.1 times higher at season peak, and 2.0–2.3 times higher at season offset compared to patients of all ages.

National Correlations and Lags

At the national level, normalized surveillance indicators were highly correlated across systems and time periods with minimal or no observed lags (Figure 2). Among all‐age indicators, the percent of RSV‐related ED visits among all patients reported to NSSP had the highest correlation with NREVSS (mean correlations: ρ = 0.99 and ρ = 0.98, respectively) at lags of 0 weeks during the prepandemic and postdisruption time periods; during the pandemic period, all‐age commercial laboratory percent positivity had the highest correlation with NREVSS (ρ = 0.97) with no observed lag (Table 6). Among pediatric indicators, NVSN percent positivity had the highest correlations with NREVSS pediatric percent positivity during the prepandemic and pandemic periods (ρ = 0.99 and ρ = 0.94, respectively) at lags of 0 weeks; pediatric commercial laboratory percent positivity had the highest correlation with NREVSS pediatric percent positivity during the postdisruption period (ρ = 0.96) with no observed lag (Table 7).

There were no observed lags between any percent positivity indicator and NREVSS (Tables 6 and 7). During the prepandemic period, all‐age and pediatric RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS all‐age and pediatric‐specific percent positivity by 1 week (ρ = 0.92 and ρ = 0.88, respectively). During the pandemic period, the percent of RSV‐related ED visits among pediatric patients reported to NSSP lagged NREVSS pediatric percent positivity by 1 week (ρ = 0.82), all‐age RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS by 2 weeks (ρ = 0.44), and pediatric RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS pediatric‐specific percent positivity by 3 weeks (ρ = 0.50). There were no observed lags between any hospital‐related indicators and NREVSS during the postdisruption period. Across all systems, mean correlations were lowest during the pandemic period, ranging from 0.44 (RSV‐NET) to 0.97 (commercial laboratories).

Although most surveillance indicators were highly correlated with NREVSS at estimated lags of 0 weeks, correlations and estimated lags varied across time windows within each period. During the prepandemic and postdisruption periods, national percent positivity from NREVSS pediatric facilities was highly correlated with all NREVSS facilities at lags of 0 weeks (ρ = 0.97 and ρ = 0.95, respectively) (Figure 3a); however, in some windows, correlations were highest when pediatric percent positivity led all‐age percent positivity by 1–2 weeks. During the pandemic period, the mean correlations were nearly the same at leads of 0 weeks (ρ = 0.87) and 1 week (ρ = 0.86). While all‐age RSV‐NET hospitalization rates during the prepandemic and pandemic periods had the highest mean correlation when lagged with NREVSS by 1 and 2 weeks, lags of 0–2 weeks during the prepandemic period and lags of up to 6 weeks during the pandemic period were observed in some windows (Figure 3b). After disruption, the estimated lag with highest mean correlation between all‐age RSV‐NET hospitalization rates and NREVSS was 0 weeks (ρ = 0.93), but RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS in certain windows by 1–2 weeks. These variations aligned with visual analyses of percent positivity and normalized indicators (Figures 1 and 2).

Regional Correlations and Lags

While there were no observed lags between NREVSS (pediatric), commercial laboratory, or NSSP ED indicators and NREVSS at the national level, in certain regions there were lags of up to 2 weeks during the prepandemic period (ρ = 0.70–0.90) and lags of up to 5 weeks during the pandemic period (ρ = 0.55–0.90) (Tables 8 and 9). In Region 4, NREVSS pediatric percent positivity led NREVSS by 1 week during the pandemic period (ρ = 0.71). Both national and regional RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS during the prepandemic and pandemic periods by 0, 1, or 2 weeks, except in Region 6 where estimated lags of 6 weeks were observed (ρ = 0.52 and ρ = 0.02). During the postdisruption period, RSV‐NET lagged NREVSS by 2 weeks in Region 6 (ρ = 0.53); nationally, there was no lag during that period (ρ = 0.93).

Regional variation was also observed between pediatric indicators and NREVSS pediatric percent positivity (Table 9). Although there were no leads or lags between pediatric indicators from NVSN or commercial laboratories and NREVSS at the national level, leads of 1–2 weeks and lags of 1–3 weeks at the regional level were observed. Nationally, RSV‐related ED visits among pediatric patients reported to NSSP lagged NREVSS by 1 week during the pandemic period; however, only Regions 4 and 8 had lags during this period. Similarly, while national pediatric RSV‐NET hospitalization rates lagged NREVSS during the prepandemic and pandemic periods by 1 and 3 weeks, lags of 0–2 weeks were observed in all regions except for Region 6 where lags of 6 weeks were observed (ρ = 0.49 and ρ = 0.01, respectively) during the same periods. Discussion

We evaluated the correlations and timeliness of RSV surveillance indicators from five surveillance systems that monitored RSV activity and outcomes in the United States between 2018 and 2024 (i.e., before, during, and immediately after the COVID‐19 pandemic). Seasons occurring after the beginning of the COVID‐19 pandemic in 2020 occurred significantly earlier than prepandemic seasons; the timing of the 2023–24 season suggests that RSV seasonality is returning to prepandemic patterns. Across all systems that collect data for all ages, pediatric indicator values were consistently earlier and greater than all‐age indicators throughout the RSV seasons. Despite differing surveillance methodologies, pediatric and all‐age indicators were highly correlated with minimal or no observed lags across systems and periods nationally. However, we observed considerable variation at the regional level and across time windows.

A previous analysis of RSV seasonality found that a 3% test positivity threshold prospectively identified a high proportion of annual PCR‐based RSV detections reported to NREVSS during epidemic periods of moderate duration [9]. Although this approach incorporates the total number of RSV tests performed, a 3% positivity threshold could result in prolonged RSV seasons for systems or populations with higher baseline RSV levels. This is likely because percent positivity varies depending on the population (i.e., pediatric vs. non‐pediatric), outpatient or inpatient healthcare setting, and testing practices (e.g., clinician‐directed vs. universal testing, use of multipathogen panels). For instance, in NVSN, a system that conducts universal, year‐round testing in pediatric populations, RSV percent positivity rarely drops below 3% for ≥ 2 weeks even during the typical offseason. For some NVSN sites, using a 3% positivity threshold would result in seasons lasting upwards of 53 weeks with no defined offseason between epidemics. Therefore, a 3% positivity threshold may not be appropriate for systems with differing diagnostic testing methodologies or populations, and programs could benefit from establishing their own thresholds using local data sources.

Differences in correlations and estimated lags varied by region, which likely reflects regional variation in the timing of seasonal RSV activity due to weather and climate, as well as differences in geographic coverage of the various surveillance systems [17]. Regional variation in RSV seasonality in the United States has been well documented [11, 18]. RSV epidemics during our study period aligned with historical geographic patterns, beginning earliest in Florida and the Southeast and later in the rest of the United States.

Subregional and substate differences in RSV seasonality have also been investigated [11, 19, 20, 21, 22]. When assessing RSV activity on a laboratory‐by‐laboratory basis, Mullins et al. concluded that the timing of RSV epidemics is specific to communities, rather than geographic region [11]. This is especially true in places like Southeast Florida and South Texas, where RSV activity typically begins earlier and can extend longer than the rest of the state [20, 21, 22]. Therefore, the geographic location of sentinel sites could help explain regional differences in correlations and lags across systems. For instance, in HHS Region 6 (Texas and surrounding states), we observed larger lags and weaker correlations between RSV‐NET hospitalization rates and NREVSS, but no lag or small leads between NVSN pediatric positivity and NREVSS. NREVSS had 46 reporting labs across all states in HHS Region 6 in 2023–24, whereas RSV‐NET has one site in New Mexico. In contrast, NVSN has a site in southeast Texas, which has documented early RSV activity compared to the rest of the state and region [22]. These findings also highlight the importance of understanding surveillance components when assessing local RSV seasonality.

Correlations and estimated lags also varied by period. We observed weaker correlations among most systems during the pandemic‐disrupted period, likely due to lower data volume during that period. Because there was limited RSV activity during 2020–21, the pandemic‐disrupted period was limited to the 2021–22 season, resulting in fewer weeks of data compared to the prepandemic and postdisruption periods. Heatmaps illustrated that correlations and estimated lags varied greatly by time window, even at the national level (Figure 3a,b). Because indicators may lead or lag at certain points in an RSV season, analysis of longer time periods (wider time windows) may mask additional within‐period and within‐season variations.

We noted key differences between systems during the postdisruption period. For example, normalized RSV‐NET hospitalization rates were up to 2 times higher in 2022–23 and 1.7 times higher in 2023–24 compared to the prepandemic maximum. While other systems experienced increases during this period, none were of the same magnitude. This is likely due to differential impacts of changing testing practices prepandemic and postdisruption in adult and pediatric populations. At the beginning of the COVID‐19 pandemic, several multipathogen panels that detect SARS‐CoV‐2, influenza viruses, and RSV became widely available [23, 24, 25]. Because unadjusted RSV‐NET hospitalization rates are dependent on clinician‐directed testing, the availability of respiratory panels, increases in testing capacity, and changes in healthcare‐seeking behavior (e.g., preferentially seeking COVID‐19 testing) may have led to increased incidental detections in patients with milder illness or in lower‐risk age groups. When comparing RSV‐NET hospitalization rates to other hospitalization rates, rates are adjusted to account for testing practices. However, other indicators used in this analysis, such as laboratory percent positivity and the percentage of ED visits associated with RSV, are calculated using denominators that account for some of these changes, resulting in less noticeable changes prepandemic and postdisruption.

Our findings have several limitations. First, RSV testing practices changed substantially following the COVID‐19 pandemic. Because the 3% threshold was developed prior to these changes, it may be useful to re‐evaluate the thresholds used to characterize RSV seasonality. Additionally, the number of laboratories that consistently report to NREVSS has changed over time, and retrospective testing data are sometimes added as new laboratories are onboarded. This variability could help explain some differences in lag across time periods. Second, the geographic representation of each system varies. While some systems receive reports from all states in a region, NVSN and RSV‐NET are sentinel systems that collect data from select counties or areas in particular states and may not capture variability in RSV circulation at the level of all jurisdictions. Third, because NREVSS does not capture patient age or exact test dates, our comparisons were limited to aggregated weekly data and pediatric‐specific facilities reporting to NREVSS, which may not be representative of trends among infants and young children at higher risk for severe RSV disease.

In most of the continental US, the recommended seasonal administration window for RSV monoclonal antibodies is October 1–March 31 [5]. However, these recommendations are intentionally flexible to optimize patient access and to account for geographic variability in the timing of RSV activity. State and local public health authorities and medical centers may elect to provide revised public health and clinical guidance regarding the timing of administration based on local epidemiology and feasibility of implementation. Because some indicators are more likely to lag others in certain regions and during specific time points within the RSV season, understanding the timing of leading and lagging indicators at a more granular level can help inform the timing of local prevention strategies (i.e., how early and late into the RSV season should prevention products be available) and enhance healthcare provider and system preparedness (e.g., hospital bed capacity, test availability, staffing levels) when seasonal shifts are observed. During the 2025–26 season, RSV activity started later and extended longer than recent, past seasons in most areas of the country. This seasonal shift was captured by national systems, with overall and pediatric‐specific laboratory test positivity data from NREVSS, NVSN, and commercial laboratories serving as early indicators of the shift. State and local partners also observed delays in RSV activity from these and other systems. Consequently, most public health jurisdictions extended their RSV immunization season through April [26]. By extending the immunization season using timely surveillance data, more infants became eligible for receipt of prevention products.

In this analysis, we evaluated correlations and timeliness among several complementary RSV surveillance systems that contribute key data to a national RSV prevention program. Our findings demonstrate that, despite differences in populations, healthcare settings, and geographic coverage, RSV surveillance indicators across these systems are highly correlated at the national and regional levels. Alone, laboratory‐based surveillance is insufficient in assessing RSV‐associated disease severity and additional data from systems like RSV‐NET are needed to capture key data points (e.g., hospitalization rates, patient‐level outcomes) for monitoring RSV burden. Surveillance data from these complementary systems, which each contribute different types of important insights into RSV activity and outcomes, provide valuable information for monitoring RSV seasonality in the United States. As RSV seasonality continues to shift and RSV prevention products become more widely available, a comprehensive understanding of RSV surveillance indicators and trends in the timing of annual RSV epidemics remains critically important.
