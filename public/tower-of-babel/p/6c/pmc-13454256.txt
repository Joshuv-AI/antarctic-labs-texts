An Electrical Capacitance Tomography Dataset for Image Reconstruction Benchmarking
Authors: Duanpeng Shi, Yuliang Wang, Xu Li, Tengchen Sun, Huaping Liu, Di Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We introduce ECT-Bench, the first large-scale, experimentally acquired dataset for planar Electrical Capacitance Tomography (ECT) designed to evaluate both imaging and dynamic tracking algorithms. The dataset contains 54,739 high-resolution mutual capacitance matrices acquired via a custom 3 × 3 electrode array. Taking advantage of a unified robotic pipeline, data was collected under comprehensive spatial variables, covering contact and multi-distance non-contact conditions (Z-axis permutations), alongside continuous spatio-temporal trajectories. Three representative materials (glass, resin, wood) and geometric shapes (circle, triangle, square) are thoroughly documented. Each sample couples raw capacitance signals with strict positional metadata and ground-truth object labels. Furthermore, ECT-Bench provides quantitative reconstructions comparing traditional physical solvers (Linear Back Projection, Landweber) against data-driven deep learning (CNNs). Unlike existing simulation-based benchmarks, ECT-Bench captures genuine soft-field characteristics, dielectric variability, positional uncertainty, and hardware noise. By openly releasing all sensor fabrication files, acquisition codes, and baselines, ECT-Bench establishes a standardized, rigorous foundation for bridging the gap between numerics and practical real-world touchless sensing.

Background & Summary

Electrical Capacitance Tomography (ECT) is a non-invasive imaging technique used to reconstruct the dielectric permittivity (ε) distribution of a target object. It operates by measuring changes in capacitance between pairs of electrodes. The principle is rooted in Maxwell’s electromagnetic theory: variations in ε affect the local electric field, leading to measurable changes in mutual capacitance. Since its introduction in the 1980s for industrial flow monitoring1, ECT has evolved into a versatile tool for biomedical diagnostics, material inspection, human-machine interaction and embodied intelligence2–5.

ECT systems typically adopt either circular or planar sensor configurations, each tailored to specific application needs. Circular sensors, with electrodes arranged around a cylindrical boundary, are well-suited for volumetric imaging tasks such as monitoring multiphase flows in pipes6. In contrast, planar ECT sensors feature a flat electrode layout, making them particularly advantageous for surface-based applications such as robot hand sensing7, object recognition8, and non-metallic non-destructive evaluation9 in flexible electronics and HCI systems. A comparative summary of circular and planar sensors is provided in Table 1. Compared to circular sensors, planar ECT sensors offer several distinct advantages. Their flat geometry allows easy integration into surfaces like walls, tables, textiles, or wearable devices. This design is more adaptable in environments where cylindrical setups are impractical. In addition, planar sensors are more sensitive to dielectric variations near the surface, enabling the detection of fine-grained interactions such as light touch or proximity. The regular electrode arrangement in planar designs also fits well with grid-based machine learning models, supporting data-driven approaches for image reconstruction and interaction analysis. While the sequential scanning inherent to mutual capacitance patterns results in a slightly lower temporal resolution compared to simultaneous excitation systems, this is a strategic trade-off for significantly higher spatial resolution and superior immunity to parasitic capacitance. The central challenge of ECT lies in solving the inverse problem, which aims to recover spatial permittivity distributions from limited and diffuse capacitance measurements. Because the number of independent measurements is small and the electric field is highly diffusive, the ECT inverse problem is inherently ill-posed and nonlinear. These characteristics result in low spatial resolution and reconstruction ambiguity. Analytical methods typically linearize the forward model using a sensitivity matrix to simplify the inverse problem. Classical techniques such as Linear Back Projection (LBP) provide fast qualitative reconstructions by projecting normalized capacitance changes onto the imaging domain10. Regularization-based methods, including Tikhonov inversion11, improve stability and resolution by introducing prior constraints such as L2, L1, or Total Variation norms. Iterative algorithms, such as the Landweber method12 and Newton-type schemes13, further enhance reconstruction accuracy by minimizing residual error through successive updates. Non-iterative methods are computationally efficient but prone to image distortion, whereas iterative methods yield higher-quality results at the cost of greater computational demand, making them more suitable for offline applications. Overall, traditional analytical methods face challenges in handling complex imaging scenarios and in achieving high-fidelity reconstructions.

To address these limitations, deep learning has recently emerged as a promising alternative for solving the ECT inverse problem. Several studies have applied machine learning (ML) techniques in this context. For instance, Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have been employed to map capacitance measurements to permittivity distributions14. Fully connected neural networks have been designed to enhance the imaging quality of LBP reconstructions by compensating for its deficiencies15. Physics-constrained deep learning frameworks integrate forward models to evaluate predicted permittivity distributions and iteratively refine the inverse mapping for improved accuracy16. Moreover, Conditional Generative Adversarial Networks (CGANs) have been proposed, in which capacitance measurements are transformed into image-like inputs to guide high-fidelity reconstructions17.

Deep learning-based methods have shown great promise in addressing the inverse problem of ECT and improving image reconstruction quality. However, the success of such approaches heavily depends on the availability of high-quality datasets for model training. Currently, most existing works rely on simulated datasets generated using finite element software such as COMSOL Multiphysics (v6.1, COMSOL AB)18. While this strategy facilitates controlled data generation with labeled ground truth, it often struggles to generalize to real-world measurements. The performance of trained models in physical experiments remains difficult to evaluate, and the simulation-to-reality gap poses a significant challenge for practical deployment. Although a benchmark dataset is proposed in 19, the real-world experiments therein only contain 14 capacitance vectors corresponding to 14 individual circular ECT sensor cases, which significantly limits its usefulness for training and evaluating data-driven models. To address these gaps, we present ECT-Bench, a comprehensive dataset of experimentally measured capacitance responses acquired with a custom planar ECT system. Methods

To enable the systematic collection of real-world ECT data of high quality, we design and implement an integrated data acquisition system composed of three primary subsystems: (1) a planar ECT sensor array with a custom-designed acquisition board, (2) a PC-based control and data logging interface, and (3) a programmable motion platform based on a motorized stepper mechanism. These components work in coordination to automate the measurement process and ensure spatially resolved, repeatable data collection under controlled conditions. In the following subsections, we first describe the ECT Data Acquisition System. Then we introduce the detailed structure and specifications of the planar ECT sensor, acquisition board and motorized scanning platform.

Unified Robotic Data Acquisition Protocol

To enable fully automated, precise, and highly repeatable ECT measurements across complex spatial configurations, we developed a unified robotic data acquisition system (Fig. 1(a),(b)). The hardware architecture comprises an ECT sensor array, a multi-channel capacitance acquisition board, a high-precision robotic manipulator, and a PC acting as the central control hub. The PC interfaces with the acquisition board via serial communication for high-frequency electrode excitation and capacity measurement, while directly commanding the robotic manipulator to execute accurate spatial translations. The test sample was mounted via a 3D-printed holder. The comprehensive acquisition protocol, formalized in Algorithm 1, operates under a unified pipeline capable of executing two distinct data collection modalities: Discrete Raster Scanning and Dynamic B-Spline Traversing. Discrete Raster Scanning (Static Dataset Collection)

For high-fidelity static imaging, the system executes a snake-grid discrete trajectory (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{W}}$$\end{document}W). At each designated spatial target (Ptarget) across varying elevation offsets (Zscan), the robot comes to a complete halt. Following a mechanical dampening delay (Tstabilize) to eliminate residual vibrations, raw mutual capacitance frames are sequentially acquired (Tcollect) and averaged by the PC to yield highly stable differential capacitance (ΔC).

Dynamic B-Spline Traversing (Dynamic Dataset Collection)

To capture spatio-temporal dynamics, the mode shifts to uninterrupted flight. The robotic manipulator glides seamlessly along dynamically fitted smooth B-spline curves (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathcal{W}}{\rm{spline}}$$\end{document}Wspline) computed from random spatial nodes. Simultaneously, the PC enters a high-frequency polling loop: it reads the instantaneous capacitance variation (ΔC) and queries the real-time exact end-effector pose (Ptcp) directly from the robot controller, appending strict time stamps (tstamp) for precise temporal synchronization. Throughout both modalities, this closed-loop, pure-robotic paradigm ensures perfectly coordinated sensing and motion. Sensor Hardware Overview

The planar ECT sensor used in this study consists of nine electrodes arranged in a 3 × 3 square matrix, as shown in Fig. 1(b). Each electrode is a 28, mm × 28, mm square, with a center-to-center spacing of 32, mm, yielding a 4, mm gap between adjacent electrodes. At the top edge of the board, ten signal interfaces are provided, including nine electrode channels and one shared ground, along with a dedicated grounding via to ensure effective electromagnetic shielding and stable electrical connectivity to the acquisition circuit.

The electrode layout is symmetric along the horizontal, vertical, and diagonal axes, enhancing sensing uniformity. The sensor is fabricated on a double-sided PCB, with both surfaces covered by a 45∘ copper mesh to improve ground referencing and suppress electromagnetic interference. To further minimize inter-electrode coupling and capacitive crosstalk, each electrode is surrounded by a 1, mm-wide grounded copper guard ring, which significantly improves the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and overall measurement stability. To validate the reliability of the collected dataset, a systematic characterization of the integrated sensor is conducted. The results (Fig. 1(f) 1)-5)) show that the temporal drift across all channels remains consistently below 0.5%, ensuring long-term measurement stability. The channel correlation matrix exhibits minimal off-diagonal values, confirming that the integrated SoC-sensor design effectively suppresses electrical crosstalk. Frequency analysis reveals a flat noise spectrum without periodic interference, reflecting the high efficiency of the hardware-level filtering. Furthermore, the narrow distribution of raw values across all channels indicates high measurement precision and uniformity. Collectively, these metrics demonstrate the robustness of the acquisition system in capturing high-fidelity capacitance data for scientific analysis.

Acquisition Circuit and Capacitance Sensing

At the core of the system is the TS3F605AQ5 system-on-chip (SoC), which integrates an ARM® Cortex®-M3 processor and a dedicated hardware module for high-resolution mutual capacitance sensing. The onboard 24-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) with capacitive-to-digital conversion (CDC) capability achieves sub-femtofarad sensitivity (resolution < 1 fF), enabling the detection of subtle permittivity variations essential for high-resolution imaging. The acquisition board provides 54 general-purpose I/O (GPIO) channels, allowing flexible configuration of transmitting (Tx) and receiving (Rx) electrodes. To preserve signal fidelity, the hardware incorporates synchronized differential excitation, hardware-level signal conditioning, and programmable digital filtering with oversampling. These design choices yield a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) exceeding 90 dB at scanning rates up to 500 Hz. To minimize parasitic capacitance and environmental noise, the TS3F605AQ5 SoC is integrated directly with the planar sensor electrodes into a compact, unified module. This proximity-based design significantly reduces the lead length, thereby preserving the integrity of the ultra-weak capacitance signals during high-speed robotic maneuvers.

The acquisition process involves connecting the planar sensor electrodes to the GPIO ports of the acquisition board. A sequential excitation strategy is employed: each electrode is activated in turn as the transmitter, while the remaining electrodes serve as receivers. For example, when electrode 1 is used as the transmitter, electrodes 2 through 9 serve as receivers; next, electrode 2 is excited and electrodes 3 through 9 receive, and so on. Figure 1(b) illustrate one measuring process. This procedure concludes with electrode 8 exciting and electrode 9 receiving, resulting in 36 unique electrode pair combinations per full scan. One scan cycle yields a 36-dimensional feature vector.

We evaluate both a conventional analytical method and a data-driven baseline for image reconstruction. As the analytical reference, we apply the Linear Back Projection (LBP) algorithm and Landweber method, which recover coarse object positions but suffers from blurring and artifacts due to the ill-posed nature of the ECT inverse problem. For the data-driven baseline, we implement a symmetric convolutional autoencoder that takes LBP reconstructions as input. The encoder comprises four convolution, batch normalization, and pooling blocks for progressive feature extraction, while the decoder contains four transposed convolution layers that upsample latent features to restore image resolution.

Spatial Information Fidelity in Static Scenarios

As established in Table 3, we benchmark traditional explicit inverse solvers, including Linear Back-Projection (LBP) and the iterative Landweber algorithm, against a data-driven Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). The metrics consistently demonstrate that the raw 36-channel capacitance signals encode precise spatial distributions of the targets. Notably, the CNN model significantly outperforms LBP and Landweber baselines across all subsets. For instance, in the challenging non-contact scenario (Wood), the CNN achieved an SSIM of 0.9322 and a PSNR of 22.1596, compared to the Landweber baseline’s 0.1237 and 11.3623, respectively. This substantial performance leap empirically dictates that the dataset’s capacitance maps possess rich, non-linear spatiotemporal correlations that deep-learning paradigms can effectively uncouple. Furthermore, the dataset exhibits strong consistency across different materials (Glass, Resin, and Wood) and varying acquisition heights, proving its robustness against environmental boundary condition shifts.

To intuitively validate the dataset’s capacity to drive advanced imaging algorithms, we present a comparative visualization across different sensing configurations (Fig. 4). Furthermore, these physics-based inverse solvers are critically sensitive to spatial distance, causing catastrophic imaging failure in non-contact scenarios. In contrast, the data-driven CNN models (Rows 6-7) successfully map the complex 36-dimensional capacitance signals to precise 2D cross-sections. Driven by the rich ground-truth labels provided in our dataset, the network effectively overcomes the soft-field effect, achieving sub-millimeter shape preservation that aligns perfectly with the target geometries. Dynamic Data Collection Validation

To assess the fidelity of data collected under continuous motion conditions, we compare the LBP tomographic reconstructions obtained in two modes: (i) discrete mode, where the robot pauses at each waypoint for 1.0 s and averages 50 sensor frames, and (ii) continuous mode, where the robot traverses the same B-spline trajectory at elevated speed and captures a single instantaneous sensor frame at each waypoint without stabilization delay. Specifically, the dynamic continuous data collection is performed by tracking the generated spatial waypoints using a predefined cartesian motion profile. The robot’s operational speed is governed by scaling down its maximum joint kinematic limits to a continuous scaling factor of 20%, where the robotic end-effector achieved an average cartesian velocity of approximately 15mm/s across the randomized B-spline trajectories. This velocity configuration facilitate a gapless, steady-state scanning motion, allowing the high-throughput ECT hardware to continuously record capacitance changes synchronously with the physical end-effector positions.

Limitations and Future Directions

While ECT-Bench provides a large-scale, experimentally collected benchmark for capacitance tomography, several aspects remain open for future extension. Material and geometry diversity. The current dataset focuses on three materials (glass, resin, wood) and three simple shapes (circle, triangle, square) under controlled laboratory conditions. While this design ensures systematic comparison, future expansions could include more complex or irregular geometries and materials with higher dielectric heterogeneity to broaden applicability in real-world scenarios.Sensor design variations. All measurements are acquired with a single 3 × 3 planar sensor configuration. Although this facilitates reproducibility, it does not cover the diversity of electrode layouts and array densities used in practical applications. Incorporating additional sensor geometries, larger arrays, or flexible substrates would further enhance the dataset’s relevance to diverse research fields.Non-contact sensing conditions. The present dataset includes both contact and non-contact acquisition. Expanding to variable distances, tilt angles, or dynamic approach/withdrawal scenarios would more closely reflect real-world touchless sensing tasks in robotics and HCI.Geometric Overfitting: A model trained only on simple shapes may struggle with irregular, high-frequency geometries. We address this by providing raw capacitance measurements, allowing researchers to apply data augmentation or physics-informed constraints to improve generalization.Dielectric Heterogeneity: Real-world subjects (e.g., a human hand) are multi-layered. While our current data focuses on single-material specimens, it provides the baseline signal characteristics needed to transition toward more complex multi-layered permittivity models.

Usage Notes

The ECT-Bench dataset is intended as a benchmark resource for both traditional reconstruction algorithms and modern machine learning approaches. We encourage researchers to use the dataset in the following ways: Algorithm Benchmarking for Inverse Solvers: The dataset provides paired raw capacitance measurements and high-resolution ground-truth labels. This enables objective performance evaluation across diverse reconstruction frameworks, including linear analytical methods (LBP), iterative optimization (Landweber), and end-to-end deep learning architectures.Multi-Task Machine Learning Training: ECT-Bench is structured for ready-to-use supervised learning. Beyond voxel-wise reconstruction, the direct mapping between capacitance vectors and material/shape metadata allows for high-level semantic tasks.Proximity vs. Contact Sensing Analysis: By providing synchronized datasets for both surface-contact and non-contact (at multiple heights) scenarios, ECT-Bench supports the development of robust sensors capable of maintaining accuracy under positional uncertainty. Researchers can evaluate how dielectric sensitivity decays with distance and design compensation algorithms for proximity-based tactile sensing.Reproducibility and Systematic Extension: To bridge the gap between simulation and reality, all acquisition hardware schematics, sensor fabrication protocols, and robotic control scripts are openly released. This allows the community to not only reproduce our benchmarks but also extend the dataset with new materials or geometric configurations under standardized conditions.
