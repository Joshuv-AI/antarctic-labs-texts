Combined effects of visual impairment and chronic kidney disease on all-cause mortality among middle-aged and older adults: a longitudinal, population-based cohort study in China
Authors: Hanyuan Ye, Ye He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveVisual impairment (VI) and chronic kidney disease (CKD) are highly prevalent and associated with increased mortality risk separately. However, their combined effects on mortality risk are not well defined.DesignLongitudinal, population-based, cohort study.SettingA nationally representative sample of community-dwelling adults aged 45 and older in China at baseline was recruited from the China Health And Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).Main outcome measuresAll the participants were classified into four groups based on the presence of CKD and VI. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Cox proportional-hazards regression analyses were used to determine the HRs of risk factors.ResultsA total of 15 054 subjects were enrolled. The participants with CKD and VI showed the highest mortality rate (19.22%). The risk of all-cause mortality was significantly greater in participants with CKD and VI (HR: 1.174; 95% CI: 1.055 to 1.293) than in those without CKD or VI, after adjusting for other potential risk factors. Among participants over 70 years old, no significant difference in mortality rate was found among the four groups (all p>0.05).ConclusionsCKD was a significant predictor for all-cause mortality, while VI was not an independent risk factor for long-term survival. Participants with VI and CKD were at an increased risk of all-cause mortality. The effects attenuated with age. Our results may reveal the underlying mechanism behind the VI–CKD–mortality association and reinforce the importance of preventing and treating CKD and VI to avoid premature mortality in middle-aged and older adults.

Introduction

More than half a billion people globally are blind or visually impaired.1 Blindness and visual impairment (VI) are most common among adults aged 50 years and older, accounting for more than 80% of people with vision loss.2 Due to the population growth and ageing, the number of individuals worldwide with VI and blindness is estimated to increase rapidly over the coming decades, with the number of individuals affected by moderate to severe VI expected to rise to 474 million, and blindness to 61 million by 2050.3

The relationship between VI and mortality has been extensively studied over the past few years. People with VI are at increased risk of falls, cognitive impairment, dementia, depression, disability and loss of independence, which all contribute to an increased risk of mortality.4 5 To date, many studies have been conducted to examine the effects of VI on mortality risk, yet with inconsistent results. Numerous studies showed a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality among visually impaired persons.5 6 However, there was also evidence showing that VI was not an independent factor associated with mortality.7 8 The conflicting evidence suggests that there may be other risk factors interacting with VI and contributing to mortality that need further study.

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a significant public health problem, affecting over 10% of the global population and carrying substantial socioeconomic burdens.9 CKD patients are at an increased risk of mortality due to the ageing population and other chronic risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension and obesity.10 This risk is further elevated for patients with End-Stage Kidney Disease, a terminal phase of the disease that requires renal replacement treatment such as dialysis or renal transplantation.11 The mortality rate of CKD has reached 1.2 million per year worldwide and is projected to rise in the ranking of years of life lost from sixteenth to fifth by 2040.12

Eyes and kidneys share numerous structural, developmental, physiological and pathogenic pathways, indicating that kidney and eye diseases may be interlinked.1315 A previous study has demonstrated the high prevalence and strong associations of VI with CKD, as well as their effects on mortality separately.16 As VI and CKD may be predictors of all-cause mortality, it is of interest to examine the combination of VI and CKD in its association with mortality. To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated the simultaneous effects of VI and CKD on mortality risk in a nationally representative sample. Hence, to address the aforementioned knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to estimate the impact of VI and CKD on mortality in a large, population-based sample derived from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), which may provide insight into the management of VI and CKD.

Patients and methods

Study population

CHARLS is a national population-based longitudinal study that was conducted among residents aged 45 years and older and their spouses.17 18 The social, economic and health circumstances of community residents were assessed to examine the primary health and financial impacts of rapid population ageing in China. The present study was a secondary data analysis based on deidentified publicly available CHARLS data; therefore, additional ethical approval was not required under the authors’ institutional guidelines. Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

The CHARLS uses a stratified, multistage probability sampling design to ensure its representativeness of the target population. The sample selection process primarily involves four stages: county-level units, village-level units, households and individuals. CHARLS applies sampling weights, including both household and individual cross-sectional weights, to account for the complex survey design, such as unequal selection and non-response probabilities. Using these weights allows researchers to produce more accurate and representative population estimates for the middle-aged and older population in China. Details about the sampling procedure have been published elsewhere.18

The national baseline survey for the study was conducted between June 2011 and March 2012 in 17 705 respondents from 10 257 households in 450 villages/urban, 28 provinces of mainland China. All participants received a face-to-face, computer-assisted household interview with a structured questionnaire and then followed up every 2 years. This study analysed data from the first four national waves of the CHARLS fielded in 2011 (wave 1), 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3) and 2018 (wave 4), expanding a total of 7 years. Anthropometric and other physical measurements, including height, weight and blood pressure, were measured by interviewers. Participants were also invited to a local office of the China Centre for Disease Prevention and Control or township hospitals for blood sample collection.

Assessment of VI

VI was assessed based on two self-reported questions in CHARLS that measured distal VI and near VI, respectively. Distal VI assessed participants’ ability to see things at a distance, analogous to recognising a friend from across the street (with glasses or corrective lenses, if applicable). In contrast, near VI assessed participants’ ability to see things up close, such as reading the ordinary printed newspaper (with glasses or corrective lenses, if applicable). The optional answers to both questions were ‘excellent’, ‘very good’, ‘good’, ‘fair’ and ‘poor’. Participants who answered ‘poor’ for either question were classified into the VI group, while the rest were classified into the reference group.

Serum measurements

Of note, 8 mL of fasting blood specimens were collected by trained nurses at township hospitals or the local CDC office after an overnight fasting of at least 8 hours. The whole blood and centrifuged serum were transported to the Chinese Centre for Disease Control at −20℃ and stored at −80℃. Afterwards, plasma glucose, lipids, glycated haemoglobin, serum creatinine and cystatin C were measured at the Capital Medical University laboratory.

Assessment of CKD

CKD was determined by either a self-reported history or an estimated glomerular filtration rate of <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 calculated from serum creatinine using the recently developed CKD–EPI equations.19

All-cause mortality follow-up

All-cause mortality refers to the death of patients due to any cause, which includes both internal causes related to the disease being studied and external causes such as accidents or other unrelated medical conditions. Participants enrolled in wave 1 (2011) were followed up in waves 2 (2013), 3 (2015) and 4 (2018). Both interview status (dead or alive) and the time of death were recorded till wave 4. The determination of death in CHARLS relied on the interview status of participants in follow-up waves, leveraging information obtained through proxy interviews with family members. For those with data on all-cause death, the survival time was calculated as the interval between the interview time in wave 1 and the date of death. If the exact death time was not available, the survival time was defined as the median of the interval between wave 1 and the specific wave with death information. For those who did not die during the follow-up period, the survival time was defined as the interval between the two interview waves. This study assumed that participants who were lost to follow-up were a random sample of the original study population.

Confounding variables

Information on age, gender, educational levels (primary or below, middle school, high school, or college or above), marital status (married or partnered, or otherwise), smoking (yes or no), drinking (drink more than once a month, drink less than once a month or none) were collected by trained interviewers with the 2011 CHARLS questionnaire. Missing values were replaced with means of the observed values. Hypertension was defined as blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg at baseline or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication. Diabetes was defined as a self-reported diagnosis of diabetes or a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥7.0 mmol/L or a glycated haemoglobin level of ≥6.5%. Dyslipidaemia was identified as total cholesterol≥6.22 mmol/L (240 mg/dL), triglyceride≥2.26 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol<1.04 mmol/L (40 mg/dL), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol≥4.14 mmol/L (160 mg/dL) or self-reported dyslipidaemia according to the Chinese guidelines on prevention and treatment of dyslipidaemia in adults.20

Statistical analysis

The Skewness-Kurtosis (SK) test was first conducted to test whether continuous variables were normally distributed. Normally distributed data were expressed as means±SDs and compared using Student’s t-test (for two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (for three or more groups). Non-normally distributed data were presented as medians and IQRs and compared using the Wilcoxon Rank-Sum test (for two groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis H test (for three or more groups).

In contrast, categorical data are presented as numbers (n) with percentages (%) and analysed using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test for group comparisons. The overall significance of univariate survival analysis was determined by the log-rank test using Kaplan-Meier analysis. Cox proportional-hazards regression analyses were used to determine the HRs of potential risk factors. All the participants were classified into four groups based on the presence of CKD and VI. The risk factors in the univariate model were selected based on the findings in group comparisons, whereas statistically significant variables in the univariate model were selected as risk factors in model 2. HR and 95% CI were given. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS V.21.0 (IBM).

Results

Discussion

Using the nationally representative CHARLS data, we examined the combined impact of VI and CKD on mortality by comparing the mortality risk among four different groups. Our results showed the highest mortality rate in the CKD (+) VI (+) group. VI was associated with increased mortality risk in univariate analysis, but not in multivariate analysis. On the other hand, CKD was associated with increased mortality risk in both univariate and multivariate analyses. Furthermore, CKD and VI seemed to have less impact on mortality risk among participants over 70 years old.

The significant association between VI and mortality in the univariate analysis disappeared after adjustment for other risk factors, indicating that VI was not an independent risk factor for long-term survival. Our findings were in contrast to many previous studies, which have shown a positive association between VI and mortality risk.5 A meta-analysis of 28 studies from 12 countries showed that the pooled HRs for all-cause mortality were 1.29 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.39) for visual acuity <6/12 versus ≥6/12.5 We propose that the differences in these findings may be due to study design, sample size, methodology, geographic environment, environmental factors and lifestyles. In addition, it may suggest that other potential risk factors may have played a role in the mortality risk in our study, which warrants further investigation.

The finding that CKD was an independent risk factor of mortality confirmed prior studies. The effect of CKD on mortality risk has been reported in many population-based studies in different regions and ethnic groups.2123 CKD is a progressive and irreversible process of nephron loss that can ultimately lead to end-stage renal disease.24 In addition, CKD is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease, which is a leading cause of mortality.25 Consequently, patients with CKD have an increased risk of death compared with those without CKD.

Our study showed that participants with both VI and CKD had the highest mortality rate. This finding suggests that VI and CKD may be interlinked, resulting in a combined effect on mortality risk. A growing body of evidence suggests a positive association between CKD and VI, which may be explained by multiple factors such as common risk factors, shared underlying pathogenesis and treatment side effects of CKD.16 26 Furthermore, the presence of VI among CKD patients implies a relatively late stage of CKD that is also associated with increased mortality risk. The prevalence of VI may rise with the increasing severity of CKD.27 As a result, participants in the CKD (+) VI (+) group had the highest mortality risk.

Interestingly, we found that CKD and VI seemed to have a less significant impact on the mortality rate among participants over 70 years old, indicating that their associations diminished with age. The deterioration of human health with age is manifested in the changes of thousands of physiological and biological variables. The declines and losses in physical and mental health functioning associated with ageing frequently manifest themselves in the form of multimorbidity, leading to death. Additionally, recent research suggests that mortality continues to grow exponentially with increasing age, which may partially explain the findings in our study.28

The strength of our study lies in its large population-based and nationally representative sample, as well as the detailed information on potential confounding factors. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to comprehensively evaluate the combined effect of VI and CKD on mortality. Additionally, we considered the same risk factors for VI and CKD and adjusted for these covariates in the multivariable Cox regression models to ensure unbiased results.

There are several limitations to our study. First, we used self-reported data to measure visual function, which may underestimate the actual burden of undiagnosed ocular diseases and introduce potential bias. Second, CHARLS did not use albuminuria to identify CKD due to logistical challenges and respondent burden, and information on the stage of CKD is lacking, which may undermine the accuracy of the diagnosis. Third, although we controlled for a range of potential confounders in our analysis, there may be other unknown or unmeasured confounders that could influence the results. Residual confounding may bias the estimated effects of CKD and VI on mortality. In addition, all-cause mortality included deaths from external causes such as accidents, which may not be directly related to disease status. Fourth, although we used a nationally representative sample for the analysis, the generalisability of the study findings may be limited to those enrolled in CHARLS.

In conclusion, CKD was a significant predictor for all-cause mortality, while VI was not an independent risk factor for long-term survival. Participants with CKD and VI had an increased risk of all-cause mortality, and the associations attenuated with age. Our findings underscore the need for public health initiatives that encourage early screening and timely intervention for CKD and VI. This will help inform public policy, improve public health planning and ensure effective allocation of limited healthcare resources to minimise the burden of these diseases. Future research exploring the mechanisms underlying the VI–CKD–mortality association, based on a more accurate classification of VI and CKD stages, is needed.
