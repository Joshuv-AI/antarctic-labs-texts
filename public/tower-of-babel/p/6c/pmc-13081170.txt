Lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide target ALDOA to inhibit growth and enhance gemcitabine efficacy in PDAC
Authors: Yan-Jie Liu, Fu-Ying Zhao, Jia-Mei Wang, Bai-Qiang Li, Shi-Yang Lu, Chao Li, Hua-Qin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains a highly lethal malignancy with limited therapeutic options. We performed a high-throughput viability screen in PDAC cell lines and identified lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide as potent candidates. In vitro, both compounds suppressed PDAC proliferation, induced G1 arrest, and promoted apoptosis. Mechanistically, proteomic and biochemical analyses identified fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A (ALDOA) as a direct target: lomitapide mesylate bound directly to active-site residues Arg-148, while lomitapide bound to active-site residues Lys-229 and Arg-148, lowering ALDOA catalytic activity. Consistent with this, oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) decreased, leading to reduced ATP production and energy stress-mediated apoptosis. The observed synergistic or additive effects with gemcitabine depend on both specific PDAC cell line and chemical form of lomitapide, underscoring complexity of personalized combination therapies and need to consider drug properties and tumor biology. These findings support therapeutic repositioning of lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide for PDAC.Subject areas: therapeutics, cell biology, cancer

Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is among the most aggressive malignancies, marked by late diagnosis, rapid progression, and profound therapeutic resistance. It accounts for over 90% of pancreatic cancers and is projected to become the second leading cause of cancer-related death by 2030.1 Despite advances in systemic therapy, the 5-year survival rate remains under 11%.2 This is largely driven by dense desmoplastic stroma, an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), and frequent KRAS mutations (>90%).3 Additionally, first-line regimens (FOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel) extend median overall survival only to ∼11–14 months. These limitations underscore the urgent need for innovative strategies that target PDAC’s distinct biology. Emerging approaches include stromal modulation (e.g., PEGPH20, FAK inhibitors),4 immunotherapy combinations (e.g., CD40 agonists combined with checkpoint blockade),5 and precision medicine for molecular subsets (e.g., KRAS G12D inhibitors such as MRTX1133, and PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutant disease). In this context, drug repurposing offers a complementary path by identifying indications for approved agents to accelerate translation.

Lipid metabolism is a critical driver of tumorigenesis and progression. Cancer cells reprogram lipid synthesis, uptake, and oxidation to support proliferation, membrane biogenesis, and intracellular signaling.6 In PDAC, dysregulated lipid pathways are well-established, and several have been proposed as therapeutic targets. However, pathway complexity and adaptive resistance remain major hurdles. Lomitapide mesylate (brand name Juxtapid), is an FDA-approved drug for the treatment of homozygous familial hypercholesterolemia (HoFH) with a well-characterized safety profile. It inhibits microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (MTTP), a key mediator of very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) assembly in the liver and chylomicron formation in the intestine.7,8,9,10,11 Clinically, it lowers cholesterol and triglycerides and improves lipid profiles.12 Its parent compound, lomitapide, has been reported to exert antitumor activity in various malignancies.13,14,15 These include AMPK/Beclin1-dependent autophagy induction in colon cancer16 and PARP14-linked suppression of drug resistance via DRP1-mediated mitophagy in multiple myeloma.15 Previous research has shown that lomitapide inhibits pancreatic cancer growth both in vitro and in vivo by targeting ZDHHC5.17 That study adopted a target-first research paradigm: it first identified ZDHHC5 as a potential antiproliferative target via single-cell transcriptome sequencing of pancreatic cancer tissues and subsequent functional validation (ZDHHC5 silencing suppressed tumor growth), followed by virtual docking screening that identified lomitapide as its inhibitor. In contrast, the present study employs a reverse research approach: first confirming the antitumor activity of lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide in PDAC, and then systematically exploring their downstream mechanisms to identify mediating targets. This represents a fundamental difference in research logic compared to the aforementioned study. The current study aims to comprehensively elucidate the molecular targets and related signaling pathways through which these two compounds exert their antitumor effects in PDAC.

Aldolase catalyzes the cleavage of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate and dihydroxyacetone phosphate during glycolysis. Fructose-bisphosphate aldolase A (ALDOA) is one of three isoforms (ALDOA, ALDOB, ALDOC). Key residues include Lys-229 for substrate binding and catalysis,18,19,20 while auxiliary sites such as Arg-148,18 Ser-271 and Asp-3321 support substrate binding and conformational stability. Regulatory residues, including Cys-72,20 Cys-339,22 and Gly-346,23 can modulate its enzymatic activity. ALDOA is widely expressed, especially in muscle tissue and erythrocytes,23 and contributes to muscle function, cell motility, and cellular ATP production.24 Tumors aberrantly upregulate ALDOA expression in multiple cancers, including PDAC, hepatocellular, and colorectal carcinomas, where it promotes tumorigenesis, proliferation, invasion, metastasis, and resistance to apoptosis.25,26,27,28,29 In PDAC, ALDOA has been implicated in carcinogenesis through the suppression of DNA damage responses and is a potential therapeutic target.30,31 To identify the potential molecular targets of lomitapide, we first performed preliminary drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assays in SW1990 cells, which revealed that lomitapide specifically stabilized ALDOA protein. Based on this initial experimental clue, combined with the antitumor activity of lomitapide in other malignancies and the glycolytic dependence of PDAC (driven by ALDOA overexpression), we hypothesized that lomitapide may act through ALDOA—rather than its canonical target MTTP—to suppress PDAC by disrupting glycolytic metabolism.

Herein, we show that lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide exhibit antitumor activity against PDAC. This occurs through a mechanism independent of lipid metabolism. Instead, they directly bind to ALDOA, inhibiting its enzymatic activity and disrupting glycolysis in PDAC cells. This ultimately induces cell apoptosis and suppresses tumor growth. This mechanism differs from their lipid-lowering action and supports the therapeutic repurposing of these compounds for PDAC treatment.

Results

Lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide suppress the viability of PDAC

A high-throughput drug viability screen identified lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide as potent inhibitors of PDAC cell viability (Figures 1A and 1B), and their chemical structures are shown in Figures 1C and 1D. Given that lomitapide mesylate is an FDA-approved drug with a well-documented safety profile, it holds significant potential for clinical translation and clinical application. We next performed CCK-8 assays, which confirmed concentration- and time-dependent growth inhibition in BxPC3 and SW1990 cells (Figures 1E and 1F). At 24 h post-treatment, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were as follows: in BxPC3 cells, the IC50 was 4.939 μM (R2 = 0.9664) for lomitapide mesylate (Figure S1A) and 4.087 μM (R2 = 0.9511) for lomitapide (Figure S1B); in SW1990 cells, it was 4.906 μM (R2 = 0.9495) for lomitapide mesylate (Figure S1C) and 3.740 μM (R2 = 0.9823) for lomitapide (Figure S1D). For subsequent experiments, we selected a treatment condition of 8 μM for 6 h, which resulted in approximately 60% cell viability. This sublethal concentration was chosen to assess the effects on long-term cell survival and proliferation. Under these treatment conditions, both compounds significantly inhibited clonogenic growth of PDAC cells (Figures 1G and 1H).

Lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide induce G1 phase cell-cycle arrest and promote apoptosis in PDAC cells

Cell cycle analysis (Figure 2C) revealed that drug treatment induced a significant G1 phase arrest in both cell lines, albeit at different time points (6 h for BxPC3 and 12 h for SW1990). Control BxPC3 cells showed a cell cycle distribution of 52.17% (G1), 37.09% (S), and 10.73% (G2/M). Treatment with lomitapide mesylate or lomitapide increased the G1 phase proportion to 60.63% (p < 0.001) and 59.44% (p < 0.01), respectively. No significant changes were observed in the S phase (28.25%/28.83%) or G2/M phase. Control SW1990 cells exhibited a distribution of 51.79% (G1), 31.38% (S), and 16.83% (G2/M). Treatment elevated the G1 phase proportion to 70.94% and 69.45% (both p < 0.01). It also significantly reduced the S phase proportion to 14.19% (p < 0.01) and 10.87% (p < 0.05). The G2/M phase remained unchanged. These results indicated that G1 arrest blocked cell cycle progression from the G1 to S phase.

Apoptosis assays (Figures 2D and 2E) demonstrated that after 24 h of drug treatment, the apoptotic rate of BxPC3 cells increased from 6.1% (control) to 73.6% (lomitapide mesylate) and 84.2% (lomitapide) (p < 0.001), and the apoptotic rate of SW1990 cells increased from 5.5% to 14.0%/17.2% (p < 0.05 and p < 0.01), with BxPC3 cells exhibiting higher apoptotic sensitivity to both compounds. A time-course analysis of BxPC3 cells (Figures 2F and 2G) revealed no significant apoptosis within 8 h of drug treatment, while the apoptotic rate began to increase at 10 h (12 h, 13.7% for lomitapide mesylate and 18.3% for lomitapide, p < 0.05) and peaked at 16 h (24.8%/48.7%, p < 0.01). These findings demonstrated that lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide induced apoptosis in PDAC cells, with BxPC3 cells displaying greater sensitivity than SW1990 cells.

Lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide suppress PDAC through mechanisms distinct from those involving lipid metabolism, autophagy inhibition, or the P38 signaling pathway

Since lomitapide and its mesylate salt are known to inhibit MTTP, we first assessed MTTP expression and intracellular lipid accumulation in PDAC cells. The Human Protein Atlas database indicates low MTTP expression in normal pancreatic tissue and PDAC cell lines (Figures 3A and 3B), which we confirmed by Western blotting in BxPC3 and SW1990 cells (Figure 3C). Oil Red O staining showed no significant change in intracellular lipid accumulation in either cell line under this treatment condition (Figure 3D). These findings suggested that the observed antitumor effects were unlikely to be mediated by MTTP inhibition.

Prior work reported lomitapide’s colon cancer activity via AMPK/Beclin1-mediated autophagy.16 In our PDAC cell models, lomitapide mesylate and lomitapide increased the protein levels of LC3B-II and p62 (Figure 3E). LC3B-II protein accumulation was further enhanced by treatment with late-stage autophagy inhibitors (chloroquine [CQ],32 NH4Cl, E64D, pepstatin A), which was consistent with impaired autophagic flux (Figure 3F). A StubRFP-sensGFP-LC3 reporter confirmed more autophagosomes and fewer autolysosomes (Figures 3G and 3H). However, neither early-stage (Wortmannin [WM]33 and 3-Methyladenine [3 MA]34), nor late-stage autophagy inhibitors altered the drug-induced loss of viability (Figures 3I and 3J), and rapamycin did not rescue viability (Figures 3I and 3J). Western blotting showed increased p-mTOR protein levels (Figure 3K) and decreased p-AMPK protein levels (Figure S2), supporting autophagy inhibition. Although P38 phosphorylation increased (Figure 3K), a P38 inhibitor did not reverse drug effects on viability (Figure 3L). Together, these data indicate that the antiproliferative activity is not mediated by MTTP, autophagy inhibition, or P38 activation.

Molecular docking supported direct binding of both lomitapide (Figure 4D) and lomitapide mesylate (Figure S3A) to ALDOA. The predicted binding pocket of lomitapide in the ALDOA substrate-binding domain comprises Ala-31, Asp-33, Gly-37, Ser-38, Lys-41, Arg-42, Lys-107, Asp-109, Lys-146, Arg-148, Glu-187, Lys-229, Leu-270, Ser-300, Tyr-301, Gly-302, Arg-303, Val-358, Asn-360, and Tyr-363 (Figure S3B). Among these residues, Lys-229, Asp-33, and Arg-148 are key for regulating ALDOA enzymatic activity (with Lys-229 as the core catalytic residue, and Asp-33/Arg-148 as auxiliary catalytic residues), while Lys-107, Lys-146, Arg-148, Lys-229, and Tyr-363 function as direct binding sites (Figure 4E). The predicted binding pocket of lomitapide mesylate includes Asp-33, Glu-34, Ser-35, Gly-37, Ser-38, Lys-41, Arg-42, Phe-79, Lys-107, Asp-109, Lys-110, Gly-111, Val-112, Arg-148, Val-150, Val-358, Asn-360, His-361, and Tyr-363 (Figure S3C). Among these, Asp-33 and Arg-148 are critical functional residues that assist ALDOA in exerting its enzymatic activity (serving as auxiliary catalytic sites), and Ser-35, Arg-148, and Asn-360 serve as direct binding residues (Figure S3D). These binding patterns indicate that both lomitapide and its mesylate salt occupy the ALDOA active pocket and interact directly with key catalytic residues, thereby disrupting the native catalytic conformation and effectively inhibiting enzymatic activity.

To experimentally validate this interaction, we performed DARTS assays. The DARTS assay results suggest that lomitapide can bind to ALDOA and enhance the stability against proteolytic digestion (Figure 4F). Moreover, lomitapide increased ALDOA stability in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 4G). Notably, drug treatment did not alter ALDOA protein abundance (Figure 4H) but significantly reduced its enzymatic activity (Figure 4I).

Public datasets revealed elevated ALDOA expression in pancreatic tumors (GEPIA; Figure 4J) and poorer outcomes associated with high ALDOA expression (TCGA and KM-plotter; Figures 4K and 4L). In a cohort of 90 paired tumors and adjacent tissues, ALDOA levels were higher in tumors and correlated with worse prognosis (Figures 4M–4O).

Given that ALDOA functions in the first phase of glycolysis,35 we assessed bioenergetics. Both compounds decreased oxygen consumption rate (OCR; Figures 4P and 4Q) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR; Figures 4R and 4S), accompanied by reduced ATP production (Figure 4T), consistent with impaired glycolysis and downstream energy metabolism.

To verify whether the drug effect depends on ALDOA, we knocked down ALDOA using ALDOA shRNA (Figure 4U). ALDOA depletion reduced proliferation (Figure 4V) and diminished the additional loss of viability upon drug treatment (Figure 4W), indicating that ALDOA inhibition is necessary for the full antiproliferative effect of the compounds.

The synergistic interaction between lomitapide and gemcitabine in PDAC is context-dependent

We evaluated gemcitabine responses with or without ALDOA knockdown. Dose-response curves showed reduced gemcitabine IC50 in both cell lines after ALDOA depletion (Figure 5A). Using SynergyFinder and concentration matrices based on each agent’s IC50, we calculated ZIP synergy scores (Figures 5B–5E). In the BxPC3 cell line, the combination of lomitapide mesylate and gemcitabine exhibited an additive antiproliferative effect, as indicated by its ZIP score of 7.918, which falls within the additive range of −10 to 10. In contrast, in the SW1990 cell line, lomitapide mesylate combined with gemcitabine demonstrated a synergistic antiproliferative effect, supported by a ZIP score of 10.433, which exceeds the synergistic threshold of ten. As shown in Figures 5D and 5E, lomitapide plus gemcitabine produced a synergistic antiproliferative effect with mean ZIP scores of 10.625 in BxPC3 and 10.934 in SW1990.

Discussion

The treatment of PDAC remains a significant global challenge. While previous studies have established the antitumor activity of lomitapide,13,14,17,36 our investigation extends this work by systematically evaluating both the parent compound (lomitapide) and its pharmacologically relevant salt form (lomitapide mesylate). Both forms effectively inhibited PDAC proliferation in vitro (Figure 1), confirming and extending prior findings.

Notably, Wang et al.17 recently reported that lomitapide exerts anti-pancreatic cancer effects by targeting ZDHHC5, a key palmitoyltransferase mediating palmitoylation of the anti-proliferative receptor SSTR5 in pancreatic cells. Their “target-first” paradigm identified ZDHHC5 as a potential anti-proliferative target via single-cell transcriptome sequencing of pancreatic cancer tissues, validated tumor growth inhibition by silencing ZDHHC5, and subsequently identified lomitapide as a ZDHHC5 inhibitor through virtual docking. Mechanistically, lomitapide blocks ZDHHC5-dependent SSTR5 palmitoylation, restoring SSTR5’s anti-proliferative function and inhibiting cancer cell growth in vitro and in vivo. This contrasts with our findings, where we employed a reverse research strategy (first confirming anti-tumor activity, then identifying targets) and identified the key glycolytic enzyme ALDOA as the direct target of lomitapide and its mesylate salt in PDAC cells. These compounds bind distinct active-site residues of ALDOA (lomitapide targets the core catalytic residue Lys-229 and auxiliary site Arg-148; lomitapide mesylate binds the auxiliary site Arg-148), inhibiting ALDOA enzymatic activity and disrupting glycolytic metabolism. This target discrepancy may arise from differing research focuses (palmitoylation signaling vs. glycolysis), lomitapide’s potential polypharmacology (simultaneously targeting multiple PDAC pathways), or altered target selectivity due to the mesylate group’s enhanced polarity. We note that molecular docking alone is insufficient to definitively assign binding residues; orthogonal target engagement assays and subsequent site-directed mutagenesis studies are required to precisely validate these interaction sites.

The relationship between lomitapide, ALDOA, and energy metabolism is central to our proposed mechanism. ALDOA catalyzes the rate-limiting cleavage of fructose-1,6-bisphosphate in glycolysis—an essential pathway for PDAC cells to produce ATP and biosynthetic precursors, even under oxygen-replete conditions (Warburg effect). Our data demonstrate that lomitapide and its mesylate salt directly bind ALDOA, reducing its catalytic activity by 15%–55% in PDAC cells (Figure 4I). This inhibition decreases glycolytic flux (reduced ECAR) and mitochondrial respiration (reduced OCR), leading to significant ATP depletion (Figure 4T). The resulting energy stress triggers G1 phase cell-cycle arrest and apoptosis, consistent with ALDOA knockdown mimicking drug effects (Figures 4V and 4W). These findings establish a direct link: lomitapide disrupts energy metabolism by targeting ALDOA, thereby inhibiting PDAC cell proliferation and survival.

These complementary discoveries underscore lomitapide’s therapeutic potential through multiple mechanisms. ZDHHC5 inhibition restores SSTR5-dependent anti-proliferative signaling, while ALDOA targeting disrupts energy metabolism—both pathways converging to suppress PDAC’s malignant phenotype. Future studies should explore their potential synergy, such as whether lomitapide-induced ALDOA inhibition enhances SSTR5 membrane localization by alleviating metabolic stress (or vice versa). Additionally, clinical samples should be analyzed to correlate ZDHHC5/ALDOA co-expression with lomitapide responsiveness, informing personalized treatment strategies.

Within 6 h of treatment, PDAC cells developed numerous vacuoles, indicative of metabolic stress or injury. Such vacuolation can be associated with autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum stress, or damage responses. These early morphological changes may represent a transient adaptation to the drug or the beginning of viability loss. In SW1990 cells, the 12-h treatment resulted in a clear G1 arrest accompanied by a significant reduction in the S phase population, effectively blocking G1 to S phase progression. In BxPC3 cells, a significant G1 arrest was already evident at the earlier 6-h time point. However, the S phase proportion remained unchanged at this stage. This suggests that the kinetics of cell-cycle arrest may differ between the two cell lines. Whether a longer treatment duration in BxPC3 cells would also lead to a subsequent depletion of the S phase population remains to be determined. It should be noted that this study focused on the phenotypic changes of the cell cycle via flow cytometry, and the expression profiles of cell cycle-related proteins (e.g., cyclins, CDKs, and CDK inhibitors) were not examined. Given that the cell-cycle arrest was observed under different experimental conditions (12 h for SW1990 vs. 6 h for BxPC3), supplementing the expression changes of these key regulatory proteins would further validate the biological relevance of the observed cell cycle block and enhance the robustness of the results. This is a limitation of the current study that we aim to address in future work. Furthermore, the observed cell-cycle arrest, particularly the blockage of G1/S progression, is often a precursor to apoptosis. This suggests that the drug-induced stress and growth arrest may subsequently trigger apoptotic pathways, ultimately leading to cell death, which aligns with the observed reduction in cell viability. Notably, while the current study demonstrates that the drug induces cell viability reduction consistent with apoptotic trends, the expression profiles of apoptosis-related proteins were not examined, which limits our understanding of the differential apoptotic responses among various cell lines. Apoptosis is tightly regulated by the balance between pro-apoptotic (e.g., Bax, caspase family members, PARP) and anti-apoptotic (e.g., Bcl-2) proteins, and the dysregulation of these key molecules is a hallmark of apoptotic activation. Therefore, supplementing the expression changes of these apoptosis-related proteins will help elucidate whether the divergent apoptotic effects of the drug across cell lines are associated with differential modulation of the intrinsic/extrinsic apoptotic pathways. This is a limitation of the present study that we intend to address in future work.

The primary known target of lomitapide is the MTTP.37 Consistent with Wang et al.’s findings, we confirmed MTTP protein to be undetectable in PDAC cell lines, which aligns with public datasets showing low MTTP expression in pancreatic cancer. However, the absence of detectable changes via Oil Red O staining does not rule out effects on lipid metabolism. Lipid pathways are highly complex, involving numerous enzymes, transporters, and signaling nodes. Oil Red O stains neutral lipids but does not capture dynamic lipid flux. Comprehensive multi-omics analyses of lipid-related genes, proteins, and metabolites are required to conclusively determine lipid pathway independence. Our data indicate that in PDAC cells, both lomitapide and its mesylate salt inhibit, rather than induce, autophagy. This contrasts with findings in colon cancer, where lomitapide was reported to induce AMPK/Beclin1-dependent autophagy.16 Neither pharmacological inhibition nor activation of autophagy modulated the drugs’ cytotoxicity, suggesting that their antiproliferative effect in PDAC is autophagy-independent. Importantly, this study did not exhaustively interrogate lipid metabolism, a gap that should be addressed in future work.

The interaction between lomitapide (both free base and mesylate salt) and gemcitabine exhibited distinct, cell line-dependent characteristics. In SW1990 cells (KRAS G12V mutant, metastatic origin), both forms of lomitapide synergized with gemcitabine, indicating that salt formation did not alter the underlying drug-target interactions or pathway synergy in this context. Conversely, in BxPC3 cells (KRAS wild-type, primary tumor origin), only the lomitapide free base maintained synergy with gemcitabine, while the mesylate salt demonstrated a merely additive effect. This discrepancy may be attributed to differences in the physicochemical properties of the two forms. The mesylate salt, designed for enhanced solubility and uptake, may be subject to differential transport or efflux by drug transporters in BxPC3 cells, resulting in suboptimal intracellular concentrations for triggering synergistic signaling with gemcitabine. Concurrently, the KRAS wild-type background of BxPC3 cells may amplify this disparity; compared to KRAS-mutant SW1990 cells, their signaling pathways likely exhibit lower dependency on the KRAS-MAPK axis, which lomitapide may indirectly affect. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the efficacy of lomitapide-gemcitabine combinations is co-determined by both the chemical form of lomitapide and the molecular phenotype of the PDAC cell line.

Due to constraints in experimental timelines, the costs of establishing animal models, and prior resource allocation, we have not yet conducted in vivo therapeutic studies. The in vivo microenvironment is considerably more complex, and factors such as drug pharmacokinetics, distribution, and host-tumor interactions may influence the manifestation of synergistic effects observed in vitro. Therefore, the translational relevance of our in vitro findings requires further validation. Future studies will include in vivo efficacy experiments, evaluating monotherapy with lomitapide (and its mesylate salt) and gemcitabine, as well as their combination, to systematically assess tumor inhibition and synergy. The safety and tolerability of each regimen will also be evaluated.

Collectively, this study systematically investigates the antitumor potential of lomitapide and its mesylate salt in PDAC. Our findings confirm that both compounds effectively inhibit PDAC cell proliferation in vitro, likely through a mechanism involving inhibition of the key glycolytic enzyme ALDOA, which triggers energy stress, cell-cycle arrest, and apoptosis. Although the two forms bind to distinct sites on ALDOA, their inhibitory potency against the enzyme and against PDAC cells is comparable, providing a rationale for selecting the more formulation-friendly mesylate salt for development. The observed synergistic or additive effects in combination with gemcitabine depend on both the specific PDAC cell line and the chemical form of lomitapide, underscoring the complexity of personalized combination therapies and the need to consider both drug properties and tumor biology. While in vivo validation is essential to fully assess translational potential, our data strongly indicate that lomitapide is a promising candidate worthy of further investigation via drug repurposing strategies for PDAC, either as a monotherapy or in combination with standard agents like gemcitabine.

Limitations of the study

Although this study provides mechanistic and preclinical evidence that lomitapide and its mesylate salt exert anti-PDAC effects by targeting ALDOA and enhancing gemcitabine efficacy, several limitations remain. First, all experiments were performed in vitro without in vivo validation, which fails to fully recapitulate the TME and host-tumor interactions; additionally, the cell line-dependent synergy between lomitapide and gemcitabine remains to be verified in in vivo models and clinical settings, limiting the translational potential of our findings. Future in vivo and clinical studies are therefore warranted to confirm therapeutic efficacy and clinical applicability. Second, we did not examine the expression of key cell cycle and apoptotic regulators, which limits our mechanistic insights into drug-induced growth arrest and cell death; further protein expression analyses will help clarify these downstream pathways. Third, lipid metabolism was only partially assessed via Oil Red O staining, and comprehensive multi-omics analyses (e.g., lipidomics, transcriptomics, etc.) are required to confirm the independence and specificity of the relevant pathways. These gaps will be addressed in future work to strengthen the clinical relevance and robustness of our findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell culture

The human PDAC cell lines BxPC3 and SW1990, and the human hepatocellular carcinoma cell line HepG2, were obtained from ATCC (USA). Cells were cultured in high-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Biological Industries) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Cell cultures were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. Only cells in the logarithmic growth phase were used for experiments.

Method details

Cell viability assay

Cells were uniformly seeded into 96-well plates at a volume of 100 μL per well. A blank control group containing only medium (without cells, 100 μL medium per well) was established simultaneously. After incubation in the incubator for 24 h to allow cell adhesion, subsequent drug treatment was performed. Cell viability was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biomake) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The formula for calculating cell viability was as follows: Cellviability(%)=[ODsample−ODblankODcontrol−ODblank]×100%. In the high-throughput screening assay, an array of 884 FDA-approved drugs was used. BxPC3 (3 × 103) and SW1990 (5 × 103) cells were seeded in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Compounds (10 μM) were added, and cells were incubated for 72 h. Candidate drugs that effectively inhibit PDAC cell viability were defined as those with a cell viability of less than 30% relative to the untreated control group.

Colony formation assay

BxPC3 and SW1990 cells were plated in 6-well plates at densities of 5 × 104 and 1 × 105 cells/well, respectively. After 24 h of incubation, cells at 60–70% confluence were treated with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, vehicle control) for 6 h. Subsequently, the cells were harvested, counted, and re-plated at low densities (100–200 BxPC3 cells per well and 300–400 SW1990 cells per well) into fresh 6-well plates. The cells were then cultured for 10–14 days, with the medium refreshed every 3–4 days. Once visible colonies had formed, they were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Colonies containing >50 cells were counted using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Cell cycle analysis

BxPC3 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at densities of 2 × 105 cells per well and 4.5 × 105 cells per well, respectively. After 24 h of incubation, the cells were treated with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 6 h (BxPC3) or 12 h (SW1990). Cell cycle distribution was analyzed using the Cell Cycle Assay Kit (KeyGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell apoptosis analysis

BxPC3 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at densities of 2 × 105 cells per well and 4.5 × 105 cells per well, respectively. After 24 h, the cells were treated with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 24 h. Apoptosis was then assessed using an Annexin V-PE/7-AAD Apoptosis Detection Kit (KeyGEN) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EDTA, 1% Triton X-100) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were resolved on 10% or 12% SDS-PAGE gels at 200 V for 60 min and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After TBST washes, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed with enhanced chemiluminescence (Tanon, China).

The following primary antibodies were used: LC3A/B, p-P38 (Thr180/Tyr182), p-AMPK (Thr172), AMPK, p-mTOR (Ser2448), mTOR, p-P70S6K (Thr389), P70S6K (Cell Signaling Technology); SQSTM1/p62 (BD); ALDOA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology); β-Actin (ImmunoWay).

StubRFP-SensGFP-LC3 lentivirus transduction and autophagic flux detection

BxPC3 and SW1990 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at densities of 2 × 104 and 4 × 104 cells per well, respectively, and allowed to reach 20–30% confluence. The cells were then transduced with StubRFP–SensGFP–LC3 lentivirus (Genechem, Shanghai) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. For autophagic flux measurements, transduced cells were trypsinized, counted, and re-seeded onto glass coverslips placed in 24-well plates at densities of 2 × 104 cells per well for BxPC3 or SW1990. After 24 h, the cells were treated with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control) for 6 h. Following treatment, fluorescence images were acquired using confocal microscopy. Red-only puncta (StubRFP+/SensGFP−) and yellow puncta (StubRFP+/SensGFP+) were quantified to assess autophagic flux.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) measurement

Cells (2 × 104 per well) were seeded in Seahorse XF24 24-well plates (Agilent/Seahorse Bioscience) and cultured overnight. Before measurement, the medium was replaced with pre-warmed XF assay medium, and the plates were equilibrated for 1 h in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) were recorded using a Seahorse XF24 Analyzer following the manufacturer’s instructions. The following reagents were added and adjusted to the indicated final concentrations: oligomycin (1 μM), carbonyl cyanide-4-(trifluoromethoxy) phenylhydrazone (FCCP, 1.5 μM), rotenone, and antimycin A (0.5 μM each). Additionally, glucose (10 mM) and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, 50 mM) were supplemented to the system.

Measurement of ATP production

Cells (5 × 103 per well) were seeded in ATP-detection-specific 96-well plates, allowed to adhere for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated for either 3 h or 6 h with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control). ATP levels were measured using the ATPlite 1-Step Luminescence Assay System (PerkinElmer) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were lysed, and the lysate was mixed with the assay reagent. Luminescence intensity was detected using a microplate reader, with ATP concentration calculated based on a standard curve generated using known concentrations of ATP.

Drug Affinity Responsive Target Stability (DARTS) assay

SW1990 cells were treated under the specified conditions and lysed using the method described in ref.38. Detailed procedures: SW1990 cells were lysed with M-PER lysis buffer, and the resulting supernatant was collected. The supernatants were incubated with DMSO, 100 μM lomitapide, or 1,000 μM lomitapide at room temperature for 1 h. Subsequently, enzymatic digestion was performed with Pronase at room temperature for 20 min to prepare the protein samples. The proteins were then separated by SDS-PAGE.

In-gel digestion and LC-MS/MS analysis: The gel resolved by SDS-PAGE was stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue or silver staining. Gel bands showing differential staining intensity between the drug-treated and control groups were excised, and the LC-MS/MS analysis of the resulting peptides was conducted by Novozymes.

Western blotting for target validation: Aliquots of the same digested protein samples were also resolved by SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked and probed with specific primary antibodies against the candidate target protein (ALDOA). Protein bands were visualized using HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and enhanced chemiluminescence.

Silver staining

Proteins from DARTS assays were resolved by SDS-PAGE and silver-stained using the Pierce Silver Stain Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Molecular docking

The crystal structure of human ALDOA (PDB ID: 2ALD) was downloaded from RCSB PDB and imported into MOE 2019. Missing hydrogen atoms and side-chain atoms were supplemented via the “Protonate 3D” module, with atomic types and protonation states (including manual adjustment of active site key residues) assigned under physiological conditions (pH 7.4, 300K) using the AMBER10:EHT force field. Non-essential crystallographic waters were removed, and rigid minimization was performed to eliminate spatial clashes. Lomitapide mesylate’s 2D structure was drawn in MOE, optimized to a stable 3D conformation via MMFF94x force field energy minimization, and hydrogen atoms were added under pH 7.4 conditions. The active site binding pocket was generated using MOE’s “Site Finder” module, with a 20 Å spherical docking box defined. Induced fit docking was performed, generating 1000 initial conformations via the “Triangle Matcher” algorithm. Conformations were scored with “London dG” and “GBVI/WSA dG” functions, and the top-scoring conformation was retained. Key interactions (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, π-interactions, charge-charge interactions) were analyzed using MOE’s “Ligand Interactions” tool, with 2D and 3D interaction diagrams generated.

ALDOA activity assay

BxPC3 (2 × 105 cells/well) and SW1990 (4.5 × 105 cells/well) were seeded into 6-well plates and incubated for 24 h. They were treated for 6 h with 8 μM lomitapide mesylate, 8 μM lomitapide, or an equivalent volume of DMSO (vehicle control). Subsequently, ALDOA enzymatic activity was measured using the ALDOA Activity Assay Kit (PerkinElmer) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Transduction of lentiviral vectors carrying ALDOA-targeting shRNA

Short hairpin RNA targeting ALDOA (sh-ALDOA) and a negative control (sh-Con) were constructed by Genechem (Shanghai, China). ALDOA expression was silenced in BxPC3 and SW1990 cells using the Lipo3000 transfection reagent. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at densities of 2 × 104 (BxPC3) and 4 × 104 (SW1990) cells per well, respectively. Transduction with sh-ALDOA or sh-Con was performed when cells cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum reached 20%–30% confluence. A mixture of 960 μL Opti-MEM medium and 40 μL HiTrans.P reagent was used at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 10. At 48 h post-transfection, a portion of the transfected cells was harvested, and knockdown efficiency was evaluated by Western blotting. The sense strand sequences were as follows: shALDOA KD1: GACCACCTGTTGGATGAGAAA; shALDOA KD2: GCAGATTGACGAGGTGGAGAA; shCon: CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG.

Drug synergism assay

Drug synergism was evaluated as previously described.39 Briefly, BxPC3 cells (3 × 103 cells/well) and SW1990 cells (5 × 103 cells/well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated overnight before drug treatment. Cytotoxicity was assessed for single agents (lomitapide mesylate or lomitapide) and combination regimens (lomitapide mesylate plus gemcitabine, or lomitapide plus gemcitabine) at specified concentrations. The concentration gradients for lomitapide mesylate, lomitapide, and gemcitabine were based on their respective half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). The following dilution schemes were used:

BxPC3 cells:

Gemcitabine: 0 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.2 μM, 0.4 μM, 0.8 μM, 1.6 μM.

Lomitapide mesylate or lomitapide: 0 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, 2 μM, 4 μM, 8 μM.

SW1990 cells:

Gemcitabine concentrations: 0 μM, 0.05 μM, 0.1 μM, 0.2 μM, 0.4 μM, 0.8 μM.

Lomitapide mesylate or lomitapide: 0 μM, 0.5 μM, 1 μM, 2 μM, 4 μM, 8 μM.

After 48 h of treatment, cell viability was measured using a CCK-8 Kit on a Cytation5 microplate reader. The inhibition index (100 – cell viability) was used to quantify drug effects. Synergy scores were computed using the online SynergyFinder tool (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi) with a response surface model and the zero interaction potency (ZIP) method. According to the ZIP model, a synergy score > 10 indicates synergism, a score between −10 and 10 indicates additivity, and a score < −10 indicates antagonism.40,41

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data from ≥3 independent experiments are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was indicated as ∗, p < 0.05, ∗∗, p < 0.01, ∗∗∗, p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗, p < 0.0001, and n.s. (not significant). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were applied. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was first conducted to assess overall differences between groups. If a significant main effect was detected (p < 0.05), Tukey’s post-hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons to identify specific group differences. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
