Microencapsulation of camellia seed oil in pea peptide-maltodextrin improves its stability, digestive release behavior, and cellular antioxidant activity
Authors: Jia Tu, Yuxi Hu, Jie Peng, Tingmin Qu, Zhihong Xiao, Jingjing Xiao, Sisi Liu, Zhou Xu, Yongjun Miao, Yan Zhong, Ying Wu, Li Wen, Changzhu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Camellia seed oil (CSO) is rich in unsaturated fatty acids; however, its application is limited by its susceptibility to oxidation. In this study, camellia seed oil was encapsulated in a pea peptide - maltodextrin (PP/MD) matrix by spray drying to form microcapsules (CSOM). The CSOM exhibited a mean particle size of 511 nm, high encapsulation efficiency, and good colloidal stability. In vitro digestion showed that CSOM exhibited a progressive release profile, with limited release during the oral and gastric phases and increased release during intestinal digestion. In H₂O₂-induced PC12 cells, CSOM significantly reduced ROS accumulation, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activity, and improved cell viability compared with free CSO. Transcriptomic, RT-qPCR, and protein expression analyses indicated that the cytoprotective effects of CSOM were associated with modulation of PI3K/Akt-related signaling and apoptosis-related proteins. Overall, PP/MD microencapsulation improved CSO stability, modulated simulated gastrointestinal release, and enhanced cellular protection against oxidative stress.

Introduction

In recent years, increasing consumer awareness of the close relationship between diet and health has stimulated considerable interest in the incorporation of functional lipids into food systems. Among these lipids, camellia seed oil (CSO) is widely recognized as a premium edible oil in Asia due to its high content of monounsaturated fatty acids, particularly oleic acid, as well as its potential health-promoting properties, including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities (Li et al., 2022b; Wang, Tung, Chen, Lee, & Yen, 2019). However, the high degree of unsaturation that underlies these beneficial effects also makes CSO vulnerable to oxidative deterioration, which may result in and loss of nutritional value during processing and storage. Microencapsulation has emerged as a promising strategy to address these challenges. By forming a protective physical barrier around lipid droplets, this technology can effectively shield sensitive oils from environmental stressors such as oxygen, light, and heat, while also improving their stability and enabling controlled release during gastrointestinal digestion. Among the available techniques, spray drying is one of the most widely used because of its high efficiency, low cost, and suitability for large scale production of powders with low water activity and good storage stability. In addition, the rapid atomization and drying process facilitates the formation of compact particles and can markedly enhance encapsulation performance and oxidative protection. For instance, spray dried fish oil microcapsules showed an encapsulation efficiency of 90.10%, higher than that obtained by freeze-drying (Cui et al., 2021). Compared with extrusion, complex coacervation, and freeze-drying, spray drying offers clear advantages in terms of scalability, product stability, and the encapsulation of lipid-based ingredients.

The functionality of spray dried microcapsules is strongly dependent on the properties of the wall materials. Among them, protein carbohydrate composites have attracted increasing attention because they combine the emulsifying and film forming properties of proteins with the gelling and barrier functions of polysaccharides. Such combinations generally improve emulsification, encapsulation efficiency, and stability under different processing conditions, including changes in pH, ionic strength, and temperature (Ahmed, Hu, & Batool, 2026; Wang et al., 2021; Wang, Shi, & Han, 2018; Zhou et al., 2017). Compared with single component systems, protein polysaccharide matrices exhibit more balanced overall performance. Polysaccharides can compensate for the limited barrier properties and environmental sensitivity of proteins, helping to maintain structural stability over a broader pH and under varying ionic strengths (Wei & Huang, 2019). Conversely, proteins improve the surface activity and emulsifying capacity of polysaccharides, which is particularly important for the encapsulation of lipophilic compounds, and may also contribute to enzyme responsive release behavior (Wang et al., 2026; Yang et al., 2025).

Previous studies have demonstrated the advantages of protein-carbohydrate systems in encapsulating hydrophobic bioactive compounds. Karaca (2020) reported that black pepper seed oil microcapsules prepared by spray drying with pea protein and maltodextrin achieved an encapsulation efficiency of 95%, with surface oil as low as approximately 0.8%. Similarly, a lotus seed protein-pectin system used for curcumin encapsulation produced smoother particles and showed high encapsulation efficiency, improved release behavior, enhanced thermal stability, and greater antioxidant activity compared with a protein-only system (Su et al., 2022). These findings indicate that protein-carbohydrate assemblies are not only effective physical barriers, but may also affect the digestion, release, and bioactivity-related performance of encapsulated compounds. Nevertheless, most existing studies have mainly focused on physicochemical characteristics such as encapsulation efficiency, particle morphology, oxidative stability, and in vitro release, while the biological activity and molecular mechanisms of encapsulated lipids delivered through structured protein-carbohydrate matrices remain poorly understood.

In our previous work, a pea peptide maltodextrin (PP/MD) composite system was developed for the spray-drying encapsulation of CSO, which improved encapsulation efficiency and oxidative stability (Hu et al., 2024). However, although the physicochemical advantages of this delivery system have been demonstrated, it remains unclear how the PP/MD matrix influences the gastrointestinal release and biological functions of encapsulated CSO. In particular, the relationship between microstructural characteristics and downstream cellular antioxidant responses has not yet been systematically elucidated. This knowledge gap limits the rational design of CSO microcapsules not only as protective carriers but also as functional food ingredients with defined bioactivities.

Therefore, this study aimed to systematically investigate the structure, digestion behavior, and antioxidant cytoprotective effects of camellia seed oil microcapsules (CSOM) prepared using a PP/MD matrix. Raman spectroscopy and transmission electron microscopy were used to characterize their structural features, while simulated gastrointestinal digestion was employed to evaluate release behavior. In addition, H₂O₂-induced PC12 cells were used as an oxidative stress model to assess antioxidant enzyme activities, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and glutathione peroxidase, in combination with transcriptomic analysis to explore the involvement of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway. The results provide new insight into the structure function relationship of protein-carbohydrate microcapsules and support the development of CSOM as a functional ingredient.

Materials and methods

Materials

Camellia seeds used in this study were obtained from Hunan Province, China, and CSO was extracted in our laboratory by cold pressing. Pea peptide (PP) was purchased from Shuangta Foods Co., Ltd. (Yantai, China). Maltodextrin (MD, CAS: 9050-36-6) was obtained from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Assay kits for malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Catalase (CAT) and reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kits were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals reagents were of analytical grade.

Microencapsulation

Camellia seed oil microcapsules (CSOM) were prepared as described in our previous study (Hu et al., 2024). The wall consisted of pea peptide and maltodextrin (PP:MD, 1:1, w/w), with a core-to-wall ratio of 2:1. The dispersion was prepared at 80 °C and pH 8.5, stirred for 30 min, mixed with CSO, and further stirred for 20 min. The emulsion was first homogenized using a shear homogenizer at 12,000 rpm for 120 s and then subjected to high-pressure homogenization at 50 MPa for two cycles. Spray drying was carried out using a Büchi B-290 spray dryer (Büchi Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland) fitted with a 0.5 mm nozzle at inlet temperature of 180 °C, a feed flow rate of 10 mL/min, and atomizing air pressure of 0.1 MPa. The resulting powder was collected and stored at 4 °C until analysis.

Physicochemical characterization of CSOM

Particle size distribution, optical microscopy (OM), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and Raman spectroscopy analyses were performed according to previously reported methods (Khan & Al-Thabaiti, 2019; Zhang et al., 2021). Detailed experimental procedures are provided in the Supplementary Information (Section S1).

In vitro digestion

In vitro gastrointestinal digestion, including oral, gastric, and intestinal phases, was performed according to the method of Meng et al. (2019) with slight modifications. After intestinal digestion, the digested samples was collected for fatty acid analysis as previously described (Otálora, Camelo, Wilches-Torres, Cárdenas-Chaparro, & Castao, 2020). The lipid extract was filtered through a 0.45 μm membrane, and the fatty acid composition was determined using an Agilent 8890/5977B gas Chromatograph-Mass Spectrometer (GC–MS; Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Extent of release

The release rate was determined according to a previously reported method (Wang et al., 2018). Free fatty acids in the digestion fluid were extracted with petroleum ether, and the amount released was quantified using anhydrous ethanol as described by Hu et al. (2024). The release percentage was calculated as follows:Released%=100×mOilMOil.where MOil represents the total mass of camellia seed oil in the microcapsules (g), and mOil represents the released mass of camellia seed oil (g).

Antioxidant capacity assays by chemical methods

The DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging activities of CSO, PP, and CSOM were evaluated, with GSH and BHA used as positive controls. Antioxidant activity was assessed according to the Chinese national standard GB/T 39100–2020, Determination of Antioxidant Activity of Peptides—DPPH and ABTS Methods. Detailed experimental procedures and calculation formulas are provided in the Supplementary Information (Section S2).

Intracellular fluorescent localization of microcapsules

For cellular uptake observation, CSOM was dual-labeled with FITC and Nile red. PC12 cells were seeded in confocal dishes and incubated with the labeled CSOM (1000 μg/mL) for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were fixed and, the nuclei were stained with DAPI. Intracellular localization was observed using an Olympus FV3000 laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Cytotoxicity assessment

Cytotoxicity of CSOM was assessed by incubating PC12 cells with different concentrations of CSOM (0, 1, 100, 500, and 1000 μg/mL) for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1–2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Cells cultured in medium only served as the control (100% viability).

H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage model establishment and cytoprotective effect assessment

PC12 cells (rat adrenal pheochromocytoma cells) were cultured in complete DMEM at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ according to a previously reported method (Wang, Wang, Chen, & Lv, 2016). Oxidative damage was induced by exposing cells to H₂O₂ (0–800 μmol/L) for 12 h, followed by incubated with CSOM, CSO, or PP at 100, 500, and 1000 μg/mL for 24 h. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay, with cells treated with H₂O₂ alone and untreated cells serving as the damage control and normal control, respectively.

Intracellular ROS and antioxidant enzyme assays

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the DCFH-DA probe (Beyotime, China) and observed by confocal microscopy. The activities of SOD, CAT, GSH-Px, together with the MDA content in cell lysates were determined using commercial kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. The total protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay kit (Beyotime, China).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from PC12 cells and sent to Wuhan Hope Group Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China) for RNA-seq library preparation and sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). DEGs were screened using the criteria of |logoffs| ≥ 1 and p < 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed, and a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed based on the STRING database.

RT-qPCR validation

RT-qPCR analysis was performed to validate the RNA-seq results. Total RNA was extracted from treated cells using lysis buffer (RLT) and purified with a commercial kit. RNA concentration and purity (OD260/280 ratio of 1.9–2.2) were determined using a microplate reader. Gene-specific primers for Mmp9, Igf2, Cxcl1, Itga11, Ace, and Lcn2 were designed based on rat mRNA sequences obtained from the NCBI database (Table S1), with primer design criteria of Tm values ranging from 57 to 63 °C, GC contents of 40–60%, and amplicon length 100–200 bp. cDNA synthesis and RT-qPCR were carried out on a real-time PCR system using β-actin as the internal control.

Western blot

Western blot analysis was performed according to a previously reported method (Cianciulli et al., 2016). Proteins were extracted from PC12 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China), incubated on ice for 30 min, and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for protein quantification. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After being blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST for 2 h at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PI3K (1:1000, #4249, CST), p-AKT (Ser473, 1:2000, #4060, CST), AKT (1:1000, #4691, CST), Bcl-2 (1:1000, #3498, CST), Bax (1:1000, #5023, CST), andβ-actin (1:5000, #AF7018, Affinity). The membranes were then incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL kit (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed at least in triplicate, and the results are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range test in SPSS software (Version 25.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Particle size distribution analysis (Fig. 1C) showed that CSOM exhibited a unimodal distribution, with a volume-average diameter of approximately 511 nm. Most particles were distributed within the 200–800 nm range, suggesting relatively uniform particle dispersion and good colloidal stability. The presence of submicron particle signals below 400 nm, together with the optical microscopy observations (Fig. 1B), suggests that particles may have originated from residual PP/MD wall material that was not fully involved in encapsulation. Optical microscopy images further showed that the microcapsules had a regular spherical morphology with generally well dispersion. However, slight adhesion and aggregation were observed among some particles, which may explain the primary cause of the slight tailing in the particle size distribution curve between 1200 nm and 2400 nm. Taken together, the microstructural and particle size results confirm the successful construction of a microcapsule delivery system with PP-MD as the wall material and CSO as the core material, showing good structural integrity and stability.

Based on the above structural characteristics, the microcapsules may possess favorable oxidative stability. Raman spectroscopy showed that, after encapsulation, the intensity of the C—H stretching vibration peak at 2919 cm−1 increased by 26% (Fig. 1D), suggesting tighter hydrophobic packing within the shell structure and the formation of a denser hydrogen-bond network. Combined with the continuous wall layer of approximately 80–120 nm observed in the TEM images (Fig. 1A), this structure may restrict oxygen diffusion and thereby contribute to improved oxidative stability (Teng et al., 2024). Previous studies have reported that similar protein-polysaccharide matrices can prolong the induction time of lipid oxidation by up to threefold (Li et al., 2024b). In addition, the complete Raman spectra obtained from the microcapsules further validated the successful encapsulation of CSO and the retention of its characteristic properties. Collectively, these structural findings suggest that the PP/MD wall material can chelate pro-oxidative metal ions, enhances the physical stability of the microcapsules and may contribute to maintaining the stability of CSO during storage and digestion (Mi et al., 2023).

Gastrointestinal release profile and fatty acid composition after digestion

Camellia seed oil microcapsules (CSOM) exhibited progressive digestive release behavior during simulated gastrointestinal digestion. In vitro simulated digestion results (Fig. 1E) reveal that the release of the core material from Camellia seed oil microcapsules (CSOM) follows a characteristic three-stage pattern. During the oral phase (0–10 min), no significant degradation of the microcapsule wall was observed, and the release of the core material was minimal, with a release rate of 1%, which was mainly attributed to the transient release of surface-associated oil (Zhu, Zhong, Chen, et al., 2019). During the gastric phase (10–130 min), pepsin partially hydrolyzed the pea peptide component of the wall material, resulting in a further increase in core release to 63.62%. These results suggest that the microcapsules maintained relatively good structural integrity under gastric conditions while allowing limited progressive release (Goyal et al., 2016). Upon entering the intestinal phase (130–250 min), the combined action of pancreatic enzymes, lipase, and bile salts substantially disrupted the capsule structure, and the core material was almost completely released after 2 h of intestinal digestion, approaching 100% (Li et al., 2024a; Liang et al., 2024). Overall, these results indicate that CSOM provided protection during the oral and gastric stages and exhibited progressively increased release during the intestinal phase. Compared with free CSO, the PP/MD wall material effectively delayed the release of encapsulated oil during the oral and gastric stages.

To further evaluate the effect of encapsulation on lipid digestion at the molecular level, changes in fatty acid composition before and after digestion were analyzed by GC–MS (Table 2). The results showed that microencapsulation affected the post-digestion distribution of several fatty acid fractions during digestion. In particular, the post-digestion content of short-chain fatty acids, such as butyrate (C4:0), increased from 1.60% to 4.60% after digestion in CSOM. Medium-chain fatty acids, such as C8:0, were also detected after digestion, indicating effective lipid hydrolysis. In contrast, the levels of long-chain fatty acid remained generally stable before and after digestion. The proportion of oleic acid (C18:1) decreased slightly after digestion, suggesting that interactions at the wall material–oil interface may have influenced the digestion behavior of the encapsulated oil. From a physiological perspective, dietary lipids are mainly digested in the small intestine, particularly in the distal duodenum and proximal jejunum, where bile salts promote emulsification and lipases catalyze the hydrolysis of triglycerides into free fatty acids and monoacylglycerols. These digestion products are subsequently absorbed by enterocytes and transported through specific metabolic pathways, with short- and medium-chain fatty acids being more readily transferred via the portal vein (Goodman, 2010). The structural disassembly and selective fatty acid distribution observed for CSOM are consistent with previous reports on encapsulated lipid systems delivered by biopolymer carriers (Fernanda et al., 2019). Taken together, these findings demonstrate, from both release kinetics and fatty acid composition analyses, that the PP/MD microencapsulation system exhibited a progressive release profile during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and influenced the distribution of fatty acid components after digestion. In vitro chemical antioxidant capacity

CSO has been reported to exhibit strong antioxidant activity and effectively scavenge free radicals and reactive oxygen species (Li et al., 2022b). To investigate whether the PP/MD carrier could preserve and influence the antioxidant activity of CSO, the antioxidant properties of different samples were evaluated by measuring their DPPH and ABTS radical scavenging capacities, as shown in Fig. S1. PP demonstrated relatively higher DPPH radical scavenging activity (Fig. S1A), which may be mainly attributed to the sulfhydryl groups of cysteine and methionine in PPI, as these groups can exert antioxidant effects through reduction reactions (Žilić, Akıllıoğlu, Serpen, Barać, & Gökmen, 2012). Both CSOM and CSO exhibited concentration-dependent radical scavenging activity over the range of 1.0–5.0 mg/mL, indicating that microencapsulation did not alter the intrinsic antioxidant properties of CSO and may have improved its interaction with free radicals. A similar trend was observed in the ABTS scavenging assay (Fig. S1B). This enhancement may be attributed to the improved solubility and dispersibility of CSO after microencapsulation, which likely increased its interaction efficiency with free radicals (Li et al., 2022a; Xiaoge Zhang et al., 2022). Overall, the PP/MD-based carrier preserved the intrinsic antioxidant activity of CSO and was associated with improved radical scavenging performance after encapsulation. This effect was mainly attributed to the improved dispersibility and solubility of CSO in the assay system, which facilitated its interaction with free radicals. A further contribution may arise from the intrinsic antioxidant activity of pea peptides, resulting in a possible synergistic effect.

Cytotoxicity and cellular uptake of CSOM

To evaluate the biological safety of CSOM, a CCK-8 assay was performed to assess the effects of different CSOM concentrations (0–1000 μg/mL) on the viability of PC12 cells. The results showed that, compared with the control group, no significant decrease in cell viability was observed at any concentration of CSOM. At concentrations ≥100 μg/mL, cell viability showed an increasing trend and reached a maximum at 1000 μg/mL (129.53%), indicating that CSOM was not cytotoxic under the tested conditions and may promote cell growth at relatively high concentrations (Fig. 2A). To establish an oxidative stress damage model, the effect of H₂O₂ on PC12 cell viability was investigated (Fig. 2B). Cell viability decreased in a concentration-dependent manner with increasing H₂O₂ concentration, and declined to approximately 69% of the control level at 550 μmol/L, indicating substantial oxidative damage to PC12 cells and approaching the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀). Based on the established model, the protective effects of CSOM, CSO, and PP against H₂O₂-induced injury were further compared (Fig. 2C). The results showed that H₂O₂ treatment markedly reduced cell viability. Compared with the damage group, the low-concentration group (100 μg/mL) showed no significant improvement in cell viability. However, at 500 μg/mL, cell viability in the CSOM group increased to 56.49% compared with the damage group and was significantly higher than that in the CSO and PP groups. At 1000 μg/mL, cell viability in the CSOM group further increased to 69.21%, which was significantly higher than that in the damage group (44.48%) and other treatment groups (p < 0.05). These results indicate that CSOM exhibits a dose-dependent cytoprotective effect that was greater than that observed for CSO or PP alone, suggesting a possible synergistic interaction between CSO and PP.

To investigate its mechanism of action, the intracellular distribution of CSOM was tracked using dual labeling with FITC and Nile Red. Immunofluorescence images (Fig. 2D) showed that the red fluorescence was widely distributed in the cytoplasm, with noticeable enrichment in the perinuclear region. This fluorescence distribution indicated the intracellular presence of CSOM. Considering that clathrin-mediated endocytosis is a common pathway for the uptake of nano-scale delivery systems, CSOM was taken up through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, although the specific uptake mechanism requires further verification (He, Zhu, Yin, & Yang, 2021). This observation suggests that CSOM can be efficiently internalized by cells and may facilitate intracellular delivery of bioactive components.

Overall, CSOM exhibits good biosafety and efficient cellular uptake under the experimental conditions and was associated with attenuating H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage. This effect may be attributed to the PP/MD carrier, which may improve the dispersion and intracellular of CSO, thereby contributing to the observed cytoprotective effect (Jwa-Jin et al., 2016).

Cellular antioxidant effects of CSOM

In this experiment, an H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress model in PC12 cells was used to investigate the protective effects of CSOM at different concentrations against oxidative stress (Fig. 3). H₂O₂ treatment induces severe oxidative stress in PC12 cells, manifested by a significant decrease in the activity of three key antioxidant enzymes, namely SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px. Meanwhile, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and malondialdehyde (MDA), a major end product of lipid peroxidation, increased markedly, ultimately leading to cellular damage. In contrast, CSOM exhibited strong antioxidant protection by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activity and reducing lipid peroxidation in a concentration-dependent manner.Fig. 3Biochemical analysis of oxidative stress markers: (A) SOD, CAT, GSH and MDA levels across treatment groups, (B) ROS levels across treatment groups, (C) DCFH-DA assay for total reactive oxygen species content.Fig. 3

Biochemical analysis of oxidative stress markers: (A) SOD, CAT, GSH and MDA levels across treatment groups, (B) ROS levels across treatment groups, (C) DCFH-DA assay for total reactive oxygen species content.

MDA is widely used as an indicator of oxidative stress-induced cellular damage. In contrast, antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, constitute an important cellular defense system against reactive oxygen species and help maintain redox balance. SOD, in particular, protects cells by scavenging superoxide anion radicals (Huang et al., 2024). Therefore, the combined determination of MDA content and antioxidant enzyme activities provides a useful means of evaluating oxidative damage and cellular antioxidant capacity. As shown in Fig. 3A, with respect to enzyme activity, compared with the control group (50.94 U/mg), SOD activity in the H₂O₂-damaged group decreased to 29.09 U/mg, representing a reduction of approximately 60%. CSOM treatment restored SOD activity in a concentration-dependent manner, and at 1000 μg/mL, the activity had essentially returned to the normal level. CAT activity decreased from 2.02 U/mg in the control group to 0.95 U/mg in the H₂O₂-treated group. Following CSOM intervention, CAT activity increased significantly with increasing concentrations, and at the highest concentration, it was slightly higher than that in the positive control (GSH) group. GSH-Px activity showed a similar trend, with significantly lower activity in H₂O₂-treated group (4.41 U/mg) than in the control group (11.46 U/mg). CSOM significantly increased GSH-Px activity at 500 and 1000 μg/mL, even exceeding that of GSH at the same concentrations. These findings suggest that CSOM was associated with enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities and reduced oxidative stress-induced damage in a dose-dependent manner. These results are consistent with previous studies on the effects of microcapsules against H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in HepG2 cells (Zhang, Ye, & Liu, 2026a), which also demonstrated a protective effect of camellia oil against H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage.

ROS levels are a key indicator of intracellular oxidative stress. In this study, ROS were detected using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe, whose fluorescence intensity is positively correlated with ROS content (Xuedi Zhang et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3B and C, the H₂O₂-treated group exhibited significantly enhanced fluorescence intensity after H₂O₂ induction, indicating substantial intracellular ROS accumulation. At the same time, cells displayed irregular morphology, an increased number of apoptotic cells, and the appearance of cellular debris, all of which suggested elevated oxidative stress and reduced cell viability. Excessive ROS is known to attack key biomolecules such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, thereby aggravating cellular injury (He et al., 2024). Following H₂O₂ induction, intracellular ROS levels in PC12 cells increased significantly. In contrast, CSOM treatment markedly reduced fluorescence intensity and improved cellular morphology, indicating effective scavenging of excess ROS and attenuation of oxidative stress. This finding is consistent with the results reported by Jiang et al. (2025) and agrees with previous reports showing that camellia seed oil alleviates oxidative stress injury in RAW264.7 and HepG2 cells by reducing ROS accumulation (Zhang et al., 2026b). Together, these results suggest that CSOM protected PC12 cells against ROS-mediated oxidative damage by lowering intracellular ROS levels and maintaining cellular integrity.

In summary, CSOM effectively alleviated H₂O₂-induced oxidative stress in PC12 cells. Pretreatment with CSOM improved cell viability and morphology, increased the activities of SOD, GSH-Px, and CAT, and reduced MDA levels. These results suggest that CSOM enhances cellular antioxidant defenses and attenuated oxidative damage in PC12 cells.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals the potential involvement of the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway

Transcriptomic analysis provided systematic evidence for the regulatory effects of CSOM on gene expression in PC12 cells under oxidative stress. Principal component analysis demonstrated good experimental reproducibility and showed that H₂O₂ treatment induced substantial transcriptional alterations (Fig. S2A, and Fig. 4A). Differential expression analysis identified 1196 significantly altered genes in H₂O₂-injured cells compared with the control group, indicating widespread disruption of transcriptional homeostasis. CSOM intervention effectively reversed this trend, suggesting its ability to restore gene expression patterns toward a normal state and promote transcriptional recovery after oxidative damage (Xiong et al., 2024). Venn diagram analysis further identified 154 overlapping differentially expressed genes among the comparison groups, including key regulators such as Igf2 and Mmp9, which are involved in oxidative stress responses and repair processes (Fig. S2B). Igf2 is a growth-regulating factor involved in embryonic development, cell proliferation, growth, and differentiation (Çiçek, 2023). Functional enrichment analysis further elucidated the biological processes modulated by CSOM treatment. Gene Ontology analysis revealed significant enrichment of growth factor activity, integrin binding, phosphatidylinositol binding, inflammation-related regulation, and calcium ion homeostasis (Fig. 4B), all of which are closely associated with cellular defense against oxidative stress (Wen, Jiang, Zhou, Bi, & Yang, 2021). Meanwhile, KEGG pathway analysis identified several pathways associated with CSOM treatment, among which the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway was the most significantly enriched (Fig. 4C). This pathway, together with the concurrent enrichment of the Rap1, MAPK, and Ras signaling pathways, may reflect a coordinated signaling response associated with cellular survival under oxidative stress conditions (Lin et al., 2023; Renzhi et al., 2022; Yu et al., 2021).

Among these, integrin-phosphoinositide signaling represents an important cellular mechanism linking the extracellular matrix to intracellular signaling pathways. By recruiting and activating effector proteins containing specific binding domains, it plays a critical role in cell adhesion, migration, proliferation, and cytoskeletal reorganization (Sun et al., 2005). Inflammation suppression is another key regulatory process for maintaining immune homeostasis, as it limits excessive or persistent inflammatory responses through anti-inflammatory cytokines, specific signaling pathways, or negative regulators, thereby preventing tissue damage, promoting tissue repair, and modulating immune function (Xu et al., 2024). In addition, Chen et al. reported that sodium hydrosulfide alleviated apoptosis both in vivo and in vitro in association with activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway, enhanced antioxidant activity, and mitochondrial protection (Chen et al., 2017). Collectively, these biological processes and pathways are closely associated with oxidative stress regulation.

The protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis identified 25 core hub genes within this protective network, with genes such as Igf2, Itga11, Csf3, Cxcl1, and Cxcl3 playing important roles in extracellular matrix signaling and the regulation of cellular functions (Fig. 4D). These genes are mainly involved in inflammatory responses and immune cell recruitment (Cxcl1, Cxcl3, Csf3, and Lcn2), tissue microenvironment remodeling (Mmp9, and Itga11), and systemic growth and blood pressure regulation (Igf2, Ace). Notably, an eight-gene module within the PI3K/Akt pathway was enriched in genes previously reported to be associated with anti-apoptotic functions. This observation was consistent with the cellular experiments showing reduced apoptosis in H₂O₂-treated cells following CSOM treatment (Yanqing et al., 2020; Zhao et al., 2024). Collectively, these transcriptomic findings suggest that the antioxidant mechanism of CSOM may extend beyond simple physical protection and involve active biological regulation. The controlled release of bioactive unsaturated fatty acids from the microcapsule structure may be associated with modulation of the PI3K/AKT related signaling and other oxidative stress-responsive pathways, thereby potentially contributing to the cytoprotective effects observed in cellular models (Jinshan et al., 2023; Renzhi et al., 2022). However, the causal role of PI3K/Akt signaling in mediating the protective effects of CSOM requires further verification through pathway inhibition or gene intervention studies.

Validation of PI3K-Akt signaling pathway related changes induced by CSOM

To validate transcriptomic predictions, the expression of key genes within the PI3K/Akt network was further verified via RT-qPCR. The results showed that CSOM treatment significantly upregulated Igf2 expression, while downregulating Cxcl1, Lcn2, Mmp9, Itga11, and Ace in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 5A). These genes are involved in oxidative stress responses and inflammatory signaling and are closely associated with the PI3K/Akt and Rap1 signaling pathways (Moe et al., 2021). The potential involvement of the PI3K/Akt pathway was further evaluated at the protein level. Western blot analysis revealed that CSOM treatment was associated with reversal of the H₂O₂-induced suppression of PI3K and phosphorylated Akt (p-Akt). In addition, CSOM treatment was associated with increased expression of the anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 and decreased expression of the pro-apoptotic protein Bax (Fig. 5B and C). The restoration of this pro-survival signaling pathway, together with modulation of the rebalancing of the Bcl-2/Bax axis, was associated with reduced apoptosis and cytoprotective effects observed following CSOM treatment. Consistent with the findings of Zhong et al. (2016), the mRNA expression levels of PI3K, AKT, Bad, and Bcl-xL were significantly increased after LPS induction, whereas curcumin treatment markedly alleviated LPS-induced hepatocyte apoptosis by modulating the expression of these genes. Similarly, Ma et al. (2024) demonstrated that 18β-glycyrrhetinic acid increased the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and Akt as well as the protein expression levels of Bcl-2, while decreasing the levels of cytochrome c and p53. These changes were associated with activation of PI3K/Akt-related signaling and protection against oxidative damage and apoptosis. Although these studies support a potential role of PI3K/Akt signaling in cellular protection, the causal involvement of this pathway in the effects of CSOM was not directly examined in the present study. Collectively, these findings suggest that the sustained release of bioactive components from CSOM may be associated with modulation of PI3K/Akt-related signaling, thereby contributing to a coordinated cellular defense response.

Based on molecular and cellular evidence obtained in this study, a putative mechanism model is proposed to explain how CSOM alleviates H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage in PC12 cells (Fig. 5D). The bioactive components released from CSOM may be associated with modulation of PI3K/Akt signaling and related downstream responses, thereby contributing to the cytoprotective effects observed in PC12 cells. This potential mechanism may involve regulation of apoptosis related proteins, including increased Bcl-2 and reduced Bax expression, which may help maintain mitochondrial membrane integrity. In addition, CSOM treatment was associated with increased activities of endogenous antioxidant enzymes, including SOD, CAT, and GSH-Px, which may collectively contribute to the attenuation of oxidative stress. However, the causal role of PI3K/Akt signaling in mediating the protective effects of CSOM requires further verification through pathway inhibition, gene knockdown, or rescue experiments.

Conclusion

In this study, camellia seed oil microcapsules (CSOM) were successfully prepared using a pea peptide–maltodextrin (PP/MD) delivery system. The resulting microcapsules exhibited favorable physicochemical properties and a progressive release profile during simulated gastrointestinal digestion. CSOM showed limited release during the oral and gastric phases and progressively increased release during the intestinal phase.

Cellular experiments demonstrated that CSOM effectively alleviated H₂O₂-induced oxidative damage in PC12 cells, as evidenced by improved cell viability, reduced ROS accumulation, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, and decreased lipid peroxidation. Transcriptomic, RT-qPCR, and Western blot analyses consistently indicated that CSOM treatment was associated with modulation of PI3K/Akt-related signaling and apoptosis-related proteins.

Collectively, these findings suggest that PP/MD microencapsulation enhanced the physicochemical stability of CSO, while CSOM exhibited a progressive release profile during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and was associated with enhanced cellular protection against oxidative stress. Further studies are required to clarify the causal role of PI3K/Akt signaling and to further evaluate the influence of microencapsulation on gastrointestinal release behavior using appropriate comparative approaches.
