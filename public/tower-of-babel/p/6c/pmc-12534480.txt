Hierarchical sparse Bayesian learning with adaptive Laplacian prior for single image super-resolution
Authors: Mingming Qi, Yue Zhou, Yiwei Hu, Cuihua Xie, Shuhua Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-image super-resolution (SISR) continues to face difficulties in reconstructing perceptually critical details from degraded low-resolution observations. While conventional Bayesian approaches utilizing Relevance Vector Machines (RVMs) provide probabilistic interpretations, their reliance on fixed blur kernel definitions and homogeneous pixel dependency models often yields artifacts in complex scenarios. To resolve these issues, this study introduces a hierarchical Bayesian architecture enhanced by an adaptive Laplacian prior, which extends the sparse Bayesian learning (SBL) paradigm. Diverging from traditional Gaussian-based frameworks, our method employs sparsity-inducing regularization to selectively prioritize structurally salient regions (e.g., edge discontinuities, texture boundaries), while dynamically quantifying reconstruction uncertainty through pixel-wise variance analysis. Additionally, a spatially adaptive optimization mechanism is designed to streamline computational workflows without compromising restoration fidelity. Evaluations across multiple benchmarks confirm the framework’s advantages: it surpasses existing state-of-the-art techniques in both quantitative metrics (PSNR, SSIM) and qualitative assessments, demonstrating superior artifact suppression in high-frequency domains. Comparative analyses against recent state-of-the-art models further validate its capability to harmonize sparse representation with structural coherence.Keywords: Single image super-resolution (SISR), Sparse bayesian learning (SBL), Adaptive laplacian prior, Hierarchical bayesian model, Pixel variance estimation, Spatial alternating optimizationSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

SISR represents a classical research topic within the field of computer vision, which has garnered significant attention in recent years. The objective of this research is to reconstruct HR images from their LR counterparts1. HR images are crucial in various applications, including biometric recognition, medical imaging, satellite remote sensing, billboard monitors, guidance technology, optical-electric measurement, and advanced interactive interfaces2. While extensive research has been conducted on SISR methodologies over the past few decades, research on efficient SR methodologies remains challenging due to issues such as blurring (geometric transformation), deformation, interpolation parameters, and noise-induced detail blurring3. Current SISR techniques exhibit three principal constraints: First, non-blind algorithms depend on precise blur kernel specifications, whereas blind techniques demonstrate limited generalization capability when handling intricate degradation patterns4,5. Second, predetermined sparse priors such as Gaussian distributions6 show insufficient flexibility for varying texture characteristics. Third, Bayesian frameworks continue to encounter efficiency challenges in computational implementation7–9.

Recently, a growing contingent of scholars have turned their attention to SISR based on sparse representation. Essentially, SISR can be categorized into three types: non-blind SR approaches, blind SR approaches, and learning-based algorithms. Blind SR techniques operate under the premise that both HR images and blur kernels are unknown, whereas non-blind techniques presuppose that only HR images are unknown, with the blur kernel being known. Non-blind SR methods can effectively enhance LR images affected by various blur kernels without the need for training, thus yielding superior SR results. However, in natural images, the blur kernel is often unknown or challenging to align with the ideal blur kernel assumption, thus constraining the applicability of this approach. Blind SR methods exhibit robust generalizability for SISR tasks, as they analyze the mapping rules from LR to HR images across numerous training samples4,5. Under this assumption, both the training and test sample sets share identical blur kernels, implying that the learning outcomes from the training datasets are transferable to the test datasets. Currently, machine learning methodologies are frequently utilized in SISR algorithms for the processes of learning and inference. Nevertheless, many of these methods primarily concentrate on image deconvolution and are heavily reliant on the choice of blur kernel, resulting in their limited adaptability, implying that SR processing methods may produce varied effects on different LR images.

Interpolation-based image SR is a classic technique known for its simplicity and ease of implementation. It employs various interpolation kernels to predict the values of missing pixels in HR images. Conventional interpolation methods encompass linear, nearest neighbor, and bicubic interpolation. However, given that these kernels usually exhibit isotropic, they fail to fully account for the anisotropic characteristics of complex image structures. This can lead to blurring effects and the loss of high-frequency details, especially at elevated magnification ratios. To address this limitation, researchers have proposed anisotropic interpolation algorithms capable of adapting to the inherent structure of images. While interpolation-based algorithms offer speed and simplicity, they are susceptible to blurring or jagged artifacts.

Learning-based algorithms are commonly employed to establish the mapping relationship from LR to HR image blocks by leveraging prior information derived from external pairs of LR and HR image blocks. While these algorithms are highly effective in reconstructing image details, the absence of non-local spatial and structural redundancy leads to the loss of certain high-frequency information, which in turn causes the reconstructed details to appear blurred. The sparse coding-based SR algorithm introduced by Yang et al.10 represents a seminal learning-based approach, with numerous extensions reported in the literature. The learning-based reconstruction algorithm addresses the inverse SR problem by incorporating specific prior information into the target HR image. Broadly speaking, these methods can be elucidated from the probabilistic standpoint within one Bayesian maximum a posteriori (MAP) framework. Common prior distributions comprise total variation, bilateral constraints, edge-oriented assumptions, and non-local priors. Motivated by the remarkable achievements of deep learning, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have recently been employed for image SR, leading to substantial enhancements in precision. Dong et al.11 introduced the SR convolutional neural network (SRCNN) and showed that CNNs are capable of effectively learning the transformation from LR to HR image blocks in a seamless end-to-end fashion. Nonetheless, the prior information within SRCNN is derived from training data, often neglecting domain expertise crucial for tackling SR problems. Additionally, these algorithms typically demand significant computational resources.

Utilizing prior knowledge of images can significantly improve image reconstruction performance12. Nevertheless, accurately acquiring this prioritized knowledge remains challenging, thereby limiting the enhancement of reconstruction performance. Recent advances in sparse prior modeling further demonstrate the importance of adaptive regularization. Recent work by Yang et al.6 demonstrates that hybrid Gaussian priors can enhance sparse coding flexibility.

Over the past decade, driven by the rapid advancement of compressed sensing techniques, sparse recovery theory has exhibited significant promise, particularly in the realm of image reconstruction. Jiang and Wang et al.13,14 introduced one SR method founded on sparse recovery, which successfully produced HR images. This algorithm exploits the property that images can be expressed as sparse linear combinations using overcomplete dictionaries15. Utilizing the sparse representation coefficients calculated from image blocks in LR image as those for HR image results in good reconstruction performance. Yan etal16. exploited the symmetry inherent in the uniform linear array of images to obtain more precise estimates via reconstruction techniques. LASSO is commonly employed in modeling image reconstruction problems, employing L1 regularization for feature selection to minimize model complexity17,18. Nevertheless, choosing regularization coefficients poses a challenge. A large regularization coefficient leads to model underfitting, while a small one fails to impose adequate constraints on the model, thus violating the Oracle property. Zou et al.19 introduced the adaptive LASSO to exploit Oracle properties20. The adaptive LASSO allocates weights adaptively according to various L1 regularization parameters or coefficients, instead of relying on a single universal regularization parameter. While these SR methods can deliver satisfactory reconstruction quality, the ambiguity of blur degradation factors in LR images makes selecting regularization parameters quite challenging21. Poor parameter selection may lead to false peaks beyond the clutter ridge22significantly reducing the precision of sparse recovery, thereby impacting the effectiveness of noise blur removal and reconstruction23. Hence, addressing the three factors that impact image quality—noise, blur, and downsampling—is crucial when utilizing these algorithms24–28. Sparse Bayesian learning offers a resilient reconstruction algorithm for addressing sparse signal recovery issues, with all parameters being adaptively adjusted throughout the learning process. Tipping proposed SBL in 200129. This marked the first application of an expectation maximization (EM)-based SBL strategy with forward-backward multiple measurement distances to HR imaging, termed EM-SBL-SR7. Li et al.8 employed a non-parametric sparse Bayesian dictionary learning algorithm to improve the spatial resolution of remote sensing imagery. Wan et al.9 proposed one robust and rapidly converging sparse Bayesian learning method to enhance convergence. Viewed through a Bayesian lens, the previously mentioned LASSO problem can be framed as estimating MAP under one Laplace prior30. Additionally, the Laplace prior distribution exhibits a sharper peak and slightly thicker tails compared to the Gaussian distribution, thereby promoting sparsity. However, due to the Laplace distribution’s lack of conjugacy with the Gaussian distribution, describing the posterior distribution becomes challenging when directly employing the Laplace prior to modeling complex signals. Consequently, Chaabene et al.31 proposed one hierarchical Bayesian framework to derive the Laplace prior for the true signal value. Natural image sparsity can be more effectively modeled using adaptive Laplacian distributions rather than Gaussian priors6,30since their extended tail behavior preserves edge features while concentrated peaks attenuate noise interference. The inherent non-conjugacy between Laplace priors and Gaussian likelihoods31 necessitates an alternative approach. Our solution introduces a hierarchical architecture that independently governs sparsity parameters and regularization intensity, facilitating concurrent optimization of reconstruction fidelity and adaptive performance. Therefore, in this article, we present a SISR method utilizing adaptive Laplacian prior sparse Bayesian estimation that jointly optimizes sparsity and regularization strength, enabling edge-aware recovery without kernel assumptions and delineate its contributions:

Experimental results

In this part, we verify our proposed SR method and analyze its performance. The primary innovation of this framework lies in combining adaptive Laplacian priors with sparse Bayesian estimation.

Experimental setup

All experiments were conducted utilizing MATLAB 2015b, operating on an Intel Core i5 processor with 12 cores clocked at 3.0 GHz and 16GB of RAM. The performance of the SR algorithm was quantitatively assessed through the quantification of peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) and structural similarity (SSIM) with respect to the original image. We assessed our proposed algorithm alongside four other state-of-the-art algorithms across five datasets. It is important to highlight that all quantitative evaluations of our proposed algorithm were derived from the mean of six independent experiments.

All parameters in the comparison algorithms of each experiment are held constant to showcase the robustness and stability of our proposed SR algorithm.

The algorithm’s hyper-parameters were carefully designed based on Bayesian principles and empirical validation. Initial values for and were set to 1 to establish unbiased starting conditions for the optimization process, while the noise precision parameter was formulated to automatically adapt to varying noise levels in input images. Weakly informative priors were implemented through small hyper-parameter values (), allowing the observed data to primarily determine the posterior distributions. Through extensive preliminary testing, we established as the optimal convergence threshold, which provided stable performance across all test cases while maintaining computational efficiency. This comprehensive parameter selection strategy ensured robust performance without requiring case-specific tuning, as demonstrated by the consistent results across diverse datasets.

Our framework employs a composite dictionary integrating analytic and learned components. The analytic basis (40%) combines Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT)38 for spectral compaction and Gabor wavelets39 for oriented edge detection. The remaining 60% are adaptively learned from DIV2K patches via K-SVD optimization42 under sparsity constraints (five non-zero coefficients per patch). Computational efficiency is achieved through Intel MKL parallelization leveraging 12-core processing.

Experimental datasets

The learning-based SISR algorithm typically comprises two main steps: firstly, the training phase is conducted to learn the weight contours; subsequently, upon completion of training, the learned weight contours are utilized to reconstruct the SR image. The training dataset chosen for this study comprises 800 h images from DIV2K40while 5 testing datasets were utilized, namely: Set541, Set1442, BSD10043, Urban10044, and DIV2K40.

Every image within the datasets is a color image. Given that human visual perception is particularly attuned to variations in color, this study initially converts these images into the YUV color space. The algorithm proposed in this study is then employed to learn the Y component and synthesize the SR image. The U and V components are processed using the conventional bicubic interpolation technique to obtain HR color components of equal dimensions. Finally, the HR YUV components are transformed into the RGB color space to yield the ultimate SR outcome. SR reconstruction should be conducted for grayscale images using learned weight contours.

Both objective and subjective indicators were employed to validate the usability of the proposed SR algorithm. In the objective evaluation, we utilized PSNR and average SSIM, which are commonly used metrics for evaluating image quality. Comparing PSNR and SSIM values of SR images acquired from various algorithms is a common method to assess their performance and adaptability. This involves computing the variance of PSNR and SSIM values across various images to derive evaluation outcomes. In subjective evaluation, we compared SR images generated by various algorithms and assessed their quality based on human visual perception of the SR effect. Four comparative algorithms, namely Bicubic, FIEF-SiSR45FRM-SiSR46and LGAN-SiSR47were selected for the experiment. These algorithms are at the cutting edge of SISR.

Limitations and future work

Despite its advantages, the proposed SBL-ALP framework has limitations that highlight future research directions.

First, the model’s noise assumption presents a constraint. The Gamma hyperprior for estimating Gaussian noise () performs suboptimally under non-Gaussian noise (e.g., salt-and-pepper or Poisson noise) or spatially correlated noise patterns. This violation of the pixel-wise independence assumption can lead to residual artifacts or oversmoothing in challenging real-world scenarios.

Second, computational efficiency remains a concern for very high-resolution applications. Although diagonal approximation reduces complexity, the per-iteration cost of becomes prohibitive with megapixel-scale images, limiting real-time deployment.

Third, the dictionary’s representational capacity is bounded by its training data. Reconstructing images containing novel, out-of-domain textures may be suboptimal if the fixed dictionary atoms cannot sparsely represent these features.

Finally, the method occasionally underperforms on extremely ill-posed details, as observed on Urban100 (×4) and DIV2K (×2). Thin structures (e.g., hair) and fine repetitive textures push the limits of the adaptive prior, sometimes resulting in blurred reconstructions where the model fails to resolve severe LR-HR ambiguities.

Future work will focus on: (1) Integrating more flexible noise models; (2) Developing accelerated inference algorithms; (3) Exploring online dictionary adaptation; and (4) Incorporating non-local self-similarity constraints to address these limitations.

Conclusion

In this research, we introduce one sparse Bayesian SR reconstruction approach that relies on adaptive Laplacian priors. This approach employs a spatial alternating minimization scheme for solving. Through the acquisition of adaptive Laplacian priors, we can learn the mean of target pixels in HR images, estimate their respective texture and edge uncertainties, thereby enhancing HR image reconstruction. Ablation studies confirm that our adaptive prior outperforms fixed/gaussian variants by 0.8-1.2dB PSNR. It’s noteworthy that this method doesn’t depend on blur kernels, thus exhibiting robust adaptability to various LR images.

Experimental outcomes indicate that our proposed approach generally delivers excellent SR performance, robust adaptability, and high efficiency. Nevertheless, it’s important to recognize that blind image SR methods are limited to addressing particular types of degradation, and real-world images frequently deviate from the degradation assumptions of individual models. Consequently, forthcoming research will center on implicit modeling and SR methods designed for single-image input. These methods will employ diverse explicit modeling strategies to address increasingly intricate degradation types and data scenarios encountered in real-world settings.
