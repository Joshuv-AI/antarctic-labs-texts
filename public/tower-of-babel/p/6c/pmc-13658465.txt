Testing the accuracy of Cameriere’s original and European formulae radiographic method of dental age estimation
Authors: Aliff Syazaryl Aiman Azhar, Janet Ann Davies, Helen Mary Liversidge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo test the accuracy of Cameriere’s radiographic method of dental age estimation.MethodsThe sample was 661 archived dental radiographs of dental patients aged 4.0-15.9 years (310 females, 351 males). Dental age was calculated using Cameriere’s original formula (Cameriere et al. 2006, Int J Legal Med, 120, 49) and European formula (Cameriere et al. 2007, Int J Legal Med, 121, 449); this measures apex width to tooth length of developing mandibular permanent teeth excluding third molars. The mean difference and absolute mean difference between dental and chronological ages were calculated and assessed using a t-test for females, males, pooled and by year group.ResultsFor the European formula, mean difference between dental and chronological ages was small for both females and males (0.04 years, SD 0.59 and 0.09 years, SD 0.66 respectively) but was significantly different in males (P < 0.05). Absolute mean difference values were 0.44 years in females and 0.48 years in males. All age categories from 6 to 13 for both sexes had mean difference and absolute mean difference values of less than six months. Age was overestimated by over one year in a large proportion of 4- and 5-year-olds. Mean difference was lower in the European formula (0.07 years) than the original formula (-0.26 years) possibly due to the larger sample size and age range for the European formula.ConclusionCameriere’s method accurately estimates age for most of our sample but may be unsuitable for children younger than 6 years. The European formula is more accurate than the original formula.Keywords: Cameriere method, Dental age estimation, Permanent teeth, Radiograph

Introduction

Age estimation is essential in various fields, including forensics, anthropology, medical treatment planning, and legal contexts such as immigration and criminal investigations [1, 2]. Accurate estimation methods aim to approximate an individual’s estimated age as closely as possible to the chronological age. The chronological age (CA) is the amount of time elapsed, in days, weeks, months and years, since a person’s birth.

Age estimation techniques are broadly categorised into skeletal and dental approaches. Skeletal methods assess human growth and bone development using indicators such as hand and wrist ossification, long bone epiphyseal fusion, and clavicle maturation [3]. Dental methods evaluate biological markers of tooth development. Teeth are widely used for age estimation due to their lower susceptibility to environmental factors compared to skeletal, and the accessibility of radiographic imaging [4].

Dental age estimation can be further classified into qualitative and quantitative methods. Qualitative methods involve visual assessments of tooth development, such as dental staging or tooth atlases, while quantitative methods employ numerical measurements to construct statistical models or regression equations for age estimation [5]. These methods provide a dental age (DA), which is an estimated age calculated using tooth markers.

This study employs Cameriere’s radiographic method, a quantitative approach that measures the lengths of open apices in developing mandibular teeth, excluding the third molar. Cameriere et al. developed a multiple linear regression model incorporating variables such as the sum of open apices, the individual’s sex, and the number of teeth with closed apices. Substituting these values into the resulting formula generates a DA, which can then be compared with the individual’s established CA [6].

The original 2006 formula, derived from analysis of dental panoramic tomography (DPT) radiographs of Italian children, was later revised in 2007 using a larger, multi-country sample from seven European nations [7]. The present study evaluates the accuracy of Cameriere’s method for dental age estimation in a London population, compares the performance of the 2006 and 2007 formulae, and examines whether accuracy varies according to sex and ethnicity.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cross-sectional study that analysed radiographs of healthy children who attended the Royal London Dental Hospital for treatment. As part of their dental check-up and treatment, all the subjects were radiographed in the paediatric dental department. Ethical approval was applied for and received by the Queen Mary Ethics of Research Committee (Reference number: QME24.0693).

Materials

The sample consisted of 661 archived DPT radiographs of local paediatric dental patients aged 4 to 16 years. Of these 661 patients, 351 were male and 310 were female. The sample comprised two ethnicities – 340 Bangladeshis and 321 British Caucasians, who attended the Paediatric Dentistry Department at the Royal London Dental Hospital for treatment. These radiographs were first used and analysed by Maber to test four dental age estimation methods – Demirjian, Willems, Nolla and Haavikko [8]. All radiographs were anonymised and do not contain identifiable information about the subjects. Only sex, date of birth and ethnicity were recorded for each radiograph. The inclusion criteria of these radiographs were as follows: dental panoramic radiographs of good quality with the presence of seven left mandibular permanent teeth, subjects who were healthy with a known and precise date of birth, normal tooth eruption and no pathological conditions affecting the alveolar bone and jaw. Exclusion criteria included radiographs that were unclear, showed gross pathology, or involved patients who were undergoing or had undergone orthodontic treatment. Further exclusions were applied to radiographs in which the apices of all seven left mandibular teeth were closed, or in which not all seven left mandibular teeth were present due to young age or hypodontia.

Methods

All radiographs were analysed using ImageJ, an open-source Java-based image processing software. The brightness, contrast, saturation, sharpness and clarity of the radiographs were adjusted to allow optimum visualisation of assessed teeth for accurate measurements of open apices and tooth length (Fig. 1).

Obtaining measurements of tooth height and open apices of developing teeth in the lower left mandibular arch for Cameriere’s method

Using Cameriere’s method of dental age estimation for each subject, the number of teeth with complete root development (closed apex) on the radiograph was first counted as N0. Developing teeth with open apices were examined and the distance between the inner sides of the open apex was measured (Ai, i = 1,…,5). For two-rooted teeth (first and second molars), the distances between the inner sides of two open apices were measured and added into one sum (Ai, i = 6,7). These measurements were then normalised by dividing them by their respective tooth length (Li, i = 1,…,7). This was done to minimise the effect of possible discrepancies from the magnification and angulation of the radiographs taken. The normalised measurements were taken for evaluation of dental maturity (xi = Ai/Li, i = 1,…,7), and the sum of all normalised open apices was tabulated as (s). All these values will then be substituted into Cameriere’s formula to obtain the DA, which will be compared with the chronological age CA of the respective subject.

As shown in Table 1, two formulae derived from Cameriere’s research were used: the 2006 formula, originally developed and validated using a sample of individuals aged 5 to 15 years, and the 2007 formula, derived from a broader age range of 4 to 16 years. To preserve methodological consistency with the original validation studies and avoid extrapolating the 2006 regression model beyond its validated age interval, 57 radiographs outside the 5–15-year age range were excluded from analysis using the 2006 formula. This approach was undertaken to ensure that each formula was evaluated within the age range for which it was originally intended and validated.

Statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS Statistics Data Editor Version 29. Intra- and inter-observer reliability were tested using intra-class correlation coefficient. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by comparing DAs from two sets of 70 randomly selected radiographs, with measurements done by the first author in two different time points, two weeks apart. Inter-observer reliability was assessed by comparing DAs from two sets of 50 randomly selected radiographs, with measurements obtained by the first author for the first set and the second author for the second set.

Accuracy was tested for each formula by obtaining the mean difference and absolute mean difference between the DA and CA of each sample. The percentages in which the difference between DA and CA were within 6 months of the total sample size were also calculated. The MD was obtained using independent one sample t-test at 95% confidence interval. The analysis also included results across one-year age categories and between male and females of the sample. An independent-sample t-test was also done at 95% confidence interval to assess any significant differences in the mean difference between the two ethnicities studied.

Cameriere’s method showed both under- and overestimation of age depending on the formula applied (Table 3). Using Cameriere et al. 2006, age was underestimated with a mean difference of − 0.26 ± 0.57 years for the combined sample, and this deviation was statistically significant (p < 0.001). Overall, 64.1% (n = 387) of dental age estimates were within 6 months of chronological age, and 89.9% (n = 543) were within 1 year. In contrast, Cameriere et al. 2007 slightly overestimated age, with a mean difference of 0.07 ± 0.63 years, also statistically significant (p < 0.005). This formula showed improved accuracy, with 67.2% (n = 444) of estimates within 6 months and 88.1% (n = 582) within 1 year of chronological age.

Comparing the two, the 2007 formula demonstrated a smaller mean difference (0.07 vs. − 0.26 years). Although it had a marginally higher standard deviation (0.63 vs. 0.57 years), the absolute mean differences were similar overall.

Results by age cohort

Age-group analysis was conducted for both Cameriere et al. 2006 and Cameriere et al. 2007 to assess accuracy across ages, with age groups having a mean difference of less than 6 months highlighted blue in (a), indicating high accuracy (Fig. 3).

For the 2006 formula, dental age was underestimated across all groups. High accuracy was observed in 5 to 12-year-olds, while accuracy declined markedly in adolescents: 14-year-olds were underestimated by 1 year and 15-year-olds by 2.1 years. The most accurate groups were ages 5, 6, and 9 (0.1 years). Absolute mean differences were less than 6 months for ages 6–12, but substantially higher in the older groups. For the 2007 formula, dental ages were underestimated in 7–13-year-olds and overestimated in the youngest (4–5 years) and oldest (14–15 years) groups. High accuracy (less than 6 months) was found in 6 to 13-year-olds. The greatest mean differences occurred in 15-year-olds (1.7 years) and 4-year-olds (1.1 years). The 7 and 9-year-old groups performed best (0.1 years).

When comparing both formulae, reduced accuracy was reported in the older age groups (14–15 years). The 2006 formula performed better for 5-year-olds, whereas the 2007 formula performed better for 13-year-olds. Overall, accuracy varied by age, with both formulae showing diminished performance beyond 14 years.

Results by sex

An independent samples t-test was used to compare mean accuracy between females and males for the Cameriere 2006 and 2007 formulae (Table 4). For females, the 2006 formula showed a significant difference between estimated and chronological age (p < 0.001), whereas the 2007 formula did not (p = 0.12). For males, mean accuracy differed significantly for both formulae (p < 0.001 for 2006; p < 0.007 for 2007). In both sexes, the 2007 formula produced smaller mean differences than the 2006 formula.

Discussion

To improve previous dental age estimation methods involving subjective teeth staging, Cameriere et al. introduced the method of measuring open apices of developing teeth to estimate dental age. This was on the basis that as children get older, the apices of developing teeth become smaller. This aspect of tooth development was studied as it can be used as a biological marker that can be measured and quantified.

Assessment of reliability is a crucial step before evaluating the accuracy of any age estimation method [9]. In methods based on morphological measurements, it is essential to ensure that the variables, specifically the length of open apices, can be measured consistently. In the present study, both intra-observer and inter-observer assessments demonstrated excellent reliability, with ICC values greater than 0.90. This is consistent with previous studies that have reported similarly high ICC scores for the Cameriere method [10–12]. The findings indicate that the Cameriere method provides reproducible measurements of developing teeth, supporting its suitability for subsequent age estimation analyses.

Both Cameriere’s formulae; the 2006 original and the 2007 European version demonstrated high overall accuracy in estimating dental age, with minimal bias. The 2007 formula was slightly more accurate, overestimating age by 0.07 years compared to the 2006 formula’s underestimation of 0.26 years. This likely reflects its derivation from a larger and more diverse sample of European Caucasian children aged 4 to 16 years across seven countries, compared with the 2006 formula’s smaller, more homogeneous cohort of Italian children aged 5 to 15 years.

Accuracy, however, varied across age groups. In children aged 4 to 5 years, dental ages were often overestimated, with a large proportion of overestimations by more than a year. This may be attributed to the early stages of crown development in mandibular teeth, particularly the second premolars and second molars, where landmarks are less distinct and wide open apices increase measurement variability. Conversely, in older adolescents above 14 years, dental ages tended to be underestimated by a year. This likely reflects the near-complete root development of most teeth in this age group, leaving only the second molars with open apices to be measured, which limits the validity of the method at this age. These findings show that the accuracy of Cameriere method can be affected by the developmental stage of the open apices and the number of measurable teeth.

A 2021 meta-analysis of Cameriere’s method, comprising of 15 studies, reported that the technique is reliable for individuals aged 7 to 14 years [13]. The present study supports this conclusion, showing minimal mean differences between chronological and dental age within the 7 to 14-year range, and substantially larger discrepancies exceeding six months in children younger than 6 years. Notably, many previous studies excluded participants under 5 years and over 15 years, resulting in a lack of comparative data in the meta-analysis for these age groups and limiting direct comparison with the current findings (Table 6).

Sex and ethnicity had minimal influence on the accuracy of Cameriere’s method in this study. For both the 2006 and 2007 formulae, mean and absolute mean differences between chronological and dental age were comparable between males and females, with the 2007 formula showing only a marginal improvement in accuracy for females (0.05 years in mean difference and 0.04 years in absolute mean difference). Both formulae include a sex variable derived from regression analysis, which likely contributes to the similar performance observed between sexes. Likewise, accuracy was almost identical between the two ethnic groups tested in the study; British Caucasian and Bangladeshi children, with negligible mean differences for both formulae and no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05). Despite the formulae being developed from White Italian and European Caucasian samples, their comparable performance across these two ethnicities supports a previous study that population-specific formulae do not substantially improve dental age estimation [14].

Other recent studies have compared Cameriere’s method with other dental age estimation approaches. A Thai study from 2026 comparing Cameriere’s method with the London Atlas and Demirjian method reported that the Demirjian method demonstrated the smallest discrepancy between DA and CA, while Cameriere performed better primarily in younger children around 7 years of age. The study also found that the accuracy of Cameriere’s method decreased in older age groups, with less consistent performance than Demirjian and London Atlas methods in adolescents. These findings are consistent with the present study, which also demonstrated that Cameriere’s method is age-dependent, with reduced accuracy in older adolescents [15]. Additionally, a 2025 study from Bangladesh using software-assisted analysis compared the Willems and Cameriere methods in children aged 6–14 years and found that Willems slightly overestimated CA, while Cameriere showed no significant difference between DA and CA. The study further confirmed the reliability and reproducibility of Cameriere’s method across different software platforms [16].

This study has several notable strengths. The large and diverse sample of 661 radiographs, with a near-balanced distribution of sexes and two ethnic groups, allowed meaningful assessment of accuracy across demographic subgroups without over-representation. The wide age range of 4.00 to 15.99 years enabled detailed age-specific analyses, while the overall sample size reduced the influence of outliers. Unlike many previous studies that evaluated only a single formula, this research compared multiple Cameriere-derived formulae, including the original and European formulae which demonstrated important performance differences among them. The finding that accuracy did not differ significantly by sex or ethnicity further supports the wider applicability of Cameriere’s method. In addition, high intra- and inter-observer reliability and the close agreement between dental and chronological ages provide validation for the use of both the 2006 and 2007 formulae in dental age estimation.

Some limitations in this study include the lack of information of the socioeconomic background of each individual. Additionally, some radiographs were affected by poor clarity in the lower anterior region due to cervical spine superimposition or patient movement, potentially reducing measurement accuracy. Excluding such images earlier may have improved this. Furthermore, the 2006 and 2007 formulae were applied to slightly different age ranges based on their original validation criteria, which may affect direct comparability between the two formulae. The study also utilised traditional manual measurements techniques in obtaining tooth apices and height length rather than recent machine learning-based approaches. Shen et al. [17] demonstrated that machine learning enhanced the accuracy of the Cameriere method by using random forest, support vector machine and linear regression models. However, despite this improvement, a recent study by Faragalli et al. [18] found that machine learning models also exhibited systematic trends in error distribution when estimating dental age, highlighting the need for further evaluation.

Despite these limitations, the study validates Cameriere’s method to be an accurate method in estimating dental age. Both the original and European formula show high accuracy and can be applied to a wide population without the need of a population-specific formula. However, it may be unsuitable for children below 5 years and above 14 years, which may require a more accurate dental age estimation method for these age groups. Future research could compare Cameriere’s method with other age estimation approaches, further explore the potential of artificial intelligence or machine learning to improve accuracy and reduce error trends, and examine the age dispersion around point estimates using this method.

Conclusion

Cameriere’s method is a reliable and accurate method for dental age estimation, performing best in children aged 7–14 years. The 2007 European formula demonstrates slightly greater accuracy than the 2006 formula, with no significant differences in performance between sexes or the ethnic groups studied for both formulae.
