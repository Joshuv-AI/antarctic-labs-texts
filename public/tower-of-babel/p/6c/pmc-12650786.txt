Effectiveness of cerebral embolic protection during transcatheter aortic valve replacement: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized trials
Authors: Rodolfo Caminiti, Alfonso Ielasi, Giampaolo Vetta, Antonio Parlavecchio, Domenico Giovanni Della Rocca, Silvia Moscardelli, Andrea Marrone, Giulia Laterra, Maurizio Tespili, Giampiero Vizzari, Marco Barbanti, Antonio Micari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Transcatheter aortic valve replacement, Cerebral embolic protection, Stroke

Introduction

Nowadays, transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has revolutionized the management of symptomatic severe aortic valve stenosis (AS) and is currently the treatment of choice for elderly patients across all surgical risk categories [1,2]. Compared to surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR), TAVR offers significant advantages, including a reduced risk of adverse events, notably fewer procedure-related cerebrovascular events (approximately 4–7 % within 30 days post-procedure) [3]. Stroke during TAVR may result from embolism, haemorrhage, or hypoperfusion occurring during valve deployment [1].

Cerebral embolism is a frequent complication at various stages of the TAVR procedure, including catheter manipulation at the aortic arch, balloon pre- and post-dilatation, valve deployment/recapture, and extended procedure duration [4]. Often, cerebral embolism can lead to clinically silent ischemia that remains undetected during or immediately after the procedure [5]. However, these silent ischemic events may contribute to long-term cognitive decline and an increased risk of dementia [6].

To mitigate these risks, cerebral embolic protection (CEP) devices have been developed specifically to prevent embolic debris from reaching the brain during TAVR procedures. Two CEP devices are currently approved for clinical use in Europe (CE marked): Sentinel (Boston Scientific, Florida, USA) and TriGuard (Keystone Heart, Florida, USA) [7]. Several previous meta-analyses have explored the role of cerebral embolic protection (CEP) during TAVR, but most included heterogeneous populations and pooled data from observational registries together with randomized trials, thereby limiting interpretability of true effectiveness of these devices. Conversely, meta-analytic evidence restricted to randomized data has thus far failed to demonstrate a consistent clinical benefit [8]. The present meta-analysis updates the existing evidence by incorporating the recently published BHF PROTECT-TAVI trial, the largest randomized controlled trial in this field, thereby providing the most comprehensive and contemporary synthesis of randomized data to date.

Methods

Data sources and searches

We systematically searched the Medline, Embase, and Scopus electronic databases for studies published from inception to 27th April 2025, focusing on RCTs comparing patients undergone TAVR procedure with CEP and non-CEP (NCEP). Given the nature of this study ethics approval and patient consent were not applicable. Two investigators (R.C. and G.V.) independently conducted searches using the following terms: “transcatheter aortic valve replacement”, “cerebral embolic protection”, “TAVR” and “CEP”. Detailed information on our literature search strategy is available in the Supplementary Appendix in the Expanded Methods.

Study selection

The preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) statement for reporting systematic reviews and meta-analyses was used in this study [9].

The predefined protocol was registered to the international prospective registry of systematic reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD420251072869). Studies had to meet the following criteria to be included in the analysis: (1) RCTs, (2) direct comparison between TAVR with or without CEP, (3) adult (>18 years) age (4) ≥ 3 years follow-up, (5) report of one or more clinical outcomes of interest. We excluded non-randomized studies, unpublished data, case reports, editorials, reviews, expert opinions, and studies not published in English language.

Data extraction and quality appraisal

Three investigators (R.C., G.V., and A.I.) extracted data from each study using standardized protocol and reporting forms. Three reviewers (R.C., G.V and A.I.) independently assessed the quality items and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Study endpoints

Primary endpoint was overall stroke (disabling and non-disabling). Secondary endpoints included disabling stroke, all-cause mortality, life-threatening or major bleeding, major vascular complications related to CEP access, and acute kidney injury.

Neurological endpoints comprised the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score and MRI-derived measures of cerebral ischemic injury (number of new lesions, lesion volume per lesion, total lesion volume, and the proportion of patients with new ischemic lesions).

Secondary analyses included a device-level comparison between Sentinel and TriGuard systems to evaluate potential differences.

All endpoints were defined according to Valve Academic Research Consortium-2 (VARC) and/or Neurologic Academic Research Consortium (type 1.a to 1.d and 2.b) recommendations.[10,11] Both primary and secondary endpoints were collected at 30 days except for two studies that reported outcomes at 72 h after the index procedure.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables, or number of cases (n) and percentage (%) for dichotomous and categorical variables. The Mantel-Haenszel risk ratio (RR) model was used to summarise the data for binary outcomes between treatment arms. For continuous variables, summary estimates and 95 % confidence intervals (CI) were reported as the standardised mean difference. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the Chi2, Tau2 and Higgins-I2 statistics, and random effects models by DerSimonian and Laird were used. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots. A subgroup analysis was performed to compare outcomes between the two currently available CEP devices − Sentinel (Boston Scientific, USA) and TriGuard/3 (Keystone Heart, USA) − to explore potential differences in device-specific efficacy and safety profiles. Heterogeneity between subgroups was assessed using the χ2 test and corresponding p-value for subgroup differences. Statistical analysis was performed with Review Manager (RevMan) (computer program) version 5.4.1, Copenhagen, Denmark: Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020.

Results

Study selection and baseline characteristics

Among 467 screened articles, 47 full texts were retrieved and reviewed for possible inclusion.

A total of 8 studies fulfilled the selection criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Evidence search and selection of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA).

The studies enrolled a total of 11,625 patients (group CEP, n = 5,843 patients; group NCEP, n = 5,782 patients). Overall, 57.3 % (95 % CI: 53.9–60.1 %) patients were male with an average age of 81.5 years (95 % CI: 79.3–83.4). Prior stroke and/or transient ischemic attack (TIA) was present in 13.8 % (95 % CI: 11.9 – 14.6 %) of the patients, 30.4 % (95 % CI: 25.2–35.7 %) suffered from diabetes and 55 % (95 % CI: 53.6–60.1 %) suffered from hyperlipidaemia.

The longest follow-up time available was 30 days (Table 1).

Primary and secondary endpoints

All the 8 RCTs evaluated reported data on primary endpoint overall stroke and for the secondary endpoints disabling stroke, all-cause mortality and acute kidney injury [1,2,7,[12], [13], [14], [15], [16]]. Life-treating bleeding was reported in 6 six trials [2], [12], [12], [13], [15], [16] and vascular complications related to the CEP system access was reported in 4 trials [1], [2], [12], [16].

No differences were found for the risk of overall stroke between TAVR with CEP vs. NCEP [2.7 % vs. 2.5 %; RR: 1.03 (95 % CI: 0.82–1.29; p = 0.81; I2 = 21 %] (Fig. 2).

Forest plots comparing the primary endpoint Overall Stroke between CEP and NCEP.

No differences were found between TAVR with CEP vs. NCEP for the risk of disabling stroke [1.1 % vs. 1.3 %; RR: 0.82 (95 % CI: 0.59–1.14; p = 0.23; I2 = 21 %] (Fig. 3, A), all-cause mortality [0.8 % vs. 0.6 %; RR: 1.18 (95 % CI: 0.77–1.80; p = 0.45; I2 = 0 %] (Fig. 3, B), life-treating bleeding [0.9 % vs. 0.6 %; RR: 1.25 (95 % CI: 0.77–2.04; p = 0.37; I2 = 28 %] (Fig. 3, C) and acute kidney injury [2.6 % vs. 2.1 %; RR: 1.20 (95 % CI: 0.94–1.52; p = 0.14; I2 = 0 %] (Fig. 3, D). Vascular complications related to CEP access were low, 1.1 % (95 % CI: −0.6 – 2.8; p = 0.21; I2 = 99 %) (Fig. 4).

Sentinel vs. triguard subgroup analysis

Device-specific subgroup analysis comparing TriGuard [7], [12], [16] and Sentinel [1], [2], [13], [14], [15] systems demonstrated consistent results across all evaluated endpoints (Supplemental Fig. 3).

For overall stroke, there was no significant difference between CEP vs. NCEP for either the TriGuard (OR 1.48, 95 % CI 0.68–3.24; p = 0.33; I2 = 0 %) or the Sentinel (OR 0.99, 95 % CI 0.78–1.26; p = 0.94; I2 = 48 %) device, with no evidence of interaction between devices (p for subgroup difference = 0.34). (Supplemental Fig. 3A) Similarly, disabling stroke occurred at comparable rates in both groups (TriGuard: OR 2.38, 95 % CI: 0.51–11.04; p = 0.27; Sentinel: OR 0.76, 95 % CI: 0.54–1.07; p = 0.12; p for subgroup difference = 0.16). (Supplemental Fig. 3B) No significant differences were observed in all-cause mortality (TriGuard: OR 1.10, 95 % CI: 0.53–2.26; Sentinel: OR 1.21, 95 % CI: 0.80–1.83) (Supplemental Fig. 3C), major or life-threatening bleeding (TriGuard: OR 1.18, 95 % CI: 0.49–2.85; Sentinel: OR 1.28, 95 % CI: 0.77–2.14) (Supplemental Fig. 3D), vascular complications related to CEP access (TriGuard: OR 1.09, 95 % CI: 0.42–2.83; Sentinel: OR 1.05, 95 % CI: 0.66–1.67) (Supplemental Fig. 3E), or acute kidney injury (TriGuard: OR 1.25, 95 % CI: 0.81–1.92; Sentinel: OR 1.18, 95 % CI: 0.89–1.56) (Supplemental Fig. 3F).

In MRI-based subgroup analyses, no significant differences were observed between CEP vs. NCEP for any imaging endpoint.

For TriGuard, the mean number of new cerebral lesions per patient (MD 0.91; 95 % CI: −0.42–2.24; p = 0.18) (Supplemental Fig. 3G), mean lesion volume (MD 2.17 mm3; 95 % CI: −0.35–4.69; p = 0.09) (Supplemental Fig. 3H), and total lesion volume (MD −58.82 mm3; 95 % CI: −204.7–87.08; p = 0.43) (Supplemental Fig. 3I) did not differ from NCEP group.

Similarly, for Sentinel, the mean number of new cerebral lesions per patient (MD −3.52; 95 % CI: −7.54–0.50; p = 0.12) (Supplemental Fig. 3G), lesion volume (MD −27.42 mm3; 95 % CI: −53.01 to −1.83; p = 0.04) (Supplemental Fig. 3H), and total lesion volume (MD − 104.82 mm3; 95 % CI − 219.43 to 9.80; p = 0.07) (Supplemental Fig. 3I) showed a nonsignificant trend toward reduced lesion burden.

A significant subgroup interaction was observed for total lesion volume (p = 0.006), suggesting divergent effects between devices.

The proportion of patients with new ischemic lesions was also comparable between CEP vs. NCEP groups (TriGuard: OR 0.82; 95 % CI: 0.42–1.61; p = 0.58; Sentinel: OR 0.44; 95 % CI: 0.07–2.80; p = 0.38). (Supplemental Fig. 3J).

No significant interaction between devices was detected across endpoints (p for subgroup differences > 0.05), confirming the absence of device-specific effects on MRI outcomes.

The NIHSS scale did not differ significantly between CEP and NCEP groups. For TriGuard, pooled analysis of two RCTs [12], [16] showed no significant difference (OR 1.92; 95 % CI: 0.57–6.48; p = 0.29), and for Sentinel, a single study [13] yielded similar results (OR 1.24; 95 % CI: 0.41–3.77; p = 0.70). No interaction was detected between devices (p for subgroup difference = 0.60; I2 = 0 %), indicating comparable neurological outcomes across CEP devices. (Supplemental Fig. 3K).

Discussion

The primary finding of our meta-analysis, which exclusively included RCTs suggests that current evidence is insufficient to support the routine use of CEP devices for preventing stroke and cardiovascular events immediately and within 30 days after TAVR [1]. However, the clinical rationale for employing CEP devices remains compelling, particularly given the known risks associated with embolic stroke during the TAVR procedure, nonetheless improved procedural techniques, enhanced operator expertise, and advancements in device technology have reduced the strokes rates during procedure [3], [6], [17], [18].

A previously published meta-analysis combining data from observational registries (OR) and RCTs by Basit et al. reported that the use of CEP devices was associated with a statistically significant reduction in disabling stroke (p = 0.003) [19]. However, this apparent benefit was largely driven by two observational registries − Kroon et al. and Seeger et al. − both of which included the now-discontinued Lotus system (Boston Scientific, USA), known to generate substantial embolic debris (≥1,000 μm) during positioning and deployment [20], [21], [22]. In these studies, self-expanding valves such as the Evolut platform were also frequently used, which are likewise associated with a higher incidence of cerebrovascular events compared with balloon-expandable devices.

Conversely, the most recent meta-analysis using trial sequential analysis and limited to RCTs, conducted by Warraich et al., failed to show a statistically significant reduction in clinical outcomes, likely due to the small number of available trials [8]. The present meta-analysis is, to date, the only study to include the recently published BHF PROTECT-TAVI trial, the largest RCT in this field, and exclusively evaluates randomized evidence from studies testing the two currently available CEP devices: Sentinel and TriGuard/3. The Sentinel device employs dual filters placed via the right radial artery (6F), protecting the brachiocephalic and left common carotid arteries, whereas the TriGuard utilizes a single filter introduced through the femoral artery (9F) positioned at the aortic arch to cover all three major cerebral vessels; the new iteration, TriGuard 3, warrant more stability [7], [14].

Despite statistically significance weren’t reached from all the studies included, the routine clinical use of CEP devices may still be justified by many trials that investigated the MRI of patients undergone TAVR procedure before, after and at the follow-up. In the DEFLECT III trial, new ischemic cerebral lesions were categorized by size (small, medium and large), the TriGuard group demonstrated a 77 % reduction in medium-sized lesions compared to controls (7.5 % vs 33 %) at 4 ± 2 days [7]. Similarly, the CLEAN TAVI trial, using an earlier iteration of the Sentinel device, reported ischemic brain lesions in 98 % of participants with a significant lowering in numbers of lesions in the filter group than in the control group at 2 days (4 vs. 10, p < 0.001) [13]. Consistent with these findings, the MISTRAL-C trial reported that 78 % of patients developed new brain lesions following TAVR. Lesions were fewer and of smaller volume in the protected lobes (0 mm3 vs. 76 mm3; p = 0.057), and 50 % of patients in the CEP group had no new lesions in the protected territories (0 % vs. 20 %, p = 0.04). These results underscore a key limitation of the Sentinel CEP device, which − due to its two-filter design − does not cover the left vertebral artery, a region where a substantial proportion of new lesions was observed [15], [23]. In the SENTINEL trial, the protected group reported a 38 % reduction in overall stroke incidence at 30 days and a 42 % decrease in new cerebral lesions identified by MRI at 2–7 days, but with no statistically significance [14]. In our analysis a significant subgroup interaction for total lesion volume (p = 0.006) suggests that the two CEP devices may differ in protective effectiveness. The TriGuard, despite its wider cerebral coverage, was associated with larger lesion volumes, possibly due to device instability or incomplete sealing, whereas the Sentinel demonstrated an opposite trend toward smaller lesion burden, consistent with its more stable dual-filter design targeting the carotid circulation.

Another important consideration is the variability in CEP device effectiveness depending on the transcatheter heart valve (THV) used. A sub-analysis from the DEFLECT III trial suggested that patients treated with the TriGuard CEP device in combination with the self-expanding Medtronic CoreValve THV experienced a higher number of new cerebral ischemic lesions compared to those receiving balloon-expandable SAPIEN XT/3 THV systems [7]. Similarly, the MISTRAL-C trial, which evaluated the SENTINEL CEP, reported a significantly greater lesion volume in the posterior lobes − primarily supplied by the unprotected left vertebral artery − when a self-expanding THV was used (405 mm3 vs. 92 mm3, p = 0.037) [15]. This finding supports existing evidence that self-expanding THVs are associated with a higher risk of dislodging larger amounts of embolic debris to the brain primarily due to the repositioning mechanism [21]. Even in the absence of a significant reduction in clinically evident stroke, CEP devices may still hold value in complex procedural scenarios at high risk of embolization, such as BASILICA or CHIMNEY techniques, or in patients with extensive aortic atheroma [24], [25]. Their use may also be reasonable in younger and low-risk TAVR patients, for whom long-term neurocognitive preservation is an important consideration.

Ongoing research should clarify whether a selective, risk-based approach to cerebral protection can be both clinically and economically justified. In these high-risk settings, CEP could theoretically reduce costs associated with prolonged hospitalizations for stroke management and mitigate the burden of cognitive decline and its related social impact.

However, it should be noted that the BHF PROTECT-TAVI trial, which also included a cost-effectiveness analysis, failed to demonstrate either a clinical benefit and related cost justification from the routine use of CEP devices [2].

Although current data do not show statistically significant short-term benefits in a heterogeneous TAVR population, onger-term studies are warranted to explore potential neuroprotective effects. The frequent detection of asymptomatic ischemic brain lesions on MRI raises concern for potential cognitive deterioration and future dementia risk, underscoring the need for extended follow-up nd standardized neurological assessment [6], [23].

Future research involving next-generation protection systems together with larger, adequately powered trials and longer follow-up periods, will be crucial to determine the true clinical relevance and cost-effectiveness of routine CEP use during TAVR [26].

Limitations

Our meta-analysis has several limitations. First, the follow-up period across all trials was relatively short (maximum 30 days), precluding assessment of long-term neurological outcomes such as cognitive decline or dementia. Second, heterogeneity in CEP device types, procedural techniques, and transcatheter heart valve (THV) platforms may have influenced the outcomes and introduced variability. Third, some endpoints, such as vascular complications and life-threatening bleeding, were inconsistently reported across studies. Moreover, some included trials evaluated the TriGuard device, which has not received FDA approval; this may limit the generalizability of our pooled findings to clinical settings where only the Sentinel device is approved and widely used. Finally, although MRI findings suggest subclinical cerebral injury, such data were not uniformly available across trials and were therefore not included in this quantitative synthesis.

Conclusions

Our meta-analysis of RCTs found no significant reduction in stroke or major complications with CEP devices during TAVR. While current evidence does not support their routine use based on short-term outcomes, emerging data suggest potential differences in efficacy related to device design. The high incidence of silent cerebral ischemia underscores the need for further research, and long-term studies are essential to assess potential cognitive benefits and better define the role of CEP in selected patients.
