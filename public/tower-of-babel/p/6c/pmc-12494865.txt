Tunable band gap and ionic conductivity in PMMA and PANI blend with WO₃ nanocomposites for optoelectronics and energy storage devices
Authors: Ahlam I Al-Sulami, Nuha Y Elamin, Eman Aldosari, M O Farea, Soad S Alzahrani, Amani M Al-Harthi, Ebtesam M Alharbi, A Rajeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work presents PMMA/PANI–WO₃ nanocomposites with tunable optical and electrical properties, designed for optoelectronic and energy storage applications. The novelty of this study lies in the incorporation of WO₃ nanoparticles into a PMMA/PANI blend, enabling a simultaneous enhancement of dielectric behavior, ionic conductivity, and optical response. XRD and FTIR analyses confirmed reduced crystallinity and strong polymer–filler interactions. Optical studies revealed a notable decrease in the band gap from 3.80 eV for pure PMMA/PANI to 3.18 eV at the highest WO₃ loading, accompanied by improved refractive index. Thermal analysis demonstrated enhanced stability, while impedance spectroscopy showed an increase in ionic conductivity by nearly three orders of magnitude (10⁻⁸ → 10−5 S cm⁻1), along with a higher dielectric constant, attributed to interfacial polarization and improved charge mobility. These results establish WO₃ as an effective nanofiller for tailoring multifunctional properties in polymer composites, making the developed materials promising candidates for flexible optoelectronic devices, supercapacitors, and advanced energy storage systems.Keywords: PMMA/PANI-WO₃, XRD, Optical parameters, Electrical properties, AC conductivitySubject terms: Applied physics; Electronics, photonics and device physics

Introduction

Polymer nanocomposites have recently garnered significant attention from both researchers and industry due to their tunable physical, chemical, and electrical properties, making them suitable for a wide range of advanced technological applications1–3. Among these, polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) stands out as a widely used thermoplastic polymer from the polyacrylic ester family. It is especially valued for its excellent optical transparency, outstanding chemical resistance, and high biocompatibility, which make it suitable for use in biomedical devices, optics, and electronics4. To further enhance the functional properties of such polymers particularly their amorphous nature and mechanical flexibility polymer blending has emerged as a promising technique. This method is not only cost-effective and environmentally friendly, but also facilitates the creation of materials with improved structural flexibility, mechanical strength, and thermal stability5,6. In this context, blending PMMA with other functional polymers can significantly extend its application potential. One such polymer is polyaniline (PANI), a conducting polymer that has attracted considerable interest due to its simple synthesis, low cost, environmental stability, and excellent electrical conductivity7. The combination of PMMA and PANI in a polymer blend offers a strategic approach to producing nanocomposites that integrate the desirable optical and structural properties of PMMA with the superior electrical performance of PANI. Such hybrid systems are especially promising for use in optoelectronic devices, sensors, and energy storage technologies.

Polymer nanocomposites are advanced composite materials composed of a polymer matrix integrated with nano- or micro-sized fillers. The integration of these fillers meaningfully improves the physiochemical properties of the polymeric matrix, often imparting novel characteristics that are not present in the pure, unmodified polymer8,9. Alsulami et al.10 fabricated PANI/PMMA blend films doped with varying concentrations of TiO₂ nanoparticles using the casting method and found that increasing TiO₂ content enhanced the amorphous nature and thermal stability of the nanocomposites. They also observed a decrease in optical band gap and significant changes in optical properties, indicating the material’s potential for high-efficiency optoelectronic device applications. Morsi et al.11 studied PVDF/PVP polymer blend nanocomposites doped with various weight percentages of SeO₂/NiO hybrid nanoparticles and observed enhanced optical and electrical properties. The incorporation of these nanoparticles led to improved chain flexibility, reduced optical band gap, and increased DC conductivity, indicating their potential for use in optoelectronic and energy storage applications. Abutalib12 prepared PMMA/PANI nanocomposites loaded with graphene oxide (GO) nanoparticles via the casting method and found that GO incorporation enhanced amorphous character, improved thermal stability, and formed strong interactions with the polymer matrix. The optical band gap decreased with higher GO content, and the electrical conductivity increased, particularly at 0.9 wt% GO, due to improved charge carrier mobility. El-Naggar et al.7 fabricated PMMA/PANI/PEO nanocomposites doped with different quantities of MnFe₂O₄ nanoparticles and observed that increasing nanoparticle content reduced both Egd and Egi while enhancing the refractive index and dielectric properties. The study also revealed improved color purity and optical characteristics, suggesting the potential of these blends for optoelectronic and energy storage applications. Almehmadi et al.13 synthesized PEO/NaAlg nanocomposite films doped with WO₃ nanoparticles using a green solvent casting method and observed strong interfacial interactions, enhanced amorphous structure. Alharbi et al.14 prepared PVP/CMC blend films incorporated with WO₃ nanoparticles using casting and pulsed laser ablation methods, and found that increasing WO₃ content enhanced thermal stability, increased dielectric parameters, and reduced the optical band gap. The nanocomposites also exhibited improved ionic conductivity, indicating their potential for applications in energy storage. Morsi et al.15 fabricated PEG/CS nanocomposite films reinforced with WO₃ nanoparticles using a solution casting method and found that WO₃ incorporation decreased crystallinity, reduced the optical band gap, and induced structural disorder. The nanocomposites exhibited enhanced dielectric properties, AC conductivity, and photoluminescence behavior, indicating their strong potential for futuristic optoelectronic and energy storage applications. Tungsten trioxide (WO₃) is an n-type semiconductor with a perovskite-like structure, known for its high surface area and abundant active sites. It displays a wide range of intriguing physical and chemical properties, making it suitable for various advanced applications16,17. These nanoparticles are non-toxic, cost-effective, and easy to synthesize, while also exhibiting excellent photochromic and electrochromic behaviors. Moreover, they have a strong oxidative ability from valence band holes and show remarkable stability against harsh environments, such as continuous solar radiation and exposure to water18. Adding WO₃ nanoparticles into different polymer matrices is a common strategy to create polymer nanocomposites and nanodielectric materials that possess a range of enhanced and multifunctional properties19.

The review of previous studies highlights a gap in the development of polymer nanocomposites based on PMMA/PANI matrices infused with WO₃ nanoparticles, as such materials have not yet been synthesized or reported. This research is directed toward investigating the use of plasticized PMMA/PANI composites incorporated with WO₃ as solid polymer electrolytes for supercapacitor technologies. The main objective is to analyze how the integration of WO₃ affects the physiochemical properties of the nanocomposites. The experimental outcomes are interpreted with a focus on their potential utility in next-generation flexible, multifunctional optoelectronic devices. Academically, this study contributes to a deeper understanding of nanoscale interactions that govern the enhanced functionalities of nanocomposite polymers.

Methodology

Chemicals

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) with a MW = 35 × 104 g/mol (SKU, Germany) was utilized in this study. Anilinium chloride (ANCl) and dimethylformamide (DMF) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Ammonium peroxydisulfate (98% analytical reagent grade) and hydrochloric acid (40% AR grade) were sourced from Merck, Darmstadt, Germany. Tungsten trioxide nanoparticles (WO₃ NPs) with a purity of 98.96% and a molecular weight of 231.85 g.mol−1 were sourced from Merck, Darmstadt, Germany.

Fabrication of polyaniline (PANi)

Polyaniline (PANi) was prepared through the oxidative polymerization of anilinium chloride (ANCl) in an aqueous medium. Initially, 30 mmol of ANCl was dissolved in 100 mL of distilled water using a volumetric flask. Separately, 50 ml of ammonium persulfate was dissolved in another 100 ml of DDW. After 20 min, the oxidant solution was gradually added to the monomer solution. The reaction mixture was stirred continuously for 12 h, resulting in the formation of a green precipitate. This precipitate was collected and sequentially washed with 2 M hydrochloric acid, DDW, and ethanol.

Preparation of the PMMA/PANI-WO3

PMMA/PANI (80:20) composite was prepared by dissolving 0.8 gm of PMMA and 0.2 g. of PANI in 100 ml of dimethylformamide (DMF) with continuous stirring at room temperature for 3 h, ensuring complete dissolution. The resulting solution was processed using the casting technique. To fabricate polymer nanocomposite films, tungsten trioxide (WO₃) nanoparticles were incorporated into the PMMA/PANI solution at varying concentrations of x (wt%): 3.0, 5.0, 7.0, and 10.0.

To prevent nanoparticle agglomeration, the required amount of WO₃ was introduced into the PMMA/PANI solution under vigorous stirring and treated with a probe sonicator. The mixture was then further homogenized using magnetic stirring for 45 min. The resulting dispersion was poured into polypropylene dishes and allowed to dry slowly, as showed in Fig. 1. The final PMMA/PANI nanocomposite films had thicknesses ranging from 0.08 mm to 1.2 mm, measured using a digital micrometer.

Preparation of PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposites using the solution casting approach.

Characterization

The structural properties of the PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposites were investigated using XRD over a 2θ = 5–70°, employing a Shimadzu XRD 6000 diffractometer with CuKα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The chemical structure and functional groups of the synthesized films were characterized by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) using a JASCO FTIR-460 Plus spectrometer with a resolution of 3 cm⁻1, in the wavenumber range of 4000–400 cm⁻1. Optical absorption measurements were carried out in the 200–1000 nm wavelength range using a Shimadzu UV-3600 UV–VIS–NIR spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan). The electrical properties were assessed at room temperature using broadband dielectric spectroscopy (Novocontrol, Turnkey, Concept-40), with the film samples placed between two circular copper electrodes, each 12 mm in diameter.

Results and discussion

XRD

X-ray diffraction (XRD) was employed to investigate the structural properties of the PMMA/PANI blend and its nanocomposites incorporating various concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles, with the results presented in Fig. 2a. In the XRD spectrum, peaks at 2θ = 16.73°, 18.97°, and 25.65° are prominently associated with the Polyaniline component. These diffractions are indexed to the (011), (100), and (111) planes of PANI, respectively20. The two broad peaks observed at 2θ = 15° and 25° in the XRD pattern are related to PMMA, and are characteristic of its amorphous structure21. Furthermore, a noticeable reduction in the intensity of the diffraction peaks is observed in the doped PMMA/PANI nanocomposite films compared to the undoped film, escalating with increasing concentrations of WO₃2. The subsequent increase in amorphous regions is highly beneficial, as it enhances ionic conductivity (a phenomenon to be discussed later) by lowering the energy barrier for ion transport and facilitating increased segmental motion of the polymer chains22. A common finding in many studies is that the incorporation of a dopant consistently enhances the electrical conductivity of polymer films, concurrent with a decrease in their crystallinity and an increase in their amorphous content23. The decrease in the crystallinity of the blend films with higher dopant concentrations in this work could be caused by the interaction between the W+3 ions and the terminal polymer chains of the blend15. FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was used to study the vibrational bands of the nanocomposites. Figure 3 shows the FTIR spectrum of PMMA/PANI filled with varying concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles, recorded in the spectral range of 400–4000 cm⁻1. Figure 3 shows that the bands appearing between 450 and 850 cm⁻1 are attributable to C–H bending vibrations, whereas those at 1076 and 1257 cm⁻1 correspond to –C–O–C– and C–O bond stretching vibrations. The presence of PANI in the prepared films was confirmed by the vibrational bands located at 1446 cm−1 and 1166 cm−1, which are attributed to the stretching vibrations of N–B–N and N=Q=N structures, respectively. The N=Q=N feature, in particular, is associated with electron delocalization27. Moreover, the carbonyl group of PMMA produces a significant absorption peak at 1726 cm−1. The spectral region from 3150 to 2750 cm−1 additionally exhibits bands characteristic of different CH₃ and CH₂ vibrational modes. A reduction in the intensities of the peaks at 1166 cm−1, 2926 cm−1, and 3400 cm−1 is noted as WO₃ is incorporated into the PMMA/PANI polymer blend. A notable decrease in peak intensities throughout the FT-IR spectrum is may indicate of intermolecular interactions or bonding occurring between the PMMA/PANI and WO₃ components28. The reduction in band intensity at 1726 cm − 1 and its shift to a lower wavenumber upon doping can be attributed to the formation of hydrogen bonding between the PMMA/PANI blend and WO₃. Consequently, the incorporated WO₃ is believed to interact with the carbonyl (C═O) groups within the PMMA backbone structure. This interaction might arise from the formation of coordination bonds between the W3⁺ nano-sized ions and the C=O group of the ester, leading to a modified polymer network configuration for the polymer blend29. The reduced intensities of the peaks in the 3150 − 2750 cm−1 range, coupled with their shift to a higher wavenumber, may result from the interactions between the PMMA/PANI blend and WO₃ nanoparticles. Figure 3 effectively illustrates these structural changes induced in the PMMA/PANI matrix by filling with varying quantities of WO₃ NPs. Analysis of these bands further shows distinct peaks, indicating the successful incorporation and existence of the WO₃ inside the main polymer chains of the PMMA/PANI blends.

Optical properties

Absorption spectrum

The diffused absorbance of PMMA/PANI doped with different concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles is presented in Fig. 4 as a function of wavelength. Analysis of these absorption spectra demonstrates the presence of two distinct characteristic absorption peaks. The absorption spectrum features a primary peak at 253 nm, corresponding to the π − π ∗ electronic transition within the benzenoid and quinoid units. A second peak is identified at 524 nm, which is designated as the polaron band30. An increase in the absorbance of the PMMA/PANI was observed as the amount of doped WO₃ nanoparticles increased. Moreover, the absorption peaks of PMMA/PANI displayed a red shift with higher concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles. This spectral shift points to a decrease in the energy gap. The noted rise in the absorbance of the PMMA/PANI polymer blends upon WO₃ nanoparticle incorporation may result from multiple contributing factors, such as the inherent absorption of the WO₃, enhanced light scattering by the dispersed nanoparticles, and potentially an increase in charge carrier concentration within the composite. Furthermore, the prominent red shift of the PMMA/PANI absorption peaks with increasing WO₃ concentration indicates a possible reduction in the energy gap of the composite. This shift is likely a consequence of strong interfacial interactions, such as charge transfer or coordination bonding, between the PMMA/PANI matrix and the WO₃ nanoparticles. Such interactions can lead to an extended electronic delocalization along the polymer chains, particularly affecting the benzenoid and quinoid units and the polaron bands, thereby lowering the energy required for electronic transitions and resulting in the observed increase in conjugate bond length.

The values for both cases were obtained by extending the linear portion of the curves to the point of zero absorption. The derived direct and indirect optical band gap values are plotted as a function of WO₃ content in Fig. 8 and listed in Table 1. As depicted in Fig. 8, the pure PMMA/PANI polymer blend exhibits direct and indirect energy gap of 3.80 eV and 2.81 eV. These values systematically decreased with increasing concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles, reaching their minimum values of 3.18 eV (direct) and 2.14 eV (indirect) at 10 wt% WO₃ loading. This observed decrease in can be attributed to the formation of localized states within the bandgap of the PMMA/PANI polymers after the incorporation of WO₃35. It is worth noting that the optical band gap values (Egin and Egd) obtained in our study are significantly lower than those reported by El-Morsy et al.36, particularly for the Cs/ZnO/GO nanocomposites, which exhibited Egin and Egd values of 5.00 eV and 3.40 eV, respectively. This indicates enhanced electronic structure tuning in our samples, possibly due to better nanoparticle dispersion and stronger interfacial interaction within the PMMA/PANI matrix.

Thermal analysis

The thermal stability of the PMMA/PANI polymer blend and its nanocomposites incorporating different weight percentages of WO₃ nanoparticles (3 wt%, 5 wt%, 7 wt%, and 10 wt%) was evaluated using thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), as illustrated in Fig. 10. The TGA curves reveal a two-step degradation behavior typical for polymeric matrices. The first minor weight loss below ~ 200 °C can be attributed to the evaporation of residual moisture and volatile components. The major thermal degradation occurs in the temperature range of approximately 300–400 °C, corresponding to the decomposition of the polymer matrix (PMMA and PANI chains). It is evident that the incorporation of WO₃ nanoparticles significantly influences the thermal stability of the polymer blend. As the concentration of WO₃ increases, the thermal degradation onset shifts toward higher temperatures, and the total mass loss decreases. Notably, the sample containing 10 wt% WO₃ exhibits the highest thermal stability, retaining a larger residue at 600 °C compared to the pure PMMA/PANI blend. This enhancement in thermal resistance can be attributed to the barrier effect and high thermal resistance of the WO₃ nanoparticles, which delay the diffusion of volatile degradation products and suppress polymer chain scission40. The residual mass increases progressively with increasing WO₃ content, indicating the inorganic nature and thermal robustness of the nanofiller. These findings confirm that the inclusion of WO₃ nanoparticles improves the thermal durability of the polymer system, making the nanocomposites more suitable for high-temperature or thermally demanding optoelectronic applications.

AC conductivity

Figure 11 illustrates the AC electrical conductivity (σac) of the PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposites. The AC conductivity values demonstrably increased as the WO₃ filling levels rose. This improvement can be explained by the dissociation of WO₃, generating additional charge carriers within the PMMA/PANI matrix. Furthermore, the reduced crystallinity, as indicated by XRD analysis, may have contributed to increased charge carrier mobility. Collectively, these factors significantly enhanced charge transport41,42. Moreover, the improved electrical conductivity observed with higher WO₃ concentrations can be attributed to the interactions between the nanoparticles and the functional groups present in the PMMA/PANI host polymers. Here, σdc represents the DC conductivity, and s denotes the exponential factor. Table 1 presents the obtained values of σdc and s. It was observed that the values of s were below 1, implying that the charge conduction within the prepared films follows a hopping mechanism44,45. The enhanced electrical conductivity observed with increased WO₃ nanoparticles content is results from the interactions between WO₃ and the PMMA/PANi blend41. The PMMA/PANI sample loaded with the highest concentration of WO₃ exhibited the highest AC conductivity, measuring 1.65 × 10−6 S/cm. At the same time, the increased σ dc values in the doped films suggest the establishment of a 3-dimensional conductive pathway within the PMMA/PANI nanocomposites by WO₃ nanoparticles46. This behavior is consistent with XRD findings, demonstrating that the film with the highest WO₃ concentration possessed a more pronounced amorphous structure relative to the other compositions. Furthermore, the electrical conductivity values obtained in our study are significantly higher than those reported by Ragab et al.47, whose highest conductivity value reached only 1.55 × 10⁻1 S/cm. This improvement highlights the enhanced charge transport properties of our nanocomposite system, likely due to the optimized filler dispersion and polymer-filler interactions. In order to further understand the conduction behavior in the PMMA/PANI–WO₃ nanocomposites, it is essential to consider the types of electrical conduction mechanisms involved. The frequency-dependent AC conductivity follows Jonscher’s universal power law, which is characteristic of disordered materials and suggests that charge transport is primarily governed by a hopping conduction mechanism. This implies that the charge carriers move via localized states between trap sites rather than through extended band conduction. The decrease in the exponential parameter s with increasing WO₃ content supports this interpretation, as it indicates enhanced charge hopping facilitated by the improved dispersion and interfacial interactions of the WO₃ nanofillers48. Additionally, the increase in DC conductivity (σdc) in conjunction with reduced crystallinity observed in the XRD patterns suggests the possible presence of thermally assisted ion hopping or polaron hopping, which becomes more effective as the polymer matrix becomes more amorphous. These findings confirm that the dominant conduction in the studied films arises from a combination of hopping mechanisms, strongly influenced by the structural and morphological modifications induced by WO₃ incorporation.

Dielectric analysis

Figure 12 and 13 show the frequency-dependent variations in the dielectric constant (ε′) and dielectric loss (ε′′) for the PMMA/PANI blend and its composites containing various concentrations of WO₃ nanoparticles. The dielectric ε′ and ε′′ exhibited an inverse relationship with frequency, increasing at lower frequencies and subsequently decreasing or stabilizing at higher frequencies. This rise in ε ′ and ε ′′ at low frequencies suggests a notable increase in the alignment of parallel dipoles within the PMMA/PANI nanocomposite49. The increase of ε ′ and ε ′′ at low frequency was due to interfacial polarization. Conversely, as frequency increased, electron polarization gained prominence over dipole and space charge polarization, which consequently reduced the dielectric constant50. The synergistic interaction between the PMMA/PANI polymer blend and the WO₃ nanoparticles led to increase ε′ and ε′′ in the doped samples in comparison with the undoped PMMA/PANI41. The parallel arrangement of dipoles is induced by electrostatic interactions between the WO₃ molecules and the PMMA/PANI matrix’s functional groups. Consequently, micro-capacitors are formed and distributed throughout the solid polymer electrolyte films51.

Following this, M′ increases nonlinearly with frequency, eventually reaching a plateau at high (f, Hz), signifying maximum polarization52. The curve of M′′, presented in Fig. 15, reveals a relaxation peak due to the M.W.S. relaxation mechanism. The movement of ionic charges between coordination sites, in conjunction with the segmental relaxation of the polymer, results in conduction within polymer electrolytes53. The peak position in the modulus loss spectra for PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposites was observed to shift to higher frequencies, which implies faster relaxation dynamics. This may be linked to the WO₃ nanoparticles’ role in diminishing the energetic barriers for dipole reorientation. For dielectric capacitor applications, this accelerated relaxation is desirable, as it directly correlates with improved charge/discharge rates.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposite solid polymer electrolytes, utilizing a low-cost solution casting approach. XRD analysis revealed a reduction in crystallinity from 34.18% to 18.53% with increasing WO₃ content, indicating enhanced amorphous regions that facilitate ionic mobility. FT-IR spectra confirmed strong interactions between WO₃ and the polymer chains. Optical measurements showed increased absorbance and redshifted peaks in UV–Vis spectra, with a notable decrease in both direct and indirect energy gaps to 3.18 eV and 2.14 eV, respectively, at 10 wt% WO₃. These effects are attributed to the formation of localized energy states within the matrix. Electrically, the addition of WO₃ improved AC conductivity, which reached a maximum of 1.65 × 10⁻⁶ S/cm. Electrical modulus and Argand plot analyses further supported faster relaxation processes and better ionic transport with increasing WO₃ loading. Overall, the improved amorphous nature, reduced band gaps, and enhanced electrical behavior suggest that PMMA/PANI-WO₃ nanocomposites are promising materials for flexible optoelectronic devices and energy storage systems.
