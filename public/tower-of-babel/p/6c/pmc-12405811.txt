Mild and Selective Trifluoromethylation of Peptides at Tryptophane Residues by a Water‐Soluble Copper Complex with Redox‐Active Ligands
Authors: Marie‐Julie Tilly, Loïc Groslambert, Nolwenn Le Breton, Sylvie Choua, Frédéric Melin, Petra Hellwig, Thomas Weissenberger, Peter Faller, Laurent Raibaut, Marine Desage‐El Murr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelective chemical modifications of biomolecules are in high demand for the development and implementation of chemical biology strategies. Methods involving radicals have emerged as powerful options yet potentially generate side reactivities when involving harsh reaction conditions. We report the development of a mild catalytic method for the selective functionalization of tryptophane residues in peptides with a trifluoromethyl group using a water‐soluble copper complex with redox‐active ligands. This method displays both inter‐ and intramolecular selectivity for tryptophane residues and its synthetic relevance is demonstrated by the selective conversion of a 13‐residue peptidic sequence and an endomorphine analogue in good yields.

Introduction

Late‐stage functionalization of aminoacids and biomolecules by synthetic groups offers attractive prospects in chemical biology and can serve several purposes, including spectroscopic detection, imaging, and subsequent chemical elaboration toward more complex architectures.[ 1 , 2 ] Selectivity and efficiency are key issues for the applicability of these strategies,[ 3 ] but mild conditions are also desirable to ensure stability of the products and avoid competing side reactions. Among groups of interest to be transferred, fluorine‐containing groups, and particularly CF3 have attracted much attention[ 4 ] due to their ability to modify metabolic and pharmacokinetic profiles of drug candidates,[ 5 ] their implication in structural proteomics[ 6 ] or their effect on protein folding.[ 7 ] Harnessing the potential of radical processes to take place in biological media,[ 8 ] several methods for the introduction of CF3 groups in aminoacids have been reported.[ 4 ] These methods mostly rely on radical manifolds using CF3SO2Na in combination with radical initiators such as tert‐butyl‐hydroperoxide (TBHP),[ 9 ] or CF3SO2Na or CF3I under photoredox catalysis with iridium complexes.[ 10 , 11 ] Under these conditions, (hetero)aromatic sites such as His, Trp, Tyr, and Phe residues or nucleophilic Cys are often reactive and this can impair selectivity, although careful tuning of the pH enhances control over the selectivity. Alternative trifluoromethylating sources include hypervalent iodine derivatives and late‐stage modification of peptides and proteins by introduction of an aryl‐CF3 group at cysteine residues with diaryliodonium salts has been reported.[ 12 ]

The indole‐based aminoacid tryptophane (Trp) is widely present in biomolecules, as at least 90% of native proteins have one Trp residue incorporated in the primary sequence, but only at a frequency of 1.4%, which makes this residue an interesting target for post‐functionalization of peptides.[ 13 ] A copper‐catalyzed trifluoromethylation of indoles using Togni reagent has been reported[ 14 ] and visible‐light‐activated methods for the introduction of CF3 groups in Trp residues using iridium‐based photoredox catalysts,[ 10 ],[ 15 ] reductive catalysis[ 16 ], or radical initiators[ 9 ] have been developed. These approaches have led the way, and synthetic protocols using mild radical generation conditions and/or earth‐abundant metals require further attention while selectivity in peptidic sequences bearing several reactive heteroaromatic residues can be difficult to achieve.[ 17 ] Figure 1.

Synthetic methods for the introduction of CF3 groups in peptides.

Drawing inspiration from metalloenzymes,[ 18 ] complexes of earth‐abundant metals bearing redox‐active ligands can engage in controlled radical mechanisms by storing and releasing electrons from their ligands.[ 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 ] This circumvents the variation of metallic oxidation state and enables them to operate under very mild conditions. A copper complex with redox‐active iminosemiquinone ligands Cu(SQ)2,[ 23 ] (Scheme 1, SQ : iminosemiquinone) has been shown to perform the controlled release of CF3 • radicals and subsequent functionalization of indolic derivatives in organic solvents.[ 24 ] Aiming to emulate this behavior in biological media, we turned our attention to the design of a water‐soluble version of this complex. Here, we report a radical trifluoromethylation protocol for indolic residues that does not require light activation or radical initiators, and performs the selective functionalization of tryptophane residues. This catalyst operates with a distinctive selectivity as tryptophane residues can be selectively functionalized in the presence of other heteroaromatic residues, such as tyrosine, histidine, and phenylalanine.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of a Water‐Soluble Copper Complex

The synthesis of a water‐soluble redox‐active copper complex was targeted by introducing a carboxylic acid functional group on the pendant aromatic ring of an aminophenol ligand. Proligand 1 was prepared by refluxing di‐tBu‐catechol and 4‐aminobenzoic acid in the presence of a catalytic amount of Et3N in 56%, yield and its structure was confirmed by X‐ray crystallography (Scheme 1). Subsequent complexation with CuCl, and using PPh4 + as a counterion, led to the isolation of copper complex 2 in 81% yield as confirmed by HRMS (high‐resolution mass spectrometry). Several recrystallization attempts led only to weakly diffracting crystals and could only confirm the connectivity, although no exploitable bond lengths could be obtained. UV‐vis spectroscopy of 2 was performed in methanol (Figure 2a) and features a broad band centered around 786 nm. In analogy with the parent Cu(SQ)2 complex, this band could be indicative of an intervalence charge transfer.[ 23 ]

The X‐band EPR spectrum of complex 2 was recorded at 100 K in methanol. As previously observed for the parent complex Cu(SQ)2, the two radical spins are strongly antiferromagnetically coupled giving rise to the observed singlet ground state, which is reflected in the EPR spectrum by an almost isolated Cu2+.[ 23 ] Indeed, the EPR parameters obtained from the simulation show an axial g value with 63,65Cu hyperfine coupling close to values expected from a single ion Cu2+ (Figure 2b).[ 23 ] These results confirm that 2 is a CuII complex with antiferromagnetically coupled radical ligands.

The electrochemical behavior of 2 was studied in both DMSO and water with a glassy carbon disk as working electrode. In DMSO (Figure 3a), the complex exhibits two reversible monoelectronic reduction waves at E1/2 = ‐0.86 V and ‐1.19 V versus Fc+/Fc, respectively (or ‐0.36 V and ‐0.69 V vs. AgCl/Ag, respectively), and two quasi‐reversible monoelectronic oxidation waves at E1/2 = ‐0.46 V and ‐0.08 V versus Fc+/Fc, respectively (or + 0.04 V and + 0.42 V vs. AgCl/Ag, respectively). As compared to the parent [Cu(SQ)2] complex,[ 23 ] all waves are shifted to more anodic potentials, as expected from the introduction of the electron‐withdrawing carboxylic acid group on the ligands. The reduction waves are attributed to the sequential reduction of the two ligands from iminosemiquinones to aminophenols, and the oxidation waves to the sequential oxidation of the two ligands from iminosemiquinones into iminobenzoquinones. In water (Figure 2b), the two reduction waves almost merge together, resulting in a main cathodic peak at ‐0.29 V versus AgCl/Ag along with a shoulder at ‐0.15 V and only one anodic peak at + 0.05 V. Similarly, only one irreversible oxidation signal is observed at Epa = +0.86 V versus AgCl/Ag. The oxidation and reduction of the two ligands occur thus almost simultaneously in water and sequentially in DMSO.

Reactivity of Complex 2

Trifluoromethylation of Boc‐tryptamine 3 catalyzed by complex 2.

Reactivity of complex 2 in radical CF3 group transfer was first assessed in comparison[ 24 ] with its nonwater‐soluble analogue Cu(SQ)2. Boc‐tryptamine 3, soluble in both CH2Cl2 and MeOH, was selected as a model substrate, and hypervalent iodine‐based electrophilic derivative 4 (Togni reagent: 1‐trifluoromethyl‐1,2‐benziodoxol‐3‐(1H)‐one) was selected as CF3 source. The corresponding monotrifluoromethylated adduct 5 functionalized at the 2‐position of the indole ring was isolated in 49% yield under the following conditions: 1.5 equiv. of 4, 20 mol% of 2 in MeOH at reflux for 7 hours (Scheme 2). Varying the catalyst loading to 10 mol% led to decreased yield, while 30 mol% led to polysubstitution and side products (Table S2). Performing the reaction with parent complex Cu(SQ)2 as a catalyst in CH2Cl2, 5 was obtained in 53% yield, confirming that both complexes have comparable catalytic activities. Switching to a model dipeptide 6 (Ac‐WG‐NH2), reaction conditions were screened (Table 1). In pure methanol (entries 1–3), the dipeptide signal disappears, suggesting that 6 either degrades or precipitates. DMSO and water are commonly used as co‐solvents for peptide functionalization under radical conditions[ 25 ],[ 26 ] and performing the reaction in DMSO/H2O 1:1 at 50 °C using 3 equiv. of Togni reagent 4 led to trifluoromethylated adduct 7 in 60% yield (entry 6). The reaction was followed by HPLC at 214 nm and full conversion of 6 into 7 is observed after 7 hours, while a small amount of bis‐trifluoromethylated by‐product was formed. All products were identified by LC‐MS (see Supporting Information Section 7.1). A blank experiment conducted in the absence of a catalyst led only to trace amounts of 7 (entry 7), thus demonstrating the essential role of complex 2 as catalyst.

The applicability of the method was investigated on two N‐acetylated tryptophan‐containing peptides of variable lengths (WG and GGWG) under the optimized conditions. Excellent yields for the CF3 incorporation were obtained in both cases (Scheme 3a). We next assessed the compatibility of complex 2 with peptide amine (H‐GGWG‐NH2) 9a containing an unprotected N‐terminus (see Supporting Information Section 7.2.1.) and unfortunately, a fast disappearance of the starting peptide was observed by HPLC but did not lead to the expected product 9b (Scheme 3b), possibly resulting from competitive chelation of the copper center by the peptide free amine terminus. The effect of a lateral chain bearing a primary amine on the reactivity was explored by synthesizing peptide Ac‐GGWGK‐NH2 10a (Supporting Information Section 7.2.2.) containing a lysine residue and led to a similar observation (Scheme 3b), thus confirming that 2 is not compatible with primary amines, either as N‐terminus NH2 or in lateral chains. The kinetics of the conversion of Ac‐GGWG‐NH2 into Ac‐GGW(CF3)G‐NH2 8b under optimized conditions was followed by HPLC, and peak areas were used to estimate conversion (Figure 4). The trifluoromethylation reaction exhibits first‐order kinetics (t1/2 = ln(2)/k) with a half‐life of 81 minutes.

Having established the efficiency of complex 2 toward the functionalization of tryptophane residues, we aimed to assess its reactivity toward other aromatic aminoacids including tyrosine (Tyr, Y), histidine (His, H), and phenylalanine (Phe, F) (method A, Scheme 4). As a comparison, oxidative conditions using CF3SO2Na as a CF3 source adapted from reported literature procedures[ 9 , 27 ] were selected (method B, Scheme 4). While both methods enable the C2‐functionalization of Trp into 8b in 64% and 70% yields, respectively, the copper‐catalyzed manifold (method A) exhibits a strong selectivity toward Trp. Low reactivity toward tyrosine residues was observed as compound 11b is obtained in 18% yield and only trace amounts of functionalized histidine 12b and phenylalanine 13b are detected. By contrast, method B performs CF3 transfer on Tyr (45% yield), His (8% yield) and Phe (9% yield), and is therefore less selective. To further evaluate the selectivity, intermolecular competition experiments were carried out on an equimolar mixture of Ac‐GGWG‐NH2 and Ac‐GGYG‐NH2 (Scheme S13). With method A, we observe a selectivity for Trp with Ac‐GGW(CF3)G‐NH2 8b formed in 64% yield and Ac‐GGY(CF3)G‐NH2 11b in 18% yield. Under these conditions, the starting Trp‐containing peptide Ac‐GGWG‐NH2 was fully consumed, and 25% of the isomer products of Ac‐GGW(CF3)2G‐NH2 with CF3 incorporation in aromatic positions were observed (Figure S50). With method B, Ac‐GGW(CF3)G‐NH2 8b is formed in 49% yield and Ac‐GGY(CF3)G‐NH2 11b in 10% yield, while unreacted Ac‐GGWG‐NH2 (26%) and Ac‐GGYG‐NH2 (90%) remain. A second addition of the reagents (6 equiv. Langlois salt CF3SO2Na, 2.5 equiv. TBHP, and 0.1 equiv. FeCl2.4H2O) to the crude reaction mixture led to full conversion into a mixture of bis‐trifluoromethylated Ac‐GGW(CF3)2G‐NH2 position isomers, and 62% Ac‐GGY(CF3)G‐NH2 was formed, while 32% Ac‐GGYG‐NH2 11a remained (Figure S51). These experiments suggest that method A offers milder reaction conditions than method B, thus avoiding poly‐trifluoromethylation of the indole motif in Trp.

To gain insights into the mechanism, a UV‐vis spectroscopic study was performed (Figure S48). The intervalence band observed for complex 2 disappears upon addition of oxidative reagent 4, thus suggesting an electron transfer between the two partners. The reaction mixture shows a similar profile, and the titration of complex 2 with increasing amounts of reagent 4 shows gradual disappearance of the bands that becomes complete upon addition of one equivalent. Addition of TEMPO to the reaction mixture shuts the reaction down, thus suggesting a radical mechanism. From these combined mechanistic observations, it can be assumed that complex 2, designed as a water‐soluble version of Cu(SQ)2, performs the reaction through a similar mechanism involving a radical manifold initiated by ligand‐based SET (single electron transfer) to generate a CF3 radical followed by addition of this radical on the substrate (Scheme 5). The resulting trifluoromethylated radical then undergoes oxidation to the cation while the catalyst is regenerated by a second SET to the singly oxidized copper intermediate. This mode of action explains the observed differences between methods A and B. The first one relies on the catalytic and controlled generation of radicals, while the second one involves a classical initiation/propagation radical chain mechanism under strongly oxidative conditions, thus leading to harsher conditions and the rise of polytrifluoromethylation. Furthermore, the selectivity of method A is also reinforced by the fact that the trifluoromethylated aminoacid becomes less nucleophilic and less reactive under these conditions.

Plausible mechanism for the trifluoromethylation reaction.

Finally, we studied intramolecular selectivity by synthesizing a peptide containing both Tyr and Trp residues within the same sequence (Scheme 6). This peptide exhibits very low solubility in water or buffer medium, and the amount of DMSO was increased (6:4 DMSO/H2O). After 5 hours, a product with a molar mass corresponding to the incorporation of one CF3 group was detected by LC‐MS analysis and could be isolated in 21% yield after preparative HPLC purification. LC‐MS/MS analysis indicated ionic fragments consistent with a trifluoromethylation of the Trp fragment, and no fragment corresponding to CF3 incorporation on Tyr was detected (Figures S55‐S58). Attempts to convert this peptide with method B were unsuccessful due to its low solubility. However, similar experiments conducted on smaller and soluble acetylated endomorphine (containing Trp, Tyr, Phe, and His) led to selective trifluoromethylation at the tryptophane residue in 23% yield with method A (and none at Tyr, Phe, and His), and no reaction with method B. These experiments confirm the excellent chemoselectivity (Trp over Tyr, Phe, and His) of the copper‐catalyzed method, which could be attributed to the easier rearomatization of the radical after addition in the case of tryptophane than in the case of tyrosine.

Conclusion

We have developed a highly chemo‐ and regioselective catalytic method for the mild and biocompatible trifluoromethylation of peptides. Although so far limited to peptides with N‐acetylated terminus, this study shows enhanced selectivity toward tryptophane residues compared to reported methods and opens the door to the development of bioinspired catalysts for biomolecule modifications.
