Denosumab stimulates spermatogenesis in infertile men with preserved Sertoli cell capacity
Authors: Christine H. Andreassen, Rune Holt, Li Juel Mortensen, Nadia Krarup Knudsen, John E. Nielsen, Nadia Nicholine Poulsen, Sam K. Yahyavi, Ida M. Boisen, Zhihui Cui, Luisina Ongaro, Jasmin P. Hjerresen, Birgitte G. Toft, Thomas Hasselager, Niklas R. Jørgensen, Daniel J. Bernard, Anders Juul, Charles O’Brien, Anne Jørgensen, Martin Blomberg Jensen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummarySperm production depends on proper Sertoli-germ cell interaction, and we hypothesized that receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL) activity in Sertoli cells may influence spermatogenesis. Treatment with the RANKL inhibitor denosumab, normally used to treat osteoporosis, increased testicular weight, inhibin B, and germ cell proliferation in ex vivo testis cultures and in vivo in a humanized RANKL mouse. The effect on germ cell proliferation was positively associated with baseline serum concentrations of anti-müllerian hormone (AMH). In accordance, denosumab increased germ cell proliferation in ex vivo human testis cultures with low/moderate but not severe impairment of Sertoli cell function. In a placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial, denosumab had no effect on semen quality but increased sperm concentration in a subgroup of infertile men with serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L at baseline. In conclusion, high serum AMH may increase the probability of a beneficial response to denosumab treatment in infertile men, thus suggesting a possible venue for precision medicine in male infertility.

Introduction

Infertility is a global health problem affecting 17.5% of all couples worldwide.1 Male factor infertility contributes to estimated 50% of all infertile cases.2,3,4,5 Despite the high incidence of male infertility, only few treatment options exist for specific conditions like varicocele,6 obstructive azoospermia,7 and hypogonadotropic hypogonadism.5 As a result, ∼90% of infertile men receive no treatment. Instead, women from infertile couples are treated with intrauterine inseminations or invasive assisted reproductive technologies (ARTs) such as in vitro fertilization (IVF) and intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).5,8 The limited number of treatment options available for male infertility may be ascribed to the few identified factors that can influence male reproduction in adulthood and the multifactorial etiologies of male infertility ranging from genetic mutations, developmental abnormalities, and acquired postnatal factors to idiopathic infertility.4,5,9 Several medical treatment options for male infertility have been investigated including clomiphene,10 follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH),11,12,13 folic acid + zinc supplementation,14 inhibitors of reactive oxygen species,15,16 and supplementation with vitamin D + calcium.17 None of these have proven effective in larger cohorts of infertile men probably due to multiple etiologies to the male infertility problem. Therefore, we suggest that infertile men should be stratified based on specific biomarkers, enabling identification of the subgroups of infertile men that are likely to benefit from drugs stimulating spermatogenesis, such as FSH or clomiphene.

Male fertility potential is evaluated by semen analysis comprising sperm count, concentration, motility, and morphology.18 Sperm concentration and motility are good predictors of male fertility potential, but total number of motile or progressive motile sperm is predominantly used in fertility clinics to guide treatment selection.19 In the testes, Sertoli cells control and support the germ cells undergoing spermatogenesis and regulate the milieu within the seminiferous tubules.20,21,22 Sertoli cells are under strong influence of endocrine factors such as FSH and testosterone.23,24 FSH stimulates sperm production and release of anti-müllerian hormone (AMH) and inhibin B. Serum and seminal fluid concentrations of AMH and inhibin B have been proposed as markers of Sertoli cell function and spermatogenesis in infertile men.25,26,27 It was recently demonstrated that Sertoli cells express receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (RANKL),28 which signals through the receptor activator of nuclear factor κB (RANK) expressed in spermatogonia and spermatocytes in mice and humans.28,29 Moreover, treatment with denosumab, an RANKL inhibitor used to treat osteoporosis,30 was shown to stimulate sperm production in a fraction of infertile men with severely impaired semen quality in a small non-placebo-controlled pilot intervention study.28 Noteworthily, the response to RANKL inhibition was either detrimental or highly beneficial. This indicates that denosumab treatment should only be used in a subgroup of infertile men, and these men should preferably be identified using yet-unidentified biomarkers before initiation of treatment. Through a translational setup comprising human and mouse testis tissue culture, animal models, and a randomized clinical trial (RCT) in infertile men, we aimed to identify a clinical marker to assist in the selection of infertile men who would benefit from treatment with RANKL inhibitors to stimulate spermatogenesis.

Results

Denosumab treatment increases testis weight, inhibin B, and sperm count in humanized RANKL mice

RANKL was predominantly expressed in the cytoplasm of Sertoli cells, spermatocytes, and round spermatids whereas the receptor RANK was expressed in most spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids in wild-type mice (Figure S1). The decoy receptor osteoprotegerin (OPG) was expressed in spermatogonia, spermatids, and in some peritubular myoid cells. In order to examine effects of denosumab treatment, a humanized RANKL mouse model was generated by inducing four point mutations in the Rankl gene, which enables denosumab to bind and inhibit RANKL signaling in these mice.31 Adult male humanized RANKL mice were randomly assigned to either a single subcutaneous injection of 5 mg/kg denosumab (n = 10) or vehicle (n = 9) and were sacrificed 6 weeks later. Denosumab treatment had no effect on body weight compared with vehicle treatment (Figure 1A, panel 1). Testicular weight was 22% higher in denosumab-treated mice compared with vehicle-treated mice (0.079 vs. 0.096 g; SEM ± 0.002 and 0.005; p = 0.006) (Figure 1A, panel 2), whereas epididymal, seminal vesicle, prostate, kidney, and femoral weights did not differ between treatment groups (Figure 1A, panels 3, 5–8). The increased testicular weight was reflected by increased sperm count (Figure 1A panel 4; 26.8 × 106 vs. 41.3 × 106; SEM ± 3.4 and 6.4; p = 0.0499) and 44% higher serum levels of inhibin B in denosumab- vs. vehicle-treated mice (487 vs. 701 pg/mL; SEM ± 39 and 56; p = 0.007) (Figure 1B, panel 1). Serum levels of AMH and FSH tended to be higher in denosumab-treated mice (52% and 18%, respectively), although not significantly different from those in the vehicle-treated group (AMH: 10.6 vs. 16.1 ng/mL; SEM ± 1.3 and 2.3; p = 0.060; FSH: 8.5 vs. 10.0 ng/mL; SEM ± 0.5 and 0.8; p = 0.124) (Figure 1B, panels 2 + 4). Serum testosterone levels in mice are highly dependent on luteinizing hormone (LH) pulsatility and did not differ between groups but tended to be higher in denosumab-treated compared to vehicle-treated mice (0.7 vs. 1.5 nmol/L; SEM ± 0.1 and 0.6; p = 0.199) (Figure 1B, panel 3). Serum LH tended to be higher in denosumab- vs. vehicle-treated mice although not statistically different (0.7 vs. 1.1 ng/mL; SEM ± 0.1 and 0.2; p = 0.071) (Figure 1B, panel 5). Inhibin B/FSH and testosterone/LH ratios were not significantly different but tended to be higher in the denosumab-treated mice (26% and 127%, respectively) compared to vehicle-treated mice (inhibin B/FSH: 58 vs. 73; SEM ± 6 and 7; p = 0.141; testosterone/LH: 1.1 vs. 2.5; SEM ± 0.2 and 1.2; p = 0.273) (Figure 1C, panels 1 + 2). Denosumab, OPG, and FSH treatment induces germ cell proliferation in ex vivo testis cultures from humanized RANKL mice

To study the impact of RANKL inhibition in both germ and Sertoli cells without systemic endocrine interference, a testis from the humanized RANKL mouse was cultured ex vivo using an approach originally established for prepubertal mouse testis tissue (Figure 1D).32 Cultures with preserved testicular morphology were used for further analyses (Figure 1E). Inhibition of RANKL signaling with denosumab (100 ng/mL, n = 11) for 48 h resulted in increased germ cell proliferation (assessed as bromodeoxyuridine [BrdU]+ cells/mm2) of 44% (100% vs. 144%; SEM ± 6.3 and 10.3; p = 0.004) relative to vehicle-treated cultures (n = 13) (Figures 1F and 1G, left panels). Inhibition of RANKL with OPG (100 ng/mL, n = 12) resulted in an increase of 51%, although not statistically significantly different (100% vs. 151%; SEM ± 6.3 and 18.3; p = 0.082). FSH treatment (100 ng/mL, n = 10) for 48 h served as a positive control and resulted in a 33% increase in germ cell proliferation (Figure 1G, left panel; 100% vs. 133%; SEM ± 6.3 and 8.9; p = 0.012). FSH (n = 10) and denosumab (n = 11) had no effect on germ cell apoptosis (determined as cleaved poly [ADP-ribose] polymerase [cPARP]+ cells/mm2), whereas OPG (n = 12) decreased germ cell apoptosis by 31% (100% vs. 69%; SEM ± 8.3 and 5.6; p = 0.019) (Figures 1F and 1G, right panels). Serum levels of AMH in mice at the day of sacrifice were positively associated (R2 = 0.6434; p = 0.009) with the effect on germ cell proliferation after treatment with denosumab in ex vivo testis cultures (n = 9) and a comparable non-significant trend for FSH (n = 8) (Figure 1H, left and right panel). This suggests that the stimulatory effect of denosumab depends on Sertoli cell function as the Sertoli cell-derived AMH level in serum was predictive of the responsiveness to stimulatory treatments. No correlation was found between serum AMH and the effect of OPG on germ cell proliferation suggesting that not all stimulatory agents are dependent on Sertoli cell function and that OPG, but not denosumab, may exert some effects through tumor necrosis factor-related apoptosis-inducing ligand (Figures 1H and S3).

OPG is expressed in Sertoli cells from dysgenetic seminiferous tubules but not in tubules with full spermatogenesis

Testes from infertile men contain varying degrees of full spermatogenesis, spermatogenic arrest, Sertoli cell-only (SCO), and hyalinized seminiferous tubules, which complicate extrapolation from the humanized RANKL mouse testis cultures to humans. Therefore, the expression of RANKL, RANK, and OPG was investigated in adult human testis tissue with various degrees of dysgenesis and fetal testis to detect possible differences in the expression pattern. In adult human testicles with full spermatogenesis, RANKL was predominantly expressed in the cytoplasm of Sertoli cells, spermatocytes, and spermatids when examined by immunofluorescence and immunohistochemistry (IHC) (Figures 2A and 2B, upper panel). Expression of RANK was most prominent in spermatogonia and less pronounced in spermatocytes and spermatids and was not detected in Sertoli cells. OPG was expressed in all stages of germ cells with the most prominent expression in spermatogonia and spermatids. OPG was also expressed in some peritubular myoid cells, at the border between peritubular cells and spermatogonia, and in few Sertoli cells (Figure 2B, upper panel). Noteworthily, OPG was expressed in some Sertoli cells of dysgenetic tubules particularly in samples with severe atrophy compared with none or few Sertoli cells expressing OPG in seminiferous tubules with full spermatogenesis (Figure 2B). In tubules with SCO, both RANKL and OPG were markedly expressed in the Sertoli cell cytoplasm while no expression of RANK was detected. To evaluate differences in OPG expression in Sertoli cell cytoplasm, human testis specimens with full spermatogenesis (n = 3) and dysgenesis (n = 3) were scored for OPG staining using an arbitrary semiquantitative scale (1, no/weak OPG expression in Sertoli cells; 2, moderate/strong expression in few Sertoli cells or weak staining in several Sertoli cells; 3, strong staining in most Sertoli cells) in 15 tubules from each patient (Figure S4; Table S1). In tissue with full spermatogenesis, the distribution of tubules in each category was 76%/24%/0% whereas the corresponding numbers in tubules with dysgenesis were 11%/22%/67% (Table S1). GATA4 and SOX9 are nuclear Sertoli cell markers expressed in testis specimens with full spermatogenesis, while vimentin is detected in the cytoplasm and MIC2 in the membrane of the Sertoli cells (Figure 2B, upper panel). Interestingly, the subcellular expressions of these Sertoli cell markers were also altered in SCO specimens. GATA4 was frequently observed in the cytoplasm (Figure 2B, lower panel), and MIC2 expression was nearly absent in SCO, suggesting altered Sertoli cell function. Additionally, these cells expressed OPG (Figure 2B, lower panel). The Sertoli cells in tubules with SCO could be more immature and thus have an expression profile resembling fetal Sertoli cells. Therefore, the expression of RANKL, RANK, and OPG was investigated in human fetal testes from gestational week 9–11 (Figure 2C; Table S2). OPG was undetectable in fetal Sertoli cells but was expressed in gonocytes and few Leydig cells. RANKL was abundantly expressed in gonocytes identified as OCT4+ on the adjacent serial section. A weak expression of RANKL was observed in the cytoplasm of some fetal Sertoli cells, which in serial sections expressed SOX9 and AMH. Expression of RANK was detected in some gonocytes and most fetal Leydig cells visualized by CYP11A1 expression in adjacent sections, and rarely in fetal Sertoli and interstitial cells. The proliferative effect of denosumab in human testis cultures depends on OPG expression and Sertoli cell function

To investigate whether OPG expression in Sertoli cells could be used as a marker for Sertoli cell dysfunction and therefore be predictive for a detrimental effect of RANKL inhibition on germ cell proliferation or apoptosis, human testis tissue cultures were exposed to 100 ng/mL of denosumab or vehicle treatment. A total of 45 hemi-orchiectomized patients were initially included (Figure 3A). In 25 patients, all tissue fragments contained germ cell neoplasia in situ (GCNIS) cells as determined by the presence of D2-40-positive cells and were excluded from further analysis. Additionally, samples containing only SCO tubules, showing severe dysgenesis, or when it was impossible to compare denosumab- and vehicle-treated samples from the same patient were discarded, leaving testicular samples from 15 patients in the study (Figures 3A and 3B). These samples had heterogeneous morphology and displayed various degrees of OPG staining (Figures 3B and 3D). Consequently, each seminiferous tubule was evaluated for OPG expression in Sertoli cell cytoplasm and stratified into three groups (OPG expression in Sertoli cells <20%, 20%–50%, or >50%) (Figures 3C and 3D). Treatment with denosumab for 48 h increased the proliferation of germ cells (BrdU+ cells/mm2) (Figures 3E and 3F, left panels) in tubules with low (<20%) and moderate (20%–50%) OPG staining (100% vs. 151%; SEM ± 14 and 15 for >20% and 100% vs. 163%; SEM ± 15 and 19 for 20%–50%; p = 0.002 and p = 0.004, respectively) but had no effect in tubules with high (>50%) OPG staining in Sertoli cells (Figure 3F, left panel). Moreover, denosumab treatment reduced the number of germ cells undergoing apoptosis (cPARP+ cells/mm2) (Figures 3E and 3F, right panels) in tubules with low (<20%) and moderate (20%–50%) OPG staining compared with vehicle (100% vs. 71%; SEM ± 28 and 6 for <20% and 100% vs. 83%; SEM ± 15 and 9 for 20%–50%; p = 0.001 and p = 0.047, respectively). Noteworthily, in tubules with high (>50%) OPG expression in Sertoli cells, apoptosis was increased (100% vs. 146%; SEM ± 16 and 23; p = 0.035) after exposure to denosumab although considerable variation was observed (Figure 3F, right panel). Low serum levels of AMH are linked with poor gonadal function in infertile men

A total of 869 men referred to a tertiary center for andrological evaluation due to impaired semen quality were assessed for eligibility to participate in the study “Copenhagen First” in the period from February 2017 to February 2019. In total, 317 men were invited to participate, and 100 men were included and randomized prospectively (Figure S5). Thus, 552 men were not eligible to participate mainly due to azoospermia, mildly impaired semen quality, severe comorbidities, poor dental status, indication for testis biopsy, chromosomal aberrations, or refusal to participate. Two men unexpectedly met an exclusion criterion after inclusion and were subsequently excluded due to malignant disease. Of these 98 men, two men from the intervention group and three from the placebo group were lost to follow-up leaving a total of 93 men completing the trial. The baseline characteristics of the 98 included men are presented in Table S3. To investigate the link between serum AMH, Sertoli cell function, and semen quality, all participating men with a serum AMH measurement at baseline (n = 95) were stratified in tertiles according to baseline serum AMH (Table 1). No effect on semen quality of denosumab vs. placebo treatment in 98 infertile men

“Copenhagen First” was a randomized clinical placebo-controlled trial designed to investigate the effect of a single dose of denosumab (60 mg) vs. placebo (saline) on semen quality in infertile men. The predefined primary outcome of the RCT was changes in sperm production (sperm concentration, sperm count, motile sperm, and progressive motile sperm) 80 days after injection, but none of these differed compared with baseline in the denosumab group (Figure 4A, panels 1–3; Table 2). Moreover, no differences were found in sperm production at day 80 between denosumab- and placebo-treated men. Men in both groups had a lower duration of abstinence at day 80 compared with baseline (ΔDmab: 3.0 vs. 4.0 days; p < 0.001 and ΔPlacebo: 3.0 vs. 3.5 days; p = 0.016), which may explain the lower semen volume at day 80 in the denosumab group compared with baseline (ΔDmab: 3.4 vs. 4.1 mL; p = 0.047). No changes from baseline or differences between treatment arms were found for sperm motility or morphology variables at day 80 (Table 2). Also, at day 160 there were no changes in semen quality variables in denosumab-treated men compared with baseline or the placebo group. In the placebo group no differences in sperm parameters were observed at day 160 except for a lower duration of abstinence (ΔPlacebo: 3.0 vs. 3.5 days; p = 0.002) and semen volume (ΔPlacebo: 3.2 vs. 3.8 mL; p = 0.024) (Table S4). A potent suppression of soluble RANKL (sRANKL) in the seminal fluid was evident as men randomized to denosumab treatment experienced a marked decrease at day 80 compared with placebo-treated men and compared with their baseline level (1.5 vs. 25 pmol/L, p < 0.001 and ΔDmab: 1.5 vs. 31 pmol/L, p < 0.001, respectively) (Figure 4A, panel 4; Table 2). There was no difference in seminal fluid OPG levels between denosumab- and placebo-treated men at day 80 (188 vs. 237 pmol/L; p = 0.212) (Figure 4A, panel 5). Moreover, men treated with denosumab had a 14% lower serum FSH (ΔDmab: 5.1 vs. 6.0 IU/L; p = 0.006) and 35% higher inhibin B/FSH ratio (ΔDmab: 38 vs. 28; p = 0.002) at day 80 compared with baseline (Figure 4B, panels 1 + 5). At day 80, denosumab did not increase serum AMH (ΔDmab: 41 vs. 39 pmol/L; p = 0.081) or testosterone (ΔDmab: 18 vs. 17 nmol/L; p = 0.076) although a tendency toward an increase was observed (Figure 4B, panels 3 + 4). Surprisingly, placebo-treated men had an 11% lower serum FSH (ΔPlacebo: 5.1 vs. 5.7 IU/L; p = 0.030) and 37% higher inhibin B/FSH ratio (ΔPlacebo: 49 vs. 36; p = 0.006) at day 80 compared with baseline (Figure 4B, panels 1 + 5). No differences in reproductive hormones were found between men treated with denosumab and placebo at day 80 (Table 2). At day 160, denosumab-treated men had a 3% lower serum AMH compared with baseline (ΔDmab: 38 vs. 39 pmol/L; p = 0.037), but no differences in reproductive hormones were found between treatment groups (Table S4). During the trial period, 71 of the participating men were actively trying to achieve a pregnancy with their partner (34 men treated with denosumab and 37 treated with placebo, respectively) and were simultaneously undergoing ART (IVF or ICSI). In denosumab-treated men, 68% achieved pregnancy with a live birth rate of 56%, whereas 65% of placebo-treated men achieved pregnancy with a live birth rate of 59% (Table S5). Adverse effects of denosumab

Adverse events were reported by 40 infertile men: 17 in the denosumab group and 23 in the placebo group. Frequent adverse effects were infection, musculoskeletal pain, and gastrointestinal symptoms (Table S6). One man treated with denosumab experienced low serum ionized calcium (1.06 mmol/L) 14 days after injection which was normalized by doubling his daily supplement of vitamin D and calcium intake for two weeks. Two participants were per protocol excluded from the trial; one man from the placebo group was diagnosed with malignant melanoma, and one man in the denosumab group was diagnosed with testis cancer three months after his last visit. A urologist evaluated the patient with testis cancer two months before inclusion due to a small process in his left testicle, which was initially assessed as benign with no need for biopsy, but ten months after his first ultrasound a new scan showed growth and a biopsy revealed testis cancer. During the trial, 39 live births were conceived. One child was born with congenital hip dysplasia, and another was diagnosed with infantile spasms. Both children were conceived from fathers in the placebo group. One participant from the denosumab group achieved a pregnancy 17 weeks before the day of inclusion. His child developed a rare type of liver cancer within the first year of life. This incident is not considered related to denosumab, primarily due to the time of conception but also the negligible concentration of denosumab in seminal fluid that cannot affect the fetus.33 A total of eight spontaneous abortions were reported, four in each group (Table S5). No difference in sperm DNA fragmentation was found between men treated with denosumab and placebo at day 80, and no change in sperm DNA fragmentation from baseline was observed (Table 2). As expected, men treated with denosumab experienced a decrease in serum sRANKL (ΔDmab: 0.07 vs. 0.21 pmol/L; p < 0.001), OPG (ΔDmab: 3.1 vs. 3.4 pmol/L; p = 0.015), and alkaline phosphatase (ΔDmab: 46 vs. 62 IU/L; p < 0.001) at day 160 compared with baseline (Table S7). The change in bone markers was mirrored by an increase in total bone mineral density (BMD) (ΔDmab: 1.32 vs. 1.31 g/cm2; p = 0.010), lumbar (L1–L4) BMD (ΔDmab: 1.27 vs. 1.23 g/cm2; p < 0.001), and total femoral BMD (ΔDmab: 1.11 vs. 1.03 g/cm2; p = 0.033). Procollagen-type I N-terminal propeptide (PINP) was lower at day 80 compared with baseline in denosumab-treated men (ΔDmab: 17 vs. 72 ng/L; p < 0.001), and type I collagen cross-linked C-telopeptide (CTX) was undetectable in all denosumab-treated men at day 80. Serum sRANKL and alkaline phosphatase were lower at day 160 in denosumab- vs. placebo-treated men (0.07 vs. 0.20 pmol/L and 46 vs. 60 IU/L; both p < 0.001, respectively) while PINP was lower at day 80 (17 vs. 71 ng/L; p < 0.001). There was no difference in BMD between treatment arms (Table S7).

Effect of denosumab vs. placebo in subgroups of infertile men with preserved Sertoli cell capacity determined by serum AMH

To identify potential biomarkers that could be used to predict treatment outcome, we conducted post hoc analyses on reproductive hormones and serum levels of sRANKL and OPG at baseline as markers of changes in sperm concentration on day 80 in men treated with denosumab and placebo. Interestingly, serum AMH was the most predictive marker (p = 0.004) in men treated with denosumab (Table S8). Further analyses revealed that in a subgroup of the infertile men (Dmab: n = 17, placebo: n = 21) with serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L at baseline, treatment with denosumab resulted in a 53% (2.9 million/mL) increase in average sperm concentration at day 80 compared with baseline (ΔDmab: 8.4 vs. 5.5 million/mL; p = 0.017), whereas no change in sperm concentration was found in placebo-treated men (Figure 5A, upper panel 1). In a model adjusted for baseline values and duration of abstinence, men treated with denosumab had a 5.6 million/mL higher sperm concentration (10.7 vs. 5.1 million/mL; p = 0.027) and a not significant but positive trend for higher total sperm count (32 vs. 17 million; p = 0.066) at day 80 compared with the placebo group (Figure 5A, upper panel 1 + 2). Interestingly, this subgroup of men treated with denosumab had a higher positive response rate (PRR) for sperm concentration (82%), total sperm count (76%), and total morphologically normal sperm (75%) at day 80 compared with placebo-treated men experiencing PRRs of 43%, 43%, and 40%, respectively (Figure 5A, lower panels 1, 2, and 4). At day 160, there were no changes or differences in semen quality (data not shown). Identical analyses were performed in the subgroup of men with low serum AMH (<38 pmol/L) at baseline. An additional combined subgroup of infertile men with serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L and high RANKL activity as characterized by a threshold set at median serum sRANKL of 0.24 pmol/L (Dmab: n = 6, placebo: n = 12) was used to examine whether effects were more pronounced in these men. In this subgroup, denosumab increased sperm concentration (ΔDmab: 11.5 vs. 4.8 million/mL; p = 0.022) and total sperm count (ΔDmab: 31 vs. 17 million; p = 0.007) at day 80 compared with baseline (Figure 5C, upper panels 1 and 2). Moreover, in a model adjusted for baseline levels and duration of abstinence, the denosumab group had 2.5-fold higher sperm concentration (ΔDmab: 11.5 vs. 3.4 million/mL; p = 0.016), sperm count (ΔDmab: 31 vs. 11 million; p = 0.043), and morphological normal sperm (ΔDmab: 1.4 vs. 0.4 million; p = 0.039) at day 80 compared with placebo-treated men (Figure 5C, upper panels 1, 2, and 4). Interestingly, in this subgroup, all men treated with denosumab had a PRR for sperm concentration and sperm count at day 80, significantly different compared with a PRR of 33% in placebo-treated men (Figure 5C, lower panels 1 +2). Denosumab-treated men had a 20% lower serum LH (ΔDmab: 3.3 vs. 4.1 IU/L; p = 0.032) and a non-significant tendency toward higher inhibin B/FSH ratio (ΔDmab: 41 vs. 25; p = 0.057) and testosterone/LH ratio (ΔDmab: 6.9 vs. 4.8; p = 0.058) at day 80 compared with baseline (Figure 5D). In the subgroup of men with low serum AMH (<38 pmol/L) and low serum RANKL (<0.24 pmol/L), denosumab had no effect on sperm production (Figure S7).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that RANKL inhibition systemically or specifically in the testis increases sperm production in mice and infertile men with preserved Sertoli cell capacity. RANKL is expressed in Sertoli cells and activates the receptor RANK in the germ cells unless this interaction is blocked by the decoy receptor OPG, which in healthy testis is produced in germ and peritubular cells. This finding is of immediate clinical interest because the RANKL inhibitor denosumab has been used to treat osteoporosis in postmenopausal women for several years.34 Denosumab injection in humanized RANKL mice demonstrated that transient systemic RANKL inhibition stimulated spermatogenesis as shown by the increase in testis size, serum inhibin B, and sperm count. The increase in serum inhibin B supports that one injection of denosumab is sufficient to stimulate Sertoli-germ cell interaction and spermatogenesis. The observed effects could be mediated indirectly by a possible pituitary effect of systemic RANKL inhibition, which was supported by the denosumab-mediated increase in serum levels of gonadotropins (FSH and LH). However, the increases in serum inhibin B/FSH ratio, testosterone/LH ratio, and testicular weight suggest a predominant testicular effect. This suggestion is strongly supported by the increased germ cell proliferation in the mouse ex vivo testis culture model, which demonstrates direct effects of denosumab on spermatogenesis independently of pituitary function.

The denosumab dose used in the ex vivo testis cultures was lower than the measured concentration in the seminal fluid following a single injection of denosumab 60 mg in young men and thus achievable in the clinical setting.33 Denosumab had, in contrast to OPG, no effect on germ cell apoptosis, which suggests that RANKL primarily influences spermatogonial cell proliferation rather than apoptosis in mice, which highlights an important difference between mice and humans where RANKL inhibition also reduces germ cell apoptosis.28 The mouse ex vivo testis culture model has several limitations, but the observed stimulatory effect of FSH on germ cell proliferation validated its application to study the Sertoli-germ cell interaction.35 Moreover, AMH concentrations measured in serum from the mice before sacrifice were associated with both FSH- and denosumab-mediated increases in germ cell proliferation in the testis cultures. Serum AMH may therefore at least in part reflect Sertoli cell function and possibly predicts the treatment-induced response of denosumab or FSH on germ cell proliferation in mice. Extrapolation to the human testis is difficult due to species-specific differences but most importantly testes from infertile men contain varying degrees of full spermatogenesis, spermatogenic arrest, SCO, and hyalinized seminiferous tubules.36 Therefore, a marker to evaluate dysgenesis was required, and expression of OPG in Sertoli cells appeared to be a good candidate since it was only found in few Sertoli cells in specimens with full spermatogenesis but in most tubules with dysgenesis and SCO. The altered OPG expression coincided with changes in the subcellular expression of classical Sertoli cell markers, which suggests aberrant Sertoli cell function.37 OPG expression in the Sertoli cells was indeed associated with the severity of testicular dysgenesis and was therefore tested as a predictor of the effect of denosumab in human testis specimens. A beneficial effect of denosumab was limited to testis specimens with preserved Sertoli cell capacity characterized by low (<20%) or moderate (20%–50%) OPG expression in Sertoli cells, while Sertoli cells with high OPG expression (<50%) had a significant denosumab-mediated increase in germ cell apoptosis. This clearly indicates that the stimulatory effect of denosumab treatment is dependent on an adequate Sertoli cell function and local RANKL signaling, which highlights the need for predictive biomarkers that can be used in the clinic to select patients prior to denosumab treatment. OPG expression cannot be used routinely, but AMH is released from Sertoli cells38,39 into circulation and serum AMH emerged as a possible marker of Sertoli cell function. In the cohort of infertile men with a long duration of infertility, low serum AMH was associated with cryptorchidism, lower testis size, higher serum FSH, and lower serum inhibin B and testosterone. Also, a significant negative trend for total and progressive motile sperm count was observed, which combined suggests that low serum AMH is linked with impaired Sertoli cell function in infertile men.

In the 98 infertile men randomized to a single subcutaneous injection with 60 mg denosumab once or placebo, no effect on semen quality was found 80 days after injection. Denosumab treatment suppressed seminal fluid sRANKL levels, which confirms that denosumab enters and blocks RANKL in the male reproductive organs. A single denosumab injection improved Sertoli cell function as demonstrated by an increase in inhibin B/FSH ratio although the placebo group experienced an unexpectedly similar increase in inhibin B/FSH ratio, which may be coincidental or influenced by the concomitant vitamin D supplementation.17 In an exploratory post hoc analysis of a subgroup of men with serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L at baseline (corresponding to 41% of all men), a significantly higher sperm concentration (5 million/mL) was observed in individuals treated with denosumab compared with placebo. This difference can be considered clinically relevant in infertile men with severely impaired semen quality and corresponds to a 100% increase. Moreover, this observation supports that serum AMH may be a tool to assist with the selection of infertile men with preserved Sertoli cell capacity that may benefit from denosumab treatment since more than 80% of infertile men in this subgroup had an increase in sperm concentration. In men with low AMH (<38 pmol/L) at baseline, denosumab decreased sperm production at day 80, indicating that denosumab treatment in men with low serum AMH may have a deteriorating effect on sperm production. To improve the proportion of beneficial responders, serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L was combined with baseline serum levels of sRANKL ≥0.24 pmol/L corresponding to 19% of the included infertile men. In this subgroup, denosumab increased sperm concentration and total sperm count in all men, indicating that a beneficial treatment response depends on both Sertoli cell function and RANKL activity as well as other possible factors.

The variable etiology of male infertility is the best explanation for the lack of existing treatment options. Our data indicate that serum AMH may be a suitable biomarker to screen candidates for stimulatory treatment; however, prospective evaluation is necessary. Moreover, serum AMH may need to be combined with other markers such as inhibin B, testosterone, testis size, DNA fragmentation, cryptorchidism, severe varicocele, age, BMI, or other comorbidities in different subtypes of infertile men. A novel prospective RCT has been initiated where the effect of denosumab versus placebo is tested in oligospermic infertile men selected based on serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L.40 Importantly, even if a subsequent clinical trial reports a positive outcome, several safety issues must be addressed before denosumab can be offered as a treatment option for male infertility. Denosumab is dosed every 6 months for osteoporosis34 and is linked with frequent mild, but also rare serious, adverse effects.41,42 In the present study, denosumab had no effects on sperm DNA fragmentation or spontaneous abortions, and no children conceived after denosumab treatment were born with diseases. However, this study was not powered to detect possible differences, and larger studies with longer follow-up are required to determine fetal safety. The risk of rebound BMD loss after denosumab discontinuation is small because the effect of a single dosage of denosumab on BMD is low in infertile men with no osteoporosis. One patient treated with denosumab was excluded from the analysis as he developed testis cancer during the study, and denosumab could theoretically stimulate the growth of this type of tumor. However, denosumab usage has not been linked with reproductive cancer incidences,43 and a recent study showed that neither denosumab nor OPG promotes the proliferation of GCNIS cells ex vivo or testicular germ cell tumors in vitro or in vivo.44

In conclusion, this study shows that RANKL inhibition influences the Sertoli-germ cell interaction and increases sperm production in mice and infertile men with preserved Sertoli cell capacity as determined by serum AMH levels. Prospective studies including men based on serum AMH levels are required to investigate whether a fraction of infertile men may benefit from stimulatory treatment with denosumab.

Limitations of the study

This translational study utilized in vivo and testicular ex vivo mouse models to determine the effects of denosumab on testicular function. However, direct extrapolation of these effects to human testes is challenging due to species-specific differences, in particular due to inherent differences between mouse testes and dysgenetic human testes with varying degrees of impaired spermatogenesis in infertile men. In the subsequent human ex vivo culture experiments, the degree of OPG expression in Sertoli cells was used as a proxy for testicular dysgenesis when examining the effects of denosumab. Although this may not be optimal, it highlights the importance of identifying suitable biomarkers that alone or in combination can be used to distinguish poor from good responders to treatments such as denosumab. Technical issues of counting sperm using NC-3000 rather than manual counting should be noted, as it makes direct comparisons with other studies difficult.

Experimental model and study participant details

Humanized RANKL mice

The humanized RANKL mouse model was kindly provided by Charles O’Brien (Arkansas, US).31 In brief, the mice were generated by altering the coding region of the murine Receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-Β ligand ((Rankl (Tnfsf11)) gene with CRISPR-based gene editing in C57BL/6 mice. Four amino acids in the gene were altered, thereby changing the epitope on Rankl where denosumab binds.45 As a result, these mice contain a humanized version of RANKL that can be inhibited by both osteoprotegerin (OPG) or denosumab which is not attainable in wildtype mice where denosumab cannot bind to Rankl. All mice used for in vivo studies (aged 14 weeks at sacrifice, n = 19) and for testis culture experiments (aged 8 weeks at sacrifice, n = 13) were male and progeny of parents homozygous for the humanized Rankl allele. For the in vivo experiments male littermates were randomly assigned to experimental groups.

All mice were genotyped at weaning (postnatal day 21)28 based on ear punches and subsequent polymerase chain reaction (PCR) with the primers listed in the key resources table. Mice were housed and cared for according to national ethical guidelines (12 h dark/light cycle and ad libitum access to food and water). Animal experiments were approved by the Danish Animal Experiments Inspectorate (license number 2022-15-0202-00166 and 2018-15-0201-01565).

Collection of mouse adult testis tissue

Male humanized RANKL mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation at 8 weeks of age. Serum was collected by cardiac puncture. The testes were removed and placed in 1 mL cold transport media (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F-12), 1X penicillin/streptomycin). The testicles were kept at 4°C until dissection (maximum 4 h from sacrifice to setup of tissue culture).

Collection of human adult testis

Patients undergoing orchiectomy due to testicular germ cell tumors (TGCTs) were included in the study when referred for cryopreservation of sperm at the Department of Growth and Reproduction at Copenhagen University Hospital (Rigshospitalet), Denmark. Inclusion was done in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and after approval from the local ethics committee (H-17004362). All men (aged between 19 and 46 years of age) gave their informed written and oral consent.

Collection of human fetal testis

Fetal testes were isolated from material available following elective termination of pregnancy during the first trimester at the Department of Gynecology at Rigshospitalet and Hvidovre Hospital, Denmark. The study was approved by the regional ethics committee (permit number H-1-2012-007) and women gave their informed written and oral consent. Human fetal testis tissue was obtained as previously described.46 None of the terminations were due to fetal abnormalities, and all fetuses exhibited normal morphology. The fetuses ranged in gestational age from week 9–12, with fetal age determined by measuring crown-rump length and evaluating foot length, as described in.47 Fetal testis/mesonephros tissue from eight male fetuses was dissected in ice-cold Dulbecco’s Phosphate-buffered saline (D-PBS) and was fixed immediately in formalin and used for IHC analyses.

Study participants

Copenhagen First was a single-center, double-blinded, randomized clinical trial conducted at the Department of Growth and Reproduction, Rigshospitalet, Denmark. The study was approved by the local ethical committee (H-16039285), Danish Medicines Agency (2016-003546-84), and The Danish Data Protection Agency (2012-58-0004) and was monitored by the Good Clinical Practices Unit, Bispebjerg Hospital, Denmark. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants. The primary aim of the study was to determine the effect of systemic inhibition of RANKL on male reproductive function by investigating whether denosumab improves semen quality, and secondarily to identify the group of men that may benefit from the treatment. The primary outcome was changes in sperm production (sperm concentration, sperm count, motile sperm, and progressive motile sperm) 80 days after injection. With a test level of 5% (level of significance), a power of 80%, and 40 men in each group we expected to detect a change in total number of motile sperm of 75%, and a change in sperm concentration (indexed to baseline value) of 25%. We expected to include a total of 100 men, to take the possibility of dropouts into account. The study protocol and plan for biostatistical analyses are available (Datas S2 and S3).

All men eligible for inclusion were part of an infertile couple whose semen analysis had shown impaired semen quality and they had been referred for andrological examination at the andrological outpatient clinic at the Department of Growth and Reproduction, Rigshospitalet. The infertile men delivered two semen samples for routine analyses and blood samples for karyotyping, analyses of microdeletions on the Y chromosome, and measurements of serum calcium, 25OHD, testosterone, estradiol, sex-hormone binding globulin (SHBG), luteinizing hormone (LH), Follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), and inhibin B before their visit at the outpatient clinic. At the clinic, an ultrasound of the testes was performed. Inclusion criteria were impaired semen quality defined as sperm concentration <15 million/mL, or < 40% progressive sperm motility, or < 4% sperm morphology, and a minimum sperm count >0.05 million/mL. Exclusion criteria were men with chronic diseases (diabetes mellitus, thyroid disease, or other endocrine diseases requiring treatment), or diseases known to be affected by or interfere with vitamin D supplements (granulomatous diseases such as sarcoidosis, tuberculosis, Wegener’s granulomatosis, vasculitis as well as inflammatory bowel diseases e.g., Crohn’s disease or ulcerative colitis, etc.), active or previous malignant disease, indication for testis biopsy, hypocalcemia (ionized calcium <1.15 mM/L or total calcium <2.14 mmol/L), poor dental status, obstructive oligospermia or vasectomy, serum inhibin B < 30 pg/mL, abnormal karyotype, or men practicing excessive exercise. All included men were randomly assigned (1:1) to a single sc. injection with denosumab (60 mg/mL, Prolia, Amgen, US) or sc. injection with 1 mL saline (0.9%). To prevent denosumab-induced hypocalcemia, all participants were recommended daily vitamin D3 (25 μg) and calcium (400 mg) (Actavis, Denmark) supplementation for the 180-day experimental period.

Method details

Denosumab treatment in adult humanized RANKL mice

For the in vivo study, 5.0 mg/kg denosumab (Prolia, Amgen, US; diluted in D-PBS) or vehicle (D-PBS) were injected once sc. into 8-week-old male mice. After six weeks, mice were sacrificed, and serum and organs were collected. Serum was collected by cardiac puncture and stored at −80°C. Group sizes vary between vehicle control (n = 9) and denosumab (n = 10) due to the failure to collect samples from one mouse in the vehicle group. To assess the reproductive and skeletal phenotypes of denosumab injection, testis, epididymis, seminal vesicle, prostate, long bones, and kidneys were dissected and weighed on an analytical balance. Organs were either snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C or fixed for approximately 36 h at 4°C in formalin and were subsequently embedded in paraffin.

Assessment of sperm count from cauda epididymis from humanized RANKL mice

Epididymal sperm count was performed on the frozen epididymis dissected from the mice from the in vivo study. Using a Vannas scissor, the vas deferens and corpus epididymis were separated from the cauda epididymis, to allow the sperm to exit with gentle assistance. The sperm was released with a 21G needle in 1 mL D-PBS (BD microlane, US) and transferred to an Eppendorf tube. The tube was centrifuged at 300 g for 15 min, the supernatant was removed leaving 150 μL D-PBS with sperm cells. The sperm suspension was gently mixed by vortexing, diluted 1:10 in D-PBS, and deposited in a Bürker Türk counting chamber. The sperm cells were counted manually in a microscope by a scientist blinded to treatment.

Assessment of testicular germ cell epithelial width and tubule diameter in humanized RANKL mice

Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) stained sections from paraffin-embedded testis from humanized RANKL mice treated with denosumab or vehicle were assessed in NDP.view v2.6.13 software (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). Tubules with proper histology were selected and staged according to the 12 stages of mouse spermatogenesis. Tubules staged 6–8 and 9–12, respectively, were pooled, if present, for each mouse. The tubule diameter and epithelial width were determined using the measuring tool in NDP.view v2.6.13 software (Hamamatsu, Japan).

Ex vivo human testis tissue culture

Human adult testes were cultured ex vivo in hanging drops as described previously with tissue from each patient used for both vehicle control and denosumab treatment.48 Effects of denosumab treatment were always determined as compared to vehicle control from the same patient. At the time of inclusion, a blood sample was drawn, and reproductive hormones were measured as described below for samples from the RCT (anti-müllerian hormone (AMH), FSH, and inhibin B). Human adult testis tissue adjacent to the TGCT was cultured as intact tissue fragments using a hanging drop culture approach as previously described.48 Briefly, tissue was cut into 1 mm3 pieces and cultured in 40 μL of culture media (DMEM/F-12, 1X penicillin/streptomycin, 1X insulin, transferrin and selenium supplement (ITSS), 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS)). All media and supplements were purchased from Gibco/Thermo Fischer Scientific (Denmark) except ITSS (Sigma-Aldrich, Broendby, Denmark). Tissue was exposed to 100 ng/mL denosumab (Prolia, Amgen, US) or vehicle (D-PBS) via the culture media for 48 h at 34°C in 5% CO2.

Ex vivo mouse testis tissue culture of humanized RANKL mice

Testes from adult humanized RANKL mice (8-week-old) were dissected and treated with RANKL inhibitors in a gel-based culture approach slightly modified from.32,49 In brief, 1.5% agarose gels (1.5 g agarose in 100 mL Milli-Q H2O (Lonza, Switzerland)) were cut into 8 × 8 × 8 mm3 cubes, placed in 4-well plates (Thermo Scientific, US) and soaked in culture media (identical to media used for human testis culture) for a minimum of 24 h before setup of tissue cultures. Mouse testis was dissected into 1.5 mm3 fragments and two pieces were placed in opposite corners on the surface of each gel cube. The tissues were cultured for 48 h at 34°C and 5% CO2. Culture media (325 μL) containing 100 ng/mL denosumab (Prolia, Amgen, US), 100 ng/mL OPG (#cyt-177, ProSpec, Israel), 100 ng/mL FSH (Puregon, Orifarm, Denmark), or vehicle (D-PBS) were added to each well. At least three replicate tissue pieces from the same mouse and treatment group were fixed in formalin followed by paraffin embedding for IHC and histological analyses. Tissue was initially evaluated for morphology and technical quality based on HE staining. For ex vivo testis culture experiments mice with serum AMH >15 ng/mL were excluded from further analyses to exclude mice with high FSH stimulation of the testis.

BrdU incorporation in human and mouse testis ex vivo culture

To determine the presence of proliferating germ cells, bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU) labeling reagent (Invitrogen, MA, USA) was added to the ex vivo (mouse and human adult testis) cultures for the final 6 h of the culture period. For the human cultures BrdU labeling reagent was diluted 1:10 in culture media, while 20 μL BrdU labeling reagent was added directly to the culture media including treatment for the cultured mice testis.

Quantification of proliferative and apoptotic germ cells in human and mouse ex vivo culture

Mouse testis cultures

To quantify proliferation and apoptosis, sections were stained for BrdU and cleaved poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (cPARP) and evaluated by manually counting the number of stained cells per area. The total area of each seminiferous tubule was determined using NDP.view v2.6.13 software (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan). One mouse was excluded due to an extremely high number of apoptotic germ cells (Figure S8).

Human testis cultures

To evaluate proliferation and apoptosis quantitatively, the number of BrdU+ and cPARP+ cells per area was counted manually in all seminiferous tubules of denosumab- and vehicle-treated samples with high technical quality. Quantification was performed with each patient as his own control. When analyzing human tissue culture, there are considerable differences between the biological replicates. This is due to the differences in the time from the surgical removal until the tissue fragments can be cultured, variation in the initial tissue quality, and differences in the degree of dysgenesis. Therefore, the treatment-induced changes are reported as compared to vehicle control-treated tissue from the same patient. Two patients were excluded from analysis: one due to a very low number of proliferative germ cells (Figure S9) and one due to the diagnosis of a non-malignant tumor with a Sertoli cell-only (SCO) pattern.

Semi-quantitative evaluation of OPG staining in sertoli cells

Human testicular tissue with full spermatogenesis or dysgenesis was immunohistochemically stained for OPG (#sc-21038, Santa Cruz, US) and Sertoli cell marker SOX9 (#AB5535, Millipore, Germany). In total, 15 tubules were selected in each specimen (n = 3 for each tissue type). All selected tubules in the testis tissue with full spermatogenesis contained germ cells at the spermatocyte-spermatid stage. OPG staining in Sertoli cell cytoplasm was quantified according to an arbitrary semiquantitative scale (1: no/weak OPG expression in Sertoli cells. 2: moderate/strong expression in few Sertoli cells or weak staining in several Sertoli cells. 3: Strong staining in most Sertoli cells).

Quantification of testosterone in mouse serum

Testosterone was measured in mice using an isotope-dilution TurboFlow-LC-MS/MS method.50 Serum was diluted 1:3 in double charcoal-stripped human serum (DC Mass Spect Gold, Golden West Biologicals, Inc., CA, US), and 100 μL were placed in a 1.5 mL DNA LoBind Eppendorf tube with 70 μL 0.3 M ZnSO4 in 80% methanol (v/v) and 30 μL of internal standard working solution. The solution was mixed by centrifuging at 2200 rpm for 10 s, following incubation for 30 min at room temperature (RT), and samples were subsequently centrifuged at 12,700 g for 10 min. Supernatant (160 μL) was transferred to a 2 mL vial with a 200 μL glass insert and placed in the auto-sampler at 10°C. Samples were analyzed within 24 h and results were based on a single determination. The analysis was performed on a Dionex UltiMate 3000 UHPLC system which had an integrated Transcend TLX TurboFlow sample preparation system (Both from Thermo Scientific, US) and was coupled to a triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (TSQ Quantiva, Thermo Scientific, US). The system was controlled by Aria MX 2.1 and Xcalibur 3.0.63 software (ThermoFinnigan, US).

Quantification of AMH in mouse serum

Serum AMH (#AL-113, Ansh Labs, US; detection range, 0.313–20 ng/mL) levels were assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). The analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All incubations were made at RT shaking at 500 revolutions per minute (rpm). In brief, the serum was diluted 1:10, subsequently, 50 μL of the calibrators and diluted samples were loaded to the plate. 50 μL AMH assay buffer was added, and the plate was incubated for 2 h. The plate was washed five times with 350 μL wash buffer (buffered saline solution, diluted 25-fold with deionized water) and 100 μL Antibody-Biotin Conjugate-Ready-to-use (RTU) was added and incubated for 1 h. Following washing, 100 μL Streptavidin-Enzyme Conjugate-RTU was added, and the plate was incubated for 30 min. After a final wash, 100 μL tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) chromogen solution was added and incubated until complete color development. 100 μL Stop solution was used to prevent color saturation, and the absorbance was read within 10 min on the FLUOstar Omega (BMG Labtech, Germany) with a dual-wavelength measurement at 450 nm/630 nm. Subsequently, the results were log-transformed in Mars version 3.31, and cubic regression curve fit was applied.

Quantification of inhibin B in mouse serum

Inhibin B (#AL-163, Ansh Labs, US; detection range, 5.2–758 pg/mL) serum levels were assessed by ELISA. The analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Incubations were made at RT shaking at 500 rpm. In brief, 50 μL calibrators, controls, and 1:4 diluted serum samples were added to the plate, following 50 μL Inhibin B Assay Buffer A and 50 μL Inhibin B Assay Buffer B and incubated for 2 h. Subsequently, the plate was manually washed and 100 μL Pre-mixed Inhibin B Antibody-Biotin Conjugate Solution was added and incubated for 1 h. Following washing in wash buffer containing phosphate buffer saline solution (diluted 25-fold with deionized water) 100 μL Streptavidin-Enzyme Conjugate-RTU was added for a 30-min incubation period. The final wash followed 100 μL TMB chromogen solution and incubation until optimal color development. The reaction was stopped with 100 μL Stopping Solution and the absorbance was measured within 10 min on the FLUOstar Omega (BMG Labtech, Germany) at 450 nm. The data was log-transformed, and the calibration curve was made with a linear regression curve-fit within the Mars program version 3.31.

Quantification of FSH in mouse serum

Serum FSH was measured using an in-house ELISA as previously described.51 In brief, serum samples were diluted 1:10 in 0.05% PBS-Tween 20 (PBS-T). Plates with 96 wells were coated with 50 μL capture antibody (polyclonal guinea pig anti-mouse-FSH antibody #AFP1760191, from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases and National Hormone and Pituitary Program (NIDDK-NHPP; RRID: AB_2665512) diluted 1:20,000 in PBS and incubated overnight at 4°C. The next day, the solution containing the capture antibody was removed and the plate was loaded with 200 μL blocking buffer (5% [weight/volume] skim milk powder in PBS-T) for 2 h at RT on an orbital shaker (40 rpm). Subsequently, plates were washed 3 times repeatedly (3 min per wash with 200 μL PBS-T per well, shaking at 40 rpm on an orbital shaker, here and in all subsequent washes). 50 μL of the standard curve (2-fold serial dilutions of rat FSH (reference preparation: #NIDDK-rFSH-RP-2 from NIDDKNHPP) in 0.2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS-T from 4 to 0.031 ng/mL) and diluted serum samples were loaded onto the plate and incubated 36 h at 4°C. Then, plates were washed as previously described and incubated for 90 min on an orbital shaker with 50 μL/well of the detection antibody (rabbit anti-rat-FSH antiserum FSH-S-11, #AFP-C0972881, NIDDK-NHPP; RRID: AB_2687903) diluted in blocking buffer to a final dilution of 1:6,250 at RT. The plate was washed and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody (#AP182P, Millipore, Germany, RRID: AB_92591) (50 μL/well) at a final concentration of 1:1000 (diluted in 2.5% milk/PBS +0.025% Tween 20) for 90 min at RT on an orbital shaker. An additional wash of the plate was performed before 100 μL of o-phenylenediamine (5 mg/tablet, #34006, Thermo Scientific) in 0.05 M phosphate-citrate buffer (pH 5.0) containing 0.1% of H2O2 (#216763, Sigma) was added to each well and incubated at RT for 45 min on an orbital shaker. To stop the reaction, 50 μL of 3 M hydrochloric acid (#A144S-500, Fisher Chemical) was added to each well. The absorbance was read at 490 nm, using 650 nm as a reference (EZ Read 2000 microplate reader, Biochrom). To obtain the FSH concentration in each sample, interpolation using a nonlinear regression of the FSH standard curve using GraphPad Prism software version 9.4.1 (CA, US) was performed.

Quantification of LH in mouse serum

Serum LH from mice was measured by an in-house ELISA previously described in52 and based on a published method.53 Briefly, 96 well high-binding polystyrene plates (#9018, Corning, US) were coated with 50 μL capture antibody solution (mouse monoclonal anti-bovine-LH 518B7, UC Davis, #518B7, RRID: AB_2665514) diluted 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 7.4; 10 mM sodium phosphate dibasic, 2 mM potassium phosphate monobasic, 2.7 mM potassium chloride, and 137 mM sodium chloride) for 16 h in a humidified chamber. The next day, the capture antibody was removed, and plates were incubated with blocking solution (5% skim milk powder, #1.15363.0500, Sigma Aldrich, US) in PBS with 0.05% PBS-T (200 μL per well) for 2 h at RT on an orbital shaker. The blocking solution was removed, and plates were washed with PBS-T (3 min per wash with 200 μL per well, shaking at 40 rpm on an orbital shaker, here and in all subsequent washes). Upon washing, 50 μL of LH standards (using 2-fold serial dilutions of mouse LH reference prep (AFP5306A, A. F. Parlow, National Hormone and Peptide Program; supplied by Golden West Biosolutions, #TLIA1053.03) in assay buffer (PBS-T with 0.2% BSA, #1073507800,1 Sigma Aldrich, US) from 1 to 0.0019531 ng/mL) and serum samples (diluted 1:20 in PBS-T with 0.2% BSA) were added and incubated for 2 h at RT on an orbital shaker. Plates were then washed and incubated with 50 μL of biotinylated-5303 SPRN-5 (biotinylation was performed as described in52) per well diluted at approximately 1:1000 to achieve a final concentration of 4.379 μg/mL for 90 min on an orbital shaker. Again, the plates were washed and subsequently incubated for 90 min with poly-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate streptavidin (#N200, Thermo Fisher, US) diluted 1:8,000 in PBS-T on an orbital shaker. Finally, plates were washed and incubated with 100 μL of o-phenylenediamine (0.5 mg/mL, #34006, Thermo Fisher, US) per well reconstituted in a solution containing 0.05 M citric acid, 0.05 M sodium phosphate (pH 5) with 0.03% H2O2, on an orbital shaker, with each plate unsealed, but covered with foil for 30 min. To stop the reaction, 50 μL of 3M hydrochloric acid per well was added. The plate was analyzed at 490 nm and with background subtracted at 659 nm (nearest available filter to previously reported 650 nm) using EZ Read 2000 microplate reader, Biochrom). LH concentration in each sample was determined by interpolating optical density (OD) values against a quadratic regression of the LH standard curve using GraphPad Prism software version 9.4.1 (CA, US).

Immunohistochemistry

Testicular tissue (adult and fetal)

IHC was performed on human and mouse testis tissue as previously described.28,48 Briefly, testis tissue was fixed in formalin immediately after orchiectomy (human tissue), after sacrifice (mouse tissue), or following ex vivo culture (mouse and human tissue). Fixed tissues were dehydrated, paraffin-embedded (FFPE), and sectioned using standard procedures as described previously.28,48 In brief, antigen-retrieval was obtained by microwaving (750 Watt for 1 min, 250 Watt for 15 min) or by placing sections in a pressure cooker (110°C for 30 min) followed by blocking of endogenous peroxidases in 0.5% (v/v) H2O2 in water for 30 min. Sections were blocked with 0.5% skim milk powder diluted in tris-buffered saline (TBS) for 30 min, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in TBS overnight at 4°C and 1 h at RT (see key resources table for antibody details). Between all protocol steps, slides were washed with TBS, and all incubations were performed in a humidity chamber. Subsequently, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies at RT for 30 min (rabbit #MP-7401, goat #MP7405, or mouse #MP7402, Vector laboratories, US). Visualization was performed with ImmPRESS AEC kit (#SK4205, Vector laboratories, US). Sections were counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin for approximately 3–15 s. Immunohistochemical stainings were visualized by scanning the slides with a NanoZoomer 2.0 HT 257 (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan) and assessed with NDP.view v2.6.13 software (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan).

Ex vivo cultures human testis tissue

At least three tissue pieces per treatment and vehicle control group from the same patient were fixed in formalin followed by paraffin embedding for IHC and histological analyses. Tissue was evaluated for morphology and technical quality based on HE-staining, while the potential presence of the testicular cancer precursor termed GCNIS was detected following IHC staining with an antibody against D2-40 (#M3619, Dako, Denmark). All patients included in the study were diagnosed with non-seminoma. IHC staining for OPG was used to assess Sertoli cell function by evaluating each seminiferous tubule in each sample according to the following categories: <20% OPG staining in Sertoli cells cytoplasm, 20–50% OPG staining in Sertoli cells cytoplasm, <50% OPG staining in Sertoli cells cytoplasm. A total of 408 seminiferous tubules from 15 patients were evaluated and the number of tubules per patient is specified in Table S10. The area of each tubule was measured using NDP.view software (Hamamatsu Photonics, Japan).

Immunofluorescence

Immunofluorescence was performed as previously described in detail.28 Sections from FFPE samples were deparaffinized 2 times 5 min in xylene, followed by 20 s in 100%, 100%, 95%, 70% ethanol and water. The sections were placed in 0.01 M citrate buffer and antigen retrieval was performed in a pressure cooker at 110°C for 30 min, followed by 2 min in Milli-Q water. Endogenous peroxidase blocking was made with 3% H2O2 in methanol for 30 min. The sections were washed 2 times for 5 min in TBS, following 30 min of blocking for unspecific binding with 5% (w/v) BSA in 20% (v/v) horse serum (ImmPRESS Kit, Vector Laboratories) and 80% (v/v) TBS. The sections were drained for excess blocking mixture, and the desired primary antibody diluted in BSA-horse serum-TBS solution (20% horse serum(v/v)/5% BSA(w/v)/PBS) (see key resources table for antibody details) was added for overnight incubation at 4°C. Next day, the sections were washed 2 times for 5 min in TBS, following 30 min incubation with the secondary antibody (chicken anti-mouse peroxidase (#sc-2962), chicken anti-rabbit peroxidase (#sc-2963) or chicken anti-goat peroxidase (#sc-2961) from Santa Cruz, US) diluted 1:200 in BSA-horse serum-TBS solution. The excess antibody was removed by washing with TBS 2 times for 5 min. From hereof the sections were kept as dark as possible. The sections were incubated for 10 min with the desired fluorescence color (green fluorescein #NEL741E001KT, blue cyanine5 #NEL745001Kt or red cyanine3 #NEL744E001KT from AKOYA biosciences, US) diluted 1:50 in fluorescence kit buffer. To apply additional primary antibodies to the sections, the protocol was repeated from the pressure cooker step until incubation with the fluorescence application following a 5-min wash with TBS. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI (#D3571, Molecular Probes, Life Technologies Corporation, US) diluted 1:600 in TBS for 10 min, washed 2 times for 5 min in TBS, and mounted with ProLongTM Gold antifade (#P36930, Life Technologies Corporation, US). Fluorescent images were captured using an Olympus BX61 microscope (Olympus, Japan).

Biochemical analysis in the randomized clinical trial

All blood samples were drawn from fasting participants (between 08:00 and 10:00 in the morning). All analyses were conducted from frozen serum after trial completion. Serum was analyzed at the Department of Growth and Reproduction, Copenhagen University Hospital – Rigshospitalet, except for type I collagen cross-linked C-telopeptide (CTX), procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (PINP), total calcium, alkaline phosphatase, 25OHD, and parathyroid hormone (PTH) which were analyzed at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital – Rigshospitalet. Quantification of reproductive hormones (LH, FSH, AMH, testosterone, and inhibin B) and calciotropic hormones/bone markers (CTX, PINP, total calcium, alkaline phosphatase, 25OHD, and PTH) were performed with accredited analyses either at the Department of Growth and Reproduction or Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital – Rigshospitalet. Soluble RANKL (sRANKL) (#BI-20462, Biomedia, Austria) and OPG (#BI-20403, Biomedia, Austria) were measured at the Department of Growth and Reproduction with commercial ELISA kits validated in previous studies.28,44

Quantification of testosterone in human serum

Human serum testosterone concentrations were measured by LC-MS/MS,50 similar to mouse serum, see the section “quantification of testosterone in mouse serum”, with the only exception that serum samples were not diluted in double charcoal-stripped human serum.

Quantification of LH in human serum

LH (coefficient of variation (CV) 6%) was measured using time-resolved immunofluorometric assay Delfia (Wallac, Finland) based on a non-competitive sandwich-type design. The kit is discontinued by the manufacturer. The lower detection range was 0.05 IU/L. The manufacturer’s instructions were followed. BIO-RAD LiquichekTM Immunoassay Plus was used as a control with reference range (low: 3.8–4.0, medium: 16.2–17.3, and high: 60.0–64.0, respectively).

Quantification of FSH in human serum

FSH (CV 4%) was measured using time-resolved immunofluorometric assay Delfia (Wallac, Finland) based on a non-competitive sandwich-type design. The kit is discontinued by the manufacturer. Lower detection range was 0.05 IU/L. The manufacturer’s instructions were followed. BIO-RAD LiquichekTM Immunoassay Plus was used as a control with reference range (low: 4.8–5.1, medium: 15.3–17.0, and high: 46.5–51.3, respectively).

Quantification of inhibin B in human serum

Inhibin B measurements (CV 11%) were performed by two-sided ELISA inhibin B gen II (A81301, Beckman Coulter, US). In brief, samples were incubated in microtitration wells that had been coated with anti-activin B antibody. After incubation and washing, the wells were incubated with a biotinylated anti-inhibin B α-subunit detection antibody. Upon a second incubation and washing step, the wells were incubated with streptavidin labeled HRP. After a third incubation and washing step, the wells were incubated with the substrate TMB and after incubation stop solution (0.2 M H2SO4 acid) was added. The degree of enzymatic turnover of the substrate was determined by measuring the absorbance at 450 nm on a microplate reader and the absorbance measured is directly proportional to the concentration of inhibin B in the samples.

Quantification of AMH in human serum

AMH (CV 8%) was analyzed by ELISA (#A79765, Beckman Coulter, US). Samples were incubated in microtitration wells which had been coated with anti-AMH antibody. After incubation and washing, anti-AMH detection antibody labeled with biotin was added to each well and incubated. The plate was washed again and HRP was added. After a third incubation and washing step, the substrate TMB was added to the wells and lastly, an acidic stopping solution was added, and reactivity was measured as described above.

Quantification of soluble RANKL in human serum and seminal fluid

Serum and seminal sRANKL (CV 12%) was measured by ELISA (#BI-20462, Biomedica, Austria). Seminal fluid samples were diluted 1:30 or 1:60 while serum samples were not diluted. The analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First, wells were prewashed with wash buffer and assay buffer was added to each well. The samples were added to the plate and incubated at RT for 2 h. Subsequently, wells were washed with wash buffer five times. The wash buffer was removed and biotinylated anti-sRANKL antibody was added to each well and incubated overnight at 4°C. The wells were again washed five times with wash buffer and antibody conjugate was added to incubate for 1 h at RT covered from light. Wells were washed with wash buffer five times and subsequently, substrate (TMB solution) was added. The plate was incubated for 30 min at RT covered from light and finally stop solution was added to each well. The absorbance was measured immediately at 450 and 630 nm for reference on an ELISA microplate reader (Tecan, Sunrise, Switzerland).

Quantification of OPG in human serum and seminal fluid

Serum and seminal OPG (CV 5%) was measured by ELISA (#BI-20403, Biomedica, Austria). Seminal fluid samples were diluted and 1:25 for OPG in DMEM (Invitrogen, CA, US), which also served as a negative control on each plate. The serum was measured undiluted. The analysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. In brief, assay buffer was added to each well. The samples were added to the plate and subsequently, biotinylated anti-OPG antibody was added. The plates were incubated at RT for 4 h. Upon incubation, wells were washed with wash buffer five times. Then streptavidin-HRP conjugate was added following a 1-h incubation step at RT. The plate was washed with wash buffer five times and subsequently, substrate (TMB solution) was added. The plate was incubated for 30 min at RT in the dark and finally, the reaction was stopped with a stop solution. The absorbance was measured immediately at 450 and 630 nm for reference at an ELISA microplate reader (Tecan, Sunrise).

Quantification of CTX and PINP in human serum

Bone markers CTX (CV 8%) and PINP (CV 8%) were measured with chemiluminescence immunoassays on i10 (IDS Plc, Tyne and Wear, France).

Quantification of total calcium, alkaline phosphatase, 25OHD, and PTH in human serum

Serum total calcium (CV 2.5%), alkaline phosphatase (CV 5.5%), 25OHD (13%), and serum PTH (CV <4%), were measured using Cobas 8000 (Roche, Switzerland), whereas serum ionized calcium (CV 3%) was measured on ABL837 (Radiometer, Denmark). For these analyses, the blood samples were transferred immediately after collection to the in-house Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital – Rigshospitalet, and analyzed according to standard guidelines.

Semen analysis in the randomized clinical trial

Male adult participants produced a semen sample by masturbation on-site in a room close to the laboratory between 08:00 and 09:00 in the morning. Self-reported information on the duration of ejaculation abstinence was noted. Trained technicians conducted the semen analyses. Sperm concentration was assessed using a Nucleocounter image cytometer NC-3000 (Chemometec, Denmark), which is an advanced cell analyzer utilizing fluorescence imaging to characterize cell properties with high reproducibility. In brief, the samples were allowed to liquefy for at least 30 min at 37°C and 20 μL of the seminal sample was diluted, in a solution of 0.6 m NaHCO3-buffer and 0.37% (v/v) formaldehyde in distilled water followed by vortex mixing, and immediate aspiration into a PI-cassette (# 941–0016, Chemometec, Denmark) that are measured in the NucleoCounter (NC-3000, Chemometec) image cytometer. Four images are produced per cassette, before NucleoView software automatically calculates the sperm concentration.54 However, when sperm concentration was below 3.0 million/mL, the samples were re-assessed using a Bürker-Türk haemocytometer.55 Semen volume was estimated by weighing. For sperm motility assessment, the sperm cells were classified as progressive motile (WHO class A + B), non-progressive motile (class C), or immotile (class D). Only spermatozoa with tails were counted. Samples were analyzed on a glass slide under a microscope at x400 magnification and examined on a heating stage kept at 37°C. Finally, smears were prepared, Papanicolaou-stained, and spermatozoa morphology was assessed according to strict criteria.56

Sperm DNA fragmentation

Sperm DNA integrity (fragmentation) was investigated at baseline and 80 days after intervention at SPZ Lab (Copenhagen, Denmark). After liquefication, 0.5 mL neat semen was diluted with a TNE buffer (0.01 M TrisCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 1 mM Disodium-EDTA, pH 7.4). Diluted semen samples were placed in Sarstedt CryoPure vials 1.8 mL (Nümbrecht, Germany) and were frozen in liquid nitrogen. The dilution and staining of samples were performed according to the SCSA procedure described by Evenson. In brief, vials were thawed in a water bath at 37°C for 4 min and then incubated on ice for 5 min. An aliquot of the sample was then diluted in TNE buffer to a concentration of 2 × 106 sperm cells/mL and a total volume of 200 μL in a 5 mL Falcon tube (Corning Science, Reynosa, Mexico). An acid detergent solution (400 μL; 0.08 M HCl, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.1% v/v Triton X-100, pH 1.2) was added, and a stopwatch was started. After precisely 30 s, 1.20 mL of AO staining solution was added (6 μg/mL acridine orange (AO, Polysciences Inc., Warrington, PA, USA) 0.037 M citric acid, 0.126 M Na2HPO4, 1.1 mM Disodium-EDTA, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 6.0). The stained sample was then analyzed using a fluorescence-activated cell sorting flow cytometer (FACSCalibur, BD Biosciences, CA). Data acquisition of 5000 events was performed 3 min after initiation of the acid detergent treatment. For each semen sample, a minimum of two aliquots were stained and analyzed. To ensure stringent quality control, all flow cytometric analyses were inspected visually. DNA fragmentation index (DFI) values for the two replicates had to agree with each other (SD below 2.5%). If this was not the case, two new replicates were analyzed.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses in tissue cultures and mouse studies

All ex vivo tissue culture and mouse data are presented as mean ± SEM. For cultured human testis samples, paired (one-tailed) sample t-tests were performed. The paired test was performed to account for the fact that denosumab and vehicle-treated tissue from the same patient were compared. The one-sided test was performed with the assumption that denosumab treatment would increase germ cell proliferation and decrease germ cell apoptosis compared with vehicle treatment. For cultured mouse testis samples evaluation was done with a Dunnett’s test to adjust for multiple comparisons and a mixed effects analysis to account for missing values. The correlation between germ cell proliferation and apoptosis of mouse testis cultures and serum values of reproductive hormones was assessed by simple linear regression analysis. For organ weights, an independent (two-tailed) t-test was used. Unpaired sample t-test was used to assess the difference in tubule diameter and epithelial width. The number of replicates in each experimental setting and statistical significance is indicated in the figures and specified in figure legends. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism software version 9.4.1 (CA, US).

Statistical analyses in RCT

Semen parameters are presented as median [interquartile range (IQR): 25th, 75th percentiles], while reproductive hormones and bone markers are shown as mean (SD) (Table S3; Table 1). Testis size was evaluated by orchidometer and is presented as the average of both testes. Following the prespecified statistical analysis plan, changes from baseline to day 80 or day 160 in semen parameters within each group were assessed by Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while reproductive hormones and bone markers were evaluated by paired sample t-test (Table 2). Differences between denosumab and placebo-treated men at day 80 and day 160 for semen parameters were assessed by Mann-Whitney U test, while reproductive hormones and bone markers were analyzed by independent t-test (Tables 2, S4, and S7). Differences in binary variables such as pregnancies and positive response rate (PRR) were assessed by Chi-square test (Tables 1, S5, and S9; Figures 5A and 5C, lower panel). Differences between groups according to baseline serum AMH in tertiles were assessed by Kruskal-Wallis test and secondary by a trend analysis (Table 1). The association between changes in sperm concentration from baseline to day 80 and selected variables such as reproductive hormones was assessed by Pearson’s correlation test (Table S8). In subgroup analyses (baseline serum AMH ≥38 pmol/L, and baseline AMH ≥38 pmol/L combined with serum sRANKL ≥0.24 pmol/L) semen parameters were ln-transformed (sperm concentration, sperm count, motile sperm, progressive motile sperm, and morphological normal sperm) to obtain a normal distribution. Changes within each group from baseline to day 80 in ln-transformed semen parameters were assessed by paired sample t-test and presented as a back-transformed mean with CI 95% (Figures 5A and 5C, upper panel). Differences in ln-transformed semen parameters between denosumab and placebo-treated men at day 80 were assessed by an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) adjusted for time of abstinence at day 80 and baseline semen parameters and are presented as back-transformed mean with CI 95% at day 80 (Figure 5A and 5CA, upper panel). For all analyses, IBM SPSS statistics version 28 (US) was used.
