Telerehabilitation of chronic low back pain: protocol of a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Cheng Fan, Qiaojun Zhang, Xiaohui Lei, Ben Ma, Boyi Li, Jiamin Yang, Yaofeng Li, Kai Wan, Lei Lu, Siduo Zhang, Bingtao Hu, Jing Yang, Weiqi Li, Peijia Fu, Hongfei Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic low back pain (CLBP) is among the most disabling musculoskeletal disorders worldwide. Traditional in-person rehabilitation is often limited by time, geographic, and financial barriers. Telerehabilitation, which delivers personalized interventions through digital technologies (e.g., mobile apps, video coaching, wearable devices, virtual reality, and artificial intelligence feedback systems), is emerging as a complementary and alternative approach to conventional rehabilitation. However, there is currently no consensus on its efficacy and safety. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of various forms of telerehabilitation in improving pain intensity, physical function, psychological symptoms, and quality of life in patients with CLBP. In addition, the study will assess intervention adherence, patient satisfaction, and safety (as measured by the incidence of adverse events) through systematic review and network meta-analysis.MethodsA systematic search will be conducted in the following databases: PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science, covering all records from database inception to 31 December 2025. Eligible studies will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults (≥ 18 years) with CLBP, comparing telerehabilitation interventions to any control intervention. The primary outcome will be pain intensity, while secondary outcomes will include physical function, psychological status, quality of life, and adverse events. The risk of bias for the included studies will be assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool. The certainty of evidence will be evaluated using the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) web application. All network meta-analyses will be conducted within a frequentist framework using Stata 15 software and will apply random-effects models. Intervention rankings will be estimated using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) curves and illustrated with forest plots showing both individual and pooled effect sizes. Additional analyses will include assessments of statistical inconsistency, publication bias, heterogeneity, sensitivity, and subgroup differences.DiscussionThis study aimed to compare and rank the available evidence on various forms of telerehabilitation for the management of CLBP, as well as to assess its safety. The findings offer valuable insights for healthcare professionals and policymakers, supporting the advancement of patient-centered rehabilitation strategies.Systematic review registrationPROSPERO CRD420251015478Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13643-026-03105-0.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is defined as pain, muscle tension, or stiffness localized between the costal margin and the subgluteal crease, with or without radiating pain to the lower extremities [1]. When symptoms persist for more than 12 weeks, the condition is classified as chronic low back pain (CLBP). Globally, LBP is the leading cause of years lived with disability [2], with a prevalence of 11–12% [3]. Epidemiological data indicate that in the USA alone, CLBP affects approximately 28% of the adult population each year, impacts up to 90% of adults at some point in their lives, and results in an economic burden exceeding $300 billion annually [4]. While clinical guidelines recommend traditional rehabilitation therapies—such as physical therapy, exercise therapy, and cognitive-behavioral interventions—as first-line treatments, patient adherence remains below 30% due to factors such as the need for frequent clinic visits, high costs, and geographic barriers [5]. These limitations significantly reduce treatment effectiveness.

Telerehabilitation (TR), a key component of digital healthcare, represents an innovative model for delivering comprehensive rehabilitation services through information and communication technologies (ICT) [6]. This technology-driven platform integrates a variety of digital tools—including mobile health applications, video consultation systems, wearable biosensors, virtual reality (VR), and artificial intelligence (AI)–assisted decision-making systems—to enable personalized rehabilitation management regardless of geographic location [7]. TR encompasses a wide range of services, including remote assessment, intervention delivery, treatment supervision, health education, efficacy monitoring, professional counseling, and disease prevention [8]. Its core advantages include improved accessibility [9], enhanced patient adherence [10], and the integration of multimodal interventions. These features are well aligned with the principles of personalized care and the biopsychosocial model of rehabilitation [11].

Currently, TR is widely utilized in the management of musculoskeletal [12], neurological [13], and cardiopulmonary conditions [14]. Emerging evidence indicates that VR-based immersive training can significantly reduce kinesiophobia in patients with CLBP [4], while artificial intelligence (AI) algorithms can dynamically optimize intervention intensity by analyzing patient gait patterns [15]. However, substantial heterogeneity exists across studies in terms of technological formats (e.g., standalone mobile applications vs. integrated multi-device systems), intervention content (e.g., exercise-only vs. multimodal therapies), and treatment duration [16]. This variability has posed challenges to establishing expert consensus regarding the efficacy and safety of TR for CLBP. Moreover, systematic reporting of technology-related adverse events remains limited, further complicating comprehensive safety assessments.

Previous systematic reviews have demonstrated that telerehabilitation is comparably effective to face-to-face and home-based interventions in alleviating pain and depressive symptoms, as well as improving functional status in patients with CLBP, with a moderate strength of evidence [17–19]. In cases where optimal in-person rehabilitation is not feasible, telerehabilitation serves as a viable alternative, offering benefits such as reduced costs and time savings [20]. However, current clinical guidelines for CLBP lack specific recommendations regarding which types of telerehabilitation methods should be implemented in practice [21, 22]. This gap may stem from earlier systematic reviews that primarily focused on traditional rehabilitation approaches and overlooked emerging digital interventions. A network meta-analysis (NMA) design offers a valuable approach to addressing this gap [23]. Unlike traditional pairwise meta-analyses, NMA enables simultaneous comparison and ranking of multiple interventions by integrating both direct and indirect evidence within a unified analytical framework [24].

Therefore, this systematic review and NMA aim to comprehensively assess RCTs investigating telerehabilitation for CLBP. Specifically, we will evaluate the effectiveness of different types of telerehabilitation interventions in improving pain intensity, functional disability, psychological symptoms, quality of life, intervention adherence, and patient satisfaction. Additionally, we will assess the relative safety of these interventions by examining the overall incidence and type-specific risk of adverse events. The efficacy and safety of each intervention type will be ranked using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking (SUCRA) probabilities [25]. The ultimate goal of this study is to provide robust, evidence-based support for clinical decision-making in CLBP management, identify the most effective forms of telerehabilitation and their moderating factors, and promote the implementation of the “Internet + Healthcare” policy in rehabilitation services.

Methods

Study design

The protocol for this systematic review and network meta-analysis has been registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; Registration No. CRD420251015478). It adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [26]. Any amendments to the protocol, if necessary, will be documented and reported in the final review.

Data sources

We will systematically search PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, the Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science from database inception to December 31, 2025, without language restrictions. Ongoing or unpublished studies will be identified through ClinicalTrials.gov, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, the EU Clinical Trials Register, and the International Clinical Trials Registry Platform. Reference lists of included studies, relevant systematic reviews, and clinical guidelines will be manually screened. The search strategy will use controlled vocabulary and free-text terms related to telerehabilitation and chronic low back pain. Searches will be updated before publication to ensure inclusion of the most recent evidence.

Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria will be defined according to the PICOS framework.

Study design

We will include randomized controlled trials (RCTs) with parallel-group, cluster, or crossover designs published in peer-reviewed journals. Non-randomized studies, observational studies, reviews, conference abstracts, and studies without accessible full text will be excluded. In line with the recommendations of the Cochrane Handbook [27], cluster RCTs will only be included if their results have been appropriately adjusted for the cluster design (e.g., analyses conducted at the level of allocation).

Participants

We will include adults with chronic low back pain (CLBP), defined according to NICE guidelines as nonspecific pain located between the lower rib margin and the gluteal crease and persisting for more than 12 weeks [28]. Studies involving serious spinal pathology will be excluded. Trials in which at least 50% of participants meet CLBP criteria will be included if outcome data for the CLBP subgroup are reported separately. In cases where eligibility is unclear, inclusion will be determined by consensus among the evaluators.

Interventions

This study will aim to compare the effectiveness and safety of different telerehabilitation interventions with other treatments for CLBP. Telerehabilitation will be defined as the delivery of rehabilitation services across geographic boundaries using information and communication technologies, including remote sensing, remote control, and data processing tools [29, 30]. To ensure comprehensive coverage, an expanded definition of telerehabilitation will be adopted, encompassing video-guided exercise, personalized app-based programs, VR training, remote monitoring with feedback, and online cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT). Studies evaluating combined telerehabilitation interventions—defined as the use of two or more telerehabilitation modalities, with or without additional non-digital co-interventions—will also be included.

Comparator interventions

Comparator interventions will not be restricted and may include usual care, waitlist control, no intervention, or conventional face-to-face treatment.

Outcomes

Outcome selection will follow the recommendations of the Initiative on Methods, Measurement, and Pain Assessment in Clinical Trials (IMMPACT) [31].

Primary outcome

The primary outcome will be pain intensity after the intervention, measured using continuous self-reported instruments such as the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), Numeric Rating Scale (NRS), SF-MPQ-2, JOABPEQ, or composite scales including the McGill Pain Questionnaire.

Secondary outcomes

Physical functioning will be assessed using validated instruments, including the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI), Roland–Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ), Quebec Back Pain Disability Scale (QBPDS), Core Outcome Measurement Index (COMI), and composite health measures such as the Short Form Health Surveys (SF-12, SF-36).

Emotional outcomes, including fear-avoidance, anxiety, and depression, will be evaluated using standardized psychological instruments, such as the Fear Avoidance Beliefs Questionnaire (FABQ), Pain Catastrophizing Scale (PCS), Tampa Scale for Kinesiophobia (TSK), Fear of Pain Questionnaire (FPQ), Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), 24-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24), Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI), and Symptom Checklist-90 (SCL-90).

Quality of life will be assessed using commonly applied measures, including the Short Form Health Surveys (SF-36, SF-12), EuroQol 5-Dimensional Scale (EQ-5D), Nottingham Health Profile (NHP), PROMIS Global Health Short Form-10 (PROMIS-GH-10), and the World Health Organization Quality of Life-Brief Version (WHOQOL-BREF). In addition, given the close association between sleep quality and overall quality of life [32], the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) will also be included.

Safety outcome

Adverse events (AEs): defined as the proportion of participants experiencing at least one adverse reaction during the intervention. Broadly defined to include any “adverse event,” “side effect,” “complication,” or event leading to termination of treatment associated with the telerehabilitation or comparative intervention, counted as the number of participants experiencing any one adverse event. The number of participants who experienced any of these adverse events is counted.

Intervention adherence and acceptability

Intervention adherence will be defined as the proportion of participants who completed the assigned intervention in either the experimental or control group. Acceptability will be assessed based on the number of participants who withdrew from the trial for any reason before treatment completion, while satisfaction will be evaluated through participants’ subjective ratings of the treatment’s effectiveness. All safety-related data included in the NMA will be assessed for these outcomes. Studies that employed alternative measurement tools were not excluded from this review.

Timing of outcome assessment

The primary analysis will be conducted at the post-intervention time point. Secondary analyses will include baseline, mid-intervention, and follow-up assessments categorized as short-term (≥ 2 to < 6 months), mid-term (≥ 6 to < 12 months), and long-term (≥ 12 months). Network meta-analyses (NMA) will be conducted separately for each of these time points.

Network processing nodes

Given the complexity and heterogeneity of telerehabilitation interventions, a multidimensional classification framework will be applied. First, intervention descriptions will be carefully reviewed, and telerehabilitation will be categorized using a split approach across four dimensions: technology platform (four treatment nodes), intervention content (three nodes), mode of interaction (two nodes), and level of technological intelligence (two nodes).

Subsequently, interventions will be further differentiated according to the presence or absence of additional non-telerehabilitation co-interventions. These co-interventions will be classified into the following categories: offline physical therapy, psychological interventions, pharmacological therapy, interventional procedures, surgical interventions, lifestyle interventions, and complementary and alternative therapies.

For studies involving combined interventions, classification will be based on the central role and proportional contribution of telerehabilitation within the overall treatment program.

Telerehabilitation-dominant interventions are those primarily delivered via telerehabilitation, with face-to-face components limited to baseline assessment or a minimal proportion of total contacts (e.g., ≤ 20%). These studies will be included in the primary analysis.

Balanced hybrid interventions involve telerehabilitation and face-to-face components delivered in approximately equal proportions and tightly integrated, such that their individual effects cannot be feasibly separated. These studies will be analyzed in a predefined combined-intervention subgroup, with outcomes interpreted as reflecting a blended treatment effect.

Face-to-face-assisted interventions are those in which conventional in-person rehabilitation constitutes the core treatment, while telerehabilitation serves only as an adjunct (e.g., reminders or supplementary education). Such studies will be excluded, as they do not permit isolation of the specific effects attributable to telerehabilitation.

Non-telerehabilitation co-interventions

Non-telerehabilitation interventions will be categorized into several treatment modalities. Physical therapy includes manual techniques such as chiropractic adjustments, joint mobilization, and soft tissue massage; exercise therapy encompassing core stability training, stretching, and strength exercises like the McKenzie method; and physical modalities such as ultrasound, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS), and thermal therapies. Pharmacological treatments consist of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen), analgesics (e.g., acetaminophen or short-term opioids), muscle relaxants (e.g., cyclobenzaprine), and antidepressants or anxiolytics (e.g., duloxetine), particularly for CLBP patients with psychological comorbidities. Psychological interventions include face-to-face cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) to address fear-avoidance beliefs, mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) to alleviate stress, and biofeedback therapy (BFRT) for autonomic regulation. Interventional procedures encompass corticosteroid or local anesthetic injections, nerve blocks (e.g., epidural or nerve root), and radiofrequency ablation for disrupting pain pathways. Surgical interventions include discectomy for herniated disc relief, spinal fusion for stabilization, and vertebroplasty for osteoporotic fractures. Lifestyle modifications involve weight management through diet and exercise, posture correction via ergonomic training, and smoking cessation counseling to support tissue healing and pain control. Lastly, complementary and alternative therapies such as acupuncture, osteopathy, yoga, and Tai Chi integrate physical movement with relaxation and mindfulness, contributing to a holistic rehabilitation approach.

Before data analysis, if an insufficient number of studies are available for a specific treatment node (e.g., fewer than two studies), we will consult with clinical experts on the review team to determine whether it is appropriate to further consolidate or reclassify treatment nodes. Any post hoc modifications to the network geometry made through this process will be documented and justified in the final review.

Selection process

In this study, Zotero (version 7.0.13) will be used for the de-duplication of references, and the Covidence platform will be employed for literature screening to enhance the accuracy and reproducibility of the selection process [33]. The screening will be conducted in two phases: in the first phase, two independent reviewers will screen the titles and abstracts of the retrieved records; in the second phase, full-text articles that pass the initial screening will be reviewed to determine final eligibility for inclusion.

In cases of duplicate data, priority will be given to studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up durations to ensure data completeness and reliability. To identify and address duplicate publications, verification will be conducted using trial registration numbers (e.g., ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers) and author information. The number of excluded duplicates, along with reasons for exclusion, will be documented in the PRISMA flowchart.

All reviewers will have comparable experience in applying the inclusion criteria and will receive training specific to the review. Any disagreements during the screening process will be resolved through discussion with a third expert in systematic reviews. If necessary, corresponding authors will be contacted via email (up to three attempts) to confirm study eligibility.

Handling of missing data

For missing continuous outcome data, several strategies will be employed to ensure completeness and accuracy of the analysis [34]. If standard deviations (SDs) are not reported, they will be calculated from available data such as standard errors (SEs), p-values, or confidence intervals (CIs). In cases where only medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) are provided, the SD will be estimated using the formula IQR ÷ 1.35, assuming a normal distribution, and the median will be used as an approximation of the mean. For data that are non-normally distributed or skewed, transformation methods (e.g., the Hozo method) will be considered, and sensitivity analyses will be conducted to assess the robustness of the estimates. If essential data are only presented graphically, appropriate extraction tools will be used to retrieve the required values. For missing dichotomous outcome data, such as adverse event rates, a worst-case scenario assumption will be applied, followed by sensitivity analyses to evaluate its influence on the findings. If none of these approaches are feasible, the study authors will be contacted directly (up to three attempts) to request the missing information.

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers will assess the risk of bias in the included studies using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 (RoB 2.0) tool [35]. The assessment will cover the following domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Each domain will be rated as having a low, some concerns, or high risk of bias. The results will be visualized using the Robvis tool [36]. All studies will be included in the primary analysis; however, studies deemed to have a high risk of bias will be excluded in sensitivity analyses to evaluate their potential influence on the findings.

Certainty of evidence

We will use the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) web application to assess the quality of evidence, following the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) framework [37]. CINeMA evaluates six domains: within-study bias, across-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. Based on these assessments, the overall confidence in the evidence will be rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. These ratings will be presented in forest plots or summary tables to help readers understand the reliability of the findings. For outcomes with low confidence, results will be interpreted with caution, and further research will be recommended.

To assess reporting bias, we will use the ROB-MEN (Risk of Bias due to Missing Evidence in Network Meta-Analysis) tool, which includes the evaluation of small-study effects [38]. The overall CINeMA rating for each comparison presented in the main text will be based on the average assessments of risk of bias and indirectness.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Effectiveness measurement

For continuous outcomes, analyses will be conducted using standardized mean differences (SMD, Hedges’ g) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). When different measurement scales are used across studies, outcome scores will be converted to a standardized 0–100 scale using min–max normalization. Effect sizes will be interpreted as follows: small effect (SMD = 0.2–0.5), medium effect (SMD = 0.5–0.8), and large effect (SMD > 0.8).

For dichotomous outcomes, odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs will be calculated, with a preference for data reported based on an intention-to-treat (ITT) or modified ITT approach.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Pairwise meta-analyses will be conducted for all direct comparisons. Heterogeneity will be assessed using the Q-test (with p < 0.10 indicating potential heterogeneity) and quantified using the I2 statistic: I2 ≤ 25% indicates low heterogeneity; I2 > 50% indicates moderate heterogeneity; and I2 ≥ 75% indicates high heterogeneity. If substantial heterogeneity is detected (I2 > 50%), potential sources will be explored through meta-regression or subgroup analyses. If data are missing and cannot be estimated or obtained from the study authors, if fewer than two studies are available for a given comparison, or if heterogeneity remains too high, a narrative synthesis will be performed in place of quantitative analysis.

Assessment of transitivity

To evaluate transitivity, we will examine the distribution of potential effect modifiers (e.g., baseline pain intensity, age, gender) across studies. Transitivity will also be assessed by calculating changes in continuous variables before and after non-telerehabilitation treatments using Hedge’s g. Additionally, consistency between direct and indirect evidence will be assessed through statistical tests for inconsistency.

Network meta-analysis

Network meta-analyses will be conducted using random-effects models within a frequentist framework, employing the mvmeta and network packages in Stata SE 15.0. Interventions will be ranked using Surface Under the Cumulative Ranking Curve (SUCRA) values, and network diagrams will be created to visualize the comparative structure. For multi-arm trials, correlations between arms will be appropriately accounted for in the model.

Inconsistency assessment

The validity of network meta-analysis (NMA) results depends on the assumption of consistency—that is, agreement between direct and indirect evidence. We assumed consistency across all treatment comparisons and a common heterogeneity parameter. Global inconsistency was evaluated using the design-by-treatment interaction model, with a p-value < 0.10 indicating significant inconsistency. Local inconsistency was assessed using node-splitting methods. If inconsistency is detected, sensitivity analyses or inconsistency models will be employed. Should significant inconsistency persist, and its source remains unexplained, we will consider refraining from conducting the NMA.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses will include: (1) exclusion of studies with a high risk of bias; (2) comparison of fixed-effect and random-effects models; and (3) exclusion of studies with small sample sizes. These analyses aim to assess the robustness and stability of the results.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup effects will be explored using network meta-regression within the NMA framework to assess differences between subgroups. When included trials report subgroup-specific data (e.g., by age or gender), we will attempt to harmonize these subgroup categories across studies to facilitate meaningful subgroup analyses within the network. Additional subgroup analyses will be conducted based on key variables such as technology platform, intervention content, mode of interaction, and level of intelligence, provided sufficient data are available, and the integrity of the original network structure is preserved.

Small-study effects and publication bias

To assess small-study effects and potential publication bias, we will use conventional funnel plots and Egger’s test, along with comparison-adjusted funnel plots tailored for network meta-analysis. Given the anticipated complexity of interventions, including multi-component and combination therapies, we will conduct a component network meta-analysis (CNMA) where feasible. In the CNMA framework, the effect of a composite intervention will be modeled either as the sum of its components (additive model) or as their interaction (interaction model). Publication bias will be further assessed by examining asymmetry in comparison-adjusted funnel plots. Additionally, meta-regressions incorporating study sample size and effect estimates will be used to detect the presence of small-study effects.

Discussion

This systematic review and NMA are designed to comprehensively evaluate the comparative effectiveness and safety of various telerehabilitation interventions for CLBP. With the rapid evolution of digital health technologies and the growing need for remote healthcare delivery—especially in the context of aging populations and global health crises like COVID-19—telerehabilitation presents a promising and scalable approach for managing CLBP [16, 39]. This study applies a rigorous methodological framework, including comprehensive database searches, standardized data extraction, risk of bias assessment, and GRADE-based evaluation of evidence credibility using the CINeMA platform [37]. These efforts aim to generate high-quality, reliable, and clinically relevant findings to inform both practice and policy. The categorization of interventions into distinct nodes (e.g., video-guided exercise, AI-enhanced systems, synchronous vs asynchronous modes) further strengthens the analytical depth of the NMA. This approach enables a more precise comparison of intervention efficacy and supports the identification of specific components that drive clinical benefit. Moreover, by employing advanced tools such as the Cochrane RoB 2.0 for bias assessment and ROB-MEN for publication bias, this study ensures a transparent and reproducible evaluation of study quality and data integrity [35, 38].

Despite its strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, telerehabilitation is a rapidly evolving field, and new interventions or updates to existing platforms may emerge after the literature search is completed, potentially affecting the relevance and timeliness of our findings. Second, considerable heterogeneity is expected among the included studies due to variations in intervention content, delivery formats, and patient populations. Although network meta-analysis allows for statistical adjustment of such variability, clinical heterogeneity may still impact the generalizability of the results. Moreover, the reliability of our conclusions is inherently tied to the quality of the primary studies. While sensitivity analyses will be conducted by excluding studies with a high risk of bias, study-level biases cannot be eliminated. Another challenge is the management of missing data. Although standard methods—such as imputing missing standard deviations and transforming non-normally distributed data—will be applied, these techniques may introduce some degree of uncertainty into the effect estimates. Finally, categorizing interventions, especially in studies involving multi-component or co-interventions, may involve a level of subjectivity. Although expert consensus was used to minimize misclassification, subtle differences in intervention design and content may still influence comparability.

From a clinical perspective, this review is expected to provide critical insights into which telerehabilitation strategies are most effective and safe for patients with CLBP. Given the significant global burden of CLBP and the growing demand for accessible and cost-effective treatment options, the results of this study may guide healthcare providers in selecting appropriate digital interventions tailored to patient needs. In addition, this research may inform the development of clinical guidelines and support decision-making for policymakers and payers considering the integration of telerehabilitation into standard care pathways.

Looking ahead, future research should explore long-term outcomes of telerehabilitation, such as sustained pain reduction, functional improvement, and quality of life, which are often underreported in current trials. Furthermore, implementation studies evaluating real-world effectiveness, patient adherence, cost-effectiveness, and equity of access—especially in underserved or rural populations—are essential to translating evidence into practice. There is also a need for standardized reporting of digital intervention characteristics to facilitate comparability across studies.

In conclusion, this systematic review and network meta-analysis will address a critical gap in the literature by evaluating and comparing the efficacy and safety of diverse telerehabilitation interventions for CLBP. By applying a transparent and robust methodological framework, the study aims to generate actionable evidence that can support clinicians, researchers, and policymakers in advancing digital rehabilitation strategies for chronic pain management.
