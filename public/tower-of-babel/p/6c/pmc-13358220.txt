Identification and Genomic Analysis of the First Salmonella typhimurium Monophasic Variant 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ Isolate Harbouring a Chromosomally Integrated bla NDM‐5
Authors: Shihan Zeng, Weilong Guo, Chengfeng Lin, Lianghui Peng, Jingjie Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report the first clinical strain of the monophasic variant of Salmonella typhimurium (serotype 1,4,[5],12:i:‐) belonging to ST34 with chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5. Although chromosomal integration of bla NDM‐5 has been described in other Enterobacterales and Salmonella serovars, this is the first such report in this high‐risk clone. This study characterises the genomic and epidemiological background of this strain. The isolate (1111‐90), obtained from a paediatric patient, was subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing, whole‐genome sequencing and comparative genomic analysis against 674 complete bla NDM‐5‐positive plasmids retrieved from the PlasmidScope database. The isolate exhibited a multidrug‐resistant phenotype, including carbapenem resistance, while retaining susceptibility to tigecycline and aztreonam. It harboured 13 resistance genes distributed across the chromosome and two plasmids. The ~11 kb chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5 segment is flanked by directly oriented IS26 elements, forming an IS26‐bounded composite transposon with no canonical target‐site duplication at its boundaries. Phylogenetic analysis suggested that this chromosomal segment likely originated from IncX3 or IncI1 plasmid lineages prevalent in China. Comparative genetic analysis highlighted the significant role of mobile genetic elements (IS26, IS5 and IS3000) in the spread of bla NDM‐5 within S. typhimurium . The chromosomal integration of an IS26‐bounded bla NDM‐5 module in S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34 suggests the emergence of a stable, high‐risk carbapenem‐resistant clone, underscoring the need for genomic surveillance to track IS26‐mediated chromosomal integration events.Keywords: chromosome, IS26‐composite transposon, monophasic variant, NDM‐5, Salmonella typhimurium

Introduction

Non‐typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is a leading cause of foodborne gastroenteritis worldwide. Among NTS serovars, Salmonella typhimurium and its monophasic variant (antigenic formula 1,4,[5],12:i:‐; hereafter referred to as S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐) have become particular public health threats because they are spreading and readily pick up multidrug resistance (MDR) (Nambiar et al. 2024; Shi et al. 2025). In China, S. typhimurium remains a dominant serovar in clinical settings (Yu et al. 2024; Kong et al. 2025). While most infections are self‐limiting, invasive disease can occur in vulnerable populations, requiring treatment with fluoroquinolones or third‐generation cephalosporins (Foley and Lynne 2008; Fernandez et al. 2018; Chen et al. 2019). However, the global emergence of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) S. typhimurium , especially those belonging to the globally disseminated sequence type 34 (ST34), has posed significant therapeutic challenges. ST34 has been identified as an epidemic clone due to its extensive drug resistance and increased virulence, complicating clinical management in many regions (Biswas et al. 2019; Gao et al. 2023; Nambiar et al. 2024). Carbapenems remain last‐resort therapeutic options for invasive infections caused by MDR S. typhimurium . Nevertheless, recent years have witnessed increasing reports of carbapenem‐resistant S. typhimurium isolates worldwide (Huang et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023; Deng et al. 2024). Carbapenem resistance in Salmonella is still much less common than in Klebsiella pneumoniae or Escherichia coli , but carbapenemase‐producing Salmonella strains are a public health worry.

Among carbapenemase genes, New‐Delhi Metallo β‐lactamase (NDM) is clinically significant due to its broad substrate spectrum and mobility (Nordmann et al. 2012; Wu et al. 2019). Many NDM variants have appeared since NDM was first discovered. NDM‐5 has taken over from NDM‐1 as the main subtype, with better carbapenem and cephalosporin hydrolysis and a possible edge in host fitness and virulence (Nordmann and Poirel 2014; Xia et al. 2024; He et al. 2026). Reports of NDM‐5 in S. typhimurium remain few but are gradually increasing. So far, bla NDM‐5 in S. typhimurium has been predominantly located on mobilizable plasmids, including IncX3, IncFII, IncHI2 and IncI1 types (Li et al. 2017; Yang et al. 2017, 2026; Gao et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2020; Zeng et al. 2023; Ke et al. 2024; Huang et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025; Zhao et al. 2025). These plasmids often harbour an array of mobile genetic elements, such as insertion sequences IS26, IS5 and ISAba125, which facilitate the mobilisation and horizontal transfer of bla NDM‐5 across different bacterial species (Li et al. 2017; Gao et al. 2020; Zhao et al. 2025). We searched PubMed up to May 25, 2026 using “Typhimurium” AND “NDM” and found no previous report of bla NDM‐5 integrated into the chromosome of S. typhimurium , although such integration has been seen in other serovars like S. kentucky and S. indiana (Zhou et al. 2025). Chromosomal integration could stabilise resistance genes, ensuring vertical inheritance and persistence even without antibiotic selection.

In this study, we characterise a clinical S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34 isolate that carries bla NDM‐5 in its chromosome. We (i) analyse its genomic and resistance profile, (ii) compare its chromosomal locus with 674 complete bla NDM‐5‐positive plasmids to infer its evolutionary origin, and (iii) assess the epidemiological context and genetic environments of bla NDM‐5 in S. typhimurium . Our results show a new way that carbapenem resistance has evolved in this high‐risk monophasic ST34 clone and make clear that genomic surveillance is urgently needed.

Materials and Methods

Bacterial Isolation and Identification

From a paediatric case of enteritis, a carbapenem‐resistant Salmonella isolate (1111–90) was isolated from a stool sample at the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China). Strain identification was confirmed using matrix‐assisted laser desorption/ionization time‐of‐flight mass spectrometry (VITEK MS PRIME, bioMérieux, France). Serotyping was performed via slide agglutination with commercial antisera (Tianrun, China) following the manufacturer's protocol.

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST)

Antimicrobial susceptibility was assessed using the VITEK‐2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) with the VITEK 2 AST‐N334 card. The minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) for a panel of antibiotics—including cefoxitin (FOX), cefuroxime (CXM), ceftriaxone (CRO), ceftazidime (CAZ), amoxicillin‐clavulanic acid (AMC), piperacillin‐tazobactam (TZP), levofloxacin (LVX), cotrimoxazole (SXT), imipenem (IPM), ertapenem (ETP) and tigecycline (TGC)—were determined. Additionally, susceptibility to aztreonam (ATM), cefepime (FEP), ceftazidime‐avibactam (CZA), ampicillin (AMP), chloramphenicol (C) and tetracycline (TE) were evaluated using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method. All procedures and interpretive criteria adhered to the standards outlined by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, M100‐Ed35). E. coli ATCC 25922 was used as the quality control strain, and its susceptibility results are provided in Table S1.

Whole‐Genome Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

Genome Sequencing and Basic Characterisation

Genomic DNA of isolate 1111‐90 was extracted, and libraries were constructed for both Illumina and Nanopore sequencing by Novogene Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China). The resulting reads were assembled into a complete genome using Unicycler (v0.4.8) (Wick et al. 2017). Serotype and sequence type (ST) were determined in silico using SISTR (v1.1.1) and MLST (v2.18.0) (Larsen et al. 2012; Yoshida et al. 2016), respectively. The genome was annotated with Prokka (v1.14.5) (Seemann 2014). Antimicrobial resistance genes and plasmid replicon types were identified using ResFinder (v4.1) and PlasmidFinder (v2.1), respectively, with thresholds of ≥ 90% identity and ≥ 60% coverage (Carattoli et al. 2014; Bortolaia et al. 2020). The lower coverage threshold was used only as a screening cutoff, and all clinically relevant assignments were manually reviewed. Insertion sequences were annotated by alignment against the ISfinder database (Siguier et al. 2006). The genetic context comparisons were illustrated using EasyFig and Adobe Illustrator (Sullivan et al. 2011).

Comparative Plasmid Genomics

To trace the potential plasmid origin of the chromosomally located bla NDM‐5 region, we analysed a curated dataset of 674 complete bla NDM‐5‐positive plasmid sequences retrieved from PlasmidScope (Li et al. 2025). Records were included when they were annotated as complete plasmid sequences carrying bla NDM‐5. All plasmids were uniformly annotated with Prokka (v1.14.5), and the pan‐genome was constructed using Panaroo (v1.2.10) in strict mode. Euclidean distances were calculated from the gene presence/absence matrix, and hierarchical clustering was performed using complete linkage with the hclust function in R. Pairwise BLASTn comparisons were then used to generate a coverage matrix, considering alignments with nucleotide identity > 80% for the heatmap visualisation. The clustering tree and corresponding coverage heatmap were integrated using the ggtree package.

Epidemiological Visualisation

A Sankey diagram was constructed to visualise the epidemiological linkages between geographical origin, host species and plasmid replicon types of bla NDM‐5‐carrying plasmids. The analysis included 778 records from PlasmidScope (representing non‐deduplicated reports, hence the larger number than in the previous section). The diagram was generated using the ggsankeyfier package (v0.1.8) in R.

Data

The Whole Genome Shotgun project of S. typhimurium strain 1111‐90 has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession JBWQDO000000000. The version described in this paper is JBWQDO010000000. The BioProject accession number is PRJNA1369658, and the BioSample accession number is SAMN53398417.

Results

Bacterial Identification, Typing and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Profile

The isolate 1111‐90 was recovered from a faecal specimen of a paediatric patient diagnosed with enteritis. In silico serotyping using SISTR identified the isolate as S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐. MLST further classified it as sequence type 34 (ST34). AST revealed a MDR phenotype. As summarised in Table 1, the isolate was resistant to all tested β‐lactam agents, including penicillins, β‐lactam/β‐lactamase inhibitor combinations, cephamycins, cephalosporins and carbapenems (ETP and IPM). Notably, it was also resistant to CZA. However, the isolate remained susceptible to the monobactam antibiotic ATM. Additionally, resistance was observed against SXT, C and TE. In contrast, the isolate remained susceptible to TGC and exhibited intermediate susceptibility to LVX.

Genomic Features and Resistance Gene Landscape

Whole‐genome sequencing and subsequent genomic analysis identified a comprehensive arsenal of antimicrobial resistance genes in strain 1111‐90. A total of 13 resistance genes were detected (Table 2). The chromosome was found to harbour seven resistance genes, namely tet(A), qnrS1, bla NDM‐5, ble MBL, sul1, aadA2 and dfrA12. The p0111‐like plasmid, designated p1111‐90‐1, carried six resistance genes, which included floR, also a second tet(A) allele, bla TEM‐1, aph(6)‐Id, aph(3″)‐Ib and sul2. Regarding the IncQ1‐type plasmid, designated p1111‐90‐2, it was identified to carry the aph(3′)‐Ia gene. The collective presence of these chromosomally‐ and plasmid‐encoded resistance genes, particularly the carbapenemase gene bla NDM‐5 along with multiple determinants conferring resistance to aminoglycosides, sulphonamides, tetracyclines and β‐lactams, provides a genetic explanation for the observed MDR phenotype.

Literature Review on the Localisation of bla NDM −5 in S. typhimurium

Based on a systematic PubMed search using the query “Typhimurium” AND “NDM” (last search date: May 25, 2026), ten original studies (published between 2017 and 2026) reporting S. typhimurium isolates carrying bla NDM‐5 were identified (Table S2). The isolates were obtained from diverse sources, including paediatric patient faeces (n = 6), retail pork (n = 2), elderly male patient faeces (n = 1) and diarrheal patients (n = 1). All isolates originated from various regions in China (Shanghai, Guangzhou, Jiangsu, Nanjing, Beijing, Chengdu and Jinhua). Among the ten studies, nine reported the sequence type, all of which were confirmed as ST34 (one study did not specify ST type). Regarding serotype, only three studies explicitly identified their isolates as the monophasic variant S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐. Critically, in nine of the ten studies, bla NDM‐5 was located on plasmids with clearly identified replicon types. The predominant plasmid replicon types among the nine studies were IncX3 (3/9), IncHI2 (3/9), IncFII (2/9) and IncI1 (1/9). This underscores the novelty of our current study, which reports the first instance of bla NDM‐5 being chromosomally integrated into an S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34 strain.

Phylogenetic Origin and Epidemiological Context of the Chromosomal bla NDM −5 Segment

We identified an ~11 kb fragment encompassing the bla NDM‐5 gene and its flanking regions on the chromosome of S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ strain 1111‐90, hereafter termed the bla NDM‐5 genomic locus. To trace the origin of the chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5, the bla NDM‐5 genomic locus from strain 1111‐90 was subjected to BLASTn analysis. The results demonstrated that this fragment shared 100% query coverage and 100% nucleotide identity with sequences found exclusively on plasmids, providing compelling evidence for its recent mobilisation from a plasmid precursor into the chromosome.

To further elucidate the evolutionary origin of this segment, a comparative pan‐genome analysis was performed using a curated collection of 674 complete bla NDM‐5‐positive plasmids (Table S3) alongside the chromosomal locus from 1111‐90. Hierarchical clustering of the gene content revealed 17 distinct clusters (Figure 1A). The pairwise coverage heatmap (Figure 1B) visualises the sequence coverage between each pair of the 674 plasmids based on BLASTn comparisons (≥ 80% nucleotide identity). High coverage values (red) were predominantly observed within the same major plasmid clusters, indicating that plasmids within each cluster share highly conserved backbone structures, whereas lower coverage (blue) between different clusters reflects greater sequence divergence. This pattern supports the clustering analysis shown in Figure 1A and confirms that the chromosomal bla NDM‐5 locus from strain 1111‐90 shares a conserved backbone with the plasmids in its corresponding cluster. The chromosomal bla NDM‐5 locus grouped specifically within Cluster 12. This cluster comprised the chromosomal locus from strain 1111‐90 and 54 plasmids (Table S4). Replicon typing of the 54 plasmids revealed a significant diversity, with the most prevalent types being IncX3 (15/54, 27.8%) and IncI1 (11/54, 20.4%). Other identified types included IncB/O/K/Z, IncFIB and IncR, while replicons were not detected in 13 of the plasmids. Epidemiologically, the plasmids in Cluster 12 were predominantly isolated from E. coli (41/54, 75.9%) and K. pneumoniae (10/54, 18.5%), with nearly half (26/54, 48.1%) originating from China, alongside other countries including Myanmar, the United States and Japan.

The epidemiological relationships between geographical origin, host species and plasmid replicon types across the entire plasmid collection are further visualised in the Sankey diagram (Figure 2). This representation corroborates the clustering analysis, confirming that bla NDM‐5‐harbouring plasmids are predominantly reported from Asia, with China being the most significant contributor (n = 220), followed by Bangladesh (n = 83), Japan (n = 18), Myanmar (n = 16) and Thailand (n = 15). In contrast, reports from Europe and North America, such as the United States (n = 62), the United Kingdom (n = 36) and Ireland (n = 18), were comparatively less frequent. The diagram also affirms that E. coli and K. pneumoniae serve as the major bacterial hosts, with IncX (particularly IncX3), IncFIA/B/C, and IncFII plasmids acting as the primary vectors for the global dissemination of bla NDM‐5 (See Table S5 for detailed metadata).

Comparative Architecture of the bla NDM −5 Genetic Environment in S. typhimurium

The bla NDM‐5 gene is located on this chromosome contig, flanked by chromosome‐associated genes—including prsE (type I secretion protein), intA (prophage integrase) and a tmRNA gene upstream, and elmGT, fes and pfeA downstream (Figure 3A). The genetic context in strain 1111‐90 was identified as “IS26‐ ΔISEcp1‐ ΔISAba125‐ bla NDM‐5‐ ble MBL‐ trpF‐ dsbC‐ IS91‐ sul2‐ emrE‐ aadA2‐ dfrA12‐ xerD‐ IS26”, representing an ~11 kb region flanked by two directly oriented IS26 elements that are responsible for mobilising a substantial resistance gene island onto the chromosome. BLASTn comparison showed that this ~11 kb region shares 99.98% nucleotide identity (over 11,321 bp) with a chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5 segment reported in an E. coli ST410 isolate (CP031653) from a dog in the UK (Figure 3A). In that isolate, the homologous region was flanked by two IS15DIV elements (an isoform of IS26 with > 99.5% identity).

A comparative analysis of the genetic environment flanking the bla NDM‐5 gene was conducted between our chromosomally integrated strain 1111‐90 and other S. typhimurium isolates carrying the gene on various plasmids (Figure 3B; plasmid details in Table S2). Of note, the core module “ΔISAba125‐ bla NDM‐5‐ ble MBL‐ trpF‐ dsbC” was consistently present across all compared genetic environments. In contrast, IS91‐family elements were exclusively identified in the chromosomal locus and the plasmid pLS20223695_NDM5, while the cutA gene was present in other plasmids but absent in these two contexts. The chromosomal structure exhibited significant structural homology to that of the IncI1 plasmid, pLS20223695_NDM5. However, the incomplete sequence availability of pLS20223695_NDM5, represented by a single contig lacking flanking regions, precluded comprehensive comparison of the complete genetic context. “IS5‐ ΔISAba125‐ bla NDM‐5‐ ble MBL‐ trpF‐ dsbC‐ cutA‐ IS26” was identified across multiple plasmid types, including IncFII, IncX3 and IncHI2/IncHI2A. However, in the IncHI2/IncHI2A plasmid pNDM_23S9 and the IncHI2 plasmid pNBFE‐049, the terminal IS26 was found to be truncated. Furthermore, the element IS3000, either intact or truncated, was consistently present in the genetic environments of these three plasmid types. It is noteworthy that all compared plasmids and the chromosomal segment originated from China.

Discussion

The global dissemination of carbapenem‐resistant Salmonella poses a significant threat to public health (Wu et al. 2023). S. typhimurium ST34 has become a dominant multidrug‐resistant clone worldwide (Mthembu et al. 2021; Zhang, Ge, et al. 2022; Chung The et al. 2023). Resistance to first‐line antibiotics is now common in ST34, but carbapenem resistance remains rare, and more concerning when it occurs. The monophasic variant S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ has been increasingly reported in China and elsewhere, often carrying multidrug resistance plasmids (Nambiar et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2026). In this study, we describe a clinical S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34 isolate in which an IS26‐bounded bla NDM‐5 module, closely related to globally disseminated plasmid contexts, was detected in the chromosome by hybrid whole‐genome assembly and comparative genomic analysis. NDM‐5 itself has spread widely in recent years, replacing NDM‐1 as the dominant variant (Zhang et al. 2017), and its enhanced hydrolytic activity against carbapenems has been increasingly reported in various bacterial species (Hornsey et al. 2011; Wang et al. 2020). The isolate exhibited an unusual susceptibility profile: carbapenem‐resistant but aztreonam‐susceptible, with an ETP MIC of 2 mg/L and a substantially higher IMP MIC (≥ 16 mg/L). This pattern is consistent with the enzymatic activity of NDM‐5 and has been documented in other NDM‐5‐producing Salmonella isolates, including a report describing two S. typhi isolates with identical MIC values (Nizamuddin et al. 2023). Such discrepancies are likely attributable to strain‐specific differences in porin expression or efflux pump activity, which can differentially affect the accumulation of distinct carbapenem agents. Beyond the immediate clinical picture, climate change is accelerating the spread of water‐ and food‐borne pathogens such as S. typhi , adding another layer of urgency to surveillance efforts (Jia et al. 2024).

A pivotal finding of our study is the chromosomal integration of bla NDM‐5 in S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34. To our knowledge, this represents the first report of such an event in this clone, marking a critical evolutionary step. Integration into the chromosome ensures stable vertical inheritance and promotes clonal expansion even without antibiotic pressure (Zongo et al. 2024). The event is driven by an IS26 composite transposon. The genetic context in strain 1111‐90 was identified as “IS26‐ ΔISEcp1‐ ΔISAba125‐ bla NDM‐5‐ ble MBL‐ trpF‐ dsbC‐ IS91‐ sul2‐ emrE‐ aadA2‐ dfrA12‐ xerD‐ IS26”. Flanking the transposon we found an 8 bp sequence (GACGGTGA) upstream of the left IS26 and a different 8 bp sequence (AATACTAC) downstream of the right IS26. When two IS26 elements are arranged in the same or opposite orientations, they typically combine to form a composite transposon, thereby facilitating the transfer of resistance genes located between them (Harmer et al. 2014, 2020). The absence of identical target‐site duplications argues against a simple replicative IS26 insertion. Nevertheless, the directly oriented IS26 boundaries are consistent with a composite transposon or translocatable unit, possibly generated by conservative transposition or subsequent recombination.

This IS26‐family transposon is highly conserved across species. The near identical structure of the bla NDM‐5‐containing IS26‐composite transposon between our S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ isolate and a canine E. coli ST410 isolate (Reynolds et al. 2019) strongly suggests that this IS26‐family transposon represents a highly conserved mobilizable unit for bla NDM‐5 capture. In the E. coli isolate, the homologous region is flanked by IS15DIV elements, a close variant of IS26 sharing > 99.5% nucleotide identity (Harmer et al. 2014). Likewise, the chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5 in S. kentucky and S. indiana also harbours multiple IS26 copies (Zhou et al. 2025). Although the integrated regions in these isolates are larger than the ~11 kb module in our strain, IS26 or IS15DIV consistently flanks the bla NDM‐5 gene in all cases, underscoring the central role of IS26‐family transposons. An independent study on S. mbandaka found an IS26‐flanked bla NDM‐1 region both in the chromosome and on an IncC plasmid, directly showing that IS26‐family transposons can transfer resistance modules bidirectionally between plasmids and the chromosome (Song et al. 2025).

Comparative genomic analysis of 674 bla NDM‐5‐positive plasmids strongly suggests that the chromosomal bla NDM‐5 locus in our strain originated from IncX3 or IncI1 plasmid lineages that are prevalent in China (Figure 2). IncX3 plasmids are known major vectors for bla NDM‐5 in China, especially in E. coli from clinical and environmental sources (Wu et al. 2019; Ma et al. 2020). IncI1 plasmids have also been implicated in the regional spread of bla NDM‐5 (Zhang, Chen, et al. 2022). Of particular interest is the IncI1 plasmid pLS20223695_NDM5, which has the closest genetic environment to our chromosomal locus. It was isolated from a paediatric patient in Zhejiang Province, and its bla NDM‐5 was acquired via an IS15DIV‐flanked composite transposon from a co‐colonising E. coli strain in the same patient (Zhao et al. 2025). This finding strongly supports our hypothesis that an IS26‐family composite transposon mediated the cross‐species mobilisation of bla NDM‐5, ultimately leading to its chromosomal integration in S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐. The high prevalence of bla NDM‐5 in Asia provides an important backdrop for understanding this mobilisation. A large‐scale analysis of 1817 closed Salmonella genomes revealed that plasmids harbour 69.8% of ARGs and the largest ARG reservoir (Jia et al. 2023). Our data show that regional dominance of IncX3 and IncI1 plasmids in E. coli and K. pneumoniae creates ample opportunities for bla NDM‐5 transmission (Figure 3). The key evolutionary step is the capture of this gene by IS26‐family composite transposons, which then facilitates its chromosomal integration.

The flanking sequences around the conserved bla NDM‐5 core module varied across different plasmid backgrounds. Both IS5 and IS3000 have been frequently reported upstream of bla NDM‐5. IS5 can enhance transcriptional activity of adjacent promoters in E. coli , while IS3000 is often associated with Klebsiella and has been implicated in reactivating bla NDM mobilisation (Schnetz and Rak 1992; Campos et al. 2015). ISCR1, an IS91‐family transposase, is typically linked to sul1 (Toleman et al. 2006; Luo et al. 2022). In our study, however, the IS91‐family element was linked to sul2, though the incomplete sequence of pLS20223695_NDM5 means the full context may be more complex. Such structural variability shows that insertion sequences like IS26, IS5, IS3000 and IS91 play a central yet flexible role in spreading and rearranging bla NDM‐5.

This study has several limitations. First, our observations are based on a single clinical isolate from one centre. Second, we did not perform conjugation or transposition assays to functionally confirm the mobility of the IS26‐bounded segment. However, recent studies have successfully used inverse PCR to detect circular translocatable units of chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5 in other Salmonella serovars (Zhou et al. 2025), providing a feasible approach for future functional validation. Third, the temporal and geographic scope of our plasmid dataset may not capture the full global diversity of bla NDM‐5‐harbouring genetic contexts in Salmonella. Fourth, the potential fitness cost associated with this chromosomal integration event remains to be assessed using in vivo models. To counter the emerging threat of such high‐risk carbapenem‐resistant clones, novel antimicrobial strategies are urgently needed. Our work shows that bla NDM‐5 has integrated into the chromosome of S. 1,4,[5],12:i:‐ ST34 via an IS26‐composite transposon, likely from IncX3 or IncI1 plasmids. This is a reminder of how mobile elements spread resistance and why genomic surveillance is urgently needed. New antimicrobial strategies, such as the engineered endolysin ENDO‐1252/KL9P, which kills multiple Salmonella serovars without outer membrane permeabilizers, offer a promising future direction (Tung et al. 2025).

Conclusion

In conclusion, we report the first clinical isolate of the monophasic variant of Salmonella typhimurium (serotype 1,4,[5],12:i:‐) ST34 with a chromosomally integrated bla NDM‐5. This event marks an evolutionary convergence of a high‐risk clone and a potent carbapenemase gene, stabilised by an IS26 composite transposon. Our data reveal that the dissemination of bla NDM‐5 is driven by conserved insertion sequences and a portable resistance module. These findings highlight the need for enhanced genomic surveillance to track the spread of such chromosomally stabilised, high‐risk clones.
