Utility of alternative specimens in the diagnosis of pulmonary tuberculosis in people living with human immunodeficiency virus in Guinea
Authors: Boubacar Djelo Diallo, Salifou Talassone Bangoura, Sidikiba Sidibé, Aboubacar Sidiki Magassouba, Fatoumata Bhoye Diallo, Alhassane Diallo, Saidouba Cherif Camara, Maladho Diaby, Kadio Jean-Jacques Olivier Kadio, Alioune Camara, Alpha-Kabinet Keita, Nagham Khanafer, Abdoulaye Touré
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis (TB) remains under-diagnosed in people living with HIV (PLHIV) and is a major cause of mortality in these population. Sputum is the primary specimen for TB diagnosis; however, many PLHIV are unable to produce quality sputum. This study assessed the diagnostic utility of alternative non-invasive specimens, stool, and urine, for TB detection in PLHIV using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in TB screening and treatment centres in Conakry, Guinea, including 300 PLHIV presumptive pulmonary TB. Sputum, stool, and urine specimens were collected and tested using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay. Diagnostic performance was assessed in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and positive and negative predictive values, with their 95% confidence intervals, using the sputum Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra results on as the reference standard. Agreement between specimen results was assessed using Cohen’s kappa.ResultsOf the 300 PLHIV included in the study, 52% (156/300) were male. Median age was 37 years (IQR: 30–45), with more than two-thirds under 45 years of age. Most patients were newly diagnosed with HIV in 2024 (93.0%; 279/300), and 80.0% (240/300) were not yet on antiretroviral therapy. TB positivity was 22.3% (67/300) in sputum, 24.7% (74/300) in stool, and 20% (60/300) in urine. The Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay demonstrated a sensitivity of 86.5% and a specificity of 93.1% in stool specimens, compared to 56.7% and 90.5% in urine specimens. Sensitivity was highest in patients with CD4 < 200 cells/mm3, 89.4% (42/47) for stool and 61.7% (29/47) for urine, while specificity remained consistently high across all CD4 categories, reaching 100% in patients with CD4 counts ≥ 500 cells/mm3. Substantial agreement was observed between stool and sputum results (kappa = 0.77; p < 0.001).ConclusionsXpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay demonstrated high diagnostic performance on stool specimens in PLHIV, with sensitivity surpassing that of urine specimens. Stool testing could serve as a complementary diagnostic approach, particularly for patients unable to produce sputum, thereby improving TB detection in PLHIV. Integrating stool testing into national TB diagnostic algorithms could enhance case detection and management in PLHIV, although implementation must be assessed for technical feasibility and cost-effectiveness.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11887-2.Keywords: People living with human immunodeficiency virus, Sputum, Stool, Tuberculosis, Urine

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains one of the leading causes of severe illness and death among people living with the human immunodeficiency virus (PLHIV) worldwide [1, 2]. According to the latest World Health Organization (WHO) Global TB Report, an estimated 662,000 people had HIV-associated TB in 2023, with 161,000 deaths attributed to the disease [2]. This accounts for one-quarter of all AIDS-related deaths globally, underscoring the severity of this co-infection and the need for integrated strategies for its prevention and management [2].

Among recent diagnostic advancements, the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay, recommended by the WHO since 2010, represents a significant innovation. This automated molecular test enables the rapid detection of TB and rifampicin resistance directly from sputum specimens [3–5]. However, its effectiveness is limited in certain settings, particularly among severely ill HIV-positive patients who often struggle to produce high-quality sputum specimens [6–11]. As a result, these patients frequently die before diagnosis or shortly after initiating treatment due to delays in TB detection [12–14]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of TB data from autopsy studies in adults revealed that 40% of TB cases in HIV-infected adult patients were diagnosed post-mortem [13].

In response to these challenges, the WHO recommends expanding the use of alternative clinical specimens, such as stool, to improve TB diagnosis in patients unable to produce sputum [15, 16]. Stool and urine have emerged as promising matrices for molecular testing [17–19]. Since 2020, WHO guidelines recommended the use of the Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays on stool specimens for the initial diagnosis of pulmonary TB in children [20].

Numerous studies have evaluated the diagnostic performance of the Xpert MTB/Rif assay on stool specimens in children, reporting variable sensitivity and specificity [21–24]. A meta-analysis of studies in children found that the assay offers a sensitivity of 67% (95% CI: 52–79%) and a specificity of 99% (95% CI: 98–99%) compared to the microbiological reference standard of conventional specimens tested by mycobacterial culture or Xpert MTB/Rif assay [25].

However, few studies have evaluated the diagnostic performance of Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays on stool samples in adults, particularly among PLHIV. A prospective multicentre study conducted among adults living with HIV reported a sensitivity of 63.2% (95% CI: 46.0–78.2) and a specificity of 94.1% (95% CI: 91.9–95.9) for the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool samples, compared to sputum Ultra as the reference assay [26]. Furthermore, another study reported a sensitivity of 63.3% (95% CI: 52.9–72.8) and a specificity of 90.9% (95% CI: 58.7–99.8) for the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool samples among PLHIV, using a composite microbiological reference standard as the reference [27]. Moreover, a study conducted in a high HIV prevalence setting showed that the sensitivity and specificity of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool samples were 90% (95% CI: 79–98) and 91% (95% CI: 76–98), respectively, using sputum culture as the reference standard [28]. Finally, a recent systematic review reported a pooled sensitivity of 83.6% and specificity of 87.2% for the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool samples for the diagnosis of pulmonary TB in adults, despite heterogeneity in the reference standards used [29].

Guinea is a high TB incidence country, with an estimated 175 cases per 100,000 inhabitants in 2023, including 30 cases per 100,000 inhabitants of TB/HIV co-infection. The TB-related mortality rate among PLHIV was estimated at 7 deaths per 100,000 inhabitants in the same year [2]. To strengthen TB management, national guidelines recommend the use of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay as a first-line diagnostic tool in all TB screening and treatment centres, including for PLHIV suspected of having TB [30].

However, given the challenges in obtaining high-quality sputum specimens from PLHIV, the risk of false negatives remains a major concern. This highlights the importance of exploring alternative specimens to improve TB detection, particularly in PLHIV suspected of having TB. By doing so, we could ensure timely and appropriate treatment, thereby significantly reducing TB/HIV co-infection-related mortality.

This study aimed to analyse the utility of alternative non-invasive specimens such as stool and urine for the diagnosis of TB in PLHIV using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay.

Methods

Study design and population

This multicentre cross-sectional study was conducted in national hospitals and TB screening and treatment centres (CDTs) in Conakry, the capital of Guinea. Study sites included the pneumo-phtisiology department of the National Hospital Ignace Deen (HNID), the Department of Infectious and Tropical Diseases of the National Hospital Donka (SMIT-HND), as well as the Carriere, Coleah, Sonfonia and Tombolia CDTs (Fig. 1).

Map of Conakry showing tuberculosis screening and treatment centres

The study included PLHIV with suspected TB co-infection, identified based on symptoms such as a persistent cough lasting more than two weeks, fever, night sweats, and/or weight loss. Eligible participants were at least 15 years old and had consented to provide sputum, stool, and urine samples.

Sample size and sampling

The minimum required sample size was determined using the Schwartz formula [31], based on the estimated prevalence of HIV/TB co-infection in Guinea at 19% in 2022 [32], with a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error and a 20% non-response rate. This calculation yielded a minimum required sample size of 300 PLHIV. Patients were consecutively enrolled at the CDTs between May 15 and October 20, 2024.

Data and specimen collection

Data were collected using a structured questionnaire (Supplementary file 1) integrated into a KoboCollect form, accessible via smartphones or tablets. All patients were initially examined by a physician, and information on socio-demographic characteristics (sex and age), medical history (year of HIV diagnosis, history of TB, and antiretroviral treatment status), as well as clinical symptoms (fever, cough, night sweats, weight loss, chest pain, dyspnoea, physical asthenia, haemoptysis, abdominal pain or diarrhoea, etc.). The presence of opportunistic infections was also documented, with specification of the type when available. CD4 cell count was measured for each patient and categorized into three groups: < 200, 200–499, and ≥ 500 cells/mm3.

A sputum, stool, and urine specimen were collected from each patient for TB diagnosis. Early morning specimens were prioritized to optimize diagnostic yield. Sputum specimens were collected in sterile 50 mL containers, stool specimens in sterile containers (at least 2.0 g of fresh stool), and urine specimens in sterile 50 mL containers. The specimens were collected simultaneously at enrolment from patients who were able to expectorate and were not constipated. For patients with difficulty expectorating or those who were constipated, the specimens were collected the following day.

Specimens processing and analysis

The macroscopic appearance of sputum, stool, and urine specimens was visually assessed according to standardized criteria. Sputum samples were classified into five categories: mucoid, purulent, salivary, mucopurulent, and blood-stained. Stool consistency was determined using the Bristol Stool Scale [33] and further categorized as solid, liquid, or semi-solid. The macroscopic appearance of urine specimens was categorized into three groups: normal, dark, and turbid. The specimens were processed and analysed at the Molecular Biology Unit of the National Reference Laboratory for Mycobacteria (LNRM) in Guinea.

Sputum specimens

Sputum specimens were processed using a decontaminating reagent composed of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and isopropanol to liquefy and decontaminate the sample. An equivalent volume of reagent was added to each sample, followed by vigorous shaking for 10 to 20 s. The mixture was then incubated at room temperature for 15 min with periodic agitation to ensure optimal homogeneity. After preparation, 2 mL of the liquefied sample were transferred into an Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra cartridge for testing.

Stool specimens

Stool specimens were processed using the Sample by One Step method [34]. Approximately 0.8 g of stool were mixed directly with a decontaminating reagent composed of sodium hydroxide and isopropanol in a sterile vial. The mixture was vigorously homogenized twice and incubated at room temperature for 10 min. After incubation, 2 mL of the supernatant, free of debris, were aspirated and transferred into an Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra cartridge for analysis.

Urine specimens

Urine specimens were processed under a biosafety level 2 cabinet. They (approximately 40 mL) were transferred into sterile 50 mL conical tubes and centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and a volume of 0.5 mL of the pellet was mixed with 1.5 mL of a decontaminating reagent provided with the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra cartridge. The mixture was vigorously shaken and incubated at room temperature for 15 min with periodic agitation. Once decontamination was complete, a portion (2 mL) of the liquefied mixture was transferred into an Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra cartridge for molecular analysis.

Specimens analysis

The Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay (Cepheid, Inc., Sunnyvale, CA, USA) was used to analyse these specimens. The automated in vitro diagnostic tool is based on nested real-time PCR and is designed for the qualitative and semi-quantitative detection of the Mycobacterium tuberculosis (MTB) complex and rifampicin (RIF) resistance. A detailed description of the assay principles and procedures is available in the WHO Xpert MTB/Rif Implementation Manual [35].

The assay results are interpreted as follows: (1) MTB is undetected when only one probe or no probe is positive; (2) MTB is detected when two or more probes show Ct values within the valid range and the minimum delta Ct is less than 2.0. MTB detection is further classified as RIF resistance detected, RIF resistance not detected, or RIF resistance indeterminate [35]. All specimens with initial invalid or error results were retested and a final reading was made.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio software (version 4.4.1, The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). To summarize the collected information, a descriptive analysis of the data was conducted. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were summarized using the median and interquartile range (IQR). The frequency of TB positivity in sputum, stool, and urine specimens was also calculated. The diagnostic performance was assessed in terms of sensitivity, specificity, as well as, positive and negative predictive values (PPV and NPV), along with their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI), using the results of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on sputum as the reference standard. To assess agreement between results, a kappa (k) concordance analysis was performed [36]. The strength of agreement was interpreted as follows: a kappa value ≤ 0 indicates no agreement, between 0.01 and 0.20 poor agreement, between 0.21 and 0.40 fair agreement, between 0.41 and 0.60 moderate agreement, between 0.61 and 0.80 substantial agreement, and between 0.81 and 1.00 almost perfect agreement [37, 38].

Results

A total of 300 PLHIV were included in the study, with a slight male predominance (156/300; 52%). The median age of participants was 37 years (IQR: 30–45), with more than two-thirds aged under 45 years. Most patients were newly diagnosed in 2024 (279/300; 93.0%) and were not receiving antiretroviral treatment (240/300; 80.0%). A large majority of patients (272/300; 90.7%) had no prior history of TB. The most common symptoms among patients were night sweats (298/300; 99.3%), followed by fever (295/300; 98.3%), physical asthenia (286/300; 95.3%), cough (252/300; 84.0%), weight loss (208/300; 69.3%), and chest pain (199/300; 66.3%). In addition, 35% (105/300) of patients presented with opportunistic infections. Finally, more than half of the patients (155/300; 51.7%) had a CD4 count of less than 200 cells/mm3 and only 4.3% (13/400) had a CD4 count greater than 500 cells/mm3 (Table 1).

This study indicate that the positivity of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay decreases with increasing CD4 cell count. Among severely immunocompromised patients (CD4 < 200 cells/mm3), TB positivity remained the highest across all specimen types, reaching 33.5% (52/155) for stool, 30.3% (47/155) for sputum, and 29.0% (45/155) for urine. This positivity decreased among patients with CD4 counts between 200 and 499 cells/mm3, with 15.2% (20/132) for stool, 12.9% (17/132) for sputum, and 10.6% (14/132) for urine. In patients with CD4 counts ≥ 500 cells/mm3, positivity remained higher in sputum specimens (23.1%; 3/13) and stool specimens (15.4%; 2/13) (Fig. 3).

Among patients with positive sputum results, 7.5% (5/67) showed rifampicin resistance. Similarly, 5.4% (4/74) of patients with positive stool assay results were resistant to rifampicin. In contrast, no rifampicin resistance was observed among patients with positive urine assay results (Fig. 2).

About 36% (35/96) of patients had positive results simultaneously for sputum, stool, and urine specimens. In contrast, 6.2% (6/96) tested positive only in sputum, 7.3% (7/96) only in stool, and 13.5% (13/96) only in urine (Fig. 4).

In contrast, the assay has a lower sensitivity in urine specimens (38/67; 56.7%), although it has good specificity (211/233; 90.5%) and a high NPV (211/240; 87.9%) (Table 2). The positive results from sputum and stool specimens were concordant in 86.6% (58/67) of patients, while the negative results were concordant in 93.1% (217/233). Cohen's kappa coefficient analysis revealed substantial agreement between the results of the two specimens (kappa = 0.77; 95% CI: 0.68—0.85; p < 0.001). In contrast, the positive results from sputum and urine specimens were concordant in 56.7% (38/67) of patients, while the negative results were concordant in 90.6% (211/233). The Cohen's kappa coefficient showed a moderate level of agreement between these two types of specimens (kappa = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.37—0.61; p < 0.001). Furthermore, this study found that 7% (16/233) of patients who were not diagnosed through sputum expectorations tested positive in stool specimens, while 9% (22/233) of patients who were not diagnosed via sputum expectorations tested positive in urine specimens (Table 3).

Discussion

Early and accurate diagnosis of TB in PLHIV is a critical pillar of the WHO strategy to eliminate TB. This is all the more important if effective diagnostic strategies are rapidly implemented to detect TB in PLHIV [39, 40].

The main findings of this study indicate that TB positivity was more frequently detected in stool specimens than in sputum or urine specimens. Moreover, the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance on stool specimens compared to urine. In addition, a high concordance was observed between results obtained from stool and those from sputum specimens.

Our results show a higher TB positivity in stool specimens compared to sputum specimens. This observation may be explained by a higher concentration of mycobacterial DNA in stool, resulting from the intestinal clearance of bacilli inhaled from the respiratory tract. Therefore, the analysis of stool specimens may play a significant role in the diagnosis of pulmonary TB in PLHIV, particularly in the absence of productive expectoration. This finding is consistent with recent reports in the literature. In a prospective multicentre study conducted among adults living with HIV, the TB positivity of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay was 9% in stool compared to 6% in sputum [26]. Similarly, another study reported a stool Ultra positivity rate of approximately 20% among PLHIV [41]. In addition, studies involving adults with presumptive TB have also reported high TB positivity from stool samples, reaching 60.4% and 58%, respectively [42, 43].

The findings of this study demonstrate good diagnostic performance of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay in stool specimens from PLHIV, with sensitivity exceeding 85% and specificity greater than 90%. These performances strengthen the relevance of stool as a reliable diagnostic alternative, particularly for patients unable to produce sputum. These observations are consistent with recent studies evaluating the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay for TB diagnosis in stool samples from adults living with HIV, which reported a sensitivity of approximately 63% and a specificity exceeding 90%, using sputum Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and a composite microbiological reference standard, respectively, as reference [26, 27]. These findings confirm the diagnostic performance of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool specimens in diverse settings and suggest that stool may serve as a valuable complementary specimen, potentially enhancing TB detection among PLHIV. It is important to note that variations in performance observed across studies may be attributable to several factors, including differences in specimens’ collection and processing protocols, the stage of disease and bacillary load in the study populations, as well as the reference standard used.

However, although the assay shows lower sensitivity for TB detection in urine specimens, it demonstrates good specificity with a satisfactory NPV. Similar results have been reported in a meta-analysis evaluating the performance of the Xpert MTB/Rif assay across different sample types, showing a relatively low sensitivity of 69% but an excellent specificity of 100% for urine specimens [44]. Furthermore, a study using the Xpert MTB/Rif assay for TB diagnosis in PLHIV reported a positivity of 64% in urine specimens [45]. In addition, the findings of a randomized trial suggest that urine-based TB screening could reduce the risk of undiagnosed TB among hospitalized PLHIV [17].

The findings showed a higher sensitivity of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on alternative specimens in patients with low CD4 counts (< 200 cells/mm3). Advanced immunosuppression is associated with higher bacillary loads and extrapulmonary dissemination, thereby increasing the likelihood of detecting MTB bacilli in non-respiratory specimens. Similar findings were reported in a prospective multicentre study, in which the sensitivity of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra on stool was higher among patients with CD4 counts ≤ 200 cells/µL, reaching 75.0% (95% CI: 48.8–94.5) using sputum Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra as the reference standard [26]. Another recent study showed that the sensitivity of Xpert Ultra on urine was also higher in patients with CD4 counts ≤ 200 cells/µL compared to those with CD4 counts > 200 cells/µL (52.3% [95% CI: 43.8–60.8] vs. 11.6% [95% CI: 7.0–18.5]) [46].

The excellent concordance between the results of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool and sputum specimens strengthens the importance of stool as a promising and reliable non-invasive alternative for diagnosing TB in PLHIV. A study conducted in southern Ethiopia among children with presumptive TB reported a high level of agreement between stool and sputum specimens using the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay (kappa coefficient = 0.83; p < 0.001) [47]. Similarly, in China, another study demonstrated good concordance between stool and sputum specimens using the same diagnostic assay [48]. Additional studies from Indonesia and Pakistan also reported good agreement between Xpert MTB/Rif results on stool and respiratory specimens [21, 49], although the stool processing methods varied, including differences in the amount of stool used.

This study suggests that stool specimens represent an acceptable and practical alternative for the diagnosis of pulmonary TB, although sputum remains the reference specimen. Their use could improve diagnostic coverage and enable earlier treatment initiation among PLHIV, particularly when sputum production is difficult or not feasible. In a similar context, stool Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra testing was reported to yield an overall diagnostic yield of 9% (60/677), exceeding that of sputum Ultra (6%, 39/677) and sputum culture (4%, 30/677) [26]. Furthermore, another study demonstrated that combining sputum and stool Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra testing could increase the proportion of microbiologically confirmed TB cases by 4.1% (95% CI: 1.6–6.6) [41], further underscoring the added value of stool specimens in TB diagnosis.

These findings are significant for the National TB Control Program in Guinea, particularly in a context where TB is associated with an increased risk of mortality among PLHIV. Consequently, they may support the integration of stool specimens into national diagnostic protocols, particularly in settings where sputum production is challenging, particularly in PLHIV with advanced immunosuppression. Despite the promising results, several challenges remain in the widespread use of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay in low- and middle-income countries [50]. The high costs of cartridges, limited device capacity, and short equipment lifespan represent major barriers to implementation [51–55]. Therefore, future studies in larger cohorts of PLHIV are needed to assess the performance of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay on stool and urine samples using mycobacterial culture as the reference standard, while also evaluating the clinical impact and cost-effectiveness of their integration into diagnostic algorithms.

This study has several limitations. First, the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay can generate false-positive results, because it cannot differentiate between live and dead mycobacteria, and MTB DNA persist even after bacterial death [23, 56]. Although this is unlikely, given that most patients in the study were newly diagnosed with HIV and had no history of TB, it cannot be entirely excluded and should be explored further in future studies. Second, the lack of radiological data limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the diagnostic performance of the Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assay in stool specimens. Further studies that incorporate clinical and radiological data are necessary to better assess Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra’s diagnostic reliability in PLHIV. Third, the use of sputum Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra as the reference standard represents also a limitation of this study, as this approach may fail to detect certain cases of TB, particularly in patients with paucibacillary disease. However, although mycobacterial culture is considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of pulmonary TB, its implementation in resource-limited settings requires substantial resources and involves a delay of up to eight weeks before results are available. This can lead to delays in diagnosis and initiation of anti-TB treatment, thereby contributing to worsening of the clinical condition, increased hospitalizations, and healthcare costs, as well as a higher risk of death. Finally, the study’s focus on a geographically restricted area limits the generalization of the results to the entire country, suggesting the need for a national-scale evaluation to examine Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra performance in various specimens, particularly in cases of paucibacillary TB.

Conclusion

This study highlights the importance of alternative specimens, particularly stool, for TB diagnosis in PLHIV. Stool specimens demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity, compared to sputum, while urine specimens showed high specificity despite lower sensitivity. These findings suggest that stool specimens may be a complementary specimen to improve TB diagnosis in PLHIV, particularly those with advanced, while urine may be of limited utility. Thus, integrating stool testing into national TB diagnostic algorithms could enhance case detection and management in PLHIV. Nevertheless, its implementation must be assessed in terms of technical feasibility and cost-effectiveness in countries with limited resources.
