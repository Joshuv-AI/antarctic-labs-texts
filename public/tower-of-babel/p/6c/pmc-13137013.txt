Renal Outcomes With Potassium-Competitive Acid Blockers Versus Proton Pump Inhibitors in CKD
Authors: Shin Yeong Kim, Hankil Lee, Jong Seung Kim, Jae Hyun Kim, Ha-Lim Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionPotassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs) have emerged as a promising alternative to proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) because of their rapid and sustained acid suppression. However, their renal safety in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) has not been thoroughly evaluated. Given concerns about PPI-associated renal risks, we conducted a target trial–emulated cohort study to compare renal safety of P-CABs versus PPIs.MethodsUsing Korean National Health Insurance data, we identified patients with CKD newly initiating P-CABs or PPIs for gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) between 2019 and 2022. Patients with previous renal replacement therapy (RRT) were excluded. The primary outcome was the first initiation of RRT. Propensity score (PS) matching (1:4) was applied, and hazard ratios (HRs) were estimated using Cox proportional hazards models. An intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis was also performed.ResultsA total of 34,077 patients were included, and 4107 P-CAB and 16,428 PPI users remained after matching. Over 60% in both groups were aged ≥ 65 years, and males were slightly more prevalent. P-CAB use was significantly associated with a lower risk of composite RRT initiation than PPI use (HR: 0.63, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.45–0.88). Although HRs for individual RRT events favored P-CABs, statistical significance was not reached. The ITT analysis showed consistent results (HR: 0.90, 95% CI: 0.82–0.99).ConclusionIn this study, P-CABs were associated with a reduced risk of renal function deterioration compared with PPIs in patients with CKD with GERD. These findings suggest the potential clinical utility of P-CABs as a safer treatment option in this high-risk population.

Patients with CKD may have a higher incidence of gastrointestinal disorders, such as GERD, than non-CKD patients.1,2 This can be attributed to reduced mucosal prostaglandin levels, hyperacidity caused by hypergastrinemia, a higher prevalence of Helicobacter pylori infection in uremic patients, and complications related to CKD.1,2 In routine clinical practice, PPIs are recommended as the standard first-line pharmacologic therapy for GERD.3,4 In addition, patients with CKD often take medications such as nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs or anticoagulants, which elevate the risk of gastrointestinal bleeding or ulcerative diseases, leading to the frequent coprescription of PPIs for preventive purposes.2 Nevertheless, previous studies have reported a potential risk of renal function deterioration associated with PPIs, raising concerns about their safety.5, 6, 7, 8 Studies have shown that patients with CKD who initiated PPIs had a significantly higher risk of progression to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) than those who started H2 receptor antagonists.9 A positive association between PPIs and the risk of acute interstitial nephritis has also been identified.10,11 Notably, labels of rabeprazole and omeprazole were specifically updated in Korea to include this risk as an adverse reaction in 2023.12,13

P-CABs, which reversibly bind to H+,K+-ATPase to suppress acid secretion, have gained increasing recognition as a promising alternative recommended alongside PPIs in clinical practice guidelines for GERD.3,14 Several studies have demonstrated that P-CABs achieve stronger acid suppression than PPIs, as well as rapid activation and prolonged duration of action, suggesting their potential clinical advantages.15, 16, 17 Vonoprazan, tegoprazan, and fexuprazan are classified as P-CABs. Particularly, tegoprazan and fexuprazan, novel P-CABs recently developed in South Korea, have garnered attention in clinical practice because of their excellent acid-suppressive effects and favorable safety profiles.18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 Tegoprazan has received approval in countries such as China, Singapore, and Latin America and is currently undergoing phase 3 clinical trials in the United States.25,26 Similarly, fexuprazan is expanding its global approvals, suggesting that the utilization of P-CABs is anticipated to increase significantly.27 However, despite their clinical advantages and global expansion, evidence on the renal safety of P-CABs remains sparse.

Therefore, we aimed to compare the risk of renal function deterioration between P-CABs and PPIs in patients with CKD undergoing treatment for GERD in the real-world setting. This study employs a target trial emulation framework to minimize bias and improve the validity of observational data.28

Methods

This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Target Trial Emulation Framework

We used a target trial emulation approach to evaluate the association between P-CABs and PPIs with the risk of renal function deterioration in a cohort study. Following the framework proposed by Hernán and Robins, we designed a hypothetical randomized clinical trial (the "target trial") to address the research question, ensuring explicit consideration of key trial elements such as eligibility criteria, treatment strategies and assignment, follow-up, outcomes, causal contrasts, and the statistical analysis plan.29 We then emulated these components using real-world data. Detailed descriptions of the target trial framework and its emulation are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data Source and Study Population

This study was conducted using the Korean National Health Insurance data, covering approximately 97% of the Korean population, from January 2018 to October 2023. The database includes demographics, health care utilization, diagnoses coded using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), medical procedures, and prescription medications. We included patients aged ≥ 20 years who were diagnosed with CKD (ICD-10 code: N18) and prescribed P-CAB or PPI at least once for GERD (K21) from 2019 to 2022. The accuracy of the GERD code has been reported to be 74%,30 and CKD was defined using ICD-10 codes consistent with previous Korean claims-based studies.31,32 The index date was the date of the first prescription of P-CAB or PPI. To ensure the inclusion of patients with established CKD, we included only those with ≥2 CKD diagnoses recorded within the year before the index date. We excluded patients who had been prescribed P-CABs or PPIs in the year before the index date to ensure the inclusion of new users. In addition, individuals with a history of RRT or a cancer diagnosis within the year before the index date were excluded from the analysis.

Drug Exposure

The exposure was defined as the initiation of P-CABs or PPIs during the study period. Patients were assigned to treatment groups based on their first recorded P-CAB or PPI prescription. P-CABs included tegoprazan, fexuprazan, and revaprazan. Vonoprazan was excluded because it was unavailable in South Korea. PPIs included esomeprazole, rabeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole, omeprazole, dexlansoprazole, ilaprazole, and s-pantoprazole. Drug codes are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

To emulate a clinical trial design, we referred to the phase 3 clinical trial of tegoprazan, a representative P-CAB, which employed a per-protocol study design using a per-protocol set for analysis.33 Following this framework, the hypothetical trial was conceptualized using a per-protocol approach, assuming perfect adherence to the assigned treatment strategies. In real-world data analysis, this framework was implemented using the as-treated approach (analogous to per-protocol design of trials), where patients were analyzed based on received treatment during the follow-up period. Patients were followed-up from the index date until the earliest occurrence of the outcome, treatment discontinuation, switching, death, or the end of the study. Treatment discontinuation was defined as no subsequent prescription for the same drug class within the 30-day grace period following the end of the previous prescription, chosen to reflect routine prescribing patterns observed in the claims data.

As a secondary analysis, we applied an ITT approach to evaluate the ITT effect, which reflects the treatment strategy regardless of adherence or switching. ITT analysis, commonly used in randomized controlled trials, evaluates outcomes based on the initial treatment assignment to provide insights into the effectiveness of a treatment strategy because it would be applied in real-world settings. This allowed us to account for potential biases due to treatment switching or discontinuation and to offer a complementary perspective to the as-treated analysis.

Outcome Definition

The primary outcome was the first occurrence of composite RRT. Secondary outcomes were the occurrences of each hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation. For each secondary outcome, other component events were not treated as competing or censoring events in the cause-specific Cox models. Outcomes were identified using special exemption codes and procedure codes, following the operational definition used in previous Korean cohort studies employing the same national database.34 A special exemption code from the Korean National Health Insurance system is granted to patients who require ESKD-related procedures upon physician confirmation and registration. Detailed codes used for identification are listed in Supplementary Table S3. Finally, we repeated the analysis with a negative control outcome, incident dry eye syndrome, to assess the possibility of unmeasured confounding that might lead to misleading causal inferences.35 Dry eye syndrome was selected based on previous evidence, including the DREAM randomized clinical trial, which had examined PPI use in relation to dry eye but does not suggest strong biological or pharmacological relevance to the study drugs.36 Dry eye syndrome was identified using the diagnosis code H04.11, and its occurrence after the index date was used to define the negative control outcome.

Covariate Assessment

Baseline covariates were assessed during the year before the index date using Korean National Health Insurance data. These included demographics (age, sex [defined as a biological variable as recorded in the claims database], and health insurance type) and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and cardiovascular disease) identified using diagnostic codes. The Charlson Comorbidity Index was calculated to summarize comorbidity burden. Baseline medication use was evaluated, including angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), diuretics, aspirin, nonsteroidal antiinflammatory drugs, statins, anticoagulants, and sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors. The presence of ESKD (defined by ICD-10 code: N185) among patients with CKD was evaluated. Health care utilization metrics, such as the number of CKD-related visits, were assessed, with CKD-related visits defined as any outpatient or inpatient encounter that included a diagnosis code for CKD (ICD-10 code: N18) during the 12 months before the index date. In addition, the treatment initiation year (index year), from 2019 to 2022, was included as a covariate to account for time trends. Detailed codes for comorbidities and baseline medication use are in Supplementary Tables S4 and S5.

Statistical Analysis

For the primary analysis, we used PS matching to balance baseline characteristics between treatment groups and to estimate the average treatment effect among patients who initiated P-CAB therapy. PS were estimated using multivariable logistic regression, including all predefined baseline covariates, such as demographic characteristics, the index year, comorbidities, baseline medication use, the presence of ESKD and health care utilization factors assessed during the 12 months before the index date. A 1:4 matching ratio was applied using a sequential digit-based nearest-neighbor matching approach without replacement. After matching, standardized mean differences were used to assess covariate balance, with a standardized mean difference < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Cox proportional hazards models were employed to estimate HRs and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests, and no evidence of violation was observed. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide 7.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Subgroup Analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to investigate the association between P-CAB or PPI use and the occurrence of RRT in specific patient populations, reflecting clinically relevant factors that may influence the relationship. The predefined subgroups included age (20–44, 45–64, ≥ 65 years), sex, history of ESKD, history of diabetes, previous use of ACE inhibitors/ARBs, drug exposure duration (1–14, 15–28, ≥ 29 days), cumulative drug dose (≤ 30, 30–120, > 120 defined daily dose [DDD]), and average daily drug dose (≤ 1, > 1 DDD). The cutoffs for drug exposure duration and cumulative drug dose were determined based on the observed distribution of exposure in the study population to ensure adequate representation across categories. Drug exposure duration was calculated as the total number of days supplied for the index drug before censoring. Average daily dose was calculated by dividing the cumulative DDD by the cumulative prescription duration. Information on the DDD for each medication is provided in Supplementary Table S6. For the ESKD subgroup, the analysis was limited to patients identified by ICD-10 codes N181–N185 to ensure a more precise evaluation of this population.

Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the results. Alternative grace periods of 15 and 45 days were applied to evaluate the impact of varying definitions of treatment discontinuation. Moreover, patients with CKD were identified based solely on the primary diagnosis code to enhance validity. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was applied to create a covariate-balanced pseudo-population and to estimate the average treatment effect in the overall population. In addition, PS was included as a separate covariate in the Cox model.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jeonbuk National University (Approval Number: [JBNU202404019-UE002]). Given the use of anonymized claims data, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Results

Baseline Characteristics of Study Population

A total of 4107 P-CAB users and 29,970 PPI users were included (Figure 1). In both groups, > 60% were aged ≥ 65 years, with a slightly higher proportion of males (Table 1). The mean Charlson Comorbidity Index scores were 1.98 and 1.96 in the P-CAB and PPI groups, respectively (standardized mean difference = 0.012). Hypertension and hyperlipidemia were prevalent in > 80% of patients, and > 50% had diabetes mellitus. ACE inhibitors or ARBs were the most used concomitant medications. In addition, 2.6% of P-CAB users and 2.8% of PPI users had ESKD. In the year before the index date, both groups had approximately 4.9 CKD-related hospital visits on average. After PS matching, 4107 patients in the P-CAB group and 16,428 patients in the PPI group were included, and the groups were well-balanced on all observed variables. Risk and Incidence of RRT Initiation Following Renal Function Deterioration

The incidence rate of composite RRT was 10.9/100 person-yrs (95% CI: 8.1–14.6) in P-CAB and 11.2/100 person-yrs (95% CI: 10.2–12.3) in PPIs (Table 2). The Kaplan–Meier curves comparing the 2 groups are presented in Supplementary Figure S2, and the log-rank test indicated no statistically significant difference between the survival curves. After PS matching and covariate adjustment, the HR for the risk of composite RRT initiation was 0.63 (95% CI: 0.45–0.88), indicating a significantly lower risk with P-CABs than with PPIs (P = 0.0070). For hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation, the HRs for each RRT consistently favored P-CAB but were not statistically significant (HR: 0.73; 95% CI: 0.52–1.03 for hemodialysis, HR: 0.70; 95% CI: 0.25–1.99 for peritoneal dialysis, HR: 0.44; 95% CI: 0.12–1.66 for kidney transplantation). For the negative control outcome, dry eye syndrome, no significant difference was identified between P-CAB and PPI (adjusted HR: 1.01, 95% CI: 0.74–1.37, P = 0.964). Subgroup and Sensitivity Analysis

Subgroup analyses revealed that the lower risk of RRT associated with P-CAB was more pronounced in specific groups (Figure 2). Among individuals aged ≥ 65 years, P-CAB was significantly associated with a lower risk compared with PPI (adjusted HR: 0.59, 95% CI: 0.36–0.95). Similarly, participants with both a history of diabetes (adjusted HR: 0.62, 95% CI: 0.41–0.94) and previous ACE-I or ARB use (adjusted HR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.39–0.91), showed a lower risk of renal function deterioration with P-CAB use. In addition, in the exposure-based groups, participants with a medication duration ≥ 29 days (adjusted HR: 0.61, 95% CI: 0.41–0.91) and those with an average daily dose of ≤1 DDD (adjusted HR: 0.43, 95% CI: 0.27–0.69) exhibited lower risks of renal function deterioration with P-CAB. Discussion

This study is a target trial emulation in adults aged ≥ 20 years with CKD and no previous history of RRT, who initiated PPIs or P-CABs for the treatment of GERD, to evaluate the association between these treatments and renal function deterioration. P-CAB was associated with a 37% lower risk of composite RRT initiation compared with PPI. Hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and kidney transplantation each showed similar trends but were not statistically significant. The ITT analysis showed a similar trend with the primary findings, though the estimated HR was slightly attenuated. The results of the sensitivity analyses were largely aligned with those of the primary analysis.

The biological mechanisms underlying the association between PPIs and an increased risk of renal function deterioration remain unclear.37 Yepuri et al.38 demonstrated that chronic PPI exposure accelerates endothelial cell senescence through impaired lysosomal acidification, contributing to oxidative stress and vascular aging, which may underlie the increased risk of kidney injury. In contrast, the rapid and potent acid suppression of P-CABs39—which might reduce the need for high-dose or prolonged use—could contribute to a safer profile regarding kidney-related adverse effects compared to PPIs. Notably, the mean follow-up in this study was slightly shorter for P-CAB users (1.18 months, SD = 3.06) than for PPI users (1.48 months, SD = 4.16), although the difference was not statistically significant. A previously published observational study in South Korea aligns closely with our findings. It reported that patients prescribed P-CAB (tegoprazan) had significantly lower risks of creatinine doubling and estimated glomerular filtration rate decline compared with those on PPI (esomeprazole), suggesting that P-CAB may be a safer option for long-term acid suppression.40

Acute interstitial nephritis has been proposed as a potential mechanism underlying the development or progression of CKD associated with PPI use.41 A postmarketing surveillance study using Japan’s adverse event reporting database identified tubulointerstitial nephritis as a significant adverse event in both PPI and P-CAB users.42 In that study, the reporting odds ratio for tubulointerstitial nephritis was lower in P-CAB compared to PPIs. Furthermore, although no signal for acute kidney injury was detected with either PPIs or vonoprazan, the reporting odds ratio for acute kidney injury was comparatively lower for vonoprazan. Considering these findings in the context of spontaneous reporting data, they provide preliminary insights into potential differences in renal safety profiles between P-CABs and PPIs. Further studies are needed to validate this possibility and explore the biological mechanisms underlying the differential renal outcomes between P-CABs and PPIs.

In the subgroup analysis, P-CAB was associated with a lower risk of renal function deterioration compared with PPI in older adults aged ≥ 65 years, individuals with a history of diabetes or previous ACE-I or ARB use, as well as those with longer medication durations or lower average daily doses. Regarding the more favorable association for P-CAB observed in the lower average daily dose group, the >1 DDD subgroup may reflect clinical situations with a higher baseline risk. In such settings, greater clinical heterogeneity and residual confounding may result in an apparently weaker association in the >1 DDD subgroup.

The stronger association observed in older adults may be biologically plausible because pharmacologic differences between drug classes and age-related vulnerability to drug-related kidney injury. PPIs have been linked to renal injury through tubulointerstitial mechanisms including acute interstitial nephritis and through systemic pathways such as an accelerated endothelial senescence.38,41 A previous observational study suggests greater kidney risk in older adults. A population-based cohort study found that PPI use in older adults was associated with a significantly increased risk of acute kidney injury.43 In older adults with reduced renal reserve, these mechanisms may have larger downstream consequences, potentially explaining a more pronounced relative renal safety signal for P-CABs.

Another study reported a higher risk of CKD associated with PPI in diabetic patients.44 Lazarus et al.5 demonstrated that the concurrent use of PPIs and ACEI or ARBs significantly increase the risk of CKD, with twice-daily PPI administration associated with a higher risk than once-daily dosing. Furthermore, prolonged or high-dose PPI use for >3 months significantly increased CKD risk.45 Although there are no head-to-head comparisons, the significant renal function deterioration effects of PPIs may have outweighed those of P-CABs, increasing the risk in this subgroup. Moreover, given the higher prevalence of diabetes and CKD among older patients and the recommended use of ACE-I/ARB in patients with both conditions, these patient characteristics frequently overlap in clinical practice. Considering renal safety, P-CABs may represent a suitable option for gastric acid suppression in this population.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, to our knowledge, our study was the first large-scale cohort study to compare the risk of initiating RRT between P-CAB and PPI in patients with CKD. Second, we employed a target trial emulation approach. In the phase 3 clinical trials of P-CABs, patients with clinically significant renal disease were not included.33,46,47 Given the small proportion of patients with CKD among patients with GERD, conducting a randomized clinical trial to compare the renal safety outcomes of P-CABs and PPIs is not feasible. Considering the challenges in prospectively evaluating renal safety in patients with CKD, our study emulated the target trial and demonstrated that P-CABs may serve as safe alternative to PPIs in patients with CKD. This method allowed us to approximate a randomized trial within the constraints of observational data, leveraging the strengths of the retrospective database to perform causal inference while minimizing potential biases.48 Lastly, various sensitivity analyses with different grace periods, patient selection criteria, follow-up approaches, and application of PS scores were conducted to confirm the robustness and consistency of our findings. Furthermore, the null results from a negative control outcome analysis indicated that such bias did not significantly affect our study findings.

Even so, we acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, this study did not include a variety of P-CAB active ingredients. During the study period, most prescribed P-CABs were tegoprazan, and extrapolation of these findings to other P-CAB active ingredients requires careful consideration. Second, the relatively short follow-up period in the as-treated analysis may not have been sufficient to fully capture long-term renal outcomes. In addition, the shorter follow-up observed in the P-CAB group raises the possibility of informative censoring, whereby earlier treatment discontinuation may have led to underascertainment of RRT events in this group. However, acid-suppressive therapies for GERD are typically prescribed for a standard treatment duration of approximately 4 to 8 weeks in clinical practice. In this context, prolonged follow-up under an ITT framework may dilute exposure–outcome associations and introduce additional sources of bias unrelated to the pharmacologic effects of the study drugs. Consistent with this consideration, the ITT sensitivity analysis showed similar results with attenuation of the effect magnitude while remaining statistically significant, which may reflect such dilution. Notably, among patients who experienced RRT, the mean follow-up duration was 4.06 months in the P-CAB group and 3.66 months in the PPI group, suggesting that clinically meaningful renal events occurred within a biologically plausible timeframe despite the overall short average follow-up in the as-treated analysis. Nevertheless, because treatment discontinuation or switching may be related to underlying patient characteristics or clinical status, the possibility of informative censoring inherent to the as-treated framework cannot be completely excluded. Third, because of the nature of claims data, we were unable to include laboratory measurements such as serum creatinine levels or estimated glomerular filtration rate in the analysis. Nonetheless, we identified patients with ESKD at baseline using diagnostic codes and incorporated the frequency of CKD-related hospital visits as a matching variable to indirectly account for the severity of CKD between the 2 treatment groups. However, unmeasured differences in baseline CKD stage between treatment groups may have contributed to residual confounding and influenced the study outcomes. Fourth, because the claims data do not provide information on medication adherence, we were unable to consider variations in adherence, which might have influenced the observed outcomes. Fifth, because this study was conducted exclusively on a South Korean population, the findings may have limited generalizability to other populations due to potential differences in racial and cultural characteristics. Sixth, residual confounding remains a potential concern in observational data analysis. To address this, we applied PS matching, multivariable adjustment, and a negative control analysis, among other approaches, to evaluate the association between P-CAB or PPI use and renal function. Seventh, we did not model glomerulonephritis because of limitations of administrative claims data, in which CKD etiology is not consistently captured. However, treatment selection between P-CABs and PPIs is primarily driven by GERD-related considerations rather than CKD etiology. Because renal adverse reactions such as acute interstitial nephritis were added to PPI labeling in Korea only in 2023 and our study period spanned 2018 to 2023, this regulatory change is unlikely to have materially influenced prescribing patterns or the comparative estimates. Eighth, although the component outcomes of RRT were analyzed separately as secondary outcomes, these events may represent competing first events in the clinical course of kidney failure. Considering that patients may transition between RRT modalities over time in real-world clinical practice, our modality-specific analyses were intended to examine associations with the initiation of each modality rather than to estimate mutually exclusive risks across modalities. Accordingly, the results of the component analyses should be interpreted as exploratory, and future studies using competing-risk or multistate modeling approaches may further clarify modality-specific treatment effects. Lastly, because only baseline covariates were included in the analyses, potential time-varying confounding could not be fully addressed. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the results.

In conclusion, we found that P-CAB initiation for GERD was associated with a lower risk of RRT compared with PPIs in patients with CKD. This association was more pronounced in older individuals, those with diabetes or ACE-I or ARB use, and patients with longer treatment durations or lower daily doses. This study provides key evidence supporting P-CAB as a safer treatment option for GERD in patients with CKD at risk of renal function deterioration. Our findings may assist physicians in making clinical decisions regarding the management of GERD in this population and offer valuable regulatory evidence for healthcare policymakers.
