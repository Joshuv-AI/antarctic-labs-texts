The transient external granular layer in human foetal cerebellum: insights into neurogenesis, genetic regulation, and developmental disorders
Authors: Aamna Kausar, Shalini Sanjeeva Nayak, Sapna Marpalli, Veeresh, Bincy Muthukattu George, Mamatha Hosapatna
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The external granular layer (EGL) is a transient yet crucial layer for the formation of cerebellar cortex. Genetic mechanisms, including the sonic hedgehog (SHH) pathway is key to the proliferation of granule cell progenitors (GCPs), and disruptions in this process can lead to cerebellar disorders like medulloblastoma and Dandy–Walker malformation (DWM). The review consolidates findings from studies on human cerebellar development, primarily focusing on neurogenesis, the role of genetic pathways in EGL regulation, and its involvement in congenital disorders. In total 42 articles were comprehensively reviewed and the research gaps in current research on human foetal cerebellum development was highlighted. The key findings from various sections of the review are summarized as follows. In terms of gestational development, the timeline for the appearance and regression of the EGL in humans remains imprecise. Regarding morphometry, the EGL thickness shows a marked increase between 16 and 28 gestational weeks, reaching an average peak of approximately 50.05 µm around 24th to 28th weeks, and then declines during the late third trimester. In the context of gene expression, SHH signalling plays a critical role in driving the proliferation of GCPs within the EGL. When considering congenital disorders, disruptions in EGL development are associated with conditions such as medulloblastomas, DWM, and pilocytic astrocytoma. The review highlights major research gaps and underscores the need for further human-specific studies to enhance understanding and guide therapies for cerebellar disorders.

Introduction

Numerous sets of discussions are centred around the cerebellum even now due to its unforeseen degree of functional variety. The cerebellum has historically been recognized for its function in fine voluntary movements, motor coordination, posture balance and many more [1-3]. Nevertheless, the cerebellum has garnered increasing interest by providing abundant evidence in its non-motor processes involvement like cognition and emotional processing [4, 5].

As it’s known that during histogenesis (microarchitecture of cells), the developing human foetal cerebellum (HFC) comprises of 4 layers namely; external granular layer (EGL), molecular layer (ML), Purkinje cell layer (PCL), and internal granular layer (IGL) [6-8]. At the foundation of cerebellum development is the EGL, which is a transient yet critical layer that plays an essential role in the formation of the cerebellar cortex [9].

Furthermore, several genetic control mechanisms are accountable for the appropriate development of the cells in this precise layer [10]. There are a few genes like- SHH that are responsible for regulation and proliferation of granule cell progenitors (GCPs) whose molecular mechanisms are still under research investigation [11, 12]. It is also reported and studied in many previously reviewed articles that the GCPs of EGL are genetically called by antiproliferative gene PC3 to indicate in shifting the process from proliferation to differentiation. Thus, any impairment in the expression of such genes responsible, will lead to various brain abnormalities and most commonly medulloblastoma [12, 13] and Joubert syndrome (JS) [14]. This gives us a concise notion that the neurogenesis (histogenesis) and genetical expression are interconnected, whose slight abnormality leads to specific cerebellar disorders.

It also becomes very important to note that the studies carried out on various species like mice, chick embryo, drosophila, zebrafish, etc., will not be fair enough to compare and study a human cerebellar development. This is so because, the mechanisms of different responsible genes vary in human species as compared to other species [15, 16]. Thus, a review confined to human studies alone in this perspective holds its importance though there are limited studies.

This narrative review aims to provide a detailed account of EGL development specifically in human foetuses. By examining studies that span various stages of gestation, we aim to capture the dynamic changes in EGL structure; highlighting its neurogenesis, cellular composition, and gene expression that underlie normal cerebellar maturation. Additionally, we explore the implications of abnormal EGL persistence or dysfunction, particularly in relation to neurodevelopmental disorders.

In doing so, this review seeks to answer critical questions: Why is the EGL transient? What is its functional significance during gestation? What are the consequences of its abnormal persistence postnatally? Finally, we aim to identify key knowledge gaps and outline directions for future human-based research in this underexplored area.

Review

To comprehensively review the transient EGL, all the potential unanswered questions in this area were identified and used to guide the literature search. Based on these questions, a set of relevant keywords was developed, including: “human foetal cerebellum,” “external granular layer,” “granular layer,” “histology,” “histogenesis,” “neurogenesis,” “gene expression,” “congenital abnormality,” “medulloblastoma,” “Joubert syndrome,” “Dandy Walker malformation,” “morphometry,” “morphology,” “thickness,” “number of neurons,” and “gestational week.”

These keywords were used in search engines namely; PubMed, Google Scholar, and ScienceDirect databases using appropriate Boolean operators like ‘AND’ and ‘OR’ to refine the search results.

In the aforementioned search engines, the keywords were used in pairs aligned with specific subtopics addressed in this review. For instance, the combination “external granular layer” AND “thickness” was applied to retrieve literature relevant to the morphometric analysis of EGL thickness. Similar keyword pairings were utilized for other subtopics accordingly.

All retrieved articles were sorted by relevance to the respective subtopics and reviewed in detail. Only English-language publications were considered, regardless of the year of publication. Furthermore, this review exclusively included studies conducted on human subjects to maintain relevance and applicability to human cerebellar development.

A total of 42 articles were ultimately included in this review. The primary aim of this article is to present a focused and comprehensive synthesis of research dedicated to the EGL within the context of the developing human cerebellum.

Neurogenesis of the external granular layer

The cerebellum has been a subject of investigation since as early as the late 19th and early 20th centuries. One of the pioneering figures in this field was Wilhelm His, who was the first to identify a specialized region located on the dorsal aspect of the hindbrain in human embryos. He observed that this region exhibited unique developmental features when compared to those found in lower vertebrates. Despite being anatomically contiguous with the rest of the hindbrain, this area demonstrated a distinct cytoarchitecture and a specialized population of mitotically active cells during advanced stages of embryogenesis.

His described how these proliferative cells originated in a region that later came to be known as the dorsal germinal zone. These cells migrated from their origin toward the cerebellar periphery, eventually forming a transient proliferative layer. His was also the first to coin the term “rautenlippe” or “rhombic lip” to describe this dorsal region, which plays a crucial role in cerebellar development. In contrast, another subset of progenitor cells remained near the roof of the fourth ventricle, forming a distinct proliferative area now known as the ventricular zone (VZ) [10]. It is also reported in other study as 2 distinct zones namely: neuroepithelium zone (thin layer of cells around fourth ventricle) and EGL zone [17]. Thus, it can be wrapped up that there are 2 distinct zones; defined, described and named variedly as:

1. The primary zone, also referred to as the neuroepithelial or VZ—a thin layer of progenitor cells surrounding the fourth ventricle.

2. The secondary zone, also known as the EGL, located superficially on the cerebellar surface (schematically illustrated in Fig. 1).

The primary zone comprises pseudostratified neuroepithelial cells that proliferate robustly and serve as the initial source of neuronal progenitors, commonly referred to as neuroepithelial progenitor cells (NEPs) [18]. These NEPs intern differentiates into multiple cerebellar cell lineages, particularly the cerebellar GABAergic neurons, which include Purkinje cells, inhibitory interneurons (namely; basket cells, stellate cells, Golgi cells), and the deep cerebellar nuclei [19, 20]. Several review articles have documented that these NEP cells undergo spatially guided migration during development, a process tightly regulated by radial glial scaffolding and a specialized type of glial cell found in the cerebellum, the Bergmann glial (BG) cells [18, 21]. These NEPs are responsible to undergo a sequential transition during development in stages to form intermediate NEPs and then finally a mature neuron [22].

In contrast, the cells originating from the secondary zone, which constitute the focus of this review, migrate much earlier into the outer surface of the rhombic lip (as mentioned above). These progenitor cells proliferate and spread across the cerebellar pial surface area of the cerebellum, establishing the EGL [18, 23]. The EGL comprises of cells that later proliferate mitotically and is known as GCPs [24].

GCPs undergo mitotic expansion within this superficial proliferative zone and later exit the cell cycle to become post-mitotic granule neurons. These post-mitotic GCPs extend their axons attaching along the BG cells fibres located in the cerebellar cortex (as mentioned earlier) [20, 25]. When they reach the PCL, they leave their connection with BG and get accumulated in a layer inner to the layer of Purkinje cell. On the course of their migration in ML, they take up 2 fashions of migration- firstly tangential and secondly radial [26]. The accumulated granule cells in the layer inner (deeper) to Purkinje cells are the mature granule cells forming IGL (schematic diagram in Fig. 2).

The formed granule cells undergo synapsis with mossy fibres through their dendrites in the form of claw endings. It is noted in animal studies that this synapse occurs after the shift in the synapse of mossy fibres with Purkinje cells [27] while it’s still unexplained in human studies.

Gestational week-wise time plan of external granular layer

It becomes very important to learn the gestational week (GW)-wise stages and their changes in human species during cerebellar development as studies on other lower species has noted varied period of gestation. For instance, in rats the thickness of EGL is maximum at 15th day postnatal and disappears by 24th day [28]. It is also reported that in rats after birth, the zone of EGL expands rapidly from single cell to eight cells thickness [29]. There are many studies which featured out detailed fate mapping of granule cell development in various species like quail chick camera, mice, rats, etc.

In humans, the third trimester is a time of significant growth in cerebellar development [30]. The growth of the cerebellum in other animal models [31, 32], is notably different from those of human species. These models contrast to humans; the cerebellum is comparatively immature at the time of birth. The foliation, IGL formation, proliferation of EGL all occurs postnatally [33, 34].

While the GW/age-wise EGL development in HFC has been noted by very few authors previously. Thus, the exact GW of appearance and disappearance of this transient layer in developing human cerebellum is not known. The authors Bell et al. [35] and Poudel et al. [6] resulted that EGL is already seen completely developed at 12th GW. While Rakic and Sidman [36], concluded that EGL appeared between 10th and 11th GW. They also mentioned roughly that the layer disappeared postnatally without pointing to any postnatal week/month [36]. Similarly, Abrahám et al. [9] reported that the EGL persisted from the 9th–11th GWs to the first postnatal year.

A recent original article on normally developing HFC reported that the initiation of increase in EGL thickness ranged from 16th to 28th GW [6]. Further, a review article mentioned that 24th–40th GWs is the period when significant neurogenesis occurs in the EGL resulting in increase in cerebellar size [14]. Pibiri et al. [18] reports in a review article-without pointing to any GW that GCP proliferation and migration occurs during all gestational ages extending into early post-natal life. While Abrahám et al. [9] highlights that 28th–34th GWs are the period when the maximum proliferation of EGL occurs, and the same layer disappears around 11th month postnatally.

To summarize, the EGL emerges completely around the 12th GW, undergoes significant proliferative activity peaking between the 28th and 34th weeks, and gradually regresses, disappearing by approximately the 11th postnatal month. This progression is crucial for the proper development and functionality of the human cerebellum.

These reported results from the articles and reviews clearly indicates varied GWs of EGL appearance and disappearance. This points out the research gap that the exact GW of appearance and disappearance of the transient EGL in human species is still vaguely known.

Morphometry of EGL

Only a limited number of human foetal studies have investigated the morphometric characteristics of the EGL across various GWs of cerebellar development. In contrast, a substantial body of research based on animal models [37, 38] has extensively explored this domain.

Thickness of EGL

Microscopically, the thickness of the developing EGL was measured in scarcely few studies and gave these results as follows.

One of the most recent studies done exclusively on normal HFC (GW-wise) reported that the thickness of EGL was maximum i.e., 50.05±34.06 µm at 24th–28th GW group and was minimum 36.06±9.36 µm at >36 GW group [6]. Even though Rakic and Sidman [36] had found the thickness in between 9–32 GW to be nearly constant at about 30–40 µm. Yamaguchi et al. [39] who morphometrically studied on human cerebellar nuclei had reported that the change in the EGL thickness occurs with the progression in foetal age. Moreover, Abrahám et al. [9] measured the thickness of EGL at paramedian level and found it to be similar at all lobules.

Number of neurons

The neuronal number in EGL has been studied previously by Poudel et al. [6]. He observed the increase in the neuronal number in the EGL with the progressive increase in the GW from 16th to 36th weeks and it ranged between 89.92±42 and 142.84±50 per Field of Vision (1000X).

These above quantitative studies enlightens that there is lack of morphometric studies on various cortical layers. The discrepancies in the above reported EGL thickness across studies may be attributed to differences in measurement techniques, sample sizes, and the precise regions of the cerebellum analysed. The consistent observation of increasing neuronal density with gestational age underscores the dynamic nature of cerebellar development during the foetal period. However, the limited number of comprehensive morphometric studies on the EGL highlights the need for further research to elucidate the precise developmental trajectory of this critical cerebellar layer. Moreover, while existing studies provide valuable insights into the morphometry of the HFC’s EGL, variations in findings emphasize the necessity for standardized methodologies and larger sample sizes in future research to achieve a more definitive understanding.

Gene expressions of external granular layer during neurogenesis

The development of the HFC is a highly orchestrated process involving the regulation of granule cell proliferation within the EGL.

Sonic hedgehog and granule cell proliferation

Central to the process of developing HFC is the sonic hedgehog (SHH) signalling pathway, which plays a crucial role in the proliferation of GCPs. The Purkinje cells release SHH, which affects the mitogenic influence on GCPs present in the EGL [13, 40-42]. Disruptions in SHH signalling can lead to aberrant cerebellar patterning and are implicated in various cerebellar pathologies.

SOX2 and PAX6 expression in developing human cerebellum

Unlike studies on other species, there are a very few studies in humans, who has reported the expression of SOX2 a transcription factor known for its role in maintaining neural progenitor identity in developing HFC [43]. It has been resulted from other species study [44] that PAX6, a transcription factor stands crucial for the development of various neural structures. In the human cerebellum, PAX6 is expressed throughout gestation by precursor cells of EGL called GCPs. The consistent expression of PAX6 underscores its importance in the proliferation and differentiation of these GCPs, which gives rise to the proper formation of mature cerebellar granule neurons in EGL [18].

Meis1 and Math1 in rhombic lip and EGL formation

Meis1 is a homeobox gene expressed in the cells of the rhombic lip, a germinal zone that gives rise to various cerebellar neurons. Meis1-positive cells migrate to the outermost layer of the cerebellum to form the EGL. Additionally, Math1 (also known as Atoh1) is a transcription factor expressed in progenitor cells of the rhombic lip, contributing to the formation of the EGL. The coordinated expression of Meis1 and Math1 is essential for the proper development of the EGL and subsequent cerebellar architecture [45].

Various congenital abnormalities occurring due to changes in external granular layer development

The dysregulation of SHH signalling mediated cerebellar granule cell proliferation, aberrant neuronal migration, and axon guidance may be the cause of originating cerebellar tumours, known as medulloblastomas, aberrant development observed in JS, DWM, and pilocytic astrocytoma [13, 14, 46, 47].

Medulloblastomas

Medulloblastoma, a brainstem tumour found in children, is considered to originate from GCPs which fail to undergo normal differentiation [23]. Medulloblastoma may also result from mutations/alterations in the PATCHED1 gene, which is triggered by SHH [42]. Indeed, previous murine model studies have concluded that the reason for origin of medulloblastomas is the persistence of EGL [48, 49]. Other murine studies resulted that the inability to downregulate Shh signalling in EGL cells underlies the development of medulloblastomas [33].

Dandy–Walker malformation

Haldipur et al. [46] in one of their recent research projects (2021) focusing DWM, concludes that there is decreased proliferation and production of GCPs in posterior lobe, leading to inferior vermian hypoplasia and further into DWM. This suggests that dysregulation of Shh signalling, which is essential for GCP proliferation, may be implicated in DWM pathogenesis.

Pilocytic astrocytoma’s

The EGL is a transient layer in the developing cerebellum where GCPs proliferate before migrating inward. Failure in the timely regression of the EGL, potentially due to aberrant Shh signalling, may contribute to tumorigenesis the development of typically benign, slow-growing brain tumours. It is also reported by Tauziède-Espariat et al. [47] that the persistence of EGL may cause slow-growing brain tumours termed as pilocytic astrocytoma.

These major congenital abnormalities mentioned above occurs due to the dysregulation of Shh signalling leading to improper proliferation and migration of the granule cell from EGL to IGL, forming tumours. Thus, understanding the nuances of SHH pathway is essential for developing targeted treatments and improving patient outcomes for such congenital abnormalities mentioned above. Conclusion

In this review, we aim to address several critical questions regarding the EGL in the developing human cerebellum: Why is the EGL transient? What is its functional significance during gestation? What are the consequences of its abnormal persistence postnatally? And what key knowledge gaps remain in this underexplored area of human neurodevelopment?

The EGL in the developing human cerebellum can be concluded as a transient but essential structure, pivotal for the proper formation and functional maturation of the cerebellar cortex. This review comprehensively consolidates the EGL’s crucial role in neurogenesis, including its cellular composition, gestational timeline, and genetic regulation. Notably, the following are key highlights from this comprehensive review:

1. The EGL becomes fully established around the 12th GW, undergoes significant proliferative activity peaking between the 28th and 34th GWs, and gradually regresses, disappearing by approximately the 11th postnatal month.

2. The EGL reaches its peak thickness of 50.05 µm between the 24th and 28th GWs, after which it gradually diminishes during the third trimester.

3. SHH gene plays a pivotal role in regulating the proliferation of GCPs within the EGL through a complex genetic signaling network.

4. Abnormality in EGL is linked to congenital disorders like medulloblastoma, JS, DWM, and pilocytic astrocytoma’s—conditions frequently linked to dysregulated SHH signalling.

5. The review underscores the pressing need for more human-specific studies to address the many gaps in our current understanding of EGL development.

Understanding the dynamics of the EGL provides valuable insights into potential congenital abnormalities—such as medulloblastomas, DWM, etc.—that arise from disrupted cerebellar development. As research on the human EGL remains limited, this narrative review emphasizes the necessity for further studies that focused exclusively on human foetal development. Such investigations are vital for uncovering the unique mechanisms that govern cerebellar maturation and for developing targeted interventions for related disorders. Expanding our knowledge of the EGL will ultimately enhance our understanding of the aetiology of various cerebellar conditions and improve clinical outcomes for affected individuals.

Several critical research gaps have been identified through this review:

1. The mechanism of synaptogenesis between granule cells and the claw endings of mossy fibres remains unexplained in human studies.

2. The precise gestational timing of EGL appearance and regression in humans is still vaguely defined, unlike in animal models where it has been extensively characterized.

3. Quantitative analyses of the EGL—and of all layers of the developing cerebellar cortex—remain insufficient. Specifically, the number of neurons within the EGL has only been reported by a single author referenced in this review.

It is also important to note that though this narrative review holds its value for providing a comprehensive overview, they may inherit limitations. They lack the systematic approach of literature search which may lead to bias and incomplete literature coverage according to the subjectivity of the review. Illustrating these limitations will enhance the transparency and credibility of this review.
