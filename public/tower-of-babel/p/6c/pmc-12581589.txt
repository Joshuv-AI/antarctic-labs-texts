Forced marriage, domestic violence, and suicidal ideation among married individuals: a population-based study from Western Iran
Authors: Omran Davarinejad, Mehdi Khezeli, Tara Yarveisi, Farzaneh Moradi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide remains a critical public health issue worldwide. In Iran, particularly in the western province of Kermanshah, forced marriage and domestic violence are prevalent social problems that may contribute to suicidal ideation among married individuals. This study aimed to examine the association between forced marriage, physical intimate partner violence (IPV), and suicidal ideation, while exploring the protective roles of social support and resilience.MethodsA cross-sectional population-based study was conducted in 2024 with 369 married adults aged 18–59 years from Kermanshah city. Participants were selected through multi-stage sampling from diverse socioeconomic areas. Data were collected using validated questionnaires including Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation, Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale, Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale, and a demographic checklist. Logistic regression was used to identify predictors of suicidal ideation.ResultsIn this study, 24.1% of people reported suicidal ideation, 33.9% experienced forced marriage, and 22.2% experienced physical IPV. Suicidal ideation was significantly higher among those exposed to forced marriage (OR = 2.47, 95% CI: 1.32–4.61, P < 0.01), physical IPV (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.24–4.74, P < 0.01), history of attempted suicide (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.01–6.22, P < 0.05), suicide attempts in the family (OR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.07–4.60, P < 0.05), history of mental disorders (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.04–4.38, P < 0.05), female gender (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.01–4.11, P < 0.05), and suicide attempt in friends (OR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.06–3.84, P < 0.05). Conversely, higher social support (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98, P < 0.05) and resilience (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, P < 0.01) were significantly protective against suicidal ideation. The model explained up to 41% of the variance in suicidal ideation.ConclusionForced marriage and domestic violence are significant risk factors for suicidal ideation among married individuals in Kermanshah, while social support and resilience serve as protective factors. These findings highlight the urgent need for culturally sensitive interventions targeting harmful social practices and strengthening psychosocial resources to reduce suicide risk in this population.Keywords: Domestic violence, Forced marriage, Resilience, Social support, Suicidal ideation

Introduction

Suicide remains a major public health concern globally, with over 700,000 deaths reported annually and millions more experiencing suicidal ideation (SI) or attempting suicide [1]. While mental disorders, particularly depression, have long been identified as key risk factors, socio-cultural and structural variables play a crucial role, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Iran [2–4]. In recent years, growing attention has been paid to the interplay between gender-based violence, structural oppression, and SI, particularly among women living in patriarchal societies [5].

Iran has witnessed significant social transformations in the past decades, but suicide remains stigmatized and underreported [6]. Studies suggest that SI is more prevalent among Iranian women, particularly in the western provinces where Kurdish ethnic identity intersects with traditional gender norms and economic disadvantage [7–9]. Kermanshah, located in western Iran, consistently reports high rates of mental health challenges and self-harm behaviors [10, 11]. Previous studies in this region has revealed a deep connection between marital discord, lack of autonomy in spousal selection, and psychological distress [4, 12].

Although the sociological literature suggests that marriage is a protective factor for suicidal behaviors, criticism of this view is growing. Studies suggest, paradoxically, that marriage alone is not a protective factor, but that family and social integration are more important [13]. Forced marriage undermines the traditionally assumed protective role of marriage against suicidal behaviors, because it can disrupt couples’ independence, positive relationships, and mental health [14, 15]. Forced marriage, defined as a union where one or both parties have not given full and free consent [13]. Studies have shown that forced marriages are associated with emotional repression, limited agency, and reduced life satisfaction, factors that may contribute to SI [16, 17]. Moreover, domestic violence, both physical and psychological remains widespread and under-acknowledged in Iranian society. Victims of intimate partner violence often face cumulative stressors such as shame, isolation, and financial dependence, increasing their vulnerability to suicidal behaviors [18, 19].

Extensive international research supports the associations between forced marriage, intimate partner violence (IPV), and SI across diverse cultural contexts. For instance, a nationally representative longitudinal study in South Korea demonstrated that both verbal abuse and physical assault within intimate relationships significantly increased the risk of subsequent suicidal ideation among married adults [20]. Similarly, cross-sectional network analysis among Arab women highlighted that psychological and economic abuse, components of IPV, are strongly interconnected with suicidal ideation [21]. In rural western China, women exposed to physical abuse were more than four times likelier to report lifetime suicidal ideation compared to non-victims [22].

In contrast, protective factors such as resilience and social support have been identified as buffers against suicidal ideation. Resilience, the psychological ability to recover from adversity, and social support networks (from family, friends, or significant others) play a central role in maintaining mental well-being, especially in high-stress environments [5, 23]. In line with the socio-ecological model, this study conceptualizes SI as the result of both individual-level vulnerabilities and broader social-structural stressors.

Gender differences are particularly notable in this context. Female gender has consistently emerged as a predictor of SI and attempt in Iran [5, 24], reflecting not only biological factors but also social inequalities, including restricted access to education, early marriage, and low decision-making power within families. This underscores the importance of adopting a gender-sensitive lens when studying suicidality in conservative societies.

Despite increasing interest in the psychosocial determinants of SI in Iran, there remains a gap in empirical research addressing the combined effects of forced marriage, domestic violence, and protective psychosocial resources. The present study seeks to fill this gap by examining the predictors of SI in a sample of married adults in Kermanshah, with a particular focus on forced marriage, partner violence, and psychological resources such as resilience and perceived social support.

Building on previous qualitative and quantitative works by the authors [4, 5, 8, 12], this study aims to contribute novel insights that may inform culturally grounded interventions and policy changes to address suicide risk in vulnerable populations. Understanding these dynamics is not only important for public health but also for advancing human rights, especially for women and marginalized groups in traditional contexts.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted in 2024 with married adults residing in Kermanshah, western Iran. Based on the study by Rezapour Shahkolai et al. [9],, where the mean and standard deviation of SI were 5.98 ± 7.79, and assuming a margin of error (d) of 0.8, the initially estimated sample size was 365 participants. To account for possible non-response or incomplete questionnaires, we applied an inflation factor of 1.2, yielding a target sample size of 438 participants. Ultimately, 369 adults completed the questionnaires, which was slightly above the original estimation and corresponded to a response rate of 84%.Inclusion criteria were being married, aged between 18 and 59 years, residing in Kermanshah city, and having no diagnosed psychotic disorders.Participants with any acute illness (e.g., influenza or COVID-19) at the time of data collection were excluded, as such conditions could influence mood, SI, or other study variables. These illnesses were assessed via self-report and by healthcare providers at the health centers.

Participants were recruited through a multi-stage sampling method. Initially, five out of 20 urban health centers in Kermanshah were randomly selected from the northern, southern, western, eastern, and central regions. To ensure socioeconomic status (SES) representation, centers were chosen based on SES classification from prior studies [25, 26], dividing neighborhoods into low, middle, and upper SES groups. Accordingly, one center was selected from the upper SES group, two from the middle SES, and two from the low SES category. In the second stage, the sample size for each selected center was determined proportionally to the size of the married adult population registered in that center. Accordingly, larger centers contributed more participants, while smaller centers contributed fewer, ensuring representativeness across different urban regions. Within each center, individuals were randomly selected from health records using the SIB online system. After explaining the study purpose and obtaining informed consent, participants completed the questionnaire, which took approximately 15 to 30 min. Data collection occurred between January and June 2024.

Measures

Demographic and background questionnaire

This included demographic items (age, education level, gender, and occupation), questions about history of suicide attempts (in friends, family members, and self), and items on mental disorder history (self-reported), type of marriage (forced vs. consent), and experience of physical intimate partner violence (IPV) within the past year. Physical IPV was assessed with a single question developed based on the World Health Organization’s [27]definition of domestic violence and the first question of the Partner Violence Screen (PVS) [28]. Physical IPV behaviors measured included slapping, hitting, kicking, burning, and hair pulling.

Beck Scale for Suicidal Ideation (BSSI)

This 19-item questionnaire evaluates the severity of SI, with items scored from 0 to 2 and a total score range of 0–38; higher scores indicate greater SI severity. The BSSI serves as a self-report tool suitable for the general population [29]. Since the BSSI lacks a defined cutoff, we applied Beck and Steer’s screening method [30], in which responses to items 4 and 5 determine the presence of active or inactive SI, prompting completion of the remaining 14 items to assess severity [31]. The psychometric properties of the Persian version have been validated, showing positive correlations with depression and the SCL-90-R severity index, and negative correlations with social support [32]. The scale demonstrated high internal consistency in Iran (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.97) [9], which aligns with the findings of the present study (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.972).

Multidimensional Perceived Social Support Scale (MSPSS)

This 12-item scale, developed by Zimet et al., [33]is designed to assess perceived social support from three sources: family, friends, and significant others. Each subscale consists of four items, rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from strongly disagree to strongly agree, with higher scores indicating greater perceived support. The MSPSS has demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.81–0.98) and reliability across different populations [34]. The Persian version was validated by Bagherian et al., [35], reporting a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.92 in the general population. Subscale reliability coefficients were reported from 0.71 to 0.79 [23].In the present study, the overall Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.80, with subscale alphas of 0.91 (family), 0.85 (friends), and 0.92 (significant others).

Connor-Davidson resilience scale (CD-RISC)

This 25-item scale assesses resilience using a 5-point Likert scale from 1 (always false) to 5 (completely true), with higher scores indicating greater resilience. The CD-RISC has demonstrated good internal consistency, with a reported Cronbach’s alpha of 0.86 [36]. Its Persian version was validated in the Iranian population by Sharif Nia et al., [37], and Cronbach’s alpha ranging from 0.84 to 0.89 across different studies [38, 39].In the present study, the internal consistency of the CD-RISC was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.84.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 18 and STATA version 8. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize demographic characteristics and suicidal ideation (SI) as means (± standard deviation) for continuous variables or percentages for categorical variables. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using chi-square (χ²) tests. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 (two-tailed).

To identify predictors of suicidal ideation, binary logistic regression was performed with the enter method, in which all relevant predictors were entered into the model simultaneously. The dependent variable was binary, based on the sum of questions 4 and 5 from the Beck Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire, classifying participants into SI and non-SI groups. The absence of multicollinearity among main continuous predictors (social support and resilience) was confirmed using correlation analysis. Observations were independent, with no repeated measures or clustering. Outliers were assessed and managed appropriately.

Ethical aspect of the study

This study received ethics approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Kermanshah University of Medical Sciences (IR.KUMS.MED.REC.1402.180). Informed consent was obtained from all participants via a form presented on the questionnaire’s first page, detailing study objectives and ensuring confidentiality.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics and SI

This study included 369 married individuals from Kermanshah, of whom 60.7% were women. The mean age of the participants was36.78 ± 9.68 years. The most frequent educational attainment was below diploma level (n = 163, 44.2%), followed by college education (n = 112, 30.4%). Regarding occupational status, housewives constituted the largest group (41.2%), followed by employees (23.6%). The lifetime prevalence of suicide attempts among participants was 9.5%. Histories of suicide attempts in family members and friends were reported by 17.9% and 29.3% of participants, respectively. Approximately one-third of participants (33.9%) had experienced forced marriage. In the past year, 22.2% (n = 82) had experienced physical intimate partner violence (IPV), and 19.2% reported a history of mental disorders. In total, 89 participants (24.1%) were in the SI group. Comparison of groups with and without SI

Table 2 compares suicide attempt history, type of marriage, physical intimate partner violence (IPV), and self-reported mental health disorders between individuals with and without suicidal ideation. Chi-square tests indicated significant associations between these factors and SI. Specifically, a history of suicide attempts was strongly linked to SI (P < 0.01), with individuals reporting such a history being more than twice as likely to report SI (54.3% vs. 21.0%). Similarly, a family history of suicide attempts was significantly associated with SI (P < 0.01), where those with affected family members showed a higher likelihood of experiencing SI (40.9%) compared to those without (20.5%). Additionally, having friends with a history of suicide attempts was also significantly related to SI (P < 0.05), with affected individuals reporting a higher rate of SI (32.4%) versus those without such connections (20.7%). The type of marriage demonstrated a strong and significant relationship with SI (P < 0.01); individuals in forced marriages reported a substantially higher prevalence of SI (41.6%) compared to those in consensual marriages (15.2%). Physical IPV was also significantly associated with SI (P < 0.01), with a much higher prevalence among those who experienced IPV (45.1%) compared to those who did not (18.1%). Finally, a history of mental health problems showed a significant association with SI (P < 0.01), with affected individuals exhibiting a higher rate of SI (39.4%) than those without such history (20.5%).

Age, occupation, and education, were not significant predictors of SI. Forced marriage emerged as the strongest predictor of SI (Odds Ratio [OR] = 2.47, 95% Confidence Interval [CI]: 1.32–4.61, P < 0.01). Individuals who had experienced physical IPV in the past year had a higher likelihood of reporting SI (OR = 2.33, 95% CI: 1.24–4.74, P < 0.01). History of attempted suicide increased the likelihood of having suicidal ideation 2.3 times (OR = 2.31, 95% CI: 1.01–6.22, P < 0.05). A history of suicide attempts in the family was also associated with a 2.2 times higher chance of SI in individuals (OR = 2.22, 95% CI: 1.07–4.60, P < 0.05). Those reporting a history of mental disorders were approximately twice as likely to report SI (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.04–4.38, P < 0.05). Female gender was another significant predictor for SI (OR = 2.13, 95% CI: 1.01–4.11, P < 0.05); women were almost twice as likely as men to report suicidal ideation. Individuals reporting a history of suicide attempts among their friends were also approximately twice as likely to report SI (OR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.06–3.84, P < 0.05). Higher scores on social support (OR = 0.93, 95% CI: 0.88–0.98, P < 0.05) and resilience (OR = 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99, P < 0.01) were associated with a lower likelihood of suicidal ideation. Specifically, for each unit increase in social support score, the likelihood of reporting SI decreased by approximately 7%, and for each unit increase in resilience score, the likelihood decreased by approximately 4%. These findings highlight the protective roles of social support and resilience in reducing the risk of suicidal ideation.

The Cox & Snell R2 and Nagelkerke R2 ​​for the model were 0.307 and 0.409, respectively. These values indicate that approximately 31% and 41% of the variance in SI can be explained by the independent variables: forced marriage, physical IPV, previous attempted suicidee, history of suicide attempts in the family, history of mental disorders, history of suicide attempts in friends, female gender, social support, and resilience.This suggests that 59% to 69% of the likelihood of SI is attributable to other unassessed variables. Given the multifactorial nature of suicidal behaviors, the explanatory power of the variables in this regression model is noteworthy.

Discussion

The findings of this study reinforce the complexity of suicidal ideation, emphasizing that it cannot be attributed solely to psychological factors but rather arises from the interaction between individual vulnerabilities and broader social determinants. This multifaceted perspective aligns with international research indicating that suicidal behavior results from a dynamic interplay of mental health issues, social environments, and cultural factors [40].

Forced marriage emerged as a particularly salient risk factor, significantly increasing the likelihood of SI among women in our sample. This finding echoes global evidence linking coerced marital arrangements with adverse mental health outcomes, including depression and suicidal behaviors [41]. In the Iranian context, prior qualitative research [4] has underscored how forced marriage functions as a form of social control that undermines women’s autonomy, contributing to feelings of entrapment and hopelessness. The lack of autonomy and the experience of being compelled into a life-altering decision contribute to a profound sense of helplessness, a critical psychological precursor to SI [17]. Forced marriage, therefore, functions not only as a personal trauma but as a structural factor embedded in cultural norms that disproportionately affect women and limit their life choices [16].Thus, forced marriage represents both a personal trauma and a systemic social constraint, highlighting the necessity of culturally sensitive interventions.

Domestic violence, particularly physical violence within the family, also showed a strong positive association with SI, consistent with a vast body of literature worldwide [42]. The psychological consequences of intimate partner violence, including post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, and hopelessness, have been robustly linked to increased suicide risk [43].The impact of violence is compounded by its disruption of social support networks and erosion of psychological resilience, two critical protective factors shown in this study to mitigate suicide risk [8]. Previous research has further highlighted how women experiencing domestic violence often face social isolation and diminished coping resources, reinforcing the interplay between social adversity and individual psychological vulnerability [4, 44]. This interaction between violence and social isolation underscores the need for comprehensive prevention programs that address both the psychological and social dimensions of abuse.

The heightened risk among women is consistent with the socio-cultural context in which gender inequality remains pervasive. Kurdish women in Kermanshah, as demonstrated in prior studies [5, 7, 10], are disproportionately exposed to socio-structural pressures, including restrictive gender roles and limited access to supportive services. These factors intersect with psychological stressors such as marital discord and mental health challenges, underscoring the need for gender-sensitive prevention strategies. In this context, gender is not merely a demographic variable but a lens through which both social and psychological factors are filtered and intensified [3].

Our results also highlight the influence of peer suicidal behavior on individual risk, illustrating the social contagion effect frequently reported in suicide research [45]. Exposure to suicide attempts among friends can normalize SI and lower psychological barriers to self-harm, emphasizing the importance of community-level interventions and peer support systems [46].In addition to peer influences, our findings highlight the critical role of personal and family history of suicide attempts in shaping suicidal ideation. Individuals with a prior suicide attempt were significantly more likely to report current SI, consistent with the well-established literature identifying personal history as one of the strongest predictors of future suicidal thoughts and behaviors [47, 48]. Similarly, a family history of suicide attempts emerged as a significant risk factor, underscoring the combined effects of genetic vulnerability, familial modeling, and exposure to suicidal behaviors during formative periods [49, 50]. These findings reinforce the notion that suicidal ideation arises from a complex interplay of individual vulnerabilities and social determinants. Just as exposure to suicidal behaviors among friends may normalize SI, a familial history of suicide can influence coping strategies, perceived norms, and emotional responses to stress, amplifying risk in those already predisposed. Clinically, these results highlight the importance of careful screening for both personal and family suicide history in prevention programs, alongside attention to social and environmental stressors such as forced marriage and domestic violence.

Psychological factors, particularly self-reported mental disorders, remain fundamental predictors of SI, as shown in numerous epidemiological studies worldwide [51, 52]. However, such mental health issues do not develop in isolation but are profoundly influenced by social conditions, including marital stress and exposure to violence. This reciprocal relationship between social adversity and psychological distress is well-documented in the literature [53, 54].

Importantly, social support and resilience emerged as robust protective factors, buffering individuals against the risk of suicide. The protective role of social support is consistently supported across diverse cultural settings, serving as an emotional resource and reducing feelings of isolation [55]. Similarly, psychological resilience, defined as the ability to adapt to stress and adversityhas been identified as a key factor that enables individuals to cope with life challenges and reduce suicidal behaviors [23]. Prior studies in Iranian populations have echoed these findings, emphasizing the critical importance of strengthening social networks and resilience, particularly among vulnerable groups such as women facing marital and societal pressures [4, 8].

Together, these findings affirm the importance of adopting an integrated, biopsychosocial approach to suicide prevention. This requires addressing not only individual psychological factors but also the social structures and cultural norms that shape mental health and behavior. For populations like married women in Kermanshah, interventions must target the reduction of harmful social practices such as forced marriage and domestic violence while simultaneously fostering supportive social environments and resilience-building strategies. This comprehensive perspective is critical to effectively reducing suicide risk in culturally complex and socially challenging contexts.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between forced marriage, domestic violence, and suicidal ideation. Longitudinal studies are needed to better understand the temporal dynamics and causal pathways. Second, data were collected through self-reported questionnaires, which may introduce reporting bias or underreporting, especially given the stigma surrounding suicide and domestic violence in Iranian society. Third, the sample was limited to married adults living in urban areas of Kermanshah, which may reduce the generalizability of results to rural populations or other regions with different socio-cultural contexts. Fourth, physical IPV was measured using a single-item question, which, although commonly used for screening purposes, may not fully capture the complexity, frequency, or severity of abuse compared to multi-item scales. This could potentially lead to underestimation or oversimplification of IPV experiences. Additionally, some important factors potentially influencing suicidal ideation, such as economic status, religious beliefs, or detailed mental health diagnoses, were not assessed in this study.

Despite these limitations, the study has several strengths. It provides valuable population-based data on a vulnerable group that is often understudied, particularly in the context of Western Iran. The multi-stage sampling method enhanced the representativeness of the sample across different socioeconomic strata of Kermanshah. The use of validated and reliable instruments to measure SI, social support, and resilience strengthens the credibility of the findings. Moreover, the study’s integrated biopsychosocial framework captures the complex interplay of individual, social, and cultural factors affecting SI, offering comprehensive insights that can inform culturally sensitive interventions and policies.

Conclusions

This study highlights the multifaceted nature of SI among married individuals in Kermanshah, emphasizing the significant roles of forced marriage, intimate partner violence, and psychological factors such as mental disorders, social support, and resilience. Forced marriage and physical domestic violence emerged as critical risk factors that substantially increase the likelihood of SI, particularly among women living in patriarchal and socio-economically disadvantaged contexts. Conversely, social support and resilience serve as vital protective buffers that reduce suicide risk, underscoring the need to strengthen these psychosocial resources within vulnerable populations.

Given these findings, it is essential to implement culturally sensitive and gender-responsive interventions that address both individual and structural determinants of suicide. Policies and programs should focus on preventing forced marriages through legal reforms, community education, and empowerment initiatives aimed at enhancing women’s autonomy and decision-making power. Additionally, comprehensive domestic violence prevention and support services must be expanded, including accessible counseling, safe shelters, and legal assistance.

Mental health services in the region should be integrated with social support systems to provide holistic care that fosters resilience and mitigates the psychological consequences of trauma and social adversity. Community-based peer support networks and educational campaigns can further promote awareness and reduce the stigma surrounding mental health and suicidal behaviors. Ultimately, suicide prevention in Kermanshah, and similar socio-cultural settingsrequires a collaborative approach involving healthcare providers, policymakers, social workers, and community leaders to create safe, supportive environments that protect vulnerable individuals and uphold their human rights.
