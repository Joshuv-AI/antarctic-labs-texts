Albuminuria and Acute Heart Failure: A New Biomarker for Congestion and Follow-Up?
Authors: Mario Galván-Ruiz, Patricia Nogueira-Salgueiro, Belén Rojas-Escrivá, María del Val Groba-Marco, Miguel Fernández-de-Sanmamed-Girón, Jesús María González-Martín, Jonathan Déniz-Rosario, Daesub Chung-Kwon, Marco Antonio Suarez-Benítez, Elvira Martín-Bou, Sara Aladro-Escribano, Juan Carlos Quevedo-Reina, Ruth Martín-Alfaro, Casimira Domínguez-Cabrera, Eduardo Caballero-Dorta, Antonio García-Quintana, Alicia Conde-Martel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study evaluated the trajectory of the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) in patients hospitalized with acute heart failure (HF) from pre-admission (baseline), through hospital admission, discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge. We also assessed its association with congestion-related biomarkers, including N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), CA 125, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte (N/L) ratio.MethodsProspective, observational study including 99 consecutive patients admitted with acute HF. Blood and urine analyses were performed at admission, at discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge. Patients were stratified according to UACR at admission and the trajectories of UACR and biomarkers were evaluated. The clinical, analytical, and echocardiographic predictors of UACR were analysed.ResultsMean age was 70.8 ± 12.2 years, 57% were men. At admission, albuminuria was present in 65.7% of patients (45.5% microalbuminuria, 20.2% macroalbuminuria, respectively). A higher UACR at admission was associated with diabetes, lower eGFR, previous HF admission, higher clinical congestion score, mixed or right-sided congestive phenotype, higher NT-proBNP, and CA 125 levels. The UACR worsened from baseline to admission and improved at discharge, reflecting the congestion dynamics. NT-proBNP, CA 125, and N/L ratio showed similar patterns. Independent predictors of higher UACR at admission were diabetes, reduced renal function, higher aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and lower vitamin D levels. In the mixed linear regression analysis, lower eGFR, and higher AST levels remained independently associated with UACR trajectory. Admission UACR correlated with congestion score and predicted higher in-hospital mortality and early HF readmissions (<30 days).ConclusionAlbuminuria is common in acute HF and exhibits a dynamic change in relation to congestion. These findings suggest a potential role as a biomarker for congestion monitoring. However, given the study limitations, the results should be considered exploratory and warrant confirmation in larger prospective studies.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is one of the most common comorbidities in patients with heart failure (HF) [1]. Its presence is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular events and becomes more significant as the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) declines [2].

Assessing renal function in patients with HF is challenging owing to the lack of early biomarkers. Elevated creatinine or decreased eGFR is delayed indicators of renal damage and their impairment reflects a substantial loss of the nephron reserve. However, albuminuria is an early marker that reflects structural damage and appears before a decrease in eGFR occurs [3].

Historically, the presence of albuminuria has been a risk factor for cardiovascular events and HF, independent of eGFR or the presence of comorbidities. The prevalence in HF is estimated to be between 20 and 40% [4]. Recently, in a sub-analysis of the Spanish Cardiorrenal Registry, it was observed that the prevalence could be higher than 40% in patients with ambulatory HF [5]. It can be measured using a single urine sample, without the need for a 24-h measurement [6].

The pathophysiology of albuminuria is multifactorial and reflects the structural damage to the glomerular filtration barrier. It is classically attributed to vascular endothelial damage, the presence of comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, or obesity) or tubular damage [7]. These alterations triggered a cascade reaction that causes inflammation and activation of the neurohormonal system. Activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) causes direct damage to the podocytes, leading to albuminuria, as does the secretion of cytokines produced by the inflammation [8].

In recent years, albuminuria due to haemodynamic changes caused by congestion has been reported in patients with HF, regardless of comorbidities [9, 10]. The rise of the central venous pressure causes an increase in renal venous pressure, reducing renal perfusion and GFR. This process causes inflammation and oxidative stress and despite the activation of compensatory mechanisms such as RAAS, it ultimately perpetuates the vicious cycle [11].

Recently, albuminuria has been correlated with congestion. The TACRAHF study analysed the trajectory of albuminuria and its relationship with N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels during acute heart failure (AHF) admission and how it is reduced after decongestion [12].

This study was designed to test the primary hypothesis that UACR behaves as a dynamic congestion-related biomarker during AHF hospitalization. In a secondary analysis, we explored whether its trajectory might provide additional prognostic information for in-hospital and early post-discharge outcomes (<30 days). Although previous studies have suggested a possible association between UACR and congestion, evidence regarding its temporal behaviour and its relationship with other biomarkers remains limited. Therefore, this study assessed the prevalence and trajectory of UACR in patients with acute HF, examined its association with analytical parameters of congestion and explored its potential prognostic value in this clinical setting.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was an observational, single-center, prospective study that included 99 consecutive patients admitted with HF, in a cardiology department of a tertiary hospital. Eligible patients were aged 18 years or older, following a clinical HF diagnosis, according to the 2021 European Society of Cardiology guidelines [13] and were included regardless of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). All patients underwent blood and urine tests upon admission.

Patients with concomitant acute coronary syndrome, those requiring renal replacement therapy prior to hospital admission and those without complete blood and urine tests were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria were conditions known to increase the UACR, such as active infections, hyperglycaemia (defined as blood glucose levels >500 mg/dL) or poorly controlled hypertension (systolic/diastolic blood pressure >180/120 mm Hg).

Blood test and urine biochemistry, including biomarkers, were performed at admission, at hospital discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge. In-hospital management was performed according to the standard clinical practice. Subsequently, a routine clinical follow-up was performed. Demographic data, comorbidities, heart disease characteristics, laboratory data at admission, discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge, as well as in-hospital mortality, HF readmissions and early mortality (<30 days after hospital discharge) were collected.

HF prognostic drug treatment was evaluated at admission and at discharged. The implementation of guideline directed medical therapy (GDMT) was evaluated in patient with LVEF ≤ 40%. Since patients with LVEF between 41 and 49% received similar treatment, although with lower guideline recommendation, they were included in the same group (<50%) to analyze the impact of early implementation of GDMT on UACR. Optimal therapy was defined as the use of β-blockers, angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors (ARNI), mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) and sodium-glucose cotransporter type 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i).

To determine the UACR trajectory at different phases, data were available for 86 patients in the pre-admission period, 99 at admission, 81 at discharge and 85 at 2 weeks post-discharge. Missing data at discharge or follow-up were due to in-hospital or early post-discharge deaths (n = 13), protocol deviations or loss to follow-up (n = 1). Patients with lacking biomarker data at any time points (admission, discharge, or 2 weeks post-discharge) were excluded from the analyses. We evaluated the trajectory of UACR according to LVEF group (<50% or ≥50%), presence of diabetes mellitus or previous use of MRA or SGLT2i.

Grading and Scoring Clinical Congestion

The clinical congestion score (CCS) is an established congestion scale ranging from 0 to 9, composed of three representative clinical signs and symptoms of congestion: oedema, jugular venous distension and orthopnea. The clinical congestion score was assessed on admission for AHF [14]. Patients who predominantly presented with oedema and jugular venous distension were classified as having a right-sided congestion phenotype, those with predominant orthopnea were classified as having a left-sided phenotype and patients exhibiting both were classified as having a mixed phenotype.

Laboratory Analysis

Blood and urine samples were collected at each study time point. Biomarkers assessed included UACR and NT-proBNP. Serum cancer antigen 125 (CA 125) levels were also measured, as this biomarker reflects tissue congestion and is produced by mesothelial cells in response to elevated hydrostatic pressure. The neutrophil-lymphocyte (N/L) ratio was also studied, which is a marker of systemic inflammation and a prognostic marker in HF [15]. When available, biomarker data or the N/L ratio before admission was collected. The period prior to admission was defined as the “pre-admission reference value,” during HF admission as “wet phase” and the period following decongestion as the “dry phase.”

At admission, patients were classified according to their UACR level at admission: A1 (normoalbuminuria, UACR < 30 mg/g), A2 (microalbuminuria, UACR 30–300 mg/g), and A3 (macroalbuminuria, UACR > 300 mg/g). Additional laboratory variables including venous blood gases analysis, renal function, urinary electrolytes (sodium, potassium and chlorine), liver function test (aspartate aminotransferase [AST], alanine aminotransferase [ALT], bilirubin, alkaline phosphatase, gamma glutamyl transferase, iron profile, calcium-phosphorus metabolism, and complete blood count were collected). The N/L ratio was calculated.

Endpoint

The primary endpoint was to evaluate the trajectory of UACR and its relationship with other biomarkers such as NT-proBNP and CA 125 during an acute HF admission. The secondary endpoint was to identify the predictors of UACR at admission, discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge based on laboratory, echocardiographic, and clinical data. As exploratory endpoints (hypothesis-generating analyses), we evaluated whether the early implementation of GDMT during hospitalization in patients with LVEF < 50% influenced the trajectory of UACR, as well as whether UACR at admission predicted a composite outcome of in-hospital and early mortality and HF readmissions within 30 days after discharge.

Statistical Analysis

A descriptive analysis of all the collected variables was performed. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges depending on whether the distribution was normal or not. The UACR, NT-proBNP, CA 125, and the N/L ratio were converted to decimal logarithms to achieve a better distribution of the data.

The Chi-square test or Fisher’s test was used to compare categorical variables. For the comparison of a qualitative variable with two categories and a quantitative variable, the Student’s T test or the Mann-Whitney U test was used, depending on whether the distribution was normal or not. When the qualitative variable had more than two ordered categories (normoalbuminuria, microalbuminuria, and macroalbuminuria), we applied linear regression to the quantitative variable to assess trends across groups and a p for trend was reported. Paired data tests were used to compare variables before and after. A Pearson correlation analysis was performed between the CCS and UACR at admission.

Predictors of UACR at each time point were first assessed using univariate analysis, followed by multivariate modelling using a stepwise forward-backward approach. A mixed linear regression model was used to evaluate the overall UACR trajectory during three phases (admission, discharge, and 2 weeks). The model was built with time as a fixed effect and each patient was included as a random effect to account for intra-subject correlation due to repeated measures across the three time points. Clinically relevant covariates were included as fixed effects based on the results of univariate and multivariate analyses.

All analyses were performed using a one-tailed hypothesis test, with a significance level of p < 0.05. Data were analysed using the statistical software Jamovi, version 2.3.21.0 and R Studio (version 4.4.2).

Results

Ninety-nine patients were included, with a mean age of 70.8 ± 12.2 years (range, 34–94 years) and 57% were male. The most common comorbidities were hypertension (70%), dyslipidaemia (57%), diabetes mellitus (44%), atrial fibrillation (43%), and chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 60 mL/min, 35%). Thirty-four percent of the patients had previous hospitalizations for HF. Regarding HF GDMT prior to admission, 45% had β-blockers, 42% angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB), 14% ARNI, 22% MRA, 35% SGLT2i, and 47% loop diuretics. The median CCS at admission was 5 (interquartile range [IQR] 4; 5).

The median LVEF was 40% (IQR 31; 55); the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) was 55 (IQR 48; 60); and the coupling interval between tricuspid annular excursion and systolic pulmonary artery pressure (PAPs) was 0.42 (IQR 0.28; 0.6). At admission median GFRe rate was 56 mL/min (IQR 36; 72); UACR, 71.4 mg/g (IQR 19.4; 212); NT-proBNP, 4,176 pg/mL (IQR 2585; 7,880); CA 125, 80 U/mL (IQR 38; 174); and N/L ratio 2.12 (IQR 1.62; 3.04).

Baseline Characteristics across UACR Categories

Patients were classified into three groups according to their UACR at admission: normoalbuminuria (grade 1, n = 34), microalbuminuria (grade 2, n = 45), and macroalbuminuria (grade 3, n = 20) (shown in Table 1). Higher UACR at admission was associated with more diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and higher previous UACR in the microalbuminuria and macroalbuminuria groups. A trend towards a higher prevalence of atrial fibrillation and chronic kidney disease were observed in grade 2–3. Clinical congestion score and mixed or right-sided congestive phenotypes were more frequent in grade 2 and 3. There were no significant differences in GDMT before HF admission, except for the higher use of ISGLT2 in grades 2 and 3.

UACR Trajectory before, during, and after Hospital Admission

Among the 86 patients with a UACR measurement within the year prior to the index admission, 59 (68.6%) had normoalbuminuria, 22 (25.6%) microalbuminuria, and five (5.8%) macroalbuminuria. At admission, the UACR worsened: 34 patients (34.3%) had normoalbuminuria, 45 (45.5%) microalbuminuria, and 20 (20.2%) macroalbuminuria. The clinical congestion score was positively correlated with higher UACR at admission (R2 = 0.254, p = 0.011).

At hospital discharge, following decongestion UACR improved: 49 (60.5%) patients had normoalbuminuria, 27 (33.3%) microalbuminuria and five (6.2%) macroalbuminuria. Two weeks after hospital discharge, 47 patients (55.3%) had normoalbuminuria, 28 microalbuminuria (32.9%) and ten macroalbuminuria (11.8%) (shown in Fig. 1a). There was a significant difference between the pre-admission reference value of UACR and the subsequent measurement time point. No significant differences were found between UACR at discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge (shown in Table 2).

NT-proBNP, CA 125, and Neutrophil-Lymphocyte Ratio Trajectory

After analysing the trajectory of NT-proBNP and CA 125, we observed a similar pattern to UACR. Both biomarkers were elevated on admission and reduced significantly at hospital discharge and 2 weeks later (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3a, b).

Variation in trajectory of NT-proBNP log (a), CA 125 log (b), and neutrophil/lymphocyte log ratio (c) at each time point.

We also evaluated the trend in the N/L ratio (Fig. 3c). After analysing the trajectory of the N/L ratio before admission, at admission, at hospital discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge, we observed a significant reduction in the N/L ratio (p < 0.001).

Factors Associated with Higher UACR on Admission, at Hospital Discharge and Two Weeks Post-Discharge

On hospital admission, the factors significantly associated with higher UACR were the presence of diabetes, lower eGFR, higher AST values, higher CA 125 levels, and lower vitamin D levels (shown in online suppl. material 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000551740). At hospital discharge, the factors significantly associated with higher UACR levels were having diabetes, lower eGFR, and lower urinary potassium levels. Low vitamin D levels and elevated alkaline phosphatase or ALT levels showed a non-significant trend towards higher UACR (shown in online suppl. material 2).

At 2 weeks post-discharge, a higher UACR was associated with the presence of diabetes, lower eGFR, and higher transaminase levels (AST and ALT). Patients with right or mixed type congestion tended to have higher albuminuria levels (shown in online suppl. material 3).

Overall Changes in UACR during Admission, Discharge, and Two Weeks Post-Discharge

A mixed linear regression model was used to evaluate the UACR changes across the three phases (admission, discharge, and two post-discharge) (shown in Table 3). The most relevant variables in the multivariate models were included: diabetes mellitus, eGFR, AST, ALT, urinary potassium, vitamin D and the biomarkers NT-proBNP, CA 125, and N/L ratio. Factors that best explained the trajectory of UACR during the three phases were the presence of diabetes mellitus, a lower eGFR, and higher AST levels, while NT-proBNP showed a trend towards statistical significance (p = 0.056).

UACR Trajectory according to GDMT and LVEF

Of the total population, patients were classified according to LVEF: 51.5% (n = 51) had HF with reduced ejection fraction, 15.2% (n = 15) had mildly reduced ejection fraction, and 33.3% (n = 33) had preserved ejection fraction.

To analyze the impact of early implementation of GDMT on UACR during AHF, we selected patients with LVEF < 50% (n = 66), excluding four who died during hospitalization. Of the remaining 62 patients, 46 were discharged on quadruple therapy (β-blocker, MRA, ARNI, and SGLT2 inhibitor).

In patients who did not receive quadruple therapy, UACR decreased from 97.2 mg/g at admission to 22.6 mg/g at discharge (a 76.7% reduction). In contrast, among those receiving optimal medical therapy, UACR decreased from 61.3 mg/g to 14.8 mg/g (a 75.9% reduction).

Albuminuria as a Possible Marker of Diuretic Resistance and Prognostic Factor

Despite the small sample size, during AHF admission, patients with higher albuminuria showed a greater proportion of thiazide, hypertonic saline, and acetazolamide use during hospitalization, as well as a trend towards more days of intravenous loop diuretic therapy. Thirteen patients died during hospitalization or in the early post-discharge phase (<30 days). The causes of death were HF (n = 8), other cardiovascular causes (n = 2), and non-cardiovascular causes (n = 3).

Patients who died during AHF admission had higher median UACR levels both prior to admission (25.6 vs. 12.1 mg/g; p = 0.060) and at admission (75.2 vs. 66.7 mg/g; p = 0.548), although these differences were not statistically significant. We conducted an exploratory analysis using a composite endpoint of in-hospital or early mortality and HF readmissions (<30 days) and observed that 14 out of 18 (77.7%) patients had either microalbuminuria or macroalbuminuria at admission.

Discussion

This study analyses biomarkers patterns, with a special focus on UACR, in patients with acute HF admitted to the cardiology department. Consistent with previous studies, the presence of albuminuria on admission (>30 mg/g) was common in this setting, reported in 82.6% of the TACRAHF cohort [12], 69% in a Japanese study [10] and 65.7% in our study. Our findings highlight the dynamic and transient nature of albuminuria increasing from a pre-admission outpatient reference value (with unknown congestion status), to higher levels at admission (“wet value,” during congestion), and decreasing again at discharge and 2 weeks post-discharge (“dry value”). This trajectory was similar to that observed for NT-proBNP and CA 125 [16, 17]. These findings support the potential role of albuminuria as a biomarker for monitoring congestion in HF patients.

This study complements previously published research on the trajectory of the UACR [10, 12]. Given its dynamic nature, UACR could serve as a biomarker for congestion, independent of the presence of diabetes, LVEF group or the prior use of MRA or SGLT2 inhibitors. One of the strengths of our study is that most patients had previous measurements available, allowing the establishment of the pre-admission reference value of UACR and enabling objective monitoring of decongestion.

This study explored whether early initiation of GDMT could influence UACR trajectories during AHF admission. In an exploratory analysis of patients with LVEF <50%, no short-term differences were observed between groups. These results suggest that rapid implementation of quadruple guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) during hospitalization was not clearly associated with the early trajectory of albuminuria, whereas effective decongestion could be associated with these changes. However, given the limited sample size and the observational design, these findings warrant cautious interpretation. Importantly, changes in UACR during hospitalization may not exclusively reflect variations in congestion status but could also be influenced by treatment intensity, including diuretic strategy and dosing, as well as the early initiation and up-titration of GDMT. Therefore, the observed UACR trajectory should be interpreted within the broader clinical context of the therapeutic interventions implemented during admission.

Another biomarker studied was the N/L ratio, which is known to play a role in systemic inflammation and is an HF prognostic marker [15]. Our findings suggest that N/L ratio may also be influenced by haemodynamic congestion and could serve as a supplementary marker for congestion monitoring. These results support the hypothesis that venous congestion contributes to inflammatory activation and that the N/L ratio may serve as a surrogate biomarker for this process. This observation is exploratory and further studies with a larger sample size are needed to understand the dynamics of the N/L ratio and its influence on congestion.

The pathophysiology of albuminuria is multifactorial and can be caused by damage to the glomerular filtration barrier, endothelial dysfunction, or tubular injury. The comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus or impaired glomerular filtration are directly associated with higher UACR, as observed in our study. The role of vitamin D in albuminuria is well established due to its involvement in the activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system, as well as in inflammatory processes and endothelial dysfunction [18]. Similarly, the direct influence of vitamin D levels on HF has been described. Low vitamin D levels have also been associated with a worse HF prognosis [19]. However, as suggested in our study, there could be a correlation between low vitamin D levels, higher UACR and the presence of congestion, although this hypothesis remains to be confirmed.

Another mechanism underlying albuminuria is the increase in central venous pressure and, consequently, renal pressure, which ultimately leads to congestive nephropathy with increased albuminuria [20, 21]. This haemodynamic mechanism has also been observed in congestive liver disease [22, 23]. In our study, higher transaminase levels were associated with higher overall UACR. Patients with higher clinical congestion scores showed a positive correlation with UACR at admission and the presence of a mixed or right-sided congestion phenotype predicted higher UACR levels 2 weeks post-discharge. Similarly, higher CA 125 levels were positively associated with UACR at hospital admission, while higher NT-proBNP levels explained higher albuminuria levels throughout the overall clinical course. These findings are consistent with those reported by other studies in both outpatient and inpatient settings [5, 12].

Despite the small sample size, patients with higher albuminuria appeared to exhibit greater diuretic resistance, as evidenced by the need for additional diuretics beyond loop diuretics. Given the observational design of the study, these findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Future prospective studies are needed to determine whether UACR levels may help to better characterize congestion profiles and optimize decongestion strategies.

In recent years, albuminuria has been suggested to play an important role in the HF prognosis. Its presence has been shown to markedly increase the HF risk, regardless of comorbidities. In the FIGARO-CKD and FIDELIO-CKD studies, finerenone reduced the composite combined renal endpoint, including albuminuria and as a secondary endpoint was associated with fewer HF events in a population with diabetic CKD [24, 25]. This indirectly suggests that albuminuria reduction could represent a potential therapeutic target in patients with HF. Our findings are consistent with the previous evidence suggesting that patients with higher UACR on admission may experience worse short-term outcomes during hospitalization and within the first 30 days after discharge. However, given the limited number of events in our cohort, these observations should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in larger studies as our study was not powered to formally assess prognosis differences.

Extended follow-up is warranted to evaluate the long-term progression of UACR and its potential renal and cardiac implications. Although our analysis focused on short-term changes in albuminuria, these variations may have important long-term implications. Whether the UACR trajectories observed here translate into renal or cardiovascular events warrants further investigation. In addition, the prognostic significance of elevated albuminuria at discharge or 2 weeks post-discharge, and its potential long-term cardiovascular and renal consequences, remains to be clarified.

The main limitations of our study are its small sample size and its single-centre design. Given its non-randomized design and baseline differences between groups, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of treatment effects, as residual confounding may have influenced the findings. In addition, long-term renal and cardiovascular outcomes were not assessed, some patients were lost to follow-up, and previous UACR reference values were not available for all patients. Congestion assessment relied mainly on a clinical congestion score, as objective techniques such as VEXUS, lung ultrasound or bioimpedance were not routinely performed. Accordingly, the relationship between UACR trajectory and congestion reflects clinically assessed rather than objectively measured haemodynamic congestion. Furthermore, patients with higher levels of albuminuria may have reflected a worse clinical status potentially limiting longitudinal analyses. Future multicenter studies incorporating propensity score adjustment or alternative causal inference strategies would be valuable to mitigate confounding and strengthen causal interpretation. Additionally, prospective studies are needed to determine whether albuminuria is merely a marker of disease severity or reflects a modifiable pathophysiological pathway in HF.

Conclusion

Albuminuria is highly prevalent among patients with acute HF. In this single-centre cohort, its dynamic and transient nature in relation to changes in congestion supports the hypothesis that it may represent a potential biomarker for assessing and monitoring congestion. However, given the limited sample size and short follow-up, these findings should be considered exploratory. Whether UACR represents a pathophysiological marker with therapeutic implications in HF warrants confirmation in larger prospective studies.
