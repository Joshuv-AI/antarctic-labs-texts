Impact of Guided Meditation on Heart Rate Variability and Psychological Well-being in Critically Ill Obstetric Patients – A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Rachit Jayeshkumar Patel, Hasmukh Shah, Jalpaba Dharmendrasinh Chudasama, Smruti Bhalendu Vaishnav, Purvi R. Patel, Abhishek M. Prajapati, Shany Thomas, Bharatkumar M. Prajapati, Bhalendu S. Vaishnav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Context:Critically ill obstetric patients face a unique confluence of life-threatening physiological complications and heightened psychological distress. This population is particularly vulnerable to autonomic nervous system dysregulation, impacting cardiovascular and emotional stability. Conventional medical interventions often focus primarily on pharmacological interventions, neglecting the significant role of psychological well-being in recovery.Aims:The aim of this study was to evaluate the impact of guided meditation (GM) on heart rate variability (HRV), hemodynamic parameters, and psychological well-being in critically ill obstetric patients.Settings and Design:A parallel-group randomized controlled trial conducted in an Indian critical care unit.Methodology:One hundred critically ill obstetric patients, excluding those with hemodynamic instability, arrhythmias, or requiring mechanical ventilation, were randomized into intervention (n = 50) and control groups. The intervention group received daily 20-min GM audio sessions for three consecutive days. The control group received standard care.Statistical Analysis Used:Within-group changes were analyzed using paired t-tests, and trends across days through repeated measures ANOVA.Results:Significant improvements were observed in HRV indices (standard deviation of normal-to-normal, root mean square of successive differences, NN50, PNN50, high-frequency, total power) in the GM group, indicating enhanced parasympathetic dominance. Significant reductions in pulse rate and blood pressure, and improvements in anxiety and well-being (Visual Analogue Scale scores) were also noted.Conclusion:GM demonstrates efficacy in improving autonomic health and psychological well-being in critically ill obstetric patients, highlighting the potential of integrating ancient Indian practices into modern critical care in this vulnerable population for promoting holistic recovery.

Introduction

Critically ill obstetric patients experience heightened physiological and psychological stress due to the burden of critical illness occurring in the unique physiological milieu of pregnancy-related homeostasis. This population is especially prone to autonomic nervous system (ANS) dysregulation and elevated anxiety, which can negatively impact maternal and fetal outcomes.[12] Heart rate variability (HRV) has emerged as a sensitive, noninvasive marker for assessing ANS function and cardiovascular regulation, making it a valuable tool in critical care research.[3]

Guided meditation (GM), a form of structured mind–body practice, has demonstrated beneficial effects on reducing psychological distress, enhancing parasympathetic tone, and improving HRV in various populations.[45] Evidence supports its role in lowering anxiety, stabilizing vital parameters, and improving overall psychological well-being. Despite this, there remains a notable gap in the literature regarding its use in obstetric critical care, where concerns about pharmacological interventions and their safety are particularly prominent.

This study addresses that gap by investigating the effects of GM on HRV and psychophysiological well-being in critically ill obstetric patients. The choice of this specific population is driven by their vulnerability to stress-induced dysautonomia and the need for safe, nonpharmacological interventions that can be feasibly implemented in high-dependency settings. By exploring the feasibility and efficacy of GM in this context, the study aims to contribute to a more holistic, patient-centred approach to obstetric critical care.

Methodology

Study setting

This randomized controlled trial was conducted in critically ill obstetric patients at a rural tertiary care teaching hospital in India between January 2023 and March 2024.

Study design

This was a parallel-group, randomized controlled trial.

Ethical approval and trial registration

The study received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC/BU/133/Faculty/15/73/2022) and was registered in the Central Trial Registry of India (CTRI/2022/11/047307).

Participants

Sample size determination

It was estimated using the primary outcome measure, SDNN (standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals in HRV). Using WINPEPI software (J.H. Abramson, Brixton Health, London, United Kingdom), with a 5% level of significance, 80% power, a pooled variance of 441, and a clinically important difference of 12 units, the calculated sample size was 98 participants (49 per group). To account for potential attrition, sample size was rounded up to 100 participants (50 per group).

Inclusion criteria

The study included critically ill obstetric patients (either antenatal or postnatal) who were conscious and not on mechanical ventilation at the time of enrolment and throughout the study period. Recruitment

The eligible patients were identified by intensive care unit (ICU) staff and approached by a research assistant after obtaining informed consent.

Randomization and allocation

Computer-based randomization was performed using WINPEPI software. To maintain allocation concealment, group assignments were placed in opaque, sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes. These envelopes were opened in order of participant enrollment to ensure unbiased allocation.

Intervention

The intervention group received a 20-min GM audio session once daily for three consecutive days, in addition to standard ICU care. The sessions were delivered through headphones or earphones in a calm ICU setting with minimal disturbances. Patients were placed in a comfortable semi-recumbent or supine position, depending on their clinical condition and tolerance, with support provided to maintain relaxation (e.g., pillows, adjustable bed positioning). They were instructed, where feasible, to close their eyes and focus on the voice guidance involving breath awareness, body relaxation, and visualization. The control group received standard ICU care without any additional intervention.

Guided meditation protocol

The audio recording was prepared by the principal investigator, who has extensive experience in conducting similar sessions and has published research on GM in indexed journals. The GM script focused on in-gathering awareness, constructing a resolve for self-recovery, rotation of consciousness, breath awareness, visualization, and imagery, which can foster recovery and healing.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the intervention, participants were not blinded. However, the outcome assessor and the statistician were blinded to group assignment.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation, median, range (minimum and maximum values), frequencies, and percentages. Within-group differences in HRV parameters, VAS scores, and physiological measures before and after the intervention were analyzed using paired t-tests. Between-group comparisons of mean values for the intervention and control groups were conducted using independent t-tests. Trends across the 3-day period were evaluated using repeated measures ANOVA. Statistical analyses were performed using STATA 14.2 software (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA), with a P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy were the most common (53%), followed by obstetric hemorrhage (25%) [Figure 1]. Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) scores were comparable between control (15.64) and intervention (16.36) groups (P = 0.66), indicating similar disease severity. All participants completed GM sessions, with no adverse events reported. Participant flow is shown in Figure 2.

Effects on heart rate variability parameters

The intervention group showed statistically significant improvements in HRV measures over 3 days: SDNN (P < 0.001), root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) (P < 0.05), and total power (P < 0.001), whereas the control group showed declines in SDNN and total power [Tables 1 and 2]. Repeated measures ANOVA revealed statistically significant increases in SDNN, RMSSD, total power, low frequency (LF) (P < 0.001), and high-frequency (HF) (P < 0.05) over the 3-day period [Figure 3]. From baseline (preday 1) to end of study (postday 3), the intervention group had statistically significant improvements in HRV parameters: SDNN (38.11–57.45; P < 0.001), RMSSD (33.39–40.33; P < 0.001), total power (319.33–812.02; P < 0.001), NN50 (39.94–49.72; P = 0.01), LF (75.76–144.09; P < 0.001), and HF (90.85–123.31; P = 0.04) [Tables 3 and 4]. Variation in some baseline autonomic parameters was observed as mentioned in tables [Tables 3 and 4], however, the clinical severity of illness, as measured by the APACHE score on admission, was comparable between the intervention and control groups, indicating baseline homogeneity in overall clinical status. This supports the internal validity of group comparisons in our randomized controlled trial.

Effects on psychological parameters

The intervention group showed significant improvements in VAS anxiety and well-being scores across 3 days (P < 0.001), unlike the control group [Tables 9 and 10]. Repeated measures ANOVA revealed a statistically significant increase in VAS anxiety and VAS well-being (P < 0.001) over the 3-day study period [Figure 5]. From baseline (preday 1) to end of study (postday 3), VAS anxiety dropped from 69 to 22.7, and well-being increased from 43.2 to 80.8 [P < 0.001; Table 11]. VAS Anxiety and VAS Well-being scales (0–100 mm horizontal lines) were used to assess anxiety and well-being, respectively. Test–retest reliability of the VAS scales was assessed using preintervention scores collected over three consecutive days in the control group. Intraclass Correlation Coefficients indicated strong reliability for both scales: VAS Anxiety (intraclass correlation coefficient [ICC] = 0.85) and VAS well-being (ICC = 0.80), suggesting good to excellent temporal stability. Reliability was not evaluated in the intervention group, as expected changes from GM would reflect true effects rather than measurement error.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates that a structured 3-day GM intervention in critically ill obstetric patients significantly improved HRV parameters, reduced anxiety, and enhanced psychological well-being. These improvements underscore the power of meditative practices, rooted in classical yoga, in restoring autonomic balance and promoting emotional resilience even in high-stress environments like the ICU. Our findings broadly align with previous studies on mind–body interventions; however, simultaneous documentation of both physiological and psychological benefits of GM in critically ill obstetric patients has not been reported in the literature.

HRV analysis provides critical insight into autonomic function in critical illness. It has been used as a prognostic marker in conditions such as multiple organ dysfunction syndrome, sepsis, and trauma.[6789] Continuous monitoring of HRV has been identified as a reliable indicator of recovery in critically ill pediatric patients.[10] A study found that a reduction in the LF component of 24-h HRV predicted mortality with an accuracy of 74%, a specificity of 81.2%, and a sensitivity of 46.7%.[11]

Effect of guided meditation on heart rate variability parameters

There was significant increases in both SDNN and RMSSD, indicating enhanced parasympathetic modulation due to short term intervention in Yoga naive population. In a study of OM meditation in experienced/nonexperienced Yoga practitioners, it was observed that yoga practitioners demonstrated a significant increase in HF Power.[12] In a review of 12 studies on the effects of Yoga and meditation on HRV, 8 studies reported beneficial effects in HRV.[13] Although many other studies support the utility of HRV for demonstrating the effects of mindfulness-based intervention, high variability in methods precludes generalization.[14] HRV is regulated over short time intervals by interactions among the autonomic, cardiovascular, central nervous, endocrine, and respiratory systems, along with baroreceptor and chemoreceptor activity. These physiological mechanisms contribute to the generation of very low to HF components within the HRV spectrum.[15]

While SDNN reflects both sympathetic and parasympathetic influences, in short-term recordings, it is primarily influenced by parasympathetic activity due to the dominance of faster, HF changes driven by the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS). An increase in SDNN suggests a shift towards enhanced vagal tone. Increase in RMSSD, a metric specifically reflecting short-term vagal modulation, supports heightened parasympathetic activity. These findings align with recommendations that RMSSD and SDNN are the most appropriate metrics for assessing PNS activity in short-term HRV analysis. Increases in NN50 and pNN50 further corroborated improved autonomic function.

These physiological changes resonate with the traditional yogic understanding that practices such as dhyana (meditation), pranayama (breath regulation), and mantra chanting can balance prana and calm the nervous system by enhancing parasympathetic activity. Techniques such as Anapanasati and Sudarshan Kriya have previously been shown to elevate vagal tone and HRV in both healthy individuals and those under stress.[1617]

The observed increases in both LF and HF power warrant further discussion. While HF power is traditionally associated with parasympathetic activity, LF power reflects both sympathetic and parasympathetic influences. The concurrent increase in both suggests a balanced autonomic response, rather than simply an isolated increase in parasympathetic tone. This balanced response is crucial for cardiovascular health, reflecting the body’s ability to effectively engage both branches of the ANS. It implies improved cardiac responsiveness and enhanced autonomic flexibility, particularly relevant in the stressful ICU environment. Importantly, no significant changes were observed in the mean RR or the LF/HF ratio, suggesting that GM specifically enhanced vagal activity without significantly altering the overall sympathovagal balance. These results are consistent with prior research demonstrating the beneficial effects of meditation on vagal tone and autonomic balance.[1819]

Comparison of baseline and day 3 post-GM data revealed sustained HRV improvements in the intervention group, while the control group experienced declines, notably in SDNN and total power. This highlights the potential for stress-related deterioration in HRV in critically ill patients in the absence of interventions like GM during ICU stay. These findings reiterate evidence that meditation practices embedded in classical yoga philosophy are effective in shifting autonomic balance toward parasympathetic dominance.[2021]

Effect of guided meditation on vital parameters

Reductions in HR, systolic and DBP, and RR following each GM session were sustained over the 3 days, highlighting the physiological benefits of GM. These findings support the growing body of evidence indicating that GM can positively influence autonomic balance, physiological stress reduction, and overall cardiovascular and respiratory function.[22] The reduction in SBP and DBP across the 3 days suggests decreased vascular resistance and overall blood pressure, consistent with findings on the effects of meditation on hypertension. The reduction in RR across all 3 days further aligns with reports suggesting that GM promotes relaxation and reduces metabolic demand.[2324] In contrast, the control group showed increases in HR and SBP, indicating potential autonomic deterioration in the absence of intervention.

The type and dosage schedule of antihypertensive medication use were balanced between the control and intervention groups, with no significant difference (P = 0.835). To minimize medication effects on HRV, GM sessions were scheduled with at least a 2-h interval after antihypertensive administration. The intervention lasted 20 min. The pre- and postintervention observations for this short duration further minimized, but did not completely obviate, the confounding effect of antihypertensives.

Effect of guided meditation on anxiety and well-being

The findings of decreased VAS Anxiety scores are consistent with the reported psychological benefits of meditation and its effects on physiological markers of stress.[425] Improvement in well-being, as indicated by the VAS well-being scores, supports the growing body of literature suggesting that meditation, rooted in yogic traditions, is effective in reducing anxiety, enhancing affect, and improving emotional regulation.[26] These results suggest that GM not only reduces negative psychological states such as anxiety but also enhances positive aspects of mental health, contributing to overall well-being. This aligns with studies indicating positive effects of meditation on emotional regulation and affect.[2728] The control group exhibited minimal changes in well-being across the 3 days. This contrast highlights the efficacy of GM in enhancing well-being, which may be due to its ability to cultivate mindfulness, reduce rumination, and promote a sense of calm and contentment. The outcomes of this study within obstetric ICU are in consonance with research in non-ICU areas, which has consistently highlighted the diverse benefits of meditation and mindfulness practices on both physiological and psychological health.[29]

This study has some limitations. The single-blind design may have introduced some bias, although efforts were made to minimize this by blinding the outcome assessor and statistician. The short duration of the intervention (3 days) limits our ability to assess the long-term effects of GM. Future studies should explore the effects of optimal duration and frequency of GM interventions. A potential limitation is the possible confounding effect of antihypertensive medications on HRV. Future research should explore the interaction between antihypertensive therapy and HRV in critically ill obstetric patients. We also did not assess other potentially relevant psychological outcomes, such as depression, sleep quality, and posttraumatic stress disorder. In addition, as participants were yoga-naïve, the degree to which they were able to actively engage with or perform the guided instructions – such as mindful breathing and visualization – may have varied. This introduces a potential performance bias, as passive listening may function more as a distraction technique than as an active meditative practice.

The 100% retention rate and absence of adverse events in this study further underscore the feasibility, safety, and high acceptability of GM in this vulnerable population.

Conclusion

GM offers a safe, noninvasive way to support ANS balance in critically ill obstetric patients, enhancing vagal tone, reducing stress responses, and improving psychological well-being. Its high acceptability and ease of use make it a feasible addition to ICU care. Through scientific documentation of benefits of GM, the study contributes in efforts to integrate ancient practices into modern medicine for achieving a more holistic, patient-centred approach.
