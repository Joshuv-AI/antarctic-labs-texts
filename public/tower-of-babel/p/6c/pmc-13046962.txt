Enhanced crystallinity, hardness and thermal stability of PEEK/TC4 composites for biomedical applications
Authors: Bakytzhan Sariyev, HJeevan Rao, Assylbek Ozhiken, Kassymbek Ozhikenov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPolyether-ether-ketone (PEEK) is widely recognized as a high-performance polymer for biomedical and structural applications, yet its limited hardness and moderate thermal resistance often restrict its use under demanding service conditions. This study presents the development of PEEK/Ti-6Al-4 V (TC4) composites processed via centrifugal powder compaction followed by vacuum sintering. Three compositions PT(20), PT(30), and PT(40), containing 20%, 30%, and 40% TC4 by weight were systematically investigated. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed homogeneous dispersion of TC4 particles in the PEEK matrix with localized interfacial bonding, as required for ensuring mechanical integrity during cyclic stressing conditions. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) showed that TC4 particles function as heterogeneous nucleating agents, which result in the enhancement of crystallinity from 41% in neat PEEK to 48% in the PT(40) composite, without significantly affecting the melting temperature. Moreover, X-ray Diffraction (XRD) supports DSC results in that metal fillers enhance the crystallization of polymer matrix. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) demonstrated improved thermal stability, with PT(40) composite retaining 75% weight at 800 °C compared to 53% of neat PEEK, which indicates suitability in temperature changing environments. Microhardness measurements indicated rising values with TC4 content, reaching a 35% improvement in PT(40). These findings demonstrate that the applied processing method enables property-tailored PEEK/TC4 composites suitable for thermally stable, mechanically robust structures demonstrating potential for demanding structural or biomedical applications.Keywords: PEEK, TC4, Crystallinity, Microhardness, Biomaterials, Powder metallurgySubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

The development of advanced materials for prosthetic applications is a frontier in contemporary medicine, driven by the increasing demand for materials with mechanical strength, thermal stability, and biological acceptance1. The challenges of contemporary orthopedic, spinal, and dental implant surgeries highlight the necessity for materials capable of resisting physiological stresses and permitting osseointegration to minimize adverse biological responses2–4. Traditional metallic prostheses, being mechanically stable, suffer from stress shielding due to elastic modulus mismatch, whereas polymeric devices are limited by insufficient wear resistance and long-term structural stability5,6. These conditions are prevalent in areas with extreme climate (hot or cold), where prosthetic materials may degrade, losing mechanical resistance and structural integrity. Therefore, there is a critical need for hybrid materials that encompass the benefits of both polymers and metals to prevent these drawbacks7.

PEEK, with its semi-crystalline, high-performance structure, has been targeted for potential use in biomaterials because it possesses very good chemical inertness, radiolucency, and an elastic modulus that approximates that of human cortical bone7,8. Such properties are believed to mitigate stress shielding effects, a prevalent issue with conventional metallic implants9,10. The thermal stability of PEEK, with its melting point above 343 °C, further guarantees dimensional stability upon sterilization and in vivo environments. Clinically, PEEK finds broad application in spinal fusion cages, trauma fixation devices, and dental prostheses11–16. Despite its advantages, neat PEEK exhibits notable drawbacks in load-bearing applications, including suboptimal hardness (20–25 HV), limited wear resistance, and bioinert surfaces that restrain osseointegration17–19. These shortcomings restrict its use in applications requiring prolonged mechanical endurance, such as joint replacements or dental implants subjected to cyclic masticatory forces20–22. Furthermore, PEEK’s low surface energy hinders cellular adhesion, often necessitating surface modifications or composite reinforcement to enhance bioactivity19. Such challenges have led researchers to explore multifunctional PEEK-based composites capable of addressing both mechanical and biological deficiencies23–25.

To overcome these limitations, researchers have explored composite strategies incorporating metallic particles, ceramics, or carbon-based reinforcements into the PEEK matrix26–31. Metallic fillers constitute a less studied but potentially impactful approach for enhancing the mechanical and thermal performance of PEEK-based composites. The addition of metals like aluminum, titanium, and cobalt-chromium can improve the structural, thermal stability and mechanical hardness of the polymer matrix32–36. Zaaba et al.37 presented an extensive review of metal-filled polymer systems and their suitability for engineering applications. Goyal et al.34 stated that addition of ceramic particles in PEEK drastically improves its thermal and mechanical properties, while Ochoa- Putman et al.38 highlighted the central role played by interfacial interactions in defining the overall efficacy of polymer-metal composites. For PEEK, Thiruchitrambalam et al.39 examined new developments in metal-reinforced PEEK composites, emphasizing the difficulty of processing as well as potential uses of the material in industrial and biomedical applications. Addition of inorganic fillers has been reported to significantly affect thermal behavior of PEEK40. Kuo et al.41 for example, examined thermal behavior of PEEK/ceramic composites and determined that the incorporation of thermally stable ceramic fillers improved heat resistance of the composite. Similarly, Doumeng et al.28 found that the crystallization behavior of PEEK was influenced by metal fillers and metal particles served as heterogeneous nucleation sites for the formation of crystalline phase.

Among metallic reinforcements, TC4 stands out due to its established biocompatibility, corrosion resistance, and superior mechanical properties42,43. Despite these advantages, TC4 in particular has not been extensively explored as a reinforcement in PEEK-based composites, and existing studies remain limited compared to ceramic- or carbon-filled systems. For instance, PEEK/TiO₂ composites demonstrate enhanced Young’s modulus and osteoconductivity, while Ti particles improve wear resistance and interfacial bonding44. Ti alloy ability to form direct bone apposition critical for osseointegration has been demonstrated in spinal cages and dental implants, where exposed Ti particles within PEEK matrices promote osteoblast adhesion and mineralization44–46. Additionally, titanium high thermal stability ensures minimal degradation during composite processing, preserving the structural integrity of the polymer matrix47.

The fabrication of PEEK/metal composites presents significant challenges due to the substantial density difference between the polymer matrix and metallic fillers, which often leads to inhomogeneous particle distribution. Conventional processing methods such as melt compounding, compression and injection molding frequently result in particle agglomeration and poor interfacial adhesion39. Kucherenko et al.48 discussed various processing techniques, highlighting that conventional fabrication methods often lead to agglomeration and non-uniform dispersion of metal fillers. Similarly, Eessaa et al.49 demonstrated that powder metallurgy approaches offered advantages in controlling the microstructure of polymer/metal composites compared to traditional melt-processing techniques. More advanced manufacturing methods have also been explored, with Chen et al.50 utilizing powder bed fusion and vacuum infiltration to fabricate PEEK/Ti composites with complex geometries for customized implant applications. Their results indicated that powder based manufactured composites exhibited promising mechanical properties, though processing parameters required careful optimization to minimize thermal degradation of the polymer matrix. To address these processing challenges, the centrifugal powder compaction technique offers a promising route for achieving improved dispersion of metallic fillers within the polymer matrix. By applying centrifugal forces prior to sintering, this method facilitates uniform particle distribution, thereby enhancing mechanical properties and minimizing void formation36,51. To overcome these dispersion challenges, we employed a centrifugal powder compaction technique as a novel processing route. By applying 3000 g of centrifugal force to the PEEK/TC4 powder mixture prior to vacuum sintering, we achieved a uniform filler distribution with minimal agglomeration, even at 40 wt% metal content. This method distinguishes our work from prior PEEK/metal composite studies that used conventional melt blending or compression molding.

In this paper, we propose to study the microstructure, thermal stability, crystallinity and hardness properties of PEEK/TC4 composites fabricated via powder-based centrifugal compaction followed by vacuum sintering. Three compositions PT(20), PT(30), and PT(40) containing 20%, 30%, and 40% TC4 by weight, respectively, were prepared and analyzed using SEM, EDS, TGA, DSC, XRD and microhardness measurement. By focusing on a powder based PEEK/TC4 system, this work addresses a critical research gap concerning the thermal and mechanical performance of titanium reinforced PEEK composites. The findings offer valuable insights into the design of high-performance polymer-metal hybrids for advanced structural and biomedical applications.

Experimental

Materials

PEEK powder (Sigma-Aldrich, EK306010) and TC4 alloy (Stanford Advanced Materials, Ti-6Al-4 V spherical powder), were used in this study. According to the manufacturer’s specification, the TC4 powder contains 6 wt% Al and 4 wt% V, with the remainder being Ti. The datasheet reports a density of 4.43 g/cm3 and a Vickers hardness in the range of 330–350 HV, consistent with the alloy’s established high strength, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility.

The particle size distribution (PSD) of both powders was characterized using laser diffraction (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern Panalytical, UK) equipped with an Aero S dry disperser operating at 1 bar. The PEEK powder exhibited a median particle diameter of approximately 50 µm. In contrast, the TC4 powder showed a broader PSD, with percentile values of D10 = 54 µm, D50 = 78 µm, and D90 = 110 µm, indicating substantial variation in particle size. These percentiles represent the particle diameters below which 10%, 50%, and 90% of the cumulative particle volume lies, respectively. Composite fabrication

PEEK and TC4 powders were weighed and mixed in proportions to yield 20%, 30%, and 40% by weight of TC4. Both PEEK and TC4 powders were pre-dried in vacuum at 100 °C for 4 h prior to mixing to remove surface moisture and volatile contaminants. Table 1 presents the weight and volume fractions of PEEK and PEEK/TC4 composites. The ready powder compound was placed into the metal molds and loaded into the centrifuge [Rotina 380R, Germany]. The molds were tilted along the horizontal axis of the centrifugal force under 3000 g for 180 min. These powder mixtures were subjected to centrifugation, which induces repetitive dispersion and homogeneous mixing of the powder bed. After mixing, the compacted green body was sintered in a vacuum furnace. The samples were gradually heated to 370 °C and held for 60 min at reached temperature stage to provide full melting of the PEEK matrix and encapsulation the metal particles. After that, the temperature was slowly reduced to avoid residual stress development. The fabricated composites were extracted and sliced into sample pieces for the further analyses.

Characterization methods

The sintered composites were cross-sectioned for analysis using a wet abrasive cut-off machine (Secotom-15, Denmark). After sectioning, samples were polished with silicon carbide papers (800, 1000, 1200 and 2000 grit), and finally by suspensions of 1 µm, 0.3 µm (Al2O3).

Microstructural examinations were performed using a SEM (ThermoFisher Axia, USA) equipped with an EDS detector, operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of 8–10 mm in secondary electron mode. A thin 5 nm Au coating was applied to all samples to ensure adequate surface conductivity. Imaging was conducted under high-vacuum conditions with magnifications ranging from × 50 to × 1200, depending on the feature scale of interest. All SEM parameters were kept consistent across measurements to ensure full repeatability.

Investigation of thermal stability of PEEK and its composites was carried out by TGA (STA 449 Netzsch, Germany) under a N2 atmosphere, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. The sample was heated from 30 to 1000 °C, followed by an isothermal stage for 90 min.

DSC was conducted by thermal analyzer (Caliris Supreme 300 Netzsch, Germany) to evaluate the melting characteristics and crystallinity of the materials. The fabricated composites were cut into small cubes of about 10 mg weight and thermally cycled from 30 to 500 °C constant heating rate of 10 °C /min in a N2 atmosphere.

Microhardness by Vickers was measured on the polished specimens using a microhardness tester (Shimadzu HMV-G31). A load of 10 N was applied for an indentation dwell time of 10 s. For each sample of PEEK and PEEK/TC4 specimens of 10 indents were made in different regions avoiding large metal particles right under the indent. The diagonal lengths of each indent were measured to calculate Vickers hardness (HV). The results were averaged and standard deviations provided.

XRD analysis was performed at room temperature using a SmartLab SE X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Japan) equipped with CuKα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The scans were carried out over a 2θ range of 10–70°, with a total scanning time of 90 min. The X-ray tube was operated at 45 kV and 40 mA.

Results and discussion

Composite interfacial characteristics

SEM imaging was conducted to evaluate the dispersion quality of TC4 particles within the PEEK matrix and to identify microstructural features such as agglomeration and interfacial bonding that directly influence the mechanical and thermal behavior of the composites. Figure 2 presents SEM micrographs of neat PEEK and PT(20), PT(30) and PT(40) composites. The hollows observed in the micrographs result from particle detachment during sample preparation.

The microstructure of a neat (a) PEEK, (b) PT(20), (c) PT(30) and (d) PT(40) composites.

The neat PEEK specimen (Fig. 2a) exhibits a homogeneous and relatively featureless morphology characteristic of thermoplastic polymers. Upon incorporation of TC4 particles, significant microstructural changes were observed across the compositional range. In PT(20) (Fig. 2b), spherical TC4 particles with diameters ranging from approximately 5–50 μm appear well-distributed throughout the PEEK matrix with minimal agglomeration.

As the TC4 content increases in PT(30) (Fig. 2c), a marked increase in particle density is observed, with interparticle distances noticeably reduced compared to PT(20) sample. The dimensional distribution of particles remains relatively consistent, indicating that the centrifugal compaction process prevents significant particle agglomeration even at higher filler content. In PT(40) (Fig. 2d), the highest particle density is observed with some evidence of particle–particle contact, suggesting the approach of a percolation threshold where continuous networks of TC4 particles begin to form within the PEEK matrix52.

The observed preservation of TC4 particle sphericity across all composites suggests that the processing conditions were well controlled, avoiding significant fragmentation of the metallic reinforcement phase. This morphological consistency is critical for minimizing local stress concentrations and maintaining predictable mechanical and thermal properties in the final composite28.

EDS analysis (Fig. 3) verified the presence of titanium, aluminum, and vanadium, the constituent elements of the TC4 alloy, within the PEEK matrix, confirming the incorporation of the metal phase. The elemental maps indicated a generally even distribution of these elements, with some localized areas showing higher concentrations corresponding to the TC4 particles observed in the SEM.

The EDS images of PT(40) composite.

A detailed examination of the regions where individual particles have detached from the matrix reveals distinct adhesion zones between the particle surfaces and the surrounding PEEK matrix, as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 4. These adhesion regions manifest as areas of intimate contact between the polymer and the particle, suggesting the existence of localized interfacial bonding. Notably, the presence of air entrapment is observed in the form of discrete voids at the particle–matrix interface. These voids, typically ranging from 1 to 3 μm in diameter, are presumed to result from incomplete wetting during the consolidation process. Although overall particle dispersion was uniform, the presence of interfacial voids may locally affect stress distribution under mechanical loading.

The microstructure of PEEK/TC4 composites interface.

The observed microstructural features, particularly the interfacial adhesion zones, air entrapment may influence the mechanical properties of the composites. The regions of good adhesion suggest effective load transfer between the matrix and reinforcement, while the entrapped air may serve as crack initiation sites under stress53. Additionally, the particle agglomerates observed at higher TC4 loadings could potentially create localized regions of stress concentration, influencing the overall mechanical response of the composite54.

Thermogravimetric analysis

TGA was employed to examine how increasing TC4 content influences the thermal degradation behavior of PEEK, providing insight into composite stability under elevated-temperature environments. The TGA curves and corresponding results are presented in Fig. 5 and Table 2, respectively. As depicted, neat PEEK and PEEK/TC4 composites begin to exhibit weight loss after approximately 500 °C, following a trend typical for high-performance thermoplastics55. The maximum rate of weight loss occurs in the range of 550–600 °C. Subsequently, the rate of degradation decelerates and approaches zero near 700 °C.

Notably, all samples initiate thermal decomposition at around 567–571 °C, a behavior attributed to the cleavage of the aromatic polymer backbone of PEEK. This degradation process is driven by conformational changes in the matrix structure and thermal accumulation, resulting in radical formation that marks the onset of polymer chain scission. As the temperature exceeds 550 °C, molecular motion becomes more intense, accelerating the breakdown of ether and ketone linkages. These interactions facilitate radical propagation and the release of volatile degradation products such as CO, CO₂, and phenolic compounds56.

A pronounced mass loss occurred between 550 and 600 °C, consistent with the formation of low molecular weight gaseous products57. Beyond 600 °C, the mass loss slows as the system transitions into a regime dominated by the carbonization of residual organic content and the accumulation of thermally stable by-products. This plateau is most pronounced in the TC4-containing composites, where the metallic phase does not undergo thermal degradation and remains as residue28.

The presence of TC4 fillers significantly suppresses the extent of mass loss during decomposition. Compared to neat PEEK, which retains 61% and 53% of its mass at 600 °C and 800 °C, respectively, the composites demonstrate enhanced thermal stability, where PT(20), PT(30), and PT(40) show higher residual weights, with PT(40) retaining 77% at 600 °C and 75% at 800 °C. These results indicate that the inclusion of thermally inert TC4 particles effectively inhibits polymer degradation and promotes char residue formation58.

Therefore, from the perspective of mass loss behavior, the decomposition process can be segmented into four stages: 25–500 °C represents the non-decomposition stage, 500–620 °C denotes the rapid decomposition stage, 620–820 °C corresponds to the slow decomposition stage, and 820–1000 °C defines the stable plateau associated with residuals59.

Differential scanning calorimetry

DSC analysis was performed to quantify the effect of TC4 addition on the crystallization behavior of PEEK. The DSC thermograms for neat PEEK and PEEK/TC4 composites are presented in Fig. 6 and the corresponding thermal parameters are summarized in Table 3. The glass transition temperature of pure PEEK is consistently observed in the range of 160–165 °C. The addition of titanium does not significantly alter this value, indicating that the amorphous phase dynamics remain largely unchanged. All samples display the typical melting behavior of semi-crystalline PEEK, characterized by a broad melting range and a distinct endothermic peak centered near 342 °C. The melting range for all specimens spans approximately 320–370 °C, with minor variations across the composite series. This is consistent with the thermal behavior of PEEK, where varying crystal sizes and degrees of perfection contribute to a wide melting interval60.

The melting peak temperature (Tm) remains relatively stable, ranging from 342 °C for pure PEEK to 343 °C for the composite containing 40 wt% TC4. This negligible shift suggests that Ti incorporation does not significantly alter the crystalline phase transition temperature of the PEEK matrix. The melting point of semi-crystalline polymers is primarily governed by lamellar thickness and crystal perfection, rather than by overall crystallinity. Since these structural parameters were not significantly modified by the presence of TC4, the melting temperature remained stable despite variations in crystalline fraction. However, the enthalpy of fusion increases progressively with filler content, from 53 J/g for neat PEEK to 63 J/g for the PT(40) composite. The degree of crystallinity shows a clear upward trend, increasing from 41 to 48% as TC4 content rises.

This enhancement in crystallinity is attributed to the heterogeneous nucleation effect by the titanium fillers. Ti particles act as nucleating agents, reducing the energy barrier for crystallization and promoting more uniform and accelerated crystal growth27,61. The increase in crystallinity correlates with the observed sharpening of the DSC melting peaks, especially in the PT(30) and PT(40) composites, indicating a more ordered and thermally stable crystalline structure.

It is important to note that the crystallinity values were calculated based on the actual polymer content in each composite, as accurate determination of the matrix fraction. Although DSC is a reliable method for assessing bulk crystallinity, it is often beneficial to combine it with additional techniques such as Raman spectroscopy and X-Ray diffraction, which can provide more insights into the crystalline lattice structure, particularly at the surface. Literature reports suggest that DSC may overestimate the degree of crystallinity in PEEK due to overlapping thermal transitions, while XRD may underestimate it because of interference from the amorphous halo. Raman spectroscopy, on the other hand, delivers localized structural information and frequently shows strong correlation with bulk methods62. Incorporating XRD or Raman analysis together with DSC in future investigations would allow a more comprehensive evaluation of crystallinity in PEEK/TC4 composites.

XRD analysis

XRD analysis was performed to obtain the data about crystalline structure and phase composition of neat PEEK and PEEK/TC4 composites. The diffraction patterns are shown in Fig. 7. Neat PEEK exhibits characteristic semi-crystalline behavior, with well-defined diffraction peaks located at 2θ ≈ 18.7°, 20.8°, 22.6°, and 28.7°, corresponding to the (110), (111), (200), and (211) planes, respectively. These peaks confirm the presence of an ordered orthorhombic crystal structure typical of PEEK63. The prominent peaks observed at 2θ ≈ 35.1°, 38.4°, 40.2°, and 53.5° correspond to the (102), (110), (103), and (112) planes of α-titanium64. The intensity of these Ti-related peaks increases with higher filler content, confirming the successful integration and crystallinity of the metal phase within the polymer matrix.

The XRD graph of neat PEEK, PT(20), PT(30) and PT(40) composites.

Meanwhile, the PEEK diffraction peaks remain visible across all composite samples, although their relative intensity gradually decreases with increasing Ti content. This is primarily due to the reduced PEEK fraction in the composite and the stronger scattering from the denser Ti phase. The absence of significant peak broadening or amorphization suggests that the Ti-based fillers do not disrupt the crystallinity of the PEEK matrix65.

In summary, the XRD results confirm the presence of crystalline phases of α-Ti and the semi-crystalline PEEK matrix. The coexistence of these phases, along with enhanced peak sharpness at higher Ti contents, supports the DSC results that Ti fillers promote nucleation and crystallization within the polymer matrix.

Microhardness analysis

Microhardness testing was conducted to assess the reinforcing effect of TC4 particles on the resistance of the PEEK matrix to localized plastic deformation, thereby providing an indirect indicator of potential improvements in bulk mechanical performance. Figure 8 illustrates the results of microhardness measurement of PEEK and its composite. The microhardness enhanced progressively from 26.5 HV for pure PEEK to 31.7 HV, 32.0 HV, and 35.8 HV for PT(20), PT(30), and PT(40), respectively. The initial incorporation of titanium particles at PT(20) produced the most significant proportional improvement (19.6%), while the transition from PT(20) to PT(30) yielded only a marginal increase in 1%. However, a more substantial 35% improvement was observed when titanium content was further increased in PT(40). The observed increase in hardness, approximately 35% in PT(40) aligns with prior studies in which PEEK composites filled with 40% ceramic particles Al2O3 demonstrated microhardness improvements in 35% increase66. Notably, PEEK reinforced with 10 wt% SiC nanoparticles exhibited hardness improvements of up to 25% in comparison with a neat PEEK30.

Microhardness of neat PEEK, PT(20), PT(30) and PT(40) composites.

To quantitatively assess this structure–property relationship, a linear regression was performed using the experimental data for neat PEEK and the PEEK/TC4 composites. The resulting linear model exhibited a strong positive correlation (R2 = 0.98), indicating that increases in crystallinity are closely associated with corresponding increases in microhardness. This statistically significant trend supports the interpretation that TC4-induced heterogeneous nucleation contributes directly to the observed hardness enhancement. In addition to this crystallinity-driven effect, the increase in microhardness also arises from the high intrinsic hardness of TC4 particles, which restrict plastic deformation in the surrounding PEEK matrix. As the TC4 content rises, the interparticle distance decreases, and the particles collectively constrain the deformation zone beneath the indenter36,66. DSC and XRD analyses further confirm that titanium promotes higher crystallinity in PEEK, increasing the fraction of rigid crystalline regions and thereby strengthening resistance to indentation. The relatively uniform dispersion of TC4 throughout the matrix amplifies these effects by minimizing weak regions and reducing local deformation. Table 4 presents a comparison between experimental hardness values and those predicted by the Halpin–Tsai model. The experimental results demonstrate excellent agreement with theoretical predictions across all titanium content levels. For PT(20), the experimental hardness of approximately 29 HV corresponds closely with the predicted value of 29.8 HV, exhibiting a deviation of only 2.8%. Similarly, PT(30) demonstrates an experimental hardness of approximately 32.2 HV compared to the predicted 32.7 HV (2.2% difference), while PT(40) exhibits experimental values of approximately 35.8 HV versus the predicted 35.2 HV (2.3% difference). This correspondence validates the applicability of the Halpin–Tsai model for predicting hardness behavior in PEEK/TC4 composites within the studied compositional range66. Although tensile and flexural tests were not performed in this study, the increase in microhardness with TC4 content is a strong indicator of improved resistance to plastic deformation. Similar PEEK/Ti composites have shown higher bulk strength, where increasing Ti content enhanced compressive stiffness and strength while preserving ductile fracture behavior66. Therefore, we expect that our composites would exhibit higher tensile and compressive moduli compared to neat PEEK, proportional to the TC4 loading.

The minor positive deviation observed PEEK/TC4 composites may be referred to synergistic effects not fully considered in the theoretical model, such as enhanced crystallinity or improvements in interfacial adhesion between matrix and filler48,65.

The observed enhancement in mechanical strength, evidenced by a 35% increase in microhardness at PT(40), suggests improved load-bearing capacity compared to neat PEEK. The microhardness of the PT(40) composite closely approaches the lower range of human cortical bone, suggesting its potential suitability for biomedical applications requiring mechanical compatibility with hard tissue. This improvement is critically important for bioprosthetic devices, where materials are required to withstand repetitive mechanical loading while maintaining long-term biocompatibility and structural stability. Relevant studies have shown that titanium-filled PEEK composites produced through similar powder-based processing routes exhibit enhanced biological performance. For example, Jung et al.66 reported that PEEK/Ti composites significantly improved osteoblast attachment, proliferation, and differentiation in vitro compared with neat PEEK, demonstrating clear cytocompatibility benefits of incorporating ductile titanium particles. These findings provide external validation that metal-reinforced PEEK systems comparable to the PEEK/TC4 composites prepared in this work can support favorable biological responses, and they further motivate future bioactivity and cell-response testing specific to PEEK/TC4. To validate biomedical applicability, future studies should conduct in vitro bioactivity assessment, such as SBF immersion, apatite precipitation analysis, cell adhesion studies, and in vivo osseointegration evaluation.

Conclusions

This study investigated the microstructure, crystallinity, thermal and microhardness behavior of PEEK/TC4 composites fabricated via centrifugal powder compaction followed by vacuum sintering. The main conclusions are outlined below:

The centrifugal compaction method effectively achieved uniform TC4 dispersion within the PEEK matrix, facilitating localized interfacial bonding while minimizing agglomeration, even at elevated filler contents.

Overall, the crystallinity of the composites increased by approximately 17% relative to neat PEEK, while microhardness improved by about 35%, confirming that TC4 reinforcement effectively enhances both the structural and mechanical performance of the material. In summary, these findings show that PEEK/TC4 composites fabricated via centrifugal powder compaction followed by vacuum sintering exhibit improved mechanical and thermal characteristics attributed to the uniform dispersion of TC4 particles, their nucleating effect, and the interfacial bonding achieved through this processing route. Such improvements indicate that PEEK/TC4 composites are promising candidates for further evaluation as potential materials for orthopedic or bioprosthetic components where increased hardness, thermal resistance, and structural reliability are beneficial.

Future research should focus on in vivo biocompatibility, tribological performance under physiological loading, tensile and bending tests, as well as non-isothermal crystallization kinetics to more comprehensively evaluate the nucleation mechanisms and crystallization dynamics of PEEK/TC4 composites. While the property enhancements demonstrated here indicate potential biomedical relevance, formal validation including in vitro bioactivity, cytotoxicity, and in vivo osseointegration studies is essential before any clinical application can be considered.
