Extensive Dataset for Peristaltic Pump Accuracy Enhancement in Pharmaceutical Environments
Authors: Davide Privitera, Alessandro Mecocci, Sandro Bartolini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We publish a comprehensive dataset of peristaltic pump dosing outputs in pharmaceutical manufacturing, where accuracy is crucial for drug quality and patient safety, consisting of 149,847 measurements spanning volumes from 0.1 to 2.0 ml. An industrial filling system was used to acquire data under controlled conditions, using calibrated weighing equipment. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first dataset documenting pump behavior across such a wide range of volumes. The dataset aims to constitute a solid tool that enables investigation from short-term precision to long-term stability standpoints, providing detailed insights into peristaltic pump behavior under various operating conditions. Additionally, the dataset incorporates compensation outcomes across multiple volumes, documenting both statistical and AI-based compensation strategies, thus exemplifying how the statistical behavior of the dosing can change in response to some compensation strategies aimed to improve dosing accuracy. This resource directly addresses pharmaceutical industry needs by supporting optimization of quality control systems and validation of novel compensation strategies.

Background & Summary

Over the last decade, interest in the precision and reliability of drug delivery systems has grown as regulatory stringency has increased and biopharmaceutical drugs have become more complex and expensive. With products costing up to $10,000 per gram, even small improvements in dosing accuracy can result in significant savings through reduced product waste1–3.

Peristaltic pumps have become prominent in pharmaceutical manufacturing processes due to their ability to handle sensitive products with minimal cross-contamination risk4,5. These devices offer substantial benefits through their simple working principle where the product contacts only easily replaceable sterile tubing. However, achieving high dosing accuracy with very small volumes remains challenging, particularly for micro-dosing applications where precision is paramount.

While the scientific community has developed various mechanical and software compensation strategies to enhance accuracy5–10, systematic assessment and comparison of approaches has been hampered by the lack of publicly available datasets. The pharmaceutical manufacturing sector presents unique challenges regarding data sharing due to intellectual property concerns and regulatory requirements, explaining the current absence of pump filling datasets in the literature.

In this paper, we present a comprehensive dataset collected in collaboration with an Original Equipment Manufacturer (OEM) comprising 149,847 dosing measurements across volumes from 0.1 to 2.0 ml. The dataset captures both uncompensated baseline performance and results from various compensation strategies called Adaptive Dosing Control Systems (ADCS), based on statistical (Autoregressive - AR) and machine learning (Gated Recurrent Unit - GRU, Long Short-Term Memory - LSTM) approaches. The selection of LSTM and GRU models for this study is based on several key considerations. The inherent nature of the dosing problem involves sequential data, where each dosage influences subsequent dosages. These architectures are specifically designed to handle time series data, excelling at capturing both short-term and long-term dependencies in sequences through their gating mechanisms. LSTM networks utilize memory cells that can store information over extended sequences, employing input gates (controlling new information), forget gates (managing retention), and output gates (regulating output information). GRU networks offer a more streamlined architecture, merging memory and hidden states into a single vector, providing computational advantages while maintaining temporal modeling capabilities. Furthermore the specific constraints of pharmaceutical equipment, including limited training data availability and computational efficiency requirements, make LSTM and GRU more suitable than more complex architectures like Transformers for our industrial application. Detailed information about this approach can be found in the dedicated study9.

The dataset addresses critical gaps in the literature by covering a wide volume range with special attention to micro-volumes (0.1-0.3 ml) where precision demands are highest11,12. Data were acquired using an industrial peristaltic system. By making this resource publicly available, we aim to support research in pump technology, precision dosing, and compensation algorithm development, particularly benefiting researchers in pharmaceutical manufacturing, control systems, and precision instrumentation.

Methods

The following section introduces an overview of our experimental approach to construct a high-fidelity dataset applicable for peristaltic pump performance analysis. By replicating industrial pharmaceutical filling processes under controlled conditions, this setup ensures accurate and reproducible data collection. We begin by describing the hardware setup, which includes the workstation components used to collect the data and their configuration and then, a second phase, we outline the structure of the dataset and detail our data collection protocol which is divided into three distinct phases: baseline performance measurement, online compensation testing, and pre-trained model validation.

Hardware Setup

To replicate a real industrial filling process under controlled conditions, we designed an experimental setup using equipment commonly employed in pharmaceutical applications worldwide. The core system consists of a Flexicon PD12 peristaltic pump controlled through an MC100 control unit running firmware version S2 (Watson-Marlow Flexicon, Ringsted, Denmark), specifically chosen for its versatility across the target volume range and widespread pharmaceutical use.

System Calibration and Measurement Accuracy

The accuracy and reliability of the dosing system were ensured through a systematic protocol designed to maintain consistent performance throughout data collection while accounting for environmental variations. Before each dataset collection experiment, we implemented a standardized initialization composed by a purging session consisting of 30 seconds of continuous pump operation to remove air bubbles from the dosing circuit, followed by the calibration procedure.

This procedure utilized the automated functions of the MC100 controller, which calculates pump parameters based on weight measurements to minimize dosing error under current pump conditions. The initial calibration sequence, repeated 5 times at the start of each experiment, ensures precise target volume achievement through iterative parameter refinement. This multiple-cycle approach is necessary because the installation of new tubing and complete circuit assembly inherently introduces calibration drift that requires systematic correction. The iterative nature is beneficial because each calibration cycle works on the improved configuration from the previous cycle: the first calibration provides coarse adjustment of the initial drift, while subsequent calibrations make progressively finer adjustments as they operate on smaller residual errors. The choice of 5 repetitions represents an empirical procedure we employed through observation that the pump consistently achieves optimal accuracy within this number of cycles. Each cycle includes: (1) dispensing of the target volume, (2) weight measurement using the precision scale, (3) transmission of measured weight to the MC100 controller, and (4) execution of the automatic calibration command that calculates and implements new pump parameters based on the current dosing error.

During experimental runs, the data were organized into subsets of 300 doses each, with single calibration cycles performed at the beginning of each subset to maintain accuracy. This periodic recalibration strategy compensates for gradual mechanical variations, environmental changes including temperature fluctuations, and systematic deviations arising from operational wear patterns. While temperature regulation was not explicitly monitored, the regular calibration protocol effectively accounts for temperature-related effects on fluid viscosity and mechanical components, ensuring dataset consistency. This approach is effective because ambient temperature variations occur over significantly longer timescales compared to our experimental cycles. By recalibrating every 300 doses, the system operates on shorter timescales than meaningful thermal drift, automatically neutralizing any temperature-related effects before they can accumulate and impact measurement accuracy. Beyond accuracy maintenance, these periodic calibrations serve also a fundamental purpose for dataset characterization: they deliberately alter the pump’s operating conditions to enable comprehensive study of system behavior across varying operational states, providing diverse operational scenarios that enhance the dataset’s value for research and algorithm development.

Measurement accuracy was guaranteed using the Wipotec scale characterized by display resolution of 0.002 g, linearity of ≤0.001 g, repeatability of ≤0.0005 g, and settling time of less than 120 ms14. Following Good Automated Manufacturing Practice (GAMP) guidelines, three-point calibration (0 g, 50 g, 100 g) using certified weights was performed before each experimental session, with acceptance criteria of ±0.010 g to account for environmental vibrations and measurement noise, consistently confirming measurements within instrument specifications. The scale employs AVC technology that actively cancels environmental vibrations, enabling accurate measurements in high-speed dosing operations14. To ensure consistent mechanical performance, new peristaltic tubing was used between each dataset creation, preventing potential degradation in accuracy due to excessive material wear.

Dataset Structure and Collection Protocol

The dataset comprises three distinct components designed to capture different aspects of peristaltic pump behavior and compensation effectiveness: (1) Core Performance Dataset establishing baseline accuracy under uncompensated conditions, (2) Online Compensation Dataset evaluating runtime adjustment capabilities using statistical and machine learning models, and (3) Pre-trained Compensation Dataset exploring static compensation strategies with neural network architectures.

To ensure consistent experimental conditions and data comparability across all components, data collection followed the standardized protocol described earlier. The subset size, composed by 300 doses, was selected based on previous research8–10 demonstrating that pump calibration significantly resets system conditions while revealing diverse behavioral patterns during normal operation. The calibration-induced diversity in pump behavior, combined with the need to collect statistically significant data within reasonable time constraints, made 300 doses optimal for capturing well-defined operating conditions.

All experiments used purified water processed through a Milli-Q®Advantage A10 system equipped with a Millipak®Express 40 filter, with the peristaltic pump operating at consistent parameters: 600 rpm speed, 200 rpm/s acceleration, and suck-back set to 1. Core Performance Dataset

The Core Performance Dataset establishes baseline system behavior across four target volumes (0.2, 0.3, 1.2, and 2.0 ml) under uncompensated conditions. This dataset serves dual purposes: characterizing natural system behavior across different volumes and providing validated reference data for evaluating compensation strategy effectiveness.

Online Compensation Dataset

The Online Compensation Dataset evaluates runtime adaptation using the ADCS methodology8–10. This approach provides continuous adaptation to both systematic and stochastic variations in dosing behavior. The system aims to maintain robust compensation parameters by continuously incorporating recent dosing measurements, thereby ensuring adaptation to current operating conditions while retaining sufficient historical context for pattern recognition.

The dataset, composed of five volumes (0.1, 0.2, 0.3, 1.2, and 2.0 ml), used two different models: an AR model of order 10 (AR(10)) that predicts future values based on linear combination of the last 10 observations, and a GRU network with one layer, 20-step time window and 6 neurons (GRU(20,6)) with tanh activation functions capable of learning more complex temporal patterns.

This 6-neuron configuration represents an optimal balance between model complexity and computational efficiency, determined through systematic parameter optimization across neuron counts from 1 to 30. Further information about this approach can be found in the dedicated study [13].

Pre-trained Compensation Dataset

The Pre-trained Compensation Dataset investigates the efficacy of static compensation strategies through models trained on historical data, as explored in9. This approach represents a significant methodological departure from online compensation, offering distinct advantages in computational efficiency. The fundamental difference lies in the absence of continuous model retraining: while online approach requires the model to be retrained at each compensation cycle (Figs. 4a,d), the pre-trained approach utilizes models that have been previously trained on historical compensation data to learn the pump’s behavior patterns during compensated operation substantially reducing runtime computational overhead and enabling faster response in production environments.

The training process implements both LSTM and GRU architectures, each configured with a single hidden layer containing 8-10 neurons, optimizing the balance between model complexity and computational efficiency. This architectural decision emerged from extensive empirical evaluation, demonstrating that more complex configurations yielded diminishing returns in compensation accuracy while increasing computational demands9.

The investigation implements two distinct feature engineering approaches, each designed to capture different aspects of the system’s temporal dynamics. The two-features methodology combines instantaneous dosing measurements with their corresponding rolling means calculated incrementally within each prediction window. This enhanced feature set enables the model to learn complex relationships between past compensation actions and their subsequent effects on system behavior. The inclusion of compensation history provides crucial information about the system’s response to previous adjustments, enabling more sophisticated prediction strategies that account for the cumulative impact of sequential compensation decisions. This approach required careful dataset curation due to the temporal dependency of the compensation feature, as values exist only during active compensation phases, necessitating the extraction of the 200 compensated doses from each 300-dose subset to maintain feature consistency.

The data collection follows the standard subset structure and the compensation formula remains the same as in the online approach.

The dataset includes complete records of raw dosing measurements, model predictions, and resulting dispensed volumes, along with the specially prepared dataset used for training the three-features models. This documentation enables analysis of how different feature combinations and model architectures process dosing information to generate compensation values. Further information about this approach can be found in the dedicated study9.

Technical Validation

This section outlines the validation framework and statistical characterization of the dataset. The analysis includes both technical validation and statistical benchmarking, providing quantitative metrics and indicators that describe the dataset’s underlying statistical characteristics. Through extensive statistical analysis and performance assessments, the dataset is evaluated to support research and system performance studies. The validation strategy consists of two phases.

The first phase addresses the core dataset, focusing on measurement system accuracy, dosing system performance, and dataset consistency across different operational volumes. This initial analysis establishes the reliability of the data collection process and measurement protocols. By validating these essential elements, the analysis confirms the quality of the entire dataset, as all subsets - both uncompensated and compensated - were collected under identical experimental conditions and measurement protocols.

The second phase evaluates the compensated dataset’s statistical characteristics. Using the 2.0 ml volume measurements as a representative example, this analysis documents the statistical properties of both uncompensated and compensated data subsets, demonstrating the dataset’s completeness and internal consistency. The analysis framework applied here is applicable across all volumes and compensation strategies included in the dataset.

Core Dataset Statistical Analysis

The statistical validation of our dataset employs a hierarchical approach to verify data quality and consistency across different measurement volumes. We focus our primary validation on the 1.2 ml target volume dataset, which contains the largest number of measurements and therefore provides the most comprehensive statistical basis for validation. For consistent analysis, all volumetric measurements were converted from milliliters (ml) to milligrams (mg) using a multiplication factor of 1000. The validation examines the data at three distinct levels: individual subset, inter-subsets relationships, and global dataset characteristics.

Individual Subset Analysis Methodology

For each 300-dose subset, we implemented a statistical analysis framework focusing on central tendency, dispersion metrics, distribution characterization, and time-series behavior. This multifaceted approach allows us to understand both the statistical nature of the measurements and their temporal evolution within each operational cycle. Our analysis begins with fundamental statistical measures: we calculate both mean and median to assess the central tendency of the distributions, where their comparison provides initial insights into potential asymmetry in the data. For dispersion characterization, we compute the standard deviation (STD) to quantify the spread around the mean, alongside the interquartile range (IQR) which provides a robust measure of statistical dispersion less sensitive to outliers. We then examine the shape and nature of the data distribution through skewness, which quantifies distribution asymmetry (positive values indicating longer right tails, negative values indicating longer left tails), and kurtosis, which measures the “tailedness” of the distribution, with higher values indicating heavier tails and sharper central peaks compared to a normal distribution.

A critical step in our analysis involves determining whether the data follows a Gaussian distribution, as this fundamentally influences the choice of subsequent statistical methods. For this purpose, we employed the Shapiro-Wilk test, which operates by comparing the ordered sample values with the corresponding normal order statistics, effectively measuring how well the data aligns with normal distribution characteristics. This test was chosen over alternatives because it offers superior power for sample sizes below 2000, making it ideal for our 300-dose subsets. Additionally, we employed the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test to assess the stationarity of each subset. In time series analysis, stationarity indicates that statistical properties such as mean and variance remain constant over time. The ADF test examines the null hypothesis that a unit root is present in the time series (indicating non-stationarity). A p-value below 0.05 leads to rejection of this hypothesis, confirming stationarity. This statistical framework, combining central tendency metrics, dispersion measures, distribution shape parameters, and temporal stability assessment, provides a robust foundation for analyzing the behavior of each subset. Compensated Dataset Statistical Analysis

As previously introduced, we provide an extensive compensated dataset that can serve as a benchmark for researchers developing and testing new compensation strategies on filling systems. To ensure completeness and facilitate comparative analysis, we analyze this compensated dataset using the same statistical framework applied to the core data, combining traditional statistical measures, distributional analysis, and statistical hypothesis testing. To illustrate this multi-faceted analytical approach, we present a detailed analysis of the AR(10) method applied to 2.0 ml dosing operations, encompassing 32 paired subsets of uncompensated and compensated dosing operations. The statistical framework established here can be extended to evaluate other volumes and techniques in the dataset, where similar distributional patterns, albeit with varying magnitudes, are typically observed.

Statistical Methodology

The compensated dataset analysis methodology follows an approach analogous to that used for the core dataset. While the detailed statistical results are collected in extensive tables as in the previous analysis, these are omitted here for brevity. Instead, we present key numerical findings from the analysis supported by qualitative visualizations, focusing on the most significant quantitative results while maintaining their relationship with the complete statistical assessment.

For each sequence pair, we compute different statistical parameters: mean, median, STD, and IQR. Time series stationarity is assessed using the ADF test with a significance level of 0.05. Distribution normality is evaluated through the Shapiro-Wilk test for individual subsets and the D’Agostino-Pearson test for aggregate data analysis.

Compensation effectiveness metrics are quantified through a direct comparison methodology where each ADCS ON phase is analyzed against its corresponding ADCS OFF phase (see Fig. 3).

To establish statistical significance of the observed improvements, we employ a battery of complementary statistical tests, including paired t-tests for mean differences, Mann-Whitney U tests for distributional changes, Levene tests for variance homogeneity, and mixed linear model analysis for hierarchical sequence evaluation. These tests provide validation of compensation effects across different statistical assumptions and methodologies.

Global performance metrics are derived from the concatenated subsets, ensuring consistent statistical treatment across the dataset.

Temporal Stability and Distribution Characteristics

Statistical analysis of AR(10) compensation reveals significant impact on process stationarity, with 90.6% of compensated subsets exhibiting stationarity compared to 34.4% in the uncompensated phase. This substantial increase in stationary behavior indicates a fundamental transformation in the temporal dynamics of the dosing process.

Temporal evolution analysis demonstrates that while both phases maintain mean values proximate to the target volume, the compensated phase exhibits markedly reduced temporal variability, quantified by a 36.4% reduction in the CV of subset means (from 0.228% to 0.145%). The STD trajectories reveal complex temporal patterns, with the compensated phase showing more constrained variance fluctuations, resulting in a global STD reduction from 6.50 mg to 5.49 mg (−15.45%).

Probability density analysis reveals distinct morphological characteristics between phases. The compensated phase exhibits increased probability mass concentration near the central tendency, manifested through higher peak density and reduced distributional spread. This structural modification is quantitatively characterized by a transition in skewness from 0.1153 to −0.2557, indicating an algorithmic reshaping of the underlying probability distribution.

The Error Cumulative Distribution (ECF) analysis provides a characterization of the error under compensation. The ECF, representing the fraction of errors smaller than or equal to a given magnitude, offers direct insight into the compensation effectiveness across different error thresholds. Unlike traditional cumulative distributions, the ECF specifically focuses on the absolute deviation from the target value, providing a more application-relevant metric for dosing system performance evaluation.

The application of these tests to the 2.0 ml portion of our dataset reveals several key characteristics. The paired t-test analysis produced a test statistic of −2.870 (p ≤ 0.01), demonstrating statistically significant differences between uncompensated and compensated operational phases. This result, considering the paired nature of the sequences, confirms that the observed improvements in mean accuracy represent systematic enhancement rather than random variation.

The Mann-Whitney U test yielded a combined statistic of 740.51 (p ≤ 0.001), with 65.6% of subset pairs showing significant distributional differences, validating fundamental changes in the underlying value distributions between phases. This non-parametric assessment confirms that compensation induces systematic changes in the system’s operational characteristics beyond simple mean adjustments.

Variance homogeneity analysis through the Levene test (combined statistic = 370.91, p ≤ 0.001) demonstrated highly significant differences in variability between phases, with 53.1% of subset pairs exhibiting significant variance changes, providing statistical evidence for enhanced precision under compensation. The mixed linear model analysis results (statistic = 105.43, p ≤ 0.001) further corroborated these findings by demonstrating significant compensation effects while properly accounting for both inter-subset and intra-subset variability in the hierarchical data structure.

These statistical results, combined with the previous distributional and error analyses, establish a robust quantitative foundation for validating the effectiveness of the AR(10) compensation strategy in enhancing dosing system performance.
