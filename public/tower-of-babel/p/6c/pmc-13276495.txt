The ultrafine-bridge-associated endonuclease ANKLE1 is stimulated by tension in DNA to process branch-points
Authors: Korak Kumar Ray, Artur P Kaczmarczyk, Alasdair D J Freeman, Faith Leow, Timothy J Wilson, David S Rueda, David M J Lilley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCovalent linkages between chromosomes form naturally as a result of recombination or DNA replication. These can persist until late mitosis, resulting in the formation of ultrafine bridges, preventing cell division and causing genome instability through mechanical DNA rupture. The endonuclease ANKLE1, selective for DNA branchpoints during cytokinesis and localizing at the cell midbody, is ideally poised to act as the ‘enzyme of last resort’ in processing interchromosomal bridges, thereby allowing cell division. How ANKLE1 catalyzes DNA cleavage under the high tension that exists in interchromosomal bridges during mitosis remains unexplored. Using optical tweezers, we show that ANKLE1 is a mechanosensitive endonuclease whose catalytic activity is stimulated by tension in its DNA substrate. At high tension, we observe that the rate at which ANKLE1 catalyzes the cleavage of DNA junctions increases continuously with applied tension, with a 20-fold increase in the cleavage rate at 60 pN. This indicates that ANKLE1 has evolved to detect and respond to tension-induced changes to the DNA structure in a manner that facilitates nucleolytic activity. This mechanoenzymological response of ANKLE1 to tension within its DNA substrate reveals how this enzyme is well suited to its suggested biological role as an ultrafine-bridge processing enzyme during late mitosis.

Introduction

DNA junctions arise as a natural consequence of recombination and DNA replication, covalently linking chromosomes together. If not completely processed, these junctions lead to the formation of ultrafine DNA bridges linking chromosomes during mitosis [1–4]. Such DNA bridges can form in each cell cycle, their number increasing with DNA damage or inhibition of DNA replication [3]. Interchromosomal bridges are thus a source of genome instability in cells—the persistence of a single linkage can prevent cell division or result in the mechanical rupture of DNA. Removing the DNA junctions that result in these bridges is so important that multiple pathways exist in eukaryotic cells for processing them. These include dissolution by BLM helicase, PICH translocase, and the topoisomerase III complex [5–11] or resolution by junction-selective nucleases, including the nuclear SLX4–SLX1–MUS81–EME1 complex [12–15] and the cytoplasmic resolving enzyme GEN1 [16–19]. Cells defective in multiple of these pathways are exquisitely sensitive to DNA damage [20–22], with a high frequency of aberrant chromosome formation [22–24]. If junctions joining chromosomes have evaded processing by these pathways in human cells, one enzyme can act late in the cell cycle to provide a final opportunity to process any remaining DNA branchpoint.

LEM-3 was first discovered as a GIY-YIG nuclease involved in DNA repair in C. elegans [25]. Its mammalian (including human) ortholog, ANKLE1 [26, 27], is a nuclease that binds and cleaves a variety of DNA junctions with nanomolar affinity, including four-way Holliday junctions, and three-way Y junctions (i.e., a DNA duplex that separates into open single-stranded regions at one end) [28, 29] (Fig. 1A and B). The Y junction was found to be the best substrate for human ANKLE1, cleaving three times faster than the four-way junction. Recent genetic and cytological evidence suggests that the biological role of LEM-3/ANKLE1 is processing ultrafine DNA bridges in anaphase. LEM-3/ANKLE1 has been shown to interact genetically with other junction-selective endonucleases such as MUS81, GEN1, SLX1, and SLX4 [4, 30]; lem-3 double mutants with slx-1 and mus-81 are embryonic lethal [4]. Furthermore, LEM-3/ANKLE1 accumulates at the cell mid-body [30, 31], a structure where the two daughter cells abscise from each other. Genetic evidence indicates that such localization is important for LEM-3/ANKLE1 activity in vivo [31]. Finally, chromosomal bridges induced by treatment with various DNA-damaging agents persist in lem-3/ANKLE1 mutants [30]. Thus, ANKLE1 is active at the right time, late mitosis, and present at the right location, the mid-body, to catch any incompletely processed junctions and disconnect the bridged chromosomes, allowing cell division to proceed. This suggests that ANKLE1 is the ‘enzyme of last resort’ in the processing of interchromosomal bridges to allow cell division to complete.

Segregation of chromosomes in anaphase requires pulling the unlinked chromosomes towards the spindle poles using pico-Newton (pN) mechanical forces generated by attached spindle fibres [32–35]. The force generated to pull apart dividing chromosomes is estimated to be at least 50 pN [35–37]. Any DNA bridging the separating chromosomes must be under tension of a similar magnitude. To process branchpoints within these bridges, ANKLE1 must therefore act on DNA substrates under significant tension. Previous studies on endonucleases have observed that their catalytic activity is inhibited by or insensitive to tension within the substrate [38–40]. In particular, tensions higher than 50 pN have been found to be inhibitory for the activity of all previously studied endonucleases [38, 41]. In contrast, based on its suggested biological role, we hypothesized that the nuclease activity of ANKLE1 would likely respond to tension within its substrate and remain active at the high substrate tensions observed in ultrafine bridges. To test this, we used single-molecule optical tweezers experiments designed to study the ANKLE1-catalyzed cleavage of DNA junctions [40] as a function of tension.

Materials and Methods

Purification of ANKLE1

Wild-type ANKLE1 (hANKLE331–615 R492H) was expressed and purified as previously described [29]. Briefly, a pGEX6P plasmid containing a GST-tagged ANKLE1 construct was transformed into E. coli Arctic Express competent cells (Agilent). The transformed cells were grown in LB containing 100 mg/L ampicillin and 20 mg/L gentamicin to an OD600 of 0.6–0.8 at 37°C, then to an OD600 of 1.0–1.5 at 12°C. GST-ANKLE1 expression was subsequently induced with the addition of 200 µM IPTG at 12°C for 24 h. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 7500 × g for 15 min at 4°C and resuspended in 40 mL of Lysis Buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 5% v/v glycerol, 0.5 mg/mL lysozyme, and 1 tablet of cOmplete EDTA-free protease inhibitor (Roche)) per liter of cell culture. The resuspended cells were incubated at room temperature for 15 min, supplemented with Triton X100 to a final concentration of 0.2% w/v, and lysed by sonication on ice for 1.5 min using 10 s pulses separated by 30 s. The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 20 000 × g for 30 min at 4°C and incubated with glutathione Sepharose 4B beads (Cytiva), which had been pre-equilibrated in GST Wash Buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 5% v/v glycerol). The beads were washed with GST Wash Buffer, and the GST tag was cleaved from the protein construct by incubating the beads with PreScission protease (GenScript) in GST Cleavage Buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 5% v/v glycerol) overnight. The cleaved ANKLE1 was purified using two rounds of cation-exchange chromatography on a 5 mL HiTrap SP HP column (Cytiva) in 25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 10% v/v glycerol with a linear gradient of 50 mM to 1 M NaCl and concentrated on a 1 mL HiTrap SP HP column (Cytiva) using a step-gradient up to 25 mM HEPES, pH = 7.5, 400 mM NaCl, 10% v/v glycerol. Protein concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4 fluorometer, and single-use aliquots were flash frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

Preparation of bubble construct for optical tweezers experiments

The custom 15 kb bubble construct consisted of two 7.5 kb dsDNA handles and a centrally located constitutive ‘bubble’, i.e., a permanent, unpaired region of 24 nt. The 7.5 kb handles were prepared in a manner analogous to the ones for the four-way junction construct by PCR amplification from a λ-phage genomic DNA template using the Biot-F primer (see above) and a second modified primer, Fluor-R. Fluor-R contained a BsaI recognition site, which could be used to generate an asymmetric 4 nt 5′ overhang in the handle for ligation to the central bubble and a 5′-fluorescein label that could be used to follow the generation of this overhang (sequence given below). The primers were synthesized on an Applied Biosystems 394 synthesizer using β-cyanoethyl phosphoramidites (Glen Research) [42, 43], deprotected by incubation with ammonium hydroxide at 55°C for 2 h, and purified by electrophoresis in polyacrylamide gels under denaturing conditions in 7 M urea. The primers were recovered from DNA-containing gel fragments using the crush-and-soak method followed by ethanol precipitation. PCR amplification of the 7.5 kb handles from a λ-phage genomic DNA template was performed using the above primers and Q5 High-Fidelity Master Mix (New England Biolabs) using the manufacturer’s recommended cycling protocol, and purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen).

The central bubble was generated from two chemically synthesized phosphorylated oligonucleotides (Thermo Scientific), denoted bub-a and bub-b (sequences given below). The oligonucleotides were purified by electrophoresis in polyacrylamide gels under denaturing conditions in 7 M urea and recovered from DNA-containing gel fragments using the crush-and-soak method followed by ethanol precipitation. The bubble was formed by mixing equimolar amounts of both oligonucleotides in Hybridization Buffer supplemented with 50 mM NaOAc, heating to 80°C, and slowly cooling to room temperature. This generated an insert with 4 nt 5′-GGTT overhangs at each end that were complementary to the 5′-AACC overhangs generated by BsaI digestion of the DNA handles, but not to themselves, thereby preventing self-ligation. The reaction was cycled in a thermocycler (Techne TC-3000G) for 30 cycles of 5 min at 37°C (for BsaI digestion) followed by 5 min at 16°C (for ligation) and terminated by purifying the product using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen). The ligated 15 kb construct was further purified by electrophoresis in a 0.8% w/v agarose gel prepared in TAE buffer and stained with 0.5x SYBR Safe DNA Gel Stain (Invitrogen). The product band was excised, and DNA was recovered using the QIAEX II Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen).

Preparation of λ-DNA construct for optical tweezers experiments

The 48.5 kb λ-DNA was prepared as previously described [44]. Briefly, 4 nM λ-phage genomic DNA (Thermo Scientific) was incubated with 100 µM dGTP, 100 µM dTTP, 80 µM biotin-14-dATP, 80 µM biotin-14-dCTP, and 0.5 U Klenow polymerase exo− (New England Biolabs) in NEB2 Buffer (New England Biolabs) at 37°C for 30 min, then at 70°C for 15 min, before being purified using the QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen).

Optical tweezers experiments

Single-molecule ANKLE1 cleavage experiments were performed on a C-trap (Lumicks), integrating optical tweezers, microfluidics, and confocal fluorescence microscopy. Prior to the experiments, all solutions were filtered using 0.2 µm syringe filters. The microfluidic flowcell and channels were passivated with 0.5% w/v Pluronic F-127 in TN50 Buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pHRT = 7.5, 50 mM NaCl) and subsequently, for channels 3 and 4 (Fig. 1), with ANKLE1 Experiment Buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, pHRT = 7.5, 40 mM NaCl, 10 mM MnCl2, 1 mg/mL BSA). This buffer differs slightly in components from those in bulk experiments below, and was chosen for compatibility with the other buffers and materials used in the optical tweezers experiments. At the start of the experiment, the following substrates were introduced into the corresponding channels by flow (Fig. 1): (i) 0.005% w/v streptavidin-coated polystyrene beads (∼4.4 µm, Spherotech) in TN50 Buffer in channel 1; (ii) ∼2 pM of biotinylated DNA substrate (four-way junction or bubble or λ-DNA construct) in TN50 Buffer in channel 2; (iii) ANKLE1 Experiment Buffer in channel 3; (iv) 500 nM ANKLE1 (wild-type or mutant) in ANKLE1 Experiment Buffer in channel 4.

The optical trap stiffness used in these experiments was 0.2–0.3 pN/nm. For the dual-trap experiments, two beads were optically trapped in channel 1 and then moved to channel 2, where a single biotinylated DNA molecule was tethered between them. The presence of the correct DNA tether was verified by moving the DNA to channel 3 and generating a tension-extension curve. For the four-way junction construct, only DNA molecules that showed a rupture of the junction were kept for further experiments. The DNA was subsequently moved to the protein channel in a relaxed state (at an extension of ∼2.5 µm for the 15 kb constructs, or of ∼8 µm for the 48.5 kb λ-DNA construct). Upon entering the protein channel and coming to rest, a force clamp was applied to stretch the DNA to the desired tension. The tension on the DNA and the corresponding distance were recorded from before the start of the application of the force clamp till cleavage of the DNA was observed (or in case of incubations with mutant ANKLE1 or just ANKLE1 Experiment Buffer, for ∼10 min if no cleavage was observed until then).

For quadruple trap experiments, a single λ-DNA molecule was tethered between each of two pairs of optically trapped beads and verified using tension-extension curves. The two DNA molecules were subsequently braided once to form a single right-handed catenation, in a manner similar to [45], by moving one pair of beads above, around, and then below the other pair (Supplementary Movie S1). Establishment of the catenation was verified by measuring simultaneous increases in the forces measured on both pairs of beads when only one pair was moved, which would only be possible if there was a braid formed between the DNA molecules tethered to both pairs of beads. The beads were then moved into the protein channel with the DNA molecules in a relaxed position. Subsequently, one of the beads was moved until a high force (∼40 pN) was measured for that bead pair. The forces on both pairs of beads and the corresponding distances were recorded till a simultaneous reduction in the forces (to zero for one bead pair and a low, non-zero value for the other) was observed, signifying cleavage on one of the two intertwined DNA molecules.

Single-molecule data analysis

All analyses of the single-molecule data generated above were performed using Python code on Jupyter notebooks. The software package pylake (Lumicks) was used for handling the raw data generated from the C-trap experiments. For the DNA cleavage assays, the application of the force clamp and the cleavage of DNA were detected using a threshold force lower than the force applied in the clamp. The activation of the clamp was detected when the measured force crossed the threshold from below, and DNA cleavage was detected when the force crossed the threshold from above. The time between these two events was denoted as the time to cleavage (tcl). For instance (in the case of incubation with mutant ANKLE1 or just ANKLE1 Experiment Buffer) where no cleavage was observed, the end of the force recording was chosen instead. Since the cleavage of DNA occurred within a single time-point and the force-clamp achieved the desired force rapidly, the specific choice of the threshold force did not affect the calculated tcl by more than a few milliseconds.

Results and Discussion

ANKLE1 is a mechanosensitive endonuclease stimulated by tension within its substrate

We measured the cleavage rate by ANKLE1 of a four-way junction under tension using optical tweezers [49]. We utilized the hANKLE331–615 R492H nuclease domain of human ANKLE1 (hereafter referred to as ANKLE1) (Fig. 1A), which has been previously shown to be very active in the cleavage of DNA branchpoints [29]. ANKLE1-catalyzed junction cleavage was measured using a 15 kbp double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) construct comprising a central four-way junction comprising two 15 bp hairpins and two 7.5 kbp handles derived from λ-phage genomic DNA that were each functionalized with biotin at the termini, as previously described [40]. Individual DNA constructs were tethered between two optically trapped streptavidin-coated polystyrene beads (Fig. 1C,D). The four-way junction has been shown to rupture at ∼20–25 pN into a bubble conformation, with complete loss of base pairing of the 15 bp helical stems (Fig. 1D) [40]. We observed a similar rupture under our current conditions, with a rupture force 23 ± 2 pN (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. S1).

To investigate the cleavage of the junction at different levels of tension, the construct was moved to a microfluidic channel containing a relatively high concentration (500 nM) of ANKLE1. We chose this concentration, around 100-fold greater than the reported at zero tension [29], to ensure that we were operating at saturating ANKLE1 concentrations, even at high tension, and therefore not limited by the binding dynamics of ANKLE1 to the substrate. After entering the channel, a force clamp was applied to hold the DNA at constant tension, which was varied between 2 and 60 pN (Fig. 1C, see Material and Methods). The cleavage time () was measured from when tension was applied until it dropped to zero, indicating double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) cleavage (Fig. 2A). Measuring junction cleavage at different levels of tension revealed a marked dependence of on the applied tension within our experimental range (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2). This tension-dependent cleavage activity was not observed when the DNA construct was incubated in a buffer channel with no ANKLE1 or with a mutant ANKLE1 (ANKLE1Y453F) that had been previously shown to be catalytically inactive at zero force [29] (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S2). Therefore, we could attribute the observed tension-dependence of to the nucleolytic activity of wild-type ANKLE1. Our results, thus, revealed that ANKLE1 is a tension-sensitive endonuclease whose enzymatic activity is modulated by the applied tension within its DNA substrate.

ANKLE1 preferentially cleaves a stretched ssDNA-dsDNA bubble in comparison to a four-way junction

We investigated the rate of cleavage of the four-way junction construct by ANKLE1 as a function of applied tension to gain further insight into the mechanism of cleavage. We calculated the rate of cleavage () at various levels of applied tension, defined as the inverse of the average at that specific force (). The logarithm of the rate of cleavage () is proportional to the free-energy barrier for the underlying enzymatic reaction. The effect of external force (F) on this free-energy barrier () can be explained by the Bell–Evans model [46], which states that the application of external force modulates by an amount equal to , where represents the distance between the reactant state and the transition state along the reaction coordinate.

We observed two distinct tension-dependent enzymatic behaviors in the cleavage experiments (Fig. 2C). At low tension (<20–25 pN), we observed that junction cleavage by ANKLE1 reduces in rate slightly with tension, characterized by = −0.4 ± 0.7 Å, indicative of a weak, negative force dependence. By contrast, at higher tension (>25 pN), we observed a larger positive force dependence, characterized by = 2.5 ± 0.6 Å. The at 60 pN is 10-fold higher than that at 20 pN. The inflection point between these two behaviors occurs at 25 ± 5 pN, coinciding with the point at which the tension disrupts the structure of the cruciform junction. This suggests that the inflection point in the force dependence of ANKLE1 enzymatic behavior arises from the change in the structure of the DNA substrate, with the lower tension-dependence regime attributable to the intact cruciform four-way junction structure and the higher tension-dependence regime to the bubble substrate (Fig. 2C).

To test this mechanistic model, we utilized a second DNA construct where the central junction was replaced by two 24-nt DNA strands incapable of base-pairing with each other. We expected this construct to form a ‘constitutive’ bubble even at low forces (Supplementary Fig. S3). In accordance with this, exhibited a linear increase over the entire tension range, and a gradient corresponding to = 2.4 ± 0.2 Å, nearly identical to the four-way junction high-force regime (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. S4). The cleavage rates of the cruciform junction and bubble constructs are also closely similar at tension above 25 pN (Fig. 3A), indicating very similar activation parameters in general for these two substrates.

Thus, we see that the effect of tension on ANKLE1-mediated junction cleavage is not merely restricted to the formation of bubbles at elevated tension. If this were the case, we would expect the rate at which ANKLE1 cleaves junctions to plateau upon the formation of the bubble. Instead, increases with the applied tension well beyond the point we expect the junction to be disrupted, up to the tension at which separating chromosomes are believed to be pulled apart. The sensitivity of ANKLE1 to tension is quite substantial. The rate of cleavage of the bubble increases 20-fold over the complete range of tension.

Previous studies of ANKLE1 nucleolytic cleavage had identified the Y junction (Fig. 1A) as its preferred substrate [28, 29]. In particular, these studies determined that it was the dsDNA region of the Y junction that was cleaved by ANKLE1 (Fig. 1B). We expect the bubble construct to contain structures similar to Y junctions (Fig. 1A) at the bubble extremities. We therefore sought to investigate how the cleavage of the bubble by ANKLE1 compared to that of the Y junction.

We conducted cleavage experiments in solution with a short bubble substrate (see Materials and Methods) and observed that ANKLE1 cleaves the bubble at the dsDNA region with a = 0.16 ± 0.01 min−1 (Fig. 3B). This is comparable to the slowest rate of cleavage that we observe for the constitutive bubble in the optical tweezers experiment ( = 0.15 ± 0.01 min−1). We next compared this cleavage pattern to a short Y junction where the dsDNA region is identical in sequence to the bubble construct. The cleavage patterns were identical for both substrates, indicating that to the enzyme active site, the ssDNA-dsDNA junctions at the ends of the bubble are identical to that at the Y junction (i.e. the best substrate for this nuclease). In both, it cleaves the dsDNA adjacent to the ssDNA-dsDNA junction in an identical manner.

Unlike the Y junction, the DNA bubble consists of two ssDNA-dsDNA junctions on either end. Yet, for a particular DNA strand, we only observe cleavage products that correspond to cleavage on one side of the junction (on the strand 5′ of the junction). No products corresponding to the other strand at the junction are observed. This suggests that the dsDNA cleavage we observe in our optical tweezers experiments corresponds to two single-strand cleavages at each end of the stretched DNA bubble, with likely one rate-determining step (see Conclusions).

ANKLE1 stimulation arises from tension-dependent stabilization of the transition state

Previous studies investigating the interaction of ANKLE1/LEM3 with DNA substrates had reported a high-affinity binding to the splayed Y junction and four-way junction (Fig. 1A) at zero tension, with dissociation constants (s) of ∼1 and ∼40 nM, respectively [29, 50]. However, in the absence of information on the affinity of ANKLE1 to our substrates as a function of tension, our results raised the question of whether the stimulation in ANKLE1 activity that we observed at high tension was due to preferential binding or activated catalysis under these conditions.

To parse out the differential contributions of binding and catalysis to ANKLE1 activity at high tension, we investigated the four-way junction cleavage kinetics of ANKLE1 while titrating the enzyme concentration. Our experiments show that in the high-tension regime (>25 pN), ANKLE1 activity was unchanged upon a fourfold reduction in concentration to 125 nM and only became binding-limited upon a 10-fold reduction to 50 nM (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S5). On the other hand, the low-tension regime (<25 pN) activity of ANKLE1 was seen to be binding-limited even at a fourfold reduction in the concentration of ANKLE1 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S5). This suggests that ANKLE1 exhibits a preferential binding to the stretched bubble DNA substrate at high tension than to the intact four-way junction substrate at low tension.

We should emphasize that the observed change in rate with tension is that of a chemical reaction, the hydrolysis of a phosphodiester bond in the DNA. The origin of this rate acceleration is not known at present, but the effect must result in an alteration in the reaction transition state that lowers the activation energy. It is likely that modulation of the structure in the active site results in ANKLE1 becoming a mechanosensitive endonuclease. It will be interesting to explore the mechanism underlying this effect in future studies.

For the low-tension regime, our analysis was limited by the inability of our experimental setup to observe very slow cleavage events (in the order of tens of minutes), leading to over-estimations of the rates of cleavage at the lowest concentrations, which, in turn, affected the global fit of these data (Supplementary Fig. S6). This, combined with the negligible force-dependence of the corresponding ( = 0 ± 2 Å), suggests that the low negative force-dependence we observed for at the single high concentration (Fig. 2A) arose not due to a tension-dependent inhibition of ANKLE1 catalytic activity for this substrate, but due to a tension-dependent decrease in affinity instead.

Our experiments, thus, reveal a differential response in terms of the tension-dependence of binding and cleavage for the ANKLE1 active site to different DNA substrates. Both in terms of affinity and activity, we find that the bubble conformation is the preferred substrate for ANKLE1-catalyzed cleavage. This is in line with the biological role of this enzyme in branchpoints contained within processing ultrafine bridges.

ANKLE1 cleaves ssDNA-dsDNA junctions within stretched DNA

Finally, we wanted to investigate if the cleavage by ANKLE1 at such ssDNA-dsDNA junctions is specific to the four-way junction/bubble constructs that we have used above. Previous studies have shown that bubbles containing stretches of single-stranded DNA can also be formed in overstretched dsDNA at tension greater than 60 pN [51, 52]. To test this, we investigated the cleavage of overstretched dsDNA by ANKLE1. We find that, while not very active on dsDNA at zero force [28], ANKLE1 is capable of cleaving overstretched dsDNA held at tension above 60pN (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Fig. S7A), when compared to incubation in buffer in the absence of ANKLE1. While the experimental conditions used for these experiments (low ionic strength) favour the formation of transiently melted bubbles [51, 52], we note here that progressive strand separation, either at the tethered ends of our constructs or at nicks formed within the dsDNA, can also lead to the formation of ssDNA-dsDNA junctions that can act as substrates for ANKLE1 at these high tensions.

While our experiments above mainly address ultrafine bridges arising from unresolved junctions, another separate, but equally prevalent class of ultrafine bridges can arise from DNA catenations, which form at the end of each round of DNA replication and can persist until anaphase [53]. Notably, DNA braids that mimic these catenations (formed between two intertwined dsDNA molecules) have previously been shown to exhibit interactions between the two intertwined dsDNA molecules at high tensions of ∼40 pN, thought to arise from local regions of denatured DNA occurring at the point of entanglement [45]. We reasoned that such local denaturation under tension can also lead to the formation of transient ssDNA-dsDNA junctions at these points of entanglement. Indeed, we observed ANKLE1-catalyzed cleavage of DNA braids intertwined in a similar manner (Supplementary Movie 1) and held at forces around 40 pN (Fig. 5B and Supplementary Fig. S7B).

Our results indicate that ANKLE1 preferentially cleaves stretched dsDNA adjacent to ssDNA–dsDNA junctions (like those of Y junctions), regardless of their origins (Fig. 5C and D). We further see that tension within the DNA at these junctions stimulates the activity of the enzyme. We expect such ssDNA–dsDNA junctions to be present in ultrafine bridges under high tension, making ANKLE1 an ideal endonuclease for the processing of all forms of anaphase bridges.

Conclusions

ANKLE1 has been proposed as the enzyme of last resort for the processing of any junction linking chromosomes. All its previously described characteristics point towards this role: it cleaves a variety of branch-points in DNA, and it acts at the right time and location consistent with the cleavage of interchromosomal bridges. In this work, we further show that ANKLE1 cleaves stretched DNA at ssDNA–dsDNA junctions. The formation of such structures within negatively supercoiled dsDNA stretched to low tension (∼5 pN) [54] can explain recent findings of ANKLE1-catalyzed cleavage of dsDNA in these mechanical regimes [55]. Such structures are expected to be very common at the significantly higher tensions (>50 pN) present within ultrafine bridges, where they are likely to be generated by incompletely processed recombination and replication intermediates or by overstretched dsDNA, in particular around catenations (Fig. 5C and D). The activity of ANKLE1 is markedly stimulated by the level of tension expected in the bridges. Intriguingly, the topoisomerase Top2α, involved in decatenating DNA catenations under low tension, is inactivated at similar high tension [56]. This is, therefore, further evidence for the proposed biological role of ANKLE1 in resolving ultrafine bridges and thus allowing cell division.

Previous studies investigating the effect of DNA tension on the activity of endonucleases have all found that increasing tension either has an inhibitory effect or results in no change to the rate of catalysis [38–40]. In particular, tensions greater than 50 pN have all been found to be overall inhibitory to endonucleolytic activity [38, 41]. Our results show that ANKLE1 exhibits differential responses to tension within DNA depending on its substrate. With the intact cruciform structure, ANKLE1 shows little to no change in catalytic activity with increasing tension. Additionally, our experiments suggest that the affinity of ANKLE1 to this substrate decreases with tension. This is consistent with that previously observed in some other endonucleases [38].

In contrast, the mechanoenzymological response of ANKLE1 to tension within its preferred DNA substrate, stretched ssDNA-dsDNA junctions, is unique, to the best of our knowledge. We show that this can be attributed to an activation in the rate of ANKLE1 enzymatic catalysis, and not a higher affinity of ANKLE1 to DNA under tension. This stands in marked contrast to other endonucleases that show increased binding to DNA under high tension [57]. Our results further suggest that ANKLE1 affinity to these junctions remains unchanged with increasing tension. In vivo studies have demonstrated that ANKLE1 is recruited to the cell midbody primarily using protein-protein interactions instead of protein–nucleic acid interactions [30, 55]. It is therefore likely that ANKLE1 has evolved to prioritize faster cleavage of DNA junctions under high tension without requiring greater affinity to these substrates, consistent with the proposed biological mechanism for ANKLE1-mediated ultrafine-bridge resolution.

The mechanistic details underlying this tension-dependent stimulation of ANKLE1 activity, and the trade-off between affinity and cleavage within the ANKLE1 active site, are at present unknown. Importantly, our experiments, showing a continuous increase of activity with tension, suggest that this behavior does not arise from a conformational change in the DNA from an inactive (or less active) substrate conformation, the intact four-way junction, to a more active conformation, the bubble. On the other hand, the continuous increase in activity points towards a tension-dependent stabilization of the transition state of the nucleolytic cleavage for this enzyme, which presents an exciting avenue for future studies of ANKLE1 and similar proteins that need to act on DNA under high tension.

Finally, our bulk experiments show that, even for a DNA bubble substrate with two ssDNA-dsDNA junctions on each end, ANKLE1 cuts only one of the two strands at each junction (specifically, the strand 5′ of the junction). This is consistent with previous biochemical studies on ANKLE1 activity. The double-strand cleavage observed in our optical tweezers experiments (and in other previous works [50, 55]), therefore, likely occurs due to cleavage on either strand 5′ of the two ssDNA-dsDNA junctions in these constructs (Fig. 5C). Under tension, the two ssDNA-dsDNA junctions in our constructs are likely to be several nanometers apart, making it highly unlikely that the same ANKLE1 molecule, bound to only one of these junctions, can execute both cleavage events. This suggests that either two ANKLE1 molecules bound at the junctions cleave the two strands, or the same bound ANKLE1 molecule traverses the bubble to cleave either strand at each junction. Further studies investigating the binding of ANKLE1 to these substrates would be required to elucidate the exact stoichiometry of ANKLE1 cleavage. Multiple such stretched ssDNA-dsDNA junctions are expected within ultrafine bridges in the cell (Fig. 5D). Thus, tension-stimulated ANKLE1 cleavage at these junctions forms the functional basis for its biological role as the “enzyme of last resort” in resolving ultrafine bridges.
