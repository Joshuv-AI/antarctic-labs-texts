Eligibility for lecanemab and donanemab in Korea under Appropriate Use Recommendations
Authors: So Yeon Jeon, Min Soo Byun, Hye Ji Choi, Yoon Hee Kim, Chung Hee Gwag, Yoonseok Oh, Jee Eun Park, Dahyun Yi, Gijung Jung, Hyejin Ahn, Bo Kyung Sohn, Joon Hyung Jung, Yoon Young Chang, Nayeong Kong, Hongyoon Choi, Koung Mi Kang, Chul‐Ho Sohn, Dong Young Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAppropriate Use Recommendations (AURs) guide real‐world use of lecanemab and donanemab in early symptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AD), but their applicability to Asian populations with diverse education backgrounds remains unclear.METHODSAmong 2726 participants who visited a Korean memory clinic, 1005 amyloid‐positive participants with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and Clinical Dementia Rating data were included. Eligibility for lecanemab and donanemab was assessed using AUR criteria, including age, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), brain MRI, apolipoprotein E genotype (for donanemab), and anticoagulant use, applying both raw and z‐score‐based MMSE thresholds.RESULTSAmong 1005 amyloid‐positive participants, 24.6% were eligible for lecanemab and 28.0% for donanemab (9.1% and 10.3% of the initial sample). Applying MMSE z‐scores increased eligibility to 38.4% and 33.7%, respectively. Over a third of participants were excluded due to MRI findings, mainly vascular lesions associated with increased amyloid‐related imaging‐abnormality risks.DISCUSSIONIncorporating demographically adjusted MMSE z‐score threshold improves real‐world eligibility and promotes equitable access to anti‐amyloid therapies.Highlights About a fourth of A+ patients in Korea met AUR criteria for anti‐amyloid therapy.Donanemab eligibility slightly exceeded lecanemab despite narrower age criteria.MMSE z‐score adjustment increased eligibility in older, less‐educated individuals.Over 30% of eligible patients were excluded due to ARIA‐related MRI findings.Culturally adapted cognitive thresholds are essential for equitable treatment access.

BACKGROUND

Recent advances in anti‐amyloid immunotherapy have shifted the treatment paradigm for early‐stage symptomatic Alzheimer's disease (AD). 1 Lecanemab received U.S. Food and Drug Administration approval in July 2023, followed by donanemab in 2024. As of March 2025, both agents have been approved in multiple regions, including East Asia, and lecanemab has been launched for clinical use in Korea. 2 , 3 , 4

However, translating clinical trial findings into real‐world practice remains challenging. Studies applying clinical trial criteria 5 , 6 to community‐based cohorts have reported low eligibility rates among amyloid‐positive (A+) cognitively impaired (CI) participants. The Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (MCSA) reported 8% eligibility for lecanemab 7 and 23% for donanemab, 8 while an Austrian study found eligibility rates of <2% for mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and 4.3% for AD dementia met eligibility criteria for lecanemab. 9 These findings from Western cohorts suggest that even A+ CI participants are often ineligible for anti‐amyloid therapies. Since clinical trial criteria are intentionally restrictive to ensure internal validity and safety, 10 , 11 they may substantially underrepresent real‐world populations. 12

To bridge the trial and clinical practice, the Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Therapeutics Work Group (ADRD TWG) proposed the Appropriate Use Recommendations (AURs) for lecanemab 13 and donanemab. 14 While based on trial criteria, AURs offer greater clinical flexibility by omitting specific episodic memory test cut‐offs and allowing physician discretion for age and comorbidities. Therefore, AUR‐based eligibility may provide more realistic estimates of treatment eligibility.

However, few studies have evaluated AUR‐based eligibility. 15 , 16 A recent study found that 31% of 887 participants with amnestic AD syndromes met lecanemab AUR criteria based on Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and post mortem‐confirmed amyloid, 15 though brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, critical for amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) risk assessment, were not included. 15 Moreover, previous eligibility studies 7 , 9 , 15 , 17 were conducted mainly in non‐Hispanic White (NHW) populations.

In CLARITY‐AD 5 and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ‐2, 6 Asian representation was limited (17.1% and 6.5%, respectively), despite growing regional approvals. 2 , 18 Given regional differences in apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele prevalence, 19 vascular comorbidity patterns, 20 , 21 and cognitive test norms, 22 it is essential to assess anti‐amyloid therapy eligibility in diverse populations. A Korean study 16 reported that approximately 50% of A+ CI participants met the eligibility criteria for lecanemab treatment under the AURs. However, this estimate was derived from a relatively small sample (n = 467) and was restricted to participants with amnestic presentations. These constraints underscore the limited generalizability of the reported eligibility rate to more heterogeneous clinical populations.

Although donanemab has not yet been approved in Korea, it differs from lecanemab in dosing schedule (monthly) and in treatment discontinuation once amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) becomes negative. 6 , 13 The AURs also differ: donanemab recommends a narrower age range (60 to 85 vs 50 to 90) and a lower MMSE threshold (20 to 30 vs 22 to 30) and requires APOE genotyping for ARIA risk management. Given these differences, parallel evaluation is needed, but no study has systematically assessed AUR‐based eligibility for both agents, particularly in Asian cohorts.

Additionally, applying fixed MMSE thresholds to identify early CI participants may be problematic in culturally diverse populations. MMSE scores are strongly influenced by demographic factors, particularly educational level, and cross‐national normative data indicate that MMSE scores of 20 to 22 may reflect substantially different levels of cognitive impairment depending on age, sex, and education. 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Given the global prevalence of low educational attainment in older adults, rigid cut‐offs risk culturally biased classification and unjust exclusion of patients who would otherwise meet AURs for lecanemab or donanemab. Using age‐, sex‐, and education‐adjusted MMSE z‐scores derived from large normative datasets, instead of fixed cut‐offs, may mitigate this bias.

In this context, we aimed to assess the proportion of A+ CI patients who are eligible for lecanemab and donanemab based on their respective AURs in a memory clinic population in Korea. We also evaluated the impact of applying MMSE z‐score thresholds derived from large Korean age‐, sex‐, and education‐stratified norms 26 alongside fixed raw‐score cut‐offs. We hypothesized that using MMSE z‐scores would increase eligibility estimates than fixed cut‐offs, particularly among older individuals with lower educational attainment, thereby supporting culturally sensitive policy decisions regarding the implementation of anti‐amyloid therapy.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed studies on real‐world eligibility for anti‐amyloid therapies for AD, especially lecanemab and donanemab. Most applied phase 3 trial criteria and focused on non‐Hispanic White populations, with limited data in Asian settings. Few employed AUR‐based criteria, and none directly compared both drugs in Asian clinics. Interpretation: Our study shows that only about one‐fourth of amyloid‐positive patients with MCI or mild dementia in a Korean memory clinic meet AUR‐based eligibility for lecanemab or donanemab. Incorporating MMSE z‐scores – adjusted for age, sex, and education – alongside raw scores increased eligibility, particularly among older and less‐educated individuals. These findings highlight the value of culturally adapted cognitive thresholds for equitable access. Future directions: Further studies should validate z‐score–based cognitive criteria across populations and examine eligibility in wider clinical contexts. Evaluating treatment outcomes among z‐score–eligible patients will provide essential clinical insight.

METHODS

Study sample

This study was conducted using real‐world data retrospectively collected from the memory clinic at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH), a tertiary hospital in Seoul, South Korea. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SNUH (IRB No. H‐2501‐069‐1604). A total of 2726 participants who visited the clinic between February 2016 and November 2024 for cognitive evaluation and underwent standardized comprehensive clinical and neuropsychological assessments using the Korean version of the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer's Disease (CERAD‐K) 25 were included as an initial sample. Among them, 2163 participants underwent [18F]‐florbetaben PET, brain MRI, and routine laboratory testing. Of these, 1076 were identified as A+. From this group, participants diagnosed with MCI 32 and assigned a global CDR of 0.5, or those diagnosed with dementia based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐4th Edition criteria 33 and assigned a global CDR score of 0.5 or 1, were selected. Thus, the final study sample comprised 1005 A+ participants with CI and a global CDR of 0.5 or 1 (Figure 1).

Flowchart of study population selection from SNUH memory clinic. Of the 2726 participants who visited the SNUH memory clinic between February 1, 2016 and November 30, 2024, 563 were excluded due to missing neuroimaging data – 199 lacked only brain MRI, 318 lacked only amyloid PET, and 46 lacked both. The primary reasons for missing scans were patient refusal (often due to cost or perceived burden), contraindications (e.g., metallic implants or pacemakers for MRI), or incomplete diagnostic work‐up. A total of 2163 participants underwent both brain MRI and amyloid PET scans. After excluding participants with amyloid‐negative PET results (n = 1087) and those without a diagnosis of MCI or mild dementia or missing global CDR scores (n = 71), a final sample of 1005 amyloid‐positive participants with MCI or mild dementia and global CDR score 0.5 to 1 was included in the analysis. Clinical and neuropsychological assessment

All participants underwent standardized clinical assessments conducted by trained psychiatrists using the CERAD‐K clinical assessment protocol. 23 , 25 Reliable informants were also interviewed, and medical records were reviewed for more accurate information if available. Global CDR scores 34 were initially assigned by trained psychiatrists during clinical evaluation and subsequently reviewed and confirmed through weekly consensus diagnostic conferences. The Korean version of the CERAD neuropsychological battery (CERAD‐K NP) 25 was administered to all participants by experienced clinical neuropsychologists or trained psychometrists, who were blinded to the participants’ clinical diagnosis, neuroimaging findings, and APOE genotype results at the time of testing. The CERAD‐K NP was originally developed and validated in 2002 25 based on the original English‐language CERAD protocol. 23 All assessments conducted between 2016 and 2024 employed a consistent version of the CERAD‐K NP, with no changes in subtests, scoring procedures, or normative data throughout the study period. The battery includes MMSE as well as various subsets such as verbal fluency, Boston Naming Test, word list memory, construction praxis, word list recall, word list recognition, construction recall, the Trail Making Test, and the Stroop Color‐Word Test.

Current and past medical comorbidities and use of medication were assessed based on systematic interviews with participants and their informants by trained psychiatrists. The presence or absence of vascular risk factors – including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, coronary heart disease, hyperlipidemia, stroke, and transient ischemic attack (TIA) – was assessed using the same approach. Medications were reviewed using prescriptions provided by participants and electronic medical records. Participants also underwent overnight fasting blood sampling for routine blood laboratory tests and APOE genotyping.

Clinical interviews, neuropsychological testing, and blood sampling were conducted on the same day during the initial diagnostic visit. Brain MRI and amyloid PET scans were scheduled on separate days, typically within a few days and in rare cases, within 1 month. For each case with completed assessments, a weekly multidisciplinary panel – comprising board‐certified psychiatrists and clinical neuropsychologists specializing in dementia – reviewed all available clinical, neuropsychological, and imaging data, as well as laboratory tests and APOE genotype information obtained during the diagnostic work‐up. Final clinical diagnoses and CDR scores were determined based on this comprehensive evaluation.

Brain MRI and amyloid PET

Brain MRI

All participants underwent brain MRI scanning using the 3T Siemens Skyra, Skyra fit, or Verio scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The sequences acquired from MRI scanners are as follows: T1‐weighted image, T2‐weighted image, fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery, diffusion‐weighted image, and susceptibility‐weighted image (SWI). MRI findings – including cerebral macrohemorrhages, cerebral microbleeds, superficial siderosis, lacunar infarcts, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), and other major intracranial pathologies – were initially interpreted by board‐certified neuroradiologists. WMH severity was graded using the Fazekas scale. 35 All findings were evaluated for their potential relevance to cognitive impairment.

Amyloid PET

For amyloid PET imaging, all participants underwent a 10‐min PET scan performed 90 min after intravenous bolus injection of 300 MBq of [18F]‐florbetaben, using dedicated PET/computed tomography scanners (Biograph mCT40; Siemens Healthcare, Germany). Amyloid PET scans were evaluated by board‐certified nuclear medicine physicians using the Brain Amyloid Plaque Load (BAPL) scoring system, 36 in which scores of 1, 2, and 3 indicate no, minor, and significant amyloid load, respectively. A BAPL score of 2 or higher was classified as A+. 36

Eligibility criteria for lecanemab and donanemab

Eligibility for each anti‐amyloid therapy was assessed according to the AURs proposed by the ADRD‐TWG, 13 , 14 which were based on published clinical trial protocols and expert consensus. While the original clinical trials required objective evidence of memory impairment (e.g., Wechsler Memory Scale–Fourth Edition (WMS‐IV) Logical Memory II < –1 SD) and excluded participants outside the body mass index (BMI) range of 17 to 35, 5 the AURs allow for physician discretion in such cases. Similarly, whereas clinical trials mandated that all concomitant medications to be stable for at least 4 to 12 weeks prior to baseline, 5 , 6 participants receiving such medications were included if the medications were not expected to interfere with cognitive assessment or treatment adherence. This pragmatic approach more accurately reflects the complexity and heterogeneity of real‐world memory clinic populations. A standardized stepwise process was used to determine treatment eligibility in this study. In the first step, inclusion criteria were applied. Eligible participants were aged 50 to 90 years for lecanemab and 60 to 85 years for donanemab. Subsequently, MMSE scores of ≥22 for lecanemab and ≥20 for donanemab were applied. As APOE genotyping is required only for donanemab according to AURs, 14 eligibility for donanemab was assessed only among participants with available APOE genotype data. Thus, donanemab eligibility was limited to this genotyped subgroup.

Exclusion criteria were then applied. Participants diagnosed with non‐AD of cognitive impairment – including vascular dementia, 37 Lewy body disease, 38 frontotemporal dementia (FTD), 39 or cognitive impairment secondary to unstable medical conditions or psychiatric disorder (e.g., major depressive disorder, psychotic disorder) – were excluded. Among those meeting the diagnostic criteria for MCI due to AD, 40 or probable/possible AD, 41 additional exclusion was based on brain MRI findings and medication use. MRI‐based exclusion criteria included a single macrohemorrhage with a largest diameter of over 10 mm, more than four microbleeds (defined as 10 mm or less at the greatest diameter), an area of superficial siderosis, more than two lacunar infarcts or infarcts involving a major vascular territory, severe WMHs consistent with a Fazekas grade 3, 35 evidence of cortical infarcts, or other major intracranial pathologies that may cause CI. 13 Additionally, participants receiving active anticoagulant therapy were excluded. Active anticoagulant use was defined as current treatment with warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC), including apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, or dabigatran. Use of antiplatelet agents (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel) was not considered grounds for exclusion. This sequential approach enabled the identification of participants eligible for lecanemab or donanemab under real‐world clinical conditions.

Eligibility analyses using different MMSE criteria

To address potential limitations of fixed raw‐score‐based MMSE thresholds (≥22 for lecanemab; ≥20 for donanemab), we conducted a sensitivity analysis additionally using MMSE z‐scores (z ≥ –2.0) calculated from large‐scale Korean normative datasets stratified by age, sex, and education. 26 Z‐scores were computed using the standard formula: z = (X − μ)/σ, where X is the participant's MMSE score, μ the mean score for the corresponding demographic subgroup, and σ the standard deviation (SD). Specifically, participants were considered cognitively eligible if they met either the original MMSE raw score threshold (≥22 for lecanemab; ≥20 for donanemab) or had a MMSE z‐score ≥ −2.0. All other eligibility criteria were applied as in the main analysis (Figure S1).

To approximate a conservative boundary for clinically significant but mild degrees of cognitive impairment, we adopted a –2.0 z‐score threshold – corresponding to performance at 2 SD below the mean, or approximately the third percentile of the normative distribution. The National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) workgroups have shown that individuals with MCI typically score from 1 to 1.5 SD below the mean on culturally appropriate age‐ and education‐adjusted normative data (i.e., −1.0 to −1.5 z‐score). 40 Similarly, the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke–Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS‐ADRDA) workgroup has suggested the fifth percentile (∼ –1.64 SD) as a threshold for abnormal performance in dementia diagnosis. 42 In this context, the –2.0 z‐score threshold was selected to span the lower bound of non‐normative cognitive performance, encompassing both the typical range of MCI and the early stage of dementia (approximately –1.0 to –2.0 z‐score). This approach was intended to capture individuals with subtle but clinically meaningful cognitive decline while minimizing false exclusions due to demographic variability.

To evaluate the impact of applying different MMSE thresholds, participants who met either the raw‐score‐based or the z‐score‐based criteria were further classified into three groups: Group 1 (both raw and z‐score eligible), Group 2 (z‐score only eligible), and Group 3 (raw score only eligible). This classification allowed comparison between participants who would be differentially included or excluded depending on whether raw‐ or z‐score‐based MMSE thresholds were used.

In addition to the main analyses, we performed a supplementary analysis focusing on the MMSE raw score range of 18 to 22, which corresponds to the lower boundary of cognitive inclusion criteria in the AURs. Because the lecanemab trial applied a higher cognitive threshold (MMSE ≥ 22) compared to the donanemab trial (MMSE ≥ 20), we sequentially lowered the cut‐off from 22 to 18 in one‐point steps to illustrate the impact of gradual reductions in raw‐score thresholds. All other eligibility criteria were applied consistently with those used in the lecanemab analyses.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical characteristics of participants were examined using independent t tests for continuous variables and chi‐squared tests for categorical variables. Descriptive statistics are presented as means (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables. Age and years of education were compared across the three MMSE classification groups using one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's Honestly Significant Difference test for post hoc comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with listwise deletion applied for handling missing data. A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Selection of study sample

An initial sample of 2726 participants who completed CERAD‐K assessments at the SNUH memory clinic was identified. Of these, 563 (20.7% of 2726) were excluded due to missing neuroimaging data. Specifically, 199 lacked brain MRI only, 318 lacked amyloid PET only, and 46 lacked both modalities. Missing data were primarily attributable to patient refusal (often related to cost or perceived burden), contraindications (e.g., metallic implants or pacemakers for MRI), or incomplete diagnostic work‐up. Among the remaining 2163 participants who underwent [18F]‐florbetaben PET, brain MRI, and blood laboratory testing, 1076 (49.7% of 2163) were identified as A+. Of this group, 1005 participants were included in the final study sample based on a clinical diagnosis of MCI or all‐cause dementia with a global CDR score of 0.5 or 1, after excluding four (0.4% of 1076) with missing global CDR scores, 17 (1.6% of 1076) with subjective cognitive decline who did not meet MCI criteria, and 50 (4.6% of 1076) with a global CDR score > 1 (Figure 1).

Characteristics of study sample

The mean age of the participants included in the final study sample was 74.7 years (SD = 7.9) and 65.4% (n = 657) were female. The mean years of education was 11.0 (SD = 4.7). APOE ε4 carriers accounted for 48.1% (n = 463). Based on global CDR scores, 73.4% (n = 738) were rated as 0.5 and 26.6% (n = 267) as 1. The mean MMSE score was 20.3 (SD = 4.9). Clinically, 62.1% (n = 624) were diagnosed with MCI and 37.9% (n = 381) with dementia (Table 1).

Eligibility of lecanemab

Among the 1005 A+ participants diagnosed with MCI or mild dementia and with a global CDR score of 0.5 or 1, 20 participants (2.0%) were excluded based on age criteria (<50 or >90 years), resulting in 985 remaining participants (Figure 2A). Of these, an additional 548 (55.6%) were excluded due to MMSE scores below 22 (n = 538, 54.6%) or missing MMSE data (n = 10, 1.0%). Consequently, 437 participants (43.5% of the study sample) met the inclusion criteria for lecanemab administration (Table 2 and Figure 2A).

Eligibility for donanemab

Eligibility for donanemab administration was also evaluated using a stepwise application of inclusion and exclusion criteria, based on age, cognitive status, APOE genotype availability, clinical diagnosis, neuroimaging findings, and anticoagulant use (Table 3). Among the 1005 participants who were A+ and had a clinical diagnosis of MCI or dementia with a global CDR score of 0.5 or 1, 139 (13.8%) participants were excluded based on age criteria (<60 or >85 years), resulting in 866 participants who met the age criterion (Figure 2B). Of these, 343 (39.6%) were excluded due to an MMSE score below 20 (n = 337, 38.9%) or missing MMSE data (n = 6, 0.7%), yielding 523 participants (52.0% of the study sample) who met the cognitive criterion. APOE genotyping data were available for 500 participants, who were subsequently included in the donanemab eligibility analysis (49.8% of the study sample, 18.3% of the initial sample) (Figure 2B and Table 3). For the remaining 23 participants, APOE genotyping data were unavailable, primarily due to lack of written informed consent, with a few cases where genotyping was not performed due to cost‐related constraints.

After excluding 57 participants with non‐AD etiologies of CI, unstable medical conditions, or psychiatric disorder affecting cognition, 443 participants (44.1% of the study sample) remained (Table 3). Following application of MRI‐based exclusion criteria, several participants were excluded due to neuroimaging abnormalities, with multiple findings allowed per individual. The most frequent exclusion findings were extensive subcortical WMHs (Fazekas grade 3), observed in 95 participants (19.0%), and more than two lacunar infarcts or a stroke involving a major vascular territory, noted in 79 (15.8%). Additional exclusionary findings included more than four cerebral microbleeds (6.8%), cortical infarcts (6.4%), superficial siderosis (2.8%), a single macrohemorrhage exceeding 1 cm in diameter (1.2%) and other significant intracranial pathologies potentially affecting cognition (4.6%) (Table 2).

Among the 500 participants who met the inclusion criteria for donanemab, 313 (62.6%) met no MRI exclusion criteria, 109 (21.8%) met one, 55 (11.0%) met two, 16 (3.2%) met three, 6 (1.2%) met four, and 1 (0.2%) met five (Figure 3B). After excluding 14 participants receiving anticoagulant therapy, the final eligible sample for donanemab consisted of 281 participants (28.0% of the study sample, 10.3% of the initial sample) (Table 3). Despite a narrower age range (60 to 85 years) and the requirement for APOE genotyping, the final eligibility rate for donanemab was slightly higher than that for lecanemab.

Eligibility analyses using different MMSE criteria

Given the potential limitations of using fixed raw‐score‐based MMSE thresholds in older Korean adults with lower educational attainment, we conducted a sensitivity analysis incorporating either a MMSE raw score ≥ 22 (or 20 for donanemab) or a MMSE z‐score ≥ –2.0 as a cognitive inclusion criterion (Figure S1). For lecanemab, 653 participants (65.0% of the study sample, 24.0% of the initial sample) met this revised cognitive criterion. After applying exclusion criteria, this number was reduced to 397 (39.5% of the study sample). With additional exclusion of those receiving anticoagulant therapy, the final eligible sample comprised 386 participants (38.4% of the study sample, 14.2% of the initial sample) (Figure 4 and Table S1). For donanemab, 620 participants (61.7% of the study sample, 22.7% of the initial sample) met the revised cognitive criterion. Of these, 589 had available APOE genotype data. After applying exclusion criteria, the final eligible sample was reduced to 339 participants (33.7% of the study sample, 12.4% of the initial sample) (Figure 4 and Table S1).

The participants who met the revised cognitive criterion were subsequently classified into three predefined groups: Group 1 (eligible by both raw score and z‐score), Group 2 (eligible by z‐score only), and Group 3 (eligible by raw score only). For lecanemab, the distribution of the participants (n = 653) across these groups was as follows: Group 1 (n = 414 [63.4%]), Group 2 (n = 203 [31.1%]), and Group 3 (n = 36 [5.5%]). There were significant group differences in both age and years of education (p < 0.001 for both): Group 2 was the oldest (mean age = 77.5 ± 6.4 years) and had the lowest education level (mean = 8.1 ± 4.3 years). In contrast, Group 3 was younger (mean age = 61.4 ± 5.1 years) and more highly educated (14.9 ± 1.4 years). Group 1 showed intermediate values for both age (74.1 ± 7.6 years) and education (12.2 ± 4.4 years) (Figure S2 and Table S2). In line with the lecanemab findings, the participants who met the revised cognitive criterion for donanemab (n = 620) were categorized into the same three predefined groups: Group 1 (n = 461 [74.4%]), Group 2 (n = 101 [16.3%]), and Group 3 (n = 58 [9.4%]). The distribution of age and years of education across groups closely mirrored the lecanemab pattern: Group 2 was the oldest (mean age = 77.4 ± 5.4 years) and had the lowest education level (mean = 8.1 ± 4.3 years), whereas Group 3 was the youngest and most educated. Group 1 showed intermediate values for both age and education (Figure S3 and Table S3).

Finally, to examine the impact of raw‐score thresholds independent of z‐score adjustment, we sequentially lowered the MMSE cut‐off from 22 to 18 by one point and evaluated the resulting eligibility rates. Eligibility increased incrementally as the cut‐off decreased (Figure S4 and Table S4). Specifically, the proportion of the study sample meeting final eligibility criteria rose from 24.6% at a MMSE cut‐off of 22 to 28.6% at 21, 30.9% at 20, 32.6% at 19, and 33.9% at 18. These findings demonstrate that even a one‐point shift in MMSE cut‐off within this range can meaningfully alter estimated eligibility.

DISCUSSION

In this large‐scale study using real‐world data from a Korean memory clinic, we found that 24.6% (lecanemab) and 28.0% (donanemab) of 1005 A+ CI participants met the AUR‐based eligibility criteria. Importantly, when both raw and z‐score‐based MMSE cut‐offs were considered, eligibility increased substantially to 38.4% (lecanemab) and 33.7% (donanemab). Over 30% of participants were excluded due to MRI findings associated with increased ARIA risk, particularly severe WMHs and multiple lacunar infarcts.

Recent Western studies reported lecanemab eligibility rates of 2% to 15% based on clinical trial criteria. 7 , 9 , 17 , 43 While direct comparisons are limited by methodological differences, the MCSA study applied phase 3 clinical criteria to A+ CI individuals and found only 8% (19/237) eligible. 7 Notably, a considerable proportion of participants were excluded based on criteria not specified in the AURs (e.g., 10% for BMI, around 20% WMS‐R Logical Memory scores). Major reasons for ineligibility included cardiopulmonary conditions, central nervous system‐related issues, and recent malignancy (17.9%). 7 In contrast, our study employed AURs, which do not require exclusion based on WMS‐R scores and allow physician discretion for extreme BMI. We excluded participants only when medical conditions were judged to significantly affect cognition or treatment safety. Thus, trial‐based criteria, while vital for internal validity, can underestimate real‐world eligibility.

Two AUR‐based studies assessed lecanemab eligibility. 15 , 16 One, using the National Alzheimer's Coordinating Center‐Uniform Data Set (NACC–UDS) and neuropathology dataset, reported 31% eligibility among 887 A+ CI participants without considering brain MRI. 15 A Korean study showed approximately 50% eligibility among 467 A+ CI participants who underwent brain MRI. 16 Both studies restricted inclusion to amnestic presentations (e.g., at least mild memory impairment per CDR plus NACC FTLD 44 or impaired delayed verbal recall < −1.0 SD 16 ) – a criterion not required by the AURs – potentially inflating eligibility.

Re‐evaluating MMSE cut‐offs and applicability of z‐scores

With fixed MMSE thresholds, a substantial proportion of A+ CI participants were excluded based on cognitive scores alone (538 of 985 [54.6%] for lecanemab, 343 of 866 [39.6%] for donanemab). In contrast, the MCSA study excluded only 35 of 817 (4.3%) under donanemab trial criteria (MMSE 20‐28). 8 This discrepancy likely reflects differences in educational backgrounds, as older adults with low education are more prevalent in Korea. 45 To address this limitation, we applied an alternative criterion: fixed MMSE threshold (≥22 or ≥20) or a z‐score ≥ –2.0 (age‐, sex‐, and education‐adjusted). As presented in Table S5, the raw‐score equivalents to −2.0 z‐score of MMSE varied substantially across demographic strata, ranging from 11.5 to 24.7 in the Korean elderly population. These findings underscore the difficulty of applying a single MMSE raw‐score cut‐off to accurately identify cognitive dysfunction in the context of anti‐amyloid treatment eligibility.

Our alternative cognitive criterion lowered MMSE‐based exclusions to 31.5% (lecanemab) and 27.7% (donanemab), yielding an additional 13.8% and 5.7% eligible participants. Using a more inclusive z ≥ −2.5 further raised eligibility to 43.4% and 37.0%, respectively (Figure 4; Table S6). These patterns align with a Korean study showing that MMSE z‐scores added 6.0% to 11.3% eligible participants, 16 supporting our approach. Subgroup analyses were concordant: Participants eligible only by z‐score (Group 2) were older and less educated than those eligible by both criteria (Group 1), while those meeting only raw‐score thresholds (Group 3) were younger and more educated. Thus, reliance on fixed cut‐offs risks underestimating CI among vulnerable individuals (e.g., older, less‐educated individuals). A review of over 100 AD trials similarly linked rigid thresholds to underrepresentation of ethnoracially and socioeconomically diverse populations. 46 Strict application of such criteria may exclude participants with early‐stage AD who could otherwise benefit from treatment. Thus, regulators and payers, as well as clinicians, should consider alternative approaches when defining eligibility and reimbursement policies to avoid systematic access barriers.

While useful, z‐score‐based thresholds require referencing normative data each time to calculate z‐scores, which can be cumbersome. Moreover, the accuracy of these thresholds is highly dependent on the quality and representativeness of the norms. To avoid these challenges and the limitations of fixed MMSE raw‐score thresholds, an alternative approach may involve using functionally grounded measures such as the CDR–Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SOB) 47 instead of cognitive scores like MMSE. CDR‐SOB provides a continuous, functionally anchored metric that is less influenced by education and shows excellent concordance (κ = 0.86 to 0.94). 48 , 49 U.S. data indicate that MMSE scores of 22 and 20 correspond to CDR‐SOB values of approximately 5.0 to 5.5 and 6.5, respectively, 50 supporting CDR‐SOB as a practical, education‐independent alternative.

Brain MRI‐based exclusion and implications for ARIA risk

A substantial proportion of participants were excluded due to brain MRI‐based findings – 36.6% (160/437) for lecanemab and 37.3% (187/500) for donanemab. The most common exclusions were severe WMHs, >2 lacunar infarcts, and ≥5 microbleeds. Given that the SWI sequence is more sensitive to microbleeds than the gradient‐recalled echo (GRE) sequence, the use of SWI in our study may have contributed to a relatively higher proportion of patients excluded based on MRI findings. This contrasts with previous clinical trials such as CLARITY‐AD 5 and TRAILBLAZER‐ALZ 2, 6 which employed GRE sequences for microbleeds detection.

While current MRI‐based exclusion criteria prioritize safety, their real‐world evidence base remains limited. Emerging data suggest that ARIA risk is associated with baseline cerebrovascular findings such as microbleeds and WMH, 51 , 52 , 53 yet the thresholds applied in AURs are conservative. Additional safety data are needed before criteria can be relaxed. Future studies should also test whether strategies like individualized titration schedules, stratified risk assessment by APOE genotype, or more frequent MRI monitoring could enable safer use in patients with more extensive brain imaging abnormalities.

Other clinical implications

Applying AUR‐based criteria and incorporating z‐score‐adjusted MMSE thresholds can expand eligibility especially for older adults with lower educational attainment. This approach enhances equity in treatment access and reflects a more inclusive strategy for identifying candidates for anti‐amyloid treatment. However, expanding the eligibility pool also raises important safety considerations. Older individuals with lower education levels tend to have higher vascular risk and greater comorbidity burden, 54 , 55 which may increase their vulnerability to treatment‐related adverse events, including ARIA. Therefore, greater clinical caution may be warranted when administering these therapies to demographically high‐risk groups. Further research should evaluate ARIA outcomes and safety profiles in demographically diverse populations, ensuring that therapeutic expansion does not compromise patient safety.

Strength and limitations

This study has several notable strengths. First, it is based on a large, well‐characterized clinical sample. Among nearly 3000 participants who initially visited the memory clinic, over 1000 A+ CI participants who also underwent brain MRI were included in the final analysis. All assessments were conducted at a single center, enhancing consistency. Second, instead of applying clinical trial criteria, we used AUR‐based criteria, which are more applicable to routine clinical decision‐making. Third, this is the first study to systematically compare eligibility for both lecanemab and donanemab using AUR criteria within the same clinical population. Finally, our comparative analyses using different MMSE thresholds – including both raw‐score‐based and z‐score‐based cut‐offs – highlight the importance of applying culturally appropriate cognitive thresholds in treatment eligibility assessments.

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the real‐world uptake of anti‐amyloid therapies may be strongly influenced by non‐clinical factors, such as financial burden (including insurance coverage), physical accessibility to infusion and imaging facilities, logistical challenges associated with repeated infusions and MRI monitoring, and reluctance or fear among patients and caregivers toward amyloid PET or infusion‐based treatments. These factors were not accounted for in our estimation of treatment eligibility and may contribute to a gap between AUR‐based frameworks and actual clinical implementation. Further research is needed to evaluate how these external factors affect treatment acceptance and feasibility in practice. Second, the study was conducted exclusively in a Korean population, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to populations with different racial or ethnic backgrounds and healthcare systems, including variations in diagnostic practices. Cultural, genetic, and systemic differences could influence both eligibility and safety profiles of anti‐amyloid therapies. Third, as the data were derived from a tertiary hospital, eligibility rates may differ in community‐based or primary‐care settings. Nonetheless, as anti‐amyloid therapy requires MRI/PET access and infusion facilities, 13 , 14 , 56 our sample likely reflects where these treatments will be implemented. Fourth, although comorbidities and medications were thoroughly assessed through clinical interviews and review of electronic medical records at evaluation, recall bias cannot be excluded.

CONCLUSION

Our study demonstrates that approximately one‐fourth of A+ CI participants in the memory clinic population met eligibility criteria for lecanemab (24.6%) and donanemab (28.0%) under current AURs. Incorporating MMSE z‐scores adjusted for age, sex, and education – alongside raw score thresholds – expanded eligibility to 38.4% (lecanemab) and 33.7% (donanemab), particularly among older and less‐educated individuals. Additionally, a substantial proportion of otherwise eligible participants were excluded due to MRI findings associated with ARIA risk, underscoring the clinical relevance of cerebrovascular comorbidities. Future work should assess real‐world safety in z‐score‐eligible groups and evaluate whether some MRI thresholds can be safely relaxed. Taken together, these findings support demographically sensitive cognitive criteria and a more individualized, inclusive implementation of anti‐amyloid therapy.
