Active edible coatings and films enriched with Salvia officinalis supercritical extract: characterization and application on Telemea cheese
Authors: Ilir Mërtiri, Leontina Grigore-Gurgu, Liliana Mihalcea, Iuliana Aprodu, Mihaela Cotârleț, Mihaela Turturică, Viorica Ghisman, Daniela Laura Buruiană, Loredana Dumitraşcu, Gabriela Râpeanu, Nicoleta Stănciuc
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the potential of supercritical fluid extract of Salvia officinalis (So-SFE) incorporated into chitosan–gelatin–glycerol to obtain active edible coatings and film composites for food applications. Listeria monocytogenes was selected as the target microorganism, and the forming solutions were prepared based on the minimal inhibitory concentration of So-SFE. Phytochemical characterization revealed So-SFE extracts rich in fatty acids, carotenoids, and phenolic compounds. Bioinformatic ADMET assessment indicated that 75.44 % of the identified compounds had high gastrointestinal absorption, while 78.95 % exhibited favorable toxicity profiles. The incorporation of So-SFE did not significantly affect film thickness, density, solubility, or swelling capacity, although scanning electron microscopy revealed modifications in film morphology and microstructure. The concentration of So-SFE significantly influenced water vapor permeability, color parameters, browning index, and UV-blocking capacity. In vitro antimicrobial tests demonstrated enhanced antilisterial activity of the So-SFE-enriched films, which remained stable during storage at 4 and 35 °C. In situ application on Telemea cheese confirmed that the coatings inhibited the growth of mesophilic bacteria, yeasts, and psychrophilic microorganisms under refrigeration conditions. The coatings exhibited a gradual reduction of L. monocytogenes populations by 1.05–1.29 log CFU/g over 21 days of storage. These findings suggests that So-SFE enriched coatings can effectively improve microbial safety and preserve the biochemical quality of cheese during storage.

Introduction

Eurostat reported that the European Union (EU) generated 79.7 million tons of packaging waste in 2023. Paper and cardboard accounted for the largest portion, making up 40.4 % of the total, followed by plastic waste with 19.8 % (Eurostat, 2025). Taking into account the impact of these waste on the environment, interest in biodegradable materials has significantly increased (Verma et al., 2024). Edible packaging indicates materials that are an integral part of a food product and are safe for consumption. This includes films, thin layers, and surface coatings. Films are self-supporting structures applied to food surfaces, while coatings are spread or sprayed directly onto the food. These packaging materials are made from natural biopolymers, such as proteins, polysaccharides, and lipids, which are biodegradable and compostable, resulting in minimal environmental residues like carbon dioxide (CO2), methane, and water. The functionality of edible films and coatings can be improved by incorporating nanomaterials, essential oils (EOs), and plant extracts. These enhancements provide added benefits, such as improved barrier properties and increased food safety, and are further referred as active packaging (Usman et al., 2025).

Due to the limitations of single-component edible films, composite films can be created by combining multiple biopolymers to achieve desirable properties. Chitosan and gelatin are biopolymers commonly used for producing high-performance edible films (Bastarrachea et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2021). Both materials are classified as safe for consumption by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2022; U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2025), EU regulation (European Commission, 2022; European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2004), and China National Standards (Health Commission of the People's Republic of China; State Administration for Market Regulation, 2024; National Health Commission of the People's Republic of China, 2013). Chitosan is the only naturally occurring cationic alkaline polysaccharide, synthesized through the deacetylation of chitin sourced from shrimp, crab, and bug shells. It is biodegradable and undergoes enzymatic breakdown by lysozyme, an enzyme found in human tears, saliva, and other physiological fluids, producing non-toxic byproducts. Gelatin, on the other hand, is derived from the hydrolytic breakdown of collagen protein found in mammalian tissues and skeletal structures (Yarahmadi et al., 2024).

The genus Listeria contains 17 species, with Listeria monocytogenes being the only known pathogenic strain. The FDA enforces a zero tolerance policy for L. monocytogenes in food products due to the high mortality rate associated with listeriosis and its uncertain infectious dose (Townsend et al., 2021). Listeriosis mainly affects vulnerable consumer groups including children, pregnant women, the elderly, and individuals with weakened immune systems. L. monocytogenes is commonly associated with pork meat and its products, fish products, mixed food, vegetables, fruit juices, and dairy products. Certain strains of L. monocytogenes can survive over long periods, posing a risk of ongoing cross-contamination due to their ability to adhere to various surfaces (Grigore-Gurgu et al., 2024). From 2023 to 2025, FDA data indicated a significant increase in recall announcements related to L. monocytogenes contamination or suspected presence across multiple food product categories. For instance, in 2024, 23.53 % of the 238 total recall announcements were linked to the presence or suspicion of L. monocytogenes, with 10.71 % of those related to dairy products (U.S. Food and Drug Administration, 2024). According to the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) and the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), EU One Health 2024 Zoonoses Report, 3,041 confirmed invasive human cases were reported in 2024, resulting in an EU notification rate of 0.69 cases per 100,000 population (). While the incidence of listeriosis is considerably lower than that of Salmonella or Campylobacter infections, its clinical severity and high case-fatality rate, highlight its ongoing public health significance. This pathogen was responsible for 1,715 hospitalizations, with a mortality rate of 15.6 % (). Several studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of sage (Salvia officinalis) extracts against L. monocytogenes (Mërtiri et al., 2025; El-sawy et al., 2024; Lopes et al., 2023).

Sage has been used throughout history for its medicinal and pharmaceutical properties, as well as for culinary flavoring (Altindal et al., 2016). The European Medicines Agency (EMA) recognizes the health benefits of sage in traditional medicine and various pharmaceutical preparations. Importantly, no cases of overdose from sage leaves have been reported. However, intake of sage oil equivalent to more than 15 g of sage leaves has been associated with symptoms such as a sensation of heat, tachycardia, vertigo, and epileptiform convulsions (seizures) (European Medicines Agency, 2016). In order to obtain safe sage oil, free of solvents, supercritical fluid extraction (SFE) was employed for extraction. CO2 is the most utilized supercritical fluid due to the friendly critical parameters (temperature 31.1 °C and pressure 7.38 MPa), non-toxicity, non-flammability, and affordability. In its supercritical state, CO2 exhibits gas-like diffusivity and liquid-like solvating power, efficiently penetration into the raw material and extract of target bioactive compounds. The advantages of this extraction method include high selectivity, minimal thermal degradation of thermo-sensitive compounds, a solvent-free extract, and reduced environmental impact (Lemoni et al., 2025).

Hence, the aim of this study is to obtain active, edible chitosan-gelatin coatings and films enriched with supercritical fluid extract of Salvia officinalis (So-SFE) for food application. Various approaches were employed to investigate the physico-mechanical properties and antimicrobial activity of the coating and film composites. The SwissADME and ProTox-3.0 prediction software was used to estimate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion and toxicity (ADMET) of the identified phenolic, lipophilic, and fatty acid compounds found in the So-SFE extracts. The target microorganism for this research was L. monocytogenes Scott A, a pathogenic clinical isolate strain, that possesses virulence traits associated with invasive listeriosis. The antilisterial activity was investigated in vitro for the composite films and in situ on coated Telemea cheese, under a simulated cross-contamination scenario.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Wild sage (Salvia officinalis) leaves were harvested in Brigjë, Shkodër, Albania (42°22′24.5"N 19°27′16.6"E) in July 2023. The harvested leaves were cleaned and dried at room conditions until the moisture content of 11.79 ± 0.05 % (moisture analyzer KERN DAB 100-3 model, Balingen, Germany) and water activity value of aw = 0.36 ± 0.01 (water activity meter Fast-lab model; GBX Scientific Ltd; Romans sur Isére Cédex, France). The plant specimen was identified with the assistance of Prof. Dr. Lulëzim Shuka (Department of Biology at the Faculty of Natural Sciences, University of Tirana). The dried leaves were kept in cotton bags until SFE.

Supercritical fluid extraction of the bioactive compounds from the wild sage leaves

Prior to extraction, the plant material was ground in a domestic grinder (Heinner HCG-150SS; Bucharest, Romania) with 180W electric power. SFE of the bioactive compounds from the wild sage leaves was performed in pilot-plant equipment manufactured by Natex (Prozesstechnologie GesmbH, Fabrication No. 10-023/2011; Ternitz, Austria) with technical CO2 (purity of 99.997 %, w/w) as a carrier. The SFE pilot plant has the following features: a stainless-steel extractor vessel with a volume of 2.0 L along with two separators (S40 and S45, with volume of 1.5 L each), independent temperature and pressure control, and cascade decompression. The extraction conditions, as previously described (Mërtiri et al., 2025), involved temperature of 49 °C, pressure of 29 MPa, and a dynamic extraction period of 1.5 h, as initially reported by Pavić et al. (2019). The extracts were fractioned by reducing pressure in S40 to 10 MPa using the temperature of 40 °C, while in the second separator (S45) the decompression up to a recirculation pressure of 5 MPa and temperature 21 °C were set (Mërtiri et al., 2025).

The SFE of 3 kg of wild sage leaves was performed discontinuously, with each batch consisting of ground dried wild sage leaves and Raschig rings (5 mm × 5 mm x 1 mm) loaded into the extractor vessel (2:1, w/w). The average of the total CO2 flow rate, measured with a Sitrans F C Mass 2100 flow meter (Siemens A/S Flow Instruments; Nordborg, Denmark), was 20.61 ± 2.55 kg/h. After three batches, the extracts accumulated in both separators were collected separately in dark containers and ethanol was used to clean the wall of the separators. The collected extracts were centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C (Universal 320R; Hettich, Germany). The supernatant was concentrated in a rotary vacuum concentrator, equipped with a vacuum pump and cooling trap (RVC 2-18 CDplus model and CT02-50, respectively; Martin Christ, Osterode am Harz, Germany), until constant weight. The concentrated extracts were stored in dark containers at 4 °C until further analysis. The global extraction yield was determined as the ratio between the mass of the concentrated extract and the mass of raw material loaded into the extraction vessel and reported as percentage (%, w/w).

The obtained So-SFE were further characterized in terms of antioxidant activity, global phytochemical content, polyphenolic, lipophilic and fatty acids profile, and antilisterial potential. Furthermore, bioinformatics analyses focusing on the identified compounds as fingerprint molecules were performed. Details regarding the methods used for analysis are provided in the Supplementary materials.

Coating and film preparation

The forming solution (FS) used for the coating and films, were prepared based on formulation reported in the literature (Brankovic et al., 2022; Jovanović et al., 2021; Hosseini et al., 2013), with some modifications. Chitosan solution (1.5 %, w/v) was prepared by dissolving chitosan, from crab shell origin, in acidified aqueous solution (1 %, v/v acetic acid) and mixed using a magnetic stirrer (IKA, Model: RCT B; Germany). The stirring speed was gradually increased to 1,000 rpm, and the solution was stirred overnight at room temperature until fully dissolved. Further, the gelatin (from bovine and porcine origin) solution (4 %, w/v) was stirred at 1,000 rpm, at 50 °C, for 30 min until complete dissolution was achieved. The two biopolymer solutions were first combined (chitosan to gelatin ratio of 80:20), and then the So-SFE, dissolved initially in a hydroethanolic solution (70 % v/v), was added at different concentration, based on the MIC determination. The mixture was stirred at 1,000 rpm, at room temperature for 10 min. Glycerol (purity of 99 %) was added, as a plasticizer, at a concentration of 50 % (w/w) relative to the weight of the biopolymers, and mixed for additional 10 min. Finally, the FS was homogenized using a Processor Ultrasonic UP100H (Hielscher; Germany) at 40 % amplitude, for 4 min, at room temperature (these parameters were selected to avoid temperature increase in FS). To create the films, 27 mL of FS was poured slowly into glass Petri dishes (9 cm in diameter) to minimize bubble formation. The Petri dishes containing the solution were dried at 40 °C, for 24 h. After the drying process, the dishes with the formed film were covered and placed overnight in a desiccator (using CaCl2 to maintain 40 % relative humidity). The films were removed on the following day, and the assessments of their characteristics was performed on the third day.

Details regarding the methods used to characterize the FS and films in terms of rheological, physico-mechanical and in vitro antilisterial properties are provided in the Supplementary materials.

Telemea cheese coating and in situ analyses

The cheese used in the study, to assess the performance of the coatings, was a matured light Telemea cheese purchased from a local store in Galați, Romania. According to the product label, each package contains 150 g of cheese made from cow milk. The other declared ingredients were salt, microbial rennet, and lactic cultures. The manufacturer recommended storing the product at a refrigerator temperature between 2 °C and 8 °C. To ensure consistency in the experiments, the same batch of product was purchased. The samples were coated 67 days before the preferred consumption date indicated on the label.

Prior to coating, the cheese samples were cut, in aseptic conditions in a biological safety cabinet, into rectangular pieces measuring approximately 10 × 10 × 40 mm, with an average weight of 10.02 ± 0.73 g. Each piece was placed in individual sterile Petri plate. The FS, also prepared in aseptic conditions and sterilized under UV light for 20 min, were applied on the entire surface of the cheese samples. Specifically, 1 ml of the FS was poured onto one side of the cheese surface and allowed to absorb for 30 min. This process was then repeated for the other side of the cheese. The coated cheese samples were stored at temperature of 3 ± 1 °C, and the microbiological analyses were performed after 0, 7, 14, and 21 days of storage. A negative control (C) consisting of cheese uncoated was included in the study. Aiming to provide a safe product from a microbiological perspective, only the FS with the highest content of tested So-SFE were evaluated for their influence on Telemea cheese. Further in situ microbiological and physio-chemical assessments of Telemea cheese were done as detained in the Supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using Minitab 19 software (Minitab LLC, State College, Pennsylvania, USA), as detailed in the Supplementary material.

The SFE of the wild sage leaves collected in the summer contained higher TPC values than the raw material collected in the winter season (41.20 ± 2.16 – 62.76 ± 3.33 mg GAE/g DW), indicating that the phenolic biosynthesis is favored during the warmer months (Mërtiri et al., 2025). In contrast, sage collected in the winter had higher flavonoid contents, possibly indicating a stress response. Antioxidant capacity, measured by DPPH-based assay, was higher for the extracts obtained from the sage collected in the summer compared with plant collected during to the winter. These results are consistent with previous findings, indicating that warmer temperatures and longer light exposure in the summer season enhance metabolite synthesis and accumulation, improving antioxidant defense mechanisms in plants, resulting also in higher extractable material (Cui et al., 2025; Vrábl et al., 2023; Cao et al., 2019).

Pearson correlation analysis was used to assess the relationships among the TPC, TFC, and antioxidant activities (DPPH and ABTS assays) in So-SFE. Both antioxidant assays revealed very strong correlations with the tested variables; the ABTS and DPPH assays exhibited very strong negative correlations with TPC, (r = −0.804; p = 0.054, and r = −0.823; p = 0.044, respectively), but only the DPPH-TPC correlation was statistically significant. In contrast, both antioxidant assays revealed significant and very strong positive correlations with TFC (r = 0.913; p = 0.011 for ABTS, and r = 0.917; p = 0.010 for DPPH). The results suggest that flavonoids are the main contributors to the antioxidant activity in the extracts, among the tested variables in the correlation. Additionally, interactions between various classes of polyphenolic compounds and other constituents with antioxidant properties (carotenoids and fatty acids) play an important role in the radical neutralization mechanisms.

Profile of the phenolic and lipophilic compounds

Table 1 presents the phenolic and lipophilic compounds identified and quantified using RP-HPLC in the S40 and S45 extracts. In the S40 extract, α-carotene was the most abundant lipophilic compound, with an average of 182.68 ± 33.09 μg/g DW, significantly higher than the 63.98 ± 17.14 μg/g DW found in the S45 extract. Zeaxanthin (78.00 ± 6.64 μg/g DW) was the second most abundant in the S40 extract.

The S45 fraction concentrated higher amounts of cafestol (5.56 ± 0.76 μg/g DW) compared to S40 (2.15 ± 1.38 μg/g DW). Regarding the flavonoids, hesperidin was exclusively found, in the highest amount, in the S45 extract (8.54 ± 0.07 μg/g DW), whereas the S40 extract concentrated mainly isorhamnetin (1.57 ± 1.05 μg/g DW) and kaempferol (1.17 ± 0.52 μg/g DW).

Seasonal variation significantly affects the phytochemical profile of the plants extract. The SFE extracts obtained from the wild sage leaves harvested during to the summer season showed a wide range of identified phenolic acids, including caffeic, cinnamic, ferulic, gallic, and syringic acids. In contrast, the SFE extracts obtained from the plant harvested in the January 2023 (Mërtiri et al., 2025) had fewer identified phenolic acids, but included compounds like chlorogenic and sinapic acids. Chlorogenic acid accumulates in response to stress, serving as a protective antioxidant compound. The increased abundance and diversity of phenolic acids in summer correlate with increased enzyme activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (Rao and Zheng, 2025). The fractioned extracts from the winter-harvested sage contained higher cafestol amounts compared to extracts obtained from the wild summer sage. The rise in terpenoids during colder months helps stabilizing the membrane and mitigate stress conditions (He et al., 2023). Flavonoid profiles also varied by season, with summer sage extracts showing greater diversity (apigenin, isorhamnetin, kaempferol, luteolin) compared to winter extracts, which were richer in quercetin, quercetin dihydrate, and hesperidin, indicating that their seasonal shifts reflect differential stress responses (Mërtiri et al., 2025). Carotenoids, such as α-carotene and zeaxanthin, were more abundant in summer sage extracts. Their increased contents are linked to higher photosynthetic activity and light intensity (Kim and Eom, 2025).

Overall, seasonal variations affect the presence and quantity of phytochemical compounds, influencing the plant extracts biological properties. The summer-harvested plants provide higher antioxidant potential, while winter-harvested plants could offer valuable stress-related phytochemicals for pharmacological application.

Fatty acid profile

The fatty acid profile of So-SFEs is presented in Table 2. A total of 39 fatty acids were successfully identified and quantified in the extracts, including saturated (SFA), monounsaturated (MUFA), and polyunsaturated (PUFA) fatty acids. Among the SFAs, nonanoic and undecanoic acids were the most abundant, followed by palmitic acid (Table 2). The content of undecanoic acid was higher in S45 (203.70 ± 7.60 mg FAME/g total FAME) compared to S40 (153.40 ± 2.10 mg FAME/g total FAME). In contrast, palmitic acid prevailed in S40 (107.10 ± 6.60 mg FAME/g total FAME) than in S45 (52.80 ± 1.00 mg FAME/g total FAME). Overall, SFAs represent the major fatty acids in both extracts, representing 63.05 % of the total fatty acids content in S40, and 67.55 % in S45. These results suggest that S45 extract exhibits a higher degree of saturation. The total MUFA content was slightly higher in S40 (11.59 %) than in S45 (10.10 %). Cis-oleic acid was the predominant MUFA, with an average of 69.60 ± 3.30 mg FAME/g total FAME in S40 and 21.70 ± 0.60 mg FAME/g FAME in S45. These results suggest that, while S45 exhibited a broader distribution of MUFAs, S40 was particularly enriched in oleic acid, which is associated with beneficial nutritional properties (Pastor et al., 2021). PUFAs were present at higher levels in S40 (15.74 %) compared to S45 (11.49 %). The predominant PUFAs in S40 were linoleic and α-linolenic acids. In contrast, S45 showed significantly higher levels of conjugated linoleic and γ-linolenic acid. The total ω3 fatty acid content was 3.27 times higher in S40 (6.41 %) than in S45 (1.96 %), whereas the ω6 fatty acids represented 9.62 % and 6.91 % of the total fatty acids content, respectively. Consequently, the ω6/ω3 ratio significantly differed between the two extracts, with S40 exhibiting a lower and more nutritionally favorable value of 1.45 compared to S45 which had a ratio of 3.54. In summary, the S45 fraction was characterized by a higher total SFAs content and higher proportions of medium-chain saturated acids and conjugated linoleic acid. Meanwhile, S40 was richer in unsaturated fatty acids, particularly oleic, α-linolenic, and long-chain ω3 fatty acids. When compared to the fatty acid profile of the S45 reported by our team for the winter sage (Mërtiri et al., 2025), can be observed that the SFAs are predominant in both winter and summer wild sage extracts, with slightly elevated content in the winter extract. The main SFAs present are nonanoic, undecanoic, and palmitic acids. A significant difference is observed in PUFAs, especially ω3 fatty acids, which are higher in the winter extract. This increase in α-linolenic and cis-linoleic acid lowered the ω6/ω3 ratio, indicating enhanced desaturation activity in cold conditions to maintain membrane fluidity. In the S45 fraction of summer wild sage extract, the elevated ω6/ω3 ratio indicates the plant adaptation to heat stress, leading to more rigid membranes. In contrast, in the S45 winter wild sage extract, the high ω3 PUFA content and the lower ω6/ω3 ratio, demonstrate increased desaturase activity to reduce cold-induced rigidity (Falcone et al., 2004; Los and Murata, 2004).

The antimicrobial activity of phytochemical compounds found in plant extracts is the result of the synergistic action of multiple compounds, which can target single or multiple sites within microorganisms (Abdallah et al., 2023). Based on the literature and the identified phytochemical compounds from RP-HPLC, and the fatty acid profile of So-SFEs, the potential compounds with antimicrobial activity include the phenolic acids (caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and gallic acid), flavonoids (apigenin, kaempferol, luteolin, quercetin, rutin, and hesperidin), SFAs (lauric, capric, palmitic, and stearic acids), MUFAs (myristoleic, palmitoleic, and oleic acids), and PUFAs (eicosapentaenoic, docosahexaenoic, and α-linolenic acids) (Mandal and Domb, 2024; Casillas-Vargas et al., 2021).

In our previous study (Mërtiri et al., 2025), the antilisterial activity of the winter sage extract fractions was reversed, with S40 having a MIC of 3.13 mg/mL, and S45 at 0.39 mg/mL. The differences arise from seasonal variations in the phytochemical profile of the raw material. The winter S45 extract, at a concentration of 0.39 mg/mL significantly affected L. monocytogenes cell, causing changes in cell monology, release of intracellular material, inhibition of protein synthesis, and the decrease of DNA content. Molecular docking suggested that the virulence expression may be suppressed as a result of the interaction of the main fatty acids present in the extract (Mërtiri et al., 2025). Lopes et al. (2023) reported for the hydroethanolic extract of sage a MIC of 0.5 mg/mL against L. monocytogenes NCTC 7973. Meanwhile, El-sawy et al. (El-sawy et al., 2024) reported an inhibition zone diameter of 24.5 mm and a MIC of 0.29 mg/mL against L. monocytogenes ATCC 7646, when investigating a sage hydroethanolic extract. Furthermore, wild sage leaves extract demonstrated 90 % biofilm inhibition, the highest among tested plant extracts, including chamomile flowers (Matricaria chamomilla), marigold flowers (Calendula officinalis), and cinnamon bark (Cinnamomum verum) (El-sawy et al., 2024). The biofilm inhibition is particularly relevant for the dairy industry, given the risk associated with L. monocytogenes in dairy products.

In silico ADMET estimation of the identified compounds

In order to estimate the absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity (ADMET) of So-SFE as a potential functional food ingredient or active agent in edible coatings/films formulations, the identified phenolic, lipophilic, and fatty acid compounds were utilized as fingerprint molecules in SwissADME and ProTox-3.0 prediction software. The parameters predicted by these theoretical and computational models are summarized in Fig. 2 and Table S1 (in Supplementary materials).Fig. 2The BOILED-Egg model prediction for the identified compounds in S. officinalis supercritical fluid extracts (S40 and S45).

In Table S1 can be observed that the phenolic and flavonoid compounds exhibited multiple hydrogen bond donors and acceptors, a structural feature known to enhance antioxidant activity through hydrogen-donation mechanisms. Most compounds followed Lipinski's Rule of Five and Veber's Rule while predicting the ADME, permeability and bioavailability potential of the identified bioactive compounds. Molecular weights ranged from 116.16 g/mol for caproic acid to 664.56 g/mol for rutin trihydrate, with the majority (especially phenolic acids and medium-chain fatty acids) below 500 g/mol, consistent with good absorption and bioavailability potential. The topological polar surface area (TPSA) values favor membrane permeability, whereas higher values indicate reduced permeability, but improved hydrophilicity. The compounds that showed low TPSA (<40 Å2) were cinnamic acid, α–carotene, β–cryptoxanthin and all of the fatty acids. Lipophilicity (WLOGP) spanned a wide range, from hydrophilic (−1.88 for rutin) to highly lipophilic (12.46 for α-carotene), indicating diverse solubility.

The BOILED-Egg (Brain Or IntestinaL EstimateD) permeation predictive model (Fig. 2) provides a visual representation of human intestinal absorption (HIA) absorption corresponding to the white region, and blood–brain barrier (BBB) penetration represented by the yellow region, for the investigated compounds (Daina and Zoete, 2016). According to the model, 49.12 % of the compounds fall within the yellow region, indicating potential BBB permeability and neuroprotective or cognitive-enhancing effects within the central nervous system. Overall, 75.44 % of the investigated compounds exhibited high gastrointestinal (GI) absorption.

P-glycoprotein (P-gp) is a membrane efflux transporter that limits the intestinal absorption and systemic accumulation of various xenobiotics (Nguyen et al., 2021). SwissADME predictions indicated that only cafestol among the So-SFE investigated compounds is a potential P-gp substrate, whereas the majority of phenolic acids, flavonoids, and fatty acids are non-substrates. This suggests that most So-SFE constituents are not actively expelled from intestinal cells, favoring higher local bioavailability and functional effectiveness. The predicted P-gp interaction of cafestol likely reflects its high lipophilicity and structure, but its low concentration in the extract minimizes its impact. Overall, the predominance of non-P-gp substrates in So-SFE supports efficient gastrointestinal absorption, minimal transporter-mediated interactions, and enhanced functional stability when incorporated into edible coatings/films or other food applications.

Bioavailability scores (BS) were categorized as low (0.17) for 10.53 % of compounds, 17.54 % of the compounds showed a medium BS (0.55 – 0.56), and the majority of the investigated compounds (71.93 %) showed a high BS (0.85), suggesting overall favorable absorption potential. Most of the compounds presented median lethal dose (LD50) values between 900 mg/kg and 10,000 mg/kg (toxicity classes IV to VI), consistent with low acute oral toxicity. Meanwhile 21.05 % of the investigated compounds appeared in toxicity classes II and III (48 – 159 mg/kg), which are considered fatal or toxic if swallowed. The model accuracy (70.92 – 100 %) reinforces confidence in these predictions. It is important to note that these LD50 values are predicted for individual pure compounds. Hesperidin, isorhamnetin, kaempferol, luteolin, naringin, quercetin, quercetin dihydrate, rutin trihydrate, and α–carotene showed positive prediction for nutritional toxicity, with moderate probabilities in the range between 0.51 and 0.66.

Considering their relatively low concentrations within So-SFE and the limited quantities likely applied in food or nutraceutical formulations, the overall risk of oral toxicity is minimal. The ADMET profiles indicate that the phytochemicals from So-SFE possess favorable characteristics, implying minimal risk of bioaccumulation or systemic toxicity when incorporated into edible coatings or films. Nevertheless, SwissADME and ProTox-3.0 predictions are based on pure compounds and do not account for possible synergistic or antagonistic interactions within the diverse compounds in the extract and/or biopolymers matrix. Therefore, these computational results should be validated by in vitro and in vivo studies to confirm the safety, stability, and functional efficacy of So-SFE in real food systems.

Knowing the rheological properties of the solutions employed for coating is of particular importance. The rheological measurements done over the small amplitude oscillatory strain sweep test indicated that all FS samples exhibited good resistance to the applied deformation, over the entire tested strain domain. Regardless of So-SFEs addition, the FS samples had significantly higher viscous modulus (G″) values compared to the elastic modulus (G′), suggesting that they have a typical liquid-like behavior. Similar observations were reported by Bertolo et al. (2021) when studying the rheological behavior of the chitosan-gelatin mixtures supplemented with pomegranate peel extract. The incorporation of So-SFEs into the chitosan-gelatin mixture led to no important increase in G″-G′ difference. For instance, at strain of 10 % the G″-G’ ranged between 0.50 and 0.58 Pa.

The steady shear measurements indicated the shear-thinning behavior of the FS samples (Fig. S2 in the Supplementary materials). As the result of the stress governed alignment of the large molecules from the mixtures, the apparent viscosity values decreased while increasing the shear rate in the 1 – 500 s−1 domain (Bertolo et al., 2021).

In order to check the time independency of the samples’ viscosity, peak rotational tests were additionally performed at constant shear rate of 1 and 500 s−1. The viscosity of the FSs was monitored over 3 min, and the result confirmed the time-independent pseudoplastic behavior of the samples. Analyzing the results presented in Table 3, one can observed that the average viscosity measured at lower shear rate was significantly higher in case of the S45(1.56) sample. On the other hand, no important differences in terms of average viscosity measured at constant shear rate of 500 s−1 were noticed between FS samples supplement with different So-SFEs (Table 3). Considering the rather low viscosity of the FS samples one can assume that no difficulties should be expected while coating different food surfaces either by immersion, as in case of the present experiment, or spraying.

Properties of the composite films

Thickness and water-related properties

Table 3 presents the physical properties of the composite films. The different formulations exhibited no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) in terms of thickness, density (ρ), moisture content (MC), water solubility (WS), and swelling rate (SR), indicating that adding So-SFE did not impact these attributes.

Film thickness is influenced by the biopolymer ratio, the amount of FS used for casting, and moisture content after drying. The thicknesses of the films obtained in the experiment ranged from 0.11 ± 0.02 mm to 0.15 ± 0.04 mm. Previous studies report various thicknesses values for the Ch-G films. Kan et al. (2019) reported 0.052 ± 0.002 mm for the control film and 0.062 ± 0.003 mm for the Ch-G film enriched with 6 % hawthorn fruits extract. Gholizadeh et al. (2022) reported thicknesses between 0.41 ± 0.01 mm and 0.47 ± 0.08 mm for Ch-G films with a 70:30 ratio, depending on the molecular weight of chitosan. Additionally, Li et al. (2024) observed no significant changes in film thickness when incorporating garlic EO and anthocyanins from purple cabbage into Ch-G FS using a free radical grafting method.

Density values ranged from 0.64 ± 0.01 to 0.86 ± 0.03 g/cm3, with no statistically significant differences (p > 0.05) observed among the film composites. A gradual decrease in density was noted with increasing concentration in the S45 formulations. This trend suggests the formation of a less compact internal structure, which may be linked to reduced intermolecular packing and structural heterogeneity caused by a higher content of extract.

MC indicates the total volume of water molecules in the film matrix (Kan et al., 2019). The MC of the films obtained in the present experiment varied from 29.52 ± 0.40 to 34.91 ± 1.26 % (Table 3). Jovanović et al. (2021) reported a similar range of 27.81-32.92 %. for Ch-G films with lemongrass EO, ZnO, or Zn-Ac. These additions did not significantly affect the MC.

WS is related to the film's barrier properties and hydrophilic interactions in the biopolymer matrix. High WS can lead to film degradation, exposing food to oxygen and microorganisms, and absorbing water from the product (Yan et al., 2023). The films obtained in the present experiment, presented WS ranging from 43.87 ± 0.99 to 47.13 ± 0.30 %, comparable to the Ch-G films enriched with quercetin (41.23 to 58.13 %), as previously reported by Yadav et al. (2020). However, the WS values obtained in the present experiment are higher than those reported by Jovanović et al. (2021) for the Ch-G films (21.7 – 31.5 %), and those with hawthorn fruits extract (26.77 ± 0.23 – 23.21 ± 0.11 %) reported by Kan et al. (2019). Meanwhile, the composite films obtained in the present experiment showed lower solubility than the 60:40 Ch-G films enriched with lemon (96.34 ± 0.70 – 86.77 ± 0.17 %) described by Tügen et al. (2020). The SR ranged from 6.10 ± 0.40 % to 7.44 ± 0.28 %, which is slightly lower than the 10 ± 2.40 - 11 ± 2.4 % values reported by Gholizadeh et al. (2022) for the Ch-G films with a 50:50 ratio.

Related to water vapor permeability rate (WVP), a clear and statistically significant differences were observed among formulations and temperatures. At all tested temperatures (4, 23, and 35 °C), WVP values ranged from 4.35 ± 0.21 to 8.18 ± 0.77 × 10−12 g/(cm·s·Pa). In particular, S40(0.39) and S45(1.56) formulations exhibited relatively lower WVP values compared to the control film, indicating improved moisture barrier performance. This improvement can be attributed to a denser polymer network structure and stronger intermolecular interactions, which reduce free volume and hinder water vapor diffusion pathways (Sánchez-González et al., 2011). In contrast, the highest WVP values observed in S45(0.78) suggest a less compact matrix. A consistent increase in WVP with temperature was observed for all formulations, confirming the thermally sensitive nature of the biopolymer matrix. This behavior is attributed to increased molecular mobility and expansion of free volume within the polymer network at elevated temperatures, which facilitates water vapor transport. The obtained WVP values in the 10−12 g/(cm·s·Pa) range, compared with the reports in the literature (Hosseini et al., 2013; Dammak et al., 2017; Haghighi et al., 2019), indicate that the developed films exhibit relatively good barrier properties for biopolymer-based materials, making them suitable candidates for biodegradable food packaging applications, especially under low to moderate temperature conditions. Activation energy (Ea) for water vapor permeability ranged from 3.55 ± 0.94 to 5.40 ± 1.08 kJ/mol, with no statistically significant differences among formulations. Although S40 formulations exhibited slightly higher Ea values, suggesting a higher sensitivity of diffusion processes to temperature changes, the overlapping standard deviations indicate similar transport mechanisms across the films. This suggests that the fundamental diffusion mechanism of water vapor remained unchanged, despite variations in formulations.

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), and Energy Dispersive X-Ray analysis (EDX)

The microstructure and internal morphology of the film matrix are influenced by the interactions, compatibility, and miscibility between the biopolymers and the addition of organic or inorganic materials. These factors influence the film's physical, mechanical, optical, and barrier properties (Kan et al., 2019; Haghighi et al., 2019). The SEM micrographs presented in Fig. 3, acquired at magnifications corresponding to 100 μm and 20 μm scale bars (between 541 and 574x, and 2,580–2,840x, respectively), show pronounced differences in surface morphology between the control film and films enriched with So-SFE. Several studies reported similar observations, emphasizing that the control films have a smooth and homogeneous structure, whereas the films enriched with extracts or EO have heterogeneous surfaces with pores, air bubbles, and aggregation. The authors attributed these irregularities to the volatility of the EO and the presence of dried droplets within the film matrix, as well as the accumulation of extract/EO in the Ch-G cross-linking zone (Li et al., 2024; Yan et al., 2023; Tügen et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021; Nxumalo and Fawole, 2023).

The EDX spectra (Fig. 4), indicates that the film samples are primarily composed of carbon (C), ranging between 53.3 ± 0.4 to 61.1 ± 0.5 wt % (weight percentage), and oxygen (O) at ranges from 26.6 ± 0.3 to 38.7 ± 0.3 wt %. The control film shows a uniform distribution of elements with minimal presence of inorganic elements. However, the S40 and S45 film samples exhibited a slight increase of Na, Cu, and Zn concentrations, being correlated with higher extract levels in the formulations. Color properties

Table S2 (in Supplementary materials) presents the results of color parameters and browning index (BI) for the composite films over a 30-day storage period. The films exhibited varying stability of the lightness value (L∗) during storage. The control film demonstrated no significant changes (p > 0.05), indicating stable lightness. In contrast, films with S40 extract exhibited noticeable changes by day 30, showing slightly better stability than films with S45 extract. The S45(0.78) film began to change by day 20, while the S45(1.56) film showed a significant decline in lightness starting on day 10, indicating progressive darkening. Throughout storage, the S45(1.56) film was consistently darker, being noticeably dark on day 3 and significantly darker by day 30.

All a∗ values are negative, indicating a greenish shade in the films. By day 30, both the control film and the films enriched with S40 extract show increasing a∗ values, suggesting a greener appearance than at day 3. The S45(1.56) film has the lowest negative a∗ value at day 3, indicating a stronger green shade. However, its value decreases over time, showing reduced green shade intensity. By day 30, both the control film and the S40(0.20) film showed a significant change in their b∗ values. The S40(0.39) film changed significantly by day 20. Films enriched with the S45 extract had higher b∗ values than the control and S40 films. The S45(1.56) film had the highest initial b∗ value on day 3, increased by day 10, and showed no significant change until day 30.

The total color difference (ΔE) is used to quantify the difference between two colors in the CIELAB color space. For the control, S40(0.20) and S45(0.78) films, the ΔE value became statistically significant after 30 days of storage period. The S40(0.39) film reached statistically significant changes after 20 days of storage. Meanwhile, the S45(1.56) film showed a statistically significant ΔE value within 10 days, with no further increase until the end of the storage period.

The film formulations with the highest concentrations of So-SFEs, specifically S40(0.39) and S45(1.56), demonstrated a significant increase in the BI from day 3 to 30. The S45(1.56) film had the highest initial BI value among all formulations. The S45(0.78) film and the control film showed a slight increase in BI value, but not statistically significant. The increase in BI for the S40(0.39) and S45(1.56) formulations suggests that the higher extract concentration may be a result of: a) higher amount of compounds that oxidize during storage, b) occurrence of catalyzed reactions (like the Maillard reaction or oxidation) in the film matrix, or c) induced structural or optical changes that improve light absorption (lower L∗ value) and increase yellowness/green (reflecting in the a∗ and/or b∗ values).

Similar results regarding color changes were observed upon the addition of sage hydroethanolic extract to chitosan-hydroxypropyl methylcellulose films (Bigi et al., 2021). Specifically, the L∗ value decreased, indicating a darker film. The negative a∗ value decreased, suggesting the transition towards greener shade, while the b∗ value increased, resulting in a stronger yellow color than the control film.

Light transmittance

The UV barrier properties for biopolymers films is crucial, especially for food packaging applications. These properties influence lipid oxidation, vitamins and other nutrients degradation, and the sensory qualities of food, ultimately influencing its shelf life (Haghighi et al., 2019; Bigi et al., 2021). Detailed measurements of the film composites’ light transmittance (LT) at wavelengths ranging from 200 to 800 nm over a 30-day storage period, are provided in Table S3 (in Supplementary materials), featuring a three-layer statistical comparison: a) changes during storage period (within the film at each wavelength); b) differences across wavelength (within the film on specific days of the storage period); and c) differences among film formulations (within each wavelength on specific days of the storage period).

The LT measurements revealed significant variations (p < 0.05) related to extract concentration, wavelength, and storage period, indicating interactions between the phytochemical compounds from So-SFEs and the films’ biopolymeric matrix. Differences in LT were first observed at 250 nm among the composite films.

Statistical analysis of the changes observed over the storage time indicated a gradual decline in transmittance values across all film formulations, suggesting an increase in light-absorbing compounds, due to oxidation, diffusion, or structural degradation.

Films enriched with So-SFE showed significantly reduced transmittance across wavelengths, indicating a concentration-dependent relationship. LT progressively increased from 250 to 800 nm, demonstrating higher transmittance in the visible region (Vis) (400–800 nm), maintaining over 50 % transparency. Statistical comparison revealed significant differences in LT among composite films at all storage times, with the lowest LT in the UV region (200–400 nm). The UV barrier properties was more pronounced in the So-SFE enriched films, particularly in S45(1.56) film, which exhibited the lowest LT compared to all films.

In terms of statistical comparison between the differences across film formulations, the concentration-dependent relationship was evident, where higher extract concentrations resulted in lower LT. The S45(1.56) film consistently demonstrated the lowest transmittance throughout the storage period, outperforming other formulations in UV protection. Meanwhile, films with S40 extract provided moderate UV protection, while maintaining good visible transparency, achieving a suitable balance for active packaging materials that require both visibility and photoprotection.

The good UV barrier properties of the Ch-G films were previously reported by Kan et al. (2019). Their performance improved with the addition of plant extracts or EO rich in polyphenols, anthocyanins, or lipophilic compounds. The improvement was attributed to several factors: changes in color (Li et al., 2024), variations in the light-scattering properties of the phytochemicals (Liu et al., 2021), and the presence of unsaturated bonds in the biopolymer matrix from the phytochemical compounds, responsible for absorbing UV radiation (Bigi et al., 2021).

To compare the effects of films enriched with So-SFE to the control film, the efficiency of LT inhibition was evaluated across different wavelengths, distinguishing also between UV range (250–400 nm) and the Vis range (450–800 nm), as shown in Fig. S3 (in Supplementary materials). Films with higher extract concentrations demonstrated higher LT inhibition in the UV region. The S45(1.56) film achieved a LT inhibition rate of 64.63 %, while the S45(0.78) film showed a LT inhibition rate of 52.12 %. In contrast, the films enriched with S40 extracts had even lower inhibition rates. These results further support the concentration-dependent relationship between film formulation and LT inhibition.

Pearson correlation analysis was performed to investigate the relationship between the BI and LT of the composite films across different wavelengths from 250 to 800 nm during storage. The results, illustrated in Fig. S4 (in Supplementary materials), display the heatmap of correlation coefficients (r). In the control film, moderate to strong negative correlations (r = −0.303 to −0.691) were observed throughout the 300–800 nm range, with statistically significant p-values (p < 0.05). These correlations suggests that color changes in the control film were mainly due to minor oxidative or biopolymer degradation during storage, resulting in gradual darkening of the film. For films enriched with So-SFE, moderate to very strong negative correlations (r = −0.466 to −0.917) with statistically significant p-values were observed at various wavelengths. This indicates that BI development in the films enhanced their optical barrier properties, particularly improving UV protection during storage. Additionally, samples with higher extract concentrations experienced more pronounced color changes over time, likely due to the gradual oxidation and polymerization of phytochemical constituents, resulting in the formation of colored complexes and inducing scattering effects within the biopolymer matrix.

In vitro antibacterial activity

Fig. S5 (in Supplementary materials) presents the antilisterial activity of the composite films. The control film (without extract addition) allowed no L. monocytogenes growth beneath its surface. Previous studies reported weak antibacterial activity from the control Ch-G film against L. monocytogenes, E. coli, and S. aureus, which was attributed to chitosan's antibacterial properties (Yan et al., 2023; Roy and Rhim, 2021). Meanwhile, films enriched with S40 or S45 extracts displayed an inhibition zone, indicating enhanced antilisterial effectiveness. This activity was consistent across all tested conditions, both before and after storage at 4 °C and 35 °C for 7 days. This suggests that the biopolymer matrix successfully preserved the antilisterial activity of both chitosan and So-SFE during storage and under varying temperature conditions.

The TMB (Fig. 5a) of the cheese sample subjected to coating was 5.45 ± 0.04 log CFU/g. The coated samples, particularly those coated with S40(0.39) and S45(1.56), exhibited significantly lower TMB counts through days 7 and 14, compared to the control cheese. By day 14, the control cheese (C-) showed a TMB of 5.83 ± 0.54 log CFU/g, while the S40(0.39) coated cheese at 4.55 ± 0.09 log CFU/g, and the S45(1.56) coated cheese at 4.08 ± 0.21 log CFU/g. This indicates that the coatings effectively acted as antimicrobial agents, by limiting oxygen transfer and moisture loss, which inhibited bacterial growth. By day 21, the control cheese (C-) TMB increased to 5.89 ± 0.50 log CFU/g, showing no significant difference from the coated samples. These results highlight continued microbial growth under refrigeration and suggest that the coatings' inhibitory effects diminished over time, possibly due to reduced coating integrity or microbial adaptation.

Psychrophilic microorganisms, often present at levels of up to 106 CFU/g in milk and dairy products, can cause important economic losses through their lipolytic and proteolytic activities (Fajardo et al., 2010).

In Fig. 5b, initially the cheese samples had a TPM count of 5.47 ± 0.01 log CFU/g. The control cheese (C-) exhibited a sharp increase by day 7, reaching 7.73 ± 0.18 log CFU/g, indicating rapid growth of “cold-loving” microorganisms. In comparison, the coated samples showed lower microbial counts. The control coating resulted in 4.65 ± 0.54 log CFU/g, while cheese coated with S40(0.39) recorded 3.28 ± 0.23 log CFU/g, and cheese with S45(1.56) had 3.27 ± 0.34 log CFU/g. The cheese coated with S45(1.56) maintained the lowest TPM count at 5.61 ± 0.60 log CFU/g by day 14, showing its strong antimicrobial properties. By day 21, the TPM counts increased in all samples, with no significant differences between the control and the coated cheese, indicating microbial growth and adaptation occurring as storage progressed.

The initial TY count (Fig. 5c) for the cheese samples was 5.57 ± 0.18 log CFU/g. By day 21, the control cheese (C-) showed a significant increase in yeast growth, reaching 9.07 ± 0.08 log CFU/g. In contrast, the coated samples, particularly S45(1.56), demonstrated effective inhibition of yeast growth throughout the storage period. By day 14, the TY count for S45(1.56) was 2 log units lower than the uncoated control, at 6.58 ± 0.04 log CFU/g, compared to 8.65 ± 0.15 log CFU/g. Even at day 21, S45(1.56) maintained a significant lower TY count than the control (C-) (8.05 ± 0.45 log CFU/g). This indicates that the coating continued to inhibit yeast growth, although its effectiveness gradually declined over time.

White-brined cheeses are ripened and preserved in brine until delivered to customers. Traditionally produced in Balkan and Middle Eastern countries, these cheeses can range from soft, semi-soft, or semi-hard varieties. Yeast counts in these cheeses typically range from 103 to 105 CFU/g (Bintsis, 2021), with common species including Kluyveromyces lactis, Pichia fermentans, and occasionally Debaryomyces hansenii. At yeast populations of 105–106 CFU/g, yeasty and fermented off-flavors may develop, and as well the produced ethanol can react with short-chain fatty acids released during lipolysis to form fruity esters. Additionally, certain yeast species produce biogenic amines, contributing to off-flavors (Fröhlich-Wyder et al., 2019).

Al-Moghazy et al. (2021) extended the shelf life of Karish cheese by one week when coated with chitosan enriched with thyme EO. Furthermore, when coated with chitosan-liposomes containing thyme EO, the shelf life increased to four weeks. This improvement was attributed to the immobilization and protection of the EO from external reactions, enhanced emulsion stability, and controlled release of the bioactive compounds. The authors reported a significant reduction in mesophilic and psychrophilic bacteria, as well as yeast and mold compared to uncoated or cheese coated only with chitosan. Kontogianni et al. (2022) incorporated rosemary and sage infusion into whey protein concentrate (WPC) to coat soft cheese. Throughout a 60-day storage at 4 °C, the coated samples showed no spoilage or pathogenic bacteria, extending shelf life by 250 % (Kontogianni et al., 2022). In comparison, WPC alone extended the cheese shelf life by 20 %. Gammariello et al. (2008) studied the effects of EO from various plants in brie, to extend the shelf life of Fior di Latte cheese. The authors found that lemon, sage, and thyme significantly increased the cheese's microbial acceptability limit. The EO inhibited the spoilage microorganisms (specifically Pseudomonas spp.), without affecting the beneficial dairy microflora.

The initial pH values of all cheese samples ranged between 4.59 ± 0.01 and 4.64 ± 0.04 (Table 4), which is typical for acid-coagulated cheeses (Lucey, 2011), indicating that the addition of the coating composites did not significantly affect the initial pH of the product. Throughout the storage period, a significant increase in pH was observed in all samples. By day 21, the uncoated cheese (C-) exhibited the highest pH of 6.70 ± 0.28, indicating faster acid neutralization. In contrast, the coated samples maintained lower pH values (5.63 ± 0.01 – 5.69 ± 0.13). The S45(1.56) coated sample demonstrated the most stable pH profile, suggesting greater microbial and biochemical stability of the cheese during storage. Yeast present in cheese uses lactic acid, lactate and amino acids as substrates for growth, producing ammonia (NH3), leading to pH increase from approximately 5.0 at the beginning of ripening up to 7.5 (Fröhlich-Wyder et al., 2019).

Total titratable acidity (TTA) displayed an inverse relationship to pH. The uncoated cheese (C-) showed a sharp decrease in TTA, dropping from 1.94 ± 0.26 to 0.10 ± 0.04 % weight of lactic acid equivalent (LAE) by day 21, reflecting lactic acid consumption (Fröhlich-Wyder et al., 2019). In contrast, the coated cheeses retained higher TTA values, in particular the S45(1.56) formulation, with an average of 1.59 ± 0.34 % LAE at day 21.

These results are correlated with the general microbiological profile, suggesting that the coatings act as a microbial inhibitor, thereby preserving the biochemical integrity of the cheese during storage. Research studying the in situ effects of the coatings on chesses, to best of our knowledge, have mainly reported cases where the pH of the cheese decreases, leading to increased acidity (Al-Moghazy et al., 2021; Nottagh et al., 2020; Ríos-de-Benito et al., 2021). These variations can be attributed to differences in cheese types and the initial dairy microflora. However, it is well known that the pH increase is associated to the yeast microbiota proteolytic activity during cheese ripening (Fröhlich-Wyder et al., 2019), consistent with our microbiological data, pH levels, and TTA results.

Antilisterial activity of the coatings

Fig. 5d illustrates the survival of L. monocytogenes in Telemea cheese during storage. The initial artificial contamination level of the cheese with L. monocytogenes was 2.22 ± 0.02 log CFU/g. Throughout the storage period, L. monocytogenes counts decreased in all samples, but the reduction rate varied among samples. Statistical differences were first observed on 14th day of the study. By the end of the storage period, the viable L. monocytogenes cells count declined to 1.45 ± 0.28 log CFU/g in the uncoated cheese (C+), while in the control coated cheese, the viable L. monocytogenes cells count dropped to 1.17 ± 0.18 log CFU/g. Cheese with S40(0.39) and S45(1.56) coatings resulted in even lower counts of 0.97 ± 0.03 and 0.93 ± 0.01 log CFU/g, respectively. In summary, after 21 days of storage, the control sample (C+) showed a 0.77 log CFU/g reduction, the control coating presented a 1.05 log CFU/g reduction, while the S40 and S45 coatings caused a greater reduction of 1.25 log CFU/g and 1.29 log CFU/g, respectively.

The reduction of L. monocytogenes count during storage indicates that, in addition to the antilisterial potential of the coating formulations, the bacterium experiences growth suppression due to non-specific competition for nutrients with the dairy microflora. This phenomenon, known as the "Jameson effect", indicates that when nutrients are no longer sufficient, the growth of the "weaker" strains stops (Boldyreva et al., 2016; Mellefont et al., 2008). The So-SFE coatings demonstrate strong suppression activity against L. monocytogenes due to gradual release of antibacterial compounds, creating additional stress conditions, unfavorable for L. monocytogenes survival rate in the cheese samples. In both in vitro and in situ assessments, the control coating exhibited antilisterial properties. Chitosan's antibacterial effect is linked to its impact on cell membrane permeability, and ability to inhibit intracellular enzyme activity by binding to metal ions and interacting with DNA/mRNA, thereby suppressing the protein synthesis (Yan et al., 2023). Low temperatures diminish chitosan's effectiveness by limiting the number of available binding sites or alterations in electronegativity. In absence of diffusion, only the microorganisms which are in direct contact with the active sites are affected. Effective coatings can both inactivate existing L. monocytogenes on cheese surfaces and provide a barrier against growth when applied before contamination (Brown et al., 2018).

El-sawy et al. (El-sawy et al., 2024) used artificially contaminated milk for white cheese preparation and added sage hydroethanolic extract to evaluate its antilisterial potential. In the control cheese sample, at day 8, L. monocytogenes count decreased from 4.41 to 3.89 log CFU/g. Meanwhile, at day 8, in sage-enriched cheese the level dropped from 3.92 to 1.44 log CFU/g. Additionally, at day 10, L. monocytogenes was not detectable in the cheese (El-sawy et al., 2024), highlighting the efficacy of sage extract to reduce the L. monocytogenes contamination.

Conclusions

This study highlights the potential of supercritical fluid extracts from Salvia officinalis, obtained through a green extraction method, as a natural bioactive agent for application in active food packaging systems. Their incorporation into chitosan–gelatin–glycerol based matrices resulted in functional coating and film formulations with antimicrobial activity and enhanced barrier performance, demonstrating their suitability for improving food preservation and extending shelf life. Overall, these findings support the valorization of sage-derived extracts as promising natural alternative additives in biodegradable packaging materials. The in situ application on Telemea cheese further emphasizes their applicability in real food systems, particularly for products susceptible to microbial spoilage, while contributing to improved food safety and potentially enhancing product quality. Although preliminary in silico ADMET predictions, indicate favorable absorption profiles and low toxicity potential, these results require experimental validation. Future research should focus on key aspects relevant to practical application, including migration behavior, release kinetics, and long-term stability of the bioactive compounds in different food matrices and various storage conditions. Special attention should be given to potential compound transfer and accumulation in foods to ensure consumer safety and regulatory compliance. In addition, toxicological studies, together with sensory evaluation and consumer acceptability assessments, will be essential to support the transition of these materials from laboratory-scale research to industrial applications in sustainable, active, and edible food packaging systems.
