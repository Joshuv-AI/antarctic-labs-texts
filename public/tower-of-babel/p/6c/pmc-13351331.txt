Comparison of a multi‐analyte algorithmic immunoassay blood test with immunoprecipitation mass spectrometry for the detection of amyloid pathology
Authors: David Wilson, Karen Copeland, Lindsey Mette, Meenakshi Khare, Matt Polak, Jorge Marques Signes, Tim Skelton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's blood‐based biomarker (BBM) tests using single biomarkers or ratios are well characterized, but head‐to‐head comparisons of multi‐analyte algorithmic tests on shared cohorts are lacking. We compared a five‐biomarker algorithmic immunoassay (LucentAD Complete) with an algorithmic immunoprecipitation mass‐spectrometry (IP‐MS) benchmark on a shared plasma cohort.METHODSSymptomatic individuals (n = 192) with longitudinal samples from Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) were analyzed versus amyloid positron emission tomography (PET). Cross‐sectional (n = 115) and longitudinal (n = 179; 12‐year span) cohorts representing disease progression were used.RESULTSThe immunoassay amyloid risk score demonstrated a 0.94 area under the curve (AUC) and 92%–93% accuracy, achieving performance parity with IP‐MS metrics on shared samples. The two‐cutoff design yielded an intermediate zone of 10.4%–12.8%.DISCUSSIONThese results establish diagnostic parity between algorithmic immunoassay and algorithmic IP‐MS assay. In addition, providing individual biomarker results alongside a validated risk score offers a more granular foundation for comprehensive management than amyloid status alone, supporting premium reimbursement for multi‐analyte algorithmic BBMs in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

The clinical workup for Alzheimer's disease (AD) is being transformed by accessible blood‐based biomarker (BBM) tests. Although the initial wave of BBMs focused on single biomarkers or ratios, the next generation of diagnostics is defined by multi‐analyte algorithmic assays, such as the Simoa‐based LucentAD Complete and the immunoprecipitation mass‐spectrometry (IP‐MS)–based PrecivityAD2 test. These platforms move beyond single analytes or ratios, where their improved patient classification rates and diagnostic resolution provide more definitive clinical decision support, carrying meaningful implications for real‐world implementation and payeer decision‐making. 1 , 2 , 3 The established clinical validity of this testing class is underscored by recent Medicare pricing decisions establishing premium rates for multi‐analyte algorithmic assays, which reflect their expanded clinical utility compared to lower‐cost, single‐marker tests. 4

Despite high clinical relevance, a direct comparison of these algorithmic platforms using a shared, external cohort is lacking, introducing uncertainty for clinicians and payers. We compared LucentAD Complete to published IP‐MS performance metrics 5 using a matched symptomatic sub‐cohort from the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH)–sponsored BBM round‐robin study. 6 We provide a transparent assessment that contextualizes the assay's ability to reduce diagnostic ambiguity, address the impact of cohort prevalence, and highlight the operational advantages of multiplexed algorithmic testing.

METHODS

Participants and sample selection

The Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) was launched in 2003 as a public‐private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The original goal of ADNI was to test whether serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessments can be combined to measure the progression of mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and early AD. The current goals include validating biomarkers for clinical trials, improving the generalizability of ADNI data by increasing diversity in the participant cohort, and providing data concerning the diagnosis and progression of AD to the scientific community.

All ADNI volunteers consented to data sharing in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. 6 To benchmark against recently published IP‐MS data, 5 we evaluated the identical cohort examined by the IP‐MS comparator (n = 191 unique participants), drawing baseline and longitudinal samples from the FNIH/ADNI dataset. Of the original 355 participants provided by FNIH, 6 176 cognitively normal individuals were excluded, leaving 192 unique symptomatic participants (with MCI or dementia). The single participant discrepancy (191 vs 192) is due to the exclusion of one additional participant from IP‐MS analysis due to missing plasma amyloid data. Performance was assessed across a cross‐sectional sub‐cohort (n = 115) with available baseline data and a longitudinal sub‐cohort (n = 179 samples); demographic data are provided in Table S1.

The longitudinal group included follow‐up visits from 102 baseline individuals plus 77 subjects with follow‐up data only, providing a broad representation of clinical latency toward dementia (12‐ to 144‐month span). This reflects the heterogeneous disease stages and higher amyloid prevalence encountered in real‐world practice (Figure 1). Unlike typical cross‐sectional studies—which often reflect an idealized, narrow window of disease—this longitudinal distribution captures the evolving pathology and clinical noise characteristic of specialist evaluations. To maintain stringency, samples were excluded if a participant's clinical classification reverted to cognitively normal; this resulted in the exclusion of 13 longitudinal samples. All samples were analyzed within 6 months of the closest amyloid PET scan. 6

Assay, Aβ PET status, and analysis

Amyloid PET imaging utilized standard protocols for florbetapir or florbetaben, with the Centiloid (CL) unit estimate applied by the ADNI PET Core. 7 Amyloid positivity was defined using the pre‐specified cohort criteria established by the FNIH Biomarkers Consortium project team. 6 IP‐MS data from the PrecivityAD2 were reported previously. 5 The multi‐analyte immunoassay leveraged the established ATN(I) pathophysiological framework 8 , 9 representing four distinct biological disease axes. These included phosphorylated tau 217 (p‐tau217; T: LucentAD p‐Tau 217, a Janssen assay design 10 ), amyloid beta (Aβ)‐42/40 ratio (A: Aβ42/40), neurofilament light chain (N: NfL), and glial fibrillary acidic protein (I:GFAP). The amyloid ratio is calculated from individual Aβ42 and Aβ40 measurements, and these four non‐tau biomarkers are quantified utilizing the multiplexed N4PE kit. The LucentAD Complete algorithm integrates all the biomarkers via a logistic regression to generate a unified amyloid risk score. Development and clinical validation of LucentAD p‐Tau 217 and LucentAD Complete were described previously. 2 , 10

Statistical analysis

All data analysis was performed using JMP Pro (v.19.0 or higher). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to calculate the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, excluding results in the intermediate zone. Continuous biomarker distributions were visualized using box‐and‐whisker plots with overlaid kernel density estimations; inter‐group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Positive predictive values (PPVs) and negative predictive values (NPVs) were calculated across a range of clinically relevant amyloid prevalences (10% to 50%) using standard Bayesian equations. Statistical parity between the immunoassay and IP‐MS benchmarks was assessed via the overlap of their respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and the proximity of their point estimates across matching diagnostic metrics. All p‐values reported are two‐tailed, with a significance threshold of α = 0.05.

RESULTS

Diagnostic performance

Applying the classification thresholds for the amyloid risk score algorithmic output reported previously, 2 the LucentAD Complete assay demonstrated diagnostic accuracy statistically equivalent to reported IP‐MS metrics 5 on the shared cohort (Table 1). The multi‐analyte algorithm also outperformed standalone p‐tau217 (Table 1). In the cross‐sectional baseline sub‐cohort (n = 115), where amyloid prevalence was 40.9%, the immunoassay algorithm achieved an AUC of 0.94 (95% CI: 0.87–0.97), with 90.0% sensitivity and 95.2% specificity. In the longitudinal sub‐cohort (n = 179), amyloid prevalence increased to 54.2%, consistent with expected disease progression. Despite the changing disease burden, performance remained consistent (AUC 0.94, 95% CI: 0.89–0.96). Diagnostic likelihood ratios were 17.36 and 16.91 for baseline and longitudinal sub‐cohorts, respectively (Table S2). The high performance of the immunoassay algorithm is supported by significant amyloid‐dependent separation mapping to the four axes of the established ATN(I) framework. 8 , 9 This includes statistically robust differentiation across the amyloid (Aβ42/40), tau (p‐tau217), and neuroinflammation (GFAP) axes (all p’s < 0.0001), as well as the neurodegeneration axis (NfL; p = 0.0024) (Figure 2), with individual biomarker correlation profiles detailed in Figure S1.

Clinical utility and diagnostic adjudication

The immunoassay algorithmic approach yielded a three‐fold reduction in the intermediate zone (10.4%–12.8%) compared to p‐tau217 alone (32.2%–38.0%). This diagnostic resolution is illustrated in Figure 3, where the integration of orthogonal biomarkers (Aβ42/40, GFAP, NfL) provides marked vertical separation between amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative individuals falling within the intermediate p‐tau217 range (0.04–0.09 pg/mL). Within this intermediate zone, the multi‐analyte risk score accurately dispositioned 68.2% of participants who would have otherwise remained unclassified, with an accuracy of 89.2% and 84.2% for low risk and high risk, respectively, and overall accuracy of 86.7% (Table S3). This resolution is driven by the synergistic integration of orthogonal biomarkers, which are applied only on p‐tau217 intermediate zone cases between 0.04 and 0.09 pg/mL, providing a significant increase in discriminatory power. Although secondary biomarkers showed modest individual performance among these intermediate cases—with AUCs for Aβ42/40 and GFAP at 0.76 and 0.70, respectively—their contributions were approximately equal to that of p‐tau217 (AUC 0.75). However, combined together, the full multi‐analyte risk score achieved an AUC of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.79–0.91) among intermediate zone cases (Table S4). This improvement indicates the non‐tau secondary biomarkers have a critical adjudication role in the difficult‐to‐classify borderline cases.

Case‐level analysis further illustrates this intermediate zone ‘rescue’ mechanism driven by multi‐axis biology. For example, the algorithm resolved borderline p‐tau217 signals by weighing the additional biological evidence of neuroinflammation and amyloid dysregulation; in some rule‐out rescue cases, low GFAP concentrations acted as an orthogonal neuroinflammatory check, correctly identifying PET‐negative individuals despite equivocal amyloid and tau marker signals. Conversely, amyloid‐positive rescues were driven by the coincident alignment of depressed Aβ42/40 ratios and elevated GFAP, providing high‐confidence adjudication where standalone p‐tau217 was non‐definitive. Longitudinally, as prevalence increased, the PPV rose to 95.2%, whereas the NPV remained robust at 88.0%—matching the IP‐MS NPV (Table 1). To facilitate clinical interpretation, we modeled predictive values across a prevalence spectrum of 10%–80% in comparison to reported IP‐MS benchmarks (Table 2). The LucentAD Complete assay exhibited an apparent PPV advantage over the IP‐MS benchmark across all modeled prevalence tiers, although this advantage did not reach statistical significance. Both assays demonstrated exceptional NPV (97% at 20% prevalence), confirming high‐confidence utility for amyloid rule‐out in lower prevalence settings such as primary care. Diagnostic accuracy remained stable across the longitudinal cohort (91.7% to 93.2%), matching or exceeding the IP‐MS results even as the number of dementia cases evolved from 2 to 28 (Table 3), validating reliability across this clinical spectrum.

Characterization of the algorithmic intermediate zone

Building on the 68.2% adjudication yield observed in Section 3.2, the remaining 31.8% of participants in the multi‐analyte intermediate zone (11.9% of the total cohort) were analyzed. MCI participants accounted for 94.3% of those remaining in this category, whereas overt dementia cases were almost entirely adjudicated into the high‐risk pool. This suggests that the uncertainty of these cases reflects early‐stage clinical latency where PET‐visible plaque density has not yet reached a positive threshold. This is supported by a detailed analysis of the underlying biomarker profiles. When compared to amyloid‐negative controls, participants in the intermediate zone exhibited a distinct pro‐pathological signature, characterized by a significant 25% elevation in GFAP (mean 143.6 vs 115.3 pg/mL; p = 0.004) and a 34% increase in NfL concentrations (23.9 vs 17.9 pg/mL; p < 0.01). This was further corroborated by a measurable 11% decrease in the Aβ42/40 ratio (p = 0.01). Taken together, these data indicate that the intermediate zone serves as a sensitive filter for early‐stage pathology, explicitly mapping a biologically active transition state characterized by reactive astrogliosis, neurodegeneration, and nascent amyloid dysregulation.

Notably, these data also highlight the limitations of diagnostic approaches that utilize a single threshold to force a binary positive or negative call. Although single‐cutoff classification models offer reporting simplicity, they inherently collapse the biological spectrum of AD into a binary that lacks clinical nuance. By forcing a positive or negative call on participants in the transition zone, such models risk misclassifying a biologically active population—who exhibit significant GFAP and NfL elevations—as either false negatives or premature positives, thereby obscuring the patients who require closer clinical vigilance.

In contrast, the three‐tier algorithmic model preserves and reports on this biological signal, providing a window for potentially high‐value, add clinical management. By identifying patients who are pathologically active but perhaps sub‐threshold for PET imaging, this approach supports a strategy of active monitoring and serial re‐testing, effectively matching the intensity of the diagnostic workup to the velocity of the underlying biological transition.

DISCUSSION

Performance parity

The diagnostic performance of the LucentAD Complete multiplexed digital immunoassay provides critical market context for multi‐analyte algorithmic tests. Our findings—including an AUC of 0.94, specificity of 94%–95%, and sensitivity of 90%—demonstrate statistical parity with reported IP‐MS benchmarks using a shared subset of individuals from the same ADNI cohort. These immunoassay performance metrics represent the aggregate diagnostic performance across both the baseline and longitudinal sub‐cohorts, confirming parity with IP‐MS benchmarks across a symptomatic spectrum rather than solely within a specific sub‐population.

This convergence of performance suggests that the immunoassay achieves parity by capturing a fuller biological footprint of the disease. By integrating markers across the four axes of the expanded ATN(I) framework, 8 , 9 the algorithmic model compensates for single‐analyte fluctuations or uncertainty to give a more robust aggregate diagnostic signal than single‐analyte or ratio immunoassay approaches. Of note, although the IP‐MS based benchmark reported a higher amyloid prevalence (56.0%) suggestive of longitudinal enrichment of their test cohort, 5 our study demonstrates that comparable high‐tier diagnostic performance is maintained across both baseline (prevalence 40.9%) and longitudinal (prevalence 54.2%) sub‐cohorts. This stability across varying prevalence tiers is an important distinction. Cohorts with more advanced pathology inherently present an easier classification target due to starker biological separation. By demonstrating performance parity in the lower‐prevalence baseline cohort—which presents a more nascent disease profile—these data confirm that the immunoassay algorithm maintains high diagnostic integrity across a spectrum of clinical progression, rather than a single sample mix weighted toward later‐stage pathology.

Although previous reports have indicated IP‐MS outperformed standard immunoassays, 11 the concordance reported here suggests that next‐generation multi‐analyte algorithmic tests have reached a ‘gold standard’ performance convergence. This parity, complemented by current Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) professional services valuations, establishes a clear clinical value for this testing class and provides a foundation for reimbursement across technologies. However, statistical parity in aggregate performance is only part of the equation; the immunoassay multi‐analyte algorithm provides greater clinical granularity by identifying the transition‐state population that binary models inherently obscure.

Reducing diagnostic ambiguity

A key clinical advancement of the immunoassay multi‐analyte approach is the significant reduction of diagnostic ambiguity. LucentAD Complete significantly reduced the intermediate zone (10.4%–12.8%), dramatically outperforming p‐tau217 alone on the same cohorts (32.2%–38.0%). Although the IP‐MS–based comparator utilizes a single‐cutoff binary result, our findings in Section 3.3 suggest that this uncertainty is not merely statistical noise. Rather, the significant elevations in GFAP and NfL within the intermediate zone identify a pathologically active transition state. By flagging this zone and reporting the underlying individual biomarker results, the two‐cutoff approach transforms a potential false certainty into a deliberate clinical signal for active monitoring. In previous analysis of IP‐MS data, ≈10% of patients fell into this intermediate zone of uncertainty where PET‐positive and PET‐negative results were statistically indistinguishable and inclusion of a defined intermediate zone did not significantly improve accuracy metrics. 5 By contrast, the two‐cutoff design explicitly flags this transition zone, a strategy validated by recent pathological staging models showing that plasma GFAP and p‐tau217 exhibit significant positive inflection across the sub‐threshold 10–30 CL boundary, reflecting reactive astrogliosis and early amyloid accumulation well before established positivity is achieved on amyloid PET. 12 This approach aligns with expert clinical recommendations ensuring that ‘high‐risk’ or ‘low‐risk’ classifications maintain the highest possible diagnostic certainty. 3

Analytical robustness and operational efficiency

Both multi‐analyte algorithmic methods prioritize the Aβ42/40 ratio for its superior analytical stability. This is a critical distinction from single‐plex chemiluminescent platforms using the p‐tau217/Aβ42 ratio, which is susceptible to pre‐analytical handling variations due to the inherent instability and rapid aggregation of Aβ42. 13 , 14 While capturing the Aβ42/40 ratio on single‐plex platforms is technically and operationally burdensome—requiring multiple separate assays—multiplexing capability allows for the facile incorporation of this ratio alongside other biomarkers in a single assay, streamlining workflow. Because the ratio acts as an internal biological control, the diagnostic signal is preserved across pre‐analytical variables, such as sample handling and thermal instability. This approach maximizes the diagnostic yield and provides critical robustness for a front‐line diagnostic intended for broad use, mitigating the risk of false results driven by protein sequestration rather than underlying pathology.

Comprehensive clinical utility

LucentAD Complete is currently the only multi‐analyte method to integrate NfL and GFAP, completing the four‐axis ATN(I) framework. Critically, the assay moves beyond a singular ‘black box’ risk score by providing individual quantitative results for each biomarker. These results are reported with reference intervals and linkage to large, age‐adjusted established normative databases, providing clinicians with the necessary interpretive context to guide disease stage and trajectory assessment. This multi‐dimensional reporting supports a more granular clinical workup: GFAP in conjunction with p‐tau217 provides prognostic insight into the progression toward tau‐positivity (A+/T+), 15 , 16 as astrocyte reactivity has been shown to be a necessary upstream mediator for the spread of tau pathology. 14 Simultaneously, the addition of NfL assists in the differential diagnosis of non‐AD neurodegeneration, such as frontotemporal dementia (FTD) or vascular pathology. 17 , 18

This distinction is noteworthy; assays limited to evaluating only amyloid status offer a restricted view of the patient's pathology. A multi‐analyte integrated reporting structure ensures that the clinician receives a more comprehensive foundation for patient management than amyloid status alone can provide, further justifying the test's value and its established premium reimbursement status. 4 Notably, our data demonstrating significant GFAP and NfL elevation in intermediate‐risk participants provide an empirical basis for serial monitoring. This allows clinicians to maintain vigilance in the nascent phase of disease, matching the intensity of the workup to the velocity of the biological change—a capability absent in standalone p‐tau217 or binary IP‐MS assays.

Methodological considerations

Several limitations warrant mention. First, although the ADNI subgroups allow for a robust comparative power, this is not a true simultaneous head‐to‐head analysis, as exact comparator sample lists were not available. Second, the ADNI cohort, although well characterized, is not representative of heterogeneous real‐world clinic populations where these assays are currently being used. In addition, while subgroup analyses regarding co‐morbidities and cognitive status were beyond the scope of this study, they remain critical areas for future investigations in more broadly powered real‐world cohorts. Finally, the reliance on PET as a surrogate gold standard introduces its own methodological variability, though this applies equally to all comparative methods. Prospective studies in diverse, real‐world clinical settings are currently ongoing to further validate these findings.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the LucentAD Complete assay achieves diagnostic performance statistically equivalent to a leading IP‐MS method while minimizing diagnostic ambiguity through a pre‐specified two‐cutoff design. By leveraging the analytical stability of the multiplexed Aβ42/40 ratio and providing granular data on neurodegeneration and astrogliosis, the assay offers a more comprehensive foundation for patient management than traditional amyloid‐only tests. In addition, the identification of a pathologically active transition state within the intermediate zone suggests a high‐value application in clinical trial design—by capturing patients with emerging pro‐pathological signatures who may be sub‐threshold for PET imaging, this multi‐analyte approach provides a promising means for a more thorough identification of candidates for early therapeutic intervention.

In summary, the LucentAD Complete assay offers expanded patient management utility, operational efficiency, and transparency, providing a strong, data‐driven rationale for its integration into clinical practice, pharmaceutical development, and favorable reimbursement policies.
