Targeting memory of embedded positive verbal content indirectly modulates negative visual memory
Authors: Stas Kozak, Taly Markovits, Noam Hacohen, Yair Bar-Haim, Nitzan Censor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryNegative visual memories are persistent distressing mental images and a hallmark symptom of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). However, ongoing attempts to directly modulate negative memories are often ineffective. Here, we show that targeting the memory with embedded positive verbal content can indirectly modulate negative visual memory. Participants encoded sequences of positive words embedded within negative pictures. Following encoding, participants were instructed to remember or to forget the embedded words. Results show that verbal memory was weaker when participants were directly instructed to forget relative to remember the words. Importantly, intentional forgetting of positive verbal memory indirectly reduced negative visual memory for pictures that followed the words. An additional experiment revealed that in the absence of verbal memory retrieval, instructions to remember the words indirectly reduced negative visual memory. These findings emphasize the potential of positive experiences to downregulate negative memories, possibly uncovering future strategies for treating psychopathologies such as PTSD.Subject areas: Neuroscience, Clinical neuroscience, Cognitive neuroscience, Psychology

Introduction

Over the course of our lives, we experience distressing events that we may prefer to forget. However, contrary to such desires, negative experiences often remain in our memory as they attract more attention and cognitive resources than neutral events and are often encoded into durable negative memories.1,2,3 Following encoding, negative memories tend to persist over time4 through repeated memory reactivations2,5 while maintaining their emotional intensity and vividness6,7,8 by engaging sensory brain areas and the amygdala during memory encoding and retrieval.1,2,9 In addition, negative memories are prioritized at retrieval and are harder to deliberately suppress relative to neutral visual memories,10,11,12,13 promoting distress long after the negative event had terminated. At an extreme, negative memories can become maladaptive as their excessive persistence and involuntary intrusive retrieval are linked to psychopathologies such as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD).14

The enduring nature of negative memories and their associated emotional burden encouraged research on direct memory modulation.15,16 However, reliable direct modulation of negative memories in humans remains elusive.16,17,18,19 Pharmacological and neuromodulation methods show some promise in modulating the emotional intensity of negative memories,20,21,22,23 but these findings do not appear to replicate consistently.16,17,24,25 Therefore, first-line clinical practices typically rely on behavioral exposure in the context of cognitive-behavioral therapy.26,27 However, direct exposure of patients to traumatic memories causes substantial distress, precipitating high dropout rates,28 and is often ineffective in preventing traumatic memory reexperiencing.29 In addition, negative emotional responses and memory intrusion sometimes relapse after treatment termination.30,31

Given the obstacles associated with direct modulation of negative memories, we tested the possibility of indirect modulation of such memories. It has been recently suggested that positive memories have the power to downregulate negative memories.13 Positive emotions tend to reduce stress-related responses32 and negative affect.13 Accordingly, the retrieval of positive memories buffers stress33,34,35,36 and improves negative mood.37,38,39 Associating negative memories with positive meaning can attenuate their negative emotional intensity,40,41 implying that positive memories may have the capacity to indirectly weaken negative memories.

Here, we aimed to indirectly modulate negative visual memories by direct modulation of adjacent positive verbal content. Humans are capable of directly modulating their memories when instructed to remember or to forget newly encoded information, as demonstrated by the list-method directed forgetting (DF) paradigm.42,43,44 Intentional forgetting of previously encoded content elicits prefrontal inhibitory control over mnemonic hippocampal processing, leading to decreased content memory strength.42,43,44 However, the intentional forgetting of negative content is generally less effective,45,46,47 and negative context can impair the intentional forgetting of neutral content.48 In turn, intentional forgetting of positive content is typically reliable,45,46 and positive content, which suggested to mitigate the effects of negative memories,13 may indirectly modulate negative memories. Leveraging approaches for indirect memory modulation,49,50,51,52,53 in a between-subjects design, we first had participants study two lists of positive words interleaved with negative pictures. After studying the first list, participants were instructed either to remember or to forget the words of the list they just saw, in line with the DF list-method paradigm.42,43,44 Importantly, the direct modulation of positive verbal memory by instruction was geared to indirectly modulate negative visual memories (Figure 1A). Upon studying a second list of words, verbal and visual memories were measured using word-free recall and two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) picture recognition, respectively (Figure 1B). We hypothesized that the instructions for the words will directly modulate positive verbal memory strength while indirectly modulating negative visual memory strength. In a second experiment, we tested whether verbal memory recall in itself54,55,56 affects indirect visual memory modulation. We reasoned that the absence of verbal memory retrieval may differentially affect negative visual memory modulation.

Study design

(A) Participants were asked to study two lists of positive words interleaved within negative pictures. Upon studying the first list of words (List1), participants were instructed to remember or to forget the studied words. Then, participants studied an additional list of words (List2) and were instructed to remember it.

(B) Prior to measuring individual verbal and visual memory strength, participants completed a distractor task to reduce deliberate rehearsal of the words. Later participants performed word-free recall (verbal memory test) and a 2AFC picture recognition (visual memory test). The pictures illustrated are from the Disgust-Related Images (DIRTI) and the Nencki Affective Picture System (NAPS) databases.57,58

Results

Direct modulation of positive verbal memory

Positive verbal memory was affected by the instructions for the words (Remember/Forget) and the study list order (List1/List2) (F(1,32) = 5.08, p = 0.031, η2p = 0.137) (Figure 2A). Verbal memory for List1 was weaker when participants were directly instructed to forget relative to remember the words, consistent with the intentional forgetting effect42,43 (t(32) = −2.47, p = 0.019, d = −0.848) (Figure 2A, left). Verbal memory strength for List2 was similar regardless of whether List1 words were instructed to be remembered or to be forgotten (t(32) = 0.79, p = 0.438, d = 0.269) (Figure 2A, right).

Direct modulation of positive verbal memory resulting in indirect modulation of subsequent negative visual memory

(A) Instructions for the positive words directly modulated verbal memory strength. The instructions to forget the words (blue) of List1 reduced verbal memory strength, compared to the instructions to remember the words (red). These instructions did not affect verbal memory for the words of List2.

(B) There were no differences in negative visual memory strength for both types of instructions (Remember/Forget) and list order (List1/List2).

(C) Visual negative memory for pictures appearing prior to the words was not affected by the direct modulation of the positive verbal memory.

(D) However, visual negative memory for pictures following the words was indirectly modulated by the instructions for the words. Visual memory strength was weaker under the instruction to forget the words (light blue) compared to the instruction to remember the words (light red). Error bars represent ±1 standard error of the mean (SEM) ∗p < 0.05.

Indirect modulation of negative visual memory

Next, we tested whether the direct modulation of positive verbal memory indirectly modulated negative visual memory. Negative visual memory showed comparable strength under the instructions to remember and to forget the words (F(1,32) = 2.202, p = 0.148, η2p = 0.064) and was not affected by the interaction between the type of instruction (Remember/Forget) and the list order (List1/List2) (F(1,32) = 0.082, p = 0.777, η2p = 0.003) (Figure 2B). Previous findings indicate that direct memory modulation can differently affect memory for preceding59,60,61,62 or following63,64,65,66,67,68,69 events; therefore, we tested whether verbal memory modulation affected negative visual memory for pictures appearing before or after the words. Visual memory strength across study lists for pictures appearing before the words was equivalent under both instruction types (Remember/Forget) (F(1,32) = 0.111, p = 0.741, η2p = 0.003) (Figure 2C). However, direct instruction to forget, relative to remember the words, indirectly reduced negative visual memory strength across study lists for pictures that followed the words (F(1,32) = 4.536, p = 0.041, η2p = 0.124) (Figure 2D), implying indirect modulation of negative visual memory.

Previous studies have shown that competition between memories could lead to indirect memory modulation.50,51,53 Therefore, to confirm and better understand the current results, we examined the interactions between verbal memory and negative visual memory at the single-trial level, using a predictive model that evaluates single-trial performance. Consistent with our previous results, the model did not predict negative visual memory strength for pictures preceding the words (χ2(3) = 0.431, p = 0.934), and none of its predictors could explain negative visual memory strength (neither the instructions for the words [β = −0.09, SE = 0.25, Z = 0.346, p = 0.730], the list order [β = 0.01, SE = 0.16, Z = 0.067, p = 0.947], nor verbal memory performance [β = 0.1, SE = 0.18, Z = 0.548, p = 0.583]) (Figure 3A, left). In contrast, the model did predict negative visual memory strength for pictures that followed the words (χ2(3) = 7.91, p = 0.048), suggesting their memory was indirectly modulated. Further, the instruction to forget the words reduced subsequent negative visual memory (β = −0.55, SE = 0.26, Z = −2.068, p = 0.039), but not list order (β = −0.28, SE = 0.16, Z = −1.764, p = 0.078) or verbal memory performance (β = 0.14, SE = 0.17, Z = 0.811, p = 0.417), suggesting that intentional forgetting applied for the words specifically lead to indirect inhibition of negative visual memory (Figure 3A, right). Additional Bayesian analysis found no evidence for the instruction type (Remember/Forget) indirectly affecting preceding visual memory (MedianF-R = −1.09, 95% highest density interval (HDI) [−8.95, 6.44], Figure 3B). However, the analysis confirmed that the instruction to forget relative to remember the words indirectly reduced negative visual memory (MedianF-R = −6.93, 95% HDI [−14.75, 0.29], Figure 3C).

(B) Bayesian analysis found no evidence for indirect negative visual memory modulation by the instruction for the words.

The second experiment tested whether verbal memory recall affects indirect visual memory modulation. Results showed that in the absence of verbal memory retrieval, negative visual memory strength was lower under the instructions to remember relative to forget the words (F(1,32) = 6.526, p = 0.016, η2p = 0.169) (Figure 4A), indicative of indirect negative memory modulation. Negative visual memory strength across the study lists for pictures appearing before the words was similar under both instruction types (Remember/Forget) (F(1,32) = 2.173, p = 0.15, η2p = 0.064) (Figure 4B). However, direct instruction to remember, relative to forget the words, indirectly reduced negative visual memory strength across study lists for pictures that followed the words (F(1,32) = 9.321, p = 0.005, η2p = 0.226) (Figure 4C).

Discussion

The current results indicate that direct modulation of positive verbal memory indirectly modulated negative visual memory. In the first experiment, the instruction to forget, relative to remember the words, resulted in weaker positive verbal memory strength, consistent with the well-established DF literature.42,43,44 Additionally, the instructions for the words indirectly modulated subsequent negative visual memory, with the instruction to forget relative to remember the positive words attenuating the negative memory. This is contrary to neutral verbal memory, which fails to elicit indirect modulation of negative visual memory,48 suggesting that positive valence may play a role in the attenuation of negative visual memories. A single-trial level model further ascertained that the instruction to forget the words, but not list order or verbal memory strength, predicted reduction in negative visual memory. In the second experiment, which did not involve word recall, negative visual memory was lower under the instructions to remember relative to forget the words. Consistent with the first experiment, the instructions for the words indirectly modulated subsequent negative visual memory but not preceding negative visual memory. Together, the current findings suggest that modulation of positive verbal memory can counteract the dominance of negative visual memory and enable its indirect modulation.

Several mechanisms may support the indirect modulation of negative visual memories. Previous studies have shown that suppressing thoughts about neutral content can indirectly modulate related negative visual memory during encoding.70,71 Reactivation of positive memories during sleep can also downregulate associated negative visual memories by inducing competition between memories,72 possibly through mechanisms of retrieval-induced forgetting.44 The current findings suggest that indirect modulation of negative visual memory can be mediated by the retrieval of adjacent verbal memory. Verbal memory retrieval may reactivate the associated visual-context memory73,74,75,76,77 and facilitate integration between verbal memory and negative visual memory,78,79,80,81 enabling synchronized intentional forgetting of both types of memories. However, in the absence of verbal memory retrieval, visual negative memory is likely to be moderately activated,66 leading to its differentiation from the verbal memory and competition between visual and verbal memories following the instruction to remember the words,82 as shown in previous studies.53 The results may also be explained in the context of retrieval-induced forgetting.44 In the second experiment, in the absence of memory retrieval, words were more accessible following the Remember instruction,83,84,85 resulting in attenuation of the memory for the pictures. Thus, mechanisms of retrieval-induced forgetting may have operated in the absence of purposeful retrieval, consistent with previous studies.53 In the first experiment, involving active recall, the difficulty in attempting to recall words in the forget condition may have led to suppression of the associated negative pictures.

The results indicate that visual memory was similarly modulated between lists. In the remember condition, since the same instructions were applied to both lists, it is conceivable that visual memory modulation was not confined to a specific list. In the forget condition, intentional forgetting may affect any competing memory that is not the verbal memory of List2,42,43,86,87,88 resulting in the observed carry-over effects across lists. Further experimentation is needed to clarify these observations.

The current study demonstrates indirect modulation of negative visual recognition memory, suggesting an attenuation in negative visual memory strength,89,90 which may be important for the downregulation of traumatic memories.91 Intrusive trauma memories and reexperiencing are mostly visual in nature14,19,92 and rely on engagement of visual sensory regions,93 implying that visual memory trace fidelity drives memory-related psychopathologies. Accordingly, visual recognition of traumatic events that lead to intrusions is sustained over time94,95 and is positively correlated with PTSD severity and intrusive memory reexperiencing.96 Interventions aimed to attenuate negative emotional intensity often do not reduce intrusive memory reexperiencing,17,19 which may persist even after their emotional intensity was decreased.22 Additionally, inhibiting the likelihood of negative visual memory retrieval when encountering a specific reminder70,71,72,97,98 can be challenging as traumatic memories are associated with many cues that require downregulation.99,100,101 Here, we show that direct targeting of positive stimuli can indirectly weaken negative visual memory fidelity. Such an approach may prove effective in attenuating the persistence of traumatic mental images91 and warrants testing in participants with PTSD.

Although usually intentional forgetting of List1 words leads to better verbal memory for List2, known as the benefit effect,42,43 this effect was not observed in the current study. When participants are instructed to recall the words of List1 first, instead of List2, the benefit effect could be impaired.102 However, previous studies indicate that when given a free recall choice as in the current study, the benefit effect is evident.103 Therefore, the negative pictures presented in our study could have elevated participant’s cognitive load, thereby reducing the benefit effect.86,104 The presence of the List1 cost effect may be consistent with previous research pointing to its independence of the List2 benefit effect,86 indicating that it may appear even when the benefit effect is absent53,105,106,107 and that it does not depend on recall output order.102 Nevertheless, future studies could determine the influence of positive word recall order on List2 memory.

While the DF list-method usually directly modulates recall memory,42,43 modulation of recognition memory could be observed when the studied items are difficult to elaboratively encode or when detailed memory retrieval is required during memory test.108 Accordingly, in the current study, participants observed scene images that are difficult to elaboratively encode and were required to discriminate them from similar lures. Importantly, the DF list-method is thought to modulate mental context109,110,111 and has previously shown to indirectly modulate recognition memory for contextual elements such as color and source,112,113 suggesting that intentional forgetting may indirectly modulate the recognition for contextual images. Accordingly, indirect modulation of contextual neutral visual memory recognition has previously been demonstrated using a similar paradigm.53 However, although previous research has indicated that DF modulates the recognition of visual perceptual details more than gist,114 future studies could examine whether indirect memory recognition modulation is specific to fine visual representations or could be extended to gist-based representations.

It is likely that the indirect modulation of negative visual memories was mediated by the reward network. Positive memories are intrinsically rewarding and activate the reward network.115 Reward network activity can elevate resilience to stress and distress,32,116,117 which reduces the reexperiencing of negative visual memories.118,119,120,121,122 In the current study, positive verbal memories may have increased individuals’ ability to withstand the aversive negative context, thus allowing the modulation of negative visual memory. Future studies should examine the interaction between reward network activity, triggered by positive valence, and frontal network engagement related to memory control,44 to disentangle the neural mechanisms of indirect negative memory attenuation.

The current findings suggest that positive emotions promote memory control under negative context. Negative context usually impairs memory for related neutral events by prioritizing attentional and encoding resources toward the negative event, at the expense of the related neutral event.3,123,124 Additionally, intentional control over neutral memories is impaired by the presence of negative visual context.48,125,126 However, here we found that individuals can preserve control over positive memories under a negative visual context. Positive emotions, compared to neutral emotions, support intentional memory upregulation by enhancing the memorability of the encoded content127,128,129 and elevating content memory resistance to interference from negative emotions.127,130,131 Further, positive mood can facilitate memory downregulation by increasing working memory capacity,132,133 which has been associated with greater intentional forgetting.134,135 Of note, negative context can also strengthen related neutral and positive memories136,137,138,139,140 when they precede negative events136,137 or are linked to a threatening context.139,140

In conclusion, comprehensive lines of evidence indicate that positive memories can alter negative emotions13; however, the capacity of positive memories to downregulate negative memories is mostly unexplored. The current study demonstrates that direct modulation of positive memories overcomes the dominance of negative visual memories and indirectly attenuates their strength. In clinical settings, intentional forgetting of positive content preceding a traumatic event may indirectly downregulate subsequent traumatic visual memory. In the absence of preceding positive experience, the valence of neutral elements associated with trauma could be elevated,141,142 potentially enabling subsequent indirect modulation. Additionally, associating consolidated traumatic memories with new positive content following deliberate traumatic memory reactivation or spontaneous intrusive memory reactivation14,16,18,143 may enable indirect modulation of traumatic memories. The integrative power of positive memories13,144,145 may also help incorporate maladaptive visual memories into coherent, adaptive memories, potentially benefiting psychopathological outcomes.146,147 These findings emphasize the potential of positive experiences to modulate negative memories, possibly uncovering future approaches to downregulate negative visual memories and open new avenues for treating psychopathologies such as PTSD.

Limitations of the study

It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the current study. Here, we demonstrated indirect modulation of recently acquired negative visual memories. Consolidated negative memories are likely harder to modulate,148,149,150 as they become distributed across multiple neural networks.150 Traumatic memories are especially challenging to downregulate, often eliciting strong dissociative reexperiencing, known as flashbacks,151 implying that they may be more potent than other negative memories and that their attenuation could require more robust intervention. Traumatic memories also exhibit distinct neural representations compared to other negative memories,152 which may be less responsive to indirect memory modulation. Additionally, we did not examine sex-specific effects of indirect memory modulation, which may be manifested differently among men and women.

Experimental model and study participant details

Thirty-seven healthy adult volunteers (24 females, Mage = 26.16 years, SD = 4.3, Range = 20–39, see Table S1) participated in the first experiment and thirty-six in the second experiment (29 females, Mage = 24.44 years, SD = 2.58, Range = 18–31, see Table S2). Participants were recruited to the study through an online advertisement. Participants provided a written consent prior to testing and were compensated $12 or course credit. All participants reported no history of neurological or mental disorders and sleeping for at least 6 hours the night before the experimental session. One participant was excluded due to difficulty in understanding task instructions, another due to reporting sleeping less than 6 hours the prior night, and three participants decided to stop participation due to the emotional content presented in the study. Sample size was determined based on a calculated effect size of a pilot study (η2p = 0.197, power = 0.9). The study was approved by the Tel Aviv University Institutional Review Board (approval no. 0000510-8) and was performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Method details

Positive words

Positive words were selected from a Hebrew emotional words database.153,154 The words were divided into pairs matched by valence (Mean = 7.83, SD = 0.47, Range = 7.14-8.81 on a 1-9 scale), arousal (Mean = 5.91, SD = 0.78, Range = 4.21-7.79 on a 1-9 scale), number of letters (Mean = 4.22, SD = 0.79, Range = 3-6), number of syllables (Mean = 2.22, SD = 0.55, Range = 1-3), and frequency of appearance in the Hebrew language (https://hlp.nite.org.il/WebCorpora.aspx). Each word from these word-pairs was randomly allocated to one of the two study lists (each list consisted of 16 words). To avoid semantic overlap between presented words and pictures, the studied words were of abstract concepts with minimum relation to the pictures.

Negative pictures

Negative pictures were selected from various emotional images databases,57,58,155,156,157 based on the criteria of valence scores lower than 4 on a 1–9 Self-Assessment Manikin scale.53 Participants’ ratings in the first experiment confirmed that the pictures were experienced as negative (M = 3.09, SE = 0.14) (rating lower than 4 on the Manikin scale), relative to neutral pictures (M = 5.76, SE = 0.09), in both experimental conditions (F(1,32) = 213.41, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.87). Participants rated the negative pictures as more arousing (M = 5.55, SE = 0.24) than neutral pictures (M = 2.62, SE = 0.24), in both experimental conditions (F(1,32) = 301.63, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.9). Participants’ ratings in the second experiment confirmed also that the pictures were experienced as negative (M = 3.19, SE = 0.1) relative to neutral pictures (M = 5.96, SE = 0.13), in both experimental conditions (F(1,32) = 348.86, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.916). Accordingly, participants rated the negative pictures as more arousing (M = 5.07, SE = 0.26) than the neutral pictures (M = 2.36, SE = 0.18), in both experimental conditions (F(1,32) = 159.521, p < 0.001, η2p = 0.833).

Procedure

Participants completed a list-method DF paradigm, combined with emotionally negative pictures.48 Each participant saw two lists containing positive words (16 per list), embedded within negative pictorial context (32 per list).57,58,155,156,157 Both words and pictures were randomized across lists. Each encoding trial featured a triplet of picture-word-picture (Figure 1A), with words presented for 3 seconds and pictures for 1 second. Inter-stimulus-interval (ISI) between trials was 3 sec. After encoding the words of List1, half of the participants received a cue to remember (n = 17) and half a cue to forget (n = 17) the just studied words. Importantly, to evaluate indirect modulation of the negative memory and to avoid deliberate rehearsal, participants did not receive any instructions regarding the negative pictures. Both groups received the instruction to remember the words of List2, since the encoding of List2 is considered vital for the intentional forgetting of List1105,109,158 and is consistent with the indirect modulation of neutral visual memory.53 To reduce deliberate words memorization and recency effects, participants solved arithmetic problems for 1 minute as a distractor prior to the memory tests48,53,110 (Figure 1B).

Following the distractor task, participants’ verbal and visual memory strength was evaluated (Figure 1B). First, participants were asked to recall and write down as many words as they can from both word lists (List1 and List2), including the list they were asked to forget (List1). Then, participants performed a 2AFC picture recognition test. In each trial participants were presented with two pictures, one that appeared previously during encoding and a foil picture with the same semantic content and similar visual features that was presented for the first time. Using the left and right arrow buttons, participants were asked to select the picture they have previously seen as quickly and accurately as possible. In the second experiment participants performed only the picture recognition memory test, without verbal memory recall.

Finally, participants rated the emotional valence and arousal of the study pictures159 to verify the pictures were indeed perceived as negative. Participants were exposed to all the negative pictures from the encoding phase (n = 64) and an additional set of neutral pictures (n = 32), operationalized as pictures with scores between 4 and 6 on a 1–9 Self-Assessment Manikin scale.53

The experiment was conducted using MATLAB (https://www.mathworks.com/).

Quantification and statistical analysis

To evaluate verbal and visual memory strength, the percent of correct word recall and picture recognition was calculated for each study list. Only exact recall of the studied words, or words with phonetically equivalent spelling mistakes (< 1.2%) were recorded as correct answers in the verbal memory test. In the picture recognition test, trials with a response time lower than 300 ms (< 0.05%) were excluded.48,53,160 Data points falling further than two standard-deviations from the mean in each experimental condition and list were winsorized to the nearest score inside the range of -2<Z<2.161 Finally, to evaluate the perceived emotional value of the study pictures, the averages of valence and arousal ratings of the negative pictures were calculated for each list.

Statistical analysis was conducted using the JASP program (https://jasp-stats.org/). A two-way mixed ANOVA was performed for word or picture memory strength, with instruction (Remember/Forget) as a between-subjects factor and list (List1/List2) as a within-subject factor. A between subjects t-test was performed to examine differences in memory performance in a specific list under different instructions (Remember/Forget).

A two-way mixed ANOVA was performed for pictures’ perceived valence and arousal, with instruction (Remember/Forget) as a between-subjects factor and list (List1/List2) as a within-subject factor.

A logistic regression was conducted to predict visual memory strength on a single trial level for pictures that appeared either before or after the words during encoding. Visual memory was predicted by the between-subject variable type of instruction (Remember/Forget) and within-subjects variables list (List1/List2) and word recall. Additionally, the model controlled for individual variance in memory performance of each participant. The predictive power of each model was evaluated by a Likelihood Ratio Test (LRT), that compared the predictive model for visual memory of pictures that appeared before or after the words to a null model constructed of an individual random effect only. Model predictors were evaluated through a Wald Chi-Squared Test.

These models were further evaluated using Bayesian analysis with the brms package of R (https://www.r-project.org/).
