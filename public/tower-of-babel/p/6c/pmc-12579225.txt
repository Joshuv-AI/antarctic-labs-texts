A machine learning approach to predict strain-specific phage-host interactions
Authors: Pamela Yael Camejo, Felipe Rojas, Antonio Ossa, Rodrigo Hurtado, Daniel Tichy, Christian Pieringer, Michael Pino, Paola Mora-Uribe, Soledad Ulloa, Rodrigo Norambuena, Eduardo Tobar-Calfucoy, Matías Aguilera, Victoria Rojas-Martínez, Onix Cifuentes, Andrea Sabag, Nicolas Cifuentes, Daniel San Martín, Claudia Infante, Pablo Cifuentes, Hans Pieringer, Luis E. León
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The use of bacteriophages for biological control of bacterial infections is a promising approach to combat antimicrobial resistant bacteria. Prediction of phage-bacteria interactions is key to identify sensitive bacterial strains to phage therapy. Since these interactions are governed by multiple biological mechanisms, it is not a simple task to predict the outcome of a phage infection, which varies even among strains from the same species. In this study, machine learning-based models capable of predicting the host range of phages from sequencing data were developed. Models were trained using phage-bacteria protein-protein interactions (PPI), predicted from PPI databases, and a host-range dataset obtained from experimental assays with 10 Salmonella enterica and 3 Escherichia coli bacteriophages. The performance of prediction models differed among bacteriophages, ranging from 78 to 92% of accuracy in the case of Salmonella and 84–94% in Escherichia phages, with the highest accuracy (94%) achieved for E. coli phage CBDS-07. Results demonstrated the effectiveness of using PPI as a feature to design ML models for phage-bacteria phenotype prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22075-2.

Introduction

Bacteriophages (or phages) constitute a group of viruses that specifically target and infect bacteria. Their biotechnological relevance stems from their unique capability to serve as natural predators of bacteria, offering a targeted and precise method for controlling microbial populations. These viruses have been studied for various purposes, ranging from bacterial detection to the development of innovative therapies and the enhancement of industrial processes1. Since phages can target many antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains, they are good candidates for biocontrol agents in the food, aquaculture, and human and animal health industries2–5. Therefore, exploring phage-bacteria interactions mechanisms has received increasing attention during recent years.

Phage-bacteria interactions start by the recognition of specific receptors (like lipopolysaccharides, teichoic acids, proteins, and flagella) on the surface of the host bacteria6,7. The great degree of specificity of this interaction results in phages infecting only specific hosts, usually a single bacterial species or even some strains within a species. After phage-bacteria attachment, phage genetic material is injected into the bacterial host cell. This triggers a chain of intracellular events, where bacteria harness defense mechanisms to destroy the phage-injected nucleic acid, while phages try to avoid these systems by adopting anti-defense strategies8. The outcome of this interplay will determine the success or failure of phage infection.

Bacterial resistance mechanisms span from modification of phage receptors to evade phage adsorption9–11, to molecular pathways encoded in the bacterial genome dedicated to preventing or suppressing phage infections. Phage resistance systems include clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats and associated proteins (CRISPR-Cas) sequence-based adaptive immunity12,13, restriction-modification (RM) enzymes that act on double-stranded (dsDNA) phages14, bacteriophage exclusion (BREX) defense system, which hampers phage replication by methylation of host DNA15, abortive infection (Abi) innate immunity mechanisms limiting viral replication16, superinfection exclusion (Sie) proteins encoded by prophages to block the entrance of other phages17,18, and many uncharacterized defense strategies. Furthermore, phage can overcome bacterial immunity via anti-defense systems encoded in their DNA (anti-restriction proteins, CRISPR–Cas inhibitors and a plethora of molecular mechanisms)19. Since phage infectivity relies on the outcome of all these potential phage-bacteria interactions, it is not a simple task to predict the host-range of a phage, even within a given bacterial species.

With the exponential increment and availability of bacteria and phage sequencing data and in vitro host range datasets, machine-learning (ML) approaches have gained considerable prominence in phage-host interactions studies20–30. Machine-learning strategies for predicting phage-hosts usually employ genomic (nucleotide frequencies, k-mer counts, codon usage bias, GC content, gene presence, etc.) and proteomic signatures (amino acid and peptide frequencies or physicochemical properties). Protein-protein interactions (PPI) between phage and bacterial hosts have also been considered as an input feature of ML models20,28,29,31. However, in most cases, host range datasets used to feed these models are inferred from predictions, and do not always represent lab-confirmed interactions between hosts and viruses, and as such they do not carry great confidence. Furthermore, these models are constructed based on taxonomic generalization, where it is assumed that phage infection affects all isolates from the same genera and/or species to the same degree. However, it has been demonstrated that genetic diversity within the same bacterial species leads to differences in the resistance phenotype between strains32. Recently, two studies have shown promising results in predicting phage-host specificity at the strain level. Boeckaerts et al.33 developed a machine learning system that predicts strain-level interactions using data from Klebsiella phage receptor-binding proteins and the bacterial surface receptors. Gaborieau et al. 34 used linear mixed models to evaluate the influence of some genomic traits over the outcome of phage lytic interactions from a diverse collection of isolates belonging to the Escherichia genera. They harnessed information of genes related to adsorption factors and defense systems annotated in the isolates genome, along with experimental results, to predict phage-host interactions. Their results demonstrated the feasibility of leveraging genomic and experimental data to predict phage-host interactions at the strain level. However, despite the promising results of this publication, the analysis performed in that study was limited to two genomic traits known to participate in phage interactions. As we already mentioned, there are several known and unknown factors involved in phage susceptibility, and therefore, a generalized approach is still needed to search for other genetic traits involved in the bacterial response to phage infection.

In this work, PPI were predicted by comparing the protein sequences of phages and bacteria with a reference PPI database. Predicted PPI and phage host-range data of a panel of genetically diverse isolates of antibiotic-resistant bacteria Salmonella enterica and Escherichia coli, were used to train ML models predicting the resistance phenotype of each isolate. Results were compared with former phage-bacteria interaction predictors, obtaining a higher performance when using the approach presented here.

Methods

Phage-host range assay

Ten Salmonella bacteriophages (SAEN098P03, CBDS-01, CBDS-02, SAEN100P08, L8, M7, STM23, CBDS-03, CBDS-04 and CBDS-05) and 3 Escherichia bacteriophages (CBDS-06, CBDS-07 and ETE131P06) were previously isolated from Salmonella and E. coli isolates as described in Aguilera et al. (2023)35 and Norambuena et al. (2025)36 (PhageLab’s property).

Quantitative host range assays were performed with a historic collection of Salmonella and Escherichia coli strains, mainly isolated from poultry farms between 2019 and 2023 (PhageLab’s property). Strains were stored at -80 °C in Luria-Bertani broth (LB) supplemented with 50% w/v of glycerol, a working stock of every strain was stored at 4 °C for up to 1 month. Assays were carried out in a 96-well plate as described by Xie et al., (2018)37 with some modifications (n = 1). Briefly, overnight cultures of bacteria were re-inoculated in fresh media and grown until ∼1E + 08 CFU/mL (OD600∼0.1, as determined previously) in TSB at 37 °C and agitation in a microplate reader EPOCH2 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California). Then, the culture was diluted to 1E + 06 CFU/mL in TSB and mixed with each bacteriophage at 2E + 08 PFU/mL for a final MOI input of 20. The microtiter plate was incubated in EPOCH2 at 37–42 °C at maximum agitation. OD600 was measured every 10 min for a total of 6 h. The area under the growth curve was used as a measurement of total growth of each isolate over the amount of time evaluated in this experiment. Growth inhibition was calculated as the percentage of reduction of the treatment compared to the area under the curve of the untreated bacteria. For binary data analyses, growth inhibition > 15% was classified as “sensitive” and < 15% as “resistant”.

For double layer plaque assays, 100 uL of overnight bacterial cultures were added to 6 mL of 0.45% LB agar and poured onto previously prepared 1.5% LB agar plates. Then, 10uL of each bacteriophage at 1E + 08 PFU/mL were spotted on the surface of bacterial lawns. Plates were incubated overnight at 37 °C. Bacteriophages lytic activities were determined by the presence of lysis halos or plaques.

Sequencing, genome assembly and annotation

DNA was extracted from concentrated bacteriophage stocks (> 1E + 08 PFU/mL) using the Phage DNA isolation kit (Norgen) and from overnight bacterial cultures at 37 °C using PureLink Genomic DNA Kit (Invitrogen). Libraries were prepared using the Nextera XT DNA library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, California), and sequencing was performed in a NextSeq 550 (Illumina, San Diego, California). Genomic analyses were performed using a custom workflow bioinformatics pipeline developed at PhageLab SpA. Briefly, genomic assembly includes a step of quality control, where reads quality was assessed, adapters were trimmed, and low-quality reads discarded to ensure data integrity and reliability, using the bioinformatic tool Fastp38 v0.23.2. High-quality reads were then utilized for de novo genome assembly, enabling the reconstruction of complete or near-complete genomes (Unicycler39 v0.5.0). Assembled genomes were evaluated to check for completeness and contamination levels (CheckM40 v1.2.2 and CheckV41 v1.0.1). The resulting bacterial and phage high-quality genomes were then subjected to gene annotation (Bakta42 v1.10.3 and Pharokka43 v1.7.0).

In silico serotyping of Salmonella genomes was performed with SeqSero244 and SISTR45. Serotyping of E. coli genomes was carried out with ECTyper46.

A heatmap with the average nucleotide identity (ANI) and average amino acid identity (AAI) of phage genomes was generated using results from fastANI47 and EzAAI48.

Protein-protein interactions (PPI)

Protein domain searches were performed using the bioinformatic program HMMER49 against the PFAM50 database to identify protein family or domain matches in each bacterium and phage genome (e-value < 10− 3). A quality score was assigned to each combination of PFAMs between phages and bacterial genomes, using as reference the Protein-Protein Interactions Domain Miner (PPIDM)51 dataset, based on the reliability of the interaction. PPIDM evaluates interactions between protein domains Pfam, both experimentally confirmed, as predicted ones, where each domain-domain interaction (DDI) is scored based on its source and evidence. When a pair of PFAMs was not found in the database, the score assigned to this pair was 0. The cumulated interaction score of each bacterial gene was calculated as the sum of all its PFAM scores (Fig. 1).

Microbial pangenomes

Pangenome construction was performed with genomes of Salmonella and Escherichia coli (one for each species) using Panaroo (v1.3.4)52 (‘strict’ mode and parameter –remove-invalid-genes). Additionally, a third pangenome was generated combining genes from both Salmonella and Escherichia (a.k.a multi-species pangenome). Gene clusters were scored based on the cumulative interaction score of gene(s) comprising the cluster in each isolate (Fig. 1).

Predictive models and machine learning

Predictive models were constructed for each phage using isolated clusters interaction scores as features and growth inhibition results from the host range experimental analysis as output (Fig. 1). Machine learning approaches included Random Forest (rf)53, Support Vector Machines (svm)54 (using both growth inhibition continuous and binary output), Gradient Boost (gb)55 (continuous variable) and k-Nearest Neighbors (knn)56 (binary variable). Methods were evaluated both individually and as an ensemble, using a strategy of simple vote (majority) and considering the probability associated with each model.

First, data was split into 80% training and 20% test data. The training set was used during model construction and hyperparameter tuning. Within the training set, tenfold cross-validation (10-fold CV) was carried out as recommended before57, to assess performance during model development. Model hyperparameters were tuned through Bayesian optimization58, implemented using the scikit-optimize Python library (‘gp_minimize’ function). After model tuning, the independent test set (20% of data) was used to compute final performance metrics of accuracy, specificity, precision, recall and F1-score (F1). In the case of models trained to predict the percentage of growth inhibition (continuous variable), predictions were transformed to binary variables for performance metrics calculation.

In the case of models trained with scores from the multi-species pangenome (Salmonella and E. coli genes), negative interactions between phages and strains from a species other than their known hosts (Salmonella-phages and E. coli isolates, for instance) were assumed. Since the number of negative interactions increased considerably with this assumption (3 to 28 times more negative than positive interactions), random oversampling from the minority class was performed to handle class imbalance.

Results

Phage-host range and isolate diversity

The individual infection pattern of 10 Salmonella and 3 Escherichia coli phages was evaluated with 544 isolates of Salmonella and 218 isolates of Escherichia coli (Fig. 2). Growth inhibition rates were converted into binary values. A cutoff of 15% of growth inhibition was used as a threshold between sensitive and resistant bacteria, based on the methodology previously employed by Aguilera et al. 202335. Additionally, the distribution of inhibition rates across all isolates evaluated in this study was examined. The histogram revealed a bimodal distribution, with one mode clustered near 0–1% inhibition (resistant strains) and another mode approaching 100% inhibition (sensitive strains) (Supplementary Figure S1). The 15% cutoff falls within these two modes, providing a threshold for binarizing this type of data while preserving the biological distinction observed in the assay results. This approach allows to explore different machine learning strategies, regression and classification, using the same experimental dataset.

The percentage of sensitive isolates varied across phages, ranging from 25% to 63% for Salmonella phages and from 29% to 42% for Escherichia phages, reflecting differences in host range breadth among the tested phages (Supplementary Table S1 and S2), respectively. Phages were evaluated with a set of diverse isolates within each species to ensure that the training data used to build the machine learning models could provide more discriminative information to infer resistance or susceptibility to a broad range of bacteria. Salmonella phages were evaluated with a set of isolates belonging to 53 different serovars, predominantly from Infantis (34.8%) and Minnesota (15.8%). Most isolates of E. coli were screened with the three E. coli phages and classified into 49 completely different serotypes (both O and H alleles predicted) and 26 serotypes with H allele prediction.

Phage genome comparison

The resulting phage genomes were classified into 6 different viral taxonomies: L8, M7 and STM23 belong to the subfamily Ounavirinae. CBDS-03, CBDS-04 and CBDS-05 were classified into the family Drexlerviridae. SAEN100P08, CBDS-06, CBDS-07 and ETE131P06 were classified into the family Strabovirida. CBDS-01, CBDS-02 and SAEN098P03 are Ackermannviridae, Demerecviridae and Jondennisvirinae phages, respectively.

The nucleotide and amino acid sequences of Salmonella and E. coli phages were compared to each other (Fig. 3). The result showed that Salmonella phages CBDS-05, CBDS-04 and CBDS-03 shared ~ 98–99% of their nucleotide sequence and ~ 97–99% of their protein sequence. Bacteriophages L8, STM23 and M7 have only 2 to 8 nucleotides of difference, which alters the protein sequence in 2 (STM23 and M7), 6 (L8 and STM23) and 8 amino acids (L8 and M7).

Pangenome and protein-protein interactions

A Salmonella pangenome, composed of 544 genomes from 53 different serovars, was constructed. The resulting pangenome consisted of 18,053 genes in total, including 3,029 core (genes present in > 99 and ≤ 100 % of isolates), 586 softcore (present in < 99 and > 95% of isolates), 1,652 shell (present in < 95 and > 15% of isolates) and 12,786 cloud genes (present in less than 15 % of isolates), close to what was formerly described by former Salmonella studies59,60. The pangenome of E. coli was constructed with 218 genomes and resulted in 2,939 core, 422 softcore, 2,686 shell, and 13,514 cloud genes (19,561 total), consistent with previous observations that this species has an open pangenome61. The resulting combined pangenome consisted of 29,687 clusters of genes, from which 2,309 corresponded to the shared core of Salmonella and E. coli.

After assembly and annotation, 12,576 and 13,553 unique PFAMs were identified within the genomic sequences of Salmonella and Escherichia isolates, respectively. Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 contain the number of bacterial PFAMs with interaction scores > 0 in the PPIDM database, with each phage evaluated. An interaction score was then calculated for each gene, composed by the cumulative individual PFAM scores. Likewise, pangenomic gene cluster scores consisted in the sum of interaction scores from genes comprising each cluster in each genome. On average, 32% and 46% of gene clusters had a positive interaction score with at least one phage, for Salmonella and E. coli respectively.

Prediction of Salmonella phages’ hosts

Interaction scores of pangenome clusters were then used as features to build machine learning models to predict phage-bacteria interactions, using experimental phage-host assay results as the model’s outcome. In preliminary evaluations, a broader set of machine learning algorithms—including logistic regression, naive Bayes, and artificial neural networks—were tested. However, several of these models either failed to converge, produced errors during training, or showed very poor predictive performance, likely due to the moderate size and dimensionality of the dataset. As a result, only Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), K-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN), and Gradient Boosting (GB) showed consistent predictive performance above baseline (accuracy above that of a majority-class) and were therefore selected for further optimization and analysis. These four machine learning were refined and tuned using a training dataset containing 80% of the isolates dataset. RF and SVM were constructed using both the continuous and binary output of growth inhibition. GB was built using the continuous output and k-NN the binary one. Ten-fold cross-validation was performed to determine the best configuration parameters. Next, trained models were run with the remaining 20% of data to validate the test set. Ensemble learning was also tested by merging the results of individual models. A single best model configuration was selected for each phage, based on the average of the resulting performance metrics. Figure 4 summarizes the parameters and performance metrics (accuracy, F1-score, precision, recall and specificity) of the best model configuration for each phage. A more detailed summary of all the models evaluated for each phage is found in Supplementary Table S3.

The model approach with the best predictive metrics varied according to the phage and the type of variable used as output. Eight of the ten best phage models were constructed using a binary output (0 for resistance and 1 for sensitive strains). Since inhibition growth exhibits a bimodal distribution, setting a cutoff to separate resistant from sensitive strains might allow models to have a better prediction of the resulting outcome. In some cases, the ensemble of two models showed better metrics than the individual outcome, demonstrating the relevance of including a bagging approach.

In addition, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted for the best ML model generated using binary input data (L8, M7, STM23, SAEN098P03, SAEN100P08, CBDS-01, CBDS-02 and CBDS-03) (Supplementary Figures S3-S4). The area under the receiver operating curve (AUROC) was calculated as a general performance metric of each model. The AUROC measures how well each model distinguishes between interacting and non-interacting phage-host pairs, estimating the probability that each model will correctly give a higher score to a pair that interacts, compared to a pair that doesn’t interact, when selected at random. In the case of Salmonella phages, models reached AUROC values between 74% and 98%.

While the model obtained for phage M7 exhibited the greatest accuracy and F1-score (combination of precision and recall), models obtained for phage variants L8 and STM23 showed poor performance, with the STM23 model very poor at detecting positive cases (low recall). Likewise, best models constructed from CBDS-03, CBDS-04 and CBDS-05 host ranges and genomic data differed in the algorithm used and output type, being the model of CBDS-04 (continuous data) the one with better metrics.

In comparison to other phage-bacteria interaction predictors, the accuracy reached by ML models constructed for phages SAEN098P03, CBDS-02 and M7 (> 89%) shows an improvement. In the development of PHIS-detector, the use of protein-protein interactions (PPI)-based features to create an RF model resulted in weak discriminating abilities, with an accuracy value close to ~ 60%. Likewise, results obtained by Leite et al., 201831 using PPI scores-based datasets to feed an artificial neural network (ANN) model reached 89.8% accuracy. Furthermore, in both cases, predictions were only made at the species level, and therefore, the predictive power of the ML models constructed here is greater at the strain level.

Prediction of Escherichia phages’ hosts

Although the number of isolates with experimental results in E. coli phages was lower than Salmonella phages, it was still possible to build machine learning models (Fig. 5) with AUROC values ranging from 91 to 98% (Supplementary Figure S4). However, the best models of phages CBDS-06 and ETE131P06 exhibited 100% precision and specificity, meaning that all isolates predicted as sensitive are correctly classified (no false positives). On the other hand, a low recall percentage in the test set indicates issues with the prediction of resistant isolates, which might be in fact sensitive. Poor performance of these two models could be attributed to the low number of isolates used to construct them. Supplementary Table S4 contains a detailed summary of the metrics obtained for these and other models evaluated for E. coli phages.

Prediction of phage hosts using the multi-species pangenome

A multi-species pangenome constitutes a genetic repertoire, where genes from both species with high identity, and presumably similar functions, are grouped together into gene clusters. These clusters served as standardized variables that could be compared across species. This tool can capture a wider range of variability and diversity across Salmonella and E. coli genomes and obtain a better resolution at the moment of identifying specific genes involved in phage recognition and defense for these species. In order to improve the predictive power ML models of each phage analyzed here, a Salmonella-E. coli pangenome was generated, which was used to calculate gene clusters interaction scores between each isolate and phage.

Multi-species ML models were built for phages with no evidence of cross-infectivity, given the assumption of no interactions in these cases. Therefore, Salmonella phages SAEN098P03, SAEN100P08, CBDS-01, CBDS-02 and CBDS-05, which exhibited infection of other species in plaque lysis assays with a subset of isolates, were removed from this analysis (Supplementary Figures S2, S6 and S7).

Metrics from the best models resulting from using a multi-species pangenome in the model construction were compared to the results from species specific pangenome in Fig. 6. In all cases, the multi-species pangenome improved the accuracy and AUROC scores obtained from the test data (Supplementary Figure S3-5). ML models of phages CBDS-06 (E. coli) and L8 (Salmonella) improved all performance metrics evaluated. In the other cases, except for STM23 (Salmonella), specificity also increased. This increment in the number of true resistant isolates is likely due to the addition of negatively assumed inter-species phage interactions to the experimental dataset. Using this approach, an increment in recall (proportion of real sensitive isolates correctly predicted) was also greater in E. coli phages ETE131P06 and CBDS-06, and Salmonella phages CBDS-04 and STM23 models. A detailed summary of all the models evaluated with this dataset can found in Supplementary Table S5.

Gene clusters involved in phage-bacteria interactions

In order to gain insights into the biological mechanisms underlying the differences between resistant and sensitive phenotype of isolates, the function of genes with the highest contribution to the overall prediction were explored. These set of genes were obtained based on the “weight” assigned by the best ML model configurations. Specifically, clusters of genes (features used in models) with the highest scores from phages M7, CBDS-02, SAEN098P03 (Salmonella phages) and CBDS-07 (E. coli phage) were explored, which had the best accuracy metric in the individual pangenome models (92%, 90%, 89% and 94%, respectively). There were two clusters of genes classified as ‘fimbrial proteins’ among the most relevant features in the design of the SAEN098P03 random-forest model. Pili or fimbriae are non-flagellar, hair-like appendages protruding from the cell surface that are critical for bacterial virulence and fitness. Phages can attach extracellular structures, such as pili or fimbriae62–65 as the first step of phage infection. In the case of Salmonella, former studies have demonstrated the phage recognition of flagellar proteins as host receptors66,67. Therefore, changes in the sequence of these extracellular structures could be an effective defense mechanism to evade phage infection.

ABC-type transport systems were also identified among the most relevant features of CBDS-02 (Salmonella phage) and CBDS-07 (E. coli phage) predictive models. These ATP-binding cassette proteins use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to energize the transport of a variety of molecules (ions, nutrients, antibiotics, etc.) across cellular membranes68. Former studies have revealed that phages can hijack these transporters for DNA injection into the host cytoplasm69,70 and therefore, mutations of these proteins are known to confer resistance to phage infection71. Transcriptomic and metabolomics approaches have also demonstrated that the development of phage-resistant cells of Vibrio alginolyticus was accompanied by changes in the levels of nutrient transport-related transcripts72. Indels and point mutations in genes encoding ABC-type transport components in phage-resistant isolates of Flavobacterium psychrophilum also suggested an implication of these proteins in phage resistance73. It is likely, therefore, that these inner membrane-related proteins could be participating as natural passages for DNA to enter the host cytoplasm in Salmonella and E. coli.

Some gene clusters encoding transcriptional regulators were also relevant in the development of these models. Host adaptation to phage infection might not always be accompanied by changes in the structure of phage receptors and/or nutrient transporters, but metabolic reprogramming by changes in the regulation of transcriptional processes could play a pivotal role in how strains face viral infection.

The most important features of the ML models built for phages M7 (Salmonella phages) and CBDS-07 (E. coli phage) using the combined pangenome are also listed in Table 2. These features were obtained from both Salmonella and E. coli genomes, and therefore, they could help detect differences in the biological mechanisms of these two species for dealing with infection of phages analyzed here. Similar to the results obtained with the individual species analyses, one of the top clusters of genes identified in Salmonella phage M7 correspond to one of the proteins forming the cytochrome c-type biogenesis system. Former studies have demonstrated that phage infection-induced upregulation of iron uptake systems might lead to oxidative stress sensitivity in the cells, altering phage infection efficiency74,75. Whole genome sequencing and ML modeling of mutations in the genome of E. coli when interacting with phage λ strains, indicated a nonsynonymous mutation in ccmA, gene encoding the cytochrome c biogenesis ATP-binding export protein CcmA, which had a significant positive effect in the interaction model76. Furthermore, four transcription factors were identified among the features with higher scores in the models built for M7 (Salmonella phage) and CBDS-07 (E. coli phage). Transcriptional regulation may also contribute to the bacterial defense against phage infection by controlling the expression of phage defense systems, as previously demonstrated in several antiviral mechanisms that become upregulated in response to viral infection77–79 and quorum sensing80,81.

Discussion

In this study, the use of experimental data of phage-bacterial infection phenotypes and protein-protein interaction scores to develop a machine learning framework capable of predicting the outcome of phage infectivity in different phage-bacterium pairs of Salmonella and E. coli strains was explored. The host-range matrix, derived from growth inhibition assays, was used to define binary sensitivity labels (sensitive or resistant) for each phage-host pair, which served as the target variable for model training. Thus, the host-range matrix was fully integrated into the modeling pipeline, linking empirical infection data with genomic features to enable predictive modeling.

This analysis revealed that phage genome mutations can lead to relevant differences in the efficiency of phage-bacteria interactions. The difference in model performance between M7 and STM23, despite their high genetic similarity, evidence that even minimal changes in phage protein sequences can significantly alter host recognition profiles in ways that affect interaction predictability. On the other side, CBDS-06 and CBDS-07 E. coli phages and SAEN100P08, a Salmonella phage, share most of their nucleotide and amino acid content (> 94%). Some of the amino acid variations among phages with high percent of identity were found to be in proteins annotated as “baseplate wedge subunit”, “baseplate protein” and “tail fiber protein”, all these classified under the category “tail function”. The phage tail, specifically the tail fibers located at the distal end, is the specialized machinery that mediates phage binding to bacterial receptors82, and therefore, alterations in the structure of these proteins might alter host recognition and range of infectivity81. Altogether, these results illustrate how minimal changes in the protein sequence of phages, especially if these mutations are located in proteins responsible for host recognition, can alter the range of bacterial hosts a phage can successfully infect. While both phage and bacterial features were incorporated into the models, the nuanced effects of such mutations may not be fully captured by current feature representations, highlighting the need for deeper integration of functional and structural phage data in future predictive frameworks.

The approach developed here, first builds a training dataset for each phage based on the predicted protein-protein interaction scores of a set of clusters of genes resulting from a species pangenome analysis. The pangenome is used as an approach to cluster orthologous genes across a set of hundreds of different strains and assign gene cluster interaction scores to each strain, based on the reliability of the predicted interaction among phage and bacterial protein domains. Subsequently, several machine-learning algorithms were evaluated on these datasets in order to select a configuration (i.e., an algorithm and its parameter values) producing the models with the best predictive power. The results obtained, with accuracy values ranging from 78% to 92% at the strain level, are promising.

The resulting performance metrics of ML models built here from PPI-interactions outperforms a former study predicting strain-level phage interactions in E. coli34, where predictions achieved an overall AUROC of 86%, and average precision and F1 score were both at 60%. In this study, eight of the species-specific models, and all the multi-species models, presented AUROC metrics above 86%. The average precision and F1 score were 86 and 97% (species-specific), and 80% and 84% (multi-species), for Salmonella and E. coli phages, respectively. This improvement in the model predictive power might result from considering the complete set of genomic traits encoded in the bacterial and phage genomes.

The use of a multi-species pangenome in the construction of ML models was also evaluated with this dataset. To assess the assumption of negative cross-species interactions used in the generation of these models, phage infectivity was screened using isolates from a species different from the original host. Contrary to the initial assumption, five Salmonella phages where capable of infecting E. coli isolates too, evidencing some degree of polyvalence. Although phages are usually species-specific, polyvalence has been observed among phages of Enterobacteria83,84 Therefore, additional host-range analyses are required before implementing the multi-species approach in these cases. In phages where not polyvalence was observed, the multi-species pangenome improved the accuracy of the best model configurations, in most cases at the cost of losing precision (percentage of isolates sensitive to phages correctly predicted). It is possible that augmenting the number of negative interactions from the assumed resistant strains, in the test data, increases the probability of the classifiers of false positive misprediction. In most cases, this reduction in precision was balanced by an improvement in the recall rate. Altogether, these results suggest that the use of a multi-species pangenome could help improve the predictive power of these models, increasing the level of resolution at which classifiers can identify relevant features associated with a specific phenotype.

Finally, by using genomic data as input features of this framework, it is feasible to identify those host-encoding protein sequences playing a determinant role in distinguishing between resistant and sensitive strains and interpret the biological function of these proteins in bacterial-phage defense mechanisms. Based on these results, it seems that the host phenotype of Salmonella and E. coli to phage infection is not the result of a unique mechanism, but a complex molecular and metabolic strategy. Among the most notable results, multiple natural nutrient channels located in the inner membrane of these gram-negative bacteria seem to be significant features of the constructed models. Bacterial membrane protein modifications could represent a defensive strategy against these bacteriophages85–87, and therefore, serve as potential candidates for experimental analyses. This illustrates the capability of this machine learning strategy to pinpoint key genes involved in phage-host interactions.

In addition to the promising results achieved in phage–host prediction, future work should aim to further enhance the performance and accuracy of these models. An avenue for future improvement in ML model development involves exploring alternatives to random oversampling. While random oversampling is a straightforward and widely used technique, it has known limitations, such as the potential for overfitting due to repeated sampling of the same minority class instances. More advanced methods, such as SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique)88, ADASYN89, or ensemble-based techniques (e.g., balanced bagging or boosting), could offer improved generalization by generating synthetic examples or leveraging class-weighted learning.

Future studies could also benefit from expanding the size and replication of experimental datasets, particularly by increasing the number and diversity of phages tested against Escherichia coli. Additionally, this predictive framework could be enhanced by incorporating other genomic features beyond protein–protein interactions, such as gene presence–absence patterns, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and defense systems. These steps will be essential to further increase the predictive power and generalizability of phage–host interaction models.

Conclusions

The results presented here demonstrate the effectiveness of using protein-protein interactions as a feature to design ML models for phage-bacteria phenotype prediction. By using data from experimental results, it is possible to develop an approach to distinguish resistance phenotype at the strain level, overcoming previous attempts to use this type of feature for phage-bacteria interaction prediction. Since the predictive power of these models is affected by mutations in phage genes key for host recognition, specific models might be required for different phages, despite similarities with phages included in this analysis. Protein-protein interaction scores set may not always capture the impact of these mutations in the infection process, and therefore, future work should consider integrating these modifications in the design of new machine-learning models.
