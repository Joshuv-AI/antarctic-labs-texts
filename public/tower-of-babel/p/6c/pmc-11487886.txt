Rating of perceived exertion as a tool for managing exercise intensity during pregnancy: a scoping review
Authors: Christina Gjestvang, Malin Landro Olsen, Emilie Mass Dalhaug, Lene A H Haakstad
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDuring pregnancy, international guidelines recommend ≥150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity per week, with an intensity perceived as fairly light to somewhat hard on the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale (ranging from 6 ‘no exertion’ to 20 ‘maximal exertion’, corresponding to 60% to 80% of maximum heart rate). However, the determination and monitoring of exercise intensity seem to be a particular source of confusion, and the most effective method to monitor exercise intensity remains uncertain. This study aimed to examine existing research on the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and maternal heart rate (MHR) for monitoring exercise intensity during pregnancy.DesignScoping review using the mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT) and the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach.Data sourcesPubMed, Web of Science and SPORTDiscus were searched from 16–17 April 2023, with a subsequent search on 1 November 2023.Eligibility criteriaWe included studies investigating the correlation between perceived intensity on the Borg RPE scale (6–20) and MHR during exercise in pregnant individuals and written in English/Scandinavian language. There were no restrictions on publication year or study design.Data extraction and synthesisTwo independent reviewers screened the articles based on title and abstract. Selected articles were read in full text and reference lists of screened articles were also checked. Out of 120 studies screened, six articles met the inclusion criteria after removing one duplicate. The results were qualitatively summarised to provide an overview of common themes and variations between studies. MMAT and GRADE assessed the risk of bias and the certainty of the evidence.ResultsThe six studies involved a total of 260 healthy pregnant individuals (gestational week: from 16 to 38), with various exercise protocols (cycling, walking, running and resistance exercise) and intensities (from light to moderate). Three studies supported the Borg RPE scale to estimate exercise intensity during pregnancy, while three found no correlation between this scale and MHR. The certainty of the evidence was graded as low to moderate, with a potential risk of bias due to small sample sizes, incomplete outcome data and inconsistencies across studies.ConclusionThe mixed results highlight the complexity of monitoring exercise intensity during pregnancy. Using both the Borg RPE scale and MHR might be better than using them separately for monitoring exercise intensity during pregnancy. Due to limited and inconsistent research, more extensive studies are needed.

Introduction

It is well documented that regular physical activity and exercise that is planned, structured and repetitive bodily movements give several maternal, fetal and neonatal health benefits.14

International guidelines recommend that pregnant individuals participate in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity aerobic physical activity per week.1 2 5 The intensity of this activity should be perceived as fairly light to somewhat hard on the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion (RPE) scale, which ranges from 6 (no exertion) to 20 (maximal exertion), corresponding to 60% to 80% of the maximum heart rate predicted by age.1 2 5

A healthy pregnancy is associated with several physiological changes, and the cardiovascular system experiences some of the most pronounced alterations.1 6 As cardiac output and resting maternal heart rate (MHR) increase, maximal MHR may be lower than normal values.6 Hence, using MHR may be an imprecise method for monitoring exercise intensity since it tends to underestimate intensity at higher work rates and overestimate it at lower work rates.7 8 This imprecision could potentially lead to inadequate exercise prescriptions, either exposing pregnant individuals to unnecessary risks or failing to provide the full benefits of exercise. Complementing MHR measurements with the Borg RPE scale, which accounts for perceived exertion, may be a suitable method to monitor exercise intensity during pregnancy.1 2 5 8 9 The 2020 American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists guideline recommends using both the Borg RPE scale and MHR for this purpose.1 The Borg RPE scale is built on a linear correlation between perceived exertion and heart rates spanning from 60 (Borg: 6) to 200 beats per minute (Borg: 20).10 In non-pregnant individuals, this linear correlation has been shown during cycling.10 11 However, during pregnancy, other physiological changes, such as gestational weight gain and decreased lung reserve, can increase the perceived exertion for a given workload.1 6 8 Additionally, variations in cardiorespiratory fitness, the type of exercise performed and the increased energy cost of any given exercise mode due to increased body weight may challenge pregnant individuals in accurately assessing their perceived exertion.8 All these factors may influence how exercise intensity is perceived as pregnancy progresses, and the accuracy of the Borg RPE scale during pregnancy is still unclear.

With limited research on the use of the Borg RPE scale during pregnancy, there exists a discrepancy in the literature regarding the optimal method for monitoring exercise intensity.8 The physiological changes during pregnancy present unique challenges in monitoring exercise intensity, making it crucial to identify reliable methods for this population. Despite recommendations to use both the Borg RPE scale and MHR for monitoring exercise intensity, supporting evidence is limited and sometimes contradictory, highlighting the need for a comprehensive overview of existing research.8 Thus, this scoping review aimed to examine existing research literature to identify knowledge gaps concerning the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR in monitoring exercise intensity among pregnant individuals. Our objective is to support healthcare providers and others working in the field of exercise and pregnancy in advising pregnant individuals on best practices for monitoring exercise intensity, ultimately promoting maternal and fetal health.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

A systematic search was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.12 13 The initial search was conducted from 16–17 April 2023, with a subsequent search on 1 November 2023. Relevant search terms were developed based on articles related to the validation and reliability of the Borg RPE scale. The following search terms were included: pregnancy and (exercise OR training OR ‘aerobic exercise’) and (Borg OR RPE OR ‘perceived exertion’). These terms were adapted to the specific search parameters of each database. Three databases, PubMed, Web of Science and SPORTDiscus, were searched for relevant studies. The inclusion criteria were studies investigating the correlation between the original Borg RPE scale (6–20) and measured MHR during exercise in pregnant individuals, and those written in English or Scandinavian languages (Norwegian, Swedish or Danish). There were no restrictions on publication year or study design in the included studies. The full search strategy used in this study is presented in an online supplemental file S1. Following the removal of duplicates, two independent reviewers (CG and MLO) screened the remaining articles based on titles and abstracts. Selected articles were read in full text. Additionally, to ensure a comprehensive search, the reference lists of screened articles were checked. This additional search did not yield any further identified articles. Out of 120 studies screened, six articles met the inclusion criteria after removing duplicates (n=1). The process of study selection is documented in a flow chart (figure 1) adopted by PRISMA.12 13

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data that were recorded included the author’s details (names), publication year, country, study design, aim, study period, sample size, gestational week, participant characteristics, exercise mode, targeted intensity, assessment tool and test protocol. The results from the six included studies were summarised qualitatively to provide an overview, highlight common themes and variations across the studies and identify knowledge gaps. Further, to evaluate methodological quality, we used the mixed methods appraisal tool (MMAT), developed for five different study design categories (qualitative research, randomised controlled trials, non-randomised studies, quantitative descriptive studies and mixed methods studies).14 15 The checklist contains seven screening questions for each study category, which can be scored as ‘yes’, ‘no’ or ‘can’t tell’ if the method is not properly described or justified.14 15 As recommended, instead of reporting an overall score, we have provided a presentation of how each of the quality criteria has been met in the results section.15 In addition, to evaluate the quality of evidence in this scoping review, The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations (GRADE) approach16 was used.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Selected studies and population characteristics

The six included studies (n=260 pregnant individuals) consisted of the following study designs: randomised controlled trial (n=1),17 cross-validation (n=1),18 non-randomised controlled trial (n=1),19 cross-sectional (n=2)20 21 and cross-over (n=1).22 The participants came from the USA,17 22 Canada,18 Australia,19 Brazil (19) and Sweden,21 with gestational weeks ranging from 16 to 38. The participants’ exercise levels varied from no regular exercise prior to pregnancy20 to regular exercise of ≥30 min 5 days/week for the past 2 years.22 All participants were classified as healthy, without gestational diabetes, hypertension, untreated thyroid disease,18 high risk or multiple pregnancies. Correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR during exercise

Two recent17 18 and one older study22 supported the usefulness of the Borg RPE scale to estimate exercise intensity during pregnancy (table 1). Brislane et al (2022)17 and Da Silva et al (2020)18 found that the Borg RPE scale correlated with MHR in estimating exercise intensity during both walking and cycling, irrespective of gestational age or exercise level. Similarly, Pivarnik et al (1993)22 found that the Borg RPE scale and MHR were correlated in both exercising and non-exercising pregnant individuals during cycling and walking. In contrast, three studies did not find a correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR during cycling, walking, aerobics, circuit training or resistance exercise.1921

Quality assessment of included studies

Based on MMAT (table 3), all except one study17 met five of seven quality criteria but lacked information on participants with missing data and the intended administration of the investigation (3.5). The study by Brislane et al (2022)17 met 3 of 7 quality criteria, with missing information about the randomisation process, if the intervention groups were comparable, adherence rates to assigned intervention, and blinding of participants or assessors. Following the GRADE approach, the evidence is evaluated to be of low to moderate quality, with a potential risk of bias due to small sample sizes, incomplete outcome data and inconsistencies across studies.16

Discussion

Our scoping review revealed mixed and inconclusive results from a limited number of studies exploring the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR to monitor exercise intensity among pregnant individuals. Three studies supported the Borg RPE scale to indicate an increase or decrease in MHR during exercise,17 18 22 while three found no correlation between the two methods.1921

The studies included pregnant individuals in the second and third trimesters with a wide range of gestational weeks (16 to 38 weeks of gestation). This could, to some extent, explain why the findings are mixed. Given the physiological changes during pregnancy, including gestational weight gain and alterations in cardiac output, ventilation and gas exchange, the rating of perceived exertion may be subject to overestimation, depending on the pregnancy stage.8 23 Further, the exercise sessions in four studies consisted of weight-bearing activities, such as walking and aerobics.1821 Increased energy expenditure due to gestational weight gain may affect perceived exertion during weight-bearing activities among pregnant individuals.2426 Hence, individuals in the third trimester, when gestational weight gain is typically more pronounced, may perceive a higher exertion during weight-bearing activities than those in the first and second trimesters. In the study by Santos et al (2016),20 most of the participants were in their third trimester and walked or ran on a treadmill. The findings indicated a gradual increase in MHR that coincided with the progression of the treadmill protocol.20 However, no correlation was observed between the Borg RPE scale and MHR.20 Similarly, Fieril et al (2016)21 and O’Neill et al (1992),19 with participants in the second or third trimesters, found no significant correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR during aerobics, walking or running.19 21 With participants in their second trimester (gestational week: 20.5±5.0), only one study found a correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR during weight-bearing activity carried out as walking.18 The mixed findings across these studies emphasise the importance of considering gestational stage and weight-bearing vs non-weight-bearing activities when investigating perceived exertion during pregnancy. In the studies by Brislane et al (2022)17 and Pivarnik et al (1993),22 the exercise sessions consisted of non-weight-bearing activities (cycling), and these authors found a correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR.17 22 Thus, this may indicate a difference in perceived exertion between weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing activities and underscores that gestational weight gain in pregnant individuals may influence the Borg RPE scoring during weight-bearing activities.2426 Adding to this, Pivarnik et al (1993)22 found that the participants’ RPE ratings were the same during pregnancy and postpartum,22 suggesting that the perception of exertion in non-weight-bearing activities may remain relatively unaffected by gestational weight gain when compared with weight-bearing exercises.

Perceived exertion can be influenced by physiological adaptations specific to certain modes of exercise.8 27 Fieril et al (2016)21 conducted a study comparing Nordic walking and resistance exercise, and they found that the score on the Borg RPE scale was significantly higher during resistance exercise than aerobic exercise.21 This suggests that muscle fatigue can be perceived as more exhausting, resulting in a higher score on the Borg RPE scale compared with aerobic exercise, without the same stress responses related to anaerobic exercises. For instance, during pregnancy, a decrease in pulmonary reserve may impair the ability to exercise anaerobically (such as resistance exercise), which could influence perceived exertion.1 Despite resistance exercise being perceived as more intense, the intensity measured as MHR was found to be similar in both aerobic and resistance exercise.21 Furthermore, individual factors such as familiarity with the specific exercise mode and personal tolerance to fatigue could also influence perceived exertion.28 Therefore, future research should explore the role of individual factors and investigate whether a correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR is consistent across different modes of exercise.

All studies evaluated Borg RPE and MHR at various time points, ranging from continuous measurements throughout the exercise sessions to singular measurements at the end of the session. The exercise sessions also varied in duration, lasting from 15 to 40 min, which could explain the discrepancies in our review. Continuous monitoring of perceived exertion and MHR captures immediate responses to exercise intensity and variations in physiological responses during the session. This approach may be the most optimal, as it enables pregnant individuals to identify intensity changes and assess whether adjustments are necessary to promote safe prenatal exercise.8 Evaluating perceived exertion and MHR toward the end of a session might only reflect the overall perceived effort and fatigue.29 To ensure consistency and comparability between studies, future research should consider the exercise modality, the timing of measurements during pregnancy and the use of continuous monitoring of exercise intensity.

Training history and current exercise levels among participants varied in the included studies and may also explain the mixed results in this scoping review. It has been shown that among occasional exercisers, heart rate does not always show a significant correlation with the Borg RPE scale, while regular exercisers are more accurately able to estimate their perceived exertion.8 30 This discrepancy may have important implications for the interpretation of our findings. Specifically, Santos et al (2016)20 and O’Neill et al (1992)19 included occasional exercisers and found no correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR, suggesting that the accuracy of the Borg RPE scale as a measure of exertion may depend on the exercise level among participants. In contrast, population studies have reported that physical activity status did not influence the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and heart rate.31 Further, prior knowledge and use of the scale may influence an individual’s rating on the Borg RPE scale.32 Fieril et al (2016),21 Santos et al (2016)20 and Pivarnik et al (1993)22 reported familiarising participants with the Borg RPE scale or informing them about the tool before the test session. We believe that well-informed participants who understand how to use the Borg RPE scale are likely to provide more accurate and consistent ratings of their perceived exertion. However, it is noteworthy that only Pivarnik et al (1993)22 found a correlation between the Borg RPE scale and heart rate, suggesting that prior knowledge of the scale alone may not be sufficient to ensure accurate ratings. While familiarising participants with the Borg RPE scale may enhance their ability to estimate perceived exertion, other factors such as exercise level or mode of exercise also play a significant role. With the small samples and mixed findings, it is uncertain whether prior knowledge of the Borg RPE scale is necessary. Still, the possible influence of familiarising participants with the Borg RPE scale and the participants’ exercise level underscores the importance of considering these factors in future research to enhance the applicability of the Borg scale in diverse populations.

The different absolute exercise intensity targets (MHR) and test protocols in included studies may have affected our results. In the studies by Pivarnik et al (1993)22 and O’Neill et al (1992),19 the participants exercised at a heart rate of 135–140 beats per minute. Consequently, the relative intensity of exercise among participants may not have been the same for all participants and may have influenced their perceived exertion. Therefore, it may be important to conduct standardised test protocols with relative intensity targets (% of estimated MHRmax) when comparing the Borg RPE scale with MHR. Furthermore, daily variations and a multitude of physical and emotional factors during pregnancy, such as frequent mood changes, emotional disturbances and hormonal fluctuations, may influence how an individual rates their exertion on the Borg RPE scale.33 34 These changes may lead to inconsistencies in perceived exertion. In addition, the possible discomfort associated with pregnancy, including symptoms such as fatigue, nausea and musculoskeletal pain, may cause greater variability in perceived exertion.34 For instance, pregnant individuals might score 14 on 1 day and 17 on another on the Borg RPE scale during an exercise session at 150 beats per minute. Future research should aim to consider the unique psychological changes during pregnancy that possibly influence perceived exertion. This may provide an increased understanding of how pregnant individuals assess and experience their physical effort.

Methodological quality of included studies

All studies included in our scoping review have methodological issues that may affect the validity and reliability of their findings. Common themes include small sample sizes and selection bias. A significant issue across the studies is the small sample size. Brislane et al (2022)17 did not provide detailed information on the randomisation process or allocation concealment, raising concerns about selection bias.35 Further, the authors did not provide detailed information on attrition rates and handling of missing data, which could lead to attrition bias.36 The study by Da Silva et al (2020)18 included only 13 pregnant individuals, leaving little room for error despite meeting the minimum required by their power analysis.37 Santos et al (2016),20 Fieril et al (2016)21 and Pivarnik et al (1993)22 had small sample sizes of 28, 20 and 16 participants, respectively, reducing statistical power and potentially failing to capture the full variability in responses among pregnant individuals.37

Selection bias is a recurring theme in the studies. The recruitment methods used by Da Silva et al (2020),18 O'Neill et al (1992),19 Santos et al (2016),20 Pivarnik et al (1993)22 and Fieril et al (2016)21 involved social media and flyers, prenatal clinics and convenience sampling, respectively. These methods likely attracted health-conscious or physically active individuals, leading to selection bias and reducing the external validity of the findings.35 Additionally, all the studies focused on participants in their second or third trimester, limiting the relevance to the early stages of pregnancy.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this scoping review is the first of its kind providing an overview of the current state of research on the Borg RPE scale and MHR during pregnancy. The strengths include the systematic and thorough review process, following the PRISMA guidelines, and the fact that the included studies are presented extensively. Furthermore, the search was carried out in multiple databases, increasing the likelihood of capturing relevant studies, and the entire methodological process is explained transparently, making it easier for the readers and researchers to follow and reproduce. Two authors screened and selected articles in this scoping review, which, in addition to clear inclusion criteria, have minimised bias. However, several limitations should be noted. A limitation is that only six studies were included and the small sample sizes in these studies lower the external validity of our findings. In addition, the studies varied significantly in their design, exercise modalities, intensity targets and gestational stages of participants, leading to heterogeneity that complicates direct comparisons and synthesis. Furthermore, some studies lacked detailed reporting on outcome data such as effect sizes, CIs and specific methodologies, which hindered a comprehensive GRADE assessment. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the participants in the included studies were characterised by a healthy pregnancy, with at least half engaging in regular exercise. Thus, it is unlikely that the participants in the included studies represent all pregnant individuals.

Perspective

The findings in this scoping review contribute valuable insights in the field of sports medicine, specifically regarding the utility of the Borg RPE scale in estimating exercise intensity during pregnancy. The included studies covered various geographical locations, a wide range of gestational ages and diverse training histories, providing a comprehensive perspective. The mixed results reflect the complexity of monitoring exercise intensity during pregnancy and emphasise the necessity for further research. Future studies should investigate how gestational age, gestational weight gain, training history, and current exercise level, weight-bearing and non-weight-bearing activities, familiarity with the scale, and psychosocial factors influence the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR. In addition, larger studies involving diverse populations are essential to offer more evidence on the correlation between the Borg RPE scale and MHR during pregnancy. Longitudinal studies are also needed to observe changes in perceived exertion and MHR responses throughout the various stages of pregnancy. Such research could have practical implications for exercise professionals in effectively monitoring exercise intensity for pregnant individuals. In terms of practice, educating healthcare providers and fitness instructors on how to guide pregnant individuals in the combined use of the Borg RPE scale and MHR monitoring may ensure a more accurate assessment of exercise intensity and compensate for the limitations of relying on a single monitoring tool.

Conclusion

Limited studies in this field yield mixed results, indicating the complexity of monitoring exercise intensity during pregnancy. Based on the divided nature of the literature, using both the Borg RPE scale and MHR may provide a more comprehensive and reliable assessment than using the two methods separately. However, more research with larger-scale studies and standardised test protocols is needed.
