Cognitive Impact of β‐Amyloid Load in the Rapid Eye Movement Sleep Behavior Disorder–Lewy Body Disease Continuum
Authors: Kyung Ah Woo, Eun Jin Yoon, Seoyeon Kim, Heejung Kim, Ryul Kim, Bora Jin, Seungmin Lee, Hyunwoong Park, Hyunwoo Nam, Yu Kyeong Kim, Jee‐Young Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is linked to the diffuse‐malignant subtype and higher cognitive burden in Lewy body disease (LBD).ObjectiveThis study explores brain β‐amyloid deposition and its association with cognitive decline across the RBD–LBD continuum.MethodsPatients with isolated RBD (iRBD), Parkinson's disease with probable RBD (PDRBD), and dementia with Lewy bodies with probable RBD (DLBRBD) underwent 18F‐florbetaben positron emission tomography, 3T magnetic resonance imaging scans, and comprehensive neuropsychological assessments. Subjects were categorized as cognitively normal (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), or dementia. Global and regional standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR) were estimated in predefined cognitive volumes of interest (VOI) derived from voxel‐wise comparison analysis among the cognitive groups, namely the prefrontal, parietal, precentral cortices, lingual gyrus, and supplementary motor area. Generalized linear models assessed the relationship between 18F‐florbetaben SUVRs and neuropsychological testing, adjusting for age and sex. Subgroup analysis focused on the polysomnography‐confirmed iRBD‐continuum subset (n = 41) encompassing phenoconverters and nonconverters in our prospective iRBD cohort.ResultsEighty‐six subjects were classified as follows: 14 NC, 54 MCI, and 18 dementia. The proportion of positive β‐amyloid scans increased with advanced cognitive stages (P = 0.038). β‐Amyloid signals in cognitive VOIs were elevated in subgroups showing impairment in Trail‐Making Test B (TMT‐B). A linear association between TMT‐B z score and global cortical β‐amyloid levels was observed in the iRBD‐continuum subset (P = 0.013).ConclusionCortical β‐amyloid accumulates with declines in executive function within the RBD–LBD continuum. TMT‐B performance may be a useful marker associating with β‐amyloid load, particularly in the iRBD population. © 2024 The Author(s). In Parkinson's disease (PD) and dementia with Lewy bodies (DLB), the underlying Lewy pathology and neurodegeneration begin well before clinical diagnoses are established. 1 Ongoing efforts aim to identify patients in the prodromal stages of these Lewy body diseases (LBD). Isolated rapid eye movement sleep behavior disorder (iRBD) represents the most potent clinical prodromal marker identified to date, with over 80% of patients with iRBD progressing to overt neurodegenerative diseases—predominantly LBDs. 2 , 3 The nature of the first phenoconversion event in the progression of iRBD allows for the classification of iRBD‐derived LBDs into either a parkinsonism‐first phenotype (namely PD) or a dementia‐first phenotype (DLB). 2 , 4

However, increasing evidence supports the notion that PD and DLB do not exist as distinct entities but rather on a clinicopathological continuum. 5 , 6 Data indicate that the risk of dementia in PD remains relatively consistent from early to late stages, suggesting that using the 1‐year rule to differentiate PD from DLB may be arbitrary. 5 , 7 This continuity is particularly evident in subgroups associated with RBD. Whereas nearly all iRBD patients phenoconvert to PD or DLB, only about 50% of those with clinically manifest PD exhibit RBD. 1 This subgroup, Parkinson's disease with probable RBD (PDRBD), is more likely to display characteristics akin to DLB, such as a higher cognitive burden and severe dysautonomia. 1 , 8 Similarly, about 75% of manifest DLB have RBD, which is associated with more pronounced parkinsonism. 1 , 9 Collectively, the RBD–LBD continuum encompassing iRBD, PDRBD, and dementia with Lewy bodies with probable RBD (DLBRBD) may represent a specific subspectrum of LBD, providing valuable opportunities to identify markers of progressive cognitive decline across its prodromal to fully manifest stages.

In this study, we explored the significance of β‐amyloid (Aβ) deposition—a hallmark of Alzheimer's disease (AD) 10 —on cognitive decline and related clinical progression across the RBD–LBD continuum, spanning from iRBD and phenoconverters, PDRBD, and DLBRBD. Aβ pathology is frequently identified in autopsies of patients with Lewy body dementia and is associated with accelerated appearance of dementia in PD. 6 , 11 , 12 Studies suggest a potential synergistic effect between Lewy body and Aβ pathologies on neurodegeneration and cognition. 7 , 13 , 14 In iRBD, cognitive changes in the executive function domain begin early—preceding phenoconversion by up to 10 years—followed by attention deficits 4 , 15 , 16 ; however, the role of Aβ in cognitive and clinical declines in iRBD‐associated prodromal LBDs remains poorly understood. Our investigation analyzed data from patients across the RBD–LBD continuum categorized by cognitive status—cognitively normal (NC), mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and dementia—and assessed global and regional cortical Aβ loads using 18F‐florbetaben positron emission tomography (PET) imaging. We explored the relationship between domain‐specific cognitive changes and Aβ deposit patterns, along with their associations with the core clinical features of DLB within the RBD–LBD continuum.

Patients and Methods

Study Subjects and Clinical Assessment

Patients with iRBD, PDRBD, and DLBRBD were recruited at our movement disorders clinic from February 2022 to June 2023. All iRBD patients and phenoconverters were confirmed as having iRBD through video‐polysomnography based on the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, 3rd edition, following the protocol of our center's iRBD cohort, as published elsewhere. 17 , 18 The PDRBD and DLBRBD groups consisted of individuals with clinically established PD and probable DLB as defined by the 2015 Movement Disorders Society (MDS) criteria and the 2017 McKeith criteria, 19 , 20 who were screened positive for a prolonged history of probable RBD (with the RBD Screening Questionnaire score ≥5). Subjects with structural and/or vascular brain lesions that can affect the clinical manifestations, including moderate to severe small‐vessel disease (higher than Fazekas scale grade I), were excluded.

All subjects underwent 18F‐florbetaben PET scans, APOE genotyping, and clinical evaluation for parkinsonian motor and nonmotor manifestations using the MDS Revised–Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale (MDS‐UPDRS) at enrollment. The presence of hallucinations and psychosis was assessed using the MDS‐UPDRS, Part I, item 2, which was defined positive if the score was 1 or higher. Genomic DNA was extracted from whole blood, and APOE genotyping was conducted 21 (Supplemental File). Subjects with at least one ε4 allele were identified as APOE ε4 carriers.

For cognitive assessment, a detailed neuropsychological test of the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery was performed, which includes the Digit Span Test, Korean‐Color Word Stroop Test, Trail‐Making Test parts A and B, Controlled Oral Word Association Test, Seoul Verbal Learning Test, Rey Complex Figure Test copy, Digit Symbol Coding, and the Korean version of the Boston Naming Test. Standardized z score adjusted for age, sex, and education was calculated for each item in each subject. The diagnosis of MCI was based on the level I category of diagnostic criteria of PD‐MCI according to the 2012 MDS Task Force Guidelines, 22 with impairment on each item defined as a performance of 1 standard deviation (1SD) below the norm, that is, z score <−1.0. The subjects were subsequently categorized into one of the three cognitive status: cognitively normal (NC), MCI, and dementia based on the neuropsychological test results.

To elucidate the cognitive effects of Aβ specifically in the subgroup of iRBD continuum, we conducted additional analysis in a special group of individuals with iRBD and PD‐ and DLB phenoconverters from our iRBD cohort. These subjects were participants enrolled in our center's ongoing prospective iRBD cohort since 2013. 17 , 18 This subgroup comprised of individuals who, at the time of their cohort enrollment, were confirmed through polysomnography to have iRBD without dementia or parkinsonism and had received regular follow‐up assessments every 4 months until their participation in the current amyloid imaging study.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the center's Institutional Review Board (IRB no.: 10‐2021‐137). All subjects provided written informed consent.

Image Acquisition and Preprocessing

18F‐Florbetaben PET and magnetic resonance imaging Procedures

PET scans were obtained using a PET/computed tomography scanner (Gemini TF64, Philips Healthcare, Best, the Netherlands). Participants received an intravenous injection of 300 ± 10% MBq of 18F‐FBB, and 90 minutes post‐injection, a 10‐minute static emission frame was obtained. PET scans underwent reconstruction using an ordered subset expectation maximization algorithm (eight iterations, five subsets). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was conducted on a Philips Ingenia 3T CX MRI scanner (Philips Medical Systems, Netherlands) for all subjects, employing a high‐resolution T1‐weighted spoiled gradient recalled three‐dimensional MRI sequence that covered the entire brain (360 slices, Repetition time (TR) = 8.3 ms, Echo time (TE) = 4.6 ms, Flip angle (FA) = 8°, Field of view (FOV) = 220 × 220 mm2, voxel size = 0.5 × 0.458 × 0.458 mm3).

18F‐Florbetaben PET Image Preprocessing and Analysis

PET data were processed using SPM12 (Statistical Parametric Mapping 12) in MATLAB R2019b (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Each participant's PET image was spatially aligned to their T1‐weighted MRI. MRI scans were segmented using unified segmentation, with templates and settings from the Mayo Clinic Adult Lifespan Template (MCALT). 23 Aligned PET images were normalized to MCALT space using estimated nonlinear spatial transformations. 18F‐Florbetaben standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR) referenced to the SUV of the cerebellar cortex were obtained. Global cortical 18F‐florbetaben SUVR was calculated for a standardized meta‐region covering the entire cortical regions in the MCALT atlas, excluding subcortical regions. Regions with significant group differences underwent atlas‐based regional cortical 18F‐florbetaben uptake determination using the MCALT_ADIR122 and the MCALT_ADIR42 atlas. These volumes of interest (VOI) included the parietal and prefrontal cortices, precentral and lingual gyri, and the supplementary motor area (SMA). Additionally, exploratory SUVR comparisons of selected limbic and striatal VOIs were conducted, encompassing the striatum, thalamus, amygdala, parahippocampal gyrus, and the anterior and posterior cingulate cortices. 18F‐Florbetaben SUVR was converted to centiloid units according to previously described procedures. 24 Subjects were classified as amyloid positive if they had a centiloid value ≥19. 25

Metabolic Brain Network Analysis

To investigate the association between cerebral amyloid deposition and metabolic covariance patterns related to PD (PD‐RP) and PDRBD (PDRBD‐RP)—both established markers of neurodegeneration and predictors of phenoconversion in iRBD 26 , 27 , 28 —we computed PD‐RP and PDRBD‐RP using 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose PET scans conducted in addition to the 18F‐florbetaben PET scans. The detailed methodologies for these procedures are outlined in our previous studies. 26 , 27

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as mean (SD) unless otherwise specified. Data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The cohort was assessed for deviations from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium using χ2 test. Group differences in age, sex, and neuropsychological test z scores were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or χ2 test. For age‐ and sex‐adjusted group comparisons of clinical characteristics, one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) or logistic regression was employed. Post hoc Bonferroni correction was applied as appropriate.

Voxel‐wise group comparisons of SUVRs were carried out using VoxelStats toolbox (https://github.com/sulantha2006/VoxelStats) with linear regression. Age and sex were included as covariates of no interest. Results were corrected for multiple comparisons using random field theory with a cluster threshold of P < 0.001. The group differences in the atlas‐based 18F‐florbetaben SUVRs were evaluated using generalized linear models, with log‐link function to account for skewness of the data. Age and sex were included in the models as covariates. A linear‐by‐linear association model examined whether there was a discernible increasing or decreasing trend in the proportion of abnormal scans concerning the ascending severity of categorized cognitive status (ie, NC, MCI, and dementia).

To assess whether the trajectories of 18F‐florbetaben SUVRs varied with increasing age among groups stratified by sex, APOE ε4 status, cognitive status, or iRBD continuum , a multivariable generalized linear model of global cortical SUVR was employed. Covariates included in each model were group stratified by the factor of interest, age, sex, and the interaction between age and group. Generalized linear models, with age and sex as covariates, were used to determine if significant linear relationships existed between neuropsychological test scores and 18F‐florbetaben SUVR.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio 4.3.0 and SPSS 23.0, with a significance level set at 0.05. Graphs were constructed using GraphPad 10.0. Multiple comparisons were corrected using the false discovery rate (FDR) method, with a significance level of 0.05 for FDR control.

Results

Clinical Characteristics of the Subjects

The final analysis included 86 subjects: 27 with iRBD, 41 PDRBD, and 18 with DLBRBD (Table S1). Nine PD converters and 5 DLB converters from the original iRBD cohort were assigned to the PDRBD and the DLBRBD groups, respectively. Based on the neuropsychological test results, subjects were reclassified into three cognitive groups: 14 with NC, 54 with MCI, and 18 with dementia. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of these groups are presented in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of age, sex, or APOE ε4 allele carriership. Subjects in the dementia group exhibited a higher frequency of hallucinations and psychosis compared to the NC group, and greater motor severity than both the NC and MCI groups. The cohort conformed to Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium assumptions regarding the APOE genotype (P = 0.66; Fig. S1).

Brain Aβ Load across the RBD–LBD Continuum

Among the three groups, there was a statistically significant upward trend in the proportion of individuals with abnormal amyloid scans with increasing cognitive severity (Fig. 1A). Quantitative analysis of global cortical 18F‐florbetaben SUVR revealed a significant elevation in the dementia group compared to the NC or MCI groups (Fig. 1B). Global uptake demonstrated an age‐related increase (P = 0.038) in a generalized linear model with sex and age–sex interaction terms as covariates. Women exhibited higher uptake than men (P = 0.018, β = 0.058; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.010–0.107) after adjusting for age, but no significant age–sex interaction effect was observed when the interaction term was included. APOE ε4 carriers exhibited higher global 18F‐florbetaben SUVR compared to noncarriers (P = 0.010, β = 0.076; 95% CI: 0.018–0.133) (Fig. 1C–E; Table S2).

18F‐Florbetaben Uptakes in Cognitive VOIs and Trail‐Making Test B Performance

Large multicenter longitudinal studies have shown that impairment in Trail‐Making Test B (TMT‐B) performance is one of the earliest indicators of cognitive decline in individuals with iRBD. 4 , 16 When 18F‐florbetaben SUVR was stratified using individual neuropsychological test scores, global cortical uptake and uptake in the five designated cognitive VOIs were significantly higher in patients with impaired TMT‐B performances, after being adjusted for age and sex (Fig. 3B). This association with Aβ loads was specific to TMT‐B, as stratifying groups based on scores from other neuropsychological test scores did not reveal any significant differences in global cortical SUVR (Table S5).

To address the potential confounding effect of motor impairment on cognitive test results, further groupwise comparisons were conducted, controlling for scores from the MDS‐UPDRS, Part III. Notably, both global cortical and regional uptakes in the SMA and the precentral gyrus remained significantly higher in the subjects with a TMT‐B z score of <−1.0 (Table S6). No significant differences were observed when groups were stratified based on the scores of other neuropsychological test items.

Brain Aβ Load and Hallucination/Psychosis

In 34 subjects with hallucination and/or psychosis, the proportion of positive Aβ scans was significantly higher compared to those without such symptoms (n = 52), after adjusting for age and sex (P = 0.046; Fig. 3C). Furthermore, the group with hallucination/psychosis demonstrated higher global Aβ loads, as well as increased Aβ deposition in the parietal and prefrontal cortices, and the SMA among the cognitive VOIs (Table S7).

Global Cortical Aβ Load and Parkinsonian Motor Severity

The dementia and nondementia (NC plus MCI) groups exhibited distinguishable trajectories between global cortical 18F‐florbetaben SUVR in relation to parkinsonian motor severity (Fig. 3D). The dementia group exhibited a positive association between higher MDS‐UPDRS, Part III, scores and global cortical 18F‐florbetaben SUVR (P = 0.011, β = 0.007; 95% CI: 0.002–0.012), whereas no significant trend was observed in the nondementia group. A comparison of these trajectories revealed that with increasing parkinsonian motor severity, the dementia group exhibited a more pronounced elevation in Aβ signal than the nondementia group (P = 0.024, β = 0.005; 95% CI: 0.001–0.009; Table S8).

The iRBD‐Continuum Analysis

A subgroup analysis was conducted on 41 subjects, comprising 27 individuals currently diagnosed with iRBD and 14 who phenoconverted to PD (n = 9) or DLB (n = 5) from previous iRBD states. These phenoconverters have been regularly monitored through clinical follow‐up as part of our center's iRBD cohort protocol, 31 and additional information on the subjects is provided in the Supplemental File. Within this subset, a distinct linear correlation was observed between the TMT‐B performances and global cortical 18F‐florbetaben SUVR (P = 0.013, β = −0.012; 95% CI: –0.022 to –0.003; Fig. 4; Table S9). Notably, this linear relationship between TMT‐B and Aβ signal was specific to this iRBD‐continuum subgroup, as no similar correlation was found in the PDRBD and DLBRBD groups who were not phenoconverters from the iRBD cohort (P = 0.174).

Global Cortical Aβ Load and PDRBD‐RP

Of the 86 participants, 74 underwent an 18F‐fluorodeoxyglucose PET scan in addition to the 18F‐florbetaben PET scan (mean interval = 41 days; Fig. S2). No significant differences in PD‐RP or PDRBD‐RP were observed among the NC, MCI, and dementia groups, contrasting with the elevated Aβ burden in the dementia group compared to NC and MCI. Additionally, no direct correlation was found between PDRBD‐RP, PD‐RP, and global cortical 18F‐florbetaben uptake. However, exploratory comparisons based on elevated PDRBD‐RP in five cognitive ROIs revealed higher precentral 18F‐florbetaben SUVR in the group with PDRBD‐RP >1.2 (+2 SDs from the healthy control (HC) average). 26

Discussion

In this cross‐sectional study on the RBD–LBD continuum of LBDs, we utilized 18F‐florbetaben PET imaging to investigate the association between cerebral cortical Aβ signals, neuropsychological profiles, and core features of DLB. Our findings underscore that within the RBD–LBD continuum, the rate of Aβ positivity increases as cognition deteriorates and as hallucinations and/or psychosis are present. The quantitative burden of Aβ was higher across multiple cortical regions in the dementia group compared to those with MCI and NC. Within the dementia group, the Aβ burden also increased with the progression of parkinsonian motor severity. Finally, individuals with impaired TMT‐B performances exhibited higher global and regional Aβ levels. This trend was particularly pronounced in a specific subgroup composed of iRBD patients and phenoconverters, where a linear association between TMT‐B performance and cortical Aβ levels was distinctly evident.

In our cohort, 33% of dementia patients exhibited Aβ positivity, aligning with prior studies reporting positivity rates of 33%–69% in DLB. 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 The observed 13% positivity in our MCI group, however, is lower than previous estimates of up to 41%. 32 This discrepancy might stem from the relatively permissive z score threshold of <−1.0 for domain‐specific cognitive impairment in this study, aimed at detecting the earlier Aβ substrate for subtle cognitive changes. Furthermore, our study focused on RBD; therefore, our MCI group included individuals who had iRBD with MCI or PDRBD with MCI, and not exclusively those expected to imminently develop dementia. This could account for the observed differences from findings in the MCI‐LB group—prodromal DLB—in previous DLB‐continuum studies. 32 This approach was adopted because iRBD patients with MCI can progress to either PD or DLB, underscoring a clinical need to focus on the RBD continuum from an iRBD perspective. The trend of increasing Aβ positivity from NC to MCI, and then to dementia, and the link between Aβ burden and motor severity among dementia subjects, supports the hypothesis of ongoing Aβ accumulation beyond the MCI phase in the RBD–LBD continuum, in contrast to plateauing at the MCI phase as in AD. 36 , 37 We also note that older age and the presence of the APOE ε4 allele are associated with a greater cortical Aβ burden in the RBD–LBD continuum. 6 Interestingly, women in our cohort exhibited higher Aβ levels, a sex difference not evident in a recent Pittsburgh compound B PET study in the DLB continuum 32 ; this discrepancy may be due to better representation of women in our cohort across all cognitive stages.

We observed increased regional Aβ loads in the dementia group, specifically in the lingual, precentral, and the SMA, as well as in the prefrontal and parietal cortices, compared to the NC or MCI groups. A few studies have explored the topographic progression of Aβ with worsening cognition in LBD. Prior PET comparisons of DLB to PD or PD dementia have documented heightened Aβ burdens in frontal and parietal regions. 38 , 39 , 40 In the field of AD, attempts to stage regional amyloid patterns have identified a highly consistent regional hierarchy; notably, the lingual gyrus is classified as one of the “last” regions of accumulation in the AD spectrum, 41 , 42 with the precentral and SMA also classified as regions with low frequencies of Aβ positivity, indicating “later” accumulation. 41 The Aβ involvement of the occipital lobe, housing the lingual gyrus, remains contentious in LBD, with some studies reporting occipital sparing of Aβ pathology in autopsy‐confirmed LBD cases, 35 whereas others suggest Aβ deposition in this region contributes to visuospatial dysfunction in LBD. 43 The lingual gyrus is involved in processing visual information, particularly visual memory, letter/word recognition, and identification. 44 , 45 Considering that visual manifestations are prominent in the development of DLB, we speculate that the lingual gyrus may be among the critical sites for Aβ accumulation in LBD's progression into DLB, specifically within the RBD–LBD continuum.

Subjects exhibiting impaired performance in TMT‐B demonstrated a higher global Aβ burden and increased regional Aβ bindings in the SMA and the precentral cortex (M1), independent of potential influences from motor deficits. This supports a regional impact of Aβ accumulation 46 on early cognitive changes in LBD. The SMA, crucial for motor planning, execution, and action sequencing, 47 projects to M1 and is closely connected to the fronto‐striatal network, 48 , 49 , 50 the anatomical correlates of executive dysfunction tested using TMT. Notably, only TMT part B showed associations with Aβ, whereas part A did not. As the motor substrates for parts A and B are identical, and part B additionally evaluates the complex executive set‐shifting component, 51 this further supports that Aβ accumulation in SMA/M1 is associated with TMT impairment through executive cognitive decline per se.

In the individuals with iRBD and phenoconverters, we identified a linear relationship between declines in TMT‐B performance and increased cortical Aβ loads, both globally and in specific frontal cognitive ROIs, including the prefrontal and precentral cortices and the SMA. Polysomnography‐confirmed iRBD is particularly critical for understanding the cognitive underpinnings of LBDs, as it represents the only group where domain‐specific cognitive changes from the prodromal phase have been thoroughly analyzed in longitudinal multicenter studies. 4 Our observations indicate that cortical Aβ accumulation is closely associated with the progression of executive cognitive decline in LBDs evolving from iRBD.

Indeed, multicenter studies in iRBD have demonstrated that cognitive decline initiates up to a decade before phenoconversion, with executive function being the first to deteriorate. 4 , 16 In particular, TMT part B shows early impairment and the highest rate of decline over time, serving as a sensitive predictor of dementia. 4 We hypothesize that Aβ accumulation may occur alongside fronto‐striatal network dysfunction from the prodromal phases in the iRBD continuum. Autopsy findings suggest that PD patients with probable RBD exhibit greater density and range of cortical Lewy pathology compared to those without probable RBD. 52 Although nigrostriatal Lewy pathology primarily impacts the fronto‐striatal network through neurodegeneration and related circuit changes, 53 pathological evidence indicates a synergistic interplay between cortical Lewy bodies and Aβ, in terms of both pathologic burdens and cognitive consequences. 6 , 7 Clinically, the prodromal subtype presenting as iRBD in LBD is thought to align with the “diffuse‐malignant” subtype of PD, which overlaps with DLB, 1 and the “body‐first” subtype of PD, characterized by widespread Lewy burden and a faster onset of dementia. 54 If cortical Lewy pathology evolves in a more “diffuse‐malignant” manner from the iRBD phase, as opposed to what the “benign” or “brain‐first” subtypes experience in their prodromal phases, we suspect it may correlate with cortical Aβ accumulation within the fronto‐striatal network. 55 This could synergistically impact cognitive functions, contributing to a progressive decline in executive functions during the prodromal phase of LBD. Further longitudinal studies are necessary to explore the causal impact of Aβ load on cognitive trajectories in individuals with iRBD.

The combined analysis of PDRBD‐RP and Aβ loads offers several additional insights. Within the iRBD continuum, PDRBD‐RP serves as a sensitive marker for neurodegeneration potentially linked to future cognitive decline. 26 , 27 , 28 It has been observed that even iRBD patients with NC exhibit elevated levels of PDRBD‐RP, comparable to those in the MCI group and higher than in healthy controls. 28 Similarly, the lack of significant differences in PDRBD‐RP levels among the NC, MCI, and dementia groups in our cohort suggests that PDRBD‐RP may occur early during the NC stage in LBD. In contrast, Aβ burden is clearly higher in the dementia group compared to the NC and MCI groups. This indicates that although both PDRBD‐RP and Aβ are associated with the earliest cognitive changes in LBD, further progression of Aβ pathology may be closely involved in the transition from MCI to dementia in later stages. With the growing interest in the substrates influencing the development of dementia in LBD, recent studies have pointed to alterations in the limbic cholinergic system as a prominent candidate. 56 , 57 Therefore, exploring the interplay between the cholinergic system and amyloid pathology at this transitional stage could provide valuable insights.

Finally, for interpreting our findings, Kehagia's dual syndrome hypothesis can serve as a valuable reference. 53 This hypothesis identifies two broad cognitive syndromes within the PD spectrum: (1) executive deficits in nondemented patients reflecting fronto‐striatal dopaminergic dysfunction and (2) visuospatial, semantic fluency, and verbal/visual memory deficits in those rapidly progressing to dementia, attributed to cholinergic dysfunction. 53 This pattern resembles the trajectory of cognitive decline seen in iRBD, where early executive dysfunction is followed by more specific verbal episodic memory decline in patients progressing to dementia. 4 Meanwhile, recent hypotheses suggest that the cholinergic system is upregulated in the frontal cortex to compensate for dopaminergic denervation in PD, making frontal executive functions particularly sensitive and vulnerable to cholinergic decline, 58 , 59 , 60 indicating that the effects of the two neurotransmitter systems are not mutually exclusive for the two cognitive syndromes. Similarly, amyloid pathology in LBDs may be linked to either or both cognitive syndromes. Furthermore, although amyloid may synergistically interact with synuclein—a major contributor to dopaminergic loss—it is implicated in age‐related cholinergic basal forebrain pathology. 61 Consequently, we believe subsequent studies will be able to determine which cognitive syndromes and/or neurotransmitter systems, as classified by Kehagia, are related to amyloid pathology in LBD.

The limitations of the present study include the cross‐sectional nature of the analysis between amyloid load and cognition. Our ongoing prospective cohort data will allow for a more in‐depth analysis of the impact of Aβ load in the future. It is crucial to recognize that cognitive trajectories are shaped by a complex interplay of various factors, including neuropathological, neurochemical, and genetic elements. 62 Due to the increase in global Aβ load with higher UPDRS motor scores in the dementia group, analyzing the association between Aβ and dopamine transporter imaging in the DLB phenotype could be an interesting endeavor. Ultimately, a comprehensive and multimodal approach integrating various aspects will be necessary. The absence of a healthy control group is also a limitation of this study. Although this factor is not within the scope of the current research, incorporating subjects with manifest LBD without RBD in future studies could provide deeper insights, especially given the active discussions regarding clinical phenotyping related to the presence or absence of RBD in PD, such as the SOC (α‐synuclein origin and connectome) model. 54 Enrolling both PD subjects with and without RBD, and healthy controls if possible, and collecting serial amyloid PET and longitudinal clinical outcomes could help elucidate the factors influencing amyloid accumulation and potentially determine the causality between dementia onset and amyloid. Additionally, a cautious interpretation is required for the decreased SUVR in the hippocampus of the dementia group. Even with partial volume correction, accurately delineating the hippocampus and extracting values from the gray matter using PET resolution can be challenging. Regional amyloid signals may appear lower in areas already undergoing atrophy 63 ; indeed, a positive correlation between regional SUVR and hippocampal volume was observed in our dementia group (Fig. S3). Prior research has reported that the severity of AD pathology (tau or amyloid) correlates with hippocampal atrophy in DLB, 64 , 65 which may have resulted in an underestimation of hippocampal uptake in our dementia group. Therefore, lower hippocampal SUVR in the dementia group does not necessarily rule out amyloid or related AD pathology in the region. Meanwhile, patterns of increased amyloid deposition in other brain regions that differ from the typical AD pattern (eg, lingual gyrus) might characterize dementia in LBD. Future studies will be necessary to verify this assumption.

Overall, our findings highlight that within the RBD–LBD continuum, cortical Aβ accumulates as cognition deteriorates, particularly alongside early declines in executive function—an early marker for dementia phenoconversion in iRBD. 4 Additionally, a higher Aβ load correlates with other clinical hallmarks of Lewy body dementia, such as parkinsonian motor symptoms and hallucinations/psychosis, across the cohort. Furthermore, the distinct linear relationship between increasing Aβ load and TMT‐B impairment in the subset of iRBD and phenoconverters offers valuable insights into the progression of Aβ co‐pathology from the prodromal stages of PD/DLB, as well as the cognitive implications of Aβ within this distinct subgroup of LBD spectrum.
