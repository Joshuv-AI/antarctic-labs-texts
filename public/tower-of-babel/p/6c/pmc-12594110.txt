Sociodemographic drivers of care disparity among patients with inborn errors of immunity
Authors: Caitlin M. Neary, Mei-Sing Ong, Jocelyn R. Farmer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewTo review the current literature surrounding impact of sociodemographic factors, including race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status, on care disparities for inborn errors of immunity (IEI).Recent findingsThere is a growing body of literature indicating significant sociodemographic and geographical disparities in diagnosis, management, and clinical outcomes of IEI. Historically underserved populations are more likely to experience underdiagnosis, diagnostic delay, and more severe adverse health events, including more infections and earlier death. Implementation of universal newborn screening and no-cost genetic testing programs have helped address the cost barriers to early diagnosis, representing important steps towards addressing diagnostic disparities in IEI. In parallel, recent advances in artificial intelligence offer promise by enhancing identification of IEI through automated analysis of clinical data. However, significant disparities remain.SummaryThis review highlights sociodemographic drivers of care disparities for IEI. More inclusive screening and diagnostic tools, such as use of unbiased molecular and artificial intelligence approaches, may offer critical opportunities to close these gaps for these most vulnerable IEI patient populations.

INTRODUCTION

Inborn errors of immunity (IEI) are a diverse collection of immune system dysregulation disorders due to underlying, inherited causes. While initially considered rare, common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) – the most common, symptomatic IEI worldwide, can be observed in up to 1 : 25 000 individuals [1]. IEI is associated with earlier mortality and more frequent infectious and noninfectious complications, particularly in the pediatric population [2–6]. Its diverse clinical manifestations, including recurrent or severe infections, abnormal development, and systemic autoimmunity or autoinflammatory disease, present considerable diagnostic challenges. Reports of diagnostic delay in published CVID cohorts range from 4 to 8 years [5–8]. Recent published studies have shown longer diagnostic delay affecting historically marginalized populations, as well as disparities in IEI screening and mortality [9▪▪,10,11▪▪]. Here, we review the use of universal newborn screening, other molecular approaches, and the application of artificial intelligence as potential solutions to ameliorate these systemic biases in care.

PREVALENCE OF INBORN ERRORS OF IMMUNITY VARIES PROPORTIONAL TO GEOGRAPHIC ACCESS TO HEALTHCARE

IEI has been predominantly studied in specific regions of the world and in narrowly defined patient populations. The European Society for Immunodeficiencies (ESID) registry captures more than 30 000 IEI patients as of 2020, with IEI prevalence estimated as 5 : 100 000 across Europe [12,13]. In contrast, countries across Africa, Asia, Central and South America have reported lower IEI prevalence rates and have fewer established patient registries [12,14–18]. IEI registries have been published in only seven African countries, and just 5.5% of the recorded patients have a confirmed molecular diagnosis [12]. Lack of access to advanced immune diagnostic testing likely contributes to this disparity. A census of CVID patients in Colombia revealed that fewer than half could independently afford the necessary immune testing, such as vaccine titers and lymphocyte subsets by flow cytometry, required to meet ESID criteria for a formal CVID diagnosis [19]. Furthermore, countries that are overrepresented in IEI registries tend to have higher Human Development Indices (HDI), a measure of a country's development, education, income, and standard of living [20]. Even within high HDI countries that have robust IEI patient registries, historically minoritized patients remain underrepresented, suggesting that IEI may be underdiagnosed among these populations. For example, enrollment in the United States Immunodeficiency Network (USIDNET) largely overrepresents White and non-Hispanic individuals when compared to the expected population demographics [21].

Thus, current IEI registries may not adequately reflect the global scope and diversity of IEI. There is, therefore, a significant risk of biases in our understanding of its prevalence, particularly in racial and ethnic minoritized populations. For example, genetic founder-effects within the United States may uniquely impact Navajo, Amish, and Mennonite communities [22,23]. Reference genome databases may also misrepresent allele frequencies in racial and ethnic minoritized populations due to underrepresentation of these populations in genomic datasets [24]. This lack of inclusion can lead to inaccurate variant interpretation and hinder equitable genetic diagnosis and research.

IMPACT OF SOCIODEMOGRAPHIC FACTORS ON INBORN ERRORS OF IMMUNITY PATIENT DIAGNOSIS

Published data demonstrate substantial socioeconomic disparities in IEI diagnosis in the United States. A recent retrospective analysis of a national healthcare claims database in the United States reported prolonged interval from clinical suspicion to diagnosis of IEI, particularly among patients from predominantly non-White neighborhoods who experienced significantly greater diagnostic delay compared to those residing in predominantly White neighborhoods, despite having more severe presenting symptoms and more occurrences of sepsis and pneumonia in the year following IEI diagnosis [9▪▪]. In a recent electronic health record (EHR)-based study of patients with an IgG 600 mg/dl or less at an academic hospital in Boston, White patients were significantly more likely to have an International Classification of Disease (ICD)-10 coded diagnosis of hypogammaglobulinemia, compared with non-White patients [odds ratio 1.46, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.32–1.60; P < 0.001] [10]. In another study in a New York hospital system, an EHR-based algorithm derived from ICD-9 codes was developed to identify patients with undiagnosed IEI. This work showed that patients with undiagnosed IEI were more likely to be Hispanic or Black and more likely to be insured by Medicaid [25]. Together, these data suggest the systematic underdiagnosis of IEI patients, by race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status.

Variability in access to immunology specialty care likely plays a major role in driving disparities in the diagnosis of IEI [26–30]. National surveys in the United States have found that most primary care providers are not confident in recognizing the signs, diagnosing, and managing IEI, underscoring the need for improved training and standardized screening protocols [31,32]. Additionally, improved survival of patients with IEI into adulthood has presented the challenge of missed/delayed diagnosis later in life [33]. Survey data have also revealed that physicians serving primarily non-White patients are less likely to refer for specialty work-up even when IEI is suspected [32]. Qualitative studies on patient lived experiences further identified several interconnected factors contributing to diagnostic delay, including institutional distrust, cultural differences in how medical problems are perceived, limited patient education and outreach, as well as barriers and breakdowns in patient–provider communication and relationship [34].

Underdiagnosis of IEI may also stem from shortcomings in medical education, including how diseases are represented in the literature. For instance, underrepresentation of patients with skin of color in medical literature has contributed to a widespread lack of clinical expertise in recognizing and diagnosing dermatological disease such as cellulitis and eczema in these populations, which in turn increases the risk of overlooking dermatological manifestations of IEI [35]. This is further complicated by the inherently unique clinical presentations of cutaneous manifestations in individuals with immune deficiency [36]. A lack of understanding of the diverse manifestations of 22q11.2 syndrome has also led to under-recognition of the condition among Black children. Specifically, the characteristic craniofacial features of 22q11.2 syndrome seen in White patients are not always present among Black patients [37].

IMPACT OF SOCIODEMOGRAPHIC FACTORS ON INBORN ERRORS OF IMMUNITY PATIENT OUTCOMES

Diagnostic delay adversely impacts IEI patient outcomes globally. Among patients with CVID, there is an increased mortality risk of 1.7% for every year of diagnostic delay as well as a 4.5% increase in mortality risk for each year of increased age of diagnosis [6]. Diagnostic delay is associated with delayed treatment (e.g. immunoglobulin replacement therapy) [9▪▪,38] and greater morbidity and mortality, including lung and other organ damage, and complications including pneumonia, bronchiectasis, and recurrent sinusitis [6,39–43]. Given that diagnostic delay is more pervasive among minoritized IEI patients, it is unsurprising that they have a higher likelihood of experiencing worse clinical outcomes. An analysis of United States national mortality from 2003 to 2018 found that among individuals with IEI listed as an underlying cause of death, Black Americans had a significantly higher age-adjusted death rate at 4.25 per one million person-years, compared to 2.01 per one million person-years for White Americans. Notably, 85% of Black patients died before the age of 65, compared to 57% of White patients. Additionally, Hispanic patients were 3.6 times more likely than non-Hispanic patients to die before the age of 24 [11▪▪].

Published studies have also highlighted disparities in the use of immunoglobulin replacement therapy (IgRT), a life-saving treatment and standard-of-care for the majority of IEI patients with compromised antibody production. In a Boston safety-net hospital that primarily serves low-income populations, patients with antibody deficiencies received IgRT at a much lower rate as compared to the national United States IEI patient registry, and pneumonia and bronchiectasis were more prevalent in Black compared to White patients [38]. Lack of access to IEI treatment likely extends beyond IgRT to gene therapy, use of biologics, and consideration of hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) [26]. Moreover, reduced access to IEI treatment is a global trend, with survey of 55 countries identifying that access to IgRT and HSCT were limited to 12.7 and 27.3% of country respondents, respectively [44].

HEALTH EQUITY USING MOLECULAR APPROACHES

Newborn screening programs have been shown to reduce healthcare disparities across many life-threatening diseases [45]. In 2010, California was the first state in the United States to implement a universal assay of T-cell receptor excision circles (TRECs) in dried blood spots of all infants for the purpose of screening for T-cell lymphopenia indicative of severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) or other related IEI [45–47]. SCID is life-threatening without early intervention. Prior to the implementation of universal newborn screening for SCID, referral for life-saving HSCT disproportionately occurred for White patients (80%) [45]. In direct contrast, in the first 2 years of California's program, the incidence of low TREC detection was proportionate to the demographics of the general patient population, including an similar representation of Black, Hispanic, and Asian children [45,47]. Another follow-up on the program through 2017 found similar rates of SCID across racial or ethnic groups [46]. While SCID newborn screening has been successful in identifying cases early, data has unfortunately shown that Black children still face higher mortality following HSCT as compared to White children [48]. Moreover, there is a lack of universal screening for the spectrum of IEI diagnoses outside of the severe T-cell immunodeficiencies, which constitutes the majority of IEI patients.

Next-generation genetic sequencing holds additional promise to improve equitable IEI diagnostic screening [28,29]. No-cost genetic testing programs, direct-to-consumer genetic testing, and universal newborn screening programs have shown similar prevalence of IEI genetic diagnosis across racial and ethnic groups [45,46,49]. No-cost genetic sequencing through the Jeffrey Modell Foundation has assessed nearly 1400 suspected IEI patients, and further demonstrated how increased access to resources like genetic testing can hasten IEI diagnosis [50]. Unfortunately, there remains significant disparities in the uptake of genetic testing among underserved populations. A recent study evaluating the impact of a sponsored genetic testing program for IEI showed persistent disparities in genetic testing for IEI by race, socioeconomic status, and geography. Specifically, while there was a small but significant increase in the proportion of individuals from socioeconomic disadvantaged background in the sponsored program, compared with the nonsponsored program, racial minoritized patients and those residing in rural regions continued to be underrepresented in both the sponsored and unsponsored testing cohorts [51▪]. Recent data has shown that the diagnostic yield of exome sequencing for pediatric-onset disease diagnosis is consistent across diverse patient genetic ancestries [52]. This further emphasizes that the overrepresentation of White children diagnosed with many rare and pediatric-onset disease is largely driven by differences in which populations are being tested. As genetic testing becomes more widely adopted in the diagnosis of IEI, it is essential to ensure that all individuals have equitable access to these diagnostic tools.

Finally, soluble serum biomarkers that are more cost affordable than current standard-of-care flow cytometry and genetic testing for IEI may be a future potential in diagnostics. Recently, levels of inflammatory serum cytokines in blood were shown to have equal utility in differentiating high-risk CVID patients as compared to the flow cytometry standard [53▪]. Whether these data will extend to more diverse patient demographics remains unknown; however, these initial results highlight the potential of using serum diagnostic tests as an alternative diagnostic tool in resource-limited settings.

HEALTH EQUITY USING COMPUTER ALGORITHMS AND ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE TECHNOLOGIES

Artificial intelligence has the potential to play a transformative role in reducing diagnostic disparities in IEI, particularly in under-resourced settings where access to immunology care is limited. For example, artificial intelligence-powered tools can assist nonspecialist healthcare providers by offering evidence-based diagnostic suggestions [54▪,55–57]. When trained on diverse datasets, artificial intelligence can identify patterns relevant to specific populations, reducing bias that traditionally stems from underrepresentation of historically marginalized populations in clinical trials or IEI data registries and genomic databases.

Early attempts at non-artificial intelligence, rule-based computer algorithms have demonstrated the role algorithmic identification of at-risk individuals could play in improving the underdiagnosis of IEI [25,58]. More recently, the Software for Primary Immunodeficiency Recognition Intervention and Tracking (SPIRIT) Analyzer was created to identify high-risk individuals. This customized computational tool leverages over 350 weighted ICD-9, ICD-10, and pharmacy codes paired to clinical metrics of impaired immune function to classify individuals as low, medium, or high risk of having an IEI [60]. Published data have demonstrated the utility of the tool in facilitating population-wide screening for IEI [61].

Several studies have further shown that systematic screening in healthcare records using artificial intelligence algorithms can enhance the detection of IEI [54▪,59,62,63]. A recent study developed a machine learning algorithm to identify undiagnosed cases of CVID using EHR data. The algorithm was able to identify 64% of individuals with CVID prior to their ICD-based diagnosis, some as early as several years before the diagnosis [64▪]. An additional study successfully combined an artificial intelligence algorithm with the SPIRIT analyzer to identify high-risk patients using healthcare claims [63].

The advent of large language models (LLM) offers the opportunity to democratize artificial intelligence, particularly in under-resourced healthcare settings. Unlike traditional artificial intelligence tools that often require specialized technical knowledge to implement or interpret, LLMs can be deployed with minimal infrastructure and used directly by clinicians, including those without formal training in data science or machine learning. These models can assist in guiding diagnostic reasoning by synthesizing complex patient data, interpreting unstructured clinical notes, and offering evidence-based suggestions in real time. Their user-friendly, conversational interfaces make them especially accessible for primary care providers and community health workers, enabling them to deliver more accurate and timely care. A recent study exploring the use of LLMs for diagnosing IEI demonstrated promising results, with OpenAI GPT model achieving an accuracy exceeding 96% [54▪]. However, there remains issues of reproducibility and hallucinations in the output of artificial intelligence models, and further refinement and improved model training is necessary to optimize clinical utility [54▪,65–67].

Several IEIs present with subtle yet distinct facial features. Subjective assessment of characteristic facial features risks biased screening across racial and ethnic groups. Facial analysis technology using machine learning can play a valuable role in screening for such conditions, particularly in resource-limited settings. For example, a study on 22q11.2 deletion syndrome evaluated 106 individuals across 11 countries, representing African, Asian, and Latin American backgrounds. Findings showed significant variability in clinical presentation by ancestry: only congenital heart disease and learning problems were common (>50%) across all groups, while other features differed significantly (P < 0.05). The study further showed that facial analysis technology is capable of identifying the condition with high accuracy across diverse ethnic groups (sensitivity and specificity >95%), highlighting the technology's potential to address gaps in diagnosis [67].

While artificial intelligence holds significant promise for reducing healthcare disparities, it is essential to ensure that algorithmic bias does not inadvertently reinforce or worsen existing inequities. Artificial intelligence systems trained on nonrepresentative or biased datasets may produce inaccurate or inequitable results, amplifying existing human biases [68,69]. Careful attention must be given to the design, validation, and ongoing monitoring of artificial intelligence tools to ensure they are inclusive, transparent, and equitable to avoid misdiagnosis or delayed care for groups underrepresented in the training data.

CONCLUSION

Disparities in the diagnosis and management of IEI is a critical public health issue globally, impacting historically minoritized populations and underserved geographic regions, driven by resource limitations and gaps in our current understanding of the diverse manifestations of IEI across different populations. While molecular and technological advancements offer the potential to improve early detection and standardize care across populations, these tools must be developed and applied with a deliberate focus on equity, incorporating diverse patient data, ensuring accessibility in low-resource settings, and promoting healthcare experience that is both patient-centered and built on trust.
