Tuberculosis infection tests for contact screening in Brazil: a cost-effectiveness analysis
Authors: Fernanda Souza, Ricardo Steffen, Marcia Pinto, Thiago Nascimento do Prado, Afranio Lineu Kritski, Anete Trajman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe World Health Organization has recommended three new tuberculosis (TB) antigen-based skin tests (TBST) (Diaskintest®, C-TST®, Cy-TB®) for the diagnosis of tuberculosis infection (TBI). Household contacts (HHC) of people with pulmonary TB are at increased risk for TBI. We evaluated the cost-effectiveness of the use of TBST and of QuantiFERON-TB Plus® (QFT-Plus®) compared to the tuberculin skin test (TST) in Brazil.MethodsA state-transition Markov model simulating four distinct hypothetical cohorts of 10,000 HIV-negative contacts of different ages (3 months–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, and 15 or older) was built over five annual cycles for TBI testing and tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT) under the Brazilian public health system perspective. Effectiveness was estimated from systematic reviews and costs were raised from the Brazilian government (TST and QFT-Plus®) and from the manufacturers (TBST). Incremental cost-effectiveness per TB case averted was calculated.FindingsTBST has similar accuracy to QFT-Plus®, and all are more specific than TST. TBST are less costly compared with TST or QFT-Plus®. All TBST were cost-saving regardless of the age group compared to TST or QFT-Plus®. Cy-Tb® was the most cost-saving, followed by Diaskintest® and C-TST®.InterpretationThe incorporation of the new TBST for screening in the Brazilian public health system would be a cost-saving alternative for contacts. Because these tests require the same infrastructure and human resources skills as TST, the tests could be readily implemented.FundingCNPq and Stop TB Partnership.Keywords: Household contacts, Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, Diagnosis, Cost-effectiveness analysis

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from November 23, 2014, to March 25, 2025, using the following strategy: (skin tests) AND (cy-tb) OR (diaskintest) OR (EC test) OR (c-tst) OR (esat 6) OR (cfp 10) OR (recombinant tuberculosis allergen) AND (tuberculosis) AND (y_10 [Filter]). We found that few studies have focused on analyzing the cost-effectiveness of tuberculosis antigen-based tests (TBST) in household contacts (HHC). A systematic review by Goscé et al. (2024) on the economic impact of novel Mycobacterium tuberculosis-specific antigen-based skin tests for detecting tuberculosis infection (TBI), compared to the tuberculin skin test (TST) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), concluded that evidence on the costs and cost-effectiveness of these novel TBSTs remains limited. Their search conducted by Goscé et al., on 30 July 2021 and updated until 22 November 2022, identified six original studies that evaluated the cost-effectiveness of TBSTs: one by Steffen et al. (2020) and five others published in Russian. Since then, three original studies were published. Bashir et al. (2024) evaluated the cost and cost-effectiveness of TBI detection methods in two high-burden countries (India and Brazil). The study compared: (a) novel skin-based tests (Diaskintest® and Cy-Tb®) with the purified protein derivative (PPD)-TST; (b) IGRAs; and (c) the most cost-effective skin-based test with the most cost-effective IGRA, using a decision tree model from a health system perspective. The study population included the general population, vulnerable groups, immunocompromised patients, and HHC. The outcome was the number of correctly detected TBI. Diaskintest® was potentially cost-saving compared to one IGRA in these high-burden TB countries, although the IGRA demonstrated greater effectiveness. Muniyandi et al. (2024) estimated the cost-effectiveness of the Cy-Tb® test compared to the TST for diagnosing TBI in HHC in India, adopting a health system perspective and using decision tree analysis. The outcomes were the number of TB cases prevented. The findings indicate that implementation of a novel diagnostic tool, the Cy-Tb® test, would be cost-effective in screening HHC for TBI in India. Goscé et al. (2023) developed a Markov model parameterized for Brazil, South Africa, and the UK to compare the cost-effectiveness of three TBI testing strategies: (1) Diaskintest®, (2) TST, and (3) an IGRA. The analysis included HHC, immunocompromised individuals, and other vulnerable populations, considering different perspectives. The cost per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) was calculated based on utility scores associated with TBI treatment. The results suggest that Diaskintest® may save costs and provide greater health benefits compared to the TST and IGRA in Brazil and South Africa. Finally, Diaskintest® was found to be less costly and more effective than the TST for people living with HIV in Brazil, according to Steffen et al. (2020). The researchers developed a Markov model parameterized from a health system perspective to evaluate QALY outcomes. We did not find any original studies that conducted a cost-effectiveness analysis comparing the three current TBSTs among HHCs of various age groups and used the number of TB cases prevented as the effectiveness measure for the evaluated strategies.

Added value of this study

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of TBST compared to currently recommended TBI tests (TST and QuantiFERON-TB Plus®–QFT-Plus®) in HHC of different ages (3 months–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, and 15 or older) without known HIV infection from the perspective of a public universal health system, over a five-year time horizon, using number of TB cases averted as the outcome. We found that incorporation of the new TBST for screening of contacts in the Brazilian public health system would be a cost-saving alternative. Because these tests require the same infrastructure and human resources skills as TST, the tests could be readily implemented and the incorporation of TBST could result in cost savings and stability of consumable stocks.

Implications of all the available evidence

Overall, the available evidence suggests that TBST can be cost-effective alternatives to the TST and QFT-Plus® approach.

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends targeted tuberculosis (TB) infection (TBI) screening and tuberculosis preventive treatment (TPT) for household contacts (HHC) of people with TB.1 Where tests are not readily available, WHO recommends offering TPT without testing for contacts and people living with HIV (PLH).1

However, a recent individual patient data meta-analysis found that those who test negative for TBI have no significant protection, apart from children under 5, who may have some protection.2 In Brazil, testing for TBI is mandatory for HHC, with few exceptions such as contacts living with HIV and neonates living with a person with pulmonary or laryngeal TB.3 Available TBI tests for contacts are tuberculin skin test (TST), for contacts under 2 and over 10, and QuantiFERON-TB® Gold Plus (QFT-Plus®, Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), for contacts aged 2–10. In addition, a chest radiograph (CXR) is mandatory to rule out TB before any TPT prescription.3

The widely available TST using purified protein derivative (PPD) has lower specificity in Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG)-vaccinated populations,4 and frequent stock-outs were reported globally, whereas the use of the interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) has restricted scalability because of the need for laboratory facilities and costs. Unlike the TST, IGRA are not affected by BCG vaccination or by infection with non-tuberculous mycobacteria, with few exceptions. Three TB antigen-based tests (TBST) were recently recommended by WHO and are currently being validated in Brazil: Diaskintest® (Generium Pharmaceutical, Moscow, Russia), C-TST® (Zhifei Longcom Biologic Pharmacy Co., Anhui, China), and Cy-TB® (Serum Institute of India, Pune, India).4 TBST are skin tests for the detection of TB infection that use M. tuberculosis (Mtb) complex-specific antigens (ESAT-6 and CFP-10). Overall, the new TBST class of tests are as sensitive as TST and IGRA, and more specific than TST, making them suitable alternatives for TBI testing.4 As TST, TBST are low-cost, portable tests and can be conducted in any facility with cold chain and trained healthcare workers. Primary care-based tests are more accessible than laboratory-based tests for the most vulnerable populations in high-burden low and middle-income countries.4 TBST were found to be less costly and more effective than TST for PLH5 and in healthcare workers6 in Brazil.

The objective of this study was to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the diagnostic algorithm for HHC of different age ranges without known HIV infection from the perspective of the Brazilian public universal health system, over a five-year time horizon.

Methods

Setting and current policies

Brazil is an upper-middle-income country with a public universal health system (Sistema Único de Saúde—SUS) and has one of the 30 highest TB burdens.7 TB treatment and prevention drug dispensation is restricted to public free-of-charge health clinics. Assessment of TB and TBI in all-age contacts of persons with pulmonary or laryngeal TB with or without bacteriological confirmation (i.e., defined by clinical and radiological criteria) is recommended since 2010.3

Asymptomatic contacts without known HIV infection aged up to 2 years or over ten years undergo TST and if negative, a second TST is performed 8–12 weeks later; if the first TST is positive or in case of conversion (a 10 mm increment over the previous induration size) and in the absence of clinical and radiological signs of TB disease, TPT is recommended (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).3

Asymptomatic contacts between 2 and 10 undergo initial testing with QFT-Plus® and CXR; the same pathway than adults is recommended thereafter (Supplementary Figure S1).3

Contacts with symptoms, regardless of age, should undergo a sputum test (smear microscopy or Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra), CXR and/or other tests, according to the symptoms. After excluding TB diagnosis, screening for TBI should be conducted as above (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2).3

The TPT regimen of choice is 3HP (3 months of weekly doses of 900 mg rifapentine and 900 mg isoniazid) for those over 40 kg and 3HR (3 months of rifampicin 75 mg and isoniazid 50 mg/day) for those between 4 and 25 kg, except for contacts of people with isoniazid monoresistant TB or intolerance to isoniazid or rifampin.8,9 4 R (4 months of rifampicin 600 mg/day) is the regimen of choice for children weighing under 4 kg, people over 50 years of age, those with chronic liver disease and those in whom 3HP or 3HR is not indicated.3 Other approved regimens are six or nine months of isoniazid 300 mg/day (6H and 9H).

Population, study perspective, time horizon and discount rate

Because prevalence, incidence, diagnostic algorithms (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2) and treatment for TBI varies among contacts according to age groups,1, 2, 3 four distinct hypothetical cohorts of 10,000 HIV-negative contacts were included, based on age: 3 months to 4 years (3 months–4 y), 5–9 years (5–9 y), 10–14 years and 15 or older (≥15 y). We adopted the Brazilian Public Health System perspective. A time horizon of five years was considered, since among those with TBI, approximately 5–15% will develop the disease, with a higher risk in the first years after infection.10 Additionally, newer technology and cost variation are expected within this period. Discount rates at 5% per year for effects and costs to increase the comparability of studies were applied, as recommended by the Brazilian guidelines for economic analyses.11

Model structure

For testing, we compared: TST (base-case) versus QFT-Plus®—the only IGRA available in the public health system–and three TBST: Diaskintest®, C-TST® and Cy-TB®. The model simulated the natural history of TBI, comparing the clinical outcomes and economic impacts of the newer diagnostic technologies to TST. An empirical treatment approach (treating without testing) was also analyzed in the under 5 group, as there is evidence for a modest protection effect even of those who test negative.2

For the base-case scenario, we considered the screening algorithm proposed by the WHO (Supplementary Figure S3).1 In the first consultation, HHC should undergo TB screening and be simultaneously tested for TBI, since sequential screening and diagnostic testing (first for TB, then for TBI) may result in substantial delays or losses in TB infection testing, especially if these tasks are performed by different healthcare professionals or in different locations.1 Therefore, during the initial healthcare encounter, it is recommended to perform CXR, microbiological tests for TB disease (molecular rapid test for the base-case and sputum smear microscopy in the sensibility analysis), and screening for TBI among contacts with suggestive TB symptoms.1 For asymptomatic contacts under the age of 10, we considered implementing various strategies for TBI screening along with CXR. For those aged 10 and older, CXR was performed only for those who had a positive result or conversion in the TBI screening test.3

All analyses were performed with TreeAge Pro Healthcare 2022 (TreeAge Software, Inc). The following health states were considered (1) no TBI in baseline; (2) TBI in baseline; (3) TB in baseline; (4) no TBI as final outcome; (5) uninfected misdiagnosed (false positive) treated; (6) uninfected misdiagnosed (false positive) not treated; (7) TBI not treated; (8) TBI treated; (9) TBI eliminated after treatment; (10) TB progression; (11) cured TB; and (12) death (Supplementary Figure S4). The effectiveness measure was the estimated number of TB cases avoided for each strategy. We also estimated the number needed to misdiagnose (NNM),12 which is defined as the number of patients that must be tested for one misdiagnosis to occur (Supplementary Material).

Testing procedures and interpretation

The skin test procedure consists of applying 2 international units of PPD Rt-23 or a TBST on the volar aspect of the forearm skin, according to the Mantoux method (Supplementary Material). The induration size is read 48–72 h after tuberculin application.1 Conversion is defined by an increment of 10 mm over the first TST; we extrapolated the same concept to TBST, despite the absence of clear recommendations by the manufacturers or available evidence. QFT-Plus® testing is based on the manufacturer's instructions using 1 ml aliquots of whole blood. When the result is indeterminate, the test is repeated once. Conversion is defined when a negative test becomes positive, and the same pathway is recommended, i.e., CXR and TPT if normal.

BCG vaccination is recommended in the first month of life in the country. The cut-off for TST interpretation does not change according to BCG status, as per the National Guidelines (5 mm),3 based on evidence that a positive TST is more likely due to contact than to BCG 2 years after vaccination.13,14

Sensitivity analyses

One-way sensitivity analysis was conducted for all model inputs. For the cost parameters, the lower limit of the range was half the price and the upper limit was twice the price.11 We used the 95% confidence interval range of transition probabilities. We applied the cost of sputum smear microscopy in sensitivity analyses instead of the rapid molecular test,1 as rapid molecular tests are only available in part of the Brazilian cities. We also evaluated screening for TBI only in asymptomatic contacts, as recommended by the NTP (Supplementary Figures S1 and S2),3 considered the QFT-Plus® test as the diagnostic reference, and the TPT with 4 R for those under 10 years of age in the sensitivity analyses.8 Finally, discount rates from 0 to 10% were also applied.11

We performed a probabilistic sensitivity analysis in a Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 iterations. A gamma distribution was assumed for costs, with the standard deviation equal to 10% of the original value. We assumed that the uncertainty in clinical probabilities and accuracies followed a beta distribution with the standard deviation estimated from data extracted from systematic reviews. The generated distributions around costs, the probability of TB cases avoided from diagnosis of TBI and its preventive treatment and the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) are shown using median and 95% uncertainty ranges (UR).

Model assumptions

Several simplifying assumptions were incorporated into the model: (1) CXR and microbiological tests are 100% accurate in ruling out TB; (2) individuals with a second indeterminate result on the QFT-Plus® test had the same risk of developing TB as those with a negative result32; (3) all patients diagnosed with TBI were prescribed TPT; (4) drop-outs and adverse events occurred early in the course of TPT and conferred no protection; (5) all individuals with TB consented to treatment; (6) TB cases were managed under DOT; (7) death from other causes had no financial impact on the Ministry of Health (MoH); (8) stability of newer recombinant reagents were comparable to that of PPD Rt-23.

Willingness-to-pay-threshold

In Brazil, the government-established cost-effectiveness threshold is US $7752 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY) gained.33 Due to the lack of a threshold reference for a TB averted case in Brazil, we opted to use the QALY threshold as a reference, which has been the metric used for the incorporation of technologies into SUS.

The Markov model projected the sustained protection rates into 5 years’ risks for each strategy (Table 3). Screening with TBST and treating TBI or TB 10,000 contacts over a 5-year period had the lowest cost, regardless of the age group. The strategy with the TST avoided a few more TB cases compared to the strategies that used TBST tests across all age groups in four hypothetical cohorts of 10,000 HHC: TST avoided 142 cases in the 3 months-4y age group, 202 in the 5–9 y age group, 213 in the 10–15 age group and 889 in the ≥15 y and older age (Table 3). For the same age groups, QFT-Plus® avoided respectively 139, 194, 207 and 948 (Table 3); Cy-TB® avoided 112, 163, 175 and 862; Diaskintest® avoided 139, 196, 209 and 932 and C-TST® avoided 132, 195, 208 and 917, respectively. On the other hand, the NNM was lower for the strategy with TST when compared to the other strategies (Supplementary Table S4).

Since the accuracy of the tests was comparable, cost-effectiveness was primarily influenced by their unit costs. TBST were cost saving regardless of the age group (Supplementary Figures S5–S8). Considering all strategies simultaneously, the Monte Carlo simulations show that, at a willingness-to-pay threshold of US$ 7752 per avoided case, Cy-TB® test had the highest net benefit in more than 95% of simulations in all age groups (Supplementary Figures S9–S12). QFT-Plus® was the least cost-effective across all age groups. In HHC 3 months–4 y, the three TBST and the treat-all strategy (no testing) were more cost-effective than both the TST and the QFT-Plus® (Table 3).

In one-way sensitivity analyses, incremental cost-effectiveness was most sensitive to QFT-Plus® cost when compared to TST, regardless of the age group (Supplementary Table S5). When comparing TST with Diaskintest®, the model was more sensitive to variations in the probability of conversion and to the cost of TPT (Supplementary Figure S6).

Replacing molecular tests with smear sputum resulted in a small cost reduction for all tests and across age groups (Supplementary Table S10). When considering a TBI prevalence of 60% across all age groups (Supplementary Table S11), screening for TBI only in asymptomatic contacts (Supplementary Table S12), using QFT-Plus® as the reference test for HHC aged 2–10 y (Supplementary Table S13), or TPT with 4 R for the same age group (Supplementary Table S14), TBST remain the lowest cost option.

Discussion

In this model, the three WHO-recommended TBST, (Diaskintest®, Cy-TB®, and C-TST®) were cost-saving when compared to the current standard of care (QFT-Plus® in children 2–10 and TST in other age groups) in Brazilian contacts of all ages. Because their accuracy is similar, except for a lower specificity of TST, this finding was mostly driven by the costs of the tests (Table 1). QFT-Plus® was the least cost-effective, and TBST and not testing (treat all strategy) were more cost-effective for contacts under 5 years of age. These findings were robust across several sensitivity analyses.

TBST have also been shown to be cost-saving in Brazilian PLH (Diaskintest®)5 and healthcare workers (Diaskintest® and Cy-Tb®).6 A similar Markov simulation study34 found that the Diaskintest® was cost-saving in Brazil and South Africa when compared to TST and IGRA in HHC, immunocompromised patients and other vulnerable populations, considering QALY as the outcome. Another cost-effectiveness analysis, when developing the decision tree model for India and Brazil from a health system perspective, identified that the strategy using Diaskintest® was the most cost-effective for Brazil in the general population.35

A different cost-effectiveness analysis of the Cy-Tb® test compared to the TST for diagnosing TBI in HHC in India, adopting a health system perspective and using decision tree analysis, also expressed the model outcomes as the number of TB cases prevented. The study concluded that implementing the Cy-Tb® test would be cost-effective for screening HHC for TBI in India.36 This study did not include other TBST or IGRA tests.

This study has several limitations. Drug-resistant TB strains were excluded from our analysis, as they have modest prevalence in Brazil and current guidelines recommend against treating contacts of individuals with drug-resistant TB.37 Furthermore, we did not consider the entire TB spectrum (incipient and sub-clinical disease),38 as no commercially available tests or treatment implications exist for those TB status. Although 10% of TBI cases are at risk of progressing to TB over a lifetime, the highest risk occurs in the first years after infection, with a peak in the first two years, which justifies the choice of a five-year time horizon for the development of the Markov model.

Decision analyses rely on the quality and accuracy of base-case parameter values. Although sensitivity analyses were conducted to test major assumptions, several parameters were fixed, which may limit the scope of the findings. Moreover, the costs of the new TBST were provided by the manufacturers, which may imply potential conflicts or limitations in these estimates. Nevertheless, the cost parameters of the different evaluated tests did not have an impact on sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Material). Additionally, our study did not model HIV infection, or loss of HHC in the initial phase of the TBI diagnosis cascade (those not identified or not screened) or skin test booster effects. However, we believe the booster effect, which is maximum at 5 weeks, is less likely than a true conversion when testing contacts with a first negative 8 weeks after the first test. Given the absence of original national studies on the subject, it was not possible to assess validation against real-world epidemiological data, the organization of health network services in Brazil, including laboratory infrastructure and sample transportation. The generalization of the study to other countries is limited, since health system structure and financing, as well as other important parameters such as TBI prevalence will differ substantially. Finally, given the absence of a specific threshold for averted TB cases in Brazil, we used the QALY threshold as a reference, as it is the standard metric for incorporating new technologies into the Brazilian public health system, despite the limitations of this comparison.11,33

Despite these limitations, our study can contribute to policy decision-making in Brazil. Frequent stockouts of PPD have resulted in substantial drawbacks in TPT scale-up in the country. The incorporation of TBST could result in cost savings, better accessibility compared to lab-based tests, and stability of stocks. However, for the incorporation of any health technology into the public universal health system, in addition to economic analyses, other criteria should be considered: clinical (efficacy, safety, effectiveness, and target population), patients’ preferences (ethics, convenience, and acceptability), and organizational (diffusion, logistics, training, and sustainability). In summary, our findings suggest that alternatives to the TST and QFT-Plus® approach are worth considering, as these new tests could potentially facilitate the scale-up of TB prevention programs and contribute to TB elimination efforts in Brazil.
