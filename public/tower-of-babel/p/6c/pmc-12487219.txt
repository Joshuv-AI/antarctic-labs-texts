The transcriptome analysis reveals the regulatory mechanisms of female sterility in Lagerstroemia speciosa ‘Zichan’
Authors: Ruoyong Yin, Shuaijie Lu, Baojin Zhang, Fei Xiao, Lan Wang, Ruchun Xi, Xiaomei Deng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvule abortion presents a major biological obstacle to hybrid breeding in the genus Lagerstroemia, but its molecular regulatory mechanisms remain inadequately understood. This study investigates the female sterility cultivar Lagerstroemia speciosa ‘Zichan’, providing a comprehensive analysis of the cytological features and molecular regulatory networks underlying female sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ for the first time through paraffin section microscopy and transcriptome profiling.ResultsMorphological analyses indicate that female sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ results from abnormal embryo sac development. Post the eight-nucleate stage, the embryo sac exhibits disordered fibrous structures, ultimately shrinking and dissolving. During the tetranuclear embryo sac stage, the ovules display irregular fibrous arrangements within the embryo sac and undergo programmed degeneration of the female gametophyte. Transcriptome analysis revealed significant downregulation of the AGL15 gene at the binucleate embryo sac stage, which may affect embryo sac development through the gibberellin metabolic pathway, while abnormal overexpression of the AG1 gene triggers a reproductive inhibition signal. In the auxin signaling pathway, accumulation of AUX/IAA suppresses ARF6 activity, and compensatory activation of ARF7/ARF9 fails to reverse the sterility process. Additionally, retention of DELLA proteins (GAI1) interferes with embryo sac maturation by inhibiting gibberellin-responsive genes. Abnormal overexpression of SPL family genes at the eight-nucleate stage may indirectly affect development by regulating the spatial distribution of auxin.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that ovule abortion in Lagerstroemia speciosa ‘Zichan’ results from the combined disruption of cytokinin, gibberellin, and auxin signaling pathways, leading to impaired embryo sac development at the eight-nucleate stage. Significant downregulation of cytokinin signaling components, abnormal accumulation of DELLA proteins due to GID1B dysfunction, and suppressed ARF6 activity collectively interfere with embryo sac maturation. Additionally, misregulation of SPL family and floral organ development genes contributes to sterility. These findings provide new molecular insights into the regulatory network of female sterility in woody plants and establish a foundation for fertility restoration through targeted gene editing or hormone treatments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07332-w.Keywords: Female sterility, Lagerstroemia speciosa, Plant hormone signaling, SPL gene family, Transcriptome analysis

Introduction

Seed abortion is a common biological phenomenon in flowering plants, playing a significant role in optimizing the genetic quality of offspring [1–3]. However, for endangered species, seed abortion represents a critical bottleneck that hinders population expansion and ex situ conservation efforts [4]. In-depth analysis of the molecular mechanisms underlying female sterility not only offers new insights into reproductive biology but also has practical value for the conservation of endangered plants. The pistil, as the central reproductive organ of angiosperms, plays a crucial role in fertilization success, with its developmental integrity directly impacting fertilization rates. The stigma, as the primary barrier for pollen recognition, has its papillar cell morphology closely linked to pollen adhesion efficiency. Studies in the Brassicaceae family have shown that the bilayer cell wall structure of papillar cells (comprising the cuticle layer and pectin-cellulose layer) is crucial for mediating pollen capture [5]. Additionally, the stigma regulates pollen hydration, germination, and pollen tube guidance through the secretion of signaling molecules [6]. During fertilization in angiosperms, the pollen tube must precisely navigate through the three barriers: the stigma, style, and ovary, ultimately delivering sperm cells to the embryo sac to complete double fertilization [7]. This process involves a complex mechanism of male-female interaction. Notably, Zhou et al. [8] discovered in the female sterility mutant of Lagerstroemia indica that the papillar cells of the stigma were significantly shortened and loosely arranged, accompanied by abnormal phenotypes, such as narrowed style channels and degenerated epidermal cell nuclei. This suggests that female sterility may be directly linked to defects in stigma development. Embryo sac development consists of two key stages: megasporogenesis (meiosis forming functional megaspores) and megagametogenesis (mitosis resulting in a seven-cell, eight-nucleus structure) [9]. Studies on the development of the Polygonum-type embryo sac, exemplified by Arabidopsis thaliana, have shown that abnormalities at any stage (such as megaspore degeneration, nuclear migration defects, or issues with cellular differentiation) can result in female sterility [10]. Therefore, systematically analyzing the dynamics of ovule development and changes in gene expression profiles is a key breakthrough in uncovering the mechanisms of sterility.

With the advancement of functional genomics, transcriptome analysis has become a central approach for unraveling complex biological processes [11]. This technology has been successfully applied to studying female gametophyte development in model plants, such as Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, resulting in the identification of numerous key genes involved in embryo sac formation and pollen tube guidance [12]. Floral organ development follows the ABCDE gene interaction model, where different categories of genes collaborate to regulate the formation of sepals, petals, stamens, and carpels [13, 14]. The C-class gene AGAMOUS regulates carpel development and collaborates with the D-class gene AGL11 (AGAMOUS-Like 11) in ovule formation [15–17]. Plant hormone genes play a crucial role in floral organ formation, including pistil development [18, 19]. The plant hormone signaling network regulates ovule primordium differentiation and embryo sac development through pathways such as auxin polar transport, the cytokinin-PIN1 regulatory axis, and the gibberellin receptor GID1 [20]. The core components of the auxin signaling pathway comprise three protein families: the F-box TRANSPORT INHIBITOR RESPONSE 1/AUXIN SIGNALING F-BOX PROTEIN (TIR1/AFB) family, the Auxin/INDOLE-3-ACETIC ACID (Aux/IAA) family, and the AUXIN RESPONSE FACTOR (ARF) family. Within this signaling cascade, TIR1/AFB functions as an auxin co-receptor, Aux/IAA proteins act as transcriptional repressors, and ARFs serve as transcriptional activators [21]. The Arabidopsis genome encodes a total of 23 ARF genes (Okushima et al., 2005). Among them, ARF5 directly represses the expression of ARR7 and ARR15 (Zhao et al., 2010), whereas ETTIN (ETT)/ARF3 and ARF4, in cooperation with MONOPTEROS (MP), regulate the transcriptional repression of SHOOT MERISTEMLESS (STM) via histone deacetylation during the reproductive phase, thereby promoting organ initiation [22]. TIR1 and AFB act as receptors mediating auxin signaling. When intracellular auxin levels rise, the TIR1/AFB–SCF complex binds auxin together with Aux/IAA substrates, leading to the ubiquitination of Aux/IAA proteins. These ubiquitinated repressors are subsequently degraded by the 26 S proteasome, thereby releasing ARFs from inhibition and enabling the transcriptional regulation of downstream target genes [23]. Auxin plays a decisive role in determining ovule density. In most auxin-related mutants, defects in carpel margin meristem formation result in reduced ovule numbers or complete ovule loss [24]. The auxin efflux carrier PIN1 is specifically expressed in sporophytic tissues of ovules, but not in gametophytic tissues, indicating that PIN1-mediated polar auxin transport in the sporophyte exerts a crucial influence on female gametophyte development in Arabidopsis [25]. Extensive studies have demonstrated that DELLA proteins, as key negative regulators of the gibberellin (GA) signaling pathway, act as growth repressors during plant development. DELLA proteins modulate hypocotyl elongation by interacting with transcription factors such as PHYTOCHROME INTERACTING FACTORS (PIFs) and BRASSINAZOLE RESISTANT 1 (BZR1) [26, 27], regulate floral development through association with SQUAMOSA PROMOTER BINDING PROTEIN-LIKE (SPL) transcription factors [28], and control fruit formation by interacting with ALCATRAZ (ALC) [29]. The widely accepted model of GA signal transduction can be summarized as follows: GA—GID1—DELLA—SCF^GID2/SLY1/SNE—floral organ development. Within this framework, DELLA proteins function as central repressors of GA signaling and floral organ growth. In the absence of bioactive GA, the GA receptor GIBBERELLIN INSENSITIVE DWARF1 (GID1) fails to interact with DELLA proteins, which remain stable and suppress the activity of their interacting partners, thereby repressing GA-responsive gene expression. Upon GA perception, GID1 interacts with DELLA, triggering SCF^GID2/SLY1/SNE-mediated ubiquitination and subsequent proteasomal degradation of DELLA proteins. The release of their interacting partners activates GA signaling, ultimately promoting floral organ growth and development [30]. In tomato, the procera mutant carries a point mutation within the conserved DNA-binding domain of the DELLA gene LeGAI, resulting in a loss of DELLA function. Consequently, this mutant exhibits constitutive GA responses and displays a parthenocarpic phenotype [31]. Integrated studies of genetics and hormone regulation demonstrate that floral organ formation involves the coordinated action of multi-layered molecular networks, offering a theoretical foundation for understanding abnormalities in pistil development.

Lagerstroemia speciosa is a large deciduous tree in the Lythraceae family and Lagerstroemia genus, native to Australia and Southeast Asia. It was introduced to southern China in the 1950s. Compared to Lagerstroemia indica, Lagerstroemia speciosa is characterized by rapid growth, larger flowers, and more vibrant colors. However, research on the breeding of L. speciosa has progressed slowly, with a limited number of cultivars available. Additionally, the plant flowers infrequently, produces a large number of capsules, and the mature fruits turn black, persisting on the tree for an extended period. The simultaneous presence of flowers and fruits significantly detracts from the landscape’s aesthetic appeal. After nearly a decade of cultivation, our team developed a new L. speciosa cultivar, L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, through open pollination using L. speciosa as the female parent. This cultivar is characterized by continuous flowering and the absence of fruit set [32]. Compared to commonly planted Lagerstroemia speciosa varieties in China, this cultivar exhibits complete pollen sterility and does not set seeds when used as a maternal parent in hybridization, demonstrating female sterility characteristics. Additionally, it flowers multiple times a year, with compact inflorescences, vibrant flower color, abundant blooms, and a long flowering period, providing excellent ornamental value.

Based on the unique phenotype of the sterile mutant L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ previously identified, this study integrates cytological and comparative transcriptomic approaches to investigate the following scientific questions: (1) whether the sterility of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is associated with stigma and pollen tube growth; (2) the key regulatory nodes and associated gene changes underlying female sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’; and (3) the identification of genes potentially regulating sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. The results of this study will enhance the understanding of female sterility mechanisms in woody plants and provide a theoretical basis for the innovation of L. speciosa germplasm.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The experiment was conducted in 2024, using 10 mature trees of Lagerstroemia speciosa and 10 of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ (a cutting-propagated cultivar), all grown under identical horticultural conditions in the experimental nursery of South China Agricultural University (113°21′26″E, 23°9′26″N). All trees were approximately 10 years old. Pistils at sequential developmental stages were collected based on floral bud diameter, starting from 1 mm and sampled at 0.5 mm intervals until full anthesis. For each stage, three floral buds were collected per tree and processed for paraffin sectioning and cytological observation. Samples at different developmental stages were collected from the same individual tree to ensure within-plant consistency. The developmental stages of embryo sacs were determined based on paraffin sectioning results. Three trees were randomly selected from a total of ten experimental trees to serve as three biological replicates. At the four-nucleate, eight-nucleate, and mature embryo sac stages, ten pistils were collected from each tree and used for transcriptome sequencing and qRT-PCR analysis.

Ultramicroscopic observation of the stigma

On the day of collection, pistils were isolated from fully bloomed flowers, and the samples were fixed using an optimized method based on Zhou et al. [8]. The pistils were initially fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde (prepared in 0.2 mol L−1 phosphate buffer) for 12 h, washed 4 times with phosphate buffer (0.2 mol L−1), and then fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (prepared in 0.2 mol L−1 phosphate buffer) for 4 h. After washing 3 times with phosphate buffer (0.2 mol L⁻¹), the samples were dehydrated through an ethanol gradient and then freeze-dried. The processed samples were placed on a sample holder and gold-coated for 20 min using an ion sputter coater. The samples were then observed and photographed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; ZEISS, EVO MA 15, Germany). Five samples were collected for each treatment, with three replicates.

Fluorescence observation of pollen tube germination

Artificial pollination was performed using L. speciosa pollen on both L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ and L. speciosa. The style and ovary were fixed in FAA fixative (70% alcohol: glacial acetic acid: formaldehyde = 90:5:5) for more than 5 days, until the style became nearly colorless. Before sectioning, the style and ovary were washed with deionized water to remove surface fixative, then placed in 1 mol/L NaOH solution at 60 °C for 80 min to soften. After washing 3 times with distilled water, the samples were stained with 0.1% aniline blue for 6 h. Pollen tube germination was observed and photographed under a Nikon Eclipse Ni-U fluorescence microscope (Japan) using an ultraviolet light filter. Five samples were collected for each treatment, with three replicates.

Paraffin sections

After the flower buds reached to 1 mm, samples were taken at 0.5 mm intervals for paraffin section preparation. Ten flower buds were collected at each developmental stage. The pistils were placed in FAA fixative (70% alcohol: glacial acetic acid: formaldehyde = 90:5:5), subjected to vacuum infiltration, and stored at 4 °C. Conventional paraffin embedding techniques were used to prepare 8 μm thick flower organ sections with an automatic microtome (Leica RM 2245, Germany). The sections were stained with 1% aniline blue and hematoxylin-eosin staining solution for 2 h [33, 34], Pollen tube germination was observed and images were captured using a fluorescence microscope(Nikon, Eclipse Ni-U, Japan).

RNA sequencing and analysis

Samples for transcriptome sequencing were collected following the method described in Sect. 2.1 and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. Total RNA was extracted from the mixed samples using the DP411 Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After assessing the quality and concentration of the extracted RNA, sequencing was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego). The sequences were further processed with a bioinformatic pipeline tool, BMK Cloud (www.biocloud.net) online platform. Raw data (raw reads) of fastq format were firstly processed through in-house perl scripts. In this step, clean data (clean reads) were obtained by removing reads containing adapter, reads containing ploy-N and low quality reads from raw data. At the same time, Q20, Q30, GC-content and sequence duplication level of the clean data were calculated. All the downstream analyses were based on clean data with high quality. Gene function was annotated based on the following databases: NR (NCBI non-redundant protein sequences); Pfam (Protein family); KOG/COG/eggNOG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups of proteins)༛Swiss-Prot (A manually annotated and reviewed protein sequence database)༛KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes)༛GO (Gene Ontology). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.20.0) for comparisons with biological replicates and EBSeq (v1.26.0) for those without replicates. Genes with a fold change ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01 were considered as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). DEGs were further categorized as up-regulated or down-regulated based on their expression levels between the control (A) and treatment (B) groups. The DEG identifiers were named in the format “A vs. B”, indicating the comparison direction.

qRT-PCR validation

Nine genes were selected from a large set of transcriptomic data for qRT-PCR to assess the reliability and reproducibility of the transcriptome data. Specific primers were designed using the NCBI Blast online tool based on the Unigenes sequences. The primers used in the expression analysis are listed in Supplementary Table S1, with RPII gene serving as the internal control [35]. Total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara, Japan), following the manufacturer’s instructions. The resulting cDNA was diluted 10-fold prior to use. qRT-PCR was carried out in a 20 µL reaction volume containing 10 µL of SYBR® Premix Ex Taq II (2×) (Takara, Tli RNaseH Plus, Dalian, China), 0.4 µL each of forward and reverse primers (10 µM), 2 µL of diluted cDNA, and 7.2 µL of RNase-free water. Reactions were performed on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s; followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Melting curve analysis was performed to confirm amplification specificity. Each gene was tested with three biological replicates and three technical replicates. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data were processed with SPSS 21.0 (IBM Statistics, USA). Adobe Photoshop CS6 software (Adobe, USA) and Adobe Illustrator 2019 (Adobe, USA) were used to typeset figures appearing in the manuscript, line graphs were generated using Origin 2024 (Origin Lab, USA).

Results and analysis

Stigma morphology and structure analysis

Observations of paraffin sections and SEM images of the pistils from L. speciosa and L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ revealed that in L. speciosa, the epidermal cells on the outer edge of the mature style were neatly arranged and were elongated in shape (Fig. 1A, B). The cells contained large nuclei, and the large vacuoles were filled with numerous starch grains and small, dense lipid bodies. Papillary cells between or on the surface of the epidermis contained abundant secretions (Fig. 1C), providing the material basis for pollen germination and recognition. The transverse section of the style was circular, with tissues evenly distributed. The outer epidermis was smooth and flat, with multiple layers of basic tissue cells arranged neatly and regularly. The inner epidermal cells consisted of dense, near-elliptical cells, and the style canal was smooth without irregular protrusions (Fig. 1D). The cells of this layer were smaller and more tightly packed than those of the basic tissue. Under the microscope, distinct nuclei, thicker cytoplasm, and features of glandular cells were observed (Fig. 1E). The epidermal cells on the outer edge of the style of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ were elongated in shape (Fig. 1F, G). The stigmatic tissue appeared to have thin and compact parenchyma cells, unevenly distributed epidermal cells, and relatively sparse secretions (Fig. 1H). The transverse section of the style was irregularly elliptical, with few indentations in the outer epidermis. The number of basic tissue cell layers was fewer than that in L. speciosa (Fig. 1I). The inner epidermis was composed of dense, nearly round cells that formed irregular protrusions, with distinct nuclei and glandular cells (Fig. 1J). However, the appearance and morphology of the stigma in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ show no significant defects, indicating that stigma morphology and structure are not the key factors contributing to its female sterility.

The pistil mutant does not inhibit pollen tube germination

In the previous section, we found that the stigma morphology and structure of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ are not the cause of its female sterility. In this section, the focus shifts to investigating whether the interspecific hybrid female sterility of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is related to obstruction of pollen tube behavior. Fluorescence microscopy was used to systematically track the developmental dynamics of the pollen tube within the pistil tissues. Within 24 hours after pollination, a large number of germinated pollen tubes were observed on the surface of the L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ pistil, with a density similar to that of the self-pollinated L. speciosa control group (Fig. 2B). In the style of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, germinated pollen tubes exhibited sustained elongation and some successfully penetrated the stylar guide tissues to reach the ovary region. Microscopic images showed that pollen tubes entering the ovary were able to grow directly along the placenta and establish contact with the micropyle of the ovule within the observation window (Fig. 2B). No large-scale callose accumulation was observed on the surface of the L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ pistil, indicating that the papillary cells of the pistil did not initiate the typical incompatible recognition response (Fig. 2B). Pollen tube growth in the style of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ was smooth and continuous, without local swelling, coiling, or arrest, which are typical indicators of pollen rejection. At the micropyle, the pollen tube tip exhibited normal morphology for content release (Fig. 2B, arrow), indicating that ovule-derived chemotactic signaling remained largely intact. Despite successful entry of pollen tubes into the ovules, extensive embryo abortion was still observed in the ovary of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. These findings suggest that embryo abortion is not due to impaired pollen tube guidance or fertilization failure, but rather intrinsic developmental defects within the ovule itself.

The arrest of ovule development impedes the formation of the female gametophyte

The stigma of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ shows no significant defects, and pollen can germinate normally on the stigma and elongate through the style to reach the embryo sac. Therefore, it is hypothesized that the female sterility of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is caused by issues in early female gametophyte development. To further investigate the stage of abnormal female gametophyte development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, paraffin sections were used to observe the embryo sac development. The results showed that the large female gametophyte of L. speciosa could develop into a mature embryo sac, containing egg cells (Fig. 3A) and synergid cells (Fig. 3B). The embryo sac belongs to the monosporic type. After the formation of the eight-nucleate embryo sac, L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ rapidly shrinks and degenerates (Fig. 3C, D, E), failing to further develop until it atrophies into rod-shaped cells at the mature stage (Fig. 3F).

Analysis of DEGs at different developmental stages of pistils in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ and L. speciosa

Microscopic examination of paraffin sections revealed that abnormalities in ovule development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ (hereafter referred to as Z) manifested subsequent to the formation of the eight-nucleate embryo sac. Accordingly, and in accordance with the sampling procedures outlined in Sect. 2.1, pistils were collected from both L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ (Z) and L. speciosa (hereafter referred to as D) at three critical developmental stages: the four-nucleate embryo sac stage (designated as Z1 and D1), the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage (Z2 and D2), and the mature embryo sac stage (Z3 and D3). Transcriptomic comparisons were performed between corresponding developmental stages of the two cultivars (D1 vs. Z1, D2 vs. Z2, D3 vs. Z3, Z1 vs. Z2, and Z2 vs. Z3) to elucidate differential gene expression patterns associated with ovule development. RNA sequencing generated 99,963 transcripts. To further identify key genes regulating pistil abortion, 38,463 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between pistils at different abortion stages were compared. In the D1 vs. Z1 group, 9,554 upregulated DEGs and 2,502 downregulated DEGs were identified. In the D2 vs. Z2 group, 9,080 upregulated DEGs and 2,965 downregulated DEGs were identified. In the D3 vs. Z3 group, 11,284 upregulated DEGs and 3,078 downregulated DEGs were identified. In addition, 1,079 upregulated and 1,157 downregulated DEGs were identified in the Z1 vs. Z2 group, while 2,420 upregulated and 2,401 downregulated DEGs were identified in the Z2 vs. Z3 group (Fig. 4A). A total of 7,050 genes exhibited significant differential expression across the three different developmental stages of the pistil (Fig. 4B). Among the three groups, the D3 vs. Z3 group had the highest number of DEGs, followed by D1 vs. Z1 and D2 vs Z2. The morphological study results showed that the pistils were completely sterile at the third stage (mature embryo sac), which may explain the larger number of (DEGs).

Enrichment analysis of DEGs

GO enrichment analysis of DEGs between Lagerstroemia speciosa and L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ indicated that, during the eight-nucleate and mature embryo sac stages associated with pistil abortion, ovule DEGs were significantly enriched in pathways including polysaccharide metabolic process, organic substance metabolic process, carbohydrate metabolic process, cellulose metabolic process, cell wall modification, and enzyme inhibitor activity (Fig. 5B, C). This suggests that the abortion of the pistil may be regulated by genes associated with various kinases, metabolic processes, and cellular structures. To further investigate the biological pathways associated with pistil abortion, KEGG analysis was performed on the DEGs at different developmental stages of the pistil. Annotation results indicate that during the octonucleate embryo sac and mature embryo sac stages, the DEGs identified from the comparison group were significantly enriched in the KEGG pathways of protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum (ko04141), plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), and plant MAPK signaling pathway (ko04016), among others (Fig. 5E, F). This suggests that these metabolic pathways may involve key regulatory genes responsible for the abortion of the ovules in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’.

At the eight-nucleate and mature embryo sac stages in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, GO enrichment of DEGs was predominantly associated with extracellular region, oxidoreductase activity, phenylpropanoid catabolic process, and carbohydrate metabolic process (Fig. 6A, B). Further KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs specifically associated with ovule abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ showed that, at both the eight-nucleate and mature embryo sac stages, these genes were mainly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction (ko04075), brassinosteroid biosynthesis (ko00905), and photosynthesis—antenna proteins (ko00196) pathways (Fig. 6C, D). These results indicate that hormone signaling, brassinosteroid metabolism, and photosynthesis-related processes may play crucial roles in regulating female gametophyte development and ovule viability in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’.

Regulation of pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ by plant hormone-related genes

GO and KEGG analysis of the DEGs revealed that a large number of the identified DEGs are involved in the metabolism and signal transduction of plant hormones. Among the DEGs related to pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, 88 DEGs associated with plant hormone regulation were identified. In the comparative analyses of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ and L. speciosa across different developmental stages, 39 of which are involved in auxin signal transduction, including genes related to TIR1 (Transport Inhibitor Response 1, auxin receptor), AUX/IAA (Auxin/Indole-3-Acetic Acid, auxin signaling repressors), ARF (Auxin Response Factor, auxin-responsive transcription factors), SAUR (Small Auxin Up RNA, auxin-induced genes promoting elongation), and GH3 (Gretchen Hagen 3, enzymes that conjugate auxin for homeostasis). The significant downregulation of ARF family genes may affect the auxin-mediated establishment of pistil polarity (Fig. 7). In the pistils of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ at different developmental stages, a total of 14 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) associated with auxin signaling were identified, encompassing members of the AUX/IAA, ARF, SAUR, and GH3 families. At the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage, ARF11, ARF9, GH3.17, and GH3.6 were significantly downregulated, whereas AUX22D and SAUR24 were significantly upregulated. At the mature embryo sac stage, IAA4, ARF9, SAUR39, SAUR50, and GH3.17 were markedly downregulated, while IAA18, IAA26, IAA7, and GH3.6 were significantly upregulated (Fig. 7).

Six genes associated with brassinosteroid signal transduction, including XTH23 (Xyloglucan Endotransglucosylase/Hydrolase 23, cell-wall remodeling), BKI1 (BRI1 Kinase Inhibitor 1, negative regulator of BR signaling), BSK5 (BR-Signaling Kinase 5, positive BR signal transducer), BSK6 (BR-Signaling Kinase 6, positive BR signal transducer), BSK7 (BR-Signaling Kinase 7, positive BR signal transducer), and BZR2 (Brassinazole-Resistant 2, BR-responsive transcription factor), suggest that brassinosteroids may participate in the abortion process by regulating cell wall remodeling (Fig. 8B). A total of five key genes associated with cytokinin signal transduction were identified, including PHP5 (Phosphoprotein Phosphatase 5, modulates hormone signaling), AHK2 (Arabidopsis Histidine Kinase 2, cytokinin receptor), AHP1 (Arabidopsis Histidine Phosphotransfer Protein 1, cytokinin signal transducer), ARR12 (Arabidopsis Response Regulator 12, type-B cytokinin response regulator), and ARR8 (Arabidopsis Response Regulator 8, type-A cytokinin response regulator). Among these, AHK2, AHP1, and ARR12 showed significantly lower expression levels in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ compared with L. speciosa. These genes encode a cytokinin receptor, a histidine phosphotransfer protein, and a type-B response regulator, respectively, suggesting that the cytokinin signaling pathway may be attenuated in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ during ovule development (Fig. 8C). Furthermore, in the pistils of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ at various developmental stages, GID1B, which is associated with gibberellin signal transduction, was significantly downregulated during the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage but was upregulated during the mature embryo sac stage(Fig. 8A). Meanwhile, the brassinosteroid (BR) signal transduction-related gene XTH23 was also significantly upregulated during the mature embryo sac stage (Fig. 8B).

Discussion

Ovule development defects are the direct cause of pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’

Flower development is a fundamental stage in the plant life cycle, and pistil abortion is a common phenomenon during this process. The location and stage of pistil abortion vary among plant species, and the pistil abortion stage is closely related to the external morphological characteristics of the flower bud [36]. This study confirmed that the female sterility of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is caused by ovule development defects, as observed through the microscopic structure of pistil paraffin sections and the morphological structure of the stigma. The tetranucleate embryo sac stage is a critical period for pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, and this type of pistil abortion is associated with abnormal ovule development.

Female sterility is common in hybrid breeding of Lagerstroemia species. Yang et al. [37] crossed Lagerstroemia speciosa and Lagerstroemia indica ‘Ebony Embers’ and found that both male and female F1 progeny were sterile. Ovule abortion in the F1 generation was characterized by the degeneration and dissolution of megaspores at the tetrad stage, followed by the formation of a cavity with traces of the embryo sac. Ju et al. [38] studied the sterile progeny of the hybrid between Lagerstroemia indica and Lagerstroemia speciosa and found that female sterility in the progeny ZD3 occurred after pollination, with the ovary beginning to shrink 72 hours post-pollination. The findings of this study are similar to the reproductive abnormalities observed by Zhou et al. [8] in Lagerstroemia species, where ovule development was also abnormal within the ovary. Notably, the ovule development defect in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ primarily occurs during the transition from the tetranucleate to the octonucleate embryo sac stage, which is later than the megaspore tetrad stage observed in L. indica ‘Xiaoxiang Huaxiu’ [37]. Microscopic observations revealed that after the eight-nucleate stage, the embryo sac of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ begins to shrink and degrade, showing disordered fibrous tissue structures. The embryo sac lacks the complete morphological characteristics of a female gametophyte and completely loses its fertilization potential. Cross-species comparative studies have shown that the phenomenon of abnormal termination of reproductive tissue development prior to gamete formation is widespread. In pomegranate [19], when the flower bud longitudinal diameter reaches 10.1–13.0 mm, aborted flower ovules exhibit shrinkage and fail to form the integument primordium. In chestnut [39], although floral organs complete morphological development, the embryo sac undergoes irreversible structural degeneration before gamete maturation. Therefore, the female sterility phenotype of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is due to abnormal embryo sac development, with the characteristic abortion occurring prior to flowering.

Key genes regulating floral organ development and pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’

Anatomical studies indicate that the sterility of the pistils in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ occurs during the development from the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage to the mature embryo sac stage. AG (AGAMOUS) [40], SPL [41], ANT, and BEL1 [42, 43] are key regulatory factors in ovule development. This study found that these four key genes are also responsible for the ovule sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’.

AG belongs to the MADS-box gene family and plays a crucial role in ovule development, with its activity also contributing to ovule morphogenesis [44]. As an important regulatory gene in ovule development, AG homologs also play a crucial role in pistil formation, before fertilization, the AGL15 gene primarily accumulates in the cytoplasm of the egg cell [45]. It regulates the formation of the somatic embryo after fertilization by directly modulating the expression of the downstream gene AtGA20 × 6, affecting the gibberellin (GA) metabolic pathway [46]. This study found that AGL15 was significantly downregulated during the binucleate embryo sac stage in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, and its expression was lower than that in L. speciosa at all three key developmental stages. This suggests that the downregulation of AGL15 may inhibit ovule development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. The MADS-BOX transcription factor AG1, which encodes a key repressor of fruit setting, plays a crucial role [47]. In this study, AG1 (TRINITY_DN12976_c0_g1) was significantly upregulated during the binucleate embryo sac stage in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, suggesting that its abnormal expression may inhibit subsequent ovule development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. ANT is a member of the AP2/ERF transcription factor family and is involved in ovule development and ovule primordium formation [48]. ANT mutations result in the failure of integument formation in the ovule, leading to abnormal ovule development and female sterility [49]. BEL1 is a homeodomain transcription factor that regulates ovule patterning, particularly determining the identity and development of the integument. In bel1 mutants, the ovule develops a single integument-like structure [50]. SPL transcription factors play a crucial regulatory role in the differentiation and development of floral organs. An ovule consists of the proximal funiculus, which connects the ovule to the ovary wall, the central integuments, and the distal nucellus. SPL is involved in the formation of all three parts. During the ovule development stage in the pistil, AtSPL uses the C-terminal EAR motif to recruit TPL/TPRs and specifically binds to TCP via its N-terminal sequence to inhibit the expression of ANT and BEL1, thereby regulating ovule development [51]. Additionally, BEL1 and SPL/NZZ are essential key regulatory factors in the auxin and cytokinin signaling pathways [52]. Studies in tomato have found that inhibiting the expression of SlySPL13 leads to a reduction in the number of flowers and fruits, accompanied by smaller fruit size [53]. In Prunus avium, the expression levels of nine SPL genes during fruit development vary significantly at different stages, playing a crucial regulatory role during maturation [54]. In this study, SPL1 (TRINITY_DN16252_c0_g1), SPL12 (TRINITY_DN83037_c0_g2, TRINITY_DN18405_c0_g), SPL13A (TRINITY_DN24580_c0_g1), SPL7 (TRINITY_DN54935_c0_g1), and SPL16 (TRINITY_DN40478_c0_g1) showed significantly increased expression during the tetranucleate embryo sac stage in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. However, the expression levels of ANTR1 (TRINITY_DN12082_c0_g1) and BEL1 (TRINITY_DN1289_c0_g1) increased during the eight-nucleate and mature embryo sac stages, suggesting that SPL, ANTR1, and BEL1 genes may not be directly involved in regulating the sterility of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. The high expression of SPL may be involved in the abnormal regulation of auxin.

In the comparison of pistil development at different stages in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, SPL16 was significantly downregulated at both the eight-nucleate and mature embryo sac stages. Previous studies have demonstrated that the SPL gene family plays a critical role in ovule and floral organ differentiation [51], and exhibits dynamic expression patterns during different developmental stages in fruit crops such as tomato and cherry [53, 54]. Thus, the abnormal downregulation of SPL16 in ‘Zichan’ suggests that it may be a pivotal regulator underlying ovule abortion. In addition, SPL13A, AGL61, AG1, and AGL12 were also markedly downregulated at the mature embryo sac stage. Among them, AGL61 has been reported to be indispensable for embryo sac maturation in model plants [55], whereas AG1 functions as a key repressor of fruit set [47]; therefore, its downregulation in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ may reflect functional divergence under distinct species or developmental contexts. Furthermore, AGL12 is known to be involved in reproductive organ development [56], and its reduced expression provides further evidence for impaired ovule development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. Taken together, these findings indicate that ovule abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ during the transition from the eight-nucleate to the mature embryo sac stage is closely associated with the misregulation of multiple key genes, which is consistent with previous findings in model plants and fruit trees, yet al.so highlights species-specific differences.

Abnormal plant hormone signal transduction leads to ovule abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’

Auxin as one of the most important plant hormones, regulates crucial processes throughout plant development, from embryogenesis to reproduction. Auxin can rapidly and efficiently induce the expression of certain genes, known as auxin early response genes (AERGs), which mainly include auxin/indole-3-acetic acid (Aux/IAA), Gretchen Hagen 3 (GH3), and small auxin-up RNA (SAUR). The auxin transcriptional regulatory mechanism mediated by TIR1/AFB is referred to as the classical auxin signaling pathway [57]. During ovule abortion in plants, the core components of the auxin signaling pathway, such as TIR1, ARF, and AUX/IAA, exhibit complex dynamic regulation. The classical auxin signaling model suggests that increased expression of the TIR1 gene may degrade AUX/IAA proteins through the ubiquitination system, releasing their inhibition of ARF transcription factors and ultimately activating the expression of downstream genes [58]. However, this study found that ARF6 expression showed a sustained decrease in the ovule abortion material L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, which may contribute to abnormal ovule development. In Arabidopsis, a reduction in ARF6 directly disrupts ovule development [59]. It has also been demonstrated in pomegranate that the ARF6 gene is involved in pistil abortion, with the expression level of ARF6 significantly lower in the aborted pistils compared to those with normal development [60]. Therefore, the ARF6 gene may be involved in regulating the ovule abortion process in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. The upregulation of other ARF isoforms (such as ARF7/ARF9) may drive the expression of SAUR and GH3 through the classical pathway, which also explains the upregulation of SAUR and GH3 gene expression in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’.

GAs, as a key endogenous hormone regulating growth and development, are involved in various developmental processes, including seed germination, stem and root elongation, flowering time, and fruit development [61–63]. GAs play an important role in ovule formation, and their regulatory effects may vary across different species. In tomato, GAs act downstream of brassinosteroids. Brassinosteroids regulate ovule number by downregulating GA biosynthesis, which stabilizes DELLA proteins and ultimately promotes the initiation of ovule primordia. In contrast, in Arabidopsis, GAs and brassinosteroids regulate ovule number independently of other hormones [64]. GAI1, a member of the DELLA protein family, is a key negative regulator in the gibberellin signaling pathway and can inhibit the expression of gibberellin-responsive genes. GID1B, as a gibberellin receptor protein, binds with GA to trigger the ubiquitination and degradation of DELLA proteins (such as GAI1), relieving inhibition and promoting growth [65]. In this study, the expression levels of GAI1 and GID1B were significantly increased at the tetranucleate embryo sac stage of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, whereas GID1B expression was markedly decreased at the eight-nucleate stage, possibly due to a functional abnormality of GID1B or impaired signal transduction (e.g., low receptor-GA binding efficiency). As a result, DELLA proteins could not be effectively degraded, leading to sustained inhibition of gibberellin-responsive genes, which in turn impairs ovule abortion.

Cytokinins are involved in a wide range of developmental processes and environmental responses in plants, including leaf senescence, apical dominance, chloroplast development, anthocyanin production, cell division, and regulation of source–sink relationships [66]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, cytokinins participate in the development of the central region of the gynoecium and in the formation of valve margins during early fruit development [67]. Arabidopsis contains three cytokinin receptors—AHK2, AHK3, and CRE1/WOL/AHK4—that function redundantly and positively in cytokinin signaling [68, 69]. AHPs (histidine phosphotransfer proteins) act as downstream targets of AHK receptors and mediate the transfer of phosphoryl groups from activated histidine kinases to response regulators (ARRs) through a multistep phosphorelay mechanism [70, 71]. There are two types of ARRs involved in cytokinin signaling: type-A and type-B. Type-A ARRs function as negative feedback regulators of the cytokinin signaling pathway, while type-B ARRs can activate the transcription of type-A ARR genes, thereby upregulating their expression [72]. Mutations in AHK cytokinin receptors lead to abnormal development of the female gametophyte and ovule abortion [73]. This finding is consistent with our results in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, where transcript levels of PHP5, AHK2, AHP1, and ARR8 were significantly downregulated during the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage. These genes represent key components of the cytokinin signaling pathway, and their reduced expression suggests that cytokinin signal transduction may be impaired during the critical stage of embryo sac development in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, potentially leading to developmental arrest. Furthermore, ARR8 is a type-A response regulator; its downregulation supports the disruption of the cytokinin-mediated negative feedback mechanism, which may interfere with the normal progression of female gametophyte development.

In addition to the differences observed between cultivars, dynamic changes in hormone signaling pathways were also detected across different developmental stages of the pistil in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. These results suggest a complex and stage-specific regulation of auxin-responsive genes during pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of precise auxin signaling dynamics for normal ovule development, with perturbations in ARF activity leading to ovule developmental arrest in Arabidopsis [59] and pistil abortion in pomegranate [60]. The observed downregulation of ARF11 and ARF9 in the eight-nucleate embryo sac may therefore indicate impaired auxin-mediated transcriptional activation, while the late-stage upregulation of certain AUX/IAA members could further disrupt the fine-tuned balance of auxin signaling, thereby contributing to embryo sac abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. In addition to auxin, gibberellin (GA) signaling was also found to undergo developmental stage-specific alterations. GID1B, the GA receptor, was significantly downregulated at the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage but upregulated at the mature embryo sac stage. Given that GA signaling requires the GA–GID1–DELLA module for activation [65], the early reduction of GID1B expression may block GA perception and delay embryo sac development, while its subsequent upregulation might represent a compensatory response that, however, fails to rescue the abortion phenotype. Similar dynamic GA signaling fluctuations have been reported in other species, where impaired GA perception or DELLA degradation resulted in ovule abortion [64]. Interestingly, the brassinosteroid-related gene XTH23 was significantly upregulated at the mature embryo sac stage. XTH genes are known to mediate cell wall remodeling and have been implicated in reproductive development in several plant species [74, 75]. The upregulation of XTH23 may indicate an attempt to promote cell wall loosening and tissue remodeling at the late stage; however, in the abortive pistil of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’, this process appears insufficient to sustain normal embryo sac development.

Together, these findings demonstrate that pistil abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ is associated not only with inter-cultivar differences in hormone signaling, but also with dynamic and stage-specific disruptions in auxin, GA, and brassinosteroid pathways during embryo sac development.

Conclusion

This study systematically revealed the mechanism of female sterility in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ through morphological anatomy and molecular regulatory network analysis. Paraffin section observations indicated that the key period for ovule sterility is the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage, characterized by embryo sac degradation and atrophic shrinkage, along with the degeneration of the female gametophyte. Compared to other varieties of Lagerstroemia (e.g., L. indica ‘Xiaozhuang Huaxiu’), where abortion occurs at the megaspore tetrad stage, this process is delayed in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. However, it shares a conserved feature with species such as pomegranate and chestnut, where degeneration occurs before embryo sac maturation. The significant downregulation of cytokinin signaling components (PHP5, AHK2, AHP1, and ARR8) at the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage suggests that impaired cytokinin signaling may contribute to defects in embryo sac development. The AGL15 gene is significantly downregulated during the four-nucleate embryo sac stage, potentially hindering endosperm development by inhibiting the gibberellin metabolic pathway. Meanwhile, the abnormal overexpression of the AG1 gene forms a reproductive inhibition signal, which together leads to the stagnation of embryo sac development. In the auxin signaling pathway, the abnormal accumulation of AUX/IAA proteins inhibits ARF6 activity. Although ARF7/ARF9 compensatorily upregulate and activate SAUR/GH3 genes, they are still unable to reverse the abortion process. Gibberellin signaling is disrupted due to the functional abnormality of the GID1B receptor, resulting in sustained accumulation of the DELLA protein GAI1. This interferes with embryo sac maturation by suppressing the expression of gibberellin-responsive genes. The abnormally high expression of SPL family genes during the tetranucleate embryo sac stage may indirectly affect development by regulating auxin distribution, although their inhibitory effect on ANT and BEL1 is not fully manifested. This study is the first to identify the eight-nucleate embryo sac stage as the critical period of ovule abortion in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’. The ovule abortion observed in L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ results from a combination of disrupted hormone signaling pathways and misregulation of floral organ development genes. The coordinated imbalance of auxin, gibberellin, and cytokinin signaling may interfere with embryo sac maturation, ultimately leading to female sterility. These findings provide new insights into the mechanisms of sterility in woody ornamental plants and lay a theoretical foundation for improving the reproductive capacity of L. speciosa ‘Zichan’ through gene editing or hormonal regulation. Future studies are needed to functionally validate key regulatory genes and to establish a quantitative model describing the crosstalk among gibberellin, auxin, and cytokinin pathways.
