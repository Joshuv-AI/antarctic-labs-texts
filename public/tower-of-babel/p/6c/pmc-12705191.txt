Is Canadian trauma care still salvageable?
Authors: Chad G Ball, Edward J Harvey
Published: 2025

Trauma care in Canada has reached a tipping point. Across the country, long-standing problems have moved from a simmer to a boil. Whether discussing trainee education, maintenance of clinician skills, disparities in access, accreditation standards, quality improvement, trauma outcomes research, post-injury care (addictions, mental health, rehabilitation), patient recidivism, or surgeon wellness, the conclusion is clear: our national trauma system is in crisis.

In preparing this editorial, we sought perspectives from 15 respected Canadian trauma surgeons, leaders in both practice and thought, representing every major geographic region, rural and urban. Their concerns were remarkably consistent, acute, and deeply troubling. Confronting these concerns is uncomfortable — even discouraging — but honesty is overdue. Canada’s trauma system is deteriorating.

Talent challenges

A broad spectrum of trauma clinicians deliver the majority of injury care in Canada, yet most face substantial barriers in acquiring and maintaining the skills required for managing high-volume, high-acuity trauma. Insufficient exposure to major trauma during general surgery training and fellowship remains a profound problem.1,2 When Canada’s busiest Level 1 trauma centre is compared with its American counterparts, the contrast is stark: approximately 1000 major trauma cases per year compared with 11 500, an 11-fold difference. This gap extends across the entire volume–outcome curve, affecting both trainees and seasoned surgeons. The notion that a service can have “too much volume” is outdated. Although courses such as Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS), Definitive Surgical Trauma Care (DSTC), Advanced Surgical Skills for Exposure in Trauma (ASSET), and Advanced Trauma Operative Management (ATOM) offer significant value, they cannot substitute for ongoing, real-world experience with severely injured patients requiring emergent, complex operative care.

Outside major trauma centres, it is increasingly common to encounter general surgeons who feel uncomfortable managing injured patients and who request urgent bypass or transfer, even for unstable cases. This discomfort is not a moral failing; it reflects a system unable to provide the volume and training needed to maintain competence. Compounding the issue is Canada’s failure to recruit back some of its most promising surgeon–scientists after they complete high-volume training in the United States. Many contemplate coming home to practise only to find there are no suitable trauma positions, no protected academic environment, and no pathway to fully utilize their skill set. These “lost stars” represent a painful, preventable exodus.

System challenges

Timely access to high-quality trauma care in a geographically vast country is inherently difficult.3 Despite decades of acknowledgment, this challenge has not improved.4–6 Chronic underfunding, fragmented coordination, increasingly restrictive administrative structures, and leadership unable to drive sustained incremental change have left most provincial trauma systems stagnant. When compared with the evolution of many high-volume trauma programs in the US, ours often appear rudimentary and stunted. Contrary to common Canadian assumptions, many US trauma systems offer robust “wrap-around” services: comprehensive rehabilitation pathways for patients with spinal, orthopedic, and brain injuries; intensive social work support to reduce recidivism; and targeted community prevention programs. While such systems require adequate funding, they also require empowered leadership and coherent strategy.

Accreditation has become another casualty. When trauma centre designation transitioned from the Trauma Association of Canada to Accreditation Canada, optimism was high. However, Accreditation Canada’s decision to withdraw from trauma centre accreditation has plunged the system into disarray. Without a stable, credible accreditation process, the foundation for continuous quality improvement is eroding. As Canada moves toward potential accreditation through the American College of Surgeons, we must carefully consider the experiences, both positive and challenging, of other countries that have pursued this route. We should also ask whether abandoning a national, context-specific accreditation model is consistent with the renewed emphasis on “Canadian-made” health care solutions.

Public perception challenges

The public perception of trauma care in Canada is starkly misaligned with our reality. Although orthopedic trauma faces its own obstacles, general surgery–based trauma care is particularly strained. Ask a member of the public what would happen if they, or a loved one, suffered a severe injury anywhere in Canada; the answer is nearly universal: they expect excellent, standardized care, regardless of geography, hospital capacity, surgeon volume, or system organization. They would believe that this care is homogeneous in every relevant domain. We know this is not the case. And we have not been honest about it.

It is a myth that any Canadian general surgeon who has obtained Royal College certification and works in a hospital with on-call services can reliably deliver excellent trauma care in all circumstances. This assumption has persisted for too long and has impeded necessary change.

A path forward

As a surgical community, we must advocate, loudly and consistently, for a trauma system capable of providing timely, high-quality injury care to all Canadians, regardless of postal code. We must also support our trauma surgical community with access to high-volume training environments, fair compensation, program autonomy, and genuine attention to physician wellness.7 Canada’s trauma system is salvageable, but only if we acknowledge the crisis and commit to rebuilding it with honesty, urgency, and ambition.
