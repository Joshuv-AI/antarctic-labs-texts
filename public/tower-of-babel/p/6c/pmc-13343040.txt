ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe Guangxi Eco-Environmental Health and Aging Study (GEHAS) was established to investigate the interplay between eco-environmental exposome, multi-omics profiles, and healthy ageing in a multi-ethnic subtropical population. This prospective cohort was designed to address knowledge gaps regarding how eco-environmental and lifestyle factors influence ageing trajectories.ParticipantsGEHAS enrolled 5645 participants (59.8% women; mean age 62.51 years) from Guilin, Guangxi, at baseline, with Yao and Han participants accounting for 60.3% and 36.2% of the cohort, respectively. Baseline data (2018–2022) included questionnaires, physical examinations, biological specimens (blood, urine, hair and nails). Two subsequently established longevity and circadian rhythm sub-cohorts (n=276 and n=596) underwent multi-omics profiling (genomics, epigenomics, proteomics, metabolomics and exposomics). Follow-up in this open cohort includes active surveys approximately every 5 years and annual passive surveillance through government health registries.Findings to dateRecent analyses highlight that improvements in air quality can mitigate frailty progression in older adults, with DNA methylation emerging as a promising biomarker that captures the biological imprint of environmental policy interventions and ageing dynamics. In parallel, local specialty foods and traditional dietary practices have been implicated as potential contributors to reducing the risk of ageing-related diseases, as demonstrated by multiple studies using baseline data. For instance, oil tea consumption has shown potential protective effects against dyslipidaemia and type 2 diabetes, while region-specific dietary patterns were associated with favourable health outcomes in middle-aged and older adults. Specifically, a dose-dependent association was observed between diets rich in vegetables and mushrooms and enhanced cognitive performance, and antioxidant-rich dietary patterns were found to attenuate biological ageing through modulation of redox homeostasis. Moreover, GEHAS studies also explored the interactions between genetic factors and heavy metal exposures in age-related diseases, underscoring the complex interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental influences in the ageing process.Future plansGEHAS will continue active follow-up surveys every 5 years, supplemented by annual passive surveillance for morbidity and mortality through health registries. The first active follow-up was initiated in 2023 and will continue until 2026/2027, with subsequent rounds planned thereafter. In addition, targeted sub-cohorts, including longevity and circadian rhythm groups, will undergo repeated assessments to capture long-term changes in health, exposures and biological ageing.

Introduction

Global population ageing is accelerating and has become an emerging health issue. By 2024, the older adults aged ≥65 years accounted for about 10% of the world’s total population, which is expected to rise to 16% by 2050.1 Population ageing is progressing even more rapidly in China. By the end of 2020, China had 264 million people aged 60 years and above, accounting for 18.7% of the total population, highlighting the growing social and family burden.2 Healthy ageing, as proposed by the WHO, emphasises the creation and maintenance of environments that support functional ability and well-being in older age.3 Exposome, the totality of environmental exposures in broad physical, chemical and psychosocial domains, is therefore an important priority for promoting healthspan in older adults.4 Rapid climate change further highlights the need to study the impact of ecological and environmental exposome on healthy ageing.5 6 Moreover, the interaction between genetic predispositions and environmental exposures has been emerging as a pivotal area of research in understanding the status of age-related diseases within populations.711 The impact of exposome further extends across multiple dimensions of age-related diseases, with evidence suggesting strong correlations between environmental exposures and disease development, along with the interactions with the multi-omics data.12 The principal aim of this field is to establish longitudinal population cohorts that capture healthy ageing, longevity and climate-related eco-environmental changes, thereby elucidating the health effects of ecological and social exposures and their underlying biological mechanisms.

Guangxi, a southern province of China, is well-suited to serve as a region for studying the eco-environmental exposure and healthy ageing. Guangxi’s environmental and ageing health challenges are uniquely pronounced. This area, because of its stable, multigenerational residency and low population mobility, has clustered longevity patterns and centenarian populations, offering a rich source of study subjects for research.13 14 Han, Yao and Zhuang populations coexist in this region, which provides an opportunity to search for gene-environment interactions in relation to ethnic, lifestyle, social exposome and genetic variation. Furthermore, environmental health is moving toward integrated assessments of complex exposure profiles, an area in which Guangxi is exceptionally well-suited to serve as a ‘living laboratory’. Its extensive karst landscapes and diverse population structure further enhance the relevance and interpretability of findings related to environmental exposures and healthy ageing. Beyond this, Guangxi’s environmental signature dovetails precisely with the global emerging priorities in air quality management and climate adaptation. Currently, ambient pollution levels in Guangxi remain comparatively low, aligning with global strategies that increasingly target low-concentration pollutants and mixed‐exposure scenarios. Simultaneously, the inexorable rise in global temperatures underscores the urgent need to understand subtropical climate dynamics.15 Focusing first on the ageing population in Guangxi thus holds dual importance, as it offers a natural model for studying the impact of climate change and other emerging environmental pollution on ageing in similar subtropical regions internationally.

Therefore, we established the Guangxi Eco-Environmental Health and Aging Study (GEHAS) in Guilin, Guangxi, to (1) assess the prevalence and projections of age-related diseases and subclinical disease phenotypes in the regional middle-aged and older population to understand the evolving epidemiological landscape; (2) identify high-risk subgroups and key eco-environmental and climate determinants of disease onset and progression; and (3) investigate the complex interactions of multi-omics, including genomics, epigenomics, proteomics, metabolomics and exposomics to establish risk prediction models for high-risk populations, thereby informing the design of targeted prevention strategies and the optimal allocation of healthcare resources to promote healthy ageing.

Cohort description

Participants and recruitment

This cohort was established in the Guilin region of Guangxi, taking into account population size and stability, local disease patterns, environmental exposure profiles and the feasibility of long-term follow-up among rural middle-aged and older residents. We have currently completed data collection in nine towns, which were intended to capture heterogeneity in local ecological environments. Sitang, Liangjiang, Lingui, Ping’an and Gongcheng were selected to represent relatively urbanised communities, whereas Xiling, Wutong, Limu and Lianhua were selected to represent predominantly rural communities, as shown in figure 1. The inclusion criteria for study participants were as follows: (1) aged 45 years or older; (2) permanent residents of the local area; (3) can understand the study procedures and communicate effectively with interviewers; and (4) voluntarily agreed to participate in the study and provided written informed consent. The cohort is an open cohort with a dynamic design, allowing newly eligible residents to be recruited during follow-up surveys. Newly enrolled participants contribute follow-up time from their own enrolment date. The baseline survey was conducted between December 2018 and December 2022, and the active follow-up is conducted approximately every five years from each participant’s individual baseline enrolment date. To maximise follow-up success, individual follow-up visits were scheduled flexibly within 4–6 years after baseline enrolment. Newly eligible residents will continue to be recruited during active follow-up waves to maintain the dynamic nature of the cohort.

Distribution of participants. We have completed data collection in nine towns across the Guilin region of Guangxi, China. Sitang, Wutong, Liangjiang, Lingui, Ping’an, Gongcheng, Xiling, Limu and Lianhua. Sitang, Wutong, Liangjiang and Lingui are collectively referred to as the Lingui region.

We recruited study participants through a two-stage approach. Stage 1: eligible candidates were mobilised to attend health screenings at village clinics through multimodal outreach strategies, including leaflet distribution, message alerts, public broadcast announcements and community-based interpersonal communication. Stage 2: questionnaire interviews and physical examinations were conducted at township health centres or village clinics, while participants with restricted mobility completed questionnaires and health assessments at home. Participants were required to fast for 8–12 hours and were registered in the GEHAS system using their national identity cards to avoid duplicate participation. Additionally, information was collected from local residents under 45 years of age who lived in the households of the participants, though these residents have not yet been formally included in the GEHAS cohort, to facilitate future longitudinal follow-up once they meet the age-specific inclusion criteria and to support potential inclusion in ancillary studies with broader eligibility criteria. The recruitment aim was to cover selected stable rural communities and enrol consenting eligible residents in the selected study sites. Before data collection, recruitment goals were established based on population size, ethnic composition and feasibility of long-term follow-up in the selected regions. Oversampling was considered for the longevity sub-cohort as these individuals are more likely to die in the next 2–3 years.

Follow-up and sub-cohorts

Between December 2018 and December 2022, we established the cohort and completed baseline investigations. Active follow-up surveys are planned approximately every 5 years from the baseline enrolment date of each participant, while passive follow-up is conducted annually from the initial recruitment date of the participant, as shown in figure 2. Passive follow-up is defined as the collection of routinely recorded public health data, including morbidity and mortality statistics, from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the National Death Surveillance System, Chronic Disease Registries and the National Central Cancer Registry. Active follow-ups are conducted through a combination of centralised health examinations and home-based follow-up visits and incorporate additional survey items beyond the baseline questionnaire. Two sub-cohorts were established to investigate longevity-specific multi-omics profiles and circadian rhythm, as shown in figure 3. Longitudinal analyses of indicator trajectories during follow-up will be conducted to identify potential factors associated with the ageing process.

The first round of active follow-up for the earliest enrolled cohort participants was initiated in 2023. As of the current assessment, follow-up data collection has been completed for 2247 participants, with 196 deaths and 422 additional participants newly enrolled. The first-round active follow-up remains ongoing and is expected to be completed in 2026/2027, with the second-round active follow-up scheduled to commence in 2028/2029.

Sub-cohort I: longevity sub-cohort

In addition to routine investigations and follow-ups, a multi-omics investigation targeting GEHAS participants aged 90 years or older who consented to additional biospecimen collection was initiated in 2024. Blood samples from nonagenarians and centenarians were additionally analysed for genomic, epigenomic, proteomic, metabolomic and exposomic profiles. Currently, 276 nonagenarians and centenarians from the Lingui region have been included, and recruitment will continue during subsequent follow-up rounds in other regions.

Sub-cohort II: circadian rhythm sub-cohort

Because circadian rhythms help humans adapt to external environments and are associated with multiple aging-related health outcomes, we hypothesised that circadian rhythm may be a key pathway linking eco-environmental exposures to aging-related health outcomes. Therefore, since 2023, we have recruited 596 GEHAS participants with high adherence who were willing to undergo repeated sleep monitoring and indoor air quality assessments. Assessments combining objective measurements and subjective reports were conducted for participants of sub-cohort II. Participants wore portable smart rings to monitor daily activities and sleep to characterise sleep architecture and physiological parameters under free-living conditions, while synchronous indoor air quality monitoring captured real-time environmental exposures. Participants additionally completed standardised sleep and air pollution questionnaires, enabling integrated analysis of both measured and perceived sleep influences. This sub-cohort will be followed every 3 years to further understand the role of circadian rhythms in ageing.

Measurements and data collection

Online supplemental table 1 summarises the measurements collected in the GEHAS. Participants completed a face-to-face electronic questionnaire and underwent a physical examination conducted by trained medical staff. Fasting blood, urine, hair and nail samples were collected from participants, with blood and urine aliquoted after centrifugation and stored at −80°C until subsequent analysis. Participants generally required 120–180 min to complete all study procedures.

Electronic questionnaire survey

The main content of our questionnaire referred to the study of Healthy Aging and Biomarkers Cohort Study as the prototype (online supplemental material S2; baseline questionnaire). Major modifications were made in sections regarding physical activity, habitual diet and psychological conditions to fully capture the unique features of the different ethnic minority groups. An air quality questionnaire was fully completed by participants who were included in sub-cohort II. The information was collected via tablet computers or mobile phones by a face-to-face interview implemented by well-trained interviewers who were typically local college students with medical backgrounds. For a skilled interviewer, an average of 90–120 min was often required to complete a questionnaire. The questionnaires may be revised as appropriate during follow-up surveys.

Physical examination

Physical examinations were conducted by professional physicians and well-trained students. Blood pressure, heart rate, height, weight, waist circumference, hip circumference and calf circumference were measured in triplicate (online supplemental material S3; health examination form). Blood pressure assessments were conducted using two different brands of devices. Final values were determined by averaging three repeated measurements, with explicit outliers systematically excluded prior to recalculation. In cases of significant inter-measurement discrepancies, aberrant values were removed before deriving the final mean values. Walking speed, lung function and grip strength were measured twice, with means calculated post-outlier exclusion. Ultrasound bone density measurement, abdominal ultrasonography, ECG and chest X-ray were interpreted by professional clinical physicians. All diagnostic instruments underwent periodic calibration following manufacturer-recommended protocols.

Laboratory tests

Routine blood tests and blood biochemical analyses were conducted at Gongcheng County People’s Hospital, Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University and Second Affiliated Hospital of Guilin Medical University. Metal concentrations in blood and urine were quantified using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS); mitochondrial DNA copy number, ribosomal DNA copy number and telomere length were measured through quantitative PCR. Genotyping of specific single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) was conducted through MassARRAY analysis (Agena Bioscience, San Diego, CA, USA). All procedures, including DNA extraction, were performed by well-trained laboratory technicians following standardised protocols. For sub-cohorts, genomic data were obtained using Infinium Asian Screening Array (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA); genome-wide DNA methylation profiles were measured using the Infinium MethylationEPIC V2.0 BeadChip (Illumina); proteomic and metabolomic profiling data were measured by using mass spectrometry-based techniques.

Eco-environmental exposure measurement

Local soil samples, drinking water of the residents and oil tea samples were collected, alongside regional ambient air monitoring data. Serum samples were collected for exposome analyses, including assessment of 310 chemicals (online supplemental data 1), using mass spectrometry-based methods. Additionally, indoor air pollution exposure metrics were specifically obtained from participants in the sub-cohort II using real-time indoor air quality monitors to capture microenvironmental determinants of health.

Planned statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of GEHAS participants will be summarised using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables will be presented as means with SD, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Associations between environmental exposures, lifestyle factors, multi-omics indicators and healthy ageing outcomes will be evaluated using regression models, mixed-effects models and Cox proportional hazards models where appropriate.

Exposure-response relationships will be assessed using restricted cubic splines. Environmental mixtures will be analysed using Bayesian kernel machine regression and weighted quantile sum regression. Multi-omics analyses will include quality control, batch-effect assessment, false-discovery-rate correction and integrative analyses using phenome-exposome wide association studies, gene-environment and methylation-environment interaction analyses and multi-omics network integration to investigate omics-phenotype associations. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation, and attrition-related selection bias will be assessed using inverse-probability weighting.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Ethics approval and informed consent

GEHAS was approved by the Guilin Medical University Ethics Committee (20180702–3, GYLL2022060) and the Peking University Biomedical Ethics Committee (IRB00001052-24195). All participants provided written informed consent.

Findings to date

Characteristics of participants in the GEHAS

Online supplemental table 2 shows that the study enrolled a total of 5645 participants, of whom 59.8% were females and 40.2% were males, with a mean age of 62.51 years. The cohort was predominantly composed of Yao individuals (60.3%), followed by Han (36.2%). According to official demographic statistics from Gongcheng Yao Autonomous County (https://gxsj.tjj.gxzf.gov.cn:18090/pub/index.htm), among residents aged 45 years and older, men and women accounted for 50% and 50% of the population, respectively, while Yao and Han ethnic groups accounted for 60.5% and 36.6%, respectively. These findings suggest that the ethnic composition of GEHAS was generally consistent with that of the local population, although women were relatively over-represented, possibly reflecting voluntary participation and male labour migration. Significant sex differences were observed in a range of clinical and biochemical indicators, including waist circumference, lipid profiles, liver and kidney function markers and haemoglobin levels. Women exhibited substantially lower bone mineral density compared with men, indicating a higher risk of osteoporosis among women. Men showed significantly higher levels of serum creatinine, uric acid and haemoglobin, while women had higher total cholesterol and high-density lipoprotein levels. Lifestyle behaviours also differed by sex, with markedly higher rates of smoking and alcohol consumption among men. Educational attainment varied substantially, with 13.1% of men having received senior school or above education, compared with 5.2% of women. Consistent with this finding, men also achieved higher scores on the Mini-Mental State Examination, suggesting better cognitive performance on average.

Key findings

Early analyses have yielded several findings related to eco-environment and ageing. Air pollution is a major contributor to global mortality and morbidity. Based on the genome-wide methylation data of participants in the sub-cohort II, GEHAS revealed that improvements in air quality mitigated frailty progression in older adults, with DNA methylation emerging as a potential biomarker capturing the biological imprint of environmental policy interventions and ageing dynamics.16 Local specialty foods and traditional dietary practices may serve as potential contributors to the attenuation of ageing-related diseases, which has been confirmed by multiple studies based on part of the baseline data.17 18 Studies on oil tea consumption suggest potential protective effects against dyslipidaemia and type 2 diabetes.19 20 GEHAS also conducted several studies into the associations between region-specific dietary patterns and health outcomes in middle-aged and older adults. One study revealed a dose-dependent correlation between diets abundant in vegetables and mushrooms and enhanced cognitive performance,21 and another found that antioxidant-rich dietary patterns may attenuate biological ageing through redox homeostasis modulation.22

We also explored the interplay of genetic factors with heavy metals in age-related diseases using baseline samples.2325 The SNP data were measured by MassARRAY genotyping and heavy metal elements in blood and urine were measured via ICP-MS. For instance, we found that the rs752760 T allele was associated with a significantly reduced risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus with moderate/low plasma zinc levels, with ORs of 0.340 (95% CI 0.161 to 0.715) and 0.583 (95% CI 0.346 to 0.981), respectively, and the rs752760 T allele was also associated with a significantly decreased risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus with moderate/low urinary zinc levels, with ORs of 0.358 (95% CI 0.201 to 0.635) and 0.321 (95% CI 0.183 to 0.562), respectively. CYP19A1 rs752760 T allele and moderate/low plasma/urinary zinc levels reduce the risk of type 2 diabetes mellitus.26 These studies showed that cellular senescence, oxidative stress and metabolic dysregulation may represent potential mechanisms underlying metal-induced metabolic dysfunction.

Strengths and weaknesses

GEHAS has several strengths. First, the GEHAS covers a large sample of rural middle-aged and older adults from selected communities in Guilin and benefits from the stable demographic structure and relatively low population mobility of these communities, which improves the feasibility of long-term follow-up. Second, the ethnic diversity of this region provides a unique opportunity to explore ageing mechanisms influenced by minority-specific genetic predispositions and cultural practices. Additionally, the coexistence of agricultural and mining zones in this area offers a natural epidemiological setting to examine the impact of environmental exposure to various metallic elements on the development and progression of ageing phenotypes, including age-related diseases. Third, a distinctive strength of GEHAS lies in its comprehensive measurement system, which integrates standardised assessments of demographics, lifestyle factors and clinical histories with culturally sensitive adaptations. These include ethnographically-informed tools to capture minority dietary practices such as the consumption of oil tea and the environmental exposure evaluations, including household pesticide inventories and air quality perception scales. Finally, we established two sub-cohorts to further explore eco-environment, longevity and circadian rhythms with multi-omics techniques. This will help researchers understand whether the specific eco-environment and living habitats of our participants promote healthy ageing and uncover the underlying biological mechanisms.

However, the GEHAS cohort also has several limitations. First, gender-based selection bias may be present due to the differential patterns of male labour migration, which may lead to the under-representation of men in the study population and potentially affect the generalisability of certain findings. As the number of all eligible residents who received the notification but did not participate was not systematically recorded, a formal response rate could not be calculated and individual-level comparisons between participants and non-participants were unavailable. Potential selection bias related to voluntary participation and labour migration should therefore be considered when interpreting the findings. Second, the comprehensive nature of the questionnaires, while valuable for data depth, necessitates extended completion time and may introduce recall bias, particularly among older participants who may have difficulty accurately recalling past events or exposures. Third, discrepancies between self-reported history of chronic diseases and those recorded in local health information systems were observed, which may result in misclassification or underestimation of disease prevalence, thereby affecting the accuracy of related epidemiological analyses. We are now updating and correcting the health records in cooperation with local CDC to increase the accuracy of our morbidity data.
