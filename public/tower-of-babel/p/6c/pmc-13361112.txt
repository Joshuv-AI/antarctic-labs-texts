Cost-effectiveness of (sub)acute rehabilitation services for spinal cord injury: a retrospective cohort analysis
Authors: Stefan Metzger, Boris Polanco, Inge Eriks-Hoogland, Armin Gemperli, Ana Oña, Diana Pacheco Barzallo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rise in disability and healthcare spending has intensified the need for cost-effective rehabilitation. Spinal cord injury/disease (SCI/D), though relatively rare, is associated with high costs and complex care needs, making it an ideal condition to study efficient resource allocation in rehabilitation.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 375 patients with SCI/D from a Swiss SCI centre. Outcome of rehabilitation was measured using the Spinal Cord Independence Measure III (SCIM III). Latent class analysis identified three patient profiles. Cost-effectiveness was assessed by relating total costs to functional improvement, with logistic regression identifying predictors of efficient rehabilitation.ResultsThree distinct patient profiles showed significant differences in costs, service utilisation, and functional gains. Average efficiency—defined as achieving above-average functional improvement at below-average cost— was estimated at ΔSCIM III of 57, with a total cost of 118,000 Swiss francs. Efficient rehabilitation was most likely among patients. in profiles 1 and 2, younger with fewer comorbidities and with severe injuries.ConclusionsPatient segmentation enables targeted, equitable resource allocation in rehabilitation. Efficiency varies by patient profile, and even modest gains may be meaningful for severely impaired patients. A stratified cost-effectiveness evaluation is vital for policy and practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00767-4.

Introduction

Global healthcare spending continues to rise. In 2022, the average health expenditure across Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) countries exceeded 9% of gross domestic product [1]. At the same time, the need for rehabilitation now affects over 2.4 billion people worldwide—a 63% increase since 1990—driven largely by the growing prevalence of disability [2]. Ensuring that rehabilitation remains cost-effective is critical for improving patient outcomes and optimising the use of resources. Health economics offers established frameworks, such as cost-effectiveness analysis, to guide decision-making [3–6]. However, unlike acute hospital care, cost-effectiveness in rehabilitation is poorly defined and applied inconsistently, which makes it difficult to measure, compare, and benchmark services [7]. This is a challenge that has also been noted by Sakai et al., who highlight that the cost-effectiveness of rehabilitation interventions is unclear in current research [8].

Spinal cord injury/disease (SCI/D) is one of the most complex forms of disability, characterised by severe functional limitations, substantial healthcare utilisation and extensive long-term care needs [9]. Although relatively rare, the incidence of SCI/D is expected to increase as population age due to the growing number of fall-related injuries among older adults [10]. The combination of high needs and high costs makes SCI/D an index case, as it provides an upper-bound estimate of the clinical and economic challenges associated with disability and rehabilitation. In fact, SCI/D is associated with some of the highest long-term healthcare costs and most complex care needs among neurological conditions [9]. For example, recent systematic reviews estimate that the direct lifetime healthcare costs for an individual with SCI/D can exceed 1 million Swiss francs (CHF), with subacute and acute rehabilitation costs alone ranging from 15,000 to 800,000 CHF depending on injury severity and country [11, 12].

SCI/D provides a particularly suitable context for analysing the clinical and economic dimensions of rehabilitation. In this paper, we focus on the (sub)acute phase as it happens in a controlled-like environment where all admitted SCI/D patients face a similar environment but unique challenges in terms of functional recovery, resource allocation, and long-term outcomes. This setting allows for systematic patient tracking and comparison by establishing standardised measures of rehabilitation to quantify its effectiveness for evaluating interventions and justifying their costs.

The (sub)acute rehabilitation phase refers to the period immediately following the initial injury. It extends from the early post-incident stage to hospital discharge [13]. During this phase, patients typically receive intensive, interprofessional rehabilitation aimed at maximising functional recovery and independence. In Switzerland, individuals with SCI/D are treated in specialised centres that operate under standardised guidelines and conduct regular assessments during first rehabilitation [14, 15]. This controlled environment enables detailed study of rehabilitation processes, costs, and outcomes.

Although numerous studies have evaluated various clinical aspects of rehabilitation for SCI/D [16], few have examined the relationship between functional outcomes and economic implications [17]. This limits our ability to assess whether rehabilitation delivers value relative to its costs. To address this issue, we focus on patient independence—measured using the Spinal Cord Independence Measure III (SCIM III)—as a clinically meaningful outcome [18]. The SCIM III is particularly well suited to this analysis, as it is a validated, reliable, and widely used instrument designed specifically to assess functional changes in individuals with SCI/D across key domains such as self-care, respiration, sphincter management, and mobility.

In the current study, we examined the relationship between improvements in independence and the costs of inpatient rehabilitation in a specialised SCI centre. We quantified the cost per unit of functional gain, explored efficiency across patient profiles, and implemented a multistep approach combining patient profiling, cost-effectiveness analysis, and efficiency estimation. This approach provides evidence to optimise resource allocation and inform policy decisions.

Methods

Data

In this retrospective observational cohort study, we used routinely collected clinical and administrative data obtained during the (sub)acute phase of rehabilitation of individuals with SCI/D at the Swiss Paraplegic Centre. This centre is the largest of four specialised SCI clinics in the country and admits approximately 1000 patients with SCI/D annually, of whom about 150 undergo (sub)acute rehabilitation [19]. The (sub)acute rehabilitation phase begins immediately after the lesion occurs, continues through the early post-incident period, and lasts until hospital discharge, often including time in an intensive care unit [13].

Data were collected from individuals admitted during their (sub)acute rehabilitation phase and subsequently discharged between January 2017 and December 2020. We excluded individuals without SCI/D and those who did not provide general consent. The dataset comprises administrative and medical information derived from routine data collection, including clinic-specific medical statistics compiled for the Swiss Federal Statistical Office, standard cost data related to Swiss diagnosis-related groups (DRGs) [20, 21], and information extracted from electronic medical records, the administrative planning system, and the clinic’s business intelligence tool.

The clinic uses the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) to collect information relevant to rehabilitation and follows a health service standard for outcome measures, the Nottwil Standard [14]. Rehabilitation is provided by a structured interprofessional team led by a rehabilitation physician and comprising physiotherapists, occupational therapists, rehabilitation nurses, psychologists, social workers, speech therapists, and dietitians.

Our reporting follows the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Study variables

The study variables were divided into three groups describing patient characteristics and functioning, the services provided, and cost information.

Patient characteristics

Demographic and injury characteristics

Demographic and injury characteristics used included age on admission, sex, aetiology of SCI/D, injury groups, insurance, comorbidities, and length of stay (LOS). Age was obtained from clinic-specific medical statistics compiled for the Swiss Federal Statistical Office. The age range was from 9 to 85 years. Sex, categorised as man or woman, is also sourced from clinic-specific medical statistics. Aetiology data distinguishing between traumatic and nontraumatic spinal cord injuries were retrieved from the clinical medical records.

Neurological impairment was classified using the American Spinal Injury Association Impairment Scale (AIS), which groups motor and sensory function ranging into grade A (complete loss) to grade E (normal function) [22]. By using the International Standards for Neurological Classification of Spinal Cord Injury (ISNCSCI) definition and AIS classification, we categorised SCI/D into four injury groups [23]: high-level tetraplegia (injury level: C1–C4, AIS grade A–C), low-level tetraplegia (C5–C8 AIS A–C), paraplegia (TH1–S5 AIS A–C), and AIS D regardless of injury level [24].

Comorbidities were identified using the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes for clinically relevant conditions in SCI/D, as reported in the Federal Statistical Office data. The total number of comorbidities per individual was then calculated. LOS was calculated as the number of days between admission and discharge. Insurance status was categorised as having accident insurance, mandatory health insurance, or others (including private payers and other insurances).

Rehabilitation outcomes: SCIM III

In (sub)acute rehabilitation for individuals with SCI/D, functional status is assessed using the SCIM III, which evaluates the impact of disability on the patient’s overall health by assessing independence in functional activities across three subdomains [25, 26]: self-care (score range: 0–20), respiration and sphincter management (0–40), and mobility (0–40). See Table 1A in the appendix for more details about SCIM III subdomains. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, with lower scores indicating higher levels of disability [27]. The SCIM III is a clinically useful measure of functional change during inpatient rehabilitation, and has been shown to be valid, reliable and efficient [18, 28]. SCIM III is a validated, SCI-specific outcome measure with well-established reliability and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha > 0.8) and was therefore applied without recalculating psychometric properties, in line with established clinical standards and national outcome reporting [14, 29, 30]. To further characterise the measurement properties and data distribution of the SCIM III, item-level descriptive statistics for all 19 SCIM III items at admission are provided in Table 2A in the appendix. Table 2A shows that respiration had the highest mean score (M = 9.02) and was the only item with negative skewness (− 1.53), whereas mobility and mobility-handling items displayed low mean scores and high positive skewness, indicating substantial dependence at admission; self-care items showed greater variability, with mean scores ranging from 1.29 to 2.73.

We assessed patient improvement as change in independence—quantified as the difference in SCIM III scores (ΔSCIM III) between discharge and admission. As no universally accepted minimal clinically important difference (MCID) for the SCIM III across injury severities and functional subdomains is currently available, changes in SCIM III were interpreted descriptively and in relation to baseline functional status rather than against a fixed clinical threshold [31].

Utilisation of services

The total utilisation of services includes information on the quantity of rehabilitation services provided to patients during (sub)acute rehabilitation. Services include diagnostic services such as imaging and laboratory; therapies such as occupational therapy, physiotherapy, psychology, speech therapy, and other therapies (social counselling, nutritional, and vocational therapy); and nursing. Service allocation is determined by health professionals based on individual patient needs and rehabilitation goals following standardised decision-making protocols [14]. While the intensity and scope of service use may vary across patients, the underlying standards ensure consistency in how these allocation decisions are applied.

These services are documented by health professionals in the respective service catalogue within the administrative or therapy planning system and reflect the frequency of services used per person during treatment. Services provided by medical professionals, as captured through a standardised survey, were excluded from the analysis. No further differentiation was made among individual treatment activities within a given service discipline.

Costs of rehabilitation services

We reported costs from the hospital perspective; other perspectives (payer, health system, societal) imply different cost components and decision-use [32]. The costs are derived from the rehabilitation services that were allocated during inpatient treatment. The services analysed in this dataset are reported in three units (tax points, Swiss francs, and minutes). A common unit is found using the costs in Swiss francs, taken from the costs reported for 2017–2020. The costs are recorded according to the Swiss hospital standard REKOLE, based on the direct costs and overheads of the respective services [33]. Imputed asset utilisation costs are not included in the calculation of severity to ensure that severity measures remain focused on clinical and operational intensity rather than on accounting artefacts.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all demographic, clinical, and economic variables. Continuous variables (e.g., age, LOS, SCIM III scores, rehabilitation costs) are reported as mean and standard deviation (SD) as well as median and quartiles (Q1, Q3). Categorical variables (e.g., sex, aetiology, injury group, insurance type) are presented as absolute frequencies and percentages.

Patient profiles

To identify different patient profiles, we implemented a latent class analysis (LCA) based on SCIM III subdomain scores at admission as indicators [34]. As people with SCI/D vary greatly in terms of their patient and injury characteristics [35], there is not a clear functional pathway, and LCA can segment the heterogeneous SCI/D rehabilitation population into more homogeneous subprofiles [36, 37]. This classification enables the examination of whether distinct baseline profiles are associated with different patterns of resource utilisation, functional improvement, and efficiency. The optimal number of classes was determined using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), selecting the model with the lowest BIC value [38].

For clinical interpretability, latent classes were further characterised using baseline functional status (SCIM III subdomains at admission), injury severity, and subsequent patterns of service utilisation and functional recovery, allowing the identification of clinically meaningful rehabilitation profiles.

To understand the characteristics of each profile, we employed t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) for visualising high-dimensional data using machine learning by giving each data point a location in a two-dimensional plot [39]. This nonlinear dimensionality reduction technique maps high-dimensional SCIM III subdomain scores onto a plot, allowing visual confirmation that the latent classes form distinct clusters. Group comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test, and categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test.

Utilisation of services

To analyse the differences in the utilisation of services, we used the Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum test with the total services reported by profile.

Cost analysis

The cost analysis comprised three steps. First, a cost-effective plane was constructed with total costs on the horizontal axis and changes in SCIM III on the vertical axis. Each patient’s cost and improvement in SCIM III was plotted, and the plane was divided into four quadrants using the sample means of total cost and change in SCIM III as reference lines. We used the mean values of both cost and effectiveness to build the four quadrants because the mean represents the average performance of the patients in our sample. The quadrant labelled efficient represents patients achieving above-average functional improvement at below-average cost.

Second, a logistic regression model was fitted to estimate the probability of being classified as efficient based on patient characteristics, injury groups, latent classes, and total rehabilitation costs. Patients located in the efficient quadrant were coded as 1 (‘efficient’), and all others as 0. The model included age at admission, sex, aetiology, injury group, number of comorbidities, SCIM III latent class, and total rehabilitation costs as predictors to control for confounding factors. Given the binary outcome, pseudo-R² indices were used to assess model fit, with results indicating good explanatory power (Nagelkerke R² = 0.528; Tjur = 0.427). Finally, the marginal effects of total rehabilitation cost and latent class were calculated to facilitate interpretation and identify relevant efficiency thresholds across patient profiles.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). There were no missing data; therefore, no imputation procedures were required. A two-sided p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive statistics

A total of 375 individuals were included in the analysis (Table 1). The mean age at admission was 53.2 years (SD: 19.4; median: 57 years). The sample was predominantly male (70.9%). The cause of injury was traumatic in 58.7% of the cases and nontraumatic in 41.3%. Paraplegia was the largest injury group (42.9%), followed by AIS D (30.9%), low-level tetraplegia (14.4%), and high-level tetraplegia (11.7%). The mean number of comorbidities was 6.9 (SD: 1.6). The average LOS in rehabilitation was 160.3 days (SD: 78.8). The mean SCIM III score was 22.5 (SD: 19.2) at admission and 58.0 (SD: 26.0) at discharge. Functional independence improved by an average of 36.0 points (SD: 22.3) during inpatient rehabilitation, indicating substantial gains.

Insurance coverage was primarily through mandatory health insurance (66.9%), followed by accident insurance (30.4%), and others (2.7%). Total inpatient rehabilitation costs averaged at 172,500 CHF (SD: 96,600 CHF).

Description of population characteristics

Patient profiles

LCA identified three distinct patient profiles, with significant differences in demographic, clinical, and economic characteristics (p < 0.05). The 3-class model provided the best fit to the data, as indicated by the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC = 11397.82), compared with models specifying 2 classes (BIC = 11869.87), 4 classes (BIC = 11592.14), 5 classes (BIC = 11937.79), and 6 classes (BIC = 12362.74). Clinically, the three latent classes reflect distinct rehabilitation profiles that differ in baseline functional independence, injury severity, intensity of service use, and magnitude of functional recovery.

Patients in Class 3 were generally younger (mean age: 46.1 years), whereas Class 1 included the oldest group (mean age: 57.3 years; p < 0.001). Sex distribution was similar across classes (Class 1: 75.0% male; Class 2: 66.9%; Class 3: 72.7%; p = 0.3). Aetiology varied markedly (p < 0.001): Class 1 had the highest proportion of traumatic injuries (71.6%), Class 2 showed a balanced distribution (54.7% traumatic), and Class 3 predominantly comprised nontraumatic cases (63.6%).

Clinical characteristics also differed substantially. LOS was longest in Class 1 (mean: 176.0 days), followed by Classes 2 (160.5 days) and 3 (117.7 days; p < 0.001), reflecting differences in case complexity. Comorbidity burden followed a similar pattern (p < 0.001), with Class 1 presenting the highest number of comorbidities (mean: 7.4), compared to Class 2 (6.9) and Class 3 (5.8). Injury group distributions were distinct (p < 0.001): Class 1 included more cervical injuries, Class 2 was primarily paraplegia, and Class 3 comprised less severe cases.

Admission SCIM III scores were lowest in Class 1 (mean: 7.4) and highest in Class 3 (mean: 58.9; p < 0.001), consistent with baseline severity. Gains in independence (ΔSCIM III) were greatest in Class 2 (mean: 40.7), followed by Classes 1 (34.8) and 3 (24.8; p < 0.001). Economic differences were pronounced (p < 0.001): total rehabilitation costs were highest for Class 1 (mean: 240,800 CHF), followed by Classes 2 (144,800 CHF) and 3 (75,200 CHF); insurance type did not differ significantly between latent classes (p = 0.30).

Utilisation of services

Table 3 presents the marked differences in service utilisation across latent classes. Overall, Class 1 demonstrated the highest utilisation, followed by Classes 2 and 3, with physiotherapy as the primary exception.

Imaging and laboratory services followed this gradient, with Class 1 showing the highest use (29.5 and 28.0), followed by Class 2 (27.4 and 23.0) and Class 3 (23.8 and 16.8; both p < 0.001). Other therapies were also highest in Class 1 (54.2), with Classes 2 and 3 at comparable levels (42.2 and 42.7; p < 0.001). Physiotherapy deviated from this pattern: Class 3 exhibited the highest mean use (124.6), followed by Class 1 (113.7) and Class 2 (111.4; p = 0.047).

Psychological services were similar in Classes 1 and 2 (11.5 each) but lower in Class 3 (7.9; p = 0.001). Speech therapy displayed a pronounced gradient, with Class 1 having the highest use (8.5), followed by Class 2 (1.3) and Class 3 (0.5; p < 0.001). Nursing care showed the most substantial differences, with markedly higher utilisation in Class 1 (424.2), followed by Class 2 (248.1) and Class 3 (117.2; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study assessed the cost-effectiveness of specialised rehabilitation services for patients with SCI/D in the (sub)acute phase by using SCIM III with its subdomains scores as the primary outcome measure. Because of the heterogeneity of patients with SCI/D, we implemented LCA, which identified three distinct patient profiles differing significantly in services utilisation, total rehabilitation costs, and functional improvement (ΔSCIM III). This approach offered a nuanced understanding of rehabilitation efficiency.

The findings revealed pronounced heterogeneity in service utilisation across latent classes, underscoring the relevance of patient segmentation for resource allocation and cost management. Overall, Class 1 consistently exhibited the highest intensity of service use and longer LOS, followed by Class 2; Class 3 demonstrated markedly lower utilisation patterns. This gradient suggests that latent class membership captures clinically and economically meaningful differences in rehabilitation needs.

For most service categories, the observed differences were both statistically significant and economically substantial. Nursing care emerged as the most divergent category, with Class 1 requiring nearly four times as many minutes as Class 3 (424.2 vs. 117.2; p < 0.001). Such variation likely reflects differences in functional dependency and case complexity, which are key cost drivers in inpatient settings. Similarly, occupational and speech therapy displayed steep gradients, indicating that rehabilitative intensity was concentrated among patients in Class 1. These findings align with prior evidence linking higher therapy intensity to greater impairment severity and longer recovery trajectories [40].

Physiotherapy deviated from this general trend, with Class 3 exhibiting the highest mean utilisation (124.6 min, p = 0.047). This apparent anomaly may reflect distinct clinical profiles within Class 3, such as patients with incomplete SCI/D requiring intensive physical rehabilitation despite lower overall resource consumption. These findings highlight the importance of moving beyond relying solely on aggregate utilisation metrics to understanding service-specific utilisation patterns.

Cost and functional outcome are associated—but not linearly

The results revealed that cost and functional outcomes are statistically associated, but the relationship is not linear. Class 2 patients achieved the highest functional improvement at a moderate cost, whereas Class 1 patients incurred the highest costs, with lower gains in SCIM III. This suggests that more spending does not automatically translate into better outcomes, and that efficiency varies across patient subgroups. This finding aligns with evidence from other rehabilitation contexts. In a systematic review of cost-effectiveness for home-based rehabilitation after hip fracture, Ipsen et al. similarly reported that higher resource use does not consistently translate into better functional outcomes [41]. This reinforces the notion that efficiency is context dependent and influenced by patient complexity rather than cost alone.

Complexity of SCI/D cases alters the interpretation of cost-effectiveness

Importantly, these findings must be interpreted by considering patient complexity. In SCI rehabilitation, especially for patients with high tetraplegia (C1–C4 AIS A–C) or patients with low tetraplegia (C5–C8 AIS A–C), even small improvements in SCIM III can represent substantial gains in independence and quality of life [42]. For example, regaining the ability to feed oneself or operate a wheelchair may only reflect a few points on the SCIM III scale, but it can dramatically improve daily functioning and reduce long-term care needs.

Thus, the lower ΔSCIM III observed in higher-cost profiles (e.g., latent Class 1) may reflect the ceiling effect of SCIM III in severely impaired patients rather than inefficiency [18]. These patients often require more intensive and prolonged rehabilitation efforts to achieve modest—but clinically meaningful—gains.

The cost-effectiveness analysis (Fig. 2) illustrated this dynamic by identifying patients achieving above-average functional improvement at below-average cost as occupying the “efficient” quadrant, representing an optimal balance between clinical benefits and economic input. Regression-based marginal effects further elucidated the cost required to achieve functional gains across latent classes (Fig. 3). Notably, Class 2 demonstrated the highest probability of efficiency across nearly all cost levels, suggesting that this subprofile responds most favourably to rehabilitation inputs. Conversely, Class 3 exhibited the lowest likelihood of efficiency, indicating that these patients are the most challenging to treat cost-effectively.

Precision rehabilitation as a path forward

The LCA approach aligns with the principles of precision rehabilitation, which advocates tailoring interventions based on patient-specific characteristics. By identifying latent classes, we can better allocate resources to those who benefit most while also recognising that high-cost cases may still be justified due to the complexity and potential impact of smaller improvements in SCIM III scores [43].

The stratification achieved through LCA supports the broader call for improved methods in economic evaluations of rehabilitation, echoing concerns raised by Jefferson et al. regarding the quality and consistency of economic evaluation reporting [44, 45]. Rather than relying solely on average cost-effectiveness metrics, future studies should incorporate stratified analyses and consider the clinical significance of functional gains, especially in complex cases [46].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although SCIM III is validated and widely used, it may not fully capture the clinical relevance of small functional gains in severely impaired patients. This could introduce measurement bias and underestimate rehabilitation effectiveness in high-complexity cases, such as tetraplegia. Moreover, the interpretation of absolute changes in SCIM III is inherently constrained by the absence of a universally accepted minimal clinically important difference across injury severities; previous evidence indicates that baseline functional status and neurological injury severity strongly shape recovery trajectories, such that even small SCIM improvements may reflect clinically meaningful gains in patients with severe impairments (e.g. complete tetraplegia) [42]. Second, we focused on changes in SCIM III during the inpatient phase and did not account for long-term functional maintenance or decline, thereby limiting the assessment of sustained cost-effectiveness, particularly for patients with slower recovery trajectories. Finally, our data were retrieved from a specialised Swiss SCI centre and may not generalise to other health systems or rehabilitation models. Differences in cost structures and rehabilitation pathways across countries (e.g., Australia and the United States) may affect external validity. To address these limitations, future studies should apply outcome measures over longer periods and aim to validate findings in broader, international contexts.

Implications

This study assessed the cost-effectiveness of specialised inpatient rehabilitation for SCI/D during the (sub)acute phase by using SCIM III as the primary functional outcome. LCA identified three distinct patient profiles, with significant differences in resource utilisation, costs, and functional gains. These findings indicate that cost and outcome have a nonlinear association: higher spending does not guarantee better results, and efficiency varies across patient subgroups [47].

Even modest improvements in SCIM III can represent clinically meaningful gains for patients with severe impairments, emphasising the need to interpret efficiency in the context of complexity. Class 2 demonstrated the greatest likelihood of achieving efficient outcomes, whereas Class 3 posed the greatest challenge for cost-effective rehabilitation.

These results highlight the importance of patient segmentation for resource allocation and reimbursement strategies. Future evaluations should incorporate stratified analyses and broader outcome metrics—such as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) and long-term functional trajectories—to capture sustained benefits [48]. Linking patient profiles to cost-effectiveness supports precision rehabilitation approaches that align economic efficiency with the equitable allocation of rehabilitation services [49].

Conclusions

Patient segmentation through LCA provides a valuable basis for improving efficiency and equity in specialised rehabilitation. Incorporating stratified evaluations and broader outcome measures into future studies and practice is essential to guide resource allocation and policy decisions.
