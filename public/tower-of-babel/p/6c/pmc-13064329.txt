Tuberculosis surveillance indicators and chest radiograph phenotype clustering in southwestern Saudi Arabia, 2010–2020

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubnational tuberculosis surveillance data in low-incidence settings are often reported descriptively, but can also support program monitoring when analyzed as performance indicators. We evaluated longitudinal trends in bacteriological confirmation and radiology-based indicators and characterized clustering of chest radiograph findings at diagnosis.MethodsWe performed a retrospective observational study using routinely collected tuberculosis surveillance data from Aseer Central Hospital (1 January 2010–31 December 2020). Patients were included if objective evidence of tuberculosis was documented by culture-confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis with or without sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli and radiological findings compatible with active disease. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and compared using Fisher’s exact test; continuous variables as medians (interquartile ranges) compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Temporal trends in yearly proportions were modeled using log-linear Poisson regression with robust standard errors and a log-denominator offset; annual percentage change and 95% confidence intervals were derived using the delta method. Sensitivity to inclusion of the COVID-19 year was assessed by comparing 2010–2019 versus 2010–2020 models. Co-occurrence of radiological findings was quantified using phi coefficients with Chi-square testing.ResultsOf 510 records reviewed, 422 patients met inclusion criteria; 386 (91.5%) had pulmonary tuberculosis and 36 (8.5%) had extrapulmonary tuberculosis. Among pulmonary cases, the proportion with abnormal chest radiographs increased from 63.6% (2010) to 93.8% (2020) (annual percentage change 2.2%; 95% confidence interval 0.7% to 3.8%). Smear positivity among patients with available smear results increased over time (annual percentage change 3.8%; 95% confidence interval 1.5% to 6.0%), while no meaningful trend was observed for advanced pulmonary tuberculosis (annual percentage change 1.5%; 95% confidence interval −3.5% to 6.4%). Trend estimates were stable when including 2020. Radiographic findings demonstrated clustering; strong co-occurrence was observed between nodules and cavitation (phi 0.46), consolidation and cavitation (phi 0.39), and nodules and consolidation (phi 0.34).ConclusionsSmear positivity and abnormal chest radiographs increased over time, while advanced pulmonary tuberculosis remained stable, showing markers and severe disease may not align. Trends were unchanged when 2020 was included. Radiographic co-occurrence patterns support structured chest radiograph reporting, training, and routine program audit for surveillance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-00945-3.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global public health problem despite the availability of effective diagnostic tools and curative treatment, with the global TB incidence rate declining by only 12.0% between 2015 and 2024, substantially below the interim target of a 50% reduction by 2025 [1]. This finding underscores persistent challenges in TB control at the global level.

Within the WHO Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR), TB epidemiology is heterogeneous. WHO surveillance data indicate that the EMR experienced a relatively modest decline in TB incidence of approximately 5.9% between 2015 and 2024 compared with other global regions [1, 2]. These regional trends highlight the importance of country-level and subnational analyses to better understand TB burden and surveillance performance within the region.

Saudi Arabia is consistently classified as a low-incidence TB country within both the EMR and the Gulf region. Recent peer-reviewed analyses based on WHO-aligned data demonstrate a sustained decline in TB incidence in the Kingdom, placing Saudi Arabia among the countries with the lowest TB incidence rates regionally. Moreover, Saudi Arabia achieved a greater than 20% reduction in TB incidence by 2020 compared with 2015, exceeding the first WHO End TB milestone [3, 4].

TB control activities in Saudi Arabia are implemented through the National Tuberculosis Program (NTP) at the ministerial level, which provides unified national guidelines and a case-based surveillance system to support standardized TB diagnosis, treatment, and reporting [5]. In parallel, international guidance from the United States Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), frequently referenced in low-incidence settings, emphasizes preventive treatment of latent tuberculosis infection using short-course, rifamycin-based regimens as a core component of TB control strategies [6, 7].

Despite declining incidence, ongoing evaluation of demographic, bacteriological, and radiological characteristics of TB cases remains essential in low-incidence settings to inform surveillance and clinical monitoring. Longitudinal analyses of these characteristics can provide insight into changes in case profiles over time and support programmatic evaluation using routinely collected data. However, subnational analyses that combine longitudinal trend modeling of surveillance indicators with structured characterization of radiographic phenotypes remain limited in low-incidence Middle Eastern settings, constraining the ability to benchmark regional program performance and identify patterns that may inform clinical training and audit.

Accordingly, this registry-based study used routinely collected TB surveillance data from the Asir Region (2010–2020) to evaluate programmatic surveillance indicators in a low-incidence setting. Specifically, we modeled temporal trends in smear positivity and the proportion of abnormal chest radiographs among pulmonary TB cases, and assessed whether these trends were materially influenced by inclusion of the COVID-19 year by comparing 2010–2019 vs 2010–2020 estimates. In addition, we quantified the distribution and co-occurrence (φ) structure of chest radiograph findings to describe radiographic phenotype clustering at diagnosis, with potential relevance for regional benchmarking, training, and audit.

Methods

Study design and setting

This retrospective observational study was conducted at Aseer Central Hospital, a tertiary-care referral center serving the Asir Region of southwestern Saudi Arabia. The hospital is a major site for the diagnosis, management, and mandatory public health notification of tuberculosis (TB) under the Saudi National Tuberculosis Program. The study evaluated TB cases recorded between 1 January 2010, and 31 December 2020, using routinely collected surveillance data generated as part of standard patient care and national reporting requirements.

Data collection and isolate selection

TB cases were identified through the public health documentation system at Aseer Central Hospital. De-identified records were extracted following ethical approval.

Of 510 TB records reviewed, patients were included if objective evidence of TB was documented through culture-confirmed Mycobacterium tuberculosis, with or without positive sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli and radiological findings compatible with active TB. Patients diagnosed solely on clinical judgment were excluded to reduce misclassification bias. Microbiological assessment

Microbiological identification of tuberculosis was based on routine sputum smear microscopy for acid-fast bacilli and mycobacterial culture performed as part of standard clinical care at Aseer Central Hospital during the study period. Smear positivity was defined as at least one sputum specimen positive for acid-fast bacilli on direct microscopy, including any positive grade (1 + , 2 + , or 3 +); grading data were recorded but not analyzed separately. No additional microbiological testing was conducted for research purposes. Microbiological data were abstracted from routine laboratory and public health TB records, and analyses involving smear positivity were restricted to patients with available smear results, consistent with standard surveillance practice.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee at King Khalid University (Approval No. KKU-178-2025-31). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and use of anonymized data. Procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision). This retrospective observational study was not prospectively registered, consistent with standard practice for analyses of routinely collected surveillance data.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages (Fisher's exact test); continuous variables as medians with interquartile ranges (Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Analyses of PTB-specific outcomes were restricted to patients with pulmonary tuberculosis.

Temporal trends were modeled using log-linear Poisson regression with robust standard errors and log-denominator offset. Additional analyses examined temporal trends across diagnostic subgroups of pulmonary tuberculosis defined by combinations of sputum smear results and chest radiograph findings (dual-confirmed, smear-only, and CXR-only).

Annual percentage change (APC) with 95% CIs was derived using the delta method. To assess COVID-19 impact, models were fitted for 2010–2019 and 2010–2020 periods; ΔAPC 95% CIs were obtained via bias-corrected bootstrap (10,000 replicates). Trends were classified as stable if the ΔAPC CI included zero.

Co-occurrence among radiological findings was quantified using phi (φ) coefficients (χ2 tests). Phi coefficients were interpreted using conventional benchmarks: |φ|< 0.10 negligible, 0.10–0.29 weak, 0.30–0.49 moderate, and ≥ 0.50 strong association. Analyses used available-case denominators without imputation. Statistical significance was P < 0.05. Analyses were performed using Stata 19.5 and Python 3.11.

Results

Study population

Between 2010 and 2020, 510 patients were identified from the Asir Region tuberculosis registry. Of these, 422 patients (82.7%) had culture-confirmed tuberculosis with compatible radiological findings were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Pulmonary tuberculosis accounted for 386 cases (91.5%), while 36 cases (8.5%) were extrapulmonary. The remaining 88 patients (17.3%) were excluded because their diagnoses were based solely on clinical criteria without bacteriologic or radiologic confirmation, including 21 pulmonary and 67 extrapulmonary cases.

The median age of the cohort was 36 years (IQR, 30 years), with no significant difference between pulmonary and extrapulmonary TB (P = 0.83). Women comprised 62.3% of patients, and the majority were Saudi nationals (78.7%). Most patients were newly diagnosed (97.2%) and received Category I treatment. Patients with extrapulmonary TB were more likely to have any radiological abnormality documented (100.0% vs 78.5%) but were significantly less likely to have abnormal chest radiographs compared with patients with pulmonary TB (38.9% vs 78.2%; P < 0.001). The higher prevalence of radiological findings among extrapulmonary TB cases reflects diagnostic classification criteria rather than increased radiological burden.

Radiological findings

The distribution of radiological findings is summarized in Table S1 and Fig. 2. Among patients with pulmonary TB (n = 386), consolidation was the most frequent chest radiograph finding (60.6%), followed by nodules (35.2%), atelectasis (28.2%), pleural effusion (27.7%), and cavitation (26.2%). Apical involvement was observed in 23.3% of patients. The proportion of pulmonary TB cases varied from 80.0% in 2010 to 100.0% in 2019. Smear positivity among tested patients increased over time, from 54.7% in 2010 to 100.0% in 2020; however, the latter estimate reflects a small number of tested patients in 2020 and should be interpreted with caution.

Among all patients, the proportion of pulmonary TB increased modestly during the baseline period (2010–2019), with an annual percentage change (APC) of 1.4% (95% CI 0.1% to 2.6%). The proportion of Saudi nationals demonstrated a small upward trend that did not meet statistical significance based on confidence interval criteria (APC 1.3%; 95% CI − 0.03% to 2.7%). In contrast, the proportion of male patients declined over time (APC − 6.1%; 95% CI − 8.9% to − 3.3%).

Among patients with pulmonary TB, the proportion with abnormal chest radiographs increased from 63.6% in 2010 to 93.8% in 2020 (APC, 2.2%; 95% CI 0.7% to 3.8%). Smear positivity also increased over the study period (APC, 3.8%; 95% CI 1.5% to 6.0%), although year-specific estimates—particularly in 2020—were influenced by limited testing denominators rather than a true epidemiologic shift. No meaningful temporal trend was observed for advanced pulmonary TB (APC 1.5%; 95% CI − 3.5% to 6.4%).

Co-occurrence of radiological findings

Patterns of co-occurrence among chest radiograph findings in pulmonary TB are illustrated in Figure S1. As expected in pulmonary tuberculosis, strong co-occurrence was observed among classic parenchymal features, including nodules and cavitation (φ = 0.46), consolidation and cavitation (φ = 0.39), and nodules and consolidation (φ = 0.34). Additional clustering was observed between atelectasis and pleural effusion (φ = 0.27), as well as between cavitation and bronchiectasis (φ = 0.20), reflecting overlapping anatomic involvement and disease extent. Nodules were also observed to co-occur with a miliary pattern (φ = 0.25). Overall, these patterns reflect the tendency of multiple radiological features to cluster within affected lung regions in pulmonary TB, representing complementary manifestations of the same disease process rather than independent pathological entities.

COVID-19 sensitivity analysis

In sensitivity analyses including the COVID-19 pandemic year (2020), all temporal trends were classified as stable (Table 2). ΔAPC values ranged from − 0.3% (95% CI − 0.7% to 0.1%) for pulmonary TB to 0.9% (95% CI − 0.7% to 2.7%) for male sex, with all confidence intervals including zero. These findings indicate that inclusion of the pandemic year did not materially alter the magnitude or direction of observed trends.

Discussion

In this registry-based analysis spanning 2010–2020, we use routinely collected subnational TB data to evaluate surveillance performance indicators in a low-incidence setting. Two indicators showed increasing trends among pulmonary TB cases: smear positivity and the proportion of abnormal chest radiographs. Importantly, these trends remained stable when assessed against inclusion of the COVID-19 year using paired 2010–2019 vs 2010–2020 modeling.

All TB cases included in this cohort were microbiologically confirmed by culture according to national TB program standards. First-line drug susceptibility testing (DST) was routinely performed and reported as part of standard case management. However, detailed DST results were not consistently captured within the surveillance registry used for this analysis, as the database was primarily structured for notification and core surveillance indicators rather than comprehensive laboratory data archiving.

In parallel, we provide a structured description of chest radiograph phenotype clustering by quantifying co-occurrence between common parenchymal findings (φ coefficients), offering a pragmatic representation of which radiographic features tend to present together at diagnosis [1–4, 8, 9].

Pulmonary TB constituted the majority of notified cases in this cohort, consistent with national TB epidemiology in Saudi Arabia, while extending these observations through detailed subnational and radiological characterization not captured by routine national surveillance reports [3, 4]. National analyses have demonstrated a sustained decline in tuberculosis incidence across the Kingdom. In this context, the present study complements existing evidence by demonstrating temporal stability in key surveillance indicators through longitudinal modeling and COVID-19 sensitivity analyses [3, 4]. The observed decline in the proportion of male patients over time should be interpreted cautiously, as registry-based surveillance data cannot distinguish true epidemiologic shifts from changes in referral patterns, access to care, or case detection practices. Within this broader national context, the modest increase in the proportion of pulmonary tuberculosis observed in our study reflects shifts in case composition among notified patients rather than population-level incidence trends, which were not estimated in this analysis. Among patients with pulmonary tuberculosis, smear positivity and abnormal chest radiograph findings increased over time. Similar patterns have been reported in regional cohort studies, where smear-positive pulmonary tuberculosis constitutes a major proportion of routinely detected cases [10, 11]. In addition, chest radiographic screening remains an important tool for identifying pulmonary abnormalities within tuberculosis programs, as reflected in national programmatic guidance and large population-based screening studies [5, 12]. While the mechanisms underlying these trends cannot be determined from registry-based data, their persistence over an extended period underscores the importance of monitoring clinical and microbiological indicators alongside incidence metrics. This is particularly relevant in low-incidence settings, where small case numbers limit the interpretability of incidence trends and where changes in notifications may reflect shifts in case detection, referral pattern or diagnostic practices rather than true epidemiologic change. World Health Organization guidance emphasizes the use of combined surveillance, programmatic, and outcome indicators including bacteriological confirmation and radiological findings to evaluate tuberculosis control beyond incidence alone and to provide a more complete assessment of control efforts over time [13].

Radiological findings in our cohort were dominated by consolidation, nodules, atelectasis, pleural effusion, and cavitation, with distinct co-occurrence patterns observed between cavitation, nodules, and consolidation. The observed co-occurrence patterns likely reflect shared anatomical localization and disease severity within affected lung regions, rather than distinct or independent pathological mechanisms. These findings are consistent with the predominance and clinical heterogeneity of pulmonary tuberculosis reported in Saudi cohort studies [4, 8]. Chest radiography remains a key component of tuberculosis assessment in routine practice. And the observed clustering of radiological abnormalities provides descriptive insight into disease presentation at diagnosis and may be useful for regional benchmarking and training as reflected in national programmatic guidance and large screening studies [5, 12].

Local data from the Asir Region further contextualize our findings. Regional analyses are particularly important in geographically diverse settings, where national aggregates may mask localized epidemiologic patterns. A recent case–control study from Aseer identified socio-demographic factors associated with pulmonary TB, including non-Saudi nationality and indicators of socioeconomic vulnerability [8]. While our analysis did not examine individual risk factors, the predominance of pulmonary disease observed in this registry-based cohort aligns with the continued relevance of pulmonary tuberculosis as the principal clinical manifestation in the region. At the national level, substantial regional heterogeneity in tuberculosis epidemiology has been documented, with marked differences observed across provinces, including those in southern Saudi Arabia [3, 4]. Historical data have also demonstrated persistent geographic variation in TB incidence within the Kingdom [9]. Although our study was not designed to compare incidence rates across regions, the Asir-specific trends described here contribute to a more granular understanding of subnational TB patterns and support the value of regionally focused analyses within national TB control frameworks.

Importantly, advanced pulmonary TB did not demonstrate a significant temporal trend in this cohort, despite increases in smear positivity and abnormal radiographs. This suggests that changes in individual disease markers do not necessarily translate into parallel shifts in the most severe disease categories as operationally defined. Such distinctions emphasize the need for cautious interpretation of severity indicators and reinforce the benefit of analyzing multiple complementary outcomes.

Sensitivity analyses incorporating the COVID-19 pandemic year showed stability of all modeled trends, indicating that inclusion of 2020 did not materially alter the direction or magnitude of observed temporal patterns. This robustness supports the internal consistency of the findings and the suitability of the analytic approach for long-term surveillance assessment.

These findings support a practical surveillance framework for low-incidence settings that goes beyond descriptive case counts by pairing longitudinal program indicators with radiographic pattern structure. First, smear positivity and abnormal chest radiograph proportions can be tracked as routine indicators of bacteriological confirmation and radiology pathway performance, but should be reported explicitly using eligible denominators (tested/available-case) to avoid misinterpretation when testing or documentation changes over time. Second, the stability of modeled trends when including the COVID-19 year suggests that this analytic approach can be applied to long surveillance windows without a single disrupted year materially altering inference, strengthening its usefulness for program monitoring. Finally, φ-based co-occurrence patterns provide an empirical basis for structured CXR reporting templates and targeted training focused on radiographic features that commonly present together, enabling more standardized documentation and audit at diagnosis.

This study has limitations. Reliance on routinely collected registry data may introduce incomplete reporting and unmeasured confounding, and analyses were based on available-case denominators without imputation. The study was limited to a single region and may not fully represent national patterns. In addition, radiological findings were derived from routine clinical reporting rather than standardized rereading. Despite these limitations, the extended observation period, consistent analytic framework, and integration with national and regional enhance the value of this study for understanding tuberculosis epidemiology and surveillance performance in southern Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

Using routinely collected TB surveillance records from southwestern Saudi Arabia (2010–2020), we demonstrate a pragmatic approach for subnational TB program monitoring in a low-incidence setting by combining longitudinal trend modeling of key surveillance indicators with radiographic pattern profiling. Smear positivity and the proportion of abnormal chest radiographs among pulmonary TB cases increased over time, whereas operationally defined advanced pulmonary TB did not show a parallel temporal increase, underscoring that single markers should not be used as stand-alone proxies for severity. All modeled trends were robust to inclusion of the COVID-19 year, supporting the internal consistency of the analytic approach for long surveillance windows. In addition, based co-occurrence analysis provides an empirical description of radiographic phenotype clustering that can inform structured CXR reporting, targeted training, and program audit. Collectively, these findings move beyond descriptive regional burden estimates by offering transferable surveillance methods and interpretable indicators to support benchmarking and quality assurance and multi-indicator program evaluation frameworks applicable to other low- and intermediate-incidence TB settings operating under routine programmatic conditions.
