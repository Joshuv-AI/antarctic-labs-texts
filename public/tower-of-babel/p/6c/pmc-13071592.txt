Female pulmonologists experienced poorer health and reduced quality of life related to their professional activities
Authors: Claire Leloup, Emilie Mesa, Damien Basille, Augustin Boudoussier, Arnaud Maurac, Léo Grassion, Camille Rolland-Debord, Iban Larrouture, Clémence Martin, Florence Jeny, Thomas Maitre, Maxime Patout, Lucile Sese, Thibaud Soumagne, Thomas Gille, Grégoire Justeau, Hélène Salvator, Jonathan Messika, Etienne-Marie Jutant, Vanessa Bironneau, Olivier Le Rouzic, Michael Duruisseaux, Bruno Ribeiro Baptista, Anne-Claire Toffart, Simon Valentin, Maéva Zysman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite increasing gender parity in medical school enrolment, disparities in career progression and physician well-being persist. This study evaluated professional experiences, health status and perceived gender-related barriers among pulmonologists in France.MethodsA nationwide, anonymous cross-sectional survey (546-item) was distributed to all French pulmonologists between July 2023 and February 2024. Data included work-related health disorders, career paths and gender perceptions. Gender differences in dissatisfaction and health issues were assessed (chi2 and Fisher's exact tests). Multivariate logistic regression models were used to evaluate the impact of gender on career outcomes.ResultsA total of 586 pulmonologists responded (21% response rate), 57% of whom were women; median age of 38 (interquartile range 32–49) years. Job satisfaction was moderate (mean±sd score of 3.5±1.0, out of 5)). Mental health issues were common. 51.5% reported anxiety, 22.0% burnout, 16.9% depression and over 60% had sleep disturbances. Women reported significantly higher rates of anxiety (58.8% versus 44.2%), burnout (27.9% versus 15.4%) and anxiolytic use (23.0% versus 14.5%) than men. Gender was a limiting factor in career choices (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.41–2.80) and opportunities (OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.57–3.15), particularly among female physicians with children. Women were more likely than men to adjust their career plans for family reasons (18% versus 12%) or job offers (67.0% versus 33.1%). In multivariate analyses, gender remained a limiting factor in career choices (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.39–2.90) and opportunities (OR 2.39, 95%CI 1.65–3.48), after adjustment for age, number of children and practice settings.ConclusionThis national survey highlights notable gender-based disparities in mental health and career advancement among French pulmonologists.

Background

In the context of ongoing transformations within the healthcare system, the medical profession is experiencing profound structural and cultural shifts. These changes are affecting both workforce dynamics and individual career trajectories. French medicine, as a whole, is facing multiple challenges, including an ageing workforce (with 42% of self-employed respiratory physicians aged 60 or over [1]), reduced attractiveness of academic hospital careers [2], the growing feminisation of the profession and its related specificities, particularly regarding maternity [3], and the general deterioration of working conditions especially after the COVID-19 pandemic. Simultaneously, several studies have investigated psychosocial risks among physicians showing a high prevalence of burnout [4].

Pulmonology reflects many of these broader trends, while also presenting specific characteristics, including intensive care [4]. It straddles hospital-based and outpatient care and plays a central role in the management of severe, chronic and often disabling conditions such as respiratory failure, COPD and lung cancer. These clinical realities translate into intense workloads and high emotional demands for practitioners. Furthermore, hospital-based pulmonology, like other specialties, is subject to increasing constraints, including night shifts and on-call duties, that impact work-life balance and professional sustainability.

Feminisation is a widespread and ongoing transformation across medical disciplines. It raises new questions around work organisation, career development and the adaptation of institutional structures to support gender equity. In pulmonology, this shift is becoming increasingly visible, prompting reflection on how to accommodate maternity, prevent gendered career inequalities and ensure long-term engagement in both hospital and private practice settings. Recent studies have highlighted the association of gender-based discrimination and burnout among faculty in urban academic medical centres; with women experiencing burnout and gender discrimination at higher rates than men. Despite the increasing feminisation of the profession, gender-related discrimination remains with women more often experiencing professional undermining, such as having their professional role challenged or their authority questioned [5, 6], a phenomenon well established in surgical disciplines [6]. These emerging challenges call for proactive strategies to support women physicians and rethink traditional career models [7, 8]. For instance, other previous data have demonstrated that supporting high-quality childcare, flexible schedules and family leave are critical for reducing burnout and depression and retaining women in medicine [9]. Similarly, interventions that reduce pay disparities and support career advancement among women physicians should be implemented broadly [10]. However, there are no specific data on pulmonologists.

Therefore, this survey-based study aimed to explore in greater depth the professional experiences, self-reported health status and perceptions of gender-related barriers among pulmonologists working in France. By capturing both objective and subjective dimensions of their professional lives, we sought to identify potential disparities linked to gender, as well as factors contributing to occupational stress, dissatisfaction or career limitation within the field of pulmonology.

Methods

Questionnaire

In this anonymous survey study, a 546-item questionnaire was widely distributed in an attempt to reach all respiratory physicians between 1 July 2023 and 15 February 2024 via email and social media (Facebook and Twitter) channels, with the support of the AJPO2 (Young Pulmonologists’ Association), CEP (Academic Board of Pulmonology), CPHG (Association of Pulmonologists Working in General Hospitals), SPLF (French Language Pulmonology Society), FFP (French Federation of Pulmonology) and SAR (National Union for Respiratory Health Professionals).

Input was obtained from 21 pulmonologists and the survey was pilot-tested twice by an independent panel of pulmonologists (n=39). The questionnaire was not specifically designed to assess gender-related differences in career progression or their psychological impact in order to minimise potential biases in the questionnaire design. The effect of gender was assessed after the results were obtained. Feedback from each round was systematically analysed to improve clarity, comprehensiveness and neutrality of wording. Particular attention was paid to sensitive items, such as those addressing experiences of discrimination or work–life balance, to ensure they were phrased in a nonleading and inclusive manner. The final instrument was posted on a secure web-based application (LimeSurvey) specifically designed for developing surveys, ensure anonymity of responses and managing the resulting database. Career choice was conceptualised as the professional trajectory deliberately chosen by the respondents, whereas career opportunities were understood as the employment positions or offers that were made available to them. The survey covered four domains, as follows: 1) sociodemographic characteristics of the participants including professional experience; 2) organisation of work time, including patient care, research, teaching and administrative tasks; 3) attractiveness/reward and career choice (advantages and disadvantages of different career routes (hospital, private practice, university)); 4) job satisfaction and career choices or opportunities and physical/psychological health repercussions. Work-related health disorders such as anxiety, nightmares, work-related depression and burnout were self-reported, in addition to medication and drug consumption (supplementary methods). We used numeric analogue scales to evaluate unidimensional parameters from 0 being the lowest to 5 being the highest. Perceived impact was dichotomised using a cut-off score of 3. Scores below 3 indicated “no impact”, while scores of 3 or higher indicated “impact” on dissatisfaction, career choices and professional opportunities. Workforce data were obtained from the French Health Insurance and the French medical council to calculate the percentage of respondents from each category of age and gender.

Our analysis is based on publicly available data from the French National Medical Council (Conseil national de l'ordre des médecins), including the total number of practicing pulmonologists in France as well as demographic information such as age and sex.

Ethics and statistics

The Institutional Review Board of the Nancy Regional University Hospital Centre approved this anonymous retrospective survey-based observational study (reference 390-2023). This report complies with the Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies. This cross-sectional study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline (www.strobe-statement.org/checklists/).

Analyses were performed by independent statisticians (I. Larrouture and E. Mesa) using R statistical software, version 4.4.2. Quantitative variables were reported as median (interquartile range) and qualitative variable as number (percentage). No imputation was performed for partial or incomplete answers. Work-related health disorders and medication use were handled as binary variables. To compare work-related dissatisfaction, health disorders and medication use between women and men the chi-squared test was used (all theoretical numbers were >5). Multivariate logistic regression models adjusted on age, number of children and practice environment (self-employed or academic practice) were performed to evaluate gender effect on career choices and opportunities. To evaluate the role of gender on career outcomes (career and opportunities) among parents, univariate logistic regression model was performed. Finally, to evaluate gender effect on career choices, opportunities and trajectory among parents in age subgroups logistic regression models with an interaction term between sex and age (categorical variable) were performed. Career choices, opportunities and trajectory among parents were handled as binary variables (an answer ≥3 corresponding to an impact on considered outcome). Statistical significance was determined based on a two-sided p-value <0.05.

Results

Out of 2807 French pulmonologists, 586 (21%) responded to the survey, with 333 (57%) being women and a median age of 38 (32–49) years, ranging from 24 to 76; 238 (41%) were working at an academic medical centre, 132 (23%) self-employed, 95 (16%) office-based residents and 60 (10%) university hospital staff (table 1). Responders were younger and more often female than nonresponders (figure 1 and supplementary table S1). Most respondents (489 (83%)) declared being in a relationship, men being significantly more often in a relationship than women (87.5% versus 80.4%, p=0.04). Most of the respondents (373 (64%)) also had children, men having children significantly more often than women (71.4% versus 57.7%, p=0.0007). Of those in a relationship, the majority (285 (58%)) had a partner working in the medical field (either as medical or paramedical staff). Finally, 560 participants (96%) were currently working full-time and 285 (49%) reported working during evenings or weekends (table 1).

Dissatisfaction associated with work

Responders reported moderate overall satisfaction with their professional status (mean±sd score of 3.5±1.0, out of 5). In addition, a significant number, 256 (44%) considered leaving their current position in the next 5 years. However, there was no significant difference between men and women (table 2). Interestingly, only 184 (31%) of the participants would encourage their children to become respiratory physicians or physicians in other specialties.

Health-reported disorders

A majority, 302 (52%), reported anxiety disorders, 129 (22%) experienced burnout, 99 (17%) described signs of depression and 40 (7%) reported suicidal ideation (table S2 and figure 2). Regarding sleep disorders, 376 (64%) pulmonologists reported sleep debt (more prevalent among the younger respondents, while 270 (46%) experienced disturbed sleep, with frequent nightmares (156 (27%)) (table S3 and figure 3). Finally, a majority 372 (64%) considered health preservation as a significant career choice (table S2).

Work-related medication use

More than one third of pulmonologists reported using medication in relation to work-related distress. The use of hypnotics was reported by 60 respondents (10%), anxiolytics by 110 (19%), all declared to be in connection with work-related issues, antidepressants by 43 (7%) and beta-blockers for noncardiovascular purposes by 45 (9%). Women were more prone to use anxiolytics than men, respectively, 75 (23%) versus 35 (15%), p=0.026 (figure 4 and table S3).

Role of gender in work-related health issues

Although the professional satisfaction was similar between men and women, the consequences on health and drug consumption were different according to gender. Univariate statistical analyses revealed that women were more prone to suffer from mental health disorders and sleep disorders. Burnout was more frequently reported by women than men (28% versus 15%, p=0.002). Women were also more likely to report anxiety disorders (59% versus 44%, p<0.001), while depressive symptoms were reported by a similar proportion of women and men (19% versus 15%, p=0.36), as was suicidal ideation (8% versus 6%, p=0.075) (table S2).

Sleep-related disturbances were very frequent globally, with anxiety interfering with sleep being reported by 67% of women and 54% of men (p=0.003). Nightmares were also more frequent among women than men (33 versus 21%, p=0.01). Daytime sleepiness, sleep debt, and fatigue impacting free time were reported at high rates in both groups, with no significant gender differences (table 3). A significant difference was observed in weight gain attributed to work, reported by 34% of women and 24% of men (p=0.035) (table S2).

Role of gender in career choices

Being a woman was significantly associated with an increased likelihood of reporting that gender had influenced career choices (OR 1.99, 95% CI 1.41–2.80, p<0.001) (table 3). 241 (41%) have changed their career paths during their working lives (e.g. giving up an academic career), 89 (15%) for family reasons and 131 (22%) for contextual reasons (such as disagreement with hospital management or burnout) or local opportunities. Women were more prone than men to modify their career plans for family reasons (18% versus 12%, p=0.04), mostly due to child plans or partner's professional career. In addition, women reported more often than men that they had to change their career path because of the job offers they received (67.0% versus 33.1%, p<0.0001).

Women were also more likely than men to report experiencing limited career opportunities because of their gender across all age groups (OR 2.22, 95% CI 1.57–3.15, p<0.001) (table 3). In addition, although women of all ages felt that their gender had a significant impact on their career choices and opportunities, the impact appears to be greatest in the 30–50-year-old age group (table 3). Finally, among parents specifically, women were significantly more likely than men to state that gender had impacted their career trajectory (OR 1.86, 95% CI 1.31–2.66, p<0.001) (table S4). After adjustment for age, number of children and practice setting, multivariate analyses confirmed that gender remained an independent barrier to career choices (OR 2.00, 95% CI 1.39–2.90, p<0.001) and access to professional opportunities (OR 2.39, 95% CI 1.65–3.48, p<0.001).

Discussion

In this large cross-sectional survey-based study of more than 500 pulmonologists, one in two respondents reported work-associated health disorders (e.g. anxiety and sleep disorders), especially in women. Female respiratory physicians also reported more frequent use of medications such as anxiolytics. Finally, women were significantly more likely than men to perceive that their gender had influenced their career choices. By contrast, age was not found to have a significant impact on health status.

Although our study was not designed to directly compare pulmonologists with the general French population, the available literature suggests that pulmonologists experience higher levels of anxiety, depression, suicidal ideation and burnout. In our study, the prevalence of depression was 17% compared with 12.5% in the French population aged from 18 to 85 years [11]. Similarly, the prevalence of suicidal thoughts was about 7% among pulmonologists in our sample, compared with approximately 4% in the general population. Regarding medication use, we observed a significantly higher intake of anxiolytics among women. This difference may not be specific to pulmonologists, as women in the general population also tend to consume more anxiolytics than men [12]. Moreover, while women in the general population are at greater risk of anxiety disorders and depression [11], our results are consistent with existing evidence highlighting specific vulnerabilities among female physicians: female doctors have an estimated 50% higher risk of suicide compared with female nonphysicians, whereas male physicians show a lower suicide risk than their nonphysician counterparts [13]. Although no studies to date have specifically focused on pulmonologists, our results align with data already reported for other specialties, both in France and abroad [4, 14].

The results of this survey also highlight the heavy workload and substantial stress experienced by the pulmonologists across France. Participants reported long working hours and most participants reported encroachment of work on their private life. Several factors have been reported to decrease the risk of excessive workload, including a good balance between work and private life. Our findings suggest a need for measures to improve working conditions, particularly for female pulmonologists. In fact, after adjustment for age, number of children and practice setting, multivariate analyses confirmed that gender remained an independent barrier to career outcomes. For instance, good relationships with colleagues and protected time for personal life may be crucial for decreasing burnout symptoms [15, 16]. Many pulmonologists regretted their career choice. This finding is of great concern. Investigations are needed to identify the determinants of career choices and the factors associated with career dissatisfaction among pulmonologists. Improving job attractiveness must be a priority given the growing needs in the field of respiratory diseases. Similarly, a survey of US surgeons found that only half the participants would encourage their children to become surgeons or physicians in other specialties [14]. Programmes that provided coping resources and time for physical activity for faculty members revealed improvements in happiness and global quality of health [17]. Such programmes should be integrated into the training of respiratory physicians.

Finally, although women of all ages felt that their gender had a significant impact on their career choices and opportunities, the impact appears to be higher in the youngest group. These results may be explained by the fear of pregnancy-related discrimination [3] and insufficient childcare support for young children, both of which have negative impact on women's careers. Numerous studies have shown that demands associated with child-bearing significantly influence female physicians’ careers, as they often face competing personal and professional responsibilities [18, 19]. Women feel they must sacrifice their career to focus on motherhood. Although women now make up half of today's medical students, they still account for less than one third of academic pulmonologists in France [1]. One study found that female academics who postpone childbearing for professional reasons may end up “involuntarily childless” having underestimated the risks of age-related infertility [20, 21]. On average, it has been published that female physicians may have their first child 7.4 years later than the general population [22]. A recent study confirmed that female oncologists face challenging career decisions and discriminatory practices when trying to start a family [3]. This finding calls for particular attention to prevent further aggravation of the reported work-related challenges. In addition, physicians’ quality of life of declined during the COVID-19 pandemic [23], which was followed by new aspiration. As in other professions, the younger generation is increasingly seeking work–life balance and modernisation, including more predictable schedules, greater use of virtual work and the adoption of tools such as artificial intelligence to reduce the administrative burden [24].

Limitations

Although we had a large number of responses, there were several limitations. Selection bias is a potential concern, as we may be targeting a more active group of participants who use social media and would engage in survey participation; thus, the results may not necessarily be representative of all pulmonologists. For instance, because of the difficulties of reaching office-based physicians, our population was more hospital based and younger. In addition, responders were younger and more often female than nonresponders. However, the medical population is feminising and females are now in the majority; therefore, data collected clearly reflects the current distribution of pulmonologists in terms of gender. This survey-based study is among the largest to date and the estimates based on available medical databases show a relatively comparable population in terms of age and gender. In addition, work-related impaired health and quality of life prevalence was within the range previously reported [25, 26]. Moreover, all participants worked in France and baseline mental health symptoms and perceptions of the work environment may vary across countries [27]. Nevertheless, work-related impaired health is similar to previous reports from other countries [27]; therefore, these findings appear to be generalisable to other countries. In addition, although there were significant differences between men and women in our study, it is possible that women were more prone to declare the difficulties they felt in terms of psychological issues [28]. Moreover, as the aim of our study was to cover a large number of qualities of life items for pulmonologists, we did not use a standardised questionnaire to screen for burnout or anxiety. Finally, given the sensitive nature of the questions, participants may have provided socially acceptable responses, thereby introducing a potential response bias.

Conclusion

This study reveals significant gender-based disparities in mental health and career progression among French pulmonologists. A substantial proportion of respondents reported psychological distress and sleep-related disorders, with women being disproportionately affected compared to men, regardless of age, number of children and practical settings. While overall job satisfaction was moderate and did not significantly differ by gender, clear disparities emerged in terms of career opportunities and health outcomes. These findings underscore the urgent need for systemic reforms, starting early in medical education and training, to promote equitable support and career advancement for all professionals in the field.
