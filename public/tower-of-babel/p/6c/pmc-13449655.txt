Relationships between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors in Chinese adolescents: results from Zhejiang childhood behavior and health cohort
Authors: Fangping Xu, Jie Hu, Mengping Yu, Meng Wang, Guoying Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSuicide is a critical public health concern among adolescents. Although sleep duration links to suicidal behaviors in children and adolescents, relevant research is insufficient in China. This study sought to explore the association between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors among Chinese adolescents.MethodsData were obtained from the baseline survey of Zhejiang Childhood Behavior and Health Cohort, including 12,779 middle and high school students aged 11–19 years. Information on sleep duration, suicidal behaviors (ideation, plans, attempts), sociodemographic variables, health behaviors, mental health-related behaviors, and other sleep-related characteristics was collected via an anonymous self-administered questionnaire. Insufficient, optimal, and excessive sleep duration were defined according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine guidelines, with cutoffs of < 9 /9–12/ >12 h / day for 6–12 year-olds and < 8 /8–10/ >10 h/day for adolescents aged ≥ 13 years. Multivariable logistic regression models were applied to estimate adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations between sleep duration categories and suicidal behaviors. Additionally, subgroup and sensitivity analyses were also performed.ResultsThe mean (SD) age of participants was 13.5 (1.4) years. In the past 12 months, the self-report rates of suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts were 18.2%, 8.8%, and 5.2%, respectively. After adjusting for potential confounders, insufficient sleep was associated with suicidal ideation, plans and attempts in adolescents (vs. optimal sleep), with OR of 1.32 (95% CI: 1.18–1.48), 1.22 (95% CI: 1.05–1.41) and 1.32 (95% CI: 1.09–1.58), respectively. Sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the results.ConclusionsIn this cross-sectional study, insufficient sleep was found to be associated with suicidal behaviors among Chinese adolescents, whereas no significant association was observed for excessive sleep.Keywords: Sleep Duration, Adolescents, Suicide, China

Introduction

Suicide is one of the leading causes of death among adolescents worldwide and constitutes a major public health challenge. Globally, approximately 100,000 suicide deaths occur annually among individuals aged 15–29, making it the fourth leading cause of death in this age group [1]. Beyond mortality, suicidal behaviors—including ideation, plans, and attempts–exert a profound impact on adolescents’ long-term development and affect family and societal stability.

In China, suicide ranked as the fourth leading cause of injury-related death in 2021 and the second among those aged 15–44 [2]. In the United States, emergency department visits for suicidal ideation or attempts among youth aged 5–18 doubled between 2007 and 2015 [3]. A multinational study involving adolescents aged 12–15 from 83 countries reported global prevalence rates of 16.5% for suicidal ideation, 16.5% for plans, and 16.4% for attempts [4], indicating that non-fatal suicidal behaviors are widespread among adolescents worldwide.

Existing evidence suggests that adolescent suicidal behaviors result from the interplay of multiple factors, including demographic characteristics (e.g., gender, race/ethnicity), psychosocial factors (e.g., academic pressure, interpersonal relationships), health behaviors (e.g., smoking, alcohol consumption, sleep duration), and mental health conditions (e.g., depression, anxiety) [5–8]. Among these factors, sleep has emerged as an important determinant of adolescent mental health and well-being. Healthy sleep is characterized by adequate duration, regular timing, good quality, stable circadian rhythms, and the absence of disturbances or disorders. As the most intuitive and quantifiable indicator of sleep health, sleep duration is widely used in research examining the relationship between sleep and health outcomes [8]. Numerous studies have shown that optimal sleep enhances attention, behavioral control, cognitive function, and emotional regulation, thereby promoting adolescents’ physical and mental well-being [9–11]. The traditional “U-shaped” association hypothesis posits that both short sleep (typically < 6 h per night) and long sleep (> 8 h per night) are associated with adverse mental health outcomes and may be linked to suicidal behaviors [12, 13].

To further explore the associations between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors among adolescents, we conducted a large-scale cross-sectional study using baseline data from the Zhejiang Childhood Behavior and Health Cohort.

Methods

Study design and participants

Participants were from the Zhejiang Childhood Behavior and Health Cohort (ZCBHC) study, conducted by the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), which aims to investigate the associations between individual behaviors and health outcomes among Chinese school-aged children and adolescents. Details of the study design and participants have been described elsewhere [14]. Briefly, the baseline survey was conducted from September 2024 to January 2025. A convenient cluster sampling method was adopted to select the study sample, with classes as the basic sampling units. Schools and classes with a higher proportion of local household registration students were prioritized, and a total of 263 classes from 24 schools were included. To maintain the cohort size and ensure follow-up stability, primary, junior and senior high school students were recruited at a ratio of approximately 5:4:1. A total of 32,027 students were invited to participate. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were strictly implemented for participant selection. Eligible participants were enrolled students from the selected schools who had provided written informed consent. Participants were excluded if they had severe physical or mental disorders that might affect the validity of the survey, refused to participate in the study, submitted ineligible questionnaires (including blank, unreturned, or logically inconsistent ones), had missing data on key variables such as anthropometric measurements and suicidal behaviors, or were primary school students (as sensitive survey items, including suicidal behaviors, were only administered to middle and high school students). A total of 12,779 eligible participants were finally included in the final analyses (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participants included in final analysis

Data on participants’ demographic characteristics and individual behaviors, including physical activity, dietary habits, cigarette smoking, alcohol drinking, sleep duration, and other health related factors were collected via a self-administered questionnaire derived from the US Youth Risk Behavior Surveillance System (YRBSS) and the Global School-based Health Survey (GSHS). Data on anthropometric measurements (e.g., body weight, standing height, waist circumference, and blood pressure, etc.) were collected via the annual school physical examination undertaken by trained health workers, with administrative support of the Ministry of Education. To make all the participants voluntary, parents / guardians of students were sent a written letter and given the option to refuse. In the informed letter, consent was also obtained from parents / guardians of the selected students to publish the collected data. Besides, all the researchers were strictly trained to protect the students’ privacy and ensure the confidentiality of the personal data. In particular, this study abided by the “Declaration of Helsinki” and was approved by the ethics committee of Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention. (Approval No.: 2024-027-01)

Assessment of sleep duration

Sleep duration: Assessed by the open question “During the past 30 days, how many hours (hours and minutes) of sleep did you get on average each day?”. Based on recommendations from the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM): [8] “insufficient sleep” was defined as < 9 h for children aged 6–12 years and < 8 h for adolescents aged ≥ 13 years; “optimal sleep”, as 9–12 h for 6–12 years and 8–10 h for ≥ 13 years; and “excessive sleep”, as > 12 h for 6–12 years and > 10 h for ≥ 13 years.

Assessment of suicidal behaviors

Suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts were assessed via the following questions, respectively, “Have you seriously considered suicide in the last 12 months?”, “Have you made a specific plan to commit suicide in the last 12 months? ”, and “Have you attempted suicide in the last 12 months? ”. Response options for all above questions were “yes” or “no”.

Other covariates

Socio-demographic characteristics, some health behaviors, mental health-related behaviors and other sleep-related characteristics potentially associated with suicidal behaviors, were also considered in this analysis. They included age (≤ 13, 14–15, ≥ 16 years), gender (boys, girls), ethnicity (Han, other), school type (middle school, academic high school), location of school (rural, urban), school performance (above average, average, below average), only-child status (only child, non-only child), parental education (middle school or below, high school, college or above), body mass index (BMI, underweight, normal weight, overweight/obesity). Besides, smoking, alcohol drinking, mental health-related behaviors of feelings of loneliness, experience of sadness / despair and other sleep-related characteristics of sleep loss due to worry, late morning awaking on weekends, late bedtime on weekends, snoring or apnea, difficulty falling asleep, frequent nocturnal awakenings, early awakening and difficulty re-sleeping, daytime sleepiness affects learning were also considered. BMI categories used age and gender-specific cut-off points for children and adolescents established internationally [15].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize students by suicidal behavior status (ideation, plan, attempt). The Mann-Whitney U test and linear-by-linear association χ² test were used to compare continuous and categorical variables, respectively, between students with and without suicidal behaviors. Multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the associations of sleep duration with suicidal behaviors. Adjustment for confounders was performed using four sequential models [16, 17]. In Model 1, socio-demographic characteristics of age, gender, ethnic group, location of school, school type, school performance, paternal education and maternal education were adjusted. Model 2 was further adjusted for health behaviors of smoking, alcohol drinking and physical measurement of BMI. Model 3 was adjusted for all variables in Model 2 plus mental health-related behaviors of feelings of loneliness and experience of sadness/despair. Model 4 was adjusted for all variables in Model 3 plus other sleep-related characteristics of difficulty falling asleep, late bedtime/wake-up on weekends, snoring, nocturnal awakenings, early morning awakening, daytime sleepiness affecting studies, sleep loss due to worry. Subgroup analyses were conducted by age, gender, school location, smoking, alcohol use, and BMI, testing for heterogeneity. Additionally, to evaluate the robustness of our estimates, sensitivity analysis was performed by repeating the main analysis after excluding smokers and drinkers. All analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 statistical package. All statistical tests were based on a two-sided 5% level of significance.

Results

Participant characteristics

Among the participants (n = 12,779) in this study, the mean (SD) age was 13.5 (1.4) years, with age ranging from 11 to 19 years. Other characteristics were shown in Table 1. Males accounted for 53.7%; 81.3% were junior high school students and 66.2% were from rural schools. Regarding sleep habits, 86.3% reported late wake-up on weekends and 67.9% reported late bedtime on weekends. Parental education levels were generally low, with 36.0% of fathers and 38.8% of mothers having a junior high school education or below. A minority of students reported smoking (4.2%) or alcohol use (20.3%); 28.6% self-reported below-average academic performance. Regarding sleep: 13.4% reported snoring or choking; 36.7% had difficulty falling asleep; 19.5% had nocturnal awakenings; 21.7% had early morning awakenings; 49.1% felt sleepiness affected their studies. Overall, 27.3% of students had insufficient sleep, and 32.7% had abnormal BMI. Reported rates for suicidal ideation, plan, and attempt were 18.2%, 8.8%, and 5.2%, respectively. Compared to students without any suicidal behavior, those with suicidal ideation, plan, or attempt tended to be younger, have a higher proportion of females, be from urban schools, be in junior high school, have below-average academic performance, and have lower maternal education. This group also had higher proportions of smoking, alcohol use, mental health-related behaviors, and poorer sleep quality (All P < 0.05).

Associations of sleep duration with suicidal behaviors

After adjustment for potential confounders, the fully-adjusted model showed that, compared to students with optimal sleep, those with insufficient sleep had significantly higher odds of suicidal ideation (OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.18–1.48), plans (OR = 1.22; 95% CI: 1.05–1.41), and attempts (OR = 1.32; 95% CI: 1.09–1.58). In contrast, excessive sleep duration was not associated with significantly increased odds for any of these outcomes: ideation (OR = 1.03; 95% CI: 0.57–1.86), plans (OR = 1.49; 95% CI: 0.78–2.86), or attempts (OR = 1.09; 95% CI: 0.45–2.63) (Table 2). No heterogeneity was observed for the associations of sleep duration with suicidal ideation, plans and attempts by alcohol drinking, although these associations differed statistically as regards age, gender, location of school, smoking and BMI (Table 3). In the sensitivity analysis, although the ORs varied slightly, the associations of sleep duration with suicidal behaviors were broadly consistent among the subsets of students without smoking and alcohol drinking (Table 4).

Discussion

Based on a large-sample cross-sectional survey of 12,779 adolescents in Zhejiang Province, this study systematically explored the associations between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors (ideation, plans, and attempts) in the overall sample and specific subgroups. After adjusting for multiple confounders, insufficient sleep was significantly associated with suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts among adolescents, whereas excessive sleep showed no significant association. Sensitivity analyses further validated the robustness of these findings, highlighting the potential importance of optimal sleep duration for suicide prevention in adolescents. Subgroup analysis revealed heterogeneity in these associations across age, gender, and alcohol use. Given the exploratory nature of these subgroup analyses and the cross-sectional design, these findings warrant further confirmation.

This study found that insufficient sleep is associated with suicidal behaviors in adolescents, consistent with the majority of global evidence. For example, a cohort study among Chinese adolescents found significant associations between insufficient sleep duration (< 8 h per night) and various suicidal behaviors [18, 19]. Goldstein et al. reported that 7% of US high school students exhibited suicidal behaviors, 14% had made a suicide attempt, and 17% had experienced suicidal ideation [20]. Unlike some prior studies that propose a “U-shaped” association between sleep duration and mental health problems [12, 13, 21], this study did not observe an association between excessive sleep and suicidal behaviors. Possible explanations for this discrepancy are as follows: first, the proportion of adolescents with excessive sleep was extremely low (0.70%), likely due to the intense academic pressure characteristic of China’s exam-oriented education system, which limits the occurrence of excessive sleep in this population. This small subgroup sample size resulted in insufficient statistical power, potentially masking a true association. Second, our models adjusted for psychosocial factors (e.g., loneliness, sadness/hopelessness) and sleep-related symptoms that may lie on the causal pathway between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors. As shown in Tables 2 and 4, although the odds ratios attenuated somewhat after including these covariates, the associations remained directionally consistent and statistically significant, suggesting that our findings are relatively robust. Taken together, these results highlight the need for future research with larger samples, continuous sleep duration modeling, or alternative categorization thresholds more suitable for Chinese adolescents to further elucidate the relationship between excessive sleep and suicidal behaviors.

The mechanisms underlying this association warrant further exploration. Of note, sleep disturbances are multidimensional, encompassing not only duration but also sleep quality, circadian timing, and disturbances such as nightmares [22, 23]. From a neurobiological perspective, sleep disturbances may influence suicide behaviors through multiple pathways. Sleep deprivation impairs prefrontal cortex function, compromising executive control and impulse regulation—a critical concern in adolescents with developing frontal circuitry [24, 25]. Concurrently, sleep disturbances may disrupt the serotonergic system, leading to receptor desensitization and reduced serotonin synthesis in the prefrontal cortex [26], thereby impairing impulse control. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are closely linked to inflammatory responses; short sleep duration has been associated with elevated levels of inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and CRP [27, 28], which in turn are related to increased suicide mortality [27]. From an emotional-cognitive perspective, sleep disturbances exacerbate emotional dysregulation, rumination, and hopelessness, all of which are known to be associated with suicidal behaviors [29–33]. At the behavioral level, sleep disturbances may lead to nighttime wakefulness, creating a “window of vulnerability” characterized by isolation and reduced support [34]. They also frequently co-occur with health-risk behaviors such as substance use [35, 36].

The key strengths of this study include a large sample of school-based Chinese students, comprehensive adjustment for potential confounders, and an in-depth examination into the association between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors. However, several limitations should be taken into consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, sleep duration was self-reported and thus susceptible to recall bias; adolescents typically underestimate their sleep duration by approximately 30 min compared with objective measures such as actigraphy [37]. Future studies should employ objective measures such as actigraphy or sleep diaries. Third, the sample size of the excessive sleep subgroup was small (0.7%), resulting in limited statistical power; thus, the null findings for this subgroup may reflect a lack of statistical power rather than a true absence of association and should be interpreted with caution. Fourth, analyzing multiple suicidal behaviors across different models and subgroups increases the risk of Type I error inflation. Because this study was exploratory, focusing on the consistency of associations between sleep duration and suicidal behaviors, we did not apply adjustments for multiple comparisons. Accordingly, the possibility of false-positive findings cannot be ruled out, and the results warrant cautious interpretation. Fifth, non-probability sampling limits generalizability to the broader Chinese adolescent population.

Conclusions

In summary, our study found that insufficient sleep was associated with suicidal ideation, suicidal plans, and suicidal attempts among Chinese middle and high school students, whereas no association was observed between excessive sleep and suicidal behaviors. These findings suggest that promoting adequate sleep duration may be relevant for adolescent suicide prevention efforts.
