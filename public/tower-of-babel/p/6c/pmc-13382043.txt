Comparative effects of high hydrostatic pressure versus thermal processing on quality attributes and volatile profiles in non-concentrated and concentrated tomato juice
Authors: Xuehua Wang, Cuifang Liu, Ye Zheng, Shuyi Li, Xiaosong Hu, Zhenzhou Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High hydrostatic pressure (HHP) is a commercial non-thermal technology for fruit juice, but the interactive effects of prior evaporation concentration and subsequent HHP pasteurization on juice quality have not been systematically evaluated. In this study, the effects of HHP and thermal processing on quality attributes and volatile compounds of both non-concentrated and concentrated tomato juice were comprehensively investigated using HPLC, LC–MS, and GC–MS. The results demonstrated that thermal processing caused significantly greater degradation of color, all-trans-lutein, all-trans-β-carotene, and ascorbic acid than HHP in both juice types. By contrast, total lycopene, total phenols, and caffeic acid remained stable across all processing conditions. Furthermore, the concentration step significantly altered the volatile profile of tomato juice and exerted a cumulative effect on the changes induced by subsequent HHP or HTST treatment. Collectively, these findings confirm that HHP offers distinct advantages for lightly concentrated tomato juice, thereby providing a theoretical basis for its production.

Introduction

Tomato (Solanum lycopersicum L.) is a widely consumed vegetable worldwide, valued not only for their sensory attributes but also for their rich profile of bioactive compounds, including carotenoids (lycopene and β-carotene), vitamins (ascorbic acid) and phenolic compounds (Salehi et al., 2019). The global tomato processing industry is substantial, with a significant proportion of the harvest transformed into value-added products such as tomato paste, sauce, and juice (Wu et al., 2022). Tomato juice, in particular, exists in various forms, from concentrated (FC) products (e.g., paste) that are later reconstituted and not from concentrated (NFC) juice (Iijima et al., 2025).

Conventional thermal processing remains the industry standard for ensuring microbiological safety and inactivating deteriorative enzymes in tomato juice. However, the inherent application of heat, even under optimized conditions, can induce detrimental chemical and physical changes. These include the degradation of heat-labile nutrients such as ascorbic acid, the isomerization and oxidation of carotenoids, the formation of unwanted processing contaminants like hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) and furan, and adverse modifications to color and flavor profiles (Liu et al., 2022; Savaş Bahçeci et al., 2015). Furthermore, the concentration process itself, commonly achieved through thermal evaporation, is a severe unit operation that exposes the juice to elevated temperatures for extended periods, often leading to a dramatic decline in overall quality, particularly in terms of volatile loss and color darkening(Capanoglu et al., 2008; Liu et al., 2022). However, tomato juice concentration has its own advantages. It is characterized by convenient storage and transport, minimal variation across seasons or harvest periods, and adaptability to different uses through dilution to the required concentration (Iijima et al., 2025).

In recent years, a growing consumer demand for minimally processed, fresh-like, and nutritious products has driven the industry to explore alternative processing technologies that can deliver safe products while better preserving quality attributes (De Corato, 2020). High hydrostatic pressure (HHP) processing is widely recognized as a highly successful non-thermal preservation approach. Operating at ambient or low temperatures, HHP inactivates spoilage microorganisms and enzymes through pressure-induced effects on cellular structures and biochemical reactions, while largely preserving color, flavor, and nutritional quality of the product (Wang et al., 2024). Numerous studies have demonstrated the superior retention of bioactive compounds (ascorbic acid, lycopene, carotenoids, total phenols and quercetin) and fresh-like characteristics (color and flavor) in HHP-processed NFC tomato juices compared to their thermally processed counterparts (Wang, Chen, Ma, Liao and Hu, 2022, Wang et al., 2023, 2026; Hsu, 2008; Yan et al., 2017; Ibrahim et al., 2021). For instance, Yan et al. (2017) and observed a significant reduction in β-carotene content after thermal treatment, but not after HHP processing (600 MPa, 5 min). Similarly, Ibrahim et al. (2021) reported that HHP-treated (600 MPa, 10 min) juice exhibited lower degradation of lycopene and β-carotene than thermally treated juice. In agreement with these findings, our previous study also demonstrated that HHP processing (550 MPa, 10 min) better preserved color, flavor, β-carotene, lycopene, and total phenol content in tomato juice than thermal treatment.

While the individual effects of thermal evaporation (concentration), thermal pasteurization, and HHP on tomato juice quality have been studied in isolation (Iijima et al., 2025; Koltun et al., 2022; Sofizadeh et al., 2023; Wang, Chen, Ma, Liao and Hu, 2022, Wang et al., 2023, 2026), a systematic and comparative evaluation of their combined and interactive effects is notably lacking. The industrial workflow for concentrated tomato pulp/juice often involves concentration prior to final pasteurization (Wu et al., 2022). Therefore, understanding how the initial concentration step modifies the subsequent response of the juice to either thermal or non-thermal pasteurization is of paramount practical importance. Specifically, it is unknown whether the extensive physicochemical changes induced by evaporation pre-condition the juice matrix, making it either more or less susceptible to further degradation during a final preservation step. Therefore, the aim of our study was to conduct a systematic, side-by-side comparison of the impact of HHP and thermal processing on the quality and flavor attributes of both non-concentrated and concentrated tomato juice. These findings provide a vital theoretical and practical framework for producing high-quality, slightly concentrated tomato juice. They guide the selection of the most suitable processing pathways, balancing safety, quality and flavor retention.

Materials and methods

Tomato juice preparation

Tomato fruits (Solanum lycopersicum L.) of the cultivar ‘Jinhui No.1’ were harvested at the commercial ripening stage from Shandong Province, China. Upon arrival at the laboratory, the fruits were processed into juice, as was previously studied (Wang et al., 2022). One portion of the homogenized juice was concentrated using a rotary evaporator (RE-5210 A, Shanghai Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory Co., Ltd., China) at 50 °C under a reduced pressure (0.1 MPa) for approximately 4 h, yielding a concentration factor of approximately 2.2. The total soluble solids (TSS) of the initial and concentrated juice were 3.93 ± 0.06°Brix and 8.67 ± 0.06°Brix, respectively. Both the original and concentrated juice samples were kept at 4 °C before the subsequent sterilization treatments. The HTST (110 °C, 8.6 s) and HHP treatments were performed according to the method described in our previous study (Wang et al., 2022). Briefly, tomato juice was packed into 30 mL PET bottles and then treated using an HHP unit (CQC30L-600; Suyuanzhongtian Scientific Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) at 550 MPa with a holding time of 10 min at room temperature (approximately 25 °C). Neither total viable count nor molds and yeasts were detected in either non-concentrated or concentrated tomato juice following HHP or HTST treatment.

Detection of color, total soluble solids, pH and titratable acidity

All color measurements were conducted under ambient conditions using a reflectance-mode spectrophotometer (Color Quest XE, Hunter Associates Laboratory, Inc., Virginia, USA). Total soluble solids (TSS) content were determined with a digital refractometer (DR-A1, Atago Co. Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), with results expressed in degrees Brix (% w/w). The pH was measured at 25 °C by means of a benchtop pH meter (model PHS-25, Shanghai Precision & Scientific Instrument Co. Ltd., China). For titratable acidity (TA), an aliquot of 10 mL of the sample was titrated against a 0.1 N NaOH solution. The TA value was calculated as grams of malic acid equivalent per 100 mL of sample (g/100 mL) as descirbled by Liu et al. (2013).

Quantification of sugars, organic acids and ascorbic acid by HPLC

The sugar and acid extracts were prepared using the method previous described (Wang et al., 2026). To analyze ascorbic acid and total vitamin C, a separate aliquot of tomato juice was combined with 2.5% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid, vortexed for 3 min, and centrifuged under identical conditions (10,000 rpm, 15 min, 4 °C). To convert dehydroascorbic acid to the reduced form, 0.5 mL of supernatant was reacted with 1.5 mL of DL-dithiothreitol solution (20 mg/mL) in the dark for 2 h, according to the method of Hsu (2008). The mixture was then filtered (0.45-μm) and kept at 4 °C until analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed on a Waters 2695 HPLC system fitted with both a refractive index detector and a photodiode array detector. Sugars were resolved on an Innoval NH₂ column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm; Agela, China) thermostated at 40 °C. An isocratic mobile phase was used, comprising 87% water (eluent A) and 13% acetonitrile (eluent B). Organic acid separation was performed on a Venusil XBP C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm; Agela, China) maintained at 35 °C using an isocratic mobile phase comprised 97% of 0.01 mol/L KH₂PO₄ (pH 2.55 with H₃PO₄) and 3% methanol at 0.5 mL/min, with detection at 210 nm, as described by Wang et al. (2026). Ascorbic acid and total vitamin C were quantified using the same Venusil XBP C18 column under isocratic conditions. The mobile phase consisted of 90% metaphosphoric acid (0.1%) and 10% methanol at 1.0 mL/min, detected at 245 nm by a photodiode array detector, as outlined in the study conducted by Wang et al. (2023).

Quantification of total phenols

The Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method (Singleton et al., 1999) was employed to determine total phenolic content. In brief, an equal-volume mixture of tomato juice and 80% methanol was subjected to ultrasonic-assisted extraction for 15 min and then centrifuged (12,000 ×g, 10 min, 4 °C). The superior portion must then be collected, after which the precipitate is to be re-extracted with an equal volume of 80% methanol solution. The two supernatants were then pooled. The colorimetric assay used 0.4 mL extract, 2 mL ten-fold diluted Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, and 1.8 mL 7.5% Na₂CO₃. After 1 h dark incubation at room temperature, absorbance at 765 nm was recorded (UV-726, Shimadzu, Shanghai, China).

Quantification of polyphenols by LC-MS

The extraction solution used here is the same as that used for total polyphenol content (refer to Section 2.4), but it must be filtered (0.22 μm) before analysis. Analyses were conducted utilizing aWaters Acquity UPLC system (Waters, Milford, MA, USA) coupled with a Waters BEH C18 column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm) according to previous method (García-Alonso et al., 2017). Chromatographic separation was performed using a mobile phase consisting of solvent A (Milli-Q water containing 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The gradient elution program was as follows: 0–3 min, linear gradient from 5% B to 20% B; 3–4.3 min, isocratic at 20% B; 4.3–9 min, linear gradient to 45% B; 9–11 min, linear gradient to 100% B; 11–13 min, hold at 100%B; 13.01–15 min, return to initial conditions (5% B). The injection volume was 1 μL, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min.

HPLC-PDA analysis of carotenoids

The extraction and quantification of carotenoids followed the protocol established by Wang et al., 2022. Briefly, tomato juice samples were subjected to three successive extractions using an organic solvent mixture. The pooled organic fractions were dried under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator. The resulting residue was reconstituted in an appropriate solvent mixture, filtered (0.45 μm), and transferred into amber glass vials for storage at −20 °C until HPLC analysis.

Chromatographic separation and detection were carried out on a Waters 2695 HPLC system with PDA 2998 and a YMC-carotenoid S-5 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase consisted of eluent A (methanol/tBME/water, 80:18:2) and eluent B (methanol/tBME/water, 8:90:2). The gradient elution was: 0–5 min, 0–30% B; 5–25 min, 30–86% B; 25–27 min, 86–100% B; 27–31 min, 100–0% B; and re-equilibration at 0% B for 31–35 min, at 1.0 mL/min. Lutein, β-carotene, and lycopene were identified and quantified by comparison with external calibration curves, with the detector wavelength set at 450 nm.

GC–MS analysis

Following Wang et al. (2022), volatile profiling of tomato juice was conducted on an Agilent 7890 A GC-5975C MS. For each analysis, a 5 mL juice sample was added to a 20 mL headspace vial with 2 g NaCl and 50 μL of 4-nonanol (5 × 10−6, internal standard). The sealed vial was equilibrated at 50 °C for 30 min, then volatiles were extracted for 30 min at 100 rpm using a 50/30 μm PDMS/DVB/CAR solid-phase microextraction (SPME) fiber. Separation used a DB-Wax column (30 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm). The GC oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature of 35 °C held for 8 min, then increased at 1.5 °C/min to 45 °C, at 3 °C/min to 150 °C, at 4 °C/min to 180 °C, and finally at 3.6 °C/min to 210 °C, held for 14.51 min. Identification was via NIST library and linear retention indices (LRIs, C₈–C₂₀ alkanes). Quantification used internal standard normalization.

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent replicates are presented as mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA with Duncan's post hoc test was applied using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, USA), with significance at p < 0.05. Graphs were generated in GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, USA). Multivariate analyses, including principal component analysis (PCA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and orthogonal partial least squares-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), were carried out in SIMCA (Version 14.1, Umetrics, Sweden), and plots were visualized by Origin 2019 (OriginLab, USA). Differential volatile compounds in each comparison group were screened by integrating fold change and variable importance in projection (VIP) values derived from the orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) model. Compounds with a fold change ≥2 or ≤ 0.5 and a VIP value ≥1 were considered significantly different.

Sugars and organic acids content

The fresh tomato juice had a total soluble solids content of 3.93 ± 0.06°Brix, a pH of 4.34 ± 0.01, and a titratable acidity of 0.26 ± 0.00%. The soluble solids content of concentrated tomato juice doubled following rotary evaporation treatment, while the pH value remained largely unchanged (Table 1). Additionally, a slight increase in the titratable acidity of HHP-treated tomato juice was observed, regardless of whether it underwent rotary evaporation, despite there being no significant change in pH (Table 1). This phenomenon may be linked to higher citric acid levels in the HHP-treated samples. High pressure likely acts as a physical stressor that compromises tomato cell integrity, thereby promoting citric acid release. This disruption causes citric acid bound within the cell walls or vacuoles to be fully released, thereby increasing the concentration of free citric acid that can be detected (Ferreira et al., 2023). The increasd malic acid content in concentrated tomato juice following treatment with either the HHP or HTST process is also due to the aforementioned reason. Concentration followed by HHP or HTST processing is more likely to damage the cellular structure of tomato juice. Interestingly, the glucose and fructose content of concentrated tomato juice decreased after HHP processing, whereas there was no significant change in non-concentrated tomato juice. Fernández-Sestelo et al. (2013) also observed a decrase in concentrations of glucose and fructose in kiwi purée after HHP processing. Therefore, the increase concentration of the tomato juice resulting from the concentration process may be part of the reason for this phenomenon. However, the rationale behind this phenomenon remains to be fully elucidated. Additionally, the results indicate that neither HHP nor HTST exerted a significant effect on glucose, fructose, pH, or malic acid in non-concentrated samples, which is consistent with our previous findings (Wang et al., 2026). This stability can be attributed to the resistance of monosaccharides and malic acid to short-term thermal and high-pressure treatments.

Carotenoids, ascorbic acid and polyphenols content in tomato juice

Carotenoids are important antioxidant components in tomatoes. The analysis revealed the presence of 12 kinds of carotenoids in the tomato juice, with the total carotenoid content measuring 45.23 ± 2.27 μg/mL and 94.14 ± 2.90 μg/mL in the non-concentrated and concentrated tomato juice, respectively (Fig. 1). Among the carotenoids, lycopene predominated, accounting for 80.14%–91.33% of the total, with β-carotene (7.46%–15.79%) and lutein (1.20%–2.76%) ranking next (Fig. 1). This finding largely aligns with the proportions of various carotenoids reported in our previous study (Wang et al., 2022). HTST processing decreased the levels of all-trans-lutein and all-trans-β-carotene in both non-concentrated and concentrated tomato juice, whereas HHP processing preserved them (Fig. 1A, B). This suggests that, in our tomato juice model system, all-trans-lutein and all-trans-β-carotene are more heat-sensitive than all-trans-lycopene, highlighting the effectiveness of HHP as a preservation technique. A study of orange juice also found that lycopene is more stable than β-carotene after heat treatment (Lu et al., 2018). However, the processing of HHP led to a decrease in 15-cis-β-carotene content whereas HTST processing resulted in an increase in concentrated tomato juice (Fig. 1B). This indicates that 15-cis-β-carotene is sensitive to high pressure. The hypothesis that elevated temperatures may promote isomerisation reactions has been postulated, thus causing trans-β-carotene to convert to the cis form. It is hypothesised that this process is responsible for the degradation of all-trans-β-carotene (Wang et al., 2022). Moreover, no substantial alterations in the total lycopene content were observed among processed tomato juices, whether concentrated or non-concentrated (Fig. 1C). This finding suggests that lycopene in our tomato juice maintains its stability under these pressure and temperature conditions, although there is a slight decrease in the content of the trans-isomer.

Ascorbic acid and polyphenols are also important antioxidant components in tomatoes. The ascorbic acid and polyphenols contents were 134.12 ± 1.28 μg/mL and 10.65 ± 0.17 μg/mL in the non-concentrated tomato juice, respectively. Rotary evaporation siginificantly increases the total phenol content and caffeic acid in tomato juice (Fig. 2B, C). This phenomenon is attributable, at least in part, to the concentration of material content, and may also be ascribed to the facilitation of release of certain bound polyphenolic compounds by heating (Ferreira et al., 2023). Polyphenols in concentrated tomato juice appeared to be relatively resistant to the effects of processing. While chlorogenic acid in concentrated tomato juice decreased after HHP processing (Fig. 2A). This finding is inconsistent with our previous research on the high-pressure processing of tomato juice, most likely because different tomato varieties were used (Wang et al., 2022). The impact of processing on nutritional components has been shown to vary depending on crop variety, according to earlier reports (Sinkora et al., 2024). However, rotary evaporation did not increase the ascorbic acid content through concentration (Fig. 2D), as prolonged high-temperature processing caused the degradation of ascorbic acid (Narra et al., 2024). Although the total vitamin C content remained marginally higher after rotary evaporation (Fig. 2E). Fresh juice was dominated by alcohols (63.43%), aldehydes (10.30%), and ketones (12.29%) (Fig. 3A). To elucidate processing-induced flavor alterations in tomato juice, an OPLS-DA model was constructed, yielding satisfactory parameters (R2X = 0.893, R2Y = 0.971, Q2 = 0.886). The OPLS biplot (Fig. 3B) clearly separated concentrated from non-concentrated samples, confirming that concentration markedly alters volatile composition and content. Furthermore, the clustering plot indicated that the HHP-processed tomato juice group exhibited greater similarity to the fresh tomato juice group (non-concentrated), whereas it became more closely associated with the HTST-processed group after concentration (Fig. 3C). Compared to non-concentrated counterparts, concentrated fresh, HHP-treated, and HTST-treated juices exhibited 51 (2 up-regulated, 6 down-regulated, 23 produced, 20 non-detected), 47 (9 down-regulated, 24 produced, 14 non-detected) and 46 (6 down-regulated, 23 produced, 17 non-detected) differential volatiles, respectively (Figs. 4A–4C). Across all three groups, rotary evaporation reduced levels of 1-hexanol, (E)-2-hexen-1-ol, 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-ol, and 6-methyl-5-hepten-2-one. These compounds—associated with green, fruity, fresh, herbal, grassy, and citrus notes (Han et al., 2023; Yue et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2022)—are typical of fresh tomato juice and were lost primarily through volatilization. Moreover, Hsu (2008) noted that thermal processing raises viscosity, which may hinder alcohol release; this is particularly relevant given the prolonged moderate-temperature (50 °C) exposure during rotary evaporation. Iijima et al. Among them, 1-penten-3-ol, 2-isobutylthiazole, (E)-3-hexen-1-ol, trans-geranial, cis-geranial, (E)-2-pentenal, (Z)-3-hexenal, and (E)-2-hexenal are significant contribution to tomato aroma (Wang et al., 2016). While linalool, (E)-2-octenol, α-terpineol, benzyl alcohol, β-cyclocitral, α-citral, 2-heptanone, 6-methyl-3,5-heptadiene-2-one, (E)-β-damascenone, farnesyl acetone, dimethyl sulfide, pentanoic acid, methyl hexanoate, methyl octanoate, methyl decanoate, 2-phenethyl acetate, methyl hexadecanoate, 8,11-octadecadienoic acid methyl ester, 4-ethyl-2-methoxy phenol, 3-ethyl-2-methyl-1,3-hexadiene, isoeugenol, 1,3-bis-(1,1dimethylethyl)-benzene, 2-bornene were only detected in concentrated tomato juice (Supplementary Table 1). β-cyclocitra, (E)-β-damascenone, and linalool are are odor-active aroma of tomato (Wang et al., 2016). This may be because the heating and concentration process raised their detection thresholds, whereas the thresholds were too low in the unconcentrated “Jinhui No. 1” tomato juice, resulting in undetectable levels. In tomato-based products that have undergone heat processing, dimethyl sulfide is one of the most dominant volatile compounds formed (Dhuey et al., 2021). In addition, the concentrated tomato juice contains a significant number of ester compounds, including methyl hexanoate, methyl octanoate, methyl decanoate, 2-phenethyl acetate, methyl hexadecanoate and 8,11-octadecadienoic acid methyl ester. This may be due to heat-induced esterification or enrichment through concentration during the production process. The ester content is lower than the aldehyde and ketone content, and esters can be produced by fatty acids, ketones, aldehydes and alcohols (Lee & Ho, 2002).

Compared with fresh concentrated samples, HHP and HTST processing of concentrated juice yielded 16 and 15 differential volatiles, respectively (Fig. 4F, G). Both processes reduced alcohols (1-hexanol, heptanol, 1-octanol, phenylethyl alcohol), octanoic acid, and 2-phenethyl acetate (Fig. 4F, G; Table 2). The lower alcohol levels after HHP may stem from evaporation during prior rotary evaporation (50 °C), while HTST-related losses align with our previous findings on thermal sensitivity and viscosity changes (Wang et al., 2022). HHP specifically increased hexanal and (E)-2-octenal (grassy/fresh aromas) due to residual LOX activity oxidizing fatty acids (Petro-Turza, 1986; Wang et al., 2026), while decreasing methyl octanoate, likely via hydrolysis (Lomelí-Martín et al., 2021). HTST specifically decreased methyl hexadecanoate, probably through heat-induced hydrolysis and oxidation.

Conclusion

In summary, this study systematically evaluated the effects of HHP and HTST processing on the color, physicochemical properties, antioxidant components, and flavor profiles of both concentrated and unconcentrated tomato juice. Our findings indicate that HTST induced more severe degradation than HHP in color parameters, all-trans-lutein, all-trans-β-carotene, and ascorbic acid, regardless of whether the juice was concentrated. In addition, HHP processing resulted in higher levels of citric acid and malic acid than HTST treatment did. By contrast, pH, total lycopene, total phenol content, and caffeic acid remained largely unaffected by either processing method, irrespective of the concentration status. Notably, the concentration step itself exerted a greater influence on flavor profiles than either sterilization method, and its interaction with subsequent HHP or HTST processing further increased the number of discriminating volatile compounds. Collectively, these findings provide practical guidance and a theoretical basis for producing lightly concentrated tomato juice, while also helping manufacturers select appropriate technologies and guiding future research directions.
