Evaluation of the relationship between malnutrition (MNA-SF), food insecurity, and compliance with the mediterranean diet in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Özge Mengi Çelik, Zilal Ağbal, Emine Merve Ekici
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is an undeniable fact that older adults constitute a particularly vulnerable segment of society, facing an elevated risk of both malnutrition and food insecurity. These are two important problems that impact their general health, in addition to their dietary status. Additionally, food insecurity and malnutrition can affect compliance with the Mediterranean diet.AimThis study aims to examine the relationship between malnutrition (assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form, MNA-SF), food insecurity (assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale, HFIAS), and adherence to the Mediterranean diet (assessed using the Mediterranean Diet Adherence Screener, MEDAS) in community-dwelling older adults.Methods1072 community-dwelling older adults participated in this cross-sectional study. Demographic characteristics of the individuals were questioned with the help of a questionnaire. Malnutrition status, food insecurity and compliance with the Mediterranean diet were assessed with questionnaires.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 71.5 ± 6.0 years, with 55.3% being female. According to the results of the study, 26.5% of the individuals were found to be at risk of malnutrition, and 4.0% were malnourished. 15% of individuals were food insecure and 54.6% were in compliance with the Mediterranean diet. Linear regression analysis was performed to assess the factors influencing the MNA-SF score. The model, which assessed the impact of gender, age, education level, income status, body mass index (BMI), and HFIAS score on MNA-SF, was found to be significant (R²=0.419; p < 0.001). A negative significant correlation was found between the HFIAS score and education level, income status, number of main meals and snacks (p < 0.05). However, no statistically significant correlation was found between the MEDAS score and HFIAS score and MNA-SF score (p > 0.05).ConclusionIn conclusion, there is a significant relationship between food insecurity and malnutrition. Developing strategies and taking precautions regarding food insecurity in the older adult group, which is quite fragile, may be effective in reducing the prevalence of malnutrition.

Introduction

Malnutrition is a condition characterized by inadequate food/nutrient intake, malnourishment or overnutrition [1]. Malnutrition is one of the nutritional problems frequently observed, especially in older adults. Older adults, who represent one of the most vulnerable groups in society, face an increased risk of malnutrition due to age-related physiological decline, existing diseases, depression, decreased appetite, limited income and difficulties in accessing food [2, 3]. Malnutrition in older adults can reach up to 50% prevalence, although it may vary depending on the screening method applied and different population subgroups [4]. Numerous factors contribute to malnutrition in older adults, including, loss of appetite, low socioeconomic status, poor physical function, malabsorption, food selection, and food insecurity [5]. Malnutrition has a wide range of adverse effects on older adults, making early diagnosis and timely intervention essential. The nutritional status of individuals can be assessed using various tools, broadly categorized as screening methods and diagnostic criteria. Nutritional screening tools aim to quickly identify individuals at risk of malnutrition, enabling early intervention. Commonly used screening instruments include the Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA), the Malnutrition Universal Screening Tool (MUST), the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI), and the Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002). In contrast, diagnostic tools, such as the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition (GLIM) criteria, are used to confirm and classify malnutrition based on a combination of phenotypic and etiologic indicators. In the present study, the MNA-SF was chosen due to its ease of use, strong validity in older populations, and its ability to rapidly assess individuals in community settings without the need for invasive procedures [1].

Food insecurity refers to the difficulties an individual experiences in accessing sufficient, safe and nutritious food needed to lead a healthy and active life [6]. It is defined as a disruption of food intake or eating patterns caused by financial constraints or limited resources. Food insecurity is a global problem and often occurs because individuals do not have the financial means to afford healthier eating patterns or because it is difficult to access healthier food options [7]. These conditions are a significant risk factor for poor health status, poor quality of life and malnutrition. Older adults are more vulnerable to food insecurity, malnutrition and various health problems compared to other age groups [8]. Considering the increasing number of older people experiencing food insecurity and the negative effects of this situation on health, early identification and management of food insecurity are critical for promoting healthy aging [9]. The number of studies on food insecurity in older adults, especially in Türkiye, is limited. This indicates a significant gap in the literature, and the lack of recent, up-to-date data deepens this gap. Various studies conducted in India, Greece, the United States, Bangladesh, and Türkiye have investigated the relationship between malnutrition and food insecurity, and have consistently indicated that food insecurity is an important predictor of poor nutritional status among older adults [8]. In previous studies conducted in Türkiye, the rate of food insecurity among older adults was found to be 21.9–29.9%, highlighting a significant prevalence of this issue [10]. Notably, food insecurity is often linked to a lack of access to a healthy and quality diet rather than directly to malnutrition, emphasizing the need for interventions that improve dietary access and quality in this vulnerable population [9, 11].

According to the Food and Agriculture Organization, the promotion and adoption of the Mediterranean diet not only supports sustainable development but also plays a crucial role in combating food insecurity and malnutrition [12]. The Mediterranean diet, which holds a unique place among healthy dietary models, is characterized by a high consumption of plant-based foods, low consumption of red meat and processed foods, moderate consumption of wine during meals, and the use of olive oil as the primary fat source. The Mediterranean diet has been proven to be effective in preventing chronic diseases such as cardiovascular diseases, obesity, and diabetes [13, 14]. It is known that the Mediterranean diet has a strong protective effect against diseases due to its anti-inflammatory properties. This diet has significant benefits, particularly in reducing the risk of diseases in older adults and supporting healthy aging [15]. It is thought that people experiencing food insecurity may have lower compliance with the Mediterranean diet, which may negatively affect the diet quality and increase the risk of malnutrition. The United Nations has set the Sustainable Development Goals, which aim to end all forms of poverty, as well as hunger and malnutrition, by 2030. These goals envisage preventing food insecurity and creating sustainable food systems for all. Addressing issues such as malnutrition and food insecurity in older adults is critical to achieving these goals. Promoting healthy eating patterns, particularly the Mediterranean diet, offers an effective approach to improving nutritional status in older adults and contributing to the sustainable development goals [16].

This study aimed to evaluate the relationship between malnutrition, food insecurity and compliance with the Mediterranean diet, a healthy dietary model, in community-dwelling older adults. While studies on older adults are limited, especially in Türkiye, there is no current data addressing food insecurity in older adults. It is thought that the current study will make a significant contribution to literature, especially since there are insufficient studies in the literature dealing with these three issues together.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences (2024 − 542). Before being included, participants completed a voluntary participation form and received complete information about the study, in accordance with the standards of the Helsinki Declaration. Participants in the study had to fulfill the following requirements: (a) 65 years of age or older; and (b) competent to speak and comprehend Turkish. Exclusion criteria encompassed non-community-dwelling individuals and those with neurological disorders. In this cross-sectional study, 1072 community-dwelling older adults were reached in Ankara, the capital of Türkiye, using the convenience sampling method. Initially, 110 primary participants, representative of the target population, were recruited through the researchers’ personal networks. These primary participants then referred additional eligible individuals for participation. Interviews were conducted in person at the Faculty of Health Sciences between June 2024 and January 2025 to gather data. A thorough questionnaire was administered that included questions about demographic characteristics (age, gender, education level, and income status), anthropometric measurements (height and weight), dietary habits (number of main meals and snacks). Food insecurity, compliance with the Mediterranean diet, and the risk of malnutrition were assessed with the help of scales.

Anthropometric measurements

Anthropometric measurements were conducted by the researcher following standard procedures. A Tanita HD 366 scale was used to measure body weight, and a stadiometer with an accuracy of 0.1 cm was used to record height. The formula for calculating body mass index (BMI) is weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). The BMI classifications were as follows: underweight (< 18.50 kg/m²), normal (18.50–24.99 kg/m²), overweight (25.00–29.99 kg/m²), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m²) [17].

Malnutrition risk

Malnutrition risk was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF), which has been approved by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism [18]. Six elements make up this verified tool, which yields a total score between 0 and 14. Based on their ratings, participants were divided into three groups: malnourished (0–7 points), at risk of malnutrition (8–11 points), and in a normal nutritional status (12–14 points) [19]. The validity study of the MNA-SF in Turkish older adults was conducted by Sarıkaya et al. [20].

Food insecurity

Food insecurity was assessed using the Household Food Insecurity Access Scale (HFIAS) [21]. The validity and reliability study of the scale for the Turkish context was conducted in 2018 [22]. The scale contains nine items that assess food insecurity throughout the previous month. A score of 0 indicates no food insecurity, whereas a score of 27 indicates extreme food insecurity. Higher scores indicate greater degrees of food insecurity. Food secure, mild food insecurity, moderate food insecurity, and severe food insecurity were the four-level categories [21].

Compliance with the mediterranean diet

The Mediterranean Diet Adherence Scale (MEDAS), created by Martínez-González et al. [14] and validated in Türkiye by Pehlivanoğlu et al. [23], was used to assess adherence to the Mediterranean diet. There are 14 elements on the scale, and scores range from 0 to 14. A score of seven or above is considered indicative of adequate adherence to the Mediterranean diet.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 22.0. The mean, standard deviation, frequency, and percentage were computed as descriptive statistics. The relationships between the variables were analyzed using the Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficient. The risk of malnutrition and adherence to the Mediterranean diet were predicted using linear regression analysis. The regression models were performed by entering all variables simultaneously in a multiple linear regression analysis. For comparisons between two groups, the Student’s t-test was employed, while one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparisons involving more than two groups. Table 4 presented the assessment of food insecurity according to general characteristics. A statistically significant difference in HFIAS scores was observed across age groups (p = 0.037), with the highest mean score seen in the 85 years and over group. Similarly, a statistically significant difference in HFIAS scores was found among different education levels (p = 0.011). Participants with primary and middle school education had the highest mean HFIAS scores, while those with postgraduate education had the lowest. Income status showed a strong and statistically significant association with food insecurity (p < 0.001). The highest HFIAS scores were reported among participants whose income exceeded their expenses. There were no statistically significant differences in HFIAS scores according to gender and BMI classification (p = 0.999 and p = 0.224, respectively), suggesting that food insecurity levels were similar across these groups. Statistically significant differences in HFIAS scores were observed according to the number of main meals and snacks consumed per day (p < 0.001 for both). Participants who reported consuming only one main meal or one snack per day had the highest mean HFIAS scores. These results suggest that food insecurity levels varied with meal and snack frequency. In addition, correlation analyses revealed both positive and negative associations between food insecurity and the studied variables. A weak but statistically significant positive correlation was found between age and food insecurity (r = 0.062, p = 0.043), indicating that food insecurity tended to increase slightly with advancing age. Conversely, education level (r = −0.072, p = 0.018), income status (r = −0.347, p < 0.001), number of main meals (r = −0.091, p = 0.003), and number of snacks (r = −0.082, p = 0.007) showed significant negative correlations with food insecurity, suggesting that higher educational attainment, better income, and more frequent consumption of main meals and snacks were associated with lower levels of food insecurity. No significant correlations were observed between gender (r = 0.002, p = 0.969) or BMI (r = −0.029, p = 0.350) and food insecurity. Discussion

The present study evaluated the relationship between malnutrition, food insecurity and adherence to the Mediterranean diet in community-dwelling older adults. There are limited studies in the literature examining the relationship between malnutrition, food insecurity and the Mediterranean diet in older adults, and there is no current study evaluating food insecurity in older adults in Türkiye. This study found that food insecurity has a significant impact on malnutrition. Advanced age, lower education level, decreasing income status and BMI were identified as factors influencing the risk of malnutrition. In addition, malnutrition was found to be higher in female individuals. On the other hand, it was found that age, education level and income status are associated with food insecurity.

In the current study population of 1072 older adults living in the community, 26.5% of the individuals were classified as at risk of malnutrition, and 4.0% were classified as malnourished, indicating a relatively low malnutrition rate in this population. In a study by Karam et al. on older adults living in the community in Lebanon, the same malnutrition screening tool was used, and the malnutrition rate was found to be 2.5% [24]. However, in a study conducted by Çelik et al. on older adults living in the community in Türkiye, the malnutrition rate was reported as 17.2%, which is significantly higher than the result of our study. In the same study, unlike our study, the rate of individuals at risk of malnutrition was found to be higher [25]. In another study conducted in Türkiye using the same malnutrition screening tool, the malnutrition rate was found to be 1.3% [26]. The discrepancy in results may be attributed to differences in poverty levels, socioeconomic status, and urban-rural living environments among the populations studied. The analysis also revealed that female gender, higher education level, and higher BMI significantly reduced the risk of malnutrition (p < 0.05). It can be suggested that, particularly with an increase in education level, older adults adopt more conscious eating habits and pay greater attention to their diet. As a result of the analysis, it was found that increasing age significantly elevated the risk of malnutrition in older adults (p < 0.001). In a study conducted by Grammatikopoulou et al. in the Greek older adult population, where the same screening tool was used, it was similarly found that the risk of malnutrition increased with advancing age, consistent with our findings [9].

Malnutrition and food insecurity stem from the challenges older adults face in achieving adequate and balanced nutrition. Malnutrition, food insecurity, and adherence to the Mediterranean diet are interconnected through various pathways and underlying factors. Food insecurity not only limits adequate energy intake but also restricts diet quality and diversity, often leading to nutrient deficiencies and poor adherence to healthy dietary patterns such as the Mediterranean diet [11]. As food insecurity worsens, individuals may prioritize cheaper, energy-dense, and nutrient-poor foods over fresh fruits, vegetables, and high-quality proteins that are central to the Mediterranean diet. This dietary compromise increases the risk of malnutrition. Therefore, through its impact on dietary quality and adherence, food insecurity plays a significant role in contributing to the risk of malnutrition among older adults. Food insecurity can cause serious negative physical, mental, and social effects by increasing the risk of malnutrition, particularly in older adults. In the current study, a significant relationship was found between malnutrition and food insecurity (p < 0.001). An increase in food insecurity scores was associated with a higher risk of malnutrition. Studies evaluating the relationship between food insecurity and malnutrition also support the idea that food insecurity is strongly associated with malnutrition [9, 27–31]. This result contributes to the existing literature and highlights the critical role of food insecurity in increasing the risk of malnutrition. Furthermore, it underscores the importance of developing effective intervention strategies targeting the most vulnerable groups in society.

Food insecurity has important consequences, including malnutrition, obesity, health problems, and economic challenges [12]. Despite its negative effects on health and its increasing prevalence in society, studies and evidence on food insecurity remain insufficient. While food insecurity is regularly monitored in countries such as the United States and Canada, there is currently no national screening program in Türkiye [32]. According to studies, the prevalence of food insecurity in Türkiye varies between 22% and 69% [26]. In our study, the average HFIAS score was 0.7 ± 2.31, and 15.0% of older adults were found to experience food insecurity. The severity of food insecurity was categorized as 7.5% mild and 2.4% severe. In a study conducted in Greece, the rate of food insecurity was found to be as high as 69% [9]. Another study conducted in Greece reported a 50.4% prevalence of food insecurity among older adults [27], whereas research from Portugal indicated a lower rate of 23% in the same population [33]. In another study conducted in Türkiye, the food insecurity rate was reported as 30%, which is notably higher than the findings of our study [26]. In the analysis, a positive and significant relationship was found between age and food insecurity (p < 0.05). Although age is considered one of the risk factors for food insecurity, there is inconsistency in the results reported in the literature [8, 10, 32]. The variations in the results may be attributed to differences in the populations and age groups studied, as well as the economic status, culture, and care systems of the countries where the studies were conducted. Additionally, factors such as reduced work opportunities with increasing age and the inability of older individuals to prepare food for themselves may contribute to these differences. In the analysis, no significant relationship was found between gender and food insecurity (p > 0.05). The effect of gender on food insecurity is also inconsistent in the literature. Similar to our findings, many studies have not observed a significant relationship between food insecurity and gender [32, 34]. However, in another study, it was observed that older men had a higher risk of food insecurity compared to women [27]. Education level, income status, number of main meals, and number of snacks were found to be negatively and significantly related to food insecurity (p < 0.05). Among these factors, income level is particularly considered one of the most important determinants of food insecurity [35]. Individuals with low income are more likely to experience higher levels of food insecurity, which, in turn, may increase the risk of malnutrition. Consistent with findings from various studies, our study also demonstrated that lower income significantly increases the risk of food insecurity [9, 10, 16]. In the study conducted by Pourebrahim et al., similar to our results, it was shown that education level has a significant effect on food insecurity, with low education levels being associated with higher food insecurity [34].

The Mediterranean diet is recognized as one of the healthiest dietary patterns. Greater adherence to the Mediterranean diet has been associated with higher intake of plant-based proteins, antioxidants, and micronutrients, along with lower consumption of saturated fats. This dietary pattern may help reduce oxidative stress and inflammation, thereby contributing positively to the overall health of older adults [36]. Studies have shown that adherence to the Mediterranean diet in older adults supports healthy aging and mental well-being [37]. Food insecurity can be a critical barrier to adherence to the Mediterranean diet. One of the underlying causes of food insecurity is the lack of access to a nutritious diet due to insufficient purchasing power [11]. Limited financial resources and low income may restrict access to essential components of this dietary pattern, such as fresh fruits, vegetables, olive oil, and fish. Additionally, food insecurity is associated with financial stress that limits access to nutritious foods [38]. This stressful condition may negatively affect food choices and eating behaviors, further hindering adherence to healthy diets. Furthermore, although diets like the Mediterranean diet are of high nutritional quality, they are also associated with higher costs [39]. This situation can act as a deterrent for individuals experiencing food insecurity, leading them to prioritize lower-cost, energy-dense food options. While food insecurity is a major barrier to adherence to the Mediterranean diet, promoting this dietary pattern through improved affordability and accessibility may help enhance food security by supporting better dietary quality, food utilization, and nutritional adequacy [12]. In the current study, no significant relationship was found between adherence to the Mediterranean diet and malnutrition or food insecurity (p > 0.05). This finding suggests that dietary adherence alone may not be sufficient to reduce nutritional risks; other environmental and individual factors should also be considered. Additionally, the HFIAS score, which we used to measure food insecurity, includes questions about access to food but does not explicitly distinguish access to healthy food [10]. In a study conducted in Greece by Theodoridis et al., it was shown that adherence to the Mediterranean diet decreased as the severity of food insecurity increased [40]. Another study conducted by Grammatikopoulou et al., food insecurity was found to be associated with low adherence to the Mediterranean diet [9]. Future research is warranted to explore the interrelationships between adherence to the Mediterranean diet, malnutrition, and food insecurity in greater depth.

In the present study, compliance with the Mediterranean diet in older adults and the factors affecting compliance were evaluated. As a result of the analysis, the MEDAS score was found to be 6.7 ± 2.47. According to the MEDAS scores, 54.6% of the participants were found to be compliant with the Mediterranean diet. The compliance rate to the Mediterranean diet may vary depending on geographical location, cultural characteristics, and dietary habits of the sample. For instance, in the study conducted by Navarro-Martínez et al. in Spain on older adults living in the community, the compliance rate to the Mediterranean diet was reported to be 23.7% [41]. In another study conducted on older Irish adults, the adherence rate to the Mediterranean diet was found to be 41% [42]. Similarly, in a study conducted in Türkiye, the compliance rate among geriatric individuals was found to be 43.8% [43]. In the analysis, higher education levels and an increased number of snacks were identified as factors that significantly improved compliance with the Mediterranean diet (p < 0.001). Conversely, female gender, lower main meal consumption, and higher BMI were found to decrease compliance (p < 0.05). As the level of education increases, individuals may gain greater knowledge about healthy eating patterns, which positively influences adherence to the Mediterranean diet. It is also known that individuals with high BMI tend to have lower adherence to healthy eating patterns and dietary modalities. However, the study conducted on older adults living in the Spanish community found no significant relationship between education level or BMI and compliance with the Mediterranean diet [41].

This study is one of the few studies evaluating the relationship between malnutrition, food insecurity and compliance with the Mediterranean diet in older adults. The fact that this study was conducted on older adults, who are considered one of the most vulnerable groups in the society, has made valuable contributions to the literature. The strengths of the study include its large sample size and the nearly equal gender distribution, which enhances the representativeness of the findings. On the other hand, this study has several limitations. First, the use of a convenience sampling method may have introduced selection bias, potentially limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of older adults. Although efforts were made to include participants from diverse backgrounds, individuals with similar sociodemographic characteristics may have been more likely to be recruited. Second, the cross-sectional design of the study prevents the establishment of causal relationships between the variables examined. Third, factors such as chronic diseases, medication use, and functional status were not assessed, which may have influenced the observed associations. However, it is believed that the findings of this study will provide significant insights and serve foundation for future research in the field.

Conclusion

Malnutrition and food insecurity are risky conditions for the health of older adults, especially one of the vulnerable groups, and should be constantly evaluated and monitored. While malnutrition can be observed because of unbalanced nutrition, food insecurity is one of the obstacles to individuals’ adequate and balanced nutrition opportunities. This study has determined that food insecurity affects malnutrition. In addition, age, gender, education level, income status and BMI are other factors affecting malnutrition. On the other hand, food insecurity is associated with age, education level and income status. This study emphasizes that ensuring food security plays a critical role in supporting healthy nutrition and preventing malnutrition among older adults.

In this context, the development of public health strategies that improve access to adequate and balanced nutrition, particularly for older adults with limited income and education levels, emerges as an important need. Efforts to improve food security may benefit from initiatives such as expanding access to nutritious foods, increasing the availability of affordable food options, and implementing supportive community-based nutrition programs. Furthermore, incorporating routine assessments of malnutrition and food insecurity into primary healthcare services through the use of validated malnutrition screening tools is considered essential for early detection and timely intervention. Improving the overall welfare and education levels of older adults also appears to be a key element in reducing both food insecurity and the risk of malnutrition.
