Music as a strategy to improve hypertension and stroke management: evidence from a crowdsourcing open call and designathon in Nigeria
Authors: Chidi Okafor, Shravya Allena, Olaoluwaposi Ogunlana, Olufunto A Olusanya, Ucheoma Nwaozuru, Ifedola Olojo, Folahanmi T Akinsolu, Hong Xian, Chizoba Ezepue, Titilola Gbaja-Biamila, Adesola Musa, Njideka Okubadejo, Rajesh Vedanthan, Collins O Airhihenbuwa, Olajide Williams, Olugbenga G Ogedegbe, Temitope Ojo, Oliver Ezechi, Joseph D Tucker, Juliet Iwelunmor

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Nigeria, cardiovascular diseases, especially hypertension, are on the rise. This increase in hypertension correlates with more strokes, significantly impacting mortality. Since hypertension often persists into adulthood, early interventions are crucial to prevent its complications. Non-invasive methods, such as music and creative activities, can effectively improve blood pressure and reduce stroke risk. This study aims to improve intergenerational awareness of hypertension and promote sustainable preventive practices by involving youth and caregivers within families and communities.MethodsWe employed a participatory, observational design, incorporating a five-month crowdsourcing open call followed by a designathon event. Participatory social and health innovations were combined and implemented as part of a larger study titled “Innovative Tool to Expand Music-Inspired Strategies for Blood Pressure and Stroke Prevention” (I-TEST BP/Stroke). Our study targeted youths aged 14 to 24, a critical period for shaping health behaviors and attitudes toward diseases. The 20 finalist textual entries were categorized into themes using the PEN-3 cultural model. The PLAN framework analyzed the effectiveness of participants’ entries in conveying public health messages.ResultsThe crowdsourced open call for musical ideas received 85 submissions between October 2023 and March 2024. More males (74.3%) than females, mainly aged 22–24, and mostly undergraduates (44.3%), participated in the open call, with 88.65% having heard of hypertension. Qualitative analysis with PEN-3 highlighted themes regarding Perceptions and Enablers, such as monitoring blood pressure, engaging in physical activity, and avoiding alcohol and smoking. The use of Pidgin English and Nigerian languages in songs represents Positive Cultural Empowerment. The Negative Cultural Empowerment domain addresses misconceptions about hypertension, including the belief that hypertension is a curse. Utilizing the PLAN framework, the submissions demonstrated an effective blend of catchy, memorable tunes with health education messages.ConclusionThe designathon produced various music genres, including afrobeats, rap, and R&B, with lyrics deemed feasible and socio-culturally appropriate. This suggests that music interventions tailored to Nigeria could enhance public awareness of hypertension and stroke prevention if scaled up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24193-y.Keywords: Stroke, Hypertension, Participatory-research, Music-intervention, Crowdsourcing

Background

Hypertension, often described as a silent killer owing to its asymptomatic nature, is a major global health problem [1]. Hypertension is a blood pressure greater than 140/90 mmHg and is considered the primary risk factor for death worldwide. One in every four adults has hypertension [1]. Despite the lack of symptoms, untreated hypertension can lead to heart failure, kidney failure, and stroke [1]. Stroke is one of the most lethal complications and a significant cause of morbidity and mortality due to hypertension [2]. Although high blood pressure is frequently evident in individuals who suffer their first stroke [3], the risk factors of hypertension are not always clear but include several predictors such as age, family history, dietary patterns, alcohol intake, weight, physical activity levels, stress, smoking, and the use of certain medications like steroids and contraceptive pills [1]. This complexity emphasizes the importance of regular blood pressure monitoring.

Awareness levels remain low worldwide, particularly in regions such as Africa, where the burden of hypertension is rising sharply, reaching 48% in women and 34% in men in 2019. Despite this high prevalence, less than 10% of men and 13% of women receive effective management [4]. Stroke is one of the leading causes of death in Africa. The continent sees up to 316 strokes per 100,000 individuals annually, which is one of the highest rates globally. In Nigeria alone, the prevalence of stroke is estimated at 1.14 per 1,000 people, with a 30-day case fatality rate as high as 40%2.

There are significant disparities in hypertension awareness and treatment across different ethnic groups, between countries, and between rural and urban areas, as well as between sexes. Among children up to 19 years of age, the global prevalence of high blood pressure (HBP) and prehypertension is 4.0% and 9.7%, respectively [5]. A global meta-analysis found that 11.2% of adolescents aged 10–19 years have HBP [5],, indicating that high blood pressure is less common in adolescents than adults [5]. In many African countries, adolescent health efforts focus primarily on sexual and reproductive health, often overlooking noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) [5]. Consequently, systematic reviews and meta-analyses on hypertension in Africa have primarily targeted adults, older adults, and pregnant women [5]. Given that hypertension at a young age tends to persist into adulthood, it is crucial to implement early interventions to prevent high blood pressure from developing at a young age [5].

The use of music for health dates back to early efforts aimed at combating diseases such as HIV/AIDS, Ebola, malaria, and COVID-19 [7]. In the 1970 s, hip-hop raised the awareness of HIV transmission. Music also promoted the use of insecticide-treated bed nets for malaria, discouraged risky behaviors during the Ebola outbreak, and disseminated information about COVID-19 safety and vaccine hesitancy. This suggests a timely opportunity to leverage music for hypertension and stroke awareness in Africa. Involving affected communities in health interventions is crucial and recognized for enhancing practices and creating sustainable, relevant health messaging. There’s a consensus on the need for inclusive public health approaches that engage target populations for effective preventive strategies [12, 18].

One way to enhance research participation is through crowdsourcing, including open calls and designathons [19, 20]. Crowdsourcing is a process where a group of people collectively work to solve a problem and share the solutions with the public. Open calls and designathons are two distinct yet related approaches within crowdsourcing. Open calls invite community input on specific topics in an organized manner, allowing for broad participation and idea generation [21]. They engage a broad audience or ‘crowd’ to collaboratively address and publicize a problem, tapping into public creativity and user insights to produce more relevant and robust solutions. Designathons, on the other hand, are intensive, participatory events based on design thinking. They involve three stages: an initial preparation phase with end-users, a focused teamwork phase that spans a few hours to several days, and follow-up activities where solutions are implemented and evaluated by experts or community partners [22]. Designathons have proven highly effective in promoting innovative solutions by engaging multiple stakeholders, including end-users [13].

For instance, a designathon tailored for HIV self-testing and preventive services in Nigeria garnered significant participation from youth aged 14–24, engaging diverse groups [14, 15]. Such designathons have been used to promote various health interventions, including digital mental health services, HIV self-testing, enhanced linkage to health services, and tailored adolescent health services. A systematic review of designathons in public health revealed that these events have been influential across 38 studies in developing a broad array of health services, prototypes, and strategies [16]. Furthermore, these studies highlighted several recurring themes across different groups, illustrating the potential of designathons to be integrated into conferences to develop actionable strategies for sexually transmitted infection (STI) control, such as using sex-positive framing in STD prevention campaigns [7]. This approach has been successful in numerous sexual health campaigns, which have employed sex-positive messages to encourage behavior change [7].

Building on this foundation, the primary objective of this research was to engage youth aged 14 to 24 in co-creating a new music-inspired campaign for hypertension and stroke prevention, focusing on youth and caregiver dyads in Nigeria. Targeting youth is crucial, as early intervention can help establish lifelong health habits and reduce the risk of hypertension that persists into adulthood. Adolescence and young adulthood (ages 14–24) are formative periods during which individuals develop behaviors that significantly influence their long-term cardiovascular health. This initiative aims to promote intergenerational awareness and sustained preventive practices within families and communities by engaging youth.

Methods

The current research is embedded within a larger study called the Innovative Tool to Expand Music-Inspired Strategies for Blood Pressure and Stroke Prevention (I-TEST BP/Stroke, NIH-RO1HL-168766-02). Our study employed a participatory, observational design, incorporating a five-month crowdsourcing open call, followed by a designathon event. This workshop-style contest, utilizing participatory social and health innovation, was organized to leverage trendy and innovative Nigerian music in promoting health and healthy habits among Nigerians. One of the primary objectives of the I-TEST BP/Stroke project is to develop a new campaign called Music4Health. This initiative targets youth and caregiver dyads, focusing on using music to promote awareness of blood pressure and stroke prevention. The campaign development includes crowdsourcing open calls and apprenticeships. The crowdsourcing open call posed a fundamental question: How might we use Nigerian music to prevent and/or control high blood pressure and increase stroke awareness in Nigeria? To be eligible to participate in the contest, participants had to be between 14 and 24 years old and apply as a team comprising a maximum of four people to create musical content. These participants sought to engage the youth in understanding health issues, such as blood pressure and stroke, that could affect them and their older adult caregivers. Participants worked together to propose solutions to these problems within the Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) framework [17].

The participatory implementation process is triphasic and starts with a crowdsourcing open call. This was followed by a sprint-like event called the designathon, which enabled participants to receive mentorship, refine their ideas, and pitch their solutions to an expert panel of judges—the third phase involved follow-up boot camp activities, as shown in Fig. 1. The crowdsourcing open call followed the guidelines outlined by WHO/TDR [21].

Overview of the key participatory implementation process

The Crowdsourcing Open Call

The crowdsourcing open call was launched on October 25, 2023, and received musical entries until March 3, 2024. Co-creation by the principal investigators and other research team members, including co-investigators, youth ambassadors, and research institutions, was crucial in ensuring the open call was socio-culturally relevant and aligned with the project’s goals. The Music4Health digital engagement platform was developed, featuring a logo, website, community platform, and social media accounts (Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram). The contest was promoted online and offline through various channels, including music summits, webinars, live media sessions, advertisements, radio and television interviews, flyers, banners, and interviews at universities and religious centers in Nigeria. This approach was designed to reach a diverse audience across various regions, age groups, and socio-economic backgrounds, including individuals without reliable internet or social media access. Entries were collated using Google Forms. Additionally, communication and guidance were provided to participants via WhatsApp®, telephone calls, and email. Through these means, the participants had access to youth ambassadors and mentors who guided them to ensure their entries were relevant to the open-call question.

Screening and selection of participants

A team of open-call judges, comprising Nigerian music industry leaders and experts, was mobilized to pre-screen the entries and ensure they met the eligibility criteria. These criteria included clarity and originality, relevance to the demographic context, novelty in presenting new solutions, feasibility and sustainability of practical implementation in diverse Nigerian settings, and health fairness and equity [24] (see Supplementary File 1). The judges were provided with a rubric of eligibility criteria to guide scoring and ensure fairness in reviewing musical entries. Ultimately, the top 15 participants whose entries met al.l the criteria were selected and invited to the designathon.

The designathon

The designathon occurred in Lagos, Nigeria, from April 3 to April 6, 2024. All 15 eligible participants from the crowdsourced open call attended the event. A handbook was created to outline the purpose of the designathon and detail the expectations for each role (see Supplementary File 2). The deliverables expected from the participants included (i) a prototype of Nigerian (Afrobeat?) Music that helps people understand and remember information about high blood pressure and stroke, stress-relieving music that encourages people to take care of their health, elevate mood, and prevent high blood pressure, and catchy and creative songs that talk about the signs of stroke and ways to prevent it. (ii) Executive summary of their ideas and approach to music making, along with three specific aims and explanations of the impact or significance of their music; and (iii) A 5-minute presentation of their final music to the audience and expert judges comprising health practitioners, musicians, and music producers.

During the designathon, all participants received guidance on expectations, health, music mentorship, and in-house studio sessions to refine and develop their ideas.

Day 1: The participants were introduced to the rules and objectives of the designathon. We set out the context, scope, and landscape of Nigerian music, Blood pressure, and stroke. Participants also learned about the expectations from experts, team building, and how to pitch and present their music ideas effectively.

Day 2: The participants shared and listened to the lived experiences of people with these health issues and engaged in panel discussions. The participants had private sessions with their mentors and worked in teams.

Day 3. The Participants had private in-house studio sessions to refine and develop their ideas, allowing them to practice on stage.

Day 4. The 15 participants pitched their ideas and inspiration for creating health-related music, presenting their final pieces to the audience and expert judges within a seven-minute time frame. Table 5 provides a brief description of each participating team’s entry.

Description of participants’ songs

The judges were a four-person panel of music professionals and medical doctors. To ensure fairness, they were provided with a grading rubric.

Participants were judged based on the following criteria: clear and concise description, relevance, novelty, feasibility, scalability/replicability, sustainability, promotion of equity, and fairness. These criteria are similar to those used to judge the open calls. Clear and concise descriptions graded the clarity and details of their music prototype, including the use of frameworks or existing work that supported the rationale for innovation. Relevance was evaluated to determine whether the music prototype would connect with their target demography. Feasibility explored whether the music prototype would be practical in promoting hypertension awareness within the Nigerian context. Scalability and replicability were graded based on how easily the music prototype could be generalized to a larger population and replicated in other settings. Sustainability evaluated the effectiveness of the music prototypes. The promotion of equity and fairness was graded based on addressing health outcomes, representation, empowerment of youth, and community members’ acknowledgment of gender and other intersectional inequalities. Submissions that met all the criteria with exceptional strength were placed in the highest score range (41–50). The top three finalists were announced and awarded a seed fund of 500,000 ($300) for first place, 300,000 ($180) for second place, and 200,000 ($125) for third place. Other recognitions, such as the most innovative, viewer choice, and peer choice, were also awarded certificates of recognition. In addition to all awards, the top six participants were notified of their successful progression to the innovative boot camp stage.

All participants, including mentors and judges, provided food, transportation, and accommodation during the designathon.

Follow-up activities

After completing the designathon, six participants advanced to the bootcamp, an intensive four-week event in which participants refined their music prototypes. The first week consisted of virtual sessions focused on conducting community assessments using the SWOT (strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats) analysis in preparation for their music interviews. Over the following three weeks, participants collaborated with experts to address potential barriers to refining their music prototypes, ensuring they remained relevant, appropriate, and acceptable to their target audience and communities. Further details regarding this phase are presented in a separate paper.

Conceptual framework

The Youth Participatory Action Research (YPAR) is a framework that actively engages young people in examining issues relevant to their lives, equipping them with the skills to analyse their social contexts and advocate for change within their communities. It emphasizes youth agency, strengths, and leadership, challenging deficit-based narratives that often frame young people as passive subjects of intervention. In the context of this study, YPAR provides a structured approach to involving youth in hypertension and stroke prevention efforts, ensuring that their perspectives inform culturally relevant, community-driven solutions [23].

The PEN 3 cultural model provides a comprehensive framework for understanding how cultural factors influence the planning, implementation, and sustainability of health interventions [8]. This model posits that culture is critical in facilitating behavioral changes, beliefs, and community engagement. Cultural factors are explained using three interrelated domains, each with three constructs, whose first letters correspond to the letters in the PEN 3 Cultural Model: Cultural Identity (Person, Extended Family, Neighbourhood), Relationships and Expectations (Perceptions, Enablers, Nurturers), and Cultural Empowerment (Positive, Existential, Negative). Textual data from the 20 best submissions were analyzed and coded using two PEN 3 Cultural Model domains: Relationships and Expectations and Cultural Empowerment.

Additionally, the PLAN Framework was used to analyze the data. The PLAN (People, Learning, Adaptation, and Nurturing) framework is a dynamic tool that simplifies the planning process for the sustainability of evidence-based interventions throughout the lifecycle of the intervention. It considers key individuals and the learning, adaptation, and nurturing processes essential for comprehending the interactions among interventions, practice environments, and the larger ecological context of implementation [9]. This comprehensive approach using the PLAN framework ensured that each song’s effectiveness in conveying public health messages to the right audience was assessed for sustainability, feasibility, and scalability.

Data analysis

A quantitative analysis of submissions received through the open call for participants aged 14 to 24 across various geographical regions was conducted. Descriptive statistics were analysed using the STATA software to examine participants’ characteristics and demographic information. Details regarding age, education level, occupation, gender, location, team dynamics, and contact address were summarized. Furthermore, data from the survey were analysed to assess knowledge levels at the designathon.

Guided by the PEN-3 cultural model, we qualitatively examined the lyrics of the 20 finalist submissions (see Tables 3 and 4) to investigate how culture influences the behaviour and attitudes of youth-caregiver pairs regarding hypertension prevention in Nigerian communities. This analysis organized the textual data into themes.

Results

Open-call submissions and demographics

The crowdsourced open call for musical ideas received 85 submissions between October 2023 and March 2024. All entries were accepted online through a link on the online advertising posters. After cleaning the data to remove the double entries, 70 entries were evaluated. Two open-call judges were selected to go through each submission, and fifteen (15) submissions were found to be eligible to be invited to the designathon. The eligible submissions were from a diverse range of music genres, including rap, Afro-fusion, rhythm and blues, and storytelling.

Submissions were received from a wide range of states across the six geopolitical zones of Nigeria (16 of 36 states), and they were all original songs written by participants. Most submissions (54.8%) were from the southwest region of Nigeria. The participant pool primarily consisted of young adults (late adolescence, 18–21 years old), with 35.7% falling within this age range. However, despite our eligibility criteria, we received submissions from those older than 24 years, which we excluded from participating in the designathon. The sample had a male majority, with 74.3% of participants identifying as male. Most participants were students (57.2%), of whom 45.7% had secondary school as their highest level of education. Qualitative analysis of participants’ top 20 entries from the crowdsourcing open call using the PEN 3 cultural model

Deductive qualitative analysis of the top 20 entries revealed themes under 4 four major constructs and from two domains of the PEN 3 cultural model: Relations and Expectations (Perception and Enablers) and Cultural Empowerment (Positive, Existential, and Negative). Numerous songs highlighted themes related to Perceptions and Enablers, such as monitoring blood pressure, engaging in physical activity, avoiding alcohol and smoking, and ensuring sufficient rest to promote healthy habits and prevent hypertension. Further analysis using the PEN-3 model showed that many participants conveyed cultural factors in their song entries, which were categorized as Perceptions and Enablers and Positive and Existential Cultural Empowerment. Analysis of the submitted entries using the PLAN framework revealed a clear alignment with public health messaging strategies aimed at fostering behavior change within diverse Nigerian communities. Across entries, the target audiences ranged from working-class individuals to families, young people, and caregivers. The use of culturally relevant language, particularly pidgin English, which is common in today’s popular Nigerian music genres such as afrobeat, which also incorporates rap, was employed to enhance relatability and engagement. This strategy ensured that health messages reached diverse socioeconomic groups, fostering inclusivity in health promotion messaging. Additionally, employing the collective pronouns “we” and “you” in the lyrics underscored a sense of shared responsibility and personal accountability by emphasizing community involvement in health promotion.

Regarding learning and adaptation, the entries demonstrated an effective blend of catchy, memorable tunes with health education messages focused on preventing and managing conditions such as hypertension and stroke, emphasizing lifestyle changes, such as exercise, stress management, and regular health monitoring. Adapting to local contexts, such as translating health terms like the FAST acronym for stroke recognition into the local language and addressing the realities of working-class Nigerians, further strengthens the message’s resonance. Finally, nurturing, a key component of the PLAN framework, is evident in the songs’ calls to action, encouraging positive lifestyle changes and early recognition of health conditions. These efforts promote long-term behavioral change and community health initiatives, underscoring the importance of sustained health awareness and intervention.

Discussion

We conducted a designathon that engaged Nigerian youth in using Nigerian music to enhance their knowledge of high blood pressure prevention and increase stroke awareness in Nigeria. This three-stage participatory process – a crowd-sourcing open call, designathon, and bootcamp – provided a structured platform for collaboration, allowing participants to generate and refine ideas that could contribute to public health outcomes through music. By employing a designathon approach, we not only crowdsourced innovative ideas but also provided an opportunity for capacity building, mentorship, and feedback that could lead to effective strategies and implementation for preventing hypertension and stroke. This approach builds on existing literature that explores the use of music as a tool for health education, offering a novel, culturally relevant method of engaging youth in hypertension and stroke prevention efforts in Nigeria.

The designathon yielded a range of musical concepts and lyrics that judges assessed as potentially feasible, innovative, relevant, impactful, and conducive to health equity. These results align with earlier studies highlighting the effectiveness of community-based crowdsourcing and co-creation in public health contexts [2, 3]. Participants’ varied backgrounds and experiences significantly contributed to the creativity and diversity of the proposed solutions. Our findings suggest that participatory activities informed by design thinking can yield high-quality health-related content, particularly when supported by preparatory sessions with end-users, intensive collaborative teamwork, and follow-up activities led by mentors, musicians, and health experts.

The designathon employs a bottom-up approach [4, 5], fostering a sense of ownership among participants and promoting community-based solutions more likely to be adopted and sustained over time. The event provided a unique environment where Nigerian youth could collaboratively create music-based interventions to address hypertension and stroke. Although the crowdsourcing process was open to individuals aged 14–24, we observed that older participants were generally more successful in presenting their ideas. We also observed that many designathon applicants resided in Lagos, Nigeria (47.1%); the majority were males (74.3%). However, the designathon ideas were neither gender nor location specific. Approximately 57.1% of the participants were students in either secondary or tertiary institutions, suggesting that schools play a vital role in organizing designathons in the community, which is consistent with earlier studies that have employed diverse groups of students to create innovative health solutions (2,3). Those who were finalists received additional incentives, including participation in an innovation boot camp, mentorship from prominent Nigerian musicians, and opportunities to engage with community leaders and organizations for local implementation and media exposure. This inclusive approach encourages community engagement and ownership among participants, emphasizing the importance of youth-led research in addressing public health issues, which is relatively rare in many hypertension and stroke programs in developing countries, such as Nigeria.

Despite these successes, our study faced several limitations. First, the music-based solutions identified as promising and impactful by the judges were selected primarily based on the teams’ pitch presentations, without immediate outcome data or pilot testing to substantiate their effectiveness. While these initial ideas were innovative and well-received, their real-world impact remains to be evaluated. To address this, future pilot studies will assess the efficacy of the developed music interventions. Additionally, the brief duration of the designathon may have limited the depth and breadth of exploration into potential music strategies for hypertension and stroke prevention, potentially constraining the development of more comprehensive or refined solutions. To address this, we invited the most promising teams to a four-week innovation boot camp designed to build entrepreneurial skills and support the further development and refinement of their music-based interventions. Additionally, the genres of music were not classified, so we could not determine which genre resonated most with and had the greatest impact on the audience. Finally, the resource-intensive nature of the designathon necessitated our reliance on local resources and volunteers in Nigeria to manage costs effectively.

Despite these constraints, our designathon successfully demonstrated the potential for engaging youth in creating innovative music-based interventions. These findings highlight the importance of participatory, bottom-up approaches in public health innovation, underscoring the role of community involvement, mentorship, and sustainability in developing countries like Nigeria.

Conclusion

Trendy Nigerian music, such as Afrobeat and related genres, can raise awareness and positively influence health behaviors, particularly in the management of hypertension and stroke. Over the course of a five-day designathon, we brought together a diverse group of young, innovative Nigerians. We provided them with comprehensive coaching and mentorship to leverage their creativity and use socio-culturally relevant songs to promote health habits that prevent high blood pressure and stroke. Participants adopted themes of health awareness, working collaboratively to produce catchy and engaging music specifically focused on preventing hypertension and stroke.

The outcomes of this designathon were promising, showcasing a variety of original compositions that resonated with the target audience. Feedback from the participants and listeners highlighted the effectiveness of using music as a medium to convey critical health messages and promote healthier lifestyle choices. These findings suggest that if implemented on a larger scale, music-based interventions tailored to the Nigerian context could significantly enhance public knowledge about hypertension and stroke prevention practices, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes across the community.
