A stromal PAI1-tPA axis orchestrates immunosuppression in pancreatic cancer
Authors: Tenzin Ngodup, Brynn Elson, Ashley M. Mello, Sean Hannifin, Miranda Liu, Yaqing Zhang, Jiaqi Shi, Yatrik M. Shah, Daniel A. Lawrence, Marina Pasca di Magliano, Kyoung Eun Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a lethal malignancy with a dense desmoplastic stroma and an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that contribute to therapeutic resistance. Here, we identify plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI1) as a stroma-derived mediator of immune evasion and tumor progression in PDAC. PAI1 is predominantly produced and secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts, and its genetic ablation in the stromal compartment impairs tumor growth. Mechanistically, hypoxia induces PAI1 expression in fibroblasts, which in turn shifts macrophages toward immunosuppressive phenotypes and suppresses CD8+ T cell infiltration and function. We further show that tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), a direct PAI1 target, is also secreted by fibroblasts and supports antitumor CD8+ T cell responses. Notably, elimination of stromal tPA promotes immunosuppressive macrophage phenotypes, reduces CD8+ T cell infiltration, and accelerates PDAC progression. These findings define a previously unrecognized PAI1-tPA regulatory axis within the tumor stroma that modulates antitumor immunity. Targeting this pathway may provide a therapeutic opportunity to overcome stroma-driven immune suppression in PDAC.

INTRODUCTION

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) is a deadly cancer, with a 5-year survival rate of 13% (1). A hallmark of PDAC is the presence of a dense fibroinflammatory stroma, composed of extracellular matrix (ECM) and stromal cells, such as fibroblasts and immune cells. Interactions between tumor cells and the associated stroma substantially contribute to disease progression, tumor heterogeneity, and therapy resistance in PDAC (2). Within the PDAC stroma, cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are a prevalent population and show considerable phenotypic and functional diversity (3). CAFs play a major role in production of growth factors and cytokines as well as synthesis and remodeling of the ECM. In addition to direct cell-cell contact, CAF-secreted factors mediate interactions between CAFs and many other cell types through paracrine signaling (2, 3). The ability of CAFs to modulate the immune system has gained increasing attention, as CAF-to-immune cell cross-talk largely leads to immunosuppression (2, 3) and thus could be targeted to induce an antitumor immune response.

Cancer is linked to a hypercoagulable state, and cancer-associated thrombosis is a leading cause of mortality (4). Among all cancer types, PDAC carries the highest risk for venous thromboembolism (VTE) (5). Plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI1; encoded by SERPINE1) is associated with VTE in patients with pancreatic cancer (6). PAI1 is a serine protease inhibitor (SERPIN) that functions as the principal inhibitor of fibrinolysis—the process breaking down blood clots—thereby promoting a hypofibrinolytic or hypercoagulable state. High levels of PAI1 in tumors correlate with a poor clinical outcome (7). PAI1 is a multifunctional protein, and its protumorigenic functions include cell proliferation, angiogenesis, inflammation, senescence, and metabolism (8–10). Most studies have focused on tumor cell–derived PAI1, while the contribution of stromal PAI1 to tumor progression remains largely unknown.

Hypoxia, a condition of insufficient oxygenation, is a risk factor for thrombosis (11) and occurs frequently in solid tumors because of their high oxygen/nutrient demand and aberrant vascularization (12). PDAC has been shown to be extremely hypovascular and hypoxic (13, 14) and is also prothrombotic (4, 5), but the relationship between hypoxia and coagulation/fibrinolysis in the pancreatic tumor microenvironment (TME) is poorly understood.

Here, we show that PAI1 is predominantly expressed in the stromal compartment in human and mouse PDAC. Genetic ablation of stromal PAI1 in an orthotopic model of PDAC leads to a dramatic increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration, reducing pancreatic tumor growth. We demonstrate that hypoxia induces PAI1 expression in pancreatic CAFs. The fibrinolytic protease tissue plasminogen activator (tPA; encoded by PLAT), a canonical target inhibited by PAI1, is mainly expressed by pancreatic CAFs. Stromal tPA ablation results in decreased CD8+ T cell infiltration and enhanced pancreatic tumor growth, indicating that PAI1 contributes to immunosuppression, at least in part, by inhibiting tPA activity. Our findings identify a stromal PAI1-tPA axis as a key mechanism of immunosuppression and tumor progression in PDAC and provide insights into the interplay between hypoxia, PAI1-tPA signaling, and immune responses during pancreatic tumorigenesis.

RESULTS

PAI1 is primarily expressed in the stromal compartment in human and mouse PDAC

To assess the expression of the PAI1 gene SERPINE1 in human PDAC, we analyzed bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data from human normal pancreas [n = 167, Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx)] (15) and PDAC samples [n = 169, The Cancer Genome Atlas–pancreatic adenocarcinoma (TCGA)-(PAAD)] (16). SERPINE1 transcripts were significantly higher in PDAC compared with normal pancreas (Fig. 1A). High SERPINE1 expression was strongly associated with worse survival in patients with PDAC (Fig. 1B). We then evaluated the expression of SERPINE1 in different cellular compartments of the pancreas using a single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset (17) that includes 16 PDAC patient tumor samples (Fig. 1C). SERPINE1 was predominantly expressed in fibroblasts and endothelial cells (Fig. 1, D and E). On the other hand, CK19+ (Cytokeratin19, KRT19) ECAD+ (Ecadherin, CDH1) ductal/ductal-like malignant cells exhibited minimal SERPINE1 expression (Fig. 1, D and E). To validate expression of the PAI1 protein, we performed coimmunofluorescence (co-IF) staining of surgically resected human PDAC and matched adjacent normal tissues for PAI1, platelet-derived growth factor receptor α/β (PDGFRα/β) (to demark fibroblasts), and Pan-cytokeratin (PanCK; to demark ductal and tumor cells). We observed pronounced increases in PAI1 expression in PDAC regions relative to adjacent normal regions (Fig. 1F and fig. S1A). Consistent with the scRNA-seq data (Fig. 1, D and E), PAI1 was expressed in PDGFRα/β+ fibroblasts but was barely detectable in PanCK+ tumor cells (Fig. 1F).

To determine whether the expression pattern of PAI1 in human PDAC is recapitulated in murine PDAC, we analyzed a murine scRNA-seq dataset (18–20) of autochthonous PDAC tissues (n = 3; Fig. 1G). The results revealed highest Serpine1 expression in fibroblasts, followed by endothelial cells (Fig. 1, H and I). Together, these findings indicate that PAI1 is significantly up-regulated in PDAC and that the tumor stroma is the primary source of PAI1. Notably, we examined SERPINE1 expression in the major pancreatic CAF populations—myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs) and inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs) (19)—and observed no significant differences between these subsets in both human and murine scRNA-seq datasets (fig. S1, B and C).

PAI1 is induced by hypoxia in pancreatic fibroblasts

CAFs are among the most abundant stromal cell types in PDAC. Because PAI1 is prominently expressed by CAFs (Fig. 1, D to F, H, and I) and hypoxia has been shown to promote PAI1 expression in other cell types (21, 22), we sought to examine the relationship between hypoxia and PAI1 expression in fibroblasts in human PDAC. To stratify fibroblasts by their exposure to hypoxia, we mapped the Hallmark hypoxia signature (MSigDB, 199 genes; SERPINE1 excluded) (23) onto the human PDAC scRNA-seq dataset (n = 16) (17) using gene module scoring (Fig. 2A). Compared with normoxic fibroblasts, hypoxic fibroblasts exhibited significantly higher SERPINE1 expression (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, scRNA-seq analysis of murine PDAC (n = 3) (18–20) showed elevated Serpine1 expression in hypoxic fibroblasts relative to normoxic fibroblasts (Fig. 2B). These observations suggest that hypoxia positively correlates with PAI1 expression in pancreatic CAFs.

To determine whether hypoxia regulates PAI1 expression, we isolated primary pancreatic stellate cells (PSCs), a precursor population of CAFs, from wild-type (WT) mice. PSCs were cultured alone or with PDAC tumor organoids and exposed to either normoxia (21% O2) or hypoxia (1% O2) (Fig. 2C). We used 1% O2 to model tumor hypoxia in vitro, as human pancreatic cancers exhibit oxygen concentrations of ~1% (13). Under hypoxic conditions, Serpine1 expression was up-regulated in PSCs (Fig. 2D). PAI1 protein levels were also elevated in conditioned media from hypoxic PSC cultures relative to their normoxic counterparts (Fig. 2E). Although hypoxia significantly increased PAI1 production in PSCs cultured alone, an increase in PAI1 expression in PSCs under hypoxia was even greater in the presence of cancer cells (Fig. 2, D and E). These observations were reproduced in a CAF line derived from mouse PDAC (fig. S2A). Notably, cancer cells increased Serpine1 expression in PSCs under physiological O2 conditions (8% O2), whereas Serpine1 levels were comparable between 8 and 21% O2 (fig. S2B), indicating that physiological oxygen does not affect Serpine1 expression. Together, these data suggest that hypoxia promotes PAI1 expression in CAFs, which is further augmented by cancer cell–derived factor(s).

Cells sense and adapt to hypoxia to survive, and these adaptive responses are primarily mediated by hypoxia-inducible factor 1α and 2α (HIF1α and HIF2α) (12). Because hypoxia induced PAI1 expression in PSCs and CAFs (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S2A), we hypothesized that HIF1α or HIF2α in fibroblasts may induce PAI1 expression under hypoxia. To test this, we exposed HIF-deficient PSCs to hypoxia and measured PAI1 expression. To generate HIF-deficient PSCs, we first crossed Hif1αfl/fl and Hif2αfl/fl mice (24, 25) with UBC-CreERT2 mice, in which a tamoxifen-inducible Cre construct is under the control of a ubiquitous promoter, leading to widespread recombination of floxed alleles after tamoxifen treatment (26). We then isolated PSCs from UBC-CreERT2;Hif1αfl/fl, UBC-CreERT2;Hif2αfl/fl, and UBC-CreERT2;Hif1αfl/fl;Hif2αfl/fl mice, and treated with 4-hydroxytamoxifen to generate Hif1α knockout, Hif2α knockout, and double-knockout PSCs (fig. S2, C and D). Compared with HIF-competent control PSCs, neither Hif1α nor Hif2α knockout individually impairs the hypoxic induction of Serpine1 in PSCs (Fig. 2F). However, double knockout of Hif1α and Hif2α significantly reduced Serpine1 induction under hypoxia (Fig. 2F). The double knockout also abrogated hypoxia-induced Serpine1 expression in PSCs when they were cocultured with tumor organoids (fig. S2E), consistent with PSCs cultured alone (Fig. 2F). These data suggest that both HIF1α and HIF2α in PSCs contribute to PAI1 up-regulation by hypoxia and can compensate for each other in this regulatory process.

Genetic ablation of stromal PAI1 suppresses PDAC growth

To determine the role of stromal PAI1 in pancreatic tumorigenesis, we orthotopically implanted pancreatic cancer cells [cell line 7940B; (27)] derived from the KrasLSL-G12D/+;Trp53LSL-R172H/+;Pdx1-Cre (KPC) mouse model of PDAC (28) into syngeneic Serpine1 knockout (Serpine1−/−) (29) and Serpine1+/+ C57BL/6 mice (Fig. 3A). Serpine1−/− mice were born at the expected Mendelian ratios (29) with histologically normal pancreatic tissue (fig. S3). However, tumors grown in PAI1-deficient mice were significantly smaller compared with tumors grown in Serpine1+/+ hosts (Fig. 3B), suggesting a protumorigenic role of stromal PAI1 in PDAC. Consistent with human tumors (Fig. 1, A and F, and fig. S1A), PAI1 protein levels were markedly elevated in mouse pancreatic tumor supernatants compared with normal pancreatic tissue supernatants (Fig. 3C). This PAI1 induction was observed in tumors from Serpine1+/+ mice but not in those from Serpine1−/− mice (Fig. 3C). Because the implanted tumor cells were PAI1-proficient, these findings strongly suggest that the majority of PAI1 protein in PDAC is produced and secreted from the stroma.

Given the previous literature linking PAI1 to fibrosis and angiogenesis (30–32), as well as our finding that fibroblasts and endothelial cells are the main cell types expressing PAI1 in PDAC (Fig. 1, D to F, H, and I), we evaluated the impact of stromal PAI1 loss on collagen deposition and vascular density. However, fibrillar collagen, as determined by picrosirius red staining, and blood vessel abundance, as determined by immunostaining for CD31, were comparable between tumors grown in Serpine1+/+ and Serpine1−/− mice (Fig. 3, D and E). We next assessed tumor cell proliferation and apoptosis in Serpine1+/+ and Serpine1−/− groups using co-IF. No significant differences were observed in the proliferation marker Ki67 within tumor cells (ECAD+) (Fig. 3F). However, tumor cell apoptosis, as indicated by cleaved caspase-3 staining, was significantly increased in tumors from Serpine1−/− mice (Fig. 3G), suggesting that tumor growth inhibition upon stromal PAI1 elimination is due to enhanced tumor cell death.

Protumorigenic effects of stromal PAI1 depend on CD8+ T cells

The pancreatic TME is highly immunosuppressive, limiting T cell infiltration and function (2). Given that CAFs can exert potent immunosuppressive effects (3) and that PAI1 is highly expressed in fibroblasts (Fig. 1, D to F, H, and I), we set out to characterize immunomodulation by PAI1 in PDAC. Flow cytometry analysis of orthotopic tumors for the identification of leukocyte populations revealed that stromal PAI1 elimination results in an increase in CD45+ immune cell infiltration (fig. S4A). Notably, frequencies of T cells and dendritic cells were significantly elevated in tumors grown in PAI1-deficient mice compared with tumors grown in control mice (Fig. 4, A and B). In contrast, frequencies of macrophages, neutrophils, and B cells were not significantly different between tumors from Serpine1+/+ and Serpine1−/− mice (fig. S4, B to D). Given that PAI1 regulates macrophage polarization (33) and tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are key drivers of immunosuppression (3), we tested whether TAM phenotypes are altered in Serpine1−/− tumors. Co-IF staining for F4/80, a macrophage marker, and arginase 1 (Arg1), a marker of immunosuppressive TAMs, revealed a reduction in the number of Arg1+ TAMs as well as total TAMs in tumors from Serpine1−/− mice (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, recombinant PAI1 increased Arg1 expression in bone marrow–derived macrophages (BMDMs) (Fig. 4D). Together, these data suggest that stromal PAI1 ablation alleviates immunosuppressive TAM phenotypes.

We further investigated changes in T cells and observed increased CD8+ T cell accumulation in Serpine1−/− tumors (Fig. 4E), whereas frequencies of CD4+ T cells and CD4+Foxp3+ regulatory T (Treg) cells were unchanged (fig. S4, E and F). Using immunohistochemistry (IHC), we confirmed that stromal ablation of PAI1 increases CD8+ T cell accumulation in PDAC (fig. S4G). When we repeated the orthotopic syngeneic transplantation experiment with a different PDAC cell line (1680), we again observed reduced tumor growth and increased CD8+ T cell accumulation in Serpine1−/− mice relative to control hosts (fig. S5).

To determine whether stromal PAI1 affects T cell function, we performed ex vivo phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin stimulation of whole tumor digests from Serpine1+/+ and Serpine1−/− mice. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that CD8+ T cells from Serpine1−/− tumors were more responsive to stimulation, as indicated by a significantly higher frequency of cells expressing granzyme B (GZMB), a cytotoxicity marker, in Serpine1−/− tumors compared with control tumors (Fig. 4F). Moreover, CD8+ T cells in Serpine1−/− tumors were more proliferative than their counterparts in Serpine1+/+ tumors (Fig. 4G). Co-IF staining for CD8 with GZMB (Fig. 4H) and Ki67 (Fig. 4I) on tumor sections further confirmed increased CD8+ GZMB levels and elevated CD8+ T cell proliferation within Serpine1−/− tumors. Together, these findings suggest that stromal PAI1 ablation relieves immunosuppressive TAM phenotypes, promotes CD8+ T cell and dendritic cell accumulation, and enhances the cytotoxic activity and proliferative capacity of CD8+ T cells.

Last, to determine whether the reduction in tumor weight resulting from stromal PAI1 elimination depends on CD8+ T cells, we depleted CD8+ T cells by administering an anti-CD8 antibody to Serpine1+/+ and Serpine1−/− mice bearing orthotopic tumors (Fig. 4J and fig. S6). CD8+ T cell depletion did not affect tumor growth in Serpine1+/+ mice (Fig. 4K). In contrast, CD8+ T cell depletion rescued the tumor growth defect in Serpine1−/− mice, restoring tumor weights to levels observed in Serpine1+/+ mice (Fig. 4K). These results suggest that the tumor growth reduction caused by stromal PAI1 ablation requires CD8+ T cells, although other cell types may also contribute.

tPA is mainly expressed in fibroblasts in human and mouse PDAC

PAI1 is the primary physiological inhibitor of tPA, which converts plasminogen into the clot-dissolving enzyme plasmin (34). To investigate whether the impact of stromal PAI1 on CD8+ T cell exclusion and tumor growth is mediated by tPA, we first examined the expression of the tPA gene PLAT in human PDAC using bulk RNA-seq data from human normal pancreatic tissue (n = 167, GTEx) (15) and PDAC samples (n = 169, TCGA-PAAD) (16). We observed a significant increase in PLAT transcript levels in PDAC compared with normal pancreatic tissue (Fig. 5A). However, PLAT expression was not associated with PDAC patient survival (Fig. 5B). Next, we mapped the expression of PLAT across the PDAC microenvironment using the human PDAC scRNA-seq dataset (n = 16) (17). PLAT expression was observed in CK19+ECAD+ malignant cells and endothelial cells; however, fibroblasts showed the highest proportion of PLAT-expressing cells (Fig. 5, C and D). To validate the expression of the tPA protein, we performed co-IF staining of resected human PDAC and matched adjacent normal tissues for tPA, PDGFRα/β, and PanCK. We observed an abundance of tPA in PDGFRα/β+ fibroblasts in PDAC regions, with little to no signal in adjacent normal regions (Fig. 5E and fig. S7A). tPA+ cells were also found among PanCK+ tumor cells (Fig. 5E), consistent with the scRNA-seq findings (Fig. 5, C and D).

To determine whether the expression pattern of tPA in human PDAC is conserved in murine PDAC, we analyzed the scRNA-seq dataset from autochthonous mouse PDAC tissues (n = 3) (18–20). As in human PDAC, Plat expression was most prominent in fibroblasts (Fig. 5, F and G). Similar to SERPINE1, PLAT expression did not differ significantly between iCAF and myCAF populations (fig. S7, B and C). Overall, these data suggest that tPA is most abundantly expressed in fibroblasts in both human and mouse PDAC.

Genetic ablation of stromal tPA inhibits CD8+ T cell infiltration and accelerates PDAC growth

To investigate the role of stromal tPA in PDAC, we orthotopically implanted PDAC cells [cell line 7940B; (27)] into Plat knockout (Plat−/−) (35) and Plat+/+ mice (Fig. 6A). Plat−/− mice develop normally (35) and exhibit histologically normal pancreatic tissue (fig. S8). Despite the aggressive nature of this PDAC model, stromal ablation of tPA significantly increased tumor weight (Fig. 6B), implicating a tumor-suppressive role for stromal tPA in PDAC. Consistent with elevated tPA expression in human PDAC (Fig. 5, A and E, and fig. S7A), tumor supernatants from Plat+/+ mice showed substantially higher levels of tPA protein relative to normal pancreas supernatants (Fig. 6C). Furthermore, tPA protein levels were significantly lower in tumor supernatants from Plat−/− mice compared with those from Plat+/+ mice (7.5 ng/ml versus 24.6 ng/ml; Fig. 6C). Given that the implanted tumor cells were tPA-proficient, these results suggest that tPA protein is produced and secreted to a greater extent by stromal cells rather than by tumor cells.

We next examined the impact of stromal tPA loss on the TME. Stromal tPA ablation led to increased collagen deposition without affecting vascular density (Fig. 6D and fig. S9A). Similar to stromal PAI1 ablation, stromal tPA loss did not alter tumor cell proliferation (fig. S9B); however, it showed a trend toward decreased tumor cell apoptosis (Fig. 6E), suggesting that enhanced tumor cell survival may contribute to the accelerated tumor growth upon stromal Plat deletion.

To investigate the effects of stromal tPA on tumor immunity, we performed flow cytometry immunophenotyping of tumors from Plat+/+ and Plat−/− mice. We observed no significant changes in total immune infiltrates (fig. S9C). Macrophage, neutrophil, and B cell frequencies also showed no differences (fig. S9, D to F). Notably, we observed a trending reduction in T cells in tumors lacking stromal tPA (Fig. 6F), accompanied by significant decreases in CD8+ T cells and dendritic cells (Fig. 6, G and H). Frequencies of CD4+ T cells and CD4+Foxp3+ Treg cells were not affected (fig. S9, G and H). IHC further confirmed that stromal tPA ablation significantly reduces CD8+ T cell accumulation in PDAC (fig. S9I). The number of Arg1+ TAMs was increased in Plat−/− tumors (Fig. 6I and fig. S9J), suggesting that stromal tPA elimination promotes immunosuppressive TAM phenotypes. Using a different PDAC cell line (1680) in the orthotopic transplantation model, we similarly observed increased tumor growth and reduced CD8+ T cell accumulation in Plat−/− mice compared with control hosts (fig. S10). Collectively, these results suggest that stromal tPA is tumor-restrictive and plays a critical role in promoting CD8+ T cell infiltration within the PDAC microenvironment.

To explore the relationship between hypoxia and tPA expression, we assessed PLAT levels in normoxic and hypoxic fibroblast clusters from the human PDAC scRNA-seq dataset (n = 16) (17), stratified by the module score for the Hallmark hypoxia signature (MSigDB, 200 genes) (23). Hypoxic fibroblasts exhibited significantly higher PLAT expression compared with normoxic fibroblasts (fig. S11A), indicating a positive correlation between hypoxia and tPA expression in fibroblasts within human PDAC. On the other hand, scRNA-seq analysis of the murine PDAC (n = 3) (18–20) showed no differences in Plat levels between normoxic and hypoxic fibroblasts (fig. S11B). Hypoxic incubation of mouse PSCs led to a statistically significant but moderate increase in Plat mRNA expression (fig. S11C); however, tPA protein levels remained unchanged under hypoxia (fig. S11D). Plat expression was comparable between 8% O2 and 21% O2 (fig. S11E). These data suggest that although hypoxia may contribute to tPA regulation, additional mechanism(s) likely control its expression in PDAC.

Tumor growth and immunosuppression driven by stromal PAI1 are tPA-dependent

Given that PAI1 negatively regulates tPA activity, the opposing phenotypes of tumors lacking stromal PAI1 versus stromal tPA suggest that PAI1 promotes pancreatic tumorigenesis by inhibiting tPA. To test this, we generated Serpine1 and Plat double-knockout (Serpine1−/−;Plat−/−) mice and performed orthotopic PDAC implantation (Fig. 7A). As expected, tumors in Serpine1−/− mice were smaller than those in WT control mice (Fig. 7B). Notably, tumors in Serpine1−/−;Plat−/− mice were significantly larger than those in Serpine1−/− mice but remained smaller than WT tumors (Fig. 7B), indicating that the reduced tumor growth caused by Serpine1 deletion is partially, yet significantly, rescued by Plat deletion.

Consistent with this rescue of tumor growth, CD8+ T cell accumulation was reduced in Serpine1−/−;Plat−/− tumors compared with Serpine1−/− tumors, although levels remained higher than in WT controls (Fig. 7C). Moreover, Plat deletion was sufficient to increase the numbers of Arg1+ TAMs and total TAMs relative to Serpine1−/− tumors, approaching those observed in WT tumors (Fig. 7D). Together, these findings provide strong genetic evidence that stromal PAI1 promotes immunosuppression and tumor progression, at least in part, via tPA inhibition.

DISCUSSION

PDAC is characterized by a highly desmoplastic, immunosuppressive, and hypoxic TME, which contributes to its profound resistance to therapy (2). Here, we show that PAI1 is up-regulated in PDAC and predominantly expressed in the stromal compartment. Furthermore, we demonstrate that stromal PAI1 promotes immunosuppressive macrophage accumulation and restricts CD8+ T cell recruitment in PDAC, contributing to tumor growth.

Increased PAI1 expression has been associated with poor clinical outcomes across multiple tumor types (7). The cell types that produce PAI1, as well as the mechanisms by which it promotes tumorigenesis, vary depending on the tumor type (8–10). In PDAC, knockdown of Serpine1 in tumor cells reduces orthotopic tumor growth, immunosuppression, and fibrosis (9). PAI1 has also been shown to function as an autophagy receptor in pancreatic cancer cells, promoting Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homologue (KRAS) degradation (36). Using scRNA-seq analysis, co-IF staining, and enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), we demonstrated that PAI1 is primarily expressed in the stroma rather than in cancer cells within PDAC. To mark fibroblasts, we used PDGFRα/β, which is also expressed in pericytes, other mesenchymal cells, and possibly a subset of tumor cells (37, 38). However, the dramatic increase in PAI1 protein observed in orthotopic tumors from PAI1-proficient—but not PAI1-deficient—hosts indicates that stromal cells are the major source of PAI1 in PDAC. Genetic ablation of stromal PAI1 significantly impaired PDAC growth and increased tumor cell apoptosis, accompanied by enhanced CD8+ T cell infiltration and cytotoxic activity. It will be interesting to investigate whether stromal PAI1 can directly affect tumor cell survival and whether it regulates other forms of cell death, such as necroptosis and ferroptosis, which can differently influence antitumor immunity (39). CD8+ T cell depletion reversed the tumor growth suppression observed in PAI1-deficient stroma, indicating that inhibition of antitumor immunity is a key mechanism by which stromal PAI1 promotes pancreatic tumorigenesis. Given the abundance of CAFs in the stroma and our finding identifying fibroblasts as a major source of PAI1 in PDAC, these results underscore the critical role of CAFs in modulating antitumor immunity. Further studies using fibroblast-specific Serpine1 deletion are warranted to precisely define the role of CAF-derived PAI1 in immunosuppression and tumor progression.

CAFs are the main producers of ECM proteins, growth factors, and cytokines. ECM-driven desmoplasia increases interstitial pressure, leading to blood vessel collapse and hypoxia (40, 41). Reciprocally, hypoxia modulates CAF phenotype and function, as well as angiogenesis (42–44). We and others have observed considerable intratumoral hypoxia heterogeneity in PDAC (42, 45, 46), which likely underlies PAI1 expression in only a fraction of CAFs, supporting a potential role for hypoxia in regulating CAF heterogeneity. It would be of interest to explore the impact of hypoxia-induced PAI1 on vascular function and ECM composition and remodeling using three-dimensional (3D) cocultures with physiologically relevant ECM (47, 48), as well as in vivo models.

In the classical coagulation/fibrinolysis cascade, PAI1 binds to tPA and inhibits its activity (34). Although tPA overexpression in human pancreatic cancer cells promotes tumor growth in nude mice (49) and its knockdown impairs proliferation in vitro (50), the role of tPA in PDAC under immune-competent conditions has remained unexplored. Here, we show that tPA expression is enriched in fibroblasts and significantly up-regulated in PDAC compared with normal pancreatic tissues. Unexpectedly, stromal loss of tPA exacerbated tumor growth and reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration, revealing a previously unrecognized tumor-suppressive role for tPA in PDAC. The opposing effects of PAI1 loss versus tPA loss, along with the partial yet significant rescue of PAI1-deficiency phenotypes by tPA ablation, suggest that PAI1 drives immune evasion and tumor progression, at least in part by suppressing tPA activity. Although future studies are needed to fully define the tPA-dependent and tPA-independent functions of PAI1 as well as the potential differential contributions of cancer cell– and stroma-derived tPA in PDAC, our findings support the notion that tPA is up-regulated and capable of promoting antitumor immunity, yet its tumor-suppressive activity is largely masked by elevated PAI1 (Fig. 7E). In line with this, tPA expression did not correlate with PDAC patient outcomes, whereas high PAI1 expression was associated with significantly poorer overall survival. Thus, targeting PAI1 may unleash tPA-driven antitumor immune responses and improve the efficacy of immunotherapy in patients with PDAC.

The impact of tPA on antitumor immunity has not been previously reported. However, tPA ablation in animal models of neuroinflammation has been shown to reduce CD4+ T cell abundance (51, 52). Although the mechanisms underlying CD4+ T cell migration and function remain largely unknown, tPA treatment promotes proliferation of splenic T cells in vitro (51), implicating potential direct T cell modulation by tPA. In PDAC, TAMs are abundant and exert potent immunosuppressive functions (3). In addition to TAM-mediated immunosuppression, a scarcity of dendritic cells in PDAC is a major contributor to impaired antitumor T cell responses (53). We observed that stromal PAI1 ablation attenuates immunosuppressive TAM phenotypes and increases intratumoral accumulation of dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells, whereas stromal tPA ablation promotes immunosuppressive TAM phenotypes and reduces the abundance of dendritic cells and CD8+ T cells. These findings suggest that PAI1 and tPA may regulate CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation by modulating TAM polarization and dendritic cell trafficking. Future studies will aim to define the molecular mechanisms underlying PAI1/tPA-mediated immune regulation in PDAC. Notably, genetic ablation of plasminogen, a downstream target of tPA in the fibrinolysis cascade, has been shown to reduce orthotopic PDAC growth without affecting T cell infiltration (54); therefore, it is likely that the immunomodulatory effects of PAI1 and tPA are independent of their canonical coagulant or fibrinolytic activities.

In summary, our data identify the stromal PAI1-tPA axis as a critical regulator of antitumor immunity and tumor progression in PDAC. We further demonstrate that PAI1 expression in pancreatic fibroblasts is induced by hypoxia in a HIF-dependent manner, implicating hypoxia-driven stromal reprogramming as a mechanism of immune suppression via PAI1 up-regulation. Our findings provide deeper insights into the interplay between hypoxia, fibrinolysis factors, and immunosuppression, highlighting the potential of targeting PAI1-tPA axis to enhance tumor vulnerability to immune-directed therapies in PDAC.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Human tissue samples

Surgical specimens were obtained from patients with PDAC who underwent Whipple or distal pancreatectomy. Written informed consent forms were obtained from the patients. The studies were approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the University of Michigan Medical School (protocol number HUM00098128) and were conducted in accordance with recognized ethical guidelines.

Mice

All animal protocols were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Michigan (protocol number PRO00012213). The Serpine1−/−, Plat−/−, Hif1αfl/fl, Hif2αfl/fl, and UBC-CreERT2 strains have been previously described (24–26, 29, 35), and WT mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (no. 000664). All mice were on the C57BL/6 genetic background. Male and female mice aged 8 to 12 weeks were enrolled for animal experiments and used for primary cell isolation.

Cell lines and cell culture

All cell lines were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; no. MT10017CV, Corning) containing 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS; no. A5209501, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (no. 15140122, Gibco). Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma using the MycoAlert PLUS Mycoplasma Detection Kit (no. LT07-710, Lonza), and cell lines used in orthotopic surgeries tested negatively prior to injection.

The 7940B (provided by G. L. Beatty) (27) and mT3 (provided by D. A. Tuveson) (55) pancreatic cancer cell lines were derived from primary murine KPC (KrasLSL-G12D/+;Trp53LSL-R172H/+;Pdx1-Cre) PDAC. The 1680 pancreatic cancer cell line was derived from primary murine KPC (KrasLSL-G12D/+;Trp53LSL-R172H/+;p48-Cre) PDAC. The FB1 CAF line (56) was generated from an iKras* p53* mouse (57) by fluorescence-activated cell sorting of PDGFRα+;EPCAM− cells. The 7940B, mT3, 1680, and FB1 cell lines were cultured no more than 15 to 20 passages.

PSC isolation and 3D culture

PSCs were isolated from WT mice (no. 000664, the Jackson Laboratory) as previously described (42). Briefly, pancreatic tissue was minced and digested for 30 min at 37°C in Gey’s balanced salt solution (GBSS) containing 0.05% collagenase P (no. 11213857001, Sigma-Aldrich), 0.02% pronase (no. 10165921001, Sigma-Aldrich), and 0.1% deoxyribonuclease I (no. 10104159001, Sigma-Aldrich). The digested tissue was filtered through a 100-μm cell strainer (no. 352360, Falcon) and washed with 0.3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in GBSS. After centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in 0.3% BSA/GBSS and mixed with an equal volume of 28.7% Nycodenz (no. AN1002423, Accurate Chemical) prepared in GBSS without NaCl. BSA/GBSS (0.3%) was gently layered on top of the cell-Nycodenz suspension to create a discontinuous gradient. The gradient was centrifuged at 1400g for 20 min at 4°C with no brake. Cells were collected from the interface between the Nycodenz and GBSS layers and washed with 0.3% BSA/GBSS.

For the experiments testing the effects of HIF1α and HIF2α on the expression of PAI1 and tPA, PSCs were isolated from Hif1αfl/fl, UBC-CreERT2;Hif1α fl/fl, Hif2α fl/fl, UBC-CreERT2;Hif2α fl/fl, Hif1α fl/fl;Hif2α fl/fl, and UBC-CreERT2;Hif1α fl/fl;Hif2α fl/fl mice and treated with 0.5 μM 4-hydroxytamoxifen (no. H7904-5 mg, Sigma-Aldrich) for 5 days, starting 3 days postisolation. Primary PSC lines between passage 2 and 4 were used for all experiments.

For 3D cocultures, PSCs were seeded in Matrigel (no. 356230, Corning) in a transwell insert (no. 662610, Greiner Bio-One) and cultured with PDAC tumor organoids in the lower compartment of the 24-well plate in DMEM containing 5% FBS (no. A5209501, Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (no. 15140122, Gibco).

BMDM generation

Bone marrow was isolated from the femurs of WT mice (no. 000664, the Jackson Laboratory) as previously described (58). Once isolated, BM cells were cultured for 5 days in DMEM (no. MT10017CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (no. A5209501, Gibco), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (no. 15140122, Gibco), and macrophage colony-stimulating factor (MCSF) (25 ng/ml; no. 3150250UG, Gibco). The resulting BMDMs were treated with recombinant mouse PAI1 (no. IMSPAI1RSM500UG, Innovative Research) at a concentration of 1.2 μg/ml for 20 hours and then harvested for RNA extraction.

Orthotopic surgeries

To establish the orthotopic pancreatic cancer model, 5 × 104 7940B or 7.5 × 104 1680 cells were injected into Serpine1−/−, Plat−/−, Serpine1−/−;Plat−/−, or control littermates. Male mice were used for all experiments using 7940B cells, and female mice were used for all experiments with 1680 cells to match the biological sex of the source cell line in each model. Cancer cells were injected into the tail of the pancreas in a 40-μl volume of 50% Matrigel (no. 356234, Corning) and 50% RPMI (no. 11875093, Gibco). Mice were euthanized, and tumors were collected at the time points specified in the figure legends, either 18 or 21 days postimplantation.

Depletion of CD8+ T cells were performed starting from 5 days following orthotopic injection of 7940B cells by administration of intraperitoneal injections of 200 μg of anti-mouse CD8α mAb (2.43, no. BE0061, BioXCell) or control isotype antibody (LTF-2, no. BE0090, BioXCell) twice per week for the entire course of the experiments.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from cells using the RNeasy mini kit (no. 74104, QIAGEN). cDNA was synthesized using a High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (no. 4368814, Applied Biosystems). Polymerase chain reactions (PCRs) were performed using SYBR Green PCR reagents (no. A25742, Applied Biosystems) mixed with cDNAs and primers in a QuantStudio Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems). Expression levels were normalized by Rps18. The following primer sequences were used: Arg1 forward 5′-CATTGGCTTGCGAGACGTAGAC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCTGAAGGTCTCTTCCATCACC-3′; Hif1α forward 5′-AGCAAGATCTCGGCGAAGCA-3′ and reverse 5′-TGTGGGGAAGTGGCAACTGA-3′; Hif2α forward 5′-AGGAGAAATCCCGTGATGCCG-3′ and reverse 5′-GGGGCAACTCATGAGCCAAC-3′; Plat forward 5′-AGCACTCTCGGGACACAGAA-3′ and reverse 5′-GTCTGCGTTGGCTCATCTCT-3′; Rps18 forward 5′-CGGAAAATAGCCTTCGCCATCAC-3′ and reverse 5′-ATCACTCGCTCCACCTCATCCT-3′; Serpine1 forward 5′-CCTCTTCCACAAGTCTGATGGC-3′ and reverse 5′-GCAGTTCCACAAC-GTCATACTCG-3′.

Histology, IHC, IF, and microscopy

Tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; 4°C, overnight) and processed for paraffin-embedding. For histological analysis, deparaffinized sections (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin or picric acid–sirius red followed by alcohol dehydration series and mounting.

For IHC, slides were quenched in 0.6% hydrogen peroxide/methanol for 15 min and boiled for 20 min in 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval. Sections were blocked with 5% serum/1% BSA/0.5% Tween 20 for 1 hour. Slides were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. Following the primary antibody, slides were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies (no. BA1000, Vector Laboratories) followed by ABC solution (no. PK6100, Vector Laboratories) and developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (no. SK4100, Vector Laboratories). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, mounted with Permount (no. SP15-500, Fisher Scientific), and then examined on an Olympus IX73 microscope. The following antibodies were used: CD8 (D4W2Z, 1:400, no. 98941S, Cell Signaling) and CD31 (D8V9E, 1:400, no. 77699S, Cell Signaling).

For IF, slides were boiled for 20 min in 10 mM sodium citrate (pH 6.0) for antigen retrieval and blocked with 5% serum in PBS for 1 hour. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA in PBS overnight at 4°C and were then incubated with Alexa Fluor–labeled secondary antibodies (Invitrogen) diluted in 1% BSA in PBS for 1 hour. Nuclei were stained by incubating slides in 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; no. D1306, Invitrogen) and mounted in Prolong Glass antifade reagent (no. P36980, Invitrogen). The Tyramide SuperBoost Kit (no. B40943 or no. B40944, Invitrogen) was used when staining with multiple primary antibodies raised in the same host species. Fluorescence images were acquired using an Olympus IX73 microscope. The following antibodies were used: Arg1 (D4E3M, 1:400, no. 93668S, Cell Signaling), cleaved caspase-3 (polyclonal, 1:100, no. 9661S, Cell Signaling), CD8 (D4W2Z, 1:200, no. 98941S, Cell Signaling), ECAD (36, 1:1000, no. 610181, BD Biosciences), F4/80 (D2S9R, 1:200, no. 70076S, Cell Signaling), GZMB (D6E9W, 1:100, no. 46890S, Cell Signaling), Ki67 (SP6, 1:200, no. MA5-14520, Invitrogen), PAI1 (polyclonal, 1:1000, no. IRBAHUPAI1AP100UG, Innovative Research), PanCK (monoclonal antibody cocktail, 1:400, no. NBP2-44368-0.1 mg, Novus), PDGFRα/β (Y92, 1:100, no. AB32570, Abcam), and tPA (polyclonal, 1:250, no. 10147-1-AP, Proteintech).

Images were quantified using ImageJ/FIJI (59). At least three separate fields of view (FOV) per section were captured and quantified.

Flow cytometry

Tissues were minced into small fragments and incubated in collagenase V (1 mg/ml; no. C9263, Sigma-Aldrich) in RPMI 1640 (no. 11875093, Gibco) at 37°C for 1 hour. Dissociated cells were passed through a 70-μm cell strainer and washed twice in PBS containing 0.5% FBS and 0.2 mM EDTA (fluorescence-activated cell sorting buffer). Fc receptors were blocked by incubating cells with rat anti-mouse CD16/CD32 antibodies (no. 156604, BioLegend) for 5 min on ice. Cell surface markers were then labeled with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 30 min on ice. Dead cells were excluded by staining with a fixable viability stain (no. 423102, BioLegend) for 15 min on ice. Staining for intracellular Foxp3 was performed using mouse Treg cell staining kit (no. 88-8118-40, Invitrogen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. For experiments measuring GZMB and Ki67 of CD8+ T cells, single-cell suspensions were incubated for 5 hours at 37°C with brefeldin A (5 μg/ml), PMA (50 ng/ml), and ionomycin (1 μg/ml). After incubation, cells were stained with cell surface markers as described above, and intracellular staining was performed using the Transcription Factor Buffer Set (no. 424401, BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Flow cytometry was performed on a ZE5 Cell Analyzer (Bio-Rad), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software. Antibodies used for flow cytometry were as follows: BV421-conjugated anti-CD3 (145-2C11, 1:100, no. 100341, BioLegend), PE-Cy7–conjugated anti-CD4 (RM4-5, 1:200, no. 100527, BioLegend), AF700-conjugated anti-CD8a (53-6.7, 1:300, no. 100730, BioLegend), APC-Cy7–conjugated anti-CD19 (6D5, 1:100, no. 115530, BioLegend), PE-Cy7–conjugated anti-CD11b (M1/70, 1:300, no. 101216, BioLegend), BV421-conjugated anti-CD11c (N418, 1:200, no. 117343, BioLegend), PE-conjugated anti-CD25 (PC61.5, 1:200, no. 12-0251-81, Invitrogen), PerCP-Cy5.5–conjugated anti-CD45 (30-F11, 1:200, no. 103132, BioLegend), APC-conjugated anti-F4/80 (BM8, 1:100, no. 123116, BioLegend), APC-conjugated anti-FoxP3 (FJK-16s, 1:50, 17-5773-82, Invitrogen), PE-Cy7–conjugated anti-GZMB (NGZB, 1:200, no. 25-8998-82, Invitrogen), APC/Fire 750–conjugated anti-Ki67 (16A8, 1:200, no. 652429, BioLegend), AF700-conjugated anti-Ly6G (1A8, 1:400, no. 127622, BioLegend), and APC-Cy7–conjugated anti-MHCII (M5/114.15.2, 1:300, no. 107628, BioLegend).

Tissue supernatant collection

Tissues were harvested, washed in PBS, weighed, and minced into small fragments with sterile scissors. Tissue supernatant was harvested by adding PBS (400 μl/100 mg tissue) and compressing tissue fragments with the plunger of a 3-ml syringe. The tissue solution was centrifuged at 18,000g for 7 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was carefully collected.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

For ELISA analysis of conditioned media, 3D cultures of PSCs and pancreatic cancer cells were grown under 21% O2 or 1% O2 for 72 hours, and the resulting conditioned media were collected and spun down. The conditioned media and tissue supernatants were assayed using the following ELISA kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions: mouse PAI1 ELISA kit (no. IMSPAIKTT, Innovative Research) and mouse tPA ELISA kit (no. IMSTPAKTT, Innovative Research).

TGCA analysis

For the analysis of gene expression in human normal pancreas and PDAC samples, we used standardized TCGA (16) and GTEx (15) data obtained from the University of California, Santa Cruz (UCSC) Xena Browser (https://xena.ucsc.edu/). All TCGA samples that were not confirmed as PDAC were removed. RNA-seq data were transformed from transcripts per million (TPM) to log2(TPM + 1) for analysis. Gene expression comparisons between normal pancreas and PDAC samples were made using Mann-Whitney test, and plots were generated with the R package ggplot2 version 3.5.2.

Clinical information and gene-expression data for human pancreatic cancer (TCGA-PAAD) were accessed from the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Genomic Data Commons Data Portal using R version 4.5.0, RStudio version 2024.12.1 + 563, and the R package TCGAbiolinks version 2.36.0. The survival analysis was performed using the R package survival version 3.8-3, and Kaplan-Meier survival plots were generated and compared using log-rank test with the R package survminer version 0.5.0.

scRNA-seq analysis

The human and murine scRNA-seq data were previously published and are from the following publicly available sources: human PDAC, NIH dbGaP no. PHS002071.v1.p1 (17); and murine spontaneous PDAC, NIH GEO no. GSM6127792 (18), GSE129455 (19), and GSM3577885 (20). When scRNA-seq datasets from different sources and/or run dates were merged, batch correction was performed using the IntegrateData workflow in Seurat (60). The merged, batch-corrected datasets were from Donahue and colleagues (61). Downstream analysis was performed using R version 4.5.0, RStudio version 2024.12.1 + 563, and the R package Seurat version 5.2.1. Hypoxia signature scoring was performed using the “HALLMARK_HYPOXIA” gene set (MSigDB, 199 genes; SERPINE1 excluded) and Seurat’s “AddModuleScore” function. The human and murine PDAC fibroblast scRNA-seq data were divided evenly into three groups based on the hypoxia signature score. Fibroblasts are classified as hypoxic when their module score is in the top tertile, and normoxic when it is in the bottom tertile. Murine CAF subtyping into myCAFs and iCAFs was performed using signatures from Elyada and colleagues (19). Human CAF subtype annotations are from Kemp and colleagues (56). To compare gene expression profiles between groups, we used FindMarkers and Mann-Whitney test with Bonferroni correction.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Normality of data distribution was tested using D’Agostino–Pearson test and/or Shapiro-Wilk test. Mann-Whitney test, t test, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way ANOVA, and log-rank test were performed as indicated in figure legends, based on data distribution and number of groups and variables tested. Holm-Sidak post hoc correction was applied for multiple comparisons. P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample sizes, experiments were not randomized, and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
