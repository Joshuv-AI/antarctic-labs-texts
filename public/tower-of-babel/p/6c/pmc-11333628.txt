Morbidity after accelerated enhanced recovery protocol for colon cancer surgery
Authors: Misha A. T. Sier, Sarah L. Dekkers, Thaís T. T. Tweed, Maikel J. A. M. Bakens, Johan Nel, James van Bastelaar, Jan Willem Greve, Jan H. M. B. Stoot
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Previous studies showed that accelerated enhanced recovery programs (ERPs) with discharge 1–3 days after colorectal surgery are feasible for specific patients without compromising patients’ safety. This study aimed to examine the incidence, severity, and treatment of complications after treatment according to an accelerated ERP (CHASE). This accelerated ERP consisted of adjustments in pre-, peri- and postoperative care. Patients treated according to the CHASE protocol were compared to a retrospective cohort of patients who received standard ERAS care. The primary outcome was the rate of severe complications. The overall complication rates were similar in both cohorts (CHASE 30.7% vs ERAS 31.4%, p = 0.958) as well as severe complications (CHASE 20.9% vs ERAS 21.4%, p = 0.950). Among the 113 patients with a complicated course, the readmission rate was significantly higher in the CHASE cohort (41.9% vs 21.4%, p = 0.020). LOS after readmission was longer in the CHASE cohort (p = 0.018), but the total LOS was shorter (4 versus 6 days, p = 0.001). This study demonstrates that accelerated recovery can be safe for ASA I-II patients and has the potential to become a standard of care. Moreover, the CHASE protocol proved to be beneficial in terms of total LOS for patients with complications.

Introduction

The positive effects of the enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) protocol have piqued the interest of clinicians to further accelerate recovery by improving perioperative care1–3.

Accelerated enhanced recovery protocols (ERPs) have been shown to be successful and safe for patients undergoing colorectal surgery1–4. Yet, widespread implementation is limited due to concerns about increased readmissions, potential delays in the treatment of complications, and uncertainty about the severity of complications.

Recent studies showed that an accelerated enhanced recovery protocol (ERP) is feasible for specific patients without compromising patient safety, with discharge of all patients on the day of surgery to three days after surgery5,6. Accelerated ERP can yield numerous advantages, such as a faster return to their habitual practice, decreased utilization of healthcare resources, a potential reduction in the risk of exposure to hospital pathogens, and the option to recover in the company of family or the comfort recovery in a familiar home environment6. The previously studied accelerated ERPs assessed protocols with adjusted preoperative care, e.g. preoperative analgesia with trans abdominal plane (TAP) block during surgery, adjusted postoperative care with home visits by a domiciliary nurse, or monitoring using a smartphone application4,7–11. Due to this heterogeneity in protocols and differences in studied patient populations, the optimal protocol and patient selection are difficult to determine.

Moreover, little is known about the postoperative course after ERPs. Therefore, there is a certain ambiguity about whether accelerated ERPs affect the incidence and severity of complications. Hence, clinicians are reluctant to implement accelerated ERPs after colorectal surgery. If there is convincing scientific evidence that ERPs are safe, ERPs are likely to be implemented in perioperative care guidelines for colorectal cancer surgery. To further investigate the safety of ERPs, a comparison of incidence and severity of complications is necessary.

The aim of this study was to determine whether accelerated enhanced recovery after surgery impacts the occurrence and severity of complications after surgery for colon cancer. For this study, the difference in the incidence, severity, treatment of complications, and recovery after treatment according to the locally studied ERP (aCcelerated 23-H enhAnced recovery after colon SurgEry; CHASE3) was compared with standard ERAS care.

The primary LOS of patients suffering from a complication was significantly shorter in the CHASE cohort compared with ERAS (1 day vs. 5 days, p = 0.001). During primary admission, 15 patients in the CHASE cohort and 52 patients in the ERAS cohort developed complications. For these patients, the median LOS after the discovery of the complication until discharge was 2 days (IQR 1–4) and 5 days (IQR 3–8), respectively (p = 0.002).

Eighteen patients (41.9%) in the CHASE cohort were readmitted, compared with 15 patients in the ERAS cohort (21.4%) (p = 0.020). For CHASE patients, their median LOS during readmission was significantly longer compared with the ERAS patients, 6 versus 2 days respectively.

The total LOS in the CHASE cohort was four days (IQR 1–7) and six days in the ERAS cohort (IQR 4–11) (p = 0.254). No difference was observed in the incidence of functional recovery 90 days after surgery comparing both cohorts, see Table 3.

Discussion

While an increasing number of studies demonstrate the feasibility and safety of accelerated ERPs after colon cancer surgery, little is known about the incidence and severity of the complications in these programs. The purpose of this retrospective analysis was to investigate the incidence and severity of complications after treatment according to an accelerated EPR protocol (CHASE) in comparison with standard ERAS care for colon cancer surgery. Both incidence and severity of complications were similar in both groups. No failure to rescue occurred. Although readmissions were higher and LOS during readmission longer, the total LOS was shorter in the CHASE cohort.

When considering the entire patient population, the overall 90-day complication rate of the CHASE cohort was higher compared with some previous accelerated ERP studies2–4, but similar to the findings of Bednarski et al.6 and lower than Studniarek et al.12. This difference could be explained by the fact that other ERP studies also included patients with benign diseases7,8,13–17 or excluded patients with T4 tumors9 or significant comorbidities such as insulin-dependent diabetes7–9, and consisted of a younger patient population7,8,13–16,18. Moreover, most studies only reported complications within 30 days after surgery instead of 90 days7–9,6–13,17,19. The most common complications were rectal blood loss, electrolyte deficiency, anastomotic leakage, and urinary retention. In the ERAS cohort, pulmonary and cardiac complications were also relatively frequent. Most complications in the CHASE cohort were unlikely to be directly related to the accelerated ERP apart from urinary retention. The CHASE protocol involves spinal anesthesia and the insertion of a urinary catheter. Spinal anesthesia has been associated with an increase in postoperative urinary retention20. This disadvantage of spinal anesthesia must be weighed against the advantages being the low pain scores and limited opioid use [unpublished data]. The lower rate of pulmonary and cardiac complications in the CHASE population may be attributable to the fact that CHASE patients start mobilizing early after surgery21–23.

The rate of severe complications among accelerated ERP patients was 6%, which corresponds to previous findings4,9. Severe complications were less frequent in the accelerated ERP cohort compared with the ERAS cohort, which affirms the safety of accelerated ERPs.

The overall readmission rate was also consistent compared with earlier findings3,4. Compared with the ERAS population, the readmission rate was higher among the accelerated ERP population. This finding corroborates the findings of Robitaille et al.19. The higher readmission rate among CHASE patients can be explained by the fact that some complications, e.g. anastomotic leakage, tend to occur not immediately after surgery but within the first two weeks postoperatively24,25. Consequently, patients treated according to the accelerated ERP with early discharge would have to be readmitted for treatment of the complication. Of the patients being readmitted, almost all patients were classified ASA II.

This is in line with previous research demonstrating the association between comorbidities and increased readmission4,26. Regarding the type of surgery, sigmoid resection or right hemicolectomy were the most performed surgical procedures. This reflects the relatively high rate of right hemicolectomies and sigmoid resections of the included population, consequently, most complications occurred in the sigmoid resection group. Most patients were readmitted after presentation in the emergency department. As described, the CHASE protocol involves telephone follow-up by the nurses on POD1 and POD3. If patients reported any alarm symptoms, they were advised to present themselves to the emergency department and the surgeon on duty was informed about their situation. No patient presented themselves at the emergency department with all sepsis criteria.

Treatment of complications frequently involved medical treatment, electrolyte suppletion, or another non-invasive intervention for both CHASE and ERAS patients. When a complication occurred during primary admission, the LOS was shorter in the CHASE cohort (2 versus 5 days). Interestingly, the ERAS population had shorter LOS (and faster functional recovery) after readmission compared with the CHASE cohort (6 versus 3 days). This can be explained by the fact among the patients readmitted, the rate of severe complications was higher in the CHASE than ERAS cohort. Since these patients required surgical intervention, a longer LOS was to be expected. We hypothesized the length of stay during readmission to be fairly short in the accelerated ERP cohort as the pre-, peri, and postoperative adjustments to the protocol caused less stress to the body and better mobility around the surgery. The data of patients with AL or rectal blood loss demonstrate that despite a longer readmission time, the total LOS is shorter in the CHASE cohort. All patients were functionally recovered within 90 days after surgery.

The findings of this study may be limited by the retrospective nature of this study. The CHASE protocol was developed to facilitate early discharge after colon cancer surgery. As clinicians were intrigued by the CHASE protocol to allow accelerated recovery, the primary length of hospital stay consequently differs from that of the ERAS cohort. Nonetheless, discharge criteria remained the same compared to the ERAS protocol. Also, the course and treatment of complications were patient-driven and not standardized in the CHASE protocol. Criteria for readmission or treatment were therefore not affected by the inclusion in the CHASE cohort. The retrospective nature of the ERAS control group could also have introduced bias, as for study patients there is a strict registration of complications and recovery. Over the years, the treatment of complications was adapted, e.g., tranexamic acid for rectal blood loss, and documentation was more elaborate in the prospective CHASE cohort. Potentially, minor postoperative complications such as rectal blood loss and electrolyte deficiency may have been underscored in the retrospective ERAS cohort.

The results may also be affected by the size and selection of the study population. The population (both CHASE and ERAS) was selective as this study applied strict inclusion criteria for the intervention and retrospective population, e.g., ASA classification I-II and an uncomplicated perioperative course. Of patients assessed for eligibility, approximately 40% were included. Additionally, this study was performed in a single-center setting. These factors may affect the generalizability of the findings. Despite the application of similar inclusion criteria, the CHASE population differed significantly from the ERAS population in age. To correct for these differences, stratification was applied demonstrating statistically significant differences in LOS during readmission among patients aged 65–80 years.

Even though CHASE patients reported high satisfaction with the protocol and early discharge27,28, this study lacks information about the quality of life. Therefore, no information can be provided about the impact on the quality of life of early discharge or readmission. Also, no cost-effectiveness analysis was performed. These factors should be investigated in future studies. In addition, further research is necessary to determine the applicability of accelerated ERPs to a wider range of study centers and patient groups.

The results suggest that the accelerated recovery with the CHASE protocol has no negative effects (i.e. failure to rescue) on the incidence and severity of complications but does affect readmission rate and LOS. Nonetheless, in the CHASE cohort, the total LOS was significantly shorter, and patients were faster functionally recovered compared to the ERAS control group among patients with complications. This study demonstrates that accelerated recovery can be safe for selected patients and has the potential to be implemented as standard care. Moreover, the CHASE protocol proved to be beneficial in terms of improved total LOS for patients with complications and a faster functional recovery.

Methods

This single-center analysis of a prospective study was conducted at Zuyderland Medical Center in the Netherlands. The study protocol was approved by the local Medical Research Ethics Committee Zuyd (NL71804.096.19; METC220190130). Informed consent was obtained from all patients involved. Patients were enrolled in the CHASE study between June 2020 and October 202327.

For the control group, patients treated according to the ERAS protocol who underwent elective colon resection between January 2018 and May 2020 were included. This study was reported according to the STROBE guidelines29. All patients included in the CHASE cohort provided written informed consent before inclusion. For the retrospective ERAS cohort, the need for informed consent was waived by the Medical Research Ethics Committee Zuyd. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Research population

The inclusion criteria of the CHASE study were also applied to the control group (ERAS). Patients were included if they were aged between ≥ 18 years ≤ 85, BMI ≤ 35 kg/m2, American Society of Anesthesiology classification I–II, diagnosed with (non-complicated) colon cancer, scheduled to undergo elective laparoscopic colon surgery, had an uncomplicated operation, had available ambulant care provided for the first 24 h after discharge, were proficient in Dutch language and writing, and adequately reachable by phone. Exclusion criteria included limited mobility and/or need to be aided/assisted when mobilizing, history of active pulmonary infection, any other active infection, any uncontrolled medical disease, contraindication for oral NSAIDs, requiring parenteral nutrition before surgery, scheduled to undergo lower rectal resections (TaTME, APR, PME), ostomy creation, preoperative complication, requiring aid with daily life activities or not, unable to provide informed consent.

CHASE intervention

This ERP comprised modifications in the pre-, peri- and postoperative care. The key elements of the protocol were optimal analgesia, minimally invasive surgery, restrictive fluid management, encouragement of mobilization, and early resumption of intake27. All steps of the protocol are described in Supplementary Information 1.

Outcomes

The primary outcome measures for this study were surgical complications ≤ 90 days after surgery, defined as any deviation from the ideal postoperative course that is not inherent in the procedure and does not involve failure to cure30. Complications that were caused by adjuvant chemotherapy, such as neuropathy were scored as non-surgical complications. Secondary outcome measures were severity of the complication, primary and secondary LOS, readmission, reintervention, duration of treatment for complication, and time to functional and full recovery. Functional recovery was defined as the absence of alarm symptoms (e.g. tachycardia, fever), independent mobilizations, sufficient intake, and pain under control (Numerical Rating Scale ≤ 4) with oral analgesics. Full recovery was defined as full recovery of complications or completed treatment for complications.

Data collection

Data for the CHASE cohort were prospectively collected from patient records. For the ERAS cohort data were collected from the National Cancer Registry, Dutch ColoRectal Audit (DCRA), and additional information was retrieved from patient records. Baseline characteristics and tumor- and surgery-related data were collected. See Supplementary Information 2 for all variables collected for this study.

Statistical analysis

Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as means with the standard deviation (SD), and non-normally distributed variables as medians with the Inter Quartile Range (IQR). Depending on the normality of the data, differences between groups were examined with the independent sample T-test or Mann–Whitney-U test for continuous data. The Chi-square test and Fisher exact test were used to analyze differences in binary and nominal data. The Fisher-Freeman-Halton test was used if 20% of the cells had an expected number of observations of 5 or lower. In case of significant differences in baseline characteristics between the two cohorts, matching or stratification was used to correct for these potential confounders. Matching would be performed depending on the distribution of matching factors, including age, sex, and type of surgery14,31. If the distribution of matching factors was unequal, stratification was used. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to determine whether variables other than the protocol had a significant influence on the occurrence of complications. All statistical tests were performed with significance set at p < 0.05. IBM SPSS software was used to conduct the statistical tests (version 25.0; IBM, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).
