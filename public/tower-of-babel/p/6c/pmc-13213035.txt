Predictors of Massive Transfusion in Adult Blunt Trauma Patients: Analysis of a Nationwide Trauma Registry in Korea
Authors: Dongmin Seo, Juyoung Kim, Kyoungwon Jung, Juhong Park, Junsik Kwon, Hye-Min Sohn, Sora Kim, Jayoung Yoo, Jae Hun Kim, Hangjoo Cho, Younghwan Kim, Taegyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMassive transfusion (MT) is critical for trauma patients with severe hemorrhage; however, predicting the need for MT upon hospital arrival remains challenging. Herein, we aimed to identify predictors of MT in adult blunt trauma patients using data from the Korean Trauma Data Bank (KTDB), a nationwide trauma registry in Korea.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from patients registered in the KTDB from 2017 to 2021. Adult blunt trauma patients who received packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusion within 24 hours of arrival were included. We divided the patients into the MT and non-MT groups, with MT defined as the administration of ≥ 10 units of pRBCs within 24 hours. We compared the characteristics of the two groups and performed multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify significant predictors of MT, using multiple imputation to address missing data.ResultsAmong 12,145 patients registered in the KTDB, 2,098 (17.3%) received MT. Compared with the non-MT group, the MT group exhibited lower initial systolic blood pressure, lower Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores, and higher heart rates compared to the non-MT group. Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified systolic blood pressure ≤ 90 mmHg (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 2.34), heart rate ≥ 120 bpm (aOR, 2.15), respiratory rate < 10 or > 29 breaths per minute (aOR, 2.16), GCS < 13 (aOR, 2.45), and a positive Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma (FAST) result (aOR, 2.35) as significant predictors of MT upon arrival.ConclusionWe identified initial systolic blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, GCS, and FAST results as significant predictors of MT in adult blunt trauma patients. These predictors may aid in the development of a Korea-specific MT prediction model, contributing to the timely activation of MT protocols and optimization of blood product utilization.Keywords: Wounds, Nonpenetrating; Blood Transfusion; Prediction Value of Tests; Wounds and Injuries; Korea

INTRODUCTION

Trauma is the sixth leading cause of death globally and the most common cause of mortality among individuals of reproductive age (under 40 years old),1,2 with hemorrhage being the leading cause of preventable death in trauma patients. Reports indicate that 33–56% of hemorrhage-related fatalities occur prior to hospitalization.3 Despite advances in trauma care, uncontrolled bleeding remains a critical challenge, particularly in settings with limited blood resources such as South Korea. Approximately 30% of patients with severe hemorrhage develop a coagulopathy, which is associated with increased mortality.4 Given these circumstances, rapid control of hemorrhage is paramount in preventing trauma-related deaths.

Damage control resuscitation (DCR) is recently proposed as an effective strategy to reduce mortality in patients with severe trauma-induced hemorrhagic shock.5,6 This strategy focuses on halting bleeding quickly while minimizing the use of traditional crystalloid-based resuscitation in favor of aggressive transfusion of blood products, such as massive transfusion protocols (MTPs). By doing so, this approach seeks to break the cycle of hemorrhagic shock more efficiently and thus mitigate the lethal triad—hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy.7 The importance of MTPs in DCR has been widely emphasized.8,9 Most specialized trauma care institutions incorporate balanced transfusion strategies into their MTPs, typically administering red blood cells, fresh frozen plasma, and platelets in a 1:1:1 ratio. This approach reportedly improves survival outcomes in trauma patients.10,11

In South Korea, with the amendment of the Emergency Medical Service Act, a nationwide trauma system was established in 2012 with the goal of reducing the high but preventable trauma mortality. Since the introduction of 17 regional trauma centers, the preventable trauma death rate has significantly decreased from over 30% to below 15% in recent assessments.12,13 A recent study on blood product utilization in South Korea has also reported a marked increase in transfusion rates among trauma patients, indicating a shift in transfusion practices.14 However, accurately and promptly predicting which patients require MTPs remains a significant challenge. Although multiple predictive models such as the Assessment of Blood Consumption (ABC) score, Trauma-Associated Severe Hemorrhage (TASH) score, and Shock Index have been developed,15,16,17 these models have not been sufficiently validated in the South Korean trauma population. This underscores the need for local validation to ensure their applicability in this setting. Furthermore, the predictors of massive transfusion (MT) remain institution-specific, as their use often depends on the available resources and capabilities of individual trauma centers. Consequently, many leading trauma centers continue to seek optimized methods for the rapid identification of patients requiring MT.

In this study, we aimed to identify key predictive factors for MT using data from the Korean Trauma Data Bank (KTDB), a national trauma patient registry primarily composed of data from regional trauma centers. By identifying these predictors, we sought to contribute to the development of a predictive model for MT, which could serve as a foundational resource for refining MTP protocols in South Korea.

METHODS

Data sources and study design

This retrospective analysis was conducted using data from the KTDB, which was created in 2013—the year following the launch of the regional trauma center support project—to collect clinical information on trauma patients treated at designated trauma centers. It serves as a foundational database for developing the national trauma care system, facilitating trauma-related research, and supporting policy development.12,13,18,19

Patients registered in the KTDB are those with trauma-related diagnoses admitted after visiting the emergency department or trauma bay, excluding discharged patients. The following data are collected in the registry: patient age, sex, nationality, residential area, injury mechanism, time of injury and arrival, mode of transport, transfer status, initial vital signs on admission (systolic blood pressure [SBP], heart rate, respiratory rate, and body temperature), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score on admission, Revised Trauma Score (RTS), Injury Severity Score (ISS), Trauma and ISS, transfusion volume, and mortality. Currently, approximately 35,000 trauma cases are collected annually from the 17 regional trauma centers. Since the end of 2023, the database has been made publicly accessible to promote quality improvement in trauma care and encourage research activity.

In the present study, we analyzed data from trauma patients recorded in the KTDB between 2017 and 2021. In South Korea, more than 95% of trauma cases are caused by blunt injuries. We included adult patients (aged ≥ 15 years) who sustained blunt trauma, given the distinct differences in the management and prediction of MT between blunt and penetrating injury mechanisms. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) non-blunt injury mechanisms (e.g., penetrating trauma, burns, drowning, or unknown causes); 2) age < 15 years; 3) ISS < 9; 4) no packed red blood cell (pRBC) transfusion within 24 hours of hospital arrival; 5) inter-hospital transfer, either from or to another facility; 6) dead on arrival; and 7) missing essential clinical data. The study population was divided into two groups: patients who received MT and those who did not, with MT defined as the administration of 10 or more units of pRBCs within 24 hours. Demographic and clinical variables were compared between the two groups. Outcomes such as mortality were also analyzed. Based on the collected data and comparative analysis of the two groups, further statistical analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with MT.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics between the MT and non-MT groups were compared using χ2 tests for categorical variables and Wilcoxon rank sum tests for continuous variables.

All logistic regression analyses, including both univariable and multivariable models, were conducted using the imputed datasets. We applied a multistep approach to screen for potential predictors of MT. First, clinically relevant variables were selected based on consensus among the trauma surgeons in our research team. Second, univariable logistic regression analysis was performed for each variable to assess its individual association with MT. Third, variables that demonstrated a potential association (P < 0.1) in the univariable analysis or were considered clinically important were retained as candidate predictors for the final model. Finally, a multivariable logistic regression model was constructed using the selected variables, incorporating both statistical significance and clinical relevance.

Among variables that showed significant differences between the two groups, the ISS and RTS were excluded from further modeling. ISS is not available in the initial resuscitation phase, and RTS includes parameters that overlap with individual predictors such as SBP, respiratory rate, and GCS. Rather than using the composite RTS score, we opted to include these variables directly in the model for more intuitive interpretation and practical applicability. For practical application in the clinical setting, continuous variables were converted into categorical variables based on previous studies and expert consensus. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC-ROC).

Missing data were handled using multiple imputation through the Fully Conditional Specification method, which supports both categorical and continuous variables. Five imputed datasets were generated with 10 iterations each. Analyses were performed on each dataset separately, and the resulting estimates were combined using standard pooling procedures. Variables with missing values included mechanism of injury (2.7%), SBP (7.3%), heart rate (3.6%), body temperature (3.4%), and GCS score (3.9%). Supplementary Table 1 outlines the missing data patterns. Supplementary Table 2 compares key variables between the original and imputed datasets. Supplementary Table 3 reports the AUC values for the univariable and multivariable models, and Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the corresponding ROC curves.

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA), and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Ajou University (AJOUIRB-EX-2022-315). The requirement for obtaining informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective nature of the study and because data was from an existing database.

RESULTS

Among the 188,589 patients registered in the KTDB from 2017 to 2021, 12,145 patients met the study’s inclusion criteria; their characteristics are presented in Table 1. Regarding age distribution, patients aged 55–64 years accounted for the largest proportion (21.7%), followed by those aged 65–74 years (17.1%). Male patients comprised 68.7% of the study population, reflecting the male predominance commonly observed in trauma populations. Traffic accidents were the most common cause of injury (55.5%), followed by falls from height (24.3%) and ground-level falls (9.6%). The median initial SBP on admission was 120 (interquartile range [IQR], 96–147) mmHg; the median heart rate was 90 (IQR, 76–108) bpm, and the median body temperature was 36.0°C (IQR, 36.0–36.0°C). The median GCS score on admission was 14 (IQR, 8–15), and the median ISS was 24 (IQR, 17–30). Additionally, 79.0% of the patients had an ISS > 15, indicating that the majority sustained severe trauma.

Among the included patients, 2,098 patients (17.3%) received MT (MT group), whereas 10,047 patients (82.7%) did not (non-MT group) (Fig. 1). A comparison between the two groups revealed significant differences in several demographic and clinical variables (Table 2). The proportion of male patients was higher in the MT group than in the non-MT group (75.4% vs. 67.3%, P < 0.001). The median GCS score on admission (10 vs. 15, P < 0.001) and median SBP (103 vs. 123 mmHg, P < 0.001) were lower in the MT group, and the median heart rate was higher (103 vs. 88 bpm, P < 0.001). Additionally, the MT group exhibited a higher proportion of abnormal respiratory rates and hypothermia compared to the non-MT group (P < 0.001). The severity of injury was greater in the MT group, as indicated by a higher median ISS (30 [IQR, 24–38] vs. 22 [IQR, 16–29], P < 0.001) and a lower median RTS (6.3756 vs. 7.8408, P < 0.001). Notably, the mortality rate was significantly higher in the MT group than in the non-MT group (51.0% vs. 18.3%, P < 0.001). Taken together, these findings suggest greater physiological derangement upon arrival in the MT group.

Missing values in key variables (e.g., SBP, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, GCS, and Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma [FAST]) were handled using multiple imputation, and the imputed datasets were used for both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses. These analyses identified SBP ≤ 90 mmHg, heart rate ≥ 120 bpm, respiratory rate < 10 or > 29 breaths per minute, GCS < 13, and a positive FAST result as significant predictors of MT. The final multivariable model demonstrated good discriminatory ability, with an AUC-ROC ranging from 0.713 to 0.714 (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed data from a large-scale trauma registry in South Korea to identify factors associated with MT in adult trauma patients requiring blood transfusion. We found that initial SBP, heart rate, respiratory rate, GCS, and a positive FAST result were significant predictors of MT upon hospital arrival. These findings can serve as fundamental evidence for the accurate and prompt identification of patients requiring MT, thereby facilitating the early activation of MT protocols.

To our knowledge, this study is the first to utilize data from the KTDB after its public release in late 2023. Considering that the coronavirus disease pandemic exacerbated the challenges associated with blood supply shortages in South Korea, this study is timely and holds particular significance in terms of guiding appropriate blood product utilization and management strategies. The KTDB is the only nationwide trauma patient registry in South Korea, collecting clinical data from 17 regional trauma centers,12,13,19 which designation was completed by the end of 2023. This study analyzed data from 14 participating centers that contributed patient records to the KTDB from 2017 to 2021.18 Supplementary Table 4 includes the list of regional trauma centers that contributed data to the KTDB during the study period. The KTDB was developed by benchmarking the National Trauma Data Bank (NTDB) in the United States and therefore shares fundamental similarities. However, unlike the NTDB, which includes data from level 1, 2, 3, and 4 trauma centers, the KTDB exclusively collects data from regional trauma centers that function as level 1 centers in South Korea. Given that few studies in South Korea have investigated MT using data from multiple trauma centers,20 this study is relevant as it provides reference data for future research in which data from trauma registries will be analyzed to explore MT- and other trauma-related topics.

Rapid recognition of hemorrhagic shock and early identification of patients requiring MT is crucial for improving survival and clinical outcomes in trauma patients. Accurate prediction of MT is also essential for the efficient use of blood products and minimizing waste.21 Various studies have introduced both individual predictors and validated scoring systems to address this need.15,16,17,22 Validated MT prediction scores incorporate clinical assessments, laboratory values, and ultrasound findings. A systematic review on MT predictors summarized various predictors and scoring systems.23 Representative examples include the ABC score, which uses SBP ≤ 90 mmHg, heart rate ≥ 120 bpm, positive FAST result, and penetrating injury as predictors; TASH score, which uses SBP-based scoring, heart rate ≥ 120 bpm, positive FAST result, femur fracture, pelvic fracture, sex, hemoglobin level, and base excess as predictors; Traumatic Bleeding Severity Score, which uses age, SBP-based scoring, positive FAST result, pelvic fracture, and lactate level as predictors; and McLaughlin score, which uses SBP, heart rate, hematocrit, and pH as predictors.

In this study, the variables significantly associated with MT were SBP ≤ 90 mmHg, heart rate ≥ 120 bpm, respiratory rate < 10 or > 29 breaths per minute, GCS < 13, and a positive FAST result. Previous studies have demonstrated the association of these variables with MT. For instance, patients requiring MT often present with lower SBP, higher heart rates, and lower GCS upon arrival.15,24 Kim et al.25 identified SBP ≤ 90 mmHg as a reliable predictor of early transfusion requirements, and Ruchholtz et al.26 reported SBP ≤ 90 mmHg as a significant predictor of MT. Heart rate ≥ 120 bpm was a significant predictor in the TASH score,27 whereas both SBP ≤ 90 mmHg and heart rate ≥ 120 bpm were key components of the ABC score.15 Callcut et al.28 highlighted SBP ≤ 90 mmHg and body temperature < 35.5°C as meaningful predictors of MT. Additionally, Yumoto et al.29 emphasized that a positive FAST result was a significant predictor, consistent with findings from the ABC score development study.

Several laboratory values, such as hemoglobin level,28 International Normalized Ratio,30 lactate level,31 base excess,32 and calcium level,33 have also been reported as predictors of MT. Research on thromboelastography has also explored its predictive potential for MT.34,35,36 However, the Advanced Trauma Life Support Guidelines emphasize that decisions regarding the activation of MT protocols should rely on rapid clinical assessment during the initial resuscitation phase. As laboratory tests often require several hours to yield results, their utility in time-critical situations is limited. Furthermore, the KTDB, akin to the American NTDB, does not collect laboratory values.

Despite its strengths, this study has some limitations. First, as a retrospective study, it is inherently susceptible to bias and the influence of unmeasured confounding factors. However, the use of data from the KTDB, a large-scale nationwide trauma registry involving multiple trauma centers, enhances the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Second, we only included patients with blunt trauma. In South Korea, where strict gun control laws are enforced, penetrating trauma is relatively rare. While this could introduce selection bias, focusing exclusively on blunt trauma patients increases the study’s relevance to the domestic trauma population. Third, laboratory values are not entered into the KTDB, limiting the evaluation of certain laboratory-based predictors of MT. Nonetheless, the clinical utility of laboratory values is often limited in the acute resuscitation phase, where timely decision-making is critical. Fourth, the South Korean trauma system is still under development, and it remains unclear whether FAST examinations are performed under consistent protocols across all trauma centers. This represents a limitation of the current study. Moreover, the KTDB does not provide detailed information regarding the timing, indication, or operator training for FAST, limiting further stratified analysis. Although we did not perform a separate analysis to compare model performance with and without FAST, future studies focusing on the contribution of FAST to MT prediction and its standardization across institutions are warranted. Fifth, pre-hospital interventions such as fluid resuscitation or airway management may have influenced the initial vital signs recorded upon hospital arrival. However, because the KTDB does not provide detailed information on pre-hospital treatments, we could not account for these potential effects, which represents another limitation of this study. Finally, although some variables had missing values (e.g., SBP, heart rate, respiratory rate, body temperature, GCS, and FAST), multiple imputation was performed to reduce potential bias and preserve statistical power. This approach allowed us to include all available cases and minimize the impact of missing data on model validity. This study underscores the importance of clinical assessment in the early identification of patients requiring MT.

Our findings could serve as a foundation for developing a Korea-specific MT prediction model, potentially contributing to the standardization of early MTP activation protocols in trauma centers nationwide. Developing such a model, tailored to the characteristics of the domestic trauma population and resource availability, could enable accurate and timely identification of patients requiring MT. Ultimately, this may help optimize blood product utilization and improve trauma care outcomes in South Korea, where blood supply constraints remain a critical concern.

Using a nationwide trauma registry in South Korea, we identified SBP ≤ 90 mmHg, heart rate ≥ 120 bpm, respiratory rate < 10 or > 29 breaths per minute, GCS < 13, and a positive FAST result as significant predictors of MT upon hospital arrival in adult patients with blunt trauma. Further research is needed to develop and validate a predictive score incorporating these variables, tailored to the Korean trauma system, to enable the early and accurate identification of patients requiring MT. In addition, determining appropriate cutoff values for key predictors can further enhance the model’s clinical applicability. Such efforts may contribute toward optimizing blood product utilization and improving trauma care, particularly in settings with limited blood supply like South Korea.
