Design and simulation of high efficiency KSnI3 based perovskite solar cells using DFT and SCAPS-1D
Authors: A Yousfi, O Saidani, A Benmakhlouf, T Helaimia, Abdullah Saad Alsubaie, Sheikh Rashel Al Ahmed, Md Rasidul Islam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a novel KSnI3-based heterostructure solar cell design, incorporating efficient electron transport layers (ETLs) such as CeO₂, and hole transport layers (HTLs) based on CBTS. Using Density Functional Theory (DFT), the electrical and optical properties of KSnI3 are characterized and implemented in SCAPS-1D to model the proposed solar cell. The numerical analysis demonstrates that the ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag structure achieves significant photo-conversion efficiency. Key factors such as KSnI3 layer thickness, series resistance, light conversion efficiency, and operating temperature are investigated to optimize performance. Additionally, the influence of ETL and absorber thickness, defect density, and electron affinity are examined. The simulation results show strong agreement with both numerical and experimental data, yielding an optimized open-circuit voltage (VOC) of 0.86 V, a short-circuit current density (JSC) of 21.5 mA/cm2, a fill factor (FF) of 86.05%, and an enhanced power conversion efficiency (PCE) that increased from 11.3 to 13.46% at a KSnI3 thickness of 1.4 μm, under a defect density of 1014 cm–3 and an electron affinity of 3.44 eV. This comprehensive simulation offers valuable insights that can guide further research on KSnI₃-based solar cells.Keywords: KSnI3, DFT, PCE, Defect density, Solar cell, CASTEP, SCAPS-1DSubject terms: Condensed-matter physics, Theory and computation

Introduction

Organic-inorganic hybrid lead halides have garnered significant attention in the realm of solar cell materials due to their remarkable properties1. These materials offer advantages such as a tunable bandgap, straightforward synthesis, high light absorption capacity, solubility, and efficient charge diffusion2. The pivotal moment arrived in 2009 with the introduction of the first organic-inorganic lead halide perovskite solar cell (PSC), initially demonstrating a power conversion efficiency (PCE) of 3.8%. Subsequent extensive research efforts have led to remarkable progress, with PSC efficiency reaching an impressive 9.28%4. However, a substantial concern surrounding lead-based PSCs is their inherent toxicity, which has prompted the exploration of alternative divalent cations with similar electron configurations, namely Ge2+ and Sn2 +5. Crucially, replacing Pb2+ with Sn2+ and Ge2+ doesn’t disrupt the perovskite crystal configuration, owing to the smaller ionic radii of Sn2+ (1.35 Å) and Ge2+ (0.73 Å) compared to Pb2+ (1.49 Å) 6,7. Moreover, Sn-based perovskites exhibit a lower bandgap compared to their lead counterparts, suggesting a higher theoretical efficiency8. This transition to “Sn” perovskites has spurred a wealth of research, both theoretical and experimental, exploring perovskite solar cells based on Sn and Ge9,10. In the pursuit of lead-free perovskite solar cells (PSCs), researchers have developed numerous innovative strategies. These include combining 2D Sn perovskites with 3D FASnI3 to achieve a remarkable 9% efficiency and doping Sn-based perovskites with Ge to enhance their effectiveness significantly11,12. Other creative approaches involve passivating film defects using a modest quantity of 2D tetra-n-butylammonium cation13introducing additional layers to suppress Sn oxidation and enhance crystallinity14and incorporating specific compounds to yield highly oriented crystals, resulting in PCEs ranging from 17.51 to 20.63% 15,16. These breakthroughs not only enhance efficiency but also improve the stability of PSCs17. On the theoretical front, researchers have explored the optical, electrical, and structural properties of various Sn-based perovskites18,19highlighting their superiority over traditional lead-based counterparts20. Comparative studies among different Sn-based perovskites have provided valuable insights into their varying efficiencies21,22. Notably, a noticeable gap exists in both experimental and theoretical research regarding orthorhombic KSnI3-based PSCs15,23. KSnI₃ perovskite has recently garnered attention due to its favourable optoelectronic properties, making it a promising candidate for lead-free perovskite solar cells. It exhibits a suitable bandgap (~ 1.84 eV), which is ideal for single-junction photovoltaic applications. Moreover, KSnI₃ demonstrates a high absorption coefficient in the visible region, ensuring efficient photon harvesting. Unlike conventional lead-based perovskites, KSnI₃ is non-toxic, environmentally friendly, and composed of earth-abundant elements, addressing critical sustainability concerns. In addition, its potential for enhanced structural and chemical stability, particularly when combined with suitable charge transport layers, further supports its viability as an absorber layer in next-generation perovskite solar cells. This unexplored territory has driven the launch of the current study24. Beyond addressing this knowledge gap, the research aligns with the broader mission of developing environmentally friendly and non-toxic PSCs, offering a potential pathway toward cleaner and more sustainable photovoltaic technologies25. Within the realm of lead-free perovskite materials, theoretical investigations have significantly enriched our understanding14,26. One pivotal study by Roknuzzamann27 delved into the opto-electrical and physical characteristics of MASnX3, where X represents I, Cl, or Br28. Their research underscored the higher performance of these tin-based perovskites compared to traditional MAPbX3, highlighting the promise of tin-based perovskites as a viable alternative. In a separate investigation, Peng and colleagues conducted a comparative analysis of two specific perovskites, MASnI3 and FASnI330. Their findings revealed that FASnI3 exhibited higher efficiency than MASnI3, underscoring the significance of these lead-free materials. Further exploration led Joshi and his team to investigate EASnI3, examining its optical, thermoelectric, structural, and electronic properties for potential application in solar energy conversion. Additionally, Density Functional Theory studies focused on CsSnI3 − xBrx, assessing its suitability for photovoltaic applications32. Hamededdine and colleagues investigated the optical and physical properties of cubic KBX3 structures, where X can be I, Cl, or Br, and B represents either Ge or Sn, shedding light on their potential applications in solar energy conversion. Chol-Hyok and his team investigated the optoelectronic and physical properties of tetragonal KB1-xMgxI3 structures, aiming to utilize them in water-splitting photocatalysis, a promising avenue for renewable energy utilization. Despite these extensive research endeavours, a significant gap remains in our knowledge. Specifically, the case of orthorhombic KSnI3, its potential application in PSC, and the feasibility of simulating solar cells using this material have yet to be explored in existing literature34. This gap in both experimental and theoretical research serves as the driving force behind the current study. In addition to addressing this knowledge void, the research aligns with the ongoing efforts to mitigate the toxicity associated with lead in PSCs. The insights garnered from this theoretical study have the potential to inform the design of environmentally friendly and non-toxic PSC, helping the broader mission of cleaner and more sustainable energy solutions. The novelty of this study lies in the combined use of density functional theory (DFT) and Solar Cell Capacitance Simulator -one-dimensional (SCAPS-1D) (Version Number: 3.3.11) (URL Link: https://scaps.elis.ugent.be) simulations to investigate and optimize a lead-free KSnI₃-based perovskite solar cell structure. While previous studies on KSnI₃ are limited, our work presents a comprehensive computational analysis that integrates material-level insights with device-level modelling. Additionally, the use of CeO₂ as an ETL and CBTS as an HTL in this context is relatively unexplored, offering a new perspective on compatible and non-toxic transport layers. The detailed optimization of critical parameters such as absorber thickness, defect density, series/shunt resistance, and electron affinity adds further originality, contributing a meaningful advancement in the design of environmentally friendly perovskite solar cells.

Device settings and simulation process

Our solar cell structure comprises three primary components: the ETL layer, the absorber layer (p-type), and the HTL layer35. After exposing our PSC to light, it generates excitons, composed of pairs of particles with a specific energy state37. Notably, these excitons are principally produced inside the absorber layer36. Composed of electrons and holes, these excitons have the advantage of comparatively long diffusion lengths, enabling them to migrate toward the p/n region for electrons and holes, respectively. At the interface of ETL/PVK, these excitons, essentially hole-electron pairs, are separated38. Following this separation, the ETL is used to transfer electrons toward their respective electrodes, while the holes effectively move over the HTL39. The critical factor contributing to this efficient movement of electrons and holes is the presence of a built-in electric field between the ETL/HTL and the PVK layer. This electric field facilitates the dissociation of excitons and promotes their movement, thereby accelerating the motion of electrons and holes toward their respective electrodes. This enhancement significantly enhances the solar cell’s overall performance40. In our simulation experiments, we have inserted innovative layers for the ETL/HTL41. Specifically, we utilized CeO2 as ETL and CBTS as HTL42,43. The outcomes of the current numerical study revealed that the suggested structures composed of ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag exhibit a remarkable level of photoconversion efficiency, as depicted in Fig. 1. This indicates that our solar cell design, utilising these specific materials and the specified structure, exhibits a notable ability to efficiently convert light into electricity, as illustrated in figure44. The simulations were conducted under ordinary settings, specifically, working with AM1.5G radiance at a temperature of 300 K. Additionally, great care is taken in defining the input parameters inside SCAPS-1D for the ETL, KSnI3, and HTL layers as elaborated in Table 1, Electrical properties of interface in Table 2 and contact parameters in Table 345,46. It’s worth noting that these parameters are pivotal for precisely modelling the solar cell’s environment and performance within the simulation framework. By meticulously setting these parameters, we ensure a comprehensive and accurate representation of the solar cell’s dynamics and functionality under the specified conditions47.

The energy band diagram demonstrates the band alignment in a solar cell structure, emphasizing both conduction and valence band offsets49. At the KSnI₃/CBTS interface, the conduction band offset (CBO) shows a small spike of around 1.9 eV, which promotes charge separation by preventing electron recombination, although a larger spike could hinder electron transport. In contrast, the valence band offset (VBO) exhibits a smooth transition, aiding efficient hole transfer50. Figure 8 (a, b) provides a visual representation of this alignment in the KSnI₃-based heterostructure, showing the Fermi levels for electrons (Fn) and holes (Fp), along with the positioning of the conduction and valence bands51. The figure reveals that Fp aligns with the valence band across different electron transport layers (ETLs), particularly those incorporating CeO₂52. In the case of the hole transport layer (HTL) using copper barium thiostannate (CBTS), Fp remains aligned with the valence band while Fn crosses into the conduction band, impacting charge carrier movement53. The rear contact (Ag) effectively gathers holes from the HTL, while the indium tin oxide (ITO) front contact efficiently collects electrons, providing a clearer understanding of the charge collection mechanisms in the heterostructure.

Effect of perovskite layer (KSnI3) thickness

Within the intricate composition of ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag solar cells, the thickness of KSnI3 intricately influences multiple key parameters that govern the device’s overall performance. In a simulated range of layer thicknesses from 0.2 to 1.4 μm, a discernible pattern emerges, revealing an augmentation in the peak of JSC until it attains a value of 18 mA/Cm2 at a material thickness of 1.4 μm. A thicker KSnI3 layer generally enhances sunlight absorption, resulting in a higher Jsc with an augmented generation of electron-hole pairs. However, this correlation is contingent on an optimal thickness, beyond which excessive thickness may induce heightened charge recombination, thereby limiting the potential improvement in Jsc. Simultaneously, the Voc is intricately linked to the thickness of KSnI3. It’s noteworthy that Voc exhibits an incremental increase until it peaks at 0.7535 (V), beyond which a significant decrease is observed. The VOC, a pivotal parameter representing the maximum voltage available when no current is flowing, is influenced by the KSnI3 layer thickness through various mechanisms. While a thicker KSnI3 material enhances Voc by improving the absorption of light and raising the quantity of electron-hole pairs,, a delicate balance must be maintained54. Excessive thickness may lead to heightened charge recombination, potentially negating the positive effects on Voc and resulting in a lower Voc. The FF representing the efficiency of charge transfer and collection, is another parameter intricately shaped by the thickness of the KSnI3 layer55. Achieving an optimal balance is crucial, as a too-thin layer may impede charge transfer, reducing the FF, while an excessively thick layer may elevate recombination losses, negatively affecting the FF. Moreover, the overall power conversion efficiency (PCE), which is the result of Jsc, FF, and Voc, is directly impacted by the thickness of KSnI3 layer. Attaining the highest level of PCE demands a meticulously tuned thickness that maximizes Jsc and Voc while maintaining a high FF. Notably, an observed increase of 11% in the overall PCE underscores the significance of precise adjustments in the KSnI3 layer thickness for optimizing the solar cell’s overall performance, as illustrated in Fig. 9.

Effect of ETL (CeO2) thickness

The PCE of a solar cell is a complex interplay of multiple factors, and one critical aspect is the thickness of its constituent materials. In the specific solar cell configuration outlined (ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag), the thickness of the ETL, comprising CeO2, acting a pivotal role in shaping key parameters like Jsc, Voc, FF, and overall PCE. In a simulated investigation covering ETL layer thicknesses ranging from 0.3 to 1.4 μm, a discernible pattern emerged. Notably, there was a light decline in the peak of JSC, diminishing from 18.33 to 18.23 mA/cm2 at a 1.4 μm ETL thickness. This reduction in Jsc was primarily attributed to the thicker ETL leading to increased recombination losses, consequently affecting Jsc adversely. Simultaneously, a drop in Voc was observed, moving between 0.7521 and 0.752 V, as the ETL thickness increased. This trend can be explained by the thicker ETL creating a longer path for charge carriers, electrons in this context, to traverse. This extended path increases the likelihood of electron-hole recombination within the CeO2 as ETL, resulting in the loss of photogenerated electrons and holes before they can contribute to the external circuit, thus reducing Voc. On the positive side, the ETL thickness displayed a noteworthy influence on the fill factor (FF), reflecting the PCE of charge relocation and collection. As the ETL thickness increased, an increase in FF was evident, rising from 78.6 to 78.7%. The reason behind this lies in the thicker ETL reducing the overall resistance within the device. Thicker ETL layers exhibit lower sheet resistance, diminishing the resistance encountered by charge carriers as they navigate the solar cell56. In Fig. 10, the reduction in series resistance is advantageous for FF, as it minimizes voltage losses and sustains a high FF. Moreover, the total PCE, resulting from the product of Jsc, FF, and Voc, was directly influenced by the ETL thickness. This revealed a notable decrease in PCE from 10.84 to 10.8% as the ETL thickness increased. The issue here is that while a thicker ETL can absorb more incoming light, fostering the formation of additional electron/hole pairs in the absorber layer (KSnI3), excessive thickness can cause light absorption losses by preventing a substantial portion of the incident light from reaching the absorber layer. This diminished light absorption helps to reduce number of the photogenerated carriers and, consequently, a drop in PCE. Hence, optimizing the ETL thickness is a delicate balance, which is crucial for achieving the highest solar cell efficiency.

Effect of CBTS thickness

The solar cell efficiency is intricately impacted by various factors, and one crucial element is the thickness of its individual layers. In the specific solar cell configuration mentioned (ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag), the thickness of the HTL, represented by CBTS, plays a crucial function in shaping key parameters like Jsc, Voc, FF, and the overall PCE. In a simulated study spanning various HTL thicknesses, from 0.2 to 1.2 μm, discernible patterns emerged. Notably, an increase in the peak of JSC was observed, rising from 18.28 to 18.33 mA/cm2 at a 1.2 μm HTL thickness. This rise in Jsc can be attributed to the thicker HTL layer’s ability to enhance light absorption, providing more opportunities for incident photons to be absorbed by the active layer (KSnI3). This leads to an increased quantity of electron-hole pairs being generated, consequently elevating Jsc. Simultaneously, there was an increase in Voc, progressing from 0.751 to 0.754 V at 1 μm HTL thickness. This occurrence is due to the fact that increasing the HTL thickness can lead to a reduction in recombination losses within the HTL itself. This means that a larger portion of photogenerated charge carriers can reach the external circuit, resulting in a higher Voc. However, it’s crucial to remember that as the HTL thickness continues to increase, it can eventually lead to a decrease in Voc. The thicker HTL creates an extended path for charge carriers, which introduces higher series resistance and voltage losses, ultimately lowering Voc. On the flip side, the HTL thickness also exerts a notable influence on the FF, the efficiency of charge transfer and collection. With increasing HTL thickness, a decrease in FF was evident, diminishing from 78.68 to 78.6%. This reduction in FF is primarily attributed to the extended path for charge carriers created by the thicker HTL, resulting in higher series resistance, which leads to increased voltage losses57. Higher series resistance impedes the efficient extraction of current, particularly at the highest power levels of PSC. Furthermore, the total power conversion efficiency, calculated as the product of Jsc, FF, and Voc, was directly affected by the HTL thickness. As presented in Fig. 11, PCE exhibited a notable increase, rising from 10.81 to 10.83% as the HTL thickness increased. This PCE increase is primarily attributed to the thicker HTL’s capacity to enhance absorption of light, producing more electron-hole pairs, that can contribute to an overall increase in efficiency. In essence, the impact of HTL thickness on PCE is a delicate balance between multiple factors, including light absorption, charge carrier recombination, series resistance, and the optimization of charge transfer and collection. The ideal HTL thickness is contingent on the specific solar cell design and material properties, and careful consideration is required to achieve the highest PCE58.

Impact of series resistance

Figure 12 illustrates the impact of series resistance (RS) in a specific solar cell configuration (ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag). Series resistance is a critical factor in this configuration, and it was varied throughout the range of 0 to 10 Ω cm2 while keeping the shunt resistance (RSh) fixed at 106 Ω cm2. RS plays a pivotal role in determining several key variables of the PSC, including JSC, Voc, FF, and overall PCE. Series resistance refers to the resistance of current within the PSC. According to Ohm’s Law (V = I×R), an increase in RS results in a larger voltage drop across RS. In the context of a solar cell, this implies that a greater portion of the generated voltage is lost as it traverses RS. Interestingly, as RS increased to 10 Ω cm2, there was a reduction in the peak of JSC, declining from 18.305 to 18.25 mA/cm2. This diminution in JSC is attributed to the augmented voltage drop across RS, which limits the current flow. Simultaneously, an increase in Voc was observed, rising from 0.7520 to 0.7530 V at 10 Ω cm2. This might seem counterintuitive, as one would expect a decrease in Voc with increased RS. However, the increased voltage drop caused by RS effectively isolates the cell from the external load, diminishing the current flowing through the cell. With reduced current, the impact of recombination and other parasitic losses is minimized, resulting in an increase in Voc59. Conversely, series resistance (RS) also had a significant impact on the fill factor, which measures the PCE of charge relocation and collection within the cell. With increasing RS, there was a noticeable decrease in FF, dropping from 78.68 to 61%. The introduction of higher series resistance in a solar cell adds more resistance to the current flow, leading to a diminution of JSC. This, in turn, results in a minimization in the FF, representing the efficiency of charge transfer and collection within the cell60. Furthermore, the overall PCE, calculated as the product of JSC, FF, and Voc, was directly affected by the series resistance. Notably, PCE exhibited a significant decrease, dropping from 10.81 to 8.2% as the series resistance increased. Series resistance can limit current passing via the PSC, as higher resistance restricts the passage of current, leading to a minimization of overall current output and, consequently, a decline in power conversion efficiency. In summary, series resistance in this solar cell configuration significantly affects how well it performs, influencing key parameters and ultimately affecting its efficiency.

Effect of shunt resistance

Non-geminated coupling losses and leakage currents are significant culprits behind power losses in Perovskite Solar Cells that result from shunt resistance. Shunt resistance, often stemming from the creation of pinholes and the intrusion of material into these pinholes, can occasionally create partial shorts within the cell’s junctions. In the practical context of solar cells, these issues are particularly relevant and illustrate the significance of addressing RSh. In Fig. 13, we delve into the effects of shunt resistance (RSh) in a specific solar cell configuration, namely, the ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag setup. Shunt resistance is a pivotal factor in this configuration, and it was deliberately varied over a range from 102 to 106 Ω cm2 while holding the RS fixed at 2 Ω cm2. RSh’s influence extends to key factors of the PSC, including JSC, VOC, FF, and the overall PCE. Interestingly, as RSh increased to an impressive 1010 Ω cm2, it is worth noting that the JSC remained consistent at 18.5 mA/cm2. JSC represents the maximum current attainable when a solar cell is exposed to sunlight with virtually no voltage across it. Without adequate shunt resistance, some of the generated currents can take unintended paths, diminishing JSC. However, increasing RSh greatly reduces the chances of this current bypass, resulting in a more stable and consistent JSC. Simultaneously, an augmentation in VOC was observed, rising from 0.735 to 0.752 V at 1010 Ω cm2. This improvement in Voc is closely tied to the reduction of recombination losses. Shunt resistance effectively minimizes leakage currents, thereby decreasing the likelihood of recombination61. By isolating the active photovoltaic region from unintended pathways, a higher concentration of photogenerated carriers is maintained, helping to raise the Voc. Conversely, RSh has a substantial influence on the fill factor, which evaluates the effectiveness of charge capture and transfer within the solar cell. With increasing RSh, there was a notable increase in FF, rising from 54 to 78%. This boost in FF can be attributed to the reduction of shunting losses. When shunting losses occur it means that the carriers produced by light are directed towards unintended paths, which reduces the amount of current that the solar cell can effectively produce. However, increased RSh mitigates these losses by offering a high-resistance path for leakage and shunt currents, ultimately improving FF. Furthermore, the overall PCE, calculated as the product of JSC, FF, and VOC, is directly influenced by the series resistance. Notably, PCE exhibited a significant increase, rising from 7.5 to 10.5% as the series resistance increased. In summary, shunt resistance plays a vital role in addressing power losses in PSCs. It stabilizes Jsc, increases Voc through the reduction of recombination losses, improves FF by mitigating shunting losses, and ultimately enhances PCE. This comprehensive analysis underscores the critical importance of managing RSh to optimize the efficiency of PSC.

Effect of temperature

Figure 14 illustrates the influence of temperature variations on the fulfillment parameters of ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag, spanning an operating temperature ranging from 280 to 360 K under 1000 W2 sunlight. In traditional semiconductor materials, the correlation between temperature and PSC properties is thoroughly-documented. Examining the remarks in Fig. 14 reveals that the primary property influenced by temperature fluctuations in conventional solar cells is the fill factor. As temperature rises, the FF diminishes leading to an ongoing increase in the overcurrent. This escalation in saturation current arises from a shift in intrinsic carrier concentrations as temperatures elevate, leading to heightened recombination rates, significantly impacting FF. The connection between short circuit current and temperature in Photovoltaic Solar Cells follows a linear pattern, akin to the relationship between temperature and VOC. In PSC, JSC is frequently expressed as the result of multiplying the collection fraction by the ideal current. The current produced if the majority of incident photons with energies greater than the band gap are absorbed without any losses is known as the ideal current. Conversely, the collection fraction is influenced by elements like charge carrier reflection, transmission, parasitic absorption, and recombination within the solar cell. Notably, in Conventional Solar Cells (CSCs), variations in CSCs with temperature are markedly lesser compared to the variations observed in VOC. Though conventional PSC, generally display a linear dependence of JSC on temperature, the behavior of CSCs with temperature in PSC remains less clear62. This uncertainty stems from the fact that an increase in the band gap in PSCs leads to a minimized ideal current, while the gathering portion typically inverses through temperature. Subsequently, the trend of change in JSC with temperature in Modified Solar Cells (MSCs) hinges on the main effect of these elements. In essence, the interaction among the decrease in ideal current and the rise in aggregation fraction dictates the direction of change in JSC as temperature fluctuates.

In the study of KSnI₃-based solar cells, our analysis is based on the generation of contour maps illustrating key performance parameters: VOC, JSC, FF, and PCE. Figure 15(a) shows that VOC decreases significantly with increasing NT across all absorber thicknesses. The highest VOC values, approximately 0.86 V, are achieved at low defect density (1013 cm− 3) and a thin absorber layer of around 0.3 μm. Beyond this thickness, and as NT increases, VOC drops, mainly due to enhanced recombination and elevated series resistance. In Figure.15(b), the behavior of JSC is primarily governed by the absorber thickness, showing a consistent increase with thickness, while remaining relatively unaffected by NT values up to 1015 cm− 3. The optimal JSC, around 18.32 mA/cm2, is obtained for absorber thicknesses greater than 1.0 μm, attributed to enhanced optical absorption, especially in the longer wavelength region, facilitated by a thicker active layer. Figure.15(c) demonstrates that FF declines with increasing NT. The highest FF values, reaching up to 86.6%, are observed for low to moderate defect densities (1013-1014 cm−3) and absorber thicknesses between 0.6 μm and 1.2 μm. This trend reflects the detrimental effect of defect-induced recombination on charge extraction and transport. Finally, Figure.15(d) reveals that the maximum PCE, approximately 13.46%, occurs at a defect density of 1013 cm− 3 and an absorber thickness of about 1.2 μm. Beyond this point, PCE declines noticeably as NT increases, consistent with the reductions in VOC and FF. This efficiency degradation results from increased charge carrier recombination and a reduction in charge collection efficiency due to the higher density of trap states.

Influence of effect electron affinity of CeO2 and CBTS

In our investigation into KSnI3-based Perovskite Solar Cells, we utilize contour maps to visually represent key parameters, including output parameters. These illustrations offer insights into the effects of varying the Electron affinity (EA) of CeO2 (in eV) from 4.5 to 4.7 eV and varying the EA of CBTS from 3.4 to 3.8 eV, as shown in Fig. 9. A noteworthy observation is that the maximum VOC values, which are within the range of 0.83–0.85 V, are attained when the EA of CeO2 (in eV) is finely tuned to 4.5 eV, and the EA of CBTS falls within the range of 3.4 to 3.8 eV. The differences in VOC due to changes in EA are attributable to the impact of EA on the energy levels in the material of the solar cell. Additionally, Fig. 16(a) provides information about how VOC progressively reduces as the EA of CeO2 falls below 4.55 eV and the EA of CBTS ranges from 3.4 to 3.8 eV. To understand this phenomenon, we need to take into account the complex interactions between the EA of CeO2 and CBTS. The decrease in VOC observed in Fig. 16(a) is due to the careful interaction between the EA of CeO2 and CBTS. When the EA of CeO2 exceeds 4.55 eV, it introduces a material energy alignment into the PSC configuration. Moving to Fig. 16(b), we explore how the Short-Circuit Current responds to variations in EA. The maximum JSC values, around 18.32 mA/cm2, manifest when the EA of CBTS equals or surpasses 4.65 eV, and the EA of CBTS falls within the range of 3.4 to 3.8 eV. This increase in JSC is due to the synergistic interaction between the EA of CeO2 and CBTS. When the EA of CeO2 reaches or exceeds 4.65 eV, it offers substantial support for efficient absorption of light, which is crucial for the generation of a greater number of electron/hole pairs, which significantly increases the JSC. In addition, selecting an EA within the specified range of 3.4–3.8 eV is essential for improving JSC, as it facilitates efficient electron-hole pair generation upon photon absorption. Transitioning to Fig. 16(c), we examine how the fill factor behaves in response to changes in the EA of CeO2. The maximum FF values, about 83%, appear when CeO2’s EA is between 4.5 and 4.6 eV, and the EA of CBTS ranges from 4.4 to 4.8 eV. The increase in FF largely depends on two important factors: the EA of CeO2 and the EA of CBTS. Optimizing the EA of CeO2 within the range of 4.5–4.7 eV produces an environment that facilitates the efficient migration of charge carriers generated by incident light to the respective electrodes. This equilibrium permits a sufficient amount of material to absorb light while ensuring that charge carriers move swiftly across the absorber layer, reducing resistive losses. Furthermore, the electron energy levels within the designated range of the CBTS align optimally with the bandgap of the absorber material. This alignment lowers recombination events and increases FF by facilitating effective charge carrier separation and collection. Deviations from this optimal range may result in a significant decrease in FF. The maximum efficiency, roughly reaching 13.46%, is attained by carefully adjusting the Electron Affinity (EA) of CeO2 in the range of 4.5 to 4.7 eV and EA of CBTS ranging from 3.4 to 3.8 eV, as depicted in Fig. 16(d). This efficiency aligns with our previous findings for JSC, FF, and VOC, peaking at 13.46% when the EA of CeO2 is optimized precisely at 4.51 eV, and the EA of CBTS is maintained within the range of 3.4 to 3.8 eV. This study emphasizes the crucial impact of the EA of CeO2 and EA of CBTS on photovoltaic parameters, providing valuable insights into optimizing PSC parameters for more effective and efficient photovoltaic systems.

Conclusion

In this study, a novel KSnI3-based heterostructure solar cell is being designed and optimized using Density Functional Theory (DFT) and SCAPS-1D simulations. The electrical and optical properties of KSnI3 are being characterized, and its integration into an ITO/CeO2/KSnI3/CBTS/Ag structure is being numerically analyzed to enhance photovoltaic performance. The optimized solar cell is achieving a PCE of 13.46%, with key parameters including a KSnI3 absorber layer thickness of 1.4 μm, defect density of 1014 cm–3, electron affinity of 3.44 eV, ETL (CeO2) thickness of 0.6 μm, HTL (CBTS) thickness of 0.8 μm, Rs of 1 Ω cm2, and Rsh of 104 Ω cm2. The optimized device is exhibiting a Voc of 0.86 V, Jsc of 21.5 mA/cm2, and FF of 86.05%. The study is demonstrating that increasing absorber thickness improves Jsc, while higher defect density reduces overall efficiency. Furthermore, tuning the electron affinity of CeO2 and CBTS is optimizing charge carrier transport and minimizing recombination losses. These results are providing valuable insights into the design of high-efficiency, lead-free KSnI₃-based perovskite solar cells, contributing to the advancement of sustainable and environmentally friendly photovoltaic technologies.
