DIVERGE v4: A Platform for Large-Scale Analysis of Functional Divergence Across Multi-Gene Families
Authors: Yichang Chen, Xiaobin Xu, Yi Pan, Siqi Wang, Wenyi Zhao, Binbin Zhou, Jingqi Zhou, Yichun Zheng, Zhan Zhou, Xun Gu, Koichiro Tamura
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene duplication and subsequent divergence are fundamental drivers in the evolution of novel gene functions. The DIVERGE software, widely utilized for identifying amino acid residues critical to functional shifts between protein subfamilies, has been substantially upgraded to version 4 to address modern bioinformatics challenges. This major release features a modular Python package for enhanced workflow integration and a novel algorithm for scalable analysis of large gene families. Notably, we introduce DIVERGE-Chat, pioneering the application of a conversational artificial intelligence (AI) agent in the field of functional divergence, enabling intuitive, code-free exploration (https://pgx.zju.edu.cn/diverge). We have also developed a complementary interactive online web server that allows users to upload local files for interactive analysis, facilitating more flexible analysis. Furthermore, we curated phylogenetic data for 4,540 human gene families and conducted functional divergence analyses, generating a comprehensive resource accessible online. Collectively, these advancements significantly improve the accuracy, scalability, usability, and integration capabilities of functional divergence analysis, empowering broader research into protein evolution, functional specificity, and biomedical applications.Keywords: functional divergence, protein evolution, protein function specificity, multi-gene family, conversational AI Agent

Introduction

Gene duplication and subsequent divergence play an important part in the evolution of novel gene functions (Wolfe and Shields 1997; Gu et al. 2002; Innan and Kondrashov 2010; Gu 2021, 2024). Functional divergence is critical in generating functional variation within protein families during evolution (Zou et al. 2011; Srivastava et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2015; Hendelman et al. 2021). Understanding the functional divergence helps researchers understand how biological functions diversify within a protein family. In recent years, considerable progress (Chagoyen et al. 2016; Penev et al. 2021; Pitarch et al. 2021; Freiberger et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023) has been made in developing computational tools for the analysis of functional divergence.

Compared to other programs, a distinctive feature of DIVERGE is its use of an explicit evolutionary framework that simultaneously integrates protein sequence data and phylogenetic relationships to assess functional divergence. DIVERGE is underpinned by a rigorous statistical inference framework (Gu 1999, 2001, 2006; Zhou et al. 2007) that employs distinct statistical models to formally test for Type-I and Type-II functional divergence, respectively, thereby ensuring a robust evaluation of statistical significance. Furthermore, DIVERGE also provides an integrated statistical toolkit (see Supplementary Material) for the comprehensive analysis of functional divergence. Type-I divergence reflects site-specific evolutionary rate changes between subfamilies, whereas Type-II divergence involves shifts in site-specific amino acid properties under functional constraints. Using user-provided multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) and predefined protein subgroups, DIVERGE identifies links between evolutionary sequence patterns and functional characteristics (Gu and Vander Velden 2002; Gu et al. 2013). Specifically, DIVERGE detects site-specific functional divergence by identifying statistically significant shifts in evolutionary rates between protein subfamilies (Gu 2001, 2006). These divergence-implicated sites, identified from MSAs alongside highly conserved positions, often serve as indicators of functionally important regions. Moreover, these functionally divergent sites are thought to contribute significantly to subfamily-specific functions within homologous protein families (Madabushi et al. 2002). They may form three-dimensional clusters constituting ligand- or protein-binding interfaces (Rausell et al. 2010; Sa et al. 2017), or represent residue networks involved in allosteric regulation (Rodriguez et al. 2010). Identifying these specific sites provides crucial insights into the atomic-level determinants of functional specificity. This understanding can enhance disease mechanism elucidation (Campbell et al. 2010; Wu et al. 2021), inform drug discovery and therapeutic targeting strategies (Brill et al. 2015; Sa et al. 2017), and guide protein engineering efforts, such as modifying enzyme substrate specificity (Espaillat et al. 2014; Ratnikov et al. 2014).

In the era of big data, reliable software for large-scale functional divergence analysis is essential for studying protein functional specificity through data-driven methods. Since its initial release, DIVERGE has gained widespread adoption (Fenton et al. 2020; Wei et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2021; Pascarelli and Laurino 2022; Dong et al. 2023) and has undergone multiple updates; however, these earlier versions were constrained in handling large-scale computations due to a basic pairwise algorithm that processed only two pre-specified duplicate clusters per round. As gene family sizes increased, this limitation resulted in exponential growth in duplicate pairs and significant estimation errors (see Materials and Methods). Furthermore, despite the critical importance of functional divergence for identifying key protein residues, user-friendly tools that integrate seamlessly into contemporary computational workflows have been lacking. To address these shortcomings, we have redeveloped the DIVERGE framework in the widely used Python language, incorporating a modular API for seamless integration into analytical pipelines; these enhancements facilitate more complex functional analyses, optimize computational performance, and introduce a new, statistically robust algorithm for large-scale functional divergence analysis in multi-gene families.

Beyond algorithmic enhancements, another major update significantly improves the usability of our analysis tools. We recognized that traditional functional divergence analysis of protein families often requires laborious phylogenetic data curation. This complexity not only complicates the interpretation of predictions but also restricts access for researchers lacking expertise in bioinformatics or phylogenetics. Inspired by the rise of innovative large language models (LLMs) and dialogue systems in bioinformatics, we have developed DIVERGE-Chat, a pioneering conversational LLM-powered agentic workflow tailored for functional divergence analysis of protein families. As the first platform of its kind, DIVERGE-Chat enables zero-code exploration through intuitive dialogues, thereby democratizing access to these powerful analyses. Furthermore, we developed a complementary interactive online web server that allows users to upload local files for interactive analysis, facilitating more flexible analysis. In addition, this work includes the curation of a comprehensive resource of functional divergence analyses for 4,540 human protein families, which users can query directly from the platform.

In summary, this updated DIVERGE framework, along with DIVERGE-Chat and associated resources, addresses key limitations in functional divergence analysis by improving scalability, usability, and integration. These advancements not only facilitate more efficient research on protein evolution but also empower a broader range of scientists to uncover functional insights in the post-genomic era.

Results

The Framework of DIVERGE v4

The DIVERGE effectively addresses two key questions: whether the functional divergence between duplicate genes is statistically significant; and if so, how to identify the amino acid sites primarily responsible for these functional divergences. Type-I functional divergence arises from significant differences in evolutionary rates at specific sites between two subgroups of a protein family, indicating that the functional constraints at these positions differ between the groups. Similarly, certain positions may exhibit a subfamily-specific conservation pattern: conserved across all subfamilies but involving different amino acids in each. This Type-II divergence results from rate changes where purifying selection leads to similar conservation levels for different amino acid types across subfamilies. Another notable feature of DIVERGE is its evaluation of a Bayesian profile for each group, measuring how individual amino acid residues contribute to the functional divergences. We have re-engineered the DIVERGE software from a C++ desktop application into a Python package. Previous versions of DIVERGE were limited to specific platforms and faced challenges with visualization and computational scalability. To address these issues, DIVERGE v4 has been redesigned as an open-source, modular, and scalable tool, featuring 10 Python modules that handle various types of analyses related to the functional divergence of protein families. This redesign enables users to seamlessly incorporate specific DIVERGE modules into their existing computational workflows. Leveraging Python’s high-performance libraries, we have enhanced DIVERGE’s computational efficiency using external packages such as scikit-bio and NumPy. Additionally, to support large-scale computations, we have implemented a new algorithm called Super-Cluster, further optimizing performance. These improvements enhance cross-platform compatibility, accessibility, and flexibility, enabling users to perform complex analyses more efficiently and effectively.

Given that many users of the DIVERGE software may lack programming expertise or sufficient computational resources, we have developed a user-friendly web server that implements the algorithmic framework via our Python package architecture. The data loading processes (e.g. subfamily division) and result displays (e.g. single-site result presentation) are designed with interactive mouse-click functionality to simplify usage. Furthermore, we have streamlined the computational workflow into a user interface, thereby reducing computational complexity by requiring only a valid MSA and the corresponding phylogenetic tree to generate and access results. For users needing advanced customization capabilities, the modular Python package remains available as an alternative option. The web server is built on the Streamlit framework (see Materials and Methods), which streamlines website development, supports individual user sessions, and eliminates the limitation of queuing during analytical tasks.For those seeking maximum flexibility and integration into bespoke pipelines, the underlying Python package remains available in its modular form for expert users. The availability of these alternative interfaces ensures that the new DIVERGE platform (Fig. 1) is accessible and adaptable to a range of user needs and expertise levels.

DIVERGE-Chat: An LLM-Powered Functional Divergence Analysis Platform

While the redesigned software framework and user-friendly web server significantly enhance the accessibility of DIVERGE, navigating complex functional divergence analysis workflows can still pose challenges for researchers without extensive bioinformatics expertise. To address this issue and further reduce the barrier to entry, we developed an innovative conversational interface powered by an LLM chatflow, named DIVERGE-Chat (Fig. 2). This agent facilitates code-free functional divergence analysis through natural language interaction. DIVERGE-Chat was developed by integrating multiple agents that support tool calls and multi-turn memory. Leveraging advanced natural language processing capabilities, DIVERGE-Chat interprets user requests expressed in a conversational manner. Based on this understanding, the agent autonomously decides on and coordinates the analysis workflow by (1) querying integrated resource modules (see Materials and Methods) to retrieve relevant, curated data on protein families and phylogenies; (2) programmatically invoking the appropriate DIVERGE computational tools to perform functional divergence analysis; and (3) systematically summarizing and analyzing the results to present key findings and their biological significance in an accessible format. All intermediate processes are presented as a chain of thought in the ’Thinking’ process and culminate in the output of a complete analysis report. This interactive process enables users to efficiently explore patterns of functional divergence within protein families. Furthermore, DIVERGE-Chat facilitates more in-depth iterative exploration through its site-specific analysis capabilities: users can request follow-up analyses, including the identification and examination of specific residue positions predicted to be critical for functional divergence.

A critical challenge in applying general LLM agent to specialised scientific domains, such as phylogenetics, is ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the underlying data. Conventional LLM-driven searches from public databases frequently fail to retrieve the precise, high-quality protein family and phylogenetic data required for robust functional divergence analysis. To address this issue, a comprehensive Resource module has been developed to empower the DIVERGE-Chat agent with reliable domain knowledge. This curated knowledge base focuses on human and vertebrate protein families, derived from systematic processing of the PANTHER database. It encompasses 4,540 divergent human protein families, including 10,133 genes, with curated phylogenetic relationships at the vertebrate level and 215,480 protein sequences for MSA (see Materials and Methods). The agent queries this Resource module to access high-fidelity data, thereby enabling accurate responses. For functional divergence tasks, the agent invokes tools to perform Type-I, Type-II, or Super-Cluster analyses, synthesizing results into comprehensive summaries that highlight key divergence patterns. For users requesting site-specific analysis, the agent isolates relevant residue positions, applies divergence metrics, and provides detailed interpretations of site-level functional shifts. The online platform’s Resource module also offers users a more direct approach, providing access to phylogenetic visualizations, divergence results, and integrated functional annotations (Gene Ontology, pathways, PANTHER protein classes) for deeper biological insights (The Gene Ontology Consortium 2019).

Super-Cluster: Divergence Profiling of Multi-Gene Families

When investigating the specific functions or functional divergence of a subfamily within a superfamily, traditional methods rely on pairwise comparisons of subfamilies, yielding numerous “one-to-one” results. This approach is highly time-consuming (see Supplementary Material), and for superfamilies with many subfamilies, it can become computationally infeasible. Additionally, the information derived from these “one-to-one” comparisons tends to be disorganized, complicating the integration of functional divergence patterns for a given subfamily and potentially leading to the omission of more complex patterns.

Here we propose an efficient method, called Super-Cluster analysis (see Materials and Methods), to tackle the challenge when the number of subfamilies (m) in a superfamily is large. By dividing the subfamilies into two groups in all possible ways without considering their evolutionary relationships, we can generate 2m−1−1 Super-Cluster pairs. We hypothesize that the DIVERGE analysis of these Super-Cluster pairs can identify functional divergence sites that capture a broad spectrum of functional divergence types. Instead of relying on traditional one-to-one comparisons, we introduce a Super-Cluster functional divergence profile (Q-profile), which integrates all possible grouping patterns, allowing users to intuitively explore the functional divergence of individual subfamilies within the protein family. Compared to conventional “one-to-one” functional divergence analysis, the Super-Cluster approach not only reduces computational time but also provides a clearer and more comprehensive description of functional divergence. Furthermore, this grouping strategy utilizes sequence information from the entire protein family to calculate an overview of the functional divergence for each subfamily, thereby yielding statistically more robust results. To evaluate the performance of Super-Cluster, we first benchmarked it against several state-of-the-art methods using simulated datasets (see Supplementary Material), following an evaluation framework consistent with previous studies (Gaston et al. 2011). Super-Cluster consistently outperformed all competing methods across key metrics, including the AUC-ROC, the AUC-PR, and the average rank of true positive sites. Furthermore, analysis of 56 real protein families with manual curation revealed that Super-Cluster successfully identifies functional divergence patterns often missed by conventional methods, particularly in scenarios where divergence is confined to a single subfamily within a large protein family (see Supplementary Table S3).

DIVERGE-Based Exploration of Subfamily Specificity in the ErbB Family

The analysis of functional divergence enhances our understanding of functional specificity within homologous protein families, which is critical for applications in biomedical and biotechnological fields. Previous studies (Sa et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2021) have highlighted the significant potential of the DIVERGE algorithm in drug target discovery and elucidating disease mechanisms. A major update in the latest version of DIVERGE is the inclusion of the Super-Cluster algorithm, which enables statistically robust functional divergence analysis and facilitates a more intuitive examination of the functional divergence characteristics of specific subfamilies within a protein family. To illustrate the features and application of the new DIVERGE, we analyzed the human ErbB (Erythroblastic Leukemia Viral Oncogene Homolog) family. The ErbB family consists of four highly homologous members: EGFR, ERBB2, ERBB3, and ERBB4, all sharing a conserved structure comprising an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain, a transmembrane domain, and a glycosylated extracellular ligand-binding domain. Notably, ERBB3 is unique among the ErbB family members, as it is generally considered to lack tyrosine kinase activity or have impaired activity. Our Super-Cluster analysis identified a substantial number of functional divergence sites within the tyrosine kinase domain of ERBB3 (Fig. 3a), suggesting that these key residues may be responsible for locking the domain in an inactive conformation, thereby resulting in a lack of catalytic kinase activity.

Furthermore, we compared the results of traditional DIVERGE analysis with those of the Super-Cluster method (Fig. 3b). The results clearly demonstrate that the Super-Cluster approach can replace numerous one-to-one comparisons with a single computation, effectively identifying functional divergence sites that might be overlooked by traditional DIVERGE analysis. For example, residue position 1262 was identified as a site of potential functional divergence in the Super-Cluster analysis, with a high posterior probability (P>0.8). This stringent threshold corresponds to a posterior odds ratio greater than 4 (see Supplementary Material). In contrast, the conventional DIVERGE method yielded a probability below 0.6 for this position. This site corresponds to amino acid 855 in ERBB3 and 858 in EGFR. The L858R mutation in the tyrosine kinase domain of EGFR is a well-known activating mutation (Shigematsu and Gazdar 2006). With this mutation, the autoinhibitory conformation of EGFR is suppressed, rendering the receptor active even without ligand binding. Similarly, the V855A mutation in ERBB3 is also a reported oncogenic mutation, but it requires co-expression with ERBB2 to confer gain of function. MSA shows that the mutated residue is highly conserved across ERBB3 homologs in different mammalian species. It is indicated that functional divergence at this site might led to the preservation of a new function, making it a crucial site for subfamily-specific functions and contributing to the functional diversity of the protein family.

This case study demonstrates that Super-Cluster can efficiently identify subfamily-specific functional sites and provide intuitive explanations for functional divergence phenomena within large protein families, thereby offering new insights into the functional diversity of protein families. Additionally, the feasibility of implementation in a big-data environment is underscored by this case.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce DIVERGE v4, an enhanced platform for functional divergence analysis. Through algorithmic improvements (Super-Cluster), a refined modular Python architecture, an improved user experience via a web server and the novel DIVERGE-Chat interface, and robust data support utilizing curated phylogenetic resources, we have significantly advanced the platform’s accuracy, scalability, usability, and integration capabilities. DIVERGE v4 not only processes large-scale datasets efficiently but also facilitates code-free exploratory analysis through DIVERGE-Chat, rendering functional divergence studies more intuitive and accessible. DIVERGE-Chat represents the first application of an LLM-powered agent in the functional divergence domain, substantially lowering the entry barrier for researchers and promoting broader recognition of the biological importance of functional divergence. A current limitation is that DIVERGE-Chat relies on our curated phylogenetic datasets, as conventional functional divergence analysis necessitates comprehensive protein family phylogenies. Consequently, a key future direction is the development of phylogeny-independent functional divergence methods to expand DIVERGE-Chat’s capabilities.

Looking ahead, the field of functional divergence analysis stands at the cusp of a significant transformation with the emergence of advanced artificial intelligence (AI) technologies. Recent advances in large-scale pretrained language models (Hsu et al. 2022; Fan et al. 2025) and deep learning methods have demonstrated remarkable capabilities in capturing evolutionary relationships between proteins and long-range co-evolutionary patterns between residues. These models (Hie et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2024; Hayes et al. 2025), trained on vast amounts of protein sequence data, naturally learn to encode evolutionary information and protein family relationships, making them particularly well-suited for evolution-related tasks. They are effective at decoding distant mutation coupling effects (Cheng et al. 2023; Jiang et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025) that traditional methods often struggle to identify. Moreover, these models’ zero-shot inference capabilities offer promising potential for predicting functional divergence in the protein “twilight zone” (Himmel et al. 2023), where sequence similarity is too low for conventional methods to make reliable predictions.

The modular architecture and curated datasets of DIVERGE v4 provide an ideal foundation for integrating these AI-driven approaches. Integrating DIVERGE’s robust statistical framework with advanced AI technologies promises enhanced sensitivity for detecting subtle patterns of functional divergence and improved predictive accuracy in challenging or low-signal cases. This integration could lead to more sophisticated analyses of protein functional divergence, particularly where traditional methods exhibit limitations. As the field continues to evolve, the flexible architecture of DIVERGE ensures its adaptability, allowing for the incorporation of new methodologies while maintaining its core strength in evolutionary analysis.

Materials and Methods

Software and Tools

All computational analyses were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel i5-13400 CPU (2.5 GHz) and 32 GB of RAM. The primary analytical environment was Python (v3.10.10), supported by the pandas (v1.5.3) and NumPy (v1.23.5) libraries for data manipulation and numerical operations, respectively. Multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) were generated using MAFFT (v7.520) with parameters set to –auto –anysymbol –quiet. The –auto option automatically selects an appropriate algorithm based on alignment size: L-INS-i for fewer than 200 sequences, FFT-NS-i for 200–2,000 sequences, or FFT-NS-2 for more than 2,000 sequences. All alignments utilized the BLOSUM62 scoring matrix with default gap penalties. Simulation datasets were generated using IQ-Tree (v3.0.1). For specialized analyses, the speer and subsite tools were executed using the source code provided in their original publications. The speer script was run using Perl (v5.38.2), while the subsite source code was compiled and executed using GCC (v13.1.0). Furthermore, the groupsim tool, originally developed for Python 2, was refactored for compatibility with Python 3 and run in the same Python (v3.10.10) environment.

Collection and Processing of Datasets

We utilized phylogenetic data from the PANTHER (Protein Analysis THrough Evolutionary Relationships) database (Thomas et al. 2022) and, after rigorous filtering and selection, constructed 4,540 “protein families” comprising 10,133 human genes and 215,480 protein sequences.

The PANTHER database generates fully reconciled phylogenetic trees through a combination of software-generated models and expert review, providing phylogenetically related data for thousands of protein families. As PANTHER was designed to model evolutionary sequence-function relationships on a large scale, many of the families it defines resemble extensive superfamilies, encompassing numerous subfamilies. In contrast, DIVERGE focuses more specifically on the functional divergence that occurs within individual protein families following gene duplication events. Consequently, the family definitions in DIVERGE are closer to a group of genes that evolved after the speciation of single-metazoan animals (Schreiber et al. 2014). In this study, we selected nodes from PANTHER-generated phylogenies where gene duplication events occurred and identified potential nodes for functional divergence, using these as candidate genes to construct the ”protein families”.

DIVERGE employs complete deletion of gaps during computation, whereas the MSAs provided by PANTHER database are generated using genomic data from 143 species. As a result, the MSA for each “protein family” tend to be lengthy and contain a significant number of gaps, leading to a reduced number of non-gap sites. When the total number of non-gap sites is low, the variance increases, leading to less accurate estimates. To mitigate this issue, we selected 19 vertebrate species for constructing the “protein families” and performed MSA on their protein sequences using the MAFFT (Katoh and Standley 2013) software with default parameters. To increase the proportion of non-gap sites, we further filtered the sequences by penalizing the gap sites in the alignment. The fewer gaps in a given column, the higher the gap penalty score assigned to that column. We then applied clustering methods to exclude species sequences that significantly contributed to the overall gap count in the alignment. For phylogenetic tree construction, we extracted subtrees directly from the large phylogenetic trees of PANTHER families. These phylogenetic trees have undergone expert review, offering more accurate evolutionary relationships compared to trees constructed solely from the MSAs of the 19 selected vertebrate species.

Construction of DIVERGE-Chat and DIVERGE Web Server

DIVERGE-Chat was developed as a conversational assistant for functional divergence analysis, utilizing a chatflow framework that integrated multiple agents supporting tool calls and multi-turn memory. Within this framework, each agent was designed using a role-based approach, where explicit instructions defined the responsibilities and behaviors of each LLM agent. During operation, when a user queried DIVERGE-Chat, the Plan agent leveraged the LLM to understand the user’s needs and assign the appropriate agent to process the task. Specifically, the agent responsible for data querying generated SQL query statements based on natural language input, aligned with the user’s requirements, to retrieve relevant data for subsequent analysis. The primary data source consisted of curated protein family phylogenetic data and associated functional annotation information, which we had previously integrated into multiple tables using PostgreSQL. While direct retrieval from public databases might offer more flexibility, querying our curated PostgreSQL database ensured that the DIVERGE-Chat utilized accurate and consistent phylogenetic data. The agent responsible for functional divergence computation primarily invoked the modular DIVERGE python API through code blocks to perform calculations. The resulting structured outputs were then summarized and analyzed by the LLM model to provide insightful interpretations of the functional divergence results. Finally, a check agent was designed to monitor key stages of the task, verifying at these points whether the user’s requirements had been met, and periodically correcting the task as needed. This approach aimed to mitigate potential inaccuracies, such as hallucinations, thereby enhancing overall output reliability. To facilitate more complex or iterative exploration of functional divergence in protein families of interest, DIVERGE-Chat was designed with multi-turn memory functionality, allowing users to follow up on previous queries. Agents were configured to collaborate in a shared environment, where they could exchange information while maintaining role-specific autonomy.

Complementing the conversational interface, the DIVERGE web server was constructed using Streamlit, a Python library for building interactive web applications. This framework facilitated the creation of a dashboard that allowed users to load local files and request remote data from external resources via real-time API calls. The use of Streamlit enabled rapid development of this web application purely from Python scripts.

Challenges of Multi-Cluster Analysis

When studying Type-I functional divergence in a large gene family, a consistent notation system is required. Suppose that an amino acid site has two possible conservation levels: one is conserved (low substitution rate), denoted by c=0, and the other is variable (high substitution rate), denoted by c=1. The conservation mode of a gene family with m duplicate clusters can be represented as C=(c1,…,cm), where each ck has two states (0 or 1). Let λ1,…,λm be the substitution rate of a site in each cluster, respectively. The relationship among these substitution rates is as follows: for any i≠j, λi=λj if ci=cj, or λi is independent of λj if ci≠cj.

The pattern of Type-I functional divergence in a gene family can be described based on specific conservation patterns. Let S0 be the case of no Type-I functional divergence, which can be written as the conservation mode (1,1,…,1) or (0,…,0): an amino acid site is in the variable state (or the conserved state) over all duplicate genes, and λ1=,…,=λm. On the other hand, let Sk be the kth pattern of Type-I functional divergence of a gene family, where the subscript k runs from 1 to M=2m−1−1. Hence, the number of patterns of Type-I functional divergence increases exponentially with the increase of gene family size.

Algorithm of Super-Cluster

To address the challenge of analyzing functional divergence in gene families with numerous duplicate clusters, we developed the Super-Cluster method. This approach is grounded in a Finite-State Conservation model (see Supplementary Material). The model posits that at any amino acid site, each duplicate cluster can exist in one of two fundamental states: a conserved state (c=0, low evolutionary rate) or a variable state (c=1, high evolutionary rate). For a gene family with m clusters, the overall conservation pattern at a site can therefore be described by a binary vector C=(c1,c2,…,cm). Each unique vector, which we term an S-pattern, corresponds to a distinct hypothesis of Type-I functional divergence. Under this model, clusters sharing the same state (e.g. all c=0 or all c=1) are assumed to have a common evolutionary rate, while those with different states evolve independently.

The operational principle of the Super-Cluster method is to directly test for these S-patterns. Any given S-pattern inherently implies a bipartite partition of the m clusters. Our algorithm leverages this by consolidating all clusters within each part of the partition into one of two opposing ’super-clusters’. This procedure effectively reduces the complex, multi-body divergence problem into a tractable pairwise comparison, which can be robustly analyzed using established methods like DIVERGE. The central hypothesis is that a one-to-one correspondence exists between a specific S-pattern and the super-cluster partition that achieves the highest posterior probability of divergence. By systematically generating and analyzing all M possible non-trivial partitions, our approach can effectively pinpoint the most probable divergence pattern across the gene family and identify the key amino acid sites involved. This strategy circumvents the need for a direct multi-cluster mixture model, which is often computationally intractable for large m, by reframing the problem as a comprehensive series of well-posed pairwise tests.
