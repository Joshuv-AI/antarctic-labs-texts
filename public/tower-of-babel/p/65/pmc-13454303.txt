Adjunctive effects of apple cider vinegar on metabolic syndrome and insulin resistance in polycystic ovary syndrome patients receiving metformin: a randomized clinical trial
Authors: Atiyeh Najafi, Shahideh Jahanian Sadatmahalleh, Ashraf Moini, Malihe Nasiri, Fariba Hajimoosayi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a common endocrine disorder in reproductive-aged women, frequently associated with insulin resistance (IR) and metabolic syndrome (MetS). Given the limitations of conventional therapies, complementary interventions targeting metabolic pathways are needed. This randomized clinical trial investigated the adjunctive effects of apple cider vinegar (ACV) on MetS components and IR in PCOS patients receiving standard metformin treatment (1500 mg/day). In this 12-week randomized controlled trial, 94 women meeting the Rotterdam criteria for PCOS, NCEP ATP III criteria for MetS, and HOMA-IR > 2.5, were randomized 1:1 to receive either ACV capsules (1500 mg/day) or placebo. Primary outcomes were changes in IR and MetS components; secondary outcomes included serum testosterone, hirsutism (modified Ferriman–Gallwey score), sexual function (FSFI), and quality of life (mPCOSQ). The results showed resolution of MetS was significantly higher in the ACV group (74.5%) than controls (40.4%) (p = 0.001). Significant reductions in IR (p = 0.01), hirsutism (p = 0.003), and improvements in sexual function (p < 0.001) and quality of life (p = 0.001) were observed in the intervention group. These results demonstrate that ACV supplementation may offer a beneficial adjunctive strategy for improving metabolic and quality-of-life parameters in women with MetS, IR, and PCOS.Trial registration: IRCT20150905023897N7, registered on 11/12/2023.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-aged women, affecting 6–13% of this population globally1. Women with PCOS may face several challenges, including menstrual irregularities, hirsutism, acne, skin-related complications, androgenic alopecia, reproductive impairments including infertility and pregnancy complications, metabolic disturbances such as insulin resistance (IR) (present in 65–70% of patients) and metabolic syndrome (MetS) (occurring in approximately 30% of cases), as well as psychological effects including depression2,3. IR is characterized by higher-than-normal insulin concentrations required to elicit normal physiological responses, leading to hyperinsulinemia and impaired insulin function4. MetS encompasses a cluster of interrelated metabolic derangements, including central adiposity, dyslipidemia, hypertension and hyperglycemia, collectively predisposing individuals to heightened cardiometabolic risk5.

Current therapeutic approaches face significant limitations, with oral contraceptives carrying increased thrombosis risk, metformin causing gastrointestinal side effects leading to 20–30% discontinuation rates, and thiazolidinediones inducing problematic weight gain6–8. Growing evidence supports the potential of phytotherapeutic interventions for PCOS management. Cinnamon supplementation has demonstrated 0.7–1.2 point reductions in Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) values9, berberine has shown efficacy comparable to metformin in randomized controlled trials10, and spearmint tea consumption has been associated with an approximate 30% decrease in free testosterone levels, as measured in serum concentrations in relevant studies11. Apple cider vinegar (ACV), a fermented product containing 4–6% acetic acid along with active polyphenolic compounds and essential micronutrients, exhibits multiple potentially beneficial mechanisms including antihyperglycemic effects through Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARγ) activation12, lipid metabolism regulation via the Adenosine Monophosphate-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK) pathway13, and possible anti-androgenic activity14.

ACV is generally well tolerated at commonly studied doses, with only mild gastrointestinal discomfort or intolerance reported in some individuals; excessive intake may increase the risk of dental enamel erosion15–18.

Although preliminary safety and mechanistic findings are encouraging, clinical evidence on ACV’s efficacy in women with PCOS remains limited. This gap in the literature motivated our randomized controlled trial examining ACV’s impact on MetS components and IR parameters in women with PCOS, employing rigorous methodology to generate high-quality evidence for this potential complementary treatment approach.

Methods and design

Study design

This study was a phase II, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial conducted at Arash Women’s Hospital, Tehran University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran, from 13/12/2023 to 28/11/2024. The trial duration was 12 weeks. The study complied with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, and ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Tarbiat Modares University, Tehran, Iran, on 30/10/2023 (IR.MODARES.RES.1402.109). The trial was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials on 11/12/2023 (IRCT20150905023897N7). All participants were informed about the study’s objectives and potential risks and provided written informed consent before participation.

Participants

Participants were women diagnosed with PCOS, MetS, and IR who met the inclusion criteria. PCOS was diagnosed based on the Rotterdam criteria, defined by the presence of at least two of the following criteria:

The diagnosis of IR was performed using the HOMA-IR formula. HOMA-IR was calculated using the formula: [fasting serum glucose (mg/dL) × fasting serum insulin (mIU/mL)] / 40522. HOMA-IR value > 2.5 was considered indicative of IR23. Resolution of IR was defined as a post-intervention HOMA-IR value below 2.5.

Inclusion criteria included: Iranian ethnicity, age 18–45 years, non-pregnant status, non-diabetic, normotensive, absence of chronic diseases influencing PCOS (e.g., hyperprolactinemia, thyroid dysfunction) and no use of medications affecting PCOS or MetS features within the three months prior to enrollment (e.g., statins, cholesterol absorption inhibitors, or metformin). Metformin administration was initiated at the first clinical visit concurrently with the commencement of ACV or placebo capsules in all participants, according to the prescribing physician’s instructions; therefore, metformin use during the trial was not considered an exclusion criterion.

Exclusion criteria included medication nonadherence (use of < 80% of prescribed treatment)24 or unwillingness to continue participation.

Randomization and blinding

Eligible participants (n = 94) were randomly allocated to intervention and control groups using a block randomization method with a fixed block size of four and a 1:1 allocation ratio. Since two groups were involved, six possible block permutations (AABB, ABAB, ABBA, BAAB, BABA, BBAA) were manually generated. At each step, one block was randomly selected by drawing lots to determine the allocation sequence. The randomization sequence was generated in advance by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant enrollment, intervention assignment, or outcome assessment. Allocation concealment was ensured using sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE). Each envelope was opened only after the participant had been enrolled and completed the baseline assessment.

The trial was conducted in a double-blind manner: both participants and researcher were unaware of group assignments. The intervention and placebo were identical in appearance, packaging, and labeling.

Intervention

ACV and placebo (corn starch) capsules were prepared by Eadeh Daru e Pars, Kazeroun, Iran. ACV capsules contained 500 mg of ACV powder, including 350 mg of the active component according to the manufacturer’s specifications. This corresponds to a total daily intake of 1050 mg acetic acid. This is comparable to the content of approximately 20–22 mL of standard 5% liquid ACV. This dosage falls within the range (5–30 mL/day) commonly used in previous randomized clinical trials investigating metabolic outcomes in adults15,25–28.

Placebo capsules, containing 500 mg corn starch, were identical to ACV capsules in size, shape, color, and smell.

Participants in the intervention group received ACV capsules, while the control group received placebo capsules. Both groups were instructed to take three capsules daily (1500 mg total) after meals for 12 weeks. All participants also received standard PCOS treatment (metformin 500 mg TID). Participants were advised to maintain their habitual lifestyle, including diet and physical activity. A researcher contacted participants every two weeks to monitor the consumption of ACV capsules and Metformin, and to assess any side effects. Additionally, participants were asked to return their medication containers at follow-up visits to verify compliance through pill counting.

Trial procedures and assessments

Participants in both groups were evaluated at baseline and after 12 weeks. Assessments included blood pressure measurements, anthropometric parameters, biochemical indicators, and self-report questionnaires.

Measurement of blood pressure and anthropometric parameters

Blood pressure was measured using an Accumed aneroid sphygmomanometer while participants were seated and relaxed. Anthropometric measurements included height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference. Weight and height were measured using calibrated Seca instruments precisions of 0.01 kg and 0.1 cm, respectively. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) / height (m²)12. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint between the iliac crest and the lower rib, while hip circumference was measured at the widest part of the buttocks.

Measurement of biochemical indicators

A 10-mL fasting blood sample was collected after a 12-hour overnight fast. Measurements included FPG, insulin, TG, HDL, low-density lipoprotein (LDL), total cholesterol (TC), and testosterone levels. IR was evaluated using the HOMA-IR. Biochemical assays were performed using a Roche kit and the COBAS C-501 analyzer. Testosterone levels were measured using electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (ECLIA) with a SIEMENS Immulite 20,000 analyzer. All intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation were < 5%.

Questionnaire

Socio-demographic data (e.g., age, education, occupation, medical history) were collected via face-to-face interviews. Participants completed the following validated questionnaires:

1- Female sexual function Index (FSFI): A 19-item questionnaire assessing desire, arousal, lubrication, satisfaction, orgasm, and pain29. Lower scores indicate impaired sexual function. The Iranian version has been validated30. It should be noted that all participants in the study were sexually active and completed the FSFI questionnaire.

2- Modified Polycystic Ovary Syndrome Health-Related Quality of Life Questionnaire (mPCOSQ): A 30-item questionnaire evaluating emotional disturbances, hirsutism, infertility, weight, menstrual issues, and acne31. Lower scores indicate poorer quality of life. The questionnaire has been validated32.

3- Modified Ferriman-Gallwey (mFG) scoring system: A standardized tool for assessing hirsutism by evaluating terminal hair growth in nine specific body areas. Higher total scores indicate more severe hirsutism33.

Sample size calculation

Based on the findings of Gheflati et al.24, which evaluated the effect of ACV on multiple parameters, including FPG, the sample size for our study was calculated using FPG as the reference outcome. The reported between-group difference in FPG (intervention: m2 = 128.4 mg/dL, SD = 29.7; control: m1 = 153.7 mg/dL, SD = 52.7) corresponds to a Cohen’s d of approximately 0.59, indicating a medium-to-large effect size. Using this effect size, a two-sided significance level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 80%, the required total sample size was estimated to be 94 participants (47 per group). To account for an anticipated dropout rate of 10%, the final sample size was increased to 49 participants per group.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Between-group comparisons at baseline were conducted using independent samples t-test for continuous variables and Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate. Within-group comparisons (pre- and post-intervention) were performed using paired t-tests. To evaluate post-intervention differences between the intervention and control groups while adjusting for baseline values, analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was employed. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 94 women with PCOS, MetS and IR were randomly assigned to the intervention group (n = 47) and the control group (n = 47) (Fig. 1).

Safety and tolerability

One participant in the ACV group withdrew due to mild gastrointestinal discomfort. No other participants experienced adverse events severe enough to lead to withdrawal, and all interventions were generally well tolerated.

Sociodemographic characteristics

There were no statistically significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of age (p = 0.57) or years of education (p = 0.87).

Regarding occupational status, although a higher proportion of participants in the control group were employed (55.3 vs. 44.7%), and more participants in the intervention group were housewives (51.1 vs. 36.2%), these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.30).

Menstrual cycle patterns were broadly comparable between groups. Most participants in both groups reported oligomenorrhea (35–60 days), with 61.7% in the intervention group and 74.5% in the control group; the differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.22).

Parity distributions also did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.17), although the intervention group had a slightly higher proportion of nulliparous women (40.4% vs. 33.3%).

However, the proportion of women with a history of abortion was notably higher in the control group (20.0%) compared to the intervention group (6.4%), and this difference was at the threshold of statistical significance (p = 0.05). (Table 1).

Anthropometric and blood pressure measurements

At baseline, there were no significant differences between the intervention and control groups in terms of systolic blood pressure (SBP; p = 0.60), diastolic blood pressure (DBP; p = 0.22), waist circumference (p = 0.80), waist-to-hip ratio (WHR; p = 0.30), or BMI (p = 0.31).

Following the treatment, the intervention group demonstrated a significant reduction in SBP compared to the control group (p = 0.001). Moreover, within-group analysis showed a statistically significant decrease in SBP in the intervention group (p < 0.001), while the change was not significant in the control group (p = 0.13). Similarly, DBP significantly decreased in the intervention group (p = 0.008), although between-group comparisons at post-intervention did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.19).

A notable reduction in waist circumference was observed within the intervention group (p < 0.001), while the control group showed only a marginal change (p = 0.05). However, the between-group difference in waist circumference at the end of the study was not statistically significant (p = 0.38). No significant changes were observed in WHR in either group (intervention: p = 0.29; control p = 0.82), and between-group differences remained non-significant throughout the study period (p = 0.66).

Regarding BMI, a statistically significant decrease was detected in the intervention group (p < 0.001), while the control group exhibited no meaningful change (p = 0.27). The between-group difference in BMI post-intervention was statistically significant (p < 0.001), suggesting a beneficial effect of the intervention on weight. (Table 2).

Biochemical indicators

At baseline, no significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups in FPG (p = 0.14), insulin levels (p = 0.43), HOMA-IR (p = 0.23), TG (p = 0.96), HDL (p = 0.81), LDL (p = 0.44) and TC (p = 0.55).

Following the intervention, the intervention group showed a significant reduction in FPG compared to the control group (p = 0.001). Within-group analysis revealed a marked decrease in FPG in the intervention group (p < 0.001), whereas no significant change was observed in the control group (p = 0.87).

Insulin levels also decreased significantly in the intervention group (p = 0.04), while the control group showed no meaningful change (p = 0.46). Between-group differences at post-intervention were statistically significant (p = 0.03).

A similar trend was observed for IR, as measured by HOMA-IR. The intervention group exhibited a significant decrease in HOMA-IR scores (p = 0.004), while the control group remained unchanged (p = 0.42). Between-group comparisons post-intervention indicated a statistically significant improvement in the intervention group (p = 0.01).

Regarding lipid profiles, TG levels significantly declined in the intervention group (p < 0.001), and between-group analysis at follow-up showed a significantly greater reduction compared to the control group (p = 0.02).

HDL levels increased markedly in the intervention group (p < 0.001), with a near-significant difference observed between the two groups at follow-up (p < 0.001).

No significant changes were observed in LDL (intervention: p = 0.55; control: p = 0.41) and TC (intervention: p = 0.24; control: p = 0.33) in either group at post-intervention, and between-group comparisons were also non-significant (LDL: p = 0.78; TC: p = 0.50).

Finally, serum testosterone levels decreased significantly in the intervention group (p = 0.001); however, the between-group difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.82). (Table 3).

Overall, after the intervention, a notable proportion of participants in the intervention group no longer met the diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome, indicating a clinically meaningful improvement in their metabolic status. Specifically, 74.5% of participants in the intervention group experienced “resolution of MetS” (i.e., a reduction in the number of MetS components to fewer than three), compared to 40.4% of participants in the control group, with the difference being statistically significant (p = 0.001).

Additionally, insulin resistance resolved in 17.0% of participants in the intervention group, compared to 8.5% in the control group, as defined by a post-intervention HOMA-IR value below 2.5. Although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.35).

Hirsutism (mFG scores)

No significant difference was observed in mFG scores between the intervention and control groups at baseline (p = 0.64). However, after the 12-week intervention, participants in the intervention group exhibited a significant reduction in mFG scores (p < 0.001) while no significant change was detected in the control group (p = 0.64). Between-group comparison at the end of the study indicated a statistically significant difference in favor of the intervention group (p = 0.003), suggesting a beneficial effect of the intervention on mFG score. Sexual function (FSFI scores)

After 12 weeks of intervention, participants in the ACV group exhibited significant improvements in various domains of sexual function, as assessed by the Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI). Within-group analysis in intervention group revealed notable increases in desire (p < 0.001), arousal (p < 0.001), lubrication (p = 0.001), orgasm (p < 0.001), and pain reduction during intercourse (p < 0.001), while satisfaction scores did not show a statistically significant change (p = 0.24). The total FSFI score also improved significantly in the intervention group compared to baseline (p < 0.001).

In terms of between-group comparisons, statistically significant improvements were observed in desire (p = 0.001), lubrication (p = 0.024), orgasm (p = 0.01), pain (p = 0.005), and total FSFI score (p < 0.001) in the intervention group compared to the control. No significant differences were found in the arousal or satisfaction domains between groups at the end of the intervention. (Table 6).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first double-blind, randomized clinical trial to investigate the effects of ACV supplementation in women with PCOS who also meet the diagnostic criteria for both MetS and IR. Given the high prevalence and clinical burden of IR and MetS in this population34,35, low-cost and accessible interventions, such as ACV may provide meaningful adjunctive benefits. This study aimed to evaluate whether daily intake of 1500 mg of ACV for 12 weeks, as an adjunct to metformin therapy, could improve metabolic, quality-of-life and sexual function outcomes in this high-risk population.

Our findings demonstrate that ACV supplementation over 12 weeks significantly reduced the prevalence of MetS in this population. The higher rate of MetS resolution in the intervention group compared to control suggests that ACV exerts beneficial effects on multiple metabolic parameters simultaneously. Improvements were particularly evident in SBP, FPG, and HDL levels, whereas TG showed a statistically significant but modest absolute reduction, suggesting limited clinical impact for this individual parameter. Although waist circumference and DBP decreased significantly within the intervention group, the between-group difference was not statistically significant, indicating that the effect on waist circumference and DBP may require a longer duration or larger sample size to become evident. Overall, these results support the potential of ACV as a multi-targeted adjunct therapy, producing clinically meaningful benefits when considering the combined improvements across multiple metabolic parameters.

ACV supplementation significantly reduced fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR scores; although the reduction in HOMA-IR was statistically significant, the absolute change was modest, suggesting limited clinical impact for this parameter. Nevertheless, the overall improvement in multiple metabolic outcomes—including FPG, insulin, HDL, and the resolution of metabolic syndrome—highlights the multi-faceted benefits of ACV in this population. Considering the central role of IR in the pathophysiology of PCOS and its related metabolic complications36, these findings suggest that ACV may contribute to better metabolic and reproductive outcomes by alleviating IR.

In the present study, the lack of improvement within the metformin-only group underscores the severity of the PCOS-MetS phenotype. With a high baseline HOMA-IR (mean 4.75), these participants exhibited a degree of metabolic resistance that was not sufficiently overcome by 1500 mg/day of metformin monotherapy alone within the 12-week timeframe. This suggests that in such high-risk individuals, standard pharmacological treatment as a single agent may not be adequate to effectively manage the progression of insulin resistance in the short term, highlighting the critical role of ACV as a synergistic agent to complement metformin and help overcome this metabolic inertia.

In this study, ACV supplementation led to a significant reduction in hirsutism scores, as assessed by the mFG questionnaire. Although serum testosterone levels did not differ significantly between the two groups post-intervention, the observed improvement in mFG scores suggests a clinical benefit in hyperandrogenic symptoms. This discrepancy between biochemical and clinical outcomes may reflect the multifactorial nature of hirsutism and the influence of peripheral androgen sensitivity37. These findings highlight the potential of ACV as a supportive approach in managing clinical hyperandrogenism in women with PCOS.

ACV supplementation was associated with statistically and clinically meaningful improvements in several domains of sexual function, as measured by the FSFI. Notable improvements were observed in desire, lubrication, orgasm, and pain, while no significant change was found in the arousal or satisfaction domains. The satisfaction domain assesses overall sexual contentment and emotional closeness, suggesting that although ACV may enhance specific physiological aspects of sexual function, broader relational or emotional satisfaction may require longer-term intervention or additional supportive measures. Beyond sexual function, ACV supplementation also contributed to improvements in quality of life, including enhanced emotional wellbeing, improved menstrual regularity, reduced weight-related concerns, and alleviated infertility-related distress.

Although LDL and total TC were measured, these parameters were not primary outcomes of the study, and no significant differences were observed between the groups.

Interestingly, BMI showed a statistically significant reduction in the intervention group compared to the control group. This finding suggests a potential beneficial effect of the intervention on adiposity, which may contribute to the overall metabolic improvements observed and warrants further investigation in future clinical trials.

These results corroborate previous findings. For instance, Wu et al.38 reported that daily consumption of 15 g of apple vinegar for 90–110 days in PCOS patients led to a reduction in HOMA-IR and improved menstrual regularity, although no changes in total testosterone levels were observed. Similarly, Jafarirad et al.26 found that 30 mL of ACV daily in patients with type 2 diabetes significantly reduced FPG and HbA1c, but unlike our study, they also observed reductions in LDL and TC. Abou-Khalil et al.15 demonstrated that daily consumption of 15 mL of ACV for 4–12 weeks resulted in significant reductions in anthropometric measures (weight, BMI), TG, and blood glucose, with a notable decrease in TC levels. In a similar vein, Khezri et al.28 reported that 30 mL of ACV daily for 12 weeks in overweight or obese individuals led to significant reductions in BMI, hip circumference, appetite score, visceral adiposity index, TG, and TC, alongside an increase in HDL levels. Furthermore, Shahinfar et al.39 found that each 30 mL daily increment in vinegar consumption reduced systolic and diastolic blood pressure by -3.25 mmHg and − 3.33 mmHg, respectively.

Mechanisms of action

Preclinical evidence

Preclinical studies indicate that the metabolic effects of ACV are largely attributable to its acetic acid content. In rodent models, acetic acid has been shown to activate hepatic AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), which regulates glucose and lipid metabolism by decreasing de novo lipogenesis, increasing fatty-acid oxidation, and enhancing mitochondrial integrity. These effects collectively contribute to improved insulin sensitivity in both experimental and clinical studies40–42. Notably, AMPK is also the principal molecular target of metformin, which was administered as background therapy in both study groups. Metformin activates AMPK primarily through inhibition of mitochondrial complex I and subsequent alterations in cellular energy balance43. Evidence suggests that agents activating AMPK through distinct upstream mechanisms may exert synergistic metabolic effects44. Accordingly, concurrent AMPK activation by acetic acid and metformin may provide a plausible mechanistic explanation for the superior metabolic outcomes observed in the ACV group compared with metformin alone.

Beyond AMPK signaling, additional metabolic pathways may also contribute to the observed effects of ACV. Although ACV is not a direct peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor-γ (PPARγ) agonist, its primary component, acetate, as a short-chain fatty acid (SCFA), can indirectly modulate metabolic pathways in adipose tissue. Dietary supplementation with SCFAs stimulates oxidative metabolism through AMPK activation and influences PPARγ-dependent gene expression, thereby shifting cellular metabolism from lipogenesis toward fatty-acid oxidation45.

Moreover, elevated circulating SCFA levels, including acetate, have been reported to be associated with improved insulin sensitivity46.

Clinical evidence

Glycaemic control

The glucose‑lowering effect of ACV is largely attributed to acetic acid, which inhibits intestinal disaccharidases and α‑amylase, slows gastric emptying, and ultimately enhances both insulin secretion and peripheral insulin sensitivity.

Lipid metabolism and weight regulation

ACV increases faecal bile‑acid excretion, energy expenditure, and satiety, and suppresses hypothalamic hunger signals26,47. These changes collectively reduce lipogenesis and promote weight loss.

Blood‑pressure modulation

ACV may lower blood pressure by improving intestinal calcium absorption and attenuating the renin–angiotensin system: acetic acid suppresses renin activity and directly inhibits angiotensin‑converting enzyme39.

Clinical manifestations of hirsutism

Improved insulin homeostasis may mitigate hirsutism independently of circulating testosterone levels, as insulin potentiates local androgenic effects in pilosebaceous units. Consequently, insulin‑sensitizing therapies, such as metformin, can lead to an observed improvement in the mFG score, even without significant changes in serum androgen levels48–51. Hirsutism severity appears to be more closely related to peripheral androgen action at the hair follicle than to circulating androgen concentrations. Reduced local androgen activity, including decreased conversion of testosterone to dihydrotestosterone and lower follicular androgen sensitivity, may contribute to early detectable improvements in mFG scores52–54. Previous studies have reported reductions in mFG scores following a 12-week intervention period55,56. However, given the prolonged hair growth cycle, a 12-week duration may be insufficient to capture clinically meaningful or sustained changes in hirsutism. Therefore, considering the subjective nature of mFG scoring, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed in longer-term studies using objective assessment methods.

Sexual function outcomes

Weight loss has been shown to improve body image57 and sexual function58. Thus, the observed improvements in sexual function in our study may be linked to the weight-reducing effects of ACV.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include its randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled design and its novelty as the first trial to investigate ACV’s effects on MetS and IR in patients with PCOS. Additionally, administering ACV alongside standard medical treatment addressed ethical concerns related to withholding treatment from the placebo group.

This study has several limitations. Participants were recruited from a referral hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of women with PCOS. Diet and physical activity were not objectively monitored, and adherence to habitual lifestyle recommendations could not be verified. The relatively short intervention duration (12 weeks) may limit conclusions regarding the long-term sustainability of the observed effects. In addition, the use of a single dose of 1500 mg/day ACV precludes assessment of a dose-response relationship.

Future studies with longer follow-up periods, evaluation of multiple doses of ACV, and objective monitoring of diet and physical activity may help determine the sustainability and dose-dependence of the observed metabolic and clinical benefits and improve control of potential confounding factors.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ACV capsules may serve as a safe and effective adjunct therapy for improving metabolic and quality-of-life parameters in women with MetS, IR, and PCOS. However, longer-term trials with varying dosages are needed to confirm these findings and establish optimal treatment protocols.
