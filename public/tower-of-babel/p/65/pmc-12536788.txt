Oncologists’ preferences for frontline TKI treatment of Ph+ acute lymphoblastic leukemia: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Ajibade Ashaye, Natasha Ramachandran, Matthew Quaife, Yanyu Wu, Álvaro Alberto Gutiérrez-Vargas, Angelica Jiongco, Vamsi Kota, Bipin Savani, Caitlin Thomas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsTo evaluate hematologist-oncologists’ preferences for frontline treatment of Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL) using tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) + chemotherapy.Participants & methodsAn online discrete choice experiment was conducted among US-based hematologist-oncologists. Participants viewed profiles of hypothetical TKI + chemotherapy treatments with varied levels of benefit and risks (minimal residual disease-negative complete remission [MRD-negative CR], arterial occlusive events, grade 3–4 hepatotoxicity, grade 3–4 hematotoxicity) and chose their recommended treatment for five patient profiles: “less-complex” baseline; age ≥ 65; ECOG score 3; diabetes; hypertension. Data were analyzed using mixed multinomial logit models.Results121 hematologist-oncologists participated. Increasing MRD−negative CR was most important to hematologist-oncologists, driving 65%–87% of decision-making across patient profiles. Relative importance of benefits/risks varied by patient profile. Pooled across patient profiles, hepatotoxicity was the most concerning risk, driving 14% of decision-making. Based on PhALLCON data and elicited preferences, hematologist-oncologists were predicted to select the profile of ponatinib + chemotherapy over imatinib + chemotherapy for all included patient profiles. Predicted probabilities of choosing ponatinib over imatinib ranged from 87%–98% across patient profiles.ConclusionsHematologist-oncologists prioritized achieving MRD-negative CR when recommending frontline treatments for Ph+ ALL, accepting some risks if offset by meaningful improvement in MRD-negative CR.

Introduction

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have transformed the treatment landscape and prognosis for Philadelphia chromosome-positive acute lymphoblastic leukemia (Ph+ ALL) [1–5], substantially improving complete remission rates [1,2,6] and overall survival (OS) [2,6]. Hematologist-oncologists in the US have routinely combined TKIs with chemotherapy to treat newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL [1,3–5,7], albeit in an off-label fashion [8–12]. The first US approval of a TKI-based combination therapy in the frontline setting was granted in March 2024, based on results of the Phase III PhALLCON trial (NCT03589326) [9,13,14].

Deciding among multiple TKI + chemotherapy treatments for frontline use may be challenging. First, each treatment has a distinct benefit–risk profile [9–13], and views about acceptable benefit–risk profiles may vary according to physicians’ and patients’ preferences, and the patient’s clinical characteristics. Further, limited evidence is available comparing these therapies as frontline treatment [13,15]. Consequently, when hematologist-oncologists and their patients engage in shared decision-making to select a TKI + chemotherapy frontline treatment, they must consider the available evidence and balance competing desires to maximize efficacy and minimize risks.

Discrete choice experiments (DCEs) are commonly used to elicit treatment preferences and trade-offs that physicians and patients make during treatment decision-making [16–18]. In a prior DCE, adult patients with Ph+ ALL repeatedly chose between hypothetical frontline TKI + chemotherapy treatment options that had different benefit–risk profiles [19]. With each choice, patients weighed the benefits of OS and remission against the risks of major cardiovascular events and myelosuppression [19]. The study showed that patients generally prioritized increasing OS over avoiding risks when selecting a frontline treatment [19]. However, preferences and benefit–risk trade-offs were heterogenous across some patient subgroups [19].

Hematologist-oncologists are critical contributors to shared decision-making in Ph+ ALL, providing in-depth knowledge, and communicating benefits and risks of available treatment options for the individual patient. It is therefore important to understand hematologist-oncologists’ perspectives when recommending frontline treatment with a TKI + chemotherapy – which benefits and risks do they consider most important to decision-making, how do they trade off between benefits and risks, and how are their priorities and trade-offs shaped by patient characteristics, such as age and comorbidities? Quantifying hematologist-oncologists’ preferences can provide insights into the clinical perspective within shared decision-making.

In this study, we conducted a DCE to understand hematologist-oncologists’ decision-making when recommending TKIs + chemotherapy for patients with newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL. We aimed to quantify how hematologist-oncologists value and trade off between key benefit and risk characteristics, how their valuations and trade-offs vary based on patient characteristics, and which TKI + chemotherapy treatment they would be predicted to choose given clinically relevant levels of benefits and risks.

Participants & methods

Study overview

US-based hematologist-oncologists completed an online DCE that elicited their preferences for TKI + reduced-intensity chemotherapy treatments in patients with newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL. The DCE was developed following International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) good practices for developing a DCE [20,21]. The treatment characteristics and levels considered in the DCE were selected based on a review of clinical data, findings from a prior patient preference study on frontline treatment of Ph+ ALL [19], and input from expert key opinion leader (KOL) hematologist-oncologists. The patient profiles considered in the DCE were selected with KOL input. The initial DCE was refined through cognitive pilot interviews and a soft launch of the survey (Supplementary Methods). Cognitive pilot interviews were conducted with 10 hematologist-oncologists to pretest the DCE, ensuring its feasibility and robustness. Feedback from these interviews was used to confirm that the chosen treatment characteristics, levels of benefit and risks, and patient profiles were relevant, complete (i.e., that nothing important was missing), and understandable, and to improve the survey presentation and wording. A soft launch was then conducted with 30 hematologist-oncologists to assess data quality and determine whether any further adjustments were needed to the attribute levels or the experimental design.

Ethical approval was granted for the original study protocol and all amendments by Salus Review Services, a fully accredited institutional review board (IRB) (#24300–01). This study is reported in alignment with the DIRECT reporting checklist for DCEs in health [22] (Appendix B).

Participants

Physicians were eligible to participate if they were currently practicing hematologist-oncologists, currently treating adult patients with Ph+ ALL, and currently residing in the US; participants self-reported their eligibility criteria in an online screening form. For the cognitive pilot interviews, participants also needed to agree to be audio-recorded. All participants gave informed written consent online before participating in the study and received compensation for their time.

Participants were recruited by the third-party recruitment vendor, Global Perspectives. For cognitive pilot interviews, participants were recruited using databases, physician networks (including local/regional hospitals), and social media. For the soft launch and main DCE survey, participants were recruited via physician panels, databases, physician networks (including local/regional hospitals), and social media. A sample of 150 participants was targeted for the DCE across the soft launch and main data collection, in line with other DCEs in oncology [16].

Development and refinement of the DCE survey

Treatment characteristics and levels

In the DCE, participants repeatedly decided which treatment to recommend for an adult patient with Ph+ ALL, selecting between two treatment profiles that were described by key benefit and risk characteristics. Following a review of available clinical data and evidence from a study of patient preferences for frontline treatment [19], key characteristics of frontline TKI + chemotherapy treatment for Ph+ ALL were selected in collaboration with two KOL hematologist-oncologists. Treatment characteristics and levels were informed by clinical efficacy and safety data from the PhALLCON trial, which compared ponatinib + reduced-intensity chemotherapy vs. imatinib + reduced-intensity chemotherapy for frontline treatment of Ph+ ALL [13,23]. PhALLCON data were used because PhALLCON is the only direct, head-to-head comparison of frontline TKI + chemotherapy treatments, and provides robust evidence on treatment risks and efficacy [13,23]. A matching-adjusted indirect comparison of frontline ponatinib vs. imatinib [15] was identified but was not used because direct evidence from a head-to-head comparison is regarded as a higher level of evidence [24].

Four treatment characteristics were selected for the DCE: the chance of being in minimal residual disease-negative complete remission (MRD−ve CR) at the end of induction, the risk of an arterial occlusive event (AOE), the risk of grade 3–4 hepatotoxicity, and the risk of grade 3–4 hematotoxicity (Table 1). The chance of being in MRD−ve CR at the end of induction was chosen as the benefit attribute because this was the primary endpoint in the PhALLCON trial [13,23] and because OS data from PhALLCON were immature [13]. Moreover, KOLs noted that achieving MRD−ve CR was the most important treatment goal when considering treatment for adult patients with Ph+ ALL. The risk of an AOE and the risk of grade 3–4 hepatotoxicity were chosen as risk characteristics because ponatinib has had a “black box” warning for both risks [9] and because KOLs indicated that these risks may influence a hematologist-oncologist’s treatment decision-making in the frontline setting. Hepatotoxicity events of grade 3–4 were selected because these more severe events merit greater clinical concern and require additional procedures for treatment and risk mitigation. The risk of grade 3–4 hematotoxicity – including anemia, thrombocytopenia, and neutropenia – was chosen as a risk attribute because hematotoxicity was frequently observed in both arms of the PhALLCON trial [13], and a treatment option without this risk may not be realistic.

Levels of each treatment characteristic (Table 1) were informed by the observed data for each treatment arm in PhALLCON, in which patients received ponatinib 30 mg/day (reduced to 15 mg/day upon achievement of MRD–ve CR at the end of induction) + reduced-intensity chemotherapy, or imatinib 600 mg/day + reduced-intensity chemotherapy [13,23]. MRD−ve CR level ranges closely mirrored the range of observed MRD−ve CR rates at the end of induction [23], while AOE [13] and hepatotoxicity (data on file) risk level ranges closely mirrored the incidences of these adverse events. However, the observed risk of hematotoxicity in PhALLCON was similar across treatment arms (73.6% to 75.3% [data on file]). To avoid participants ignoring the risk of hematotoxicity when making choices in the DCE [29,30], the level range was widened to 65%–85%. This was selected to encourage trade-offs, as DCE preference studies are premised on eliciting and understanding trade-offs across included treatment characteristics [29–31]. Treatment characteristic levels were confirmed to be relevant based on feedback from the cognitive pilot interviews and quantitative data from the soft launch.

Patient profiles

KOLs highlighted that a patient’s characteristics (e.g., age, comorbidities) may impact hematologist-oncologists’ treatment decision-making. To make the DCE more clinically realistic and relevant, participants were asked to select their preferred treatment for five different patient profiles. Profiles were informed by KOL input on patient characteristics that are known to influence treatment selection.

The final DCE included a “less complex” baseline patient profile, defined as a 45-year-old who has no comorbidities and an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score of 0, indicating that they are fully active, and four additional patient profiles: a patient aged ≥65 years; a patient with an ECOG performance status score of 3, indicating significant functional impairment; a patient with diabetes; and a patient with hypertension (Figure 1). The “less complex” patient profile was included to provide a baseline understanding of hematologists-oncologists’ treatment preferences for patients without clinical complications. Participants viewed a description of the “less complex” patient characteristics but were not shown the wording “less complex” or “baseline.” The age of the “less complex” patient was lowered from 52 years to 45 years for the final DCE based on feedback from the cognitive interviews that a lower age would help to differentiate this profile from the “older” patient profile; KOLs agreed with this change.

The patient profile with age ≥65 years was included to capture preferences for treating “older” patients, as KOLs indicated that some physicians may be more concerned about potential side effects in patients who are older. The age of this patient profile was initially 60 years, based on the cutoff for an age subgroup analysis in the PhALLCON trial, and was raised based on feedback from the cognitive interviews that an older age would better differentiate this profile from the “less complex” patient profile and would be needed for them to consider the patient frailer. KOLs agreed to update the age and suggested the cutoff of 65 years.

The patient with an ECOG performance status score of 3 (poor – capable of limited self-care) was included because participants in the cognitive pilot interviews advised that some physicians may be more concerned about potential side effects in patients with worse ECOG performance status scores. The ECOG score was raised (i.e., worsened) from the initially proposed score of 2–3 in the final DCE based on feedback in the cognitive interviews that a score of 3 would better differentiate this profile from the “less complex” baseline patient profile.

Two patient profiles with comorbid conditions were included in the DCE. The patient with diabetes was included because KOLs suggested that some physicians may be more concerned about the risk of an AOE, as diabetes is associated with an increased risk of vascular events. In addition, steroids may be used to treat certain side effects associated with Ph+ ALL treatments, and steroids may affect blood sugar levels. Finally, the patient with hypertension was included because the risk of an AOE was included as an attribute in this study and individuals with hypertension may be at higher risk for AOEs.

DCE survey design

The DCE experimental design varied the efficacy and risk levels (Table 1) across treatment profiles and tasks, to generate as much information as possible about how participants are willing to trade off between efficacy and risks. To ensure that participants were given a feasible number of tasks and that the subsets of tasks had desirable statistical properties, a D-efficient design was generated in Ngene software version 1.3.018 (Supplementary Methods). Briefly, the design included 24 experimental DCE tasks that were split into two blocks of 12 tasks. Each participant was randomly assigned to one of the blocks, and the order of choice tasks was randomly assigned across participants within each block. The locations of treatment alternatives were randomly assigned across participants, as were the ordering of benefits and risks and the ordering of characteristics within the risk group. Patient profiles were shown in the same order across all participants.

To encourage greater trade-offs between risks and efficacy, the D-efficient design was updated following the soft launch; in the updated design, the minimum (15%) and maximum (35%) levels of MRD−ve CR were not presented together in one choice task.

DCE procedures

Following screening and consent, participants read introductory information about the study and descriptions of the patient profiles and treatment characteristics included in the DCE. They then completed two abbreviated example tasks to familiarize themselves with the DCE format, followed by a full “walkthrough” practice task. Participants proceeded to complete 12 experimental choice tasks and two tasks that were used to assess internal validity: a choice stability test and a dominance test (Supplementary Methods). Finally, participants answered questions about their demographics and clinical experience. The full DCE survey is provided in Appendix A.

In each DCE task, participants were shown two hypothetical, unlabeled TKI + chemotherapy treatment profiles (Figure 1). No treatment names were mentioned in the study. Treatment alternatives were depicted with different benefit–risk profiles in each task, such that participants faced trade-offs between different benefit and risk characteristics when choosing their recommended treatment for a given patient (Table 1). The benefit–risk profiles were based on the experimental design and did not directly represent ponatinib or imatinib. Participants were asked to select which treatment they would recommend as frontline therapy, first for a “less complex” baseline patient and then for each of the four other included patient profiles. Participants were asked to assume that the treatments were identical except for the included characteristics and without considering cost or insurance coverage. Participants needed to choose between the two presented options and were not given a “no treatment” or opt-out alternative, as KOLs advised that hematologist-oncologists would always offer patients a treatment and there is not one standard of care that could be presented as a fixed opt-out alternative.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics and data quality. Preference data were analyzed using mixed multinomial logit (MXL) models within the random utility maximization framework [32,33] (Supplementary Methods). Linear MXL models quantified how much utility participants gained with a one-unit change in the level of an individual efficacy or risk characteristic (i.e., estimated additional benefit), allowed preferences to vary across individuals, and generated preference estimates that were averaged across participants.

Analyses were run pooled across all five patient profiles and separately for each individual patient profile. Pooled analyses additively combined choices made for each of the five patient profiles. Individual profile analyses were run using separate linearly coded mixed multinomial logit models for each profile.

Participants’ willingness to trade off between incremental changes in MRD−ve CR and individual risks were calculated. Mean participant preference estimates from the DCE were used to calculate the minimum acceptable %-point increase in MRD−ve CR needed to tolerate a 1%-point increase in each risk.

Clinically relevant relative attribute importance (cRAI) was estimated to quantify how participants prioritized treatment benefit and risks. cRAI was calculated based on mean participant preference estimates from the DCE and observed benefit and risk outcomes from the PhALLCON trial [13,23]. Assessing importance over clinically relevant outcomes observed in PhALLCON was important since the level range for the risk of hematotoxicity in the DCE (65%–85%) was much wider than the risks of hematotoxicity observed in the PhALLCON trial for the two treatment options of interest (73.6% for ponatinib and 73.5% for imatinib [data on file]) [13]. cRAI was compared across subgroups to understand whether preferences varied with participants’ sociodemographic and clinical experience characteristics.

Predicted choice probabilities (PCPs) were estimated using a logit formula and based on mean participant preference estimates from the DCE and observed benefit and risk outcomes observed in the PhALLCON trial [13,23] (Table 1).

Analyses were conducted in R software (version 4.3.3) using the logitr (version 1.1.2), gmnl (version 1.1–3.2), and mlogit (version 1.1.1) packages.

Participants had a median age of 45 years and had been practicing as hematologist-oncologists for a median 14 years. Participants had treated a median of 40 Ph+ ALL patients in the past 12 months. Practice areas were spread across the US, and almost half of participants were located in the South (n = 51, 42%). Data quality

The results of internal data validity assessments and DCE completion times suggest that data quality was in line with that observed in other DCEs (Supplementary Table S2) [34–36]. Most participants (n = 97, 80%) selected the treatment option that was superior across all characteristics in the dominance test. A similar proportion (n = 99, 82%) provided the same response in a repeated choice question (choice stability test). Nearly all participants (n = 119, 98%) made choices considering multiple characteristics, indicating that their decision-making was not dominated by any single benefit or risk attribute across all experimental tasks, and no participants (n = 0, 0%) always chose the option labeled “TKI A” or “TKI B,” suggesting engagement with the survey. Further, most participants (n = 101, 83%) varied their treatment choices when selecting treatments for the different patient profiles (i.e., did not always choose the same treatment option for all patient profiles). Participants spent a median 6.0 minutes (interquartile range: 2.7–10.7) completing the 12 experimental DCE tasks, which is approximately 30 seconds per task.

Preferences for improving each treatment attribute

The mixed multinomial logit model had the best fit of the candidate models used in analyzing preferences (Supplementary Table S3).

All mean preference estimates were in the expected direction (Figure 2), demonstrating participants’ desire for greater efficacy and lower risks, thus providing face validity. When pooled across the five patient profiles, each attribute had a significant effect on participants’ preferences when selecting a treatment recommendation (p < 0.001) (Figure 2(A); Supplementary Table S4). Pooled across patient profiles, improving the chance of being in MRD−ve CR from 15% to 35% had the largest influence on treatment recommendations (preference estimate, 2.72 [standard deviation (SD), 0.24]), followed by improving the chance of MRD−ve CR from 15% to 25% (preference estimate, 1.36 [SD, 0.12]), and reducing the risk of grade 3–4 hepatotoxicity from 25% to 10% (preference estimate, 0.72 [SD, 0.13]) (Supplementary Table S4). Willingness to trade off

When choices were pooled across the five patient profiles, participants required a 2.1%-point or greater increase in the chance of MRD−ve CR to recommend a treatment with a 1%-point higher risk of an AOE (Supplementary Figure S3). Participants required smaller increases in MRD−ve CR (0.3%–0.4%-point or greater) to recommend a treatment with 1%-point increases in risks of hematotoxicity or hepatotoxicity.

Participants traded off differently when considering the different patient profiles. Participants were generally more willing to recommend a treatment with increased risks for the “less complex” baseline patient profile than for the other patient profiles. Across all risks and patient profiles, participants were least willing to recommend a treatment with an increased risk of an AOE in patients with diabetes – requiring a 4.0%-point or greater increase in MRD−ve CR to recommend a treatment with a 1%-point increase in the chance of an AOE.

Clinically relevant relative attribute importance

When pooling across patient profiles and accounting for clinically relevant ranges of each attribute (i.e., the levels of each benefit and risk observed in the PhALLCON trial [13,23]) (Table 1), participants’ treatment recommendations were most influenced by increasing the chance of MRD−ve CR at the end of induction (cRAI, 75.8%; Figure 3). Improving MRD−ve CR was at least 5.5× as important as reducing any individual risk over clinically relevant ranges. Hepatotoxicity was the most important risk (cRAI, 13.9%), followed by the risk of an AOE (cRAI, 8.5%). The importance of individual risks to treatment recommendations also varied across the five patient profiles (Figure 3). Reducing the risk of hepatotoxicity was most important when recommending a treatment for a patient with an ECOG score of 3 (cRAI, 19.5%) and was significantly more important than when recommending a treatment for a “less complex” patient (cRAI, 8.9%; p < 0.05). In addition, reducing the risk of an AOE was significantly less important when recommending a treatment for a “less complex” patient (cRAI, 2.0%) than when recommending a treatment for patients with hypertension (cRAI, 15.2%; p < 0.01) or diabetes (cRAI, 18.7%; p < 0.001). Finally, reducing the risk of hematotoxicity was most important when recommending a treatment for a patient with an ECOG score of 3 (cRAI, 3.3%), significantly greater than when recommending a treatment for a “less complex” patient (cRAI, 1.7%; p < 0.05) or for patients with diabetes (cRAI, 1.5%; p < 0.05) or hypertension (cRAI, 1.2%; p < 0.05).

Preference heterogeneity

No significant differences in preferences were observed based on participants’ age ( < 45 years, ≥45 years); institution type (community/hybrid, academic); practice location (rural, suburban, urban); number of adult patients with Ph+ ALL treated in the past 12 months ( < 40, ≥40); years of practicing medicine ( < 15, ≥15); US region where currently practicing (South, West, Other [Northeast, North Central/Midwest]); and number of hematologist-oncologists currently working in their practice (≤10, > 10).

Only one significant difference was identified when comparing cRAI across subgroups of participants based on collected sociodemographic characteristics and clinical experience. Female participants (n = 26) placed a significantly lower importance on reducing the risk of hepatotoxicity (cRAI, 0.8%) than male participants (n = 95; cRAI, 15.6%) (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Figure S2).

The only large correlation identified between characteristics was between age and years practicing medicine, with older participants having practiced medicine for a longer period (Supplementary Table S5). The difference between female and male participants’ preferences for reducing the risk of hepatotoxicity was therefore unlikely to be due to a correlation with any of the sociodemographic or clinical experience information collected.

Predicted treatment choice

Predicted treatment choice probabilities, calculated by combining elicited preferences with the benefit and risk levels observed in the PhALLCON trial [13,23], showed that when considering all patient profiles (pooled), there was a 95.5% (95% confidence interval [CI], 92.6%–97.1%) chance that participants would prefer ponatinib + chemotherapy over imatinib + chemotherapy as frontline treatment (p < 0.001) (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S6). Participants were also predicted to prefer ponatinib + chemotherapy over imatinib + chemotherapy if recommending treatment for each of the five patient profiles considered (PCPs of 87.5%–97.9%; each p < 0.001) (Figure 4; Supplementary Table S6). The PCP of ponatinib being preferred as a treatment recommendation was highest (97.9%; 95% CI, 94.4%–99.1%) for the “less complex” baseline profile; this was significantly higher than for patient profiles with an ECOG score of 3 (p < 0.01), hypertension (p < 0.01), diabetes (p < 0.01), or patient age ≥65 years (p < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S7). Discussion

This study quantified the preferences of 121 US-based hematologist-oncologists for recommending frontline TKI + chemotherapy treatment to an adult with Ph+ ALL. Hematologist-oncologists made treatment choices based on key benefits and risks of candidate treatments for five different patient profiles. Pooled across all five patient profiles, 76% of decision-making was driven by improving MRD−ve CR. Participants prioritized increasing efficacy over avoiding risks when recommending a treatment for each patient profile; clinically relevant improvements in MRD−ve CR (i.e., those observed in PhALLCON) had at least a 3–10 times greater influence on decision-making than clinically relevant reductions in any included risk. Estimates of participants’ predicted treatment choice between ponatinib + chemotherapy and imatinib + chemotherapy, calculated by combining preference estimates with clinical performance data from PhALLCON [13,23], predict that most participants would recommend ponatinib + chemotherapy over imatinib + chemotherapy (96% pooled across all profiles), regardless of the patient profile (87%–98% per individual patient profile). These results suggest that the ponatinib regimen is expected to be preferred for both low-complexity patients and for patients who are older or who have hypertension, diabetes, or significant functional impairment, given the higher MRD–ve CR associated with ponatinib.

While efficacy was by far the largest driver of preferences, each risk attribute also had a significant influence on preferences. Pooled across all five patient profiles, the risk of hepatotoxicity was the most concerning risk attribute (pooled cRAI = 13.9%), followed by the risk of an AOE (pooled cRAI = 8.5%). However, the risk of an AOE was more concerning than the risk of hepatotoxicity when considering patients with hypertension (cRAI = 15.2% vs. 9.9%) or diabetes (cRAI = 18.7% vs. 10.7%), conditions with higher cardiovascular risk [38–40]. Hematotoxicity was the least important attribute for all patient profiles, with cRAI ranging from only 1%–3% across profiles. Participants were willing to trade off between efficacy and risks, but willingness to trade off differed when considering different patient profiles.

Preferences were similar across nearly all participant subgroups, with no differences identified for years of practicing medicine, number of patients treated, institution type, and geographic region. It is unclear whether the only subgroup difference observed – less importance placed on hepatotoxicity by female vs. male participants – is a true difference. Instead, this difference may be due to the small number of female participants (n = 26). Analyses comparing cRAI in female vs. male participants were run due to a male-skewed sample, despite the female sample size being lower than the target sample size of < 30 for such subgroup analyses.

In an earlier DCE study involving patients with Ph+ ALL, patients also prioritized increasing efficacy over avoiding risks when choosing a frontline treatment [19]. This similar interest between hematologist-oncologists and patients in increasing efficacy suggests that efficacy is likely a key focus in shared decision-making. In addition, a quantitative benefit–risk analysis based on the patient DCE [19] and clinical data from the PhALLCON trial [13,23] provides evidence that patients would be more likely to choose ponatinib over imatinib. This mirrors the predicted choice of ponatinib over imatinib for hematologist-oncologists in the present study based on DCE and PhALLCON data. Notably, specific preferences cannot be directly compared between the present DCE and the patient DCE because the patient DCE used different benefit and risk characteristics (duration of OS, duration of remission, risk of major cardiovascular events, and risk of myelosuppression) [19]. However, when physician and patient preferences are considered together, commonalities are observed in the prioritization of efficacy over risks and the lower tolerance for cardiovascular event risks in older patients [19]. The two studies should be considered jointly to understand treatment decision-making preferences and can be used to guide shared decision-making discussions.

A major strength of this study is that the DCE was developed in close collaboration with KOL hematologist-oncologists and in alignment with the ISPOR best practices for preference elicitation [20,21]. Characteristics and patient profiles were developed based on a review of head-to-head clinical data and KOL input on considerations during frontline treatment decision-making. Treatment characteristics, levels of benefit and risks, patient profiles, wording, and other aspects of the DCE were confirmed or iteratively refined during cognitive pilot interviews and a soft launch, both with hematologist-oncologist participants, ensuring the clinical accuracy of the study. Another strength is that the benefit–risk level ranges used in the DCE were informed by outcomes data from the PhALLCON trial [13,23]. Because the starting dosages of ponatinib and imatinib in PhALLCON align with those recommended by the FDA for ponatinib in newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL [9] and imatinib in relapsed/refractory Ph+ ALL [10], the benefit–risk ranges considered in our study should be similar to the benefit and risk probabilities expected when prescribing with FDA-recommended dosing. Moreover, cRAI was calculated using observed MRD–ve CR and adverse event data from PhALLCON, which allows us to understand the relative importance of treatment characteristics at clinically relevant levels. Importantly, our DCE included treatment decisions for five patient profiles, which differs from the typical design of a DCE, in which decisions are made based on a single profile. The use of multiple patient profiles increases the generalizability of these results to a wider range of patients and provides important information on how benefits and risks impact hematologist-oncologists’ treatment decision-making for varied patient profiles. Finally, our data quality indicators suggest that participants were engaged in the study, with similar performance on choice stability and dominance tests [41,42] and comparable completion times to those observed [42] and expected [43] in other physician DCEs.

As with any DCE study, scenarios and choices are hypothetical and may not fully capture the complexity of treatment decisions in the real world. Other factors likely influence decision-making, including benefit and risk characteristics not included in the study, patient characteristics not covered in the five included profiles, and other considerations, such as institutional protocols. Importantly, each patient profile was defined as a single, binary characteristic (e.g., age ≥65 years) due to methodological constraints. Real-world decision-making is likely to be more complex when patients have multiple clinical considerations and is often influenced by the severity of complications. Another limitation of this study is that real-world treatment choices are not limited to the TKI combination regimens considered in this study. Dasatinib and nilotinib are other TKIs routinely used off-label in combination with chemotherapy to treat adults with newly diagnosed Ph+ ALL [6,11,12,44], and they have different benefit–risk profiles than imatinib or ponatinib [11,12]. Dasatinib, nilotinib, and other TKIs were not considered in this study because head-to-head or indirect comparison data in a frontline setting are not available, and comparable data are required to conduct predicted treatment choice analyses. Physicians may also consider whether allogenic stem cell transplantation is needed and how the benefit–risk balance for transplantation might change in patients who achieve MRD–ve CR [1–4,6,26–28,45,46], given that achievement of MRD–ve CR may reduce the need for a stem cell transplant [46] or place patients in the best position to receive a stem cell transplant if desired [27,28]. The challenging clinical scenarios surrounding transplant are beyond the scope of this study and merit further research. Notably, this study involved only participants practicing in the US. Prescribing patterns [47], drug access [47–49], and regulatory status [49,50] of cancer treatments differ across countries, with notable variation in the frequency of off-label prescribing [51]. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to hematologist-oncologists practicing outside the US. In addition, women comprised 21% of our study population, while estimates suggest that 36% of US oncologists are female [52]. Finally, the years of hematology-oncology practice experience reported for our sample may be imprecise, as participants did not receive guidance on whether to include their years of residency or fellowship training when answering the relevant survey question.

Conclusions

When recommending a TKI + chemotherapy to treat an adult with Ph+ ALL in the frontline setting, hematologist-oncologists in this study prioritized treatments with a higher chance of MRD−ve complete remission and would tolerate some risks to achieve this. The relative importance of MRD–ve CR and treatment risks to hematologist-oncologists’ treatment recommendations varied across patient profiles: although they prioritized efficacy for all patient profiles, they were more tolerant of risks for the “less complex” patient profile. Predicted treatment choices based on elicited preferences for the included treatment characteristics and observed treatment performance data from the PhALLCON trial suggest that hematologist-oncologists are more likely to recommend ponatinib over imatinib for patients with a variety of clinical profiles, including patients who have hypertension or diabetes and patients who are aged ≥65 years or have an ECOG status score of 3.
