Enhanced invasiveness promotes the dominance of a widely-distributed carbapenem-resistant virulence-plasmid-carrying Klebsiella pneumoniae sublineage
Authors: Yongqiang Yang, Jiayuan Qin, Yu Feng, Junna Wang, Hongxia Wen, Huan Kuang, Miao Tang, Meilin Li, Yan Feng, Qingqing Fang, Xiaopan Guo, Shikai Wu, Yang Zeng, Qianhua Zhang, Li Wei, Huan Luo, Yuling Xiao, Xikun Zhou, Alan McNally, Zhiyong Zong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP), a WHO’s critical priority pathogen, continuously evolves to generate health-threatening high-risk (sub)lineages. Here, we conducted a 10-year surveillance of nosocomial CRKP infections, collecting and whole-genome sequencing 1513 clinical isolates, accompanied by clinical data. We applied fine-scale genome analysis for 60,724 non-local public-available K. pneumoniae genomes. We identified a predominant ST11-KL64 sublineage widely-disseminated across China and internationally-distributed. Isolates of this sublineage were more frequently recovered during seasonal influenza peaks and harbored plasmids encoding ‘hypervirulence’ factors but caused no increase in patient mortality. Instead, they exhibited enhanced invasiveness and translocation capacity. Mechanistically, this suggested highly invasive CRKP (hiCRKP) sublineage showed elevated resistance to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis, partly due to the virulence-encoding plasmid and the loss of two chromosomal fimD gene copies. Additionally, hiCRKP isolates carried more antimicrobial resistance genes, resulting in enhanced resistance to clinically important quinolones and tetracyclines. To address the hiCRKP-imposed challenge, we constituted a phage cocktail, which significantly improved survival in a murine infection model. Our findings unveil a clinically-relevant high-risk sublineage resulted from the ongoing evolutionary diversification of CRKP. Importantly, the ‘hypervirulent’ CRKP is more potent to cause diseases rather than the thought-to-be more deaths, explaining its rapid emergence in healthcare settings.

Introduction

Global antimicrobial resistance burden is predicted to increase to 1.91 million attributable deaths and 8.22 million associated deaths in 20501. Klebsiella pneumoniae is a leading cause of severe healthcare-associated infections, contributing to an estimated 642,000 resistance-associated deaths in 20192. Carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP), as a WHO critical priority pathogen, leading to prolonged hospital stays and elevated mortality rates in infected patients, posing a serious threat to public health3. The emergence and dissemination of CRKP are largely driven by certain high-risk lineages such as sequence types (ST) 258, 11, 307, and 1474–8. ST11 CRKP are prevalent in Asia9 and South America10. ST11 strains can be further assigned to different capsular types such as KL15, KL24, KL47, KL64, and KL10511 and even within the same KL type, multiple clades or clones have been identified12,13, suggesting remarkable clonal heterogeneity. Intriguingly, certain strains of ST11 appear to be particularly widely disseminated12. The reasons for the successful spread of ST11 as a whole and within ST11 certain subtypes remains relatively uncharacterized, requiring fine-scale genomic analysis and genome-informed hypothesis-driven experiments to uncover the underlying mechanisms or driving factors. In addition to the dominant lineages such as ST11, CRKP belonging to many other STs are continuously emerging and disseminating, such as ST1016,7 and ST156,7.

CRKP strains harboring virulence-encoding plasmids such as pLVPK-like plasmids (which carry the hypermucoviscous regulator rmpA/rmpA214 and siderophore-biosynthesis genes iuc and iro15), are increasingly being reported in China12,13 and many other countries16, raising serious concerns due to the potential combination of carbapenem resistance and hypervirulence. However, it remains controversial whether the acquisition of virulence-encoding plasmids by CRKP results in increased pathogenicity of the host strain and worsened clinical outcomes.

To address these gaps, we performed a 10-year longitudinal genomic epidemiology study incorporated with detailed clinical analysis of all CRKP affected patients in the hospital, a set of hypothesis-driven experiments, and additional fine-scale genome analysis of 60,724 non-local publicly-available K. pneumoniae genomes. This integrated approach allows us to identify a particular dominant sublineage, detect its fluctuated prevalence and clinical significance, and uncover a mechanism involving chromosome-plasmid evolutionary synergy but without worsening affected patients’ outcomes. These findings may have implications for multiple settings such as clinical management and infection control practice.

Results

ST11 is the predominant sequence type among CRKP clinical isolates

We included all available non-duplicate CRKP clinical isolates (n = 1545) recovered over a 10-year period in our hospital, and screening isolates were excluded. Following whole-genome sequencing (WGS), carbapenemase-encoding genes were identified in 1533 isolates (Supplementary Data 1). The remaining 12 isolates were phenotypically resistant to carbapenems but lacked detectable carbapenemase genes (Supplementary Table 1) and were excluded. We also excluded 17 CRKP isolates due to incomplete clinical data and three belonged to Klebsiella quasipneumoniae (n = 2) or Klebsiella variicola (n = 1). Consequently, our clinical and genomic analysis focused on the 1513 CRKP identified as K. pneumoniae (sensu stricto). The 1513 CRKP isolates were most frequently recovered from sputum (n = 765, 50.6%), followed by blood (n = 238, 15.7%). Pneumonia, bacteremia, urinary tract infection, and intra-abdominal infection were the primary clinical syndromes associated with CRKP in this cohort (Supplementary Data 2).

The 1513 CRKP isolates were assigned to 46 STs (Supplementary Data 1). ST11 was the most prevalent, accounting for 85.8% (1298/1513) of all isolates. Other relatively common types included ST15 (n = 49), ST45 (n = 31), ST709 (n = 28), ST16 (n = 24), and ST307 (n = 15). The most frequent capsular type was KL64 (65.7%, 994/1513), followed by KL47 (15.5%, 234/1,513) (Supplementary Data 1). A total of 993 ST11-KL64 and 232 ST11-KL47 isolates were identified. Notably, there were 56 ST11-KL62 isolates (3.7%, 56/1,513), while KL62 is a capsular type uncommonly reported either in China or globally.

Four different carbapenemase genes, blaKPC (blaKPC-2, n = 1430; blaKPC-75, n = 1), blaNDM (blaNDM-5, n = 68; blaNDM-1, n = 26; blaNDM-4, n = 1; blaNDM-41, n = 1), blaIMP (blaIMP-4, n = 1; blaIMP-8, n = 1; blaIMP-38, n = 1), and blaOXA-48-like (blaOXA-48, n = 3; blaOXA-181, n = 2; blaOXA-232, n = 1), were identified among the 1513 CRKP isolates (Supplementary Data 1). The blaKPC-2 gene was predominant (94.5%, 1430/1513) either as the sole carbapenemase gene (n = 1410) or co-existing with blaNDM-1 (n = 10), blaNDM-5 (n = 9) or blaIMP-38 (n = 1). The blaNDM-5 and blaNDM-1 genes were identified in 68 (4.4%; 57 alone, 9 with blaKPC-2, and 2 with blaOXA-48) and 26 (1.7%; 15 alone, 10 with blaKPC-2, and 1 with blaIMP-4) isolates, respectively (Supplementary Data 1).

Expansion of KL64, persistence of KL47, and emergence of KL62 within ST11 CRKP

We analyzed the temporal distribution of KL types within the ST11 population. ST11-KL64 was first detected in 2015, represented by only two isolates, and subsequently expanded to 219 isolates by 2023, becoming the most prevalent subtype since 2017 (Fig. 1a). The proportion of ST11-KL64 among all ST11 CRKP isolates increased from 33.3% in 2015 to a peak of 93.4% in 2021, before declining to 67.8% in 2023 (Fig. 1b). In contrast, the prevalence of ST11-KL47 remained relatively stable of 19.8% and 15.8% in 2022 and 2023 throughout the study period, except a peak of 35.7% in 2018 (Fig. 1b). To assess whether the epidemiological expansion of ST11-KL64 affected clinical outcomes, we compared clinical outcomes of patients infected with ST11-KL64 versus ST11-KL47 isolates. No significant differences were observed between patients infected with ST11-KL64 (n = 993) and those infected with ST11-KL47 (n = 232) regarding demographic characteristics and clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 2). Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed to balance baseline clinical characteristics between groups. After data cleaning and application of the nearest-neighbor matching algorithm (1:1 ratio, caliper = 0.02), 149 matched pairs were obtained. Baseline characteristics including sex, age, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and malignancy), and ICU admission were well balanced between groups (Supplementary Table 3). Clinical outcomes were comparable between the two groups, including in-hospital all-cause mortality (16.8% [25/149] vs. 12.1% [18/149], P = 0.2485), predicted all-cause mortality (comprising in-hospital death and predicted death when discharged at patients’ own wills, 25.5% [38/149] vs. 24.8% [37/149], P = 0.8938), hospital-acquired infection-attributable in-hospital mortality (12.8% [19/149] vs. 9.4% [14/149], P = 0.3560), length of hospital stay (median 34 vs. 38 days, P = 0.3061), and duration from the first CRKP-positive culture to discharge or death (median 19 vs. 20 days, P = 0.8171) (Supplementary Table 3).

A dominant phylogroup of ST11 CRKP spreads across China and is also internationally disseminated

To investigate the molecular characteristics of dominant CRKP lineages, we constructed a phylogenomic tree based on core genome single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) derived from all 1,298 ST11 CRKP isolates. This analysis revealed four major phylogroups: A (n = 635), B (n = 419), C (n = 132), and D (n = 104), with 8 isolates displaying substantial genetic divergence from these clusters within ST11 (Fig. 1c). Phylogroup A, the largest, was primarily composed of KL64 (90.9%, 577/635) and KL62 (8.8%, 56/635) isolates (Fig. 1d). Phylogroup B was also dominated by KL64 (99.3%, 416/419), followed by three KL25 isolates (Fig. 1d). In contrast, phylogroups C and D were mainly comprised of KL47 isolates, accounting for 100% (132/132) and 96.2% (100/104), respectively, and the remaining four in phylogroup D were KL25 (Fig. 1d). Temporal analysis of phylogroup distribution revealed that phylogroup B emerged earlier, first identified in December 2015, whereas phylogroup A was detected starting in August 2018 (Fig. 1e). Despite its later emergence, phylogroup A rapidly expanded in prevalence beginning in 2019 and surpassed phylogroup B in 2020, subsequently becoming the dominant lineage (Fig. 1e). Notably, ST11-KL62 isolates (n = 56), which cluster within phylogroup A, first appeared in 2022 (n = 4) and sharply increased to 52 isolates in 2023 (Fig. 1a). By recombination and temporal analysis, ST11-KL62 likely originated from ST11-KL64 by swapping a capsule polysaccharides synthesis (cps)-containing fragment between setB and lysR from ST45-KL62 (Supplementary Fig. 1). The cps region of ST11-KL62 strains exhibits two distinct structural types. Eleven out of the 56 ST11-KL62 isolates harbored an interrupted manB (encodes phosphomannomutase) with an insertion of ltrA (encodes a group II intron) which likely originated from a KPC-2 plasmid (pKPC2_141091, Supplementary Fig. 1). Those manB-interrupted strains appeared multiple times and formed diverse clusters (Supplementary Fig. 2). The remaining 45 isolates retained an intact manB gene without the ltrA insertion. Excluding KL62, phylogroup A and B isolates in 2023 numbered 187 and 33, respectively.

To avoid bias introduced by clonal or outbreak-related strains, we de-duplicated the dataset by removing clonal isolates using a time-resolved definition derived from a recombination-aware, dated phylogeny. This approach identified 539 phylogroup A and 377 phylogroup B strains (Supplementary Data 1), demonstrating that the majority of isolates were non-clonal and phylogroup A remained the dominant lineage after removal of clonal strains (Supplementary Fig. 3). These findings indicate that the ST11-KL64 lineage has diverged into two distinct phylogroups, with phylogroup A emerging more recently yet overtaking phylogroup B in prevalence, suggesting potential evolutionary advantages.

To assess whether the dominance of phylogroup A is a hospital-specific phenomenon or a broader epidemiological trend, we incorporated all quality-controlled clinical ST11-KL64 K. pneumoniae assemblies released before 2024 from the NCBI GenBank database (n = 3401) (Supplementary Data 3). We combined these with 993 ST11-KL64 genomes of this study and inferred a comprehensive phylogenetic tree. The resulting phylogenetic tree confirmed the presence of two distinct phylogroups, A and B. Phylogroup A (n = 2205) exhibited a clear epidemiological advantage over phylogroup B (n = 898) without counting new data from this study (Supplementary Fig. 4). Phylogroup A isolate was found to be prevalent across multiple provinces in China. Additional phylogroup A isolates were recovered from USA (n = 5, year: 2017 and 2023), Japan (n = 2, year: 2020), and France (n = 1, year: 2018) (Supplementary Data 3). These findings suggest that the phylogenetic divergence of ST11-KL64 and the predominance of phylogroup A reflect a nation-wide dissemination with sporadic international distribution.

Dominance of phylogroup A is further uncovered by evolutionary and population analyses

To trace the evolutionary emergence of each phylogroup identified in this study, we dated the phylogenomic tree of our isolates and estimated the time to the most recent common ancestor (MRCA) for each group. The MRCA of phylogroup A was estimated to be July 2011 (95% CI: March 2009 to July 2013), which appears to be later than that of phylogroup B, estimated as June 2009 (September 2007 to January 2011) (Fig. 2a). The root of all four phylogroups which represented the MRCA of all strains analyzed was dated to November 2004 (March 2002 to May 2007) (Fig. 2a). Our previous study together with others have found that ST11-KL64 emerges from ST11-KL47 by capsular switching11,18. In this study, given that the MRCA of all ST11-KL64 strains was estimated as August 2008 (August 2006 to April 2010), capsular switching likely occurred between 2004 and 2008 (Fig. 2a). During the ongoing evolution process, CRKP genomes are open to acquire or exchange exogenous genes or genetic elements, and clade-specific genes between phylogroup A and B groups were identified on both mobile genetic elements and chromosomes (Fig. 2b). As this study is based on isolates recovered from clinical samples, the absence of colonizing ones and those failed to culture may introduce sampling bias. To address this, we reconstructed the historical effective population size (Ne) of each phylogroup as a proxy for past population dynamics. We also performed dedicated sensitivity analyses to evaluate and control for sampling bias by year-phylogroup rarefaction and outbreak-oversampling control (Supplementary Figs. 6, 7). The overall Ne trends mirrored the distribution of sampled isolates, supporting the representativeness of the dataset (Fig. 2c). Specifically, the effective population size of phylogroup C became estimable in late 2007, followed by phylogroup B in early 2009 (Fig. 2c). After 2016, phylogroup B surpassed phylogroup C but was subsequently overtaken by phylogroup A, which emerged in 2011 and began to rapidly expand from 2018 onward-nearly doubling its Ne each year (Fig. 2c). By the end of the study, phylogroup A had become the dominant lineage, with an Ne approximately 4-fold, 5-fold, and 50-fold greater than that of phylogroups B, C, and D, respectively (Fig. 2c). This suggests that phylogroup A is likely even more successful in clinical settings than inferred solely from the number of sampled isolates.

Dominant phylogroup A harbors virulence-encoding plasmids but does not lead to poorer outcome

Although both phylogroups A and B mainly belong to ST11-KL64, phylogroup A exhibited a clear epidemiological advantage over phylogroup B. To investigate the molecular basis underlying this difference, we compared the genomic characteristics of isolates from the two phylogroups. Remarkably, 94.5% (600/635) of the phylogroup A isolates carried at least one copy of rmpA or rmpA2 (including truncated forms), whereas only 0.7% (3/419) of the phylogroup B isolates carried these genes (Supplementary Data 4). Specifically, for rmpA, among phylogroup A isolates, 337 carried intact genes, 171 were non-intact (disrupted, n = 122; interrupted by an insertion sequence, n = 48; truncated, n = 1), 8 exhibited a frameshift caused by a single G insertion within the poly-G tract (nt 276-285), causing a premature stop codon at amino acid position 100, and 84 lacked rmpA (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Data 4). All three phylogroup B isolates carried intact rmpA. The mRNA expression of rmpA was detected in strains carrying an intact rmpA gene (Supplementary Table 5). For rmpA2, 539 phylogroup A isolates exhibited frameshift mutations caused by nucleotide deletions, leading to premature stop codons; 57 lacked rmpA2; and 4 were disrupted by an insertion sequence (ISKpn26, n = 3; ISKpn14, n = 1). All three phylogroup B isolates carried frameshifted rmpA2 (Supplementary Data 4). Furthermore, among the 600 rmpA/rmpA2-positive phylogroup A strains, intact aerobactin-encoding genes (iucABCD-iutA) and salmonellin genes (iroBCDN) were detected in 89.0% (534/600) and 0.3% (2/600) of isolates, respectively (Supplementary Data 4).

We mapped genome assemblies to four reference virulence-encoding plasmids, pLVPK (accession no. NC_005249.1), pPBIO1953_NDM-1 (ERX4362872), pKpn290 (NZ_JAMCDN010000002.1), and p1108-IncFIB (NZ_MG825378.1), which represent the major virulence-encoding plasmid types in CRKP19. A plasmid was considered present if the assembly showed ≥70% coverage of the reference virulence plasmid and contained the corresponding replicon. Based on this criterion, 569 isolates in phylogroup A carried rmpA/A2 on a pLVPK-like plasmid that contains two replicons of IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR) and repB_KLEB_VIR (coverage 71.8-90.6%), while 31 had lower coverage (<60%). Among these 31 isolates, four contained both replicons, 25 lacked the IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR) replicon, and two (141560 and 141587) lacked both replicons. For three rmpA/A2-positive phylogroup B isolates, one (140471, year: 2021) harbored rmpA/A2 on pLVPK-like (90.8% coverage) plasmid, and one (090492, year: 2019) on pPBIO1953_NDM-1-like (80% coverage) plasmid. The third strain (090276, year: 2019) showed only 63.5% coverage to pPBIO1953_NDM-1. We selected three phylogroup A isolates (141508, 141812, and 141780) harboring a pLVPK-like plasmid (coverage 90.4-90.5%) for serial passage experiments. The virulence-encoding plasmid in each strain remained stably present at the experimental endpoint of 16 days, indicating its stability in the host strain.

For rmpA/A2-positive isolates that were untypeable by direct mapping (n = 31), we selected representative strains from untypeable groups for long-read sequencing to obtain complete plasmid sequences. The pVir_090527 from phylogroup A strain 090527 was identified as a degenerated variant of pLVPK (40.1% coverage, lacking IncHI1B[pNDM-MAR]). Notably, 29 of the 31 untypeable phylogroup A isolates harbored a similar plasmid to that in 090527 (average coverage 99.6%). For the remaining two isolates, in strain 141587, a 57.9-Kb fragment highly similar to that of pVir_090527 was identified as integrated into the chromosome (Supplementary Fig. 8). The remaining strain 141560, after obtained its fully assembled genome, was confirmed to carry rmpA/rmpA2 on an IncFII hybrid plasmid that also harbors blaKPC-2. The pVir_090276 from phylogroup B strain 090276 was a degenerated form of pPBIO1953_NDM-1 lacking IncHI1B(pNDM-MAR). Therefore, the virulence-encoding plasmid location and plasmid type could be determined for all the rmpA/rmpA2-positive isolates. We also searched for virulence-encoding plasmid sequences in rmpA/rmpA2-negative isolates from phylogroup A and B using the plasmid detection criteria described above. Overall, 626 out of the 635 (597 rmpA/A2-positive and 29 rmpA/A2-negative) phylogroup A isolates carried virulence-encoding plasmids, whereas only six out of the 419 (three rmpA/A2-positive and three rmpA/A2-negative) phylogroup B isolates did (Supplementary Data 5). Different virulence-encoding plasmids showed evolutionary dynamics across years (Fig. 2D) and polyphyletic distribution (Supplementary Fig. 9). The variation in the genetic context of the same type of virulence-encoding plasmids was observed among different isolates (Supplementary Figs. 10–12; Supplementary text). We further analyzed 123 hybrid plasmids co-harboring rmpA/rmpA2 and carbapenemase-encoding genes using the NCBI plasmid collection (n = 115,036; accessed on December 10, 2025) and found no significant association between specific carbapenemase gene and virulence-encoding plasmids (Supplementary text; Supplementary Data 6). Together, these findings highlight the acquisition of pLVPK-like virulence-encoding plasmids as a major distinguishing feature of phylogroup A.

Given the presence of pLVPK-like virulence-encoding plasmids and genes such as rmpA in phylogroup A isolates, we assessed whether these molecular features conferred increased virulence compared to phylogroup B. First, we compared the clinical outcomes of patients infected with isolates from phylogroups A and B using all the strains. The two groups exhibited comparable baseline characteristics, the source of infection, and clinical outcomes (Supplementary Table 6). PSM was then performed to balance baseline clinical characteristics between phylogroups A and B, resulting in 213 matched pairs. Again, no significant differences were observed in clinical outcomes, including in-hospital all-cause mortality (15.0% [32/213] vs. 16.0% [34/213], P = 0.7889), predicted all-cause mortality (27.7% [59/213] vs. 29.6% [63/213], P = 0.6681), infection-attributable in-hospital mortality (10.8% [23/213] vs. 12.2% [26/213], P = 0.6487), the length of hospital stay (median 32 vs. 34 days, P = 0.5333), and time from the first CRKP-positive culture to discharge or death (median 15 vs. 18 days, P = 0.5572) (Table 1). To further assess the risk factors for overall in-hospital mortality in phylogroups A and B, we conducted multivariable binary logistic regression analysis after adjusting for standard covariates using PSM. Dominant phylogroup A exhibits higher resistance rates to tetracyclines and higher resistance levels to quinolones

In addition to the distinct differences in the carriage of pLVPK-like virulence-encoding plasmids, we further analyzed whether transferable antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) differed between phylogroups A and B. A total of 71 ARGs were identified across the isolates from both phylogroups (Fig. 3a). The average number of ARGs per isolate was significantly higher in phylogroup A isolates compared to phylogroup B ones (median 16 vs. 11). Specifically, tet(A) (resistance to tetracycline; 83.6% vs. 13.6%), qnrS1 (to quinolone; 86.1% vs. 5.0%), sul2 (to sulfonamides; 83.3% vs. 8.6%), blaLAP-2 (to β-lactams, 83.0% vs. 1.4%), catA2 (chloramphenicol; 61.3% vs. 7.2%), blaTEM-1 (to β-lactams, 91.7% vs. 69.9%), and rmtB1 (to aminoglycosides; 85.2% vs. 67.1%) were significantly more prevalent in phylogroup A isolates (Fig. 3b). We found that tet(A), qnrS1, sul2, blaLAP-2, and catA2 were present on the same plasmid, pLAP2_090527 (87,095 bp, IncFII(pSDP9R)), in phylogroup A strain 090527 by long-reads WGS. To further evaluate the phenotypic relevance of these findings, we first compared the clinical antimicrobial resistance profiles between all phylogroups A and B strains (Supplementary Data 9). Phylogroup A isolates exhibited higher resistance rates than phylogroup B isolates to amikacin (88.5% vs. 65.0%), tobramycin (89.0% vs. 65.2%), gentamicin (89.1% vs. 65.0%), tetracycline (87.2% vs. 29.0%), minocycline (81.0% vs. 22.6%), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (80.4% vs. 48.7%) (Supplementary Table 10). After removing clonal strains, the similar differences were observed (Supplementary Table 11). Secondly, to quantitatively assess the level of antimicrobial resistance, we randomly selected 100 isolates from each phylogroup to test the MICs of clinically relevant antimicrobial agents, including moxifloxacin, ciprofloxacin, minocycline, and tigecycline. The results showed that, compared with phylogroup B, phylogroup A isolates had significantly higher MIC values for all four agents: moxifloxacin (median 512 vs. 64 mg/L), ciprofloxacin (median 512 vs. 96 mg/L), minocycline (median 32 vs. 12 mg/L), and tigecycline (median 8 vs. 2 mg/L) (Fig. 3c). We evaluated whether specific bacterial features independently predicted clinical outcomes among 213 matched patient pairs, but none were identified as independent predictors of in-hospital mortality (Supplementary Table 12).

Dominant phylogroup A exhibits greater invasiveness and resistance to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis

Since all 1513 isolates in this study were clinical strains recovered from infected patients and phylogroup A was the dominant lineage, and given the strong association between CRKP intestinal colonization and subsequent infection, we performed murine gut colonization and bacterial translocation experiments. While phylogroups A and B representative isolates exhibited comparable levels of intestinal colonization and persistence (median 6.6 × 109 vs. 2.4 × 109 CFUs, P = 0.3097) (Supplementary Fig. 14a), phylogroup A isolates were detected in a higher proportion of mice with bacterial translocation. They also showed a higher bacterial burden across multiple extraintestinal organs, including the liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart, although only the spleen reached statistical significance, likely due to the large proportion of zero values in several organs (Fig. 4a). Upon intestinal barrier breach, bacteria typically reach the liver, which acts as a critical second-line defense against systemic dissemination, via the portal vein20. To assess the role of virulence-encoding plasmids in this process, we cured the plasmid from a representative phylogroup A isolate (strain 141508) carrying a prevalent virulence-encoding plasmid with an intact rmpA and frameshift rmpA2. In the plasmid-cured strain, translocation to the liver was observed in 2 of 10 mice, with no subsequent dissemination to other organs, whereas the wild-type strain 141508 translocated in 6 of 10 mice and exhibited bacterial dissemination to the spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart (Fig. 4a). Repeating the experiment using another strain, 141288, and its plasmid-cured derivative yielded similar results (Supplementary Fig. 14b, c). RNA-seq analyses showed that under anaerobic conditions in M9 medium, the succinate dehydrogenase genes (sdhABC) were upregulated in the strain harboring the virulence-encoding plasmid (Supplementary Table 13). Succinate is an intermediate metabolite or end product of many intestinal microorganisms and has been reported to regulate host-microbe interactions21. These results suggest that the virulence-encoding plasmid-positive strain could have an enhanced capacity to utilize microbiota-derived metabolites for contributing to intestinal adaptation. Alternatively, this upregulation might compensate for increased metabolic burden imposed by plasmid carriage, thus supporting persistence in oxygen-limited environments. Collectively, these findings indicate that phylogroup A isolates have an enhanced ability to breach intestinal defenses and disseminate systemically. Once translocated, CRKP was not readily cleared, with macrophages playing a crucial role in pathogen elimination20. We found that immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) exhibited significantly higher phagocytic uptake of phylogroup B isolates compared to phylogroup A (median 34,050 vs. 960 CFUs, P < 0.0001), indicating that phylogroup A isolates are more resistant to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis (Fig. 4b). Interestingly, pan-genome analysis revealed that 99% of phylogroup A isolates lacked two fimD genes compared to 99% of phylogroup B isolates that did have this fragment (Fig. 4c). There are 5 and 6 fimD gene clusters in phylogroup A and B strains, respectively, but the similarity of fimD in each cluster was very limited (Supplementary Fig. 15). FimD is essential for the transport and assembly of type 1 fimbriae tipped with the adhesin FimH22, and type 1 fimbriae promotes adhesion to phagocytic cells. RNA-Seq analysis further showed significantly lower mRNA expression of fimD and other fim genes in phylogroup A isolates (n = 5) than phylogroup B isolates (n = 5) (Fig. 4d). Complementation of fimD in phylogroup A strains promoted fimbrial formation, enhanced adhesion, and macrophage phagocytosis (median 1361 vs. 42,150, P = 0.0286) (Fig. 4e, f). These findings suggest that phylogroup A isolates exhibit enhanced invasiveness, increased resistance to macrophage-mediated clearance, and higher tolerance to antimicrobial pressure, posing significant challenges for infection control and treatment. Rather than categorizing them as hypervirulent CRKP, we suggest considering phylogroup A as highly invasive CRKP (hiCRKP).

Dominant phylogroup A was frequently detected during seasons with epidemic respiratory viruses and can be controlled by targeted bacteriophage treatment

Notably, in 2023, a striking difference was observed in the number of isolates between phylogroups A and B, with 239 and 33 isolates, respectively, suggesting that 2023 is a critical time point for the divergence in prevalence. We further conducted a month-by-month analysis of strain prevalence in 2023. We found that nearly 75% of phylogroup A isolates were recovered during the spring and winter months, temporally overlapping with peaks in influenza virus cases both at our hospital (Fig. 5a) and in national surveillance data (Supplementary Fig. 16). Except for influenza virus, respiratory viruses such as COVID-19 virus, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, and metapneumovirus also exhibit seasonal epidemic patterns23–25, and they were known to facilitate secondary bacterial infections26–28. We therefore amplified our analysis of concurrent infections to common respiratory viruses comprising influenza A virus, influenza B virus, parainfluenza virus, SARS-CoV-2, and metapneumovirus in our CRKP cases and investigated the potential risk factors for phylogroup A infections. Notably, pulmonary concurrent infection in 2023 was significantly more frequent among patients infected with phylogroup A isolates compared to phylogroup B (18.0% [43/239] vs. 3.0% [1/33], P = 0.0287) (Fig. 5b). The only case in phylogroup B was also infected with SARS-CoV-2. After adjusted for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, ICU admission, and the presence of invasive devices, respiratory viral infection remained significantly associated with an increased risk of phylogroup A CRKP infection. Bacteriophages (phages) typically target encapsulated bacteria at the capsular type level29. We constructed a cocktail comprising three phages, which we isolated previously29, capable of lysing ST11-KL64 CRKP isolates. In vitro lysis assays demonstrated that the phage cocktail with a low multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.01 effectively lysed phylogroup A isolates for 6-8 hours (Fig. 5c, d). A murine peritoneal infection model was established using the phylogroup A strain 141508. Intraperitoneal administration of the phage cocktail significantly improved survival compared to untreated controls: 73.3% (11/15) of treated mice survived at 3 days, compared to 13.3% (2/15) in the untreated infection group (P = 0.0006) (Fig. 5e). No mortality was observed in the PBS control or in the group receiving the phage cocktail alone (data not shown). We further established an acute pneumonia murine model caused by strain 141508 and observed that phage cocktail treatment significantly reduced pulmonary bacterial loads (median 1.85 × 108 vs. 6.17 × 104 CFUs; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 5f). In addition, serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, were significantly reduced following phage cocktail treatment (Fig. 5g–i). Histopathological analysis of lung tissues by Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining revealed that the treatment group exhibited substantially attenuated lung injury, characterized by largely preserved alveolar architecture, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration, and only focal or mild peribronchial inflammation (Fig. 5j). These results suggest that phage-based targeted therapy is promising for treating infections caused by dominant ST11-KL64 lineages.

Discussion

ST11-KL64 is the major type of CRKP in China17 and in this study we further identified two major phylogroups within ST11-KL64, both of which are circulating across China and internationally distributed, representing a globally clinically-important problem. Particularly, phylogroup A emerged in 2018 and became the dominant group by 2020. The rapid rise and predominance of phylogroup A suggest that specific evolutionary or biological advantages may underlie its epidemiological success. Through fine-scale genomic characterization and experimental validation, we found that the prevalence of this dominant CRKP sublineage is the result of multifaceted contributing factors including the acquisition of virulence-encoding plasmids and enhanced ability to evade phagocytosis.

First, the most significant difference between phylogroups A and B is the presence of virulence-encoding plasmids, with phylogroup B nearly carried no such plasmids. However, the acquisition of virulence-encoding plasmids by CRKP and its impact on pathogenicity remains controversial. Some studies have suggested that K. pneumoniae strains, after acquiring virulence-encoding plasmids, exhibit reduced host adaptability and inhibited intestinal colonization30. The expression of rmpA is a crucial factor influencing host phenotype, with distinct rmpA promoter sequences exerting control over the mucoid phenotype, contributing to the evolution of CRKP into infectious and virulent strains31. Previous studies have also shown that KL1 CR-HvKP, KL2 CR-HvKP, and ST11-KL64-HvKP did not exhibit significant differences in virulence32. However, a KL64 hypervirulent sublineage with enhanced resistance to phagocytosis, sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim, and tetracycline has been identified32. Another study has found that 58.3% (457/784) CRKP harbored virulence-encoding plasmids, but only 13.1% (60/457) exhibited a hypervirulent phenotype based on the string test and neutrophil assay33. Our study also demonstrated that there were no significant differences in clinical outcomes between phylogroups A and B. We show categorically that the so-called ‘hypervirulence’ imposed upon CRKP leads to no increase in mortality of affected patients but actually enhances invasiveness, thus elevating the likelihood to cause diseases (rather than asymptomatic colonization). This provides a viable and much-needed explanation for the increased carriage of virulence-encoding plasmids in CRKP and moves a critical step forward to solve the controversy over whether the ‘hypervirulent’ CRKP truly possesses increased virulence.

We have recommended to prudent use of the term hypervirulence in CRKP to avoid exaggerate anxiety among clinicians and negatively affect predict the outcomes, antimicrobial stewardship, and infection control16. When clinical or animal data is not available, the term “virulence plasmid-harboring K. pneumoniae (pVir-CRKP)” has been proposed to reflect the genomic profile without over interpreting phenotypic consequences34. However, such a genomic profile-based term may not be clear and effortless to comprehend in clinical settings, infection control practice, and public health domains. Based on our findings, we propose to use the term highly invasive CRKP (hiCRKP) instead, which can better capture the actual biological characteristics of these strains. It should also be noted that CRKP carrying virulence-encoding plasmids still represents a highly diverse population, with considerable strain diversity and heterogeneity. Moreover, virulence-encoding plasmids exhibit substantial variation in genetic content, including frequent truncations of rmpA and rmpA2, while the precise biological roles of other genes, such as iuc and iro, remain incompletely understood. Further fine-scale investigations are required to clarify the overall functional contribution of virulence-encoding plasmids, as well as the biological significance of specific virulence-associated and regulatory genes, including whether differences in rmpA expression contribute to the observed phenotypes. In addition, hiCRKP isolates causing infection may originate from immunocompromised patients including those in the ICU, which could make CRKP more likely to cause invasive infection. Therefore, in future studies, it will be important to precisely define the functional characteristics of isolates according to the specific features of different study cohorts.

Second, we observed the detection of phylogroup A strains with increased prevalence during epidemic respiratory viruses which was not observed for phylogroup B. Respiratory viral infections can augment bacterial attachment by increasing expression of host receptors or providing coupling agents for bacteria to host tissues35. For example, influenza virus can bind to sialosaccharides on epithelial surfaces via its hemagglutinin protein, leading to epithelial sloughing, necrosis, and an exaggerated host immune response36. Given the enhanced invasiveness and resistance to macrophage-mediated phagocytosis observed in phylogroup A strains, these bacteria may gain a competitive advantage during or after influenza infection. Mechanistically, we uncovered that phylogroup A strains have acquired virulence-encoding plasmids while losing a genomic fragment containing fimD. Type 1 fimbriae promote adhesion to phagocytic cells and facilitate bacterial clearance through phagocytosis37. The loss of fimD might therefore confer resistance to immune clearance in phagocyte-rich environments, such as in the lungs and bloodstream. Our results suggest that phylogroup A has undergone evolutionary adaptations that may confer improved survival and prevalence under changing environmental conditions. However, further studies are required to better understand the complexity of CRKP and host interactions.

Third, by genotypic and phenotypic analyses, we revealed that phylogroup A strains exhibit higher resistance rates to clinically important antimicrobial agents such as tigecycline. Since its introduction into the Chinese market in 2012, tigecycline has been recommended as a monotherapy or in combination for the treatment of extensively drug-resistant Gram-negative bacterial infections38. The selective pressure exerted by widespread tigecycline use may have contributed to the dissemination of specific CRKP strains30,39,40. These findings are important as they indicate that the use of carbapenem antibiotics has driven the overall spread of CRKP, while the use of other antimicrobial agents, such as tigecycline, might contribute to the expansion of phylogroup A lineages.

Our findings may have several implications. First, the discovery of the ST11-KL62 clade calls for continuous longitudinal genomic surveillance. Emerging lineages, such as KL62 identified in this study, require further investigation into their epidemiological drivers and biological functions to better understand the evolutionary trajectory of the CRKP population. Further, the detection of the phylogroup shift within ST11-KL64 highlights the need of fine-scale genomic surveillance based on higher resolution analysis such as SNP in addition to strain typing based on ST and capsular types. More importantly, by integrating detailed clinical data and experimental validation, we were able to identify key driving factors behind the clonal expansion of phylogroup A, thereby closing the gap between discovery and reasons. Collectively, functional fine-scale genomic surveillance is a powerful tool to identify emerging lineages and understand the driving factors or underlying mechanisms for WHO-listed top priority pathogens such as CRKP. Second, our findings support the development of targeted host-pathogen combination therapies against more invasive strains, as microbiome-based interventions have strong potential to prevent K. pneumoniae colonization and infection41. Specifically, the strategies aimed at eliminating virulence-encoding plasmids in CRKP, potentially in conjunction with host immune activation approaches, such as IFN-γ42 or granulocyte macrophage-colony stimulating factor43, to enhance phagocytic clearance are warranted to be further explored. Third, our study underscores the broader implications of prudent use of antimicrobial agents. In addition to the well-recognized role of antimicrobial agents in creating direct selective pressure, our findings once again highlight the need to consider their collateral damage and the potential for promoting cross-resistance.

This study also has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center; however, patients at this national referral hospital were from across China, and the 10-year continuous sample collection contributes to the representativeness of the isolates and allows for detailed clinical and fine-scale genomic analyses. Furthermore, our epidemiological finding of the shift from KL47 to KL64 is consistent with nationwide surveillance data, suggesting the robustness of our data. Second, our study focused exclusively on CRKP clinical isolates and did not include colonizing or environmental strains. This focus aligns with our primary goal to understand the clinical relevance and pathogenicity of dominant CRKP clones in infected patients, rather than their transmission dynamics between patients and the environment. Third, we observed a striking temporal association between the prevalence of dominant CRKP clones and seasonal influenza prevalence. While this association is intriguing, further studies are needed to establish causality and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

In summary, we performed a systematic 10-year longitudinal study of CRKP clinical isolates from a single hospital, with patients originating across the country. Our findings identified a KL62 lineage with increasing numbers of isolation. We illustrated the expansion of ST11-KL64 and its divergence into two major phylogroups, A and B, primarily distinguished by the presence of virulence-encoding plasmids and differences in antimicrobial resistance genes and functional components of the accessory genome. Phylogroup A has gained a clear epidemiological advantage over phylogroup B, likely due to its enhanced invasiveness and survival both within the host and under selective pressures in the hospital environment. To address the controversy surrounding whether ‘hypervirulent’ CRKP truly exhibits increased virulence, we propose using the term hiCRKP instead. Furthermore, we provide a targeted phage-based intervention strategy specifically against the hiCRKP sublineage, offering a viable precision therapeutic approach.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study complies with all relevant ethical regulations. Human studies were approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration and its amendments (2013). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective observational design of this study. All patient data were anonymized, and no identifiable individual participant information is included in this manuscript. All mice experiments in this study were approved by the Ethics Committee for Laboratory Animals of West China Hospital of Sichuan University (approval number: 20241101009).

Settings and patients

West China Hospital of Sichuan University is a large tertiary care center and a major referral hospital in western China and has a capacity of about 5,000 beds across 54 clinical departments. Demographic and clinical data of patients were retrieved from electronic health records and cross-verified to ensure data reliability. The demographic and clinical data of patients with CRKP were collected, including age, gender, obesity (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, malignancy, organ transplantation, concurrent infection with respiratory viruses, sample types, ICU admission, department of hospitalization, time to first CRKP isolation, presence of invasive devices, site of CRKP infection, and length of hospital stay. Outcomes, including in-hospital all-cause mortality, attributable in-hospital mortality, and time from the first CRKP-positive culture to discharge or death were also collected. All data were systematically gathered and stored securely using a standardized format. In addition, influenza surveillance data was collected from West China Hospital and the China National Influenza Center, respectively. Attributable mortality and causes of death were classified based on CDC guidelines (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/hus/sources-definitions/cause-of-death.htm). Patients who were discharged on their own wills but were expected to die based on the independent assessment of two clinicians were classified as predicted deaths.

Isolates

The isolates were recovered from clinical samples that were collected as part of routine care for patients with infections and were retrospectively included into this study. The non-duplicate clinical isolates were preliminarily identified as K. pneumoniae using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization-time-of-flight mass (MALDI-TOF MS; Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). The strains were resistant to at least one of the tested carbapenems (doripenem, ertapenem, imipenem, and meropenem) using Vitek II automated microbiology system (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France). For patients with multiple positive samples, only the first isolate was included.

Whole-genome sequencing

All isolates were streaked onto LB agar plates, and a single colony from each isolate was inoculated into LB broth for overnight incubation at 37 °C. Thereafter, cell pellets were collected. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and subsequently fragmented by sonication. A 350-bp insert library was constructed using the NEBNext Ultra II Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA). Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) with 150-bp paired-end layout. Additionally, for selected isolates (Supplementary Table 15), such as those used as the reference chromosome for genomic mapping or as reference plasmids for plasmid profiling, DNA from the same batch was prepared for long-read sequencing using the Rapid Sequencing Kit V14 (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and sequencing was conducted on the MinION platform using the R10.4.1 flow cell.

Genome assembly

For draft genome assembly, raw reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.3944 and subsequently reduced to a coverage depth of 150× through random subsampling with SeqKit v2.8.245. Genome assembly was then performed using SPAdes v3.15.546 in isolate mode. For complete genome assembly, raw reads from the MinION sequencing runs were basecalled with Dorado v1.3 ((https://github.com/nanoporetech/dorado)) using the super-accurate (sup) model dna_r10.4.1_e8.2_400bps_sup@v5.2.0 on R10.4.1 (FLO-MIN114) flow cells. The raw long reads were filtered to remove reads shorter than 1,000 bp and those with an average quality score below Q8 using NanoFilt v2.8.047. These filtered reads were then randomly divided into 20 subsets for de novo genome assembly, performed separately using Canu v2.248, Flye v2.9.549, Miniasm v0.3 (https://github.com/lh3/miniasm) with Minipolish v0.1.350, and Raven v1.8.3 (https://github.com/lbcb-sci/raven). A consensus assembly was generated using Trycycler v0.5.551, followed by three rounds of polishing with long reads using Medaka v1.12.1 (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka). Finally, the long-read polished genome was further refined using the corresponding short reads with Polypolish v0.6.052 and pypolca v0.3.153.

Genome profiling

Species identification was performed using average nucleotide identity analysis with FastANI v1.3454, comparing the query strains to the type strain K. pneumoniae ATCC 13883 T (accession no. CP064368.1). Isolates were assigned to a ST using mlst v2.23 (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst). Antimicrobial resistance genes were identified with AMRFinderPlus v3.12.855, while virulence factors and capsular types were determined using Kleborate v2.4.156. Plasmid replicons were identified through PlasmidFinder v2.157.

SNP calling and phylogenomic tree inference

The complete chromosome of strain 095649, the earliest ST11-KL64 strain in the dataset, was selected as the reference in mapping-based approaches. For new sequencing genomes in this study, quality-controlled reads from query genomes were aligned to the reference using the Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy). The resulting individual variants-applied sequences were compiled into a pseudo-alignment and a phylogenomic tree was then inferred using RAxML v8.2.12 under GTR + Γ model with four discrete rate categories, accounting for recombination, using Gubbins v3.4.358, with 10 iterations and branch support was assessed with 1000 bootstrap replicates. To infer a phylogenetic tree from all high-quality ST11-KL64 genomes, all non-local K. pneumoniae assemblies (n = 60,724) released before 2024 were retrieved from NCBI GenBank Assembly database and subjected to quality control using CheckM2 v1.1.059, and ST assignment and capsular locus typing using Kleborate v3.1.356. Assemblies with <97% completeness (n = 3), >5% contamination (n = 1), more than 500 contigs (n = 54), failed NCBI RefSeq checks (n = 1560), non-ST11 genomes (n = 50,240), non-ST11-KL64 capsular type (n = 5348) or non-clinical origin (n = 117) were discarded. After this curation, 3401 high-quality, unique ST11-KL64 assemblies of clinical origin were obtained and combined with the 993 local genomes of this study. These combined assemblies were processed as mentioned above, and a recombination-aware phylogenomic tree was inferred.

Dating phylogeny and population dynamics estimation

The recombination-filtered phylogenetic tree generated by Gubbins was time-calibrated using BactDating v1.1.260 under mixedcarc model. Each run consisted of a Markov chain Monte Carlo chain of 10⁷ iterations, performed in 10 replicates to ensure convergence. Effective sample sizes (ESS) for all posterior parameters were evaluated individually and only runs with ESS values exceeding 300 were considered reliable. Among these, the run with the highest posterior likelihood was selected as the representative dated tree for downstream analysis. To determine whether the sampling in this study reflects real population trends, effective population sizes for each phylogroup were estimated, and historical dynamics were inferred using the R package mlesky v0.1.861 under skykappa coalescent model. Analyses were performed with 1000 bootstrap replicates to assess the robustness of population size estimates over time.

Temporal signal was evaluated before molecular dating. Root-to-tip regressions using BactDating were performed on the recombination-masked phylogeny for the full dataset and, separately, within each phylogroup. For each analysis, the regression slope, R², and P value, together with the inferred substitution rate and root date, were recorded. In parallel, heterochronous and isochronous models with identical priors and MCMC settings were fitted to the same trees, and model fit was compared using the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC), summarized as ΔDIC = DIC_iso − DIC_het (ΔDIC > 10 interpreted as strong temporal signal). Concordance between whole-tree and per-phylogroup results was used to screen for date–clade confounding.

Identification of clonal clusters

To increase phylogenetic resolution, reads were mapped within each phylogroup to a phylogroup-specific reference (phylogroup A: strain 090174; B: 095649; C: 085085; D: 020098). A recombination-aware phylogeny was inferred with Gubbins (10 iterations), and a time-scaled tree was obtained with BactDating using 10 independent MCMC replicates (each with 107 iterations). A 50% burn-in was applied and effective sample sizes for all parameters exceeded 300, indicating adequate mixing and convergence.

Clonality was defined from the posterior distribution of the time to the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA) for every isolate pair, integrating tree topology, divergence after recombination masking, sampling dates, and parameter uncertainty. For each chain, 500 posterior samples were retained after burn-in and thinning; pooling across the 10 replicates yielded 5,000 posterior draws per pair. A pair was called clonal if the 95% upper credible limit of its pooled tMRCA distribution was ≤0.5 years. Clusters were then formed by single-linkage under this criterion, where isolates belong to the same clonal cluster if any link within the group meets the 0.5-year threshold. For downstream analyses, one representative isolate per clonal cluster was retained, prioritizing strains with MIC data and otherwise selecting the earliest sample.

Effective population size analyses under rarefaction and decloning

Sensitivity to sampling intensity and clustered transmission was assessed using two complementary resampling frameworks designed to decouple inferred population dynamics from the number of isolates sequenced. First, year-stratified rarefaction was used to reduce temporal and between-phylogroup sampling imbalance: isolates were randomly downsampled within each calendar year-and, where applicable, within each phylogroup–year stratum-to a maximum of 20 strains, and the resulting rarefied datasets were carried forward to the Ne inference workflow. Second, decloning was used to mitigate oversampling of closely related isolates arising from recent transmission: clonal clusters were defined on the recombination-masked, time-scaled phylogeny using pairwise tMRCA thresholds spanning 0.25–1.0 years; clusters were then formed by single linkage under this criterion (as described in the clonal transmission section), and one representative isolate per cluster was randomly retained. For both rarefaction and de-cloning, multiple stringency settings were evaluated (including scenarios in which all phylogroups were resampled versus only phylogroup A), and each setting was repeated over 100 independent replicates to quantify variability. Effective population size trajectories (Ne) were re-estimated for each replicate using mlesky in skykappa mode, and phylogroup dominance was assessed using paired nonparametric tests (Friedman test with post hoc paired Wilcoxon tests and Benjamini–Hochberg correction).

Comparative genomics and identification of clade-specific gene

Assembled genomes were annotated using Prokka v1.14.662. Predicted coding sequences were clustered using Panaroo v1.5.263 in sensitive mode to generate a gene presence/absence matrix. To identify group-specific genes, each gene’s distribution was assessed in the context of group designations. A gene was considered group-specific if it was present in ≥90% of genomes within the target group and absent in ≥90% of genomes outside that group.

RNA-Seq library preparation, sequencing, and analysis

Five isolates from each phylogroup were randomly selected to represent multiple dominant sublineages and used for the phagocytosis assays. All selected phylogroup A isolates were confirmed to carry rmpA/rmpA2, whereas those from phylogroup B were negative. Furthermore, to investigate the impact of the virulence-encoding plasmid on the host adaptability and colonization of the host strain, we selected the phylogroup A strain 141508 that used in the mice trial and its virulence-encoding plasmid-cured derivative. To partially mimic intestinal conditions in vitro, the strains were cultured in M9 medium under both anaerobic and aerobic conditions. For each group, four independent clones were selected for RNA-seq and downstream analysis (Supplementary Table 16).

Total RNA was extracted using the HiPure Universal RNA Mini Kit (Magen, Guangzhou, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was detected using the Agilent 4200 system (Agilent Technologies, Waldbron, Germany). Library preparation was built using VAHTS Universal V10 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The library was sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq X Plus system in PE150 mode. Clean reads were selected by removing low-quality reads (i.e., > 10% bases with Q ≤ 20) and adaptors using Cutadapt v3.564. The trimmed reads were then aligned to the reference genome (phylogroup A strain 140949) using HISAT2 v2.2.1 with default parameters65. Gene-level read counts were estimated with using featureCounts v2.0.166 to generate a count matrix. Differential gene expression was performed using DESeq2 v1.30.167, with significantly differentially expressed genes defined by False Discovery Rate (FDR)-adjusted P ≤ 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change |log2FC | ≥ 1. For cross-sample visualization of expression levels, normalized abundances were calculated as transcripts per million using R package edgeR v3.32.1.

In vitro and in vivo analyses

In vitro study

Minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs)

The MICs of meropenem, imipenem, and ertapenem were determined for CRKP isolates that lacked detectable carbapenemase-encoding genes. Moreover, the MICs of moxifloxacin, ciprofloxacin, minocycline, and tigecycline were determined for phylogroup A and phylogroup B isolates. All MICs were determined using the broth microdilution method according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI)68 with Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 as the quality control strain. All tests were performed in triplicate. CLSI-defined breakpoints were applied for all antimicrobial agents except tigecycline. Currently, neither the CLSI nor the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST, http://www.eucast.org) provides MIC breakpoints for tigecycline against K. pneumoniae. However, EUCAST has established tigecycline MIC breakpoints for E. coli and Citrobacter koseri (S ≤ 0.5 mg/L; R > 0.5 mg/L). In this study, the tigecycline MIC breakpoints for K. pneumoniae were interpreted based on those defined for E. coli and C. koseri.

Serum resistance assays

To assess whether the strains were cleared by complement and other substances in serum, serum resistance assays were conducted69. Bacterial cultures were grown in LB broth to the logarithmic phase and adjusted to an optical density of 0.5 McF (approximately 108 CFU/mL). The cultures were then diluted in saline to achieve a concentration of 2 × 106 CFU/mL. A total of 25 μL of bacterial suspension was mixed with 75 μL of fresh human serum (obtained from healthy volunteers, approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital with informed consent being obtained) in 96-well plates. The mixtures were incubated at 37 °C, and the culture was taken per hour for a 10-fold dilution series using saline until three hours. Subsequently, 100 μL from each dilution was spread onto LB agar plates using sterile glass rods and incubated overnight at 37 °C for colony counting. A time-kill curve was generated with time plotted on the x-axis and bacterial concentration on the y-axis.

Virulence-encoding plasmid curing

Plasmid curing was performed as previously described70 with modifications. Strains harboring pSGKP-km (37 °C) and pCasKp-Apr (30 °C) were streaked onto plates and incubated overnight. A colony containing pSGKP-km was cultured in LB with kanamycin (50 mg/L), and the plasmid was extracted. A spacer targeting the rmpA gene was constructed by annealing phosphorylated single-stranded oligonucleotides using T4 polynucleotide kinase. The spacer was cloned into pSGKP-km using BsaI-HF and T4 DNA ligase, generating Spacer+pSGKP-km. This construct was transformed into E. coli DH5α, and colonies were screened by PCR with RMPA-F and M13R primers. Competent cells of phylogroup A isolate 141508 were prepared using the Competent Cell Preps Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). pCasKp-Apr was introduced by electroporation, and a positive clone was induced with 20% L-arabinose to activate CRISPR-Cas expression. Spacer+pSGKP-km was then electroporated into the positive clone. After overnight incubation at 30 °C, colonies were streaked onto 5% sucrose plates for counterselection. Three purified clones were selected for WGS to confirm plasmid loss.

Stability of virulence-encoding plasmid

Bacterial cultures were grown to the logarithmic phase, and 10 µL of the culture was added to 0.05% SDS-containing broth, followed by overnight shaking incubation. Fresh medium was replaced every 24 hours for 16 days. Every 3 days, 50 µL of the culture was serially diluted to 106 CFUs, and 100 µL of the diluted suspension was plated onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C. On the second day, colonies from the LB plates were replica plated using sterile velvet onto LB agar plates containing potassium tellurite resistance. Colonies were subjected to PCR and Sanger sequencing to determine whether the virulence-encoding plasmid was lost. The primers used for PCR were listed in Supplementary Table 17.

fimD complementation in CRKP

We cloned a 3,550 bp DNA fragment containing the complete fimD gene along with its promoter region that predicted by BPROM71 into the pBCSK(+) vector (Miaolingbio, Wuhan, China) using the In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). The pBCSK(+) plasmid was extracted using a plasmid extraction kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China). Primers used for cloning were synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology (Beijing, China), and their sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 17. The resulting plasmid was introduced into phylogroup A strain 141323, 140637, 140693, and 141560. A pBCSK(+) vector lacking fimD was constructed and used as a negative control. Transformants were selected on LB agar supplemented with 30 mg/L chloramphenicol. The presence of the yraL-fimD fragment and/or the pBCSK(+) plasmid in the transformants was confirmed by PCR and validated by Sanger sequencing.

Macrophage phagocytosis assays

The phagocytosis assays were performed as previously described72,73. Immortalized bone marrow-derived macrophages (iBMDMs) cell line was donated by the laboratory of Dr. Shao Feng at the National Institute for Biological Sciences (Beijing, China). The cells were cultured in DMEM (Cytiva, Marlborough, MA, USA) containing 50 U/mL penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco, Carlsbad, CA, USA) and 10% fetal bovine serum (PAN-Biotech, Aidenbach, Germany), and seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells per well and incubated for 16-24 hours before infection. Freshly sub-cultured CRKP strains were washed and resuspended in sterile saline, then added to the wells at a MOI of 20. After 1 hour of incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, the medium was removed, and the cells were gently washed by antimicrobial-free DMEM. Cells were then incubated for another hour in DMEM containing gentamicin (500 mg/L) to kill extracellular bacteria. After washing three times with sterile saline, the solution was plated to confirm complete removal of extracellular bacteria. Cells were then lysed with 1 mL of PBS containing 0.1% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes. Lysates were serially diluted and plated on LB agar to quantify intracellular bacteria. Wild-type Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 was used as a positive control.

SEM and TEM were used to examine the bacterial morphology and adhesion to BMDMs of phylogroup A strain 141323 and its fimD-complemented clone. For SEM observation of bacterial adhesion to BMDMs, cells were seeded onto glass coverslips and incubated with bacteria for 30 min. After incubation, the samples were washed to remove non-adherent extracellular bacteria and fixed with 3% glutaraldehyde. For TEM analysis, bacterial suspensions were deposited onto sample grids and negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid for 1-2 min. Sample processing and imaging for both SEM and TEM were performed at the Lilai Biomedicine Experiment Center (Chengdu, China).

In vivo study

G. mellonella larvae assay

G. mellonella larvae assays were performed as described previously74,75. Healthy larvae (Huiyude, Tianjin, China), measuring approximately 2-2.5 cm in length and weighing between 250-350 mg, were selected for the experiment. Sixteen larvae were placed in each 9-cm-diameter disposable petri dish. The dishes were then incubated in the dark at 37 °C for 24 hours to allow them for recovery. Nine isolates were selected from phylogroup A and B, respectively, with bacterial loads injected at concentrations of 105, 104, and 103 CFUs, while a PBS group with sixteen larvae served as a negative control. The larvae were incubated in the dark at 37 °C. Every 12 hours, the petri dishes were removed, and the survival of the larvae was recorded over a total observation period of 72 hours.

CRKP intraperitoneal infection and gut translocation in mice

Wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old) were maintained in a standard animal facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Sichuan University. Mice were intraperitoneally injected with 200 μL of CRKP suspensions at two bacterial concentrations (5 × 10⁹ and 2 × 10⁸ CFU/mouse). Mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 10 per group): phylogroup A strain 141508, phylogroup B strain 141501, and a PBS control group. Mice were monitored daily for mortality over a 7-day period.

The gut translocation model was adapted from a previously described protocol76. Wild-type male C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks old) received antimicrobial-containing drinking water for 5 days, consisting of a cocktail of vancomycin (0.5 g/L), metronidazole (0.5 g/L), ampicillin (0.5 g/L), and neomycin (0.5 g/L). On day 6, mice were switched to 15% Miralax (Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany) in water. On day 7, mice were orally gavaged with 2 × 10⁹ CFU of CRKP. Preliminary experiments showed that mice exhibited weight loss from 1 to 5 days post-gavage, suggesting that they remained in an infected state during this period. By day 6, however, the mice began to regain weight (Supplementary Fig. 17). Subsequent sacrifice of mice and plating revealed that CRKP had been cleared from the mice. Therefore, in the formal experiments, mice were euthanized and analyzed on day 5. On day 12 (5 days post-gavage), mice were euthanized, and organs (liver, spleen, kidneys, lungs, and heart) were collected, homogenized, and plated on Simmons’ Citrate Agar for overnight incubation at 37 °C. Colony counts were used to determine bacterial burden in translocated organs.

Phage lysis assay and treatment in murine abdominal infection

Previously, we have isolated three phages, P24 (genus Przondovirus, family Autographiviridae)29, P39 (genus Webervirus, family Drexlerviridae)29, and P49 (genus Epseptimavirus, family Demerecviridae, unpublished), against ST11-KL64 CRKP isolates and were therefore used in this study. In vitro lysis kinetics of the three phages in combination of 1:1:1 volume were evaluated by mixing 180 µL of bacterial suspension (strain 141508 of phylogroup A, 1 × 108 CFU/mL) with 20 µL of phage solution (1 × 107 plague-forming units [PFU]/mL), followed by measurement of optical density every 15 min at 600 nm. A phage cocktail was prepared by mixing equal volumes of P24, P39, and P49 to a final titer of 1 × 10¹⁰ PFU/mL for subsequent in vivo testing.

Wild-type female C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) were randomly assigned into four groups: CRKP infection group (n = 15), phage treatment group (n = 15), PBS group (n = 5), and phage-only group (n = 5). Log-phase cultures of the phylogroup A strain 141508 were adjusted to a concentration of 6.6 × 10⁸ CFU/mL, and mice in the infection and treatment groups were intraperitoneally injected with 200 µL of the bacterial suspension. One-hour post-infection, mice in the treatment group received 100 µL of the phage cocktail (MOI = 10) via intraperitoneal injection. Survival was monitored daily for 7 days following infection.

Phage cocktail treatment in a murine acute pneumonia model

Wild-type female C57BL/6 mice (6 weeks old) were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions for two weeks prior to experiments. Immunosuppression was induced by intraperitoneal injection of cyclophosphamide (20 mg/mL) at 150 μL per mouse 4 days before infection and 100 μL per mouse 1 day before infection. Strain 141508 had a concentration of 4.3 × 10⁷ CFU/mL, three phages (P24, P39, and P49) were mixed at a 1:1:1 ratio to generate a phage cocktail with a final titer of 1 × 10¹⁰ PFU/mL. Mice were randomly divided into five groups: baseline (n = 4), infection (n = 15), phage treatment (n = 15), phage control (n = 3), and blank control (n = 3). Mice were intranasally inoculated with 20 μL bacterial suspension or PBS, followed by 40 μL PBS or phage cocktail as indicated. Baseline mice were sacrificed 1 hour post-infection to determine the initial bacterial load. The remaining mice were sacrificed at 24 hours post-treatment. Lung tissues were collected for bacterial enumeration, blood samples were obtained for inflammatory cytokine analysis, and selected lungs were used for histopathological examination. Formalin-fixed lungs samples were embedded in paraffin and stained with H&E at the Core Facility of West China Hospital, Sichuan University. Inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α in serum was measured by ELISA (Boster, Wuhan, China).

Statistical analysis

For clinical analysis, categorical variables were compared using the chi-square or Fisher’s exact test and reported as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using independent-samples t test, one-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) or the Mann-Whitney U test. If a significant result (P < 0.05) was detected for tests involving multiple group comparisons, pairwise comparisons were performed with the α level adjusted using Holm’s sequential Bonferroni adjustment to control for type I error. Continuous variables were reported as either mean and standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data or median and interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed data.

For comparisons between patients infected with ST11-KL64 and those infected with ST11-KL47, as well as between phylogroups A and B, PSM was used to address imbalances in baseline characteristics between the groups. Propensity scores were calculated using logistic regression models based on demographic characteristics and comorbidities. PSM was then performed with a 1:1 ratio and a 0.2 caliper value using the nearest neighbor method. The matched cohorts, which were balanced for all included variables, were further compared for clinical outcomes. Binary logistic regression analysis was applied to identify risk factors for overall in-hospital mortality among patients with CRKP infection. For univariate analysis, the power of the test was amplified to 0.1. The magnitudes of the associations between outcomes and explanatory variables were assessed using odds ratios (ORs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

For association analysis between respiratory viral infection and phylogroup A CRKP infection, multivariable regression analysis was performed, adjusting for age, sex, diabetes mellitus, malignancy, ICU admission, and the presence of invasive devices to control for potential confounders. Due to the substantial imbalance in the distribution of respiratory viral infections between the phylogroup A and phylogroup B groups, Firth logistic regression was employed to reduce bias and improve the accuracy of parameter estimates. ORs with 95% CIs were calculated. A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using R v4.5.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

For mouse or in vitro experiments, results are expressed as mean ± the standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons between two groups were made using the Mann-Whitney-Wilcoxon rank-sum test for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. For comparisons involving three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc test or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test followed by FDR procedures was used. A two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics v29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), JMP Pro 14 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) or GraphPad Prism v8.2.1 (Graphpad Software Inc, La Jolla, CA, USA).
