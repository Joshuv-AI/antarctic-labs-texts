Clomiphene Citrate in Men With Hypogonadism—Do we Need to Monitor IGF-1 in All Patients?
Authors: José Miguel Hinojosa-Amaya, Elena V Varlamov, Maria Fleseriu
Published: 2025

Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) emerged as a plausible option for acromegaly treatment due to their estrogen-like effect of reducing insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) [1]. SERMs act by activating the estrogen receptor in the liver, which in turn downregulates JAK-STAT pathway of growth hormone (GH) signaling cascade, thus reducing hepatic production of IGF-1 [2]. Clomiphene and tamoxifen additionally block estrogen receptors at the pituitary-hypothalamic level, stimulating gonadotrophin release, ovulation in women, and testosterone production in men [1].

Importantly, estrogen and SERMs have been shown to reduce IGF-1 levels in individuals without GH disorders. Tamoxifen reduces IGF-1 levels in women with breast cancer by 17% to 38% and in normal men by 25%. Clomiphene citrate reduces IGF-1 by approximately 32% to 35% in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and by approximately 20% in controls [2]. This finding led to their experimental use in acromegaly.

A meta-analysis of observational studies including 63 patients corroborated a significant decrease of IGF-1 levels in postmenopausal women (n = 49) with acromegaly using oral (n = 22) or transdermal (n = 1) estradiol, tamoxifen (n = 19), or raloxifene (n = 21). Interestingly, IGF-1 reduction in men (n = 14) was not statistically significant after a maximum of 2 months receiving SERMs [3]. A prospective open-label trial of clomiphene 50 mg/day as an add-on therapy for 16 men with uncontrolled acromegaly and low testosterone levels showed a decrease in IGF-1 levels by 41%, and normalization in 7 (44%). Additionally, testosterone levels rose 209% in this group, with normalization in 4 (67%) [4]. Given the lack of larger and longer efficacy and safety studies, SERMs are not routinely used in patients with acromegaly.

However, could SERMs cause IGF-1 deficiency in patients without acromegaly, and particularly in those with hypopituitarism? [1]. Given the increase of clomiphene use for hypogonadism in men (off-label in the United States), this question becomes even more relevant in current practice.

Mogar and colleagues [4] recently compared in a retrospective study changes in IGF-1 levels before and after “real-world” clomiphene citrate (50 mg thrice per week for ≥3 months) to treat 20 men with central hypogonadism (15 with structural pituitary disease and 5 with an idiopathic cause). Total testosterone levels increased in all patients (free testosterone in 45%), and IGF-1 levels changed by −0.60 (interquartile range −1.2 to −0.0) SDs in 15 (75%) participants. Notably, in 2 patients IGF-1 decreased more than 2.0 SD [4], suggesting that some hypogonadal men treated with clomiphene may, indeed, have low IGF-1. As significant IGF-1 lowering could mimic GH deficiency (GHD), concerns regarding fatigue, impaired glucose metabolism, adverse changes in body composition and bone mass, and impaired quality of life are important.

Therefore, the authors recommended IGF-1 levels monitoring in hypogonadal patients treated with clomiphene citrate. However, we consider there are some caveats that limit this interpretation.

First, there is an intraindividual variability of IGF-1 assays, ranging from 10% to 30% on a week-to-week basis, thought to be driven by physiological factors such as protein intake, intercurrent illness, exercise, stress, circadian rhythm, and others [5]. Additionally, there is significant interlaboratory variability, both for immunoassays and mass spectrometry methods, that makes it challenging to compare values in routine clinical practice. Between-laboratory variability has been reported to range from 6.9% to 19% depending on local or central calibration, and interlaboratory agreement on reactants and processes [5].

Second, decline in IGF-1 may not equate with clinical manifestations of GHD in all patients. Furthermore, intrinsic estrogen-like clomiphene effects and its secondary effects via testosterone augmentation may counterbalance the consequences of IGF-1 lowering.

Limited preclinical data indicate that clomiphene may induce white adipose tissue browning and reduce fat mass in mice. This effect has not yet been replicated in humans [6]. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial of men with obesity, impaired glucose tolerance or type 2 diabetes (all received metformin), and functional hypogonadism (without evidence of organic or genetic cause), 3 months of clomiphene (25 mg daily) resulted in a significant reduction of fasting glucose, insulin, and homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance, and a trend toward reduction in body mass index and waist circumference [7]. In parallel with these changes, a significant testosterone and gonadotropin increase was observed. However, longer-term and larger studies in men, and those with structural pituitary disease, are lacking. In women with PCOS, fasting insulin or insulin resistance was stable despite significant IGF-I reductions and increases in IGF binding protein-1 levels during one course of ovulation induction [1].

The effects of clomiphene on bone seem to be overall positive, driven by bone-selective estrogen agonist activity. It has been shown to prevent bone loss in animal models. A retrospective study of hypogonadal men treated for at least 12 months found significant improvements in mean femoral neck and lumbar spine bone density scores in parallel with testosterone increases, leading the authors to conclude that both direct estrogen-like activity and increases in sex steroids via central stimulation of gonadotrophins contribute to bone improvement [8].

Though it is unclear if clomiphene-induced IGF-1 reduction is symptomatic, clinical repercussions of persistent IGF-1 decrease are unknown. Further research needs rigorous clinical assessment of such patients, especially those with hypopituitarism.

Another interesting finding in the study by Mogar and colleagues [4] is the lack of a statistically significant correlation (r = 0.2; P = .45) between the increased free testosterone levels and decreased IGF-1 levels. This suggests that testosterone aromatization to estrogen may play a lesser role in stimulating estrogen receptors than the direct action of clomiphene. However, estrogen levels were not measured in this study and further research to determine clomiphene effects is needed.

In conclusion, Mogar et al [4] reaffirm the known IGF-1–lowering effect of clomiphene, which may be beneficial in men with hypogonadism and acromegaly, but raises important questions about clinical consequences in patients with unrecognized or untreated GHD. Current clinical and preclinical data do not support adverse effects of clomiphene on weight, body composition, glucose metabolism, and bone health; therefore, reduction of IGF-1 levels by clomiphene may not have the same clinical consequences as GHD. However, patients with structural pituitary disease and hypopituitarism treated with clomiphene require further study. Measurement of IGF-1 and testing for GHD may be justified in those who exhibit signs and symptoms of GHD on clomiphene or may also be considered in men prior to initiation of clomiphene. Additionally, further research is needed to see if patients treated for GHD would require GH dose adjustment.
