Early urinary decompression and mortality in septic obstructing ureteral stones: a nationwide comparative-effectiveness study
Authors: Mohamed Mahmoud Dogha, Atef A Hassan, Osama Mostafa Mohamed, Mahmoud Farag, Islam S Nouh, Mohamed Hamdy Ibrahim, Assem Abdelaziz Mesbah, Mohamed F Elebiary, Nader A Abdelkhalek, Adel Moalwi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSeptic obstructing ureteral stones are a time-critical urological emergency. Although urgent drainage is recommended, uncertainty persists regarding the comparative effectiveness of decompression strategies and the influence of timing in real-world practice.MethodsWe analyzed adults with sepsis or septic shock and obstructing ureteral stones (n = 9,172) from the U.S. HCUP National Inpatient Sample (2016–2022). Exposures included any decompression (ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy [PCN]) versus conservative care, and decompression timing (same-day [day 0] vs. ≥ day 1; ≤ 24 h vs. > 24 h). The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality; secondary outcomes were acute kidney injury (AKI), mechanical ventilation, dialysis, and length of stay. Adjusted associations were estimated using propensity score matching, overlap weighting, instrumental variable (IV) analysis, and landmark/time-dependent survival models with a prespecified 12-variable adjustment set.ResultsOverall mortality was 2.5%. Decompression was associated with lower mortality than conservative care (1.6% vs. 4.0%; risk difference − 2.4% [95% CI − 3.2 to − 1.7]; number needed to treat ≈ 41). Late decompression (> 24 h) versus early (≤ 24 h) was associated with higher odds of mortality (OR 2.17 [1.38–3.41]), AKI, ventilation, and dialysis. In exploratory analyses among decompressed patients, PCN showed higher adjusted mortality than stenting (5.5% vs. 2.8%; OR 2.05 [1.31–3.09]); IV results were directionally consistent but likely reflect residual confounding by indication and feasibility.ConclusionUrgent decompression, particularly within 24 h, was associated with lower in-hospital mortality and fewer organ-failure outcomes. Modality differences should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating; PCN remains essential when retrograde stenting is not feasible.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02119-z.Keywords: obstructing ureteral stone, urosepsis, urinary decompression, ureteral stent, percutaneous nephrostomy, treatment timing, causal inference

Introduction

Septic obstructing ureteral stones represent a critical urological emergency; reported mortality varies widely, often in the low single digits in contemporary urosepsis cohorts, but substantially higher in septic shock and selected severe presentations, reaching 25.9% in severe cases [1]. With kidney stones affecting approximately 10.1% of the American population, the potential for life-threatening obstructive complications continues to escalate [2]. This condition merges urinary tract obstruction with systemic infection, creating a medical emergency that requires immediate intervention.

The clinical burden is substantial, with urosepsis carrying significant morbidity and mortality rates. Globally, the incidence of urolithiasis has increased substantially over recent decades, affecting millions of patients annually [3]. In the United States, sepsis is the third leading cause of death in hospitals, with associated healthcare costs exceeding billions of dollars annually [4].

Timely source control through urgent decompression is the cornerstone of management and is considered critical for improving outcomes, although much of the comparative evidence is observational. Current guidelines recommend emergency decompression within specific timeframes, with evidence suggesting that earlier intervention may optimize outcomes. Delayed decompression significantly increases the risk of mortality [5].

The primary decompression modalities are ureteral stenting and percutaneous nephrostomy (PCN). Recent systematic analyses have found variable success rates between techniques, with each modality offering distinct advantages depending on the clinical circumstances [6]. Clinical prediction models have identified key sepsis risk factors, including demographic factors, laboratory parameters, and infection markers [7].

Despite a consensus on the urgency of intervention, clinical uncertainty persists regarding the optimal timing and modality selection. Previous research has been limited by methodological constraints, including confounding bias and insufficient adjustment for illness severity. Notable healthcare disparities exist in the delivery of timely care across different patient populations and clinical settings [8].

This study addresses two primary questions in the management of septic obstructing ureteral stones: Does urgent decompression reduce mortality compared with conservative approaches? How does the timing of intervention modify patient outcomes? The head-to-head comparison of decompression modalities (ureteral stenting versus PCN) was examined as a secondary, exploratory analysis.

Using nationally representative data and prespecified analytic methods, this study aimed to provide observational comparative-effectiveness evidence to inform clinical decision-making for this life-threatening urological emergency.

Methods

Data source and study design

Retrospective cohort study of 9,172 adult hospitalizations for septic obstructing ureteral stones from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) National Inpatient Sample (NIS), 2016–2022. The NIS approximates a 20% stratified sample of U.S. community hospital discharges. Primary comparative-effectiveness analyses used unweighted data (propensity score matching [PSM], overlap weighting [OW], and instrumental variable [IV] methods are incompatible with discharge weights); survey-weighted results were limited to descriptive sensitivity analyses. Cohort assembly, variable construction, and statistical analyses were executed via a fully scripted, reproducible workflow; all tables/figures were programmatically generated and archived (Fig. 1). Analyses complied with the HCUP Data Use Agreement; no hospital or state identifiers are reported [9].

STROBE/RECORD flow diagram of cohort assembly

Cohort construction

Adults (≥ 18 years) hospitalized with sepsis/septic shock and obstructing ureteral stones identified from diagnosis fields; procedure fields scanned for urinary decompression (ureteral stent or percutaneous nephrostomy). The pipeline extracted the initial decompression modality (STENT, PCN, or NONE), procedure timing, crossover, Charlson comorbidity index/components, hospital attributes, and equity variables (race/ethnicity, payer, neighborhood income quartile). All diagnosis/procedure definitions are presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Exposures

Primary

Any decompression (STENT or PCN) versus conservative management (NONE).

Head-to-head

PCN versus STENT among decompressed patients.

Timing

Examined same-day (day 0) vs. ≥ day 1 and 24-hour cutoffs from admission using landmark and time-dependent approaches to mitigate immortal-time bias. Because NIS records the procedure day as a calendar-day integer (day 0, day 1, day 2, …) and we derived timing as 24 × procedure day, all timing values are multiples of 24 h (0, 24, 48 h, etc.), with no intermediate values between 0 and 24 h available in the dataset. As reported in the Abstract, comparisons of ≤ 24 h versus > 24 h therefore correspond to day 0 (coded as 0 h) versus day ≥ 1 (coded as ≥ 24 h). The pre-specified sensitivity analysis reclassified procedures at exactly 24 h from “early (≤ 24 h)” to “late (> 24 h)”; early mortality remained lower (45/3,821 [1.18%] vs. 48/1,893 [2.54%]), corroborating the primary results (66/4,795 [1.38%] vs. 27/919 [2.94%]).

Outcomes

We prespecified in-hospital mortality as the primary outcome of this study. Analyses of acute kidney injury (AKI), mechanical ventilation, dialysis, vasopressors, and length of stay (LOS) were secondary and exploratory. Given the strong coherence in direction and magnitude across analytic approaches, we present two-sided p-values without formal family-wise error control; the interpretation emphasizes effect sizes and precision. (In a sensitivity analysis, Holm–Bonferroni adjustments did not alter the qualitative conclusions.)

Covariates (pre-exposure adjustment set)

We prespecified a 12-variable adjustment set measured at or before exposure: age, sex, Charlson index, weekend admission, inter-facility transfer, teaching hospital status, hospital region, race/ethnicity, ZIP income quartile, insurance/payer, shock on admission, and acute renal failure on day 0. These variables informed confounding control across analyses and were harmonized from the core dataset fields and derived features.

Statistical analysis

Primary causal identification strategies

Propensity score matching (PSM): decompression versus conservative

Propensity scores were estimated using 12 covariates: 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement (calliper 0.2 SD logit-PS). Of the 5,714 treated patients, 3,262 (57.1%) matched, yielding 3,262 pairs. Balance was assessed via standardized mean differences (SMDs); post-match |SMD| < 0.10 for all covariates (mean ≈ 0.019). The reported matched risk difference in mortality was significant [10].

Overlap weighting (OW): PCN vs. STENT

Propensity scores for PCN were estimated using logistic regression on 12 covariates. Overlap weights applied without trimming; covariate balance targeted |SMD| < 0.10. Effective weighted sample size: 1,836. The reported weighted risks, risk differences, odds ratios (95% CIs), and NNT/NNH were calculated using hospital-clustered robust standard errors [11]. (Supplementary Material 2).

Instrumental variables (IV): PCN vs. STENT. For each hospital, we computed the prior-year share of PCN among all decompressions for septic obstructing stones and categorized hospitals into tertiles (PCN-preference). The unit of analysis for the instrument was the hospital identifier available in NIS; no hospital identifiers are reported per HCUP DUA. The first stage regressed PCN on the instrument and covariates; the second stage estimated the association between instrument-predicted modality and in-hospital mortality using an IV approach specified for a binary outcome, with hospital-clustered standard errors. Instrument strength was F = 66.85; covariate balance and over-identification checks are reported [12].

Time-dependent Cox models treated decompression as time-varying (switching from 0 to 1 at decompression time), adjusting for age, sex, Charlson index, shock, and acute renal failure on day 0. Proportional hazards were verified (Schoenfeld global p = 0.23; treatment p = 0.31). Landmark analyses at the admission day (day 0) and at 24 h from admission compared patients treated after each landmark with those untreated at the landmark, eliminating immortal-time bias.

Additional analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves were generated by modality, extended Cox models, early versus-late timing summaries, hospital-level practice variation, and equity analyses by race/ethnicity, payer, and neighborhood income. As a transparency supplement (predictive, not causal), eight pre-treatment models were trained via hospital-grouped 10-fold cross-validation (training + held-out test sets) to limit information leakage using only pre-treatment variables: K-Nearest Neighbors (k = 5, Euclidean), SVM, RF, GBM, and XGBoost with class_weight=’balanced’/scale_pos_weight where applicable. All outcome models used hospital-clustered robust standard errors.

Propensity distributions showed adequate common support, and post-matching/weighting balance met |SMD| < 0.10. Using the full adjustment set, we estimated the standardized treated/untreated risks, risk difference, risk ratio, and NNT with bootstrap confidence intervals.

Primary comparative-effectiveness analyses used unweighted data because NIS discharge weights distort individual-level causal estimands (matching, overlap weighting, IV). Descriptive survey-weighted sensitivity analyses were planned separately.

Ethics, data use, and reporting

Retrospective analysis of de-identified HCUP National Inpatient Sample (NIS) data. Per federal regulations, de-identified secondary data analyses do not constitute human subjects research and require no IRB/informed consent. Investigators completed the HCUP DUA training and complied with the Data Use Agreement: no cell sizes ≤ 10 published, no hospital/state identification, research-only use. Version-controlled code and synthetic non-identifiable data artifacts archived; scripts reproduce all tables/figures. Reporting followed the STROBE and RECORD guidelines [13, 14].

Results

Cohort characteristics

The final cohort included 9,172 patients: conservative, 3,458 (37.7%); STENT, 4,982 (54.3%); and PCN, 732 (8.0%). Overall, the in-hospital mortality rate was 2.5% (228 deaths). Compared with the STENT and conservative groups, PCN patients had greater acuity and comorbidity: higher transfer-in rates (37.4% vs. 17.5% in the STENT group; 23.1% in the conservative group), higher Charlson index (3.0 vs. 1.9 in the STENT group; 2.6 in the conservative group), and higher frequencies of AKI (75.8%), vasopressor use (11.6%), and mechanical ventilation (13.5%). The LOS was longest with PCN (mean 11.0 ± 10.4 days) and shortest with STENT (6.7 ± 8.1 days) (Table 1).

Primary outcome patterns by modality

Unadjusted mortality differed markedly: conservative 3.90% (135/3,458), STENT 1.06% (53/4,982), and PCN 5.46% (40/732). Kaplan–Meier curves show lower in-hospital mortality with STENT than with PCN or conservative management, with a divergence within approximately 5–10 days that persists through discharge. (Fig. 2).

Decompression vs. conservative (primary question)

Propensity score–matched analysis (3,262 matched pairs; 57.1% of treated matched) demonstrated a − 2.5% point absolute reduction in mortality with decompression (p < 0.0001) and excellent covariate balance (mean post-match SMD ≈ 0.019) (Table 2). Marginal standardized risks (g-computation) yielded a treated risk of 1.6% versus an untreated risk of 4.0%; RD − 2.4% (95% CI − 3.2% to − 1.7%), RR 0.39 (95% CI 0.30–0.50), NNT 41.

PCN vs. STENT (head-to-head among decompressed)

Overlap weighting achieved a near-perfect balance (all |SMD|<0.10; mean ≈ 0.007). Weighted mortality was 5.5% (PCN) vs. 2.8% (STENT), RD + 2.7%, OR 2.05 (95% CI 1.31–3.09). Instrumental variable analysis using hospital PCN-preference tertiles (first-stage F = 66.85) yielded results that were directionally consistent with those from overlap weighting. Under LATE assumptions among compliers, the IV estimate suggested higher mortality with PCN versus stenting (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.13–1.47; p = 0.00019) but remains sensitive to IV assumptions.

Timing of decompression and immortal-time–bias-free estimates

Unadjusted comparisons showed higher mortality when decompression occurred on or after day 1 and after 24 h. In landmark analyses that removed immortal time, patients decompressed on the admission day (day 0) had lower mortality than patients not yet decompressed at day 0 (i.e., decompressed on/after day 1 or not decompressed) (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.48–0.94; p = 0.020). At 24 h, the association attenuated and was not significant (OR 0.91, 95% CI 0.60–1.40; p = 0.750). Consistently, late (> 24 h) versus early (≤ 24 h) decompression showed higher odds across outcomes: mortality OR 2.17 (95% CI 1.38–3.41), AKI OR 1.41 (95% CI 1.22–1.63), mechanical ventilation OR 8.69, and dialysis OR 4.36 (all p < 0.001, except where noted) [16]. Most decompressions occurred early: 83.9% ≤ 24 h (4,795/5,714 with decompression) (Table 4; Fig. 3).

Time-to-event modeling

In the time-dependent Cox models, treatment status switched from 0 to 1 at the time of decompression. We fit (i) a parsimonious model adjusting for age, sex, Charlson index, shock on admission, and acute renal failure on day 0, and (ii) an extended model with the full pre-exposure covariate set; Table 5 reports the extended model. Proportional-hazards assumptions were met in both models (Schoenfeld test: global p = 0.23; treatment p = 0.31). In the extended model, STENT vs. conservative had HR 0.82 (95% CI 0.73–0.93; p = 0.001), whereas PCN vs. conservative had HR 1.04 (95% CI 0.85–1.27; p = 0.707). Significant risk factors included inter-facility transfer (HR 1.35, 95% CI 1.10–1.66; p = 0.004), shock on admission (HR 1.27, 95% CI 1.02–1.60; p = 0.036), and higher ZIP-code income quartile (HR 1.08, 95% CI 1.02–1.14; p = 0.006) (Table 5; Fig. 4).

Secondary outcomes

The patterns paralleled mortality. AKI occurred in 49.1% (conservative), 50.6% (STENT), and 75.8% (PCN). Mechanical ventilation occurred in 9.0% (conservative), 5.4% (STENT), and 13.5% (PCN), and dialysis in 2.5%, 1.2%, and 3.4%, respectively. LOS was shortest with STENT (mean 6.7 days), intermediate with conservative treatment (7.7), and longest with PCN (11.0) (Fig. 5; Table 2).

Three-way modality comparisons for clinical outcomes

Late decompression (> 24 h) was consistently associated with worse outcomes than early decompression (≤ 24 h): ventilation occurred in 22.9% vs. 3.3% (OR ≈ 8.69; RR ≈ 6.93; absolute risk increase [ARI] + 19.6 pp; NNH ≈ 5), acute kidney injury (AKI) in 60.9% vs. 52.5% (OR ≈ 1.41; RR ≈ 1.16; ARI + 8.5 pp; NNH ≈ 12), dialysis in 4.1% vs. 1.0% (OR ≈ 4.36; RR ≈ 4.22; ARI + 3.2 pp; NNH ≈ 32), and mortality in 2.9% vs. 1.4% (OR ≈ 2.17; RR ≈ 2.13; ARI + 1.6 pp; NNH ≈ 64). By modality, the early advantage was pronounced for STENT (e.g., ventilation 21.0% late vs. 2.7% early; mortality 2.9% vs. 0.8%), whereas PCN showed higher crude event rates overall and an apparent early PCN- mortality inversion (6.3% early vs. 3.1% late); this pattern is consistent with severity-driven- selection into immediate PCN and does not persist after covariate balancing (Table 3).

Equity and hospital-level variation

Across hospitals with ≥ 10 cases, crude mortality appeared to range from 0% to 50%; because these are small denominators, we provide empirical Bayes- shrinkage estimates and 95% intervals in a supplementary figure to mitigate spurious extremes. Equity stratifications showed consistent modality gradients within strata PCN > Conservative > STENT for crude mortality, together with payer-, race/ethnicity-, and income-linked differences. By payer, crude conservative care- mortality was 6% in Medicaid (21/351) and 13% in self-pay- (12/92); among decompressed patients, PCN mortality exceeded STENT in both Medicare (28/481 [5.8%] vs. 39/2,737 [1.4%]) and private insurance (12/128 [9.4%] vs. 14/1,221 [1.1%]) (Supplementary Material 3).

By race/ethnicity, PCN mortality was highest among Black patients (≈ 19%), whereas STENT mortality was ~ 0–1% across groups, and conservative mortality for White patients was ~ 4%. These estimates are based on small cells and should be interpreted cautiously, with exact binomial 95% CIs shown in (Supplementary Material 4).

By neighborhood income, quartile 4 PCN mortality was 11.2% (33/294) versus ~ 0–4% in lower quartiles, while STENT mortality remained ~ 0–2% across quartiles and conservative care- mortality ranged 2–5%. (Supplementary Material 5).

Machine-learning prediction

Machine-learning prediction models are detailed in Supplementary Materials 6–8. In brief, across eight pre-treatment models evaluated via hospital-grouped 10-fold cross-validation, test-set AUCs ranged from 0.794 (Logistic Regression) to 0.992 (K-Nearest Neighbors). Feature importance (Random Forest) prioritized age, Charlson index, acute renal failure on day 0, and shock on admission. These models served as a supplement to transparency for predictive insight only and did not inform causal claims.

Discussion

This nationally representative study of 9,172 adult hospitalizations for septic obstructing ureteral stones found that urinary decompression was associated with lower in-hospital mortality compared with conservative management and that outcomes differed by timing and decompression modality. Using complementary confounding-control and time-to-event methods propensity score matching, overlap weighting, instrumental variable analysis, and immortal-time-bias-free survival models we observed three consistent patterns: (1) decompression versus conservative management was associated with a 2.4% point absolute difference in mortality reduction (NNT ≈ 41; RR 0.39; 95% CI 0.30–0.50); (2) among decompressed patients, percutaneous nephrostomy is associated with higher mortality than ureteral stenting (overlap-weighted OR 2.05; 95% CI 1.31–3.09), though severity confounding likely contributes; and (3) early decompression (≤ 24 h) was associated with better in-hospital outcomes than delayed intervention, with late decompression being associated with higher odds of mortality (OR 2.17; 95% CI 1.38–3.41), acute kidney injury, mechanical ventilation, and dialysis. These findings provide clinically interpretable, population-level observational comparative-effectiveness evidence for emergency management, while remaining subject to residual confounding inherent to observational data.

Our findings align with prior studies and provide more robust observational comparative-effectiveness evidence through complementary analytic approaches and bias-mitigation strategies. Borofsky et al., analyzing an earlier NIS cohort (2006–2009), reported decreased mortality with surgical decompression in septic ureteral calculi, though without propensity score methods or immortal-time bias correction [17]. We demonstrated persistence after rigorous confounding control (E-value 4.57), indicating that an unmeasured confounder would need exceptional strength to nullify the effect. The 2.4% point absolute risk reduction (one life saved per 41 decompressed patients) provides a clinically interpretable benchmark.

Biological plausibility is established: obstructive pyelonephritis creates a closed-space infection in which infected urine under pressure promotes bacteremia, endotoxin translocation, and systemic inflammation, consistent with the Sepsis-3 definition [18]. Antibiotic therapy alone cannot adequately treat this condition; urgent drainage is necessary to reduce the bacterial load, decompress the collecting system, and restore renal function. Mechanistically, decompression interrupts this cascade by relieving obstruction, reducing intrarenal pressure, facilitating bacterial clearance through restored urinary flow, enabling antibiotic tissue penetration, and preventing ischemic renal injury. Our findings are consistent with the biological rationale for urgent source control [19]. The comparative effectiveness of PCN versus retrograde ureteral stenting remains debated [20, 21]. A systematic review and meta-analysis found no significant differences in temperature normalization, white blood cell normalization, hospital stay, or procedure success among patients with severe urinary tract infections complicated by obstructive urolithiasis. A propensity-matched NIS analysis found that mortality differences disappeared after matching [22].

Importantly, modality choice is often driven by feasibility rather than preference alone. Stone location and cause of obstruction (e.g., distal ureteral stone, staghorn/large stone burden, extrinsic compression from a pelvic mass), failed retrograde access, anticoagulation, body habitus, need for prone positioning, availability of interventional radiology, and goals of care can all steer clinicians toward PCN or stenting. These factors are incompletely captured in administrative data and are plausible residual confounders in the stent-versus-PCN comparison.

Our findings diverge, showing persistent mortality signals favoring stenting after overlap weighting and IV analysis. Several factors may explain this: (1) our cohort specifically includes documented sepsis/septic shock, representing higher acuity than studies of obstructive pyelonephritis without systemic manifestations; (2) multiple causal inference strategies, including hospital preference-based IV, may better control severity-driven selection bias; and (3) crude early PCN mortality inversion (6.3% early vs. 3.1% late; stenting: 0.8% early vs. 2.9% late) suggests that the sickest patients are triaged to immediate PCN. The preferential use of PCN in critically ill patients is supported by baseline characteristics: higher transfer rates (37.4% vs. 17.5%), higher Charlson index (3.0 vs. 1.9), higher vasopressor use (11.6% vs. 5.3%), and higher mechanical ventilation (13.5% vs. 5.4%). This “confounding by indication” resists full adjustment; unmeasured severity markers—hemodynamic instability, failed stenting attempts, and anatomic barriers—likely influence modality selection. Notably, some urologists preferentially recommend PCN for critically ill patients because interventional radiologists can perform PCN under sedation, which may cause less hemodynamic compromise than ureteral stenting under general anesthesia; this clinical reasoning further supports the hypothesis that PCN recipients represent a sicker population, and this severity-driven selection effect cannot be fully adjusted away in administrative data. Although our E-value (3.52) suggests a moderately robust association, clinicians should interpret head-to-head comparisons cautiously, recognizing that PCN may mark rather than cause worse outcomes; in hospitals that preferentially use PCN, sicker case-mix may persist despite balancing, and the IV estimate reflects a LATE among compliers only.

Recent evidence demonstrated that delayed decompression beyond 48 h in obstructive acute pyelonephritis significantly increases mortality and urosepsis risk, whereas intervention within 48 h improves survival and reduces hospital stay [23]. Our findings corroborate and refine this via dose-response relationship: same-day (day 0) decompression conferred lower mortality than delayed treatment (landmark OR 0.67; 95% CI 0.48–0.94), and late intervention (> 24 h) associated with substantially worse outcomes across all endpoints [24].

Timing effect magnitudes are clinically striking. For mechanical ventilation, late versus early decompression yielded 19.6% point absolute risk increase (NNH ≈ 5)—for every five patients with delayed decompression, one additional requires mechanical ventilation. Mortality risk near-doubling (OR 2.17) underscores physiologic urgency. Prior work showed delayed operative intervention for acute nephrolithiasis increases mortality, with non-medical factors (weekend effect, race, insurance) significantly influencing timing, highlighting systemic barriers [25].

These findings parallel broader sepsis literature emphasizing time-to-treatment. Our study focuses on source control rather than antibiotics, yet the principle applies equally: patients with ureteral stones and SIRS experience rapid renal function loss and progression to urosepsis/septic shock within hours, with 2–9% mortality [26]. Decompression functions as critical source control, analogous to abscess drainage or necrotic tissue debridement; delays beyond 24 h may be associated with progression of organ dysfunction that may not be fully reversible despite subsequent decompression [27].

Equity analyses revealed troubling disparities by payer, race/ethnicity, and neighborhood income. Medicaid and self-pay patients experienced higher crude conservative-care mortality (6% and 13%, respectively); Black patients undergoing PCN had highest mortality (≈ 19%, though small cells). These gradients persisted within modality strata, suggesting multilevel disparity mechanisms: differential access to timely decompression, differential modality selection, differential post-procedural care. Such disparities pervade U.S. acute care, reflecting structural barriers including insurance gaps, geographic maldistribution of urologic expertise, implicit bias, and variable follow-up quality. Our findings underscore that optimal outcomes require not only evidence-based protocols but also systemic interventions that ensure equitable emergency urologic access.

The superiority of early intervention likely relates to the concept of a therapeutic window: once septic shock, AKI, and multi-organ dysfunction are established, decompression may alleviate the precipitating obstruction but cannot fully reverse downstream inflammatory cascades, endothelial injury, and mitochondrial dysfunction. This is consistent with our landmark analysis showing attenuation of the decompression benefit at 24 h (OR 0.91 at 24 h vs. 0.67 at baseline), suggesting that the effectiveness of decompression diminishes as irreversible organ damage accumulates.

Clinically, our findings underscore the importance of prioritizing timely decompression as source control. For patients presenting with fever, flank pain, and imaging-confirmed obstructing nephrolithiasis, prompt urologic consultation and rapid triage should be prioritized. Systems-level interventions, such as expedited imaging protocols, 24/7 urologic decompression availability, and standing order sets linking diagnosis to intervention, may reduce time-to-treatment and improve population outcomes. Regarding modality, both ureteral stenting and PCN are accepted options for urgent drainage; selection should be individualized based on anatomy, feasibility, and local expertise, and should not delay decompression. When retrograde access is feasible and can be achieved promptly, stenting is reasonable; when it is not feasible or would delay drainage, PCN is essential. Comparative mortality signals likely reflect severity confounding rather than a direct causal disadvantage of PCN [28].

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several methodologic strengths. Large, nationally representative dataset (HCUP-NIS) spanning seven years provides statistical power and U.S. hospital generalizability. Multiple complementary confounding-control and quasi-experimental strategies—PSM with excellent balance (mean SMD ≈ 0.019), OW achieving near-perfect balance (mean SMD ≈ 0.007), IV leveraging hospital-level preference variation (first-stage F = 66.85), and time-dependent survival models—each with distinct assumptions, showed concordant findings strengthening causal interpretation. Immortal-time bias addressed through landmark analyses and time-varying exposure coding. Fully scripted, reproducible analytic workflow ensures transparency, auditability, and replicability. Sensitivity analyses (E-value calculations, threshold reclassification, Holm-Bonferroni adjustments) demonstrated robustness [29].

Despite these strengths, important limitations warrant discussion. Consistent with the editor’s guidance, we interpret all estimates as associations rather than proof of causation. Although we used multiple complementary methods, unmeasured confounding and selection into timing and modality remain possible, particularly for the PCN-versus-stent comparison. As an observational study using administrative data, unmeasured confounding remains a concern. While E-value analyses suggest strong confounding needed to nullify effects, we cannot measure hemodynamic parameters, SOFA scores, lactate, pathogens, antibiotic susceptibility, or real-time clinical judgment. Residual confounding likely contributes to the PCN versus stenting comparison. NIS lacks post-discharge follow-up, limiting outcomes to in-hospital mortality/morbidity; it cannot assess readmissions, CKD progression, quality of life, or long-term mortality. Procedure timing recorded in 24-hour increments limits precision; we cannot make true ≤ 12 h inferences and therefore interpret ‘same-day (day 0)’ vs. ≥ day 1 comparisons. ICD-10 coding inaccuracies may introduce misclassification bias; in particular, the relatively low mortality in the conservative group (3.9%) may partly reflect coding limitations whereby some patients classified as “conservative” underwent decompression procedures not captured by our specific ICD-10 procedure codes or may represent patients with less severe presentations appropriately managed without intervention rather than patients for whom decompression was indicated but withheld. We could not assess procedural technical success, complications, or crossovers in detail. IV analysis relies on the exclusion restriction assumption that hospital PCN preference influences outcomes only through modality, not unmeasured hospital quality. Equity analyses revealed disparities, but cannot disentangle mechanisms; small cell sizes limit precision. As a U.S.-based study using 2016–2022 data, findings may not generalise to other healthcare systems, time periods, or populations. We could not evaluate antibiotic selection, timing, duration, fluid resuscitation, vasopressor choice, or ICU utilization—co-interventions that likely modify the effects of decompression.

Future research directions

Prospective, multicenter RCTs comparing PCN versus stenting in septic obstructing stones would provide definitive evidence; however, such trials face substantial feasibility challenges—including the clinical urgency of these cases, ethical constraints on randomization when one modality may be anatomically or hemodynamically preferred, and difficulty achieving equipoise—and may require adaptive or pragmatic trial designs.

If conducted, such trials should employ rigorous sepsis phenotyping (SOFA, lactate) and measure procedural outcomes (technical success, complications, time-to-drainage), as well as long-term follow-up of kidney function, quality of life, and mortality. Implementation science research should evaluate strategies to reduce time-to-decompression for vulnerable populations, including automated sepsis alerts linked to imaging, telemedicine urologic consultations for rural hospitals, and system redesign to ensure 24/7 emergency decompression. Precision medicine approaches using machine learning could enhance risk stratification and decision support; external validation, calibration assessment, and clinical impact trials are essential before deployment.

Mechanistic studies employing biomarkers (cytokines, cell-free DNA, NGAL, KIM-1) could illuminate pathophysiologic pathways and identify therapeutic targets. Health economics research should quantify the cost-effectiveness of early versus delayed decompression and PCN versus stenting. Qualitative research exploring clinician decision-making and institutional barriers may also yield valuable insights, although the acute and emergent nature of these presentations limits the feasibility of collecting patient-reported outcomes prospectively during the index episode. International collaborations that leverage diverse healthcare systems could enhance external validity and inform globally applicable clinical guidelines.

Conclusion

This large, nationally representative study found that urinary decompression was associated with lower in-hospital mortality in adults hospitalized with septic obstructing ureteral stones, and that earlier decompression was associated with better in-hospital outcomes. Stenting was associated with lower mortality than PCN in adjusted analyses, but this should be interpreted cautiously because residual confounding by indication/feasibility is likely. The observed association between decompression within 24 h and better outcomes, coupled with observed disparities by race, insurance, and income, underscores the need for system-level interventions ensuring timely, equitable access to emergency urologic care. These findings should inform clinical practice guidelines, quality improvement initiatives, and future research to improve outcomes for this life-threatening condition.
