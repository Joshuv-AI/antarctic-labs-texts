Multiple prognostic prediction models for subglottic laryngeal cancer based on machine learning
Authors: Chenqi Ji, Li Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSubglottic laryngeal cancer is a relatively rare subtype of laryngeal cancer, and its limited case number makes prognostic prediction challenging. This study aimed to compare multiple prognostic models for subglottic laryngeal cancer and to determine whether incorporating data from other laryngeal subsites could improve predictive performance for this rare subtype.MethodsA total of 4,701 eligible laryngeal cancer cases were extracted from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. According to tumor location, cases were classified into LarynxSubglottic (n=75), LarynxGlottic (n=2,691), LarynxSupraglottic (n=1,514), and LarynxOther (n=421). Three combined subsets were further created: subset 1 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxGlottic), subset 2 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxSupraglottic), and subset 3 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxOther). Four prognostic models were developed, including stepwise Cox regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, random survival forest (RSF), and deep survival (DeepSurv). Model performance for subglottic cases was evaluated using the concordance index (C-index) and time-dependent areas under the curve (AUCs) for 5-year and 10-year survival.ResultsFor models trained on the complete cohort, the 5-year/10-year AUCs for subglottic cases were 0.748/0.676 for stepwise Cox, 0.666/0.677 for LASSO Cox, and 0.716/0.665 for RSF; the DeepSurv model achieved a C-index of 0.690. In subset 1, the corresponding values were 0.739/0.680, 0.663/0.671, and 0.716/0.626, with a DeepSurv C-index of 0.624. In subset 2, the values were 0.679/0.620, 0.698/0.756, and 0.713/0.792, with a DeepSurv C-index of 0.701. In subset 3, the values were 0.731/0.648, 0.671/0.694, and 0.725/0.722, with a DeepSurv C-index of 0.672. Overall, subset 2 showed the most favorable performance in LASSO Cox and DeepSurv, whereas the complete cohort performed best in stepwise Cox and subset 3 performed best in RSF.ConclusionsMultiple prognostic models showed potential utility for predicting outcomes in subglottic laryngeal cancer. Incorporating supraglottic cases into the training cohort may improve prognostic prediction for this rare subtype. Among the evaluated approaches, the stepwise Cox model demonstrated the most robust and stable overall performance under the present study conditions.Keywords: Laryngeal cancer, LarynxSubglottic, survival analysis, machine learning algorithms, deep survival (DeepSurv)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Using Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) data from 4,701 patients with laryngeal cancer, we developed and compared four prognostic models for subglottic laryngeal cancer: stepwise Cox regression, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression, random survival forest (RSF), and deep survival (DeepSurv).

• To address the rarity of subglottic cases, we constructed three mixed-subtype training cohorts and found that model performance varied according to training-cohort composition.

• The complete-cohort stepwise Cox model achieved the highest 5-year area under the curve (AUC) for subglottic cases (0.748), while the subglottic + supraglottic cohort showed favorable performance in LASSO Cox and DeepSurv.

• Overall, Cox-based models showed robust and practical performance for this small-sample subgroup.

What is known and what is new?

• Subglottic laryngeal cancer is a rare anatomical subtype, and its limited sample size makes dedicated prognostic modeling difficult.

• Although machine learning methods may capture complex relationships, their value in rare laryngeal cancer subgroups remains unclear.

• This study specifically focused on prognostic prediction in subglottic laryngeal cancer and compared four survival modeling approaches across different subtype-combined training cohorts.

• Our findings suggest that adding supraglottic cases may improve prognostic prediction for subglottic disease and that conventional Cox-based models remain competitive in this setting.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• For rare cancers, a subtype-informed mixed-cohort strategy may be a practical approach to improve prognostic modeling when same-subtype data are limited.

• Training-cohort design should be considered as carefully as model selection in future prognostic studies of rare malignancies.

• Larger multicenter cohorts and external validation are needed before these models can be considered for clinical application.

Introduction

In 2020, an estimated 19.3 million new cancer cases and nearly 10 million cancer-related deaths occurred worldwide. Among these, approximately 184,615 were newly diagnosed cases of laryngeal cancer, with an age-standardized incidence rate (ASR) of 2 per 100,000 population. The number of laryngeal cancer-related deaths was around 99,840, corresponding to a global ASR of approximately 1 per 100,000 population (1-3). Although laryngeal cancer is not among the top ten most common cancers globally, it still poses a significant public health challenge, and both its incidence and mortality rates are showing an upward trend (4,5).

It is important to note that while early detection and accurate diagnosis are crucial for the effective treatment of laryngeal cancer, prognostic analysis also plays a vital role in extending patient survival (6,7). Clinically, laryngeal cancer is classified into several subtypes based on tumor location: LarynxGlottic, LarynxSubglottic, LarynxSupraglottic, and LarynxOther. Among these, LarynxSubglottic cases are relatively rare, making prognostic evaluation more challenging.

Traditionally, the Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) 6th or 7th staging systems have been used in clinical practice to provide a comprehensive understanding of long-term (over 5 years) prognosis. However, quantitatively describing and interpreting these outcomes remain difficult (8,9). The Cox proportional hazards (CoxPH) model offers a flexible and effective method for identifying associations between predictors (e.g., age, sex, race) and survival outcomes, but it is not well-suited for high-dimensional data (10,11).

Compared to the CoxPH model, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression model is better adapted to handle high-dimensional datasets; however, it still struggles to capture complex interactions among variables (12-14). Machine learning approaches and deep neural networks such as deep survival (DeepSurv) have shown promise in modeling large-scale and nonlinear data structures, but these methods often suffer from limited interpretability (14-26).

In recent years, intelligent diagnostic technologies for laryngeal cancer have developed rapidly, especially in histopathological image analysis, lesion classification, and automated tumor grading. Deep learning frameworks, including convolutional neural networks, attention-based methods, and transformer-based architectures, have shown promising performance in recognizing tumor morphology and assisting pathological diagnosis. These approaches offer strong feature extraction capability and high classification accuracy, but they are often dependent on large annotated image datasets and may have limited interpretability in clinical deployment. In contrast, traditional regression and survival-learning models based on structured clinicopathological variables are more suitable for time-to-event prediction and may be easier to integrate into routine prognostic assessment. However, such models may be less capable of capturing highly nonlinear relationships. Importantly, most previous artificial intelligence (AI) studies in laryngeal cancer focused on diagnosis, grading, or pathological image classification, whereas studies specifically addressing survival prediction for the rare subglottic subtype remain limited.

Accurately predicting the survival time of patients with subglottic laryngeal cancer (LarynxSubglottic) has become a key clinical challenge. Therefore, in this study, we used data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to select laryngeal cancer patients suitable for prognostic analysis. First, we grouped all laryngeal cancer cases into a complete cohort dataset. Based on tumor location, the cases were then divided into four datasets: LarynxSubglottic, LarynxGlottic, LarynxSupraglottic, and LarynxOther. We subsequently formed three combined subsets: subset 1 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxGlottic), subset 2 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxSupraglottic), and subset 3 (LarynxSubglottic + LarynxOther).

For each of the complete cohort dataset and the three subsets, we trained four prognostic models: stepwise Cox regression, LASSO Cox regression, random survival forest (RSF), and DeepSurv. The performance of each model was then validated using LarynxSubglottic cases from each respective set. Finally, the trained models were applied to individual patients to predict personalized prognoses, aligning the research more closely with real-world clinical applications. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1038/rc).

Methods

Data collection

In this study, data were collected from the SEER program database (SEERStat Databases: SEER November 2023 Submission). Using the SEERStat software (version 8.4.3), we retrieved records of patients diagnosed with laryngeal cancer according to the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3). The dataset covered cases processed between 2004 and 2021. Additionally, clinical information from patients diagnosed with laryngeal cancer between 2014 and 2024 was collected from Department of Otorhinolaryngology Head and Neck Surgery, Zhongshan Hospital Xiamen University for this study. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhongshan Hospital Xiamen University (ethical approval time: 31st, March, 2025, ethical code No. MR-35-25-026861). Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals included in this study.

Data cleaning

A total of 39,513 laryngeal cancer patients were initially identified from the SEER database. The following exclusion criteria were applied to clean the data:

Patients with limited follow-up information;

Patients with limited follow-up information;

Patients lacking data on American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) T 7th (AJCC T 7th), AJCC N 7th stage (AJCC N 7th), AJCC M 7th stage (AJCC M 7th), or overall AJCC stage 7th classification (AJCC stage 7th).

Patients lacking data on American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) T 7th (AJCC T 7th), AJCC N 7th stage (AJCC N 7th), AJCC M 7th stage (AJCC M 7th), or overall AJCC stage 7th classification (AJCC stage 7th).

After applying these exclusion criteria, 4,701 cases were retained for analysis. The median follow-up time for the included cases exceeded 60 months.

In addition, 132 clinical laryngeal cancer cases were collected separately. Some of these records exhibited missing or distorted data due to limitations in the clinical environment at the time of diagnosis.

Variables with incomplete observations were reviewed before model development. Missing values were handled using (median imputation for continuous variables and mode imputation for categorical variables/complete-case analysis, if applicable). Because the proportion of missingness was limited and the study sample was relatively small, a simple prespecified imputation strategy was adopted. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that more rigorous approaches such as multiple imputation may further improve robustness in future studies.

Feature selection

Based on clinical experience, we selected a total of 20 feature variables. These included variables directly related to clinical factors such as age, race, and gender. We also selected features related to tumor staging and clinical assessment, including AJCC T 7th, AJCC N 7th, AJCC M 7th, AJCC stage 7th, the third edition of the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3), collaborative stage (CS) lymph node involvement, number of regional lymph node positives, tumor size, and tumor number to evaluate the patient’s health status. Additionally, variables related to treatment modalities, such as radiotherapy, surgery, and chemotherapy, were included. Survival time and survival status were selected as the primary prognostic prediction outcomes.

Survival analysis models

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of different modeling approaches in the survival analysis of LarynxSubglottic patients, we selected four representative models for comparative analysis. These models included: stepwise Cox regression, LASSO Cox regression, RSF, and DeepSurv (a deep neural network survival model). The stepwise Cox and LASSO Cox models provide relative risk estimation through regression coefficients, while RSF and DeepSurv were mainly used as nonlinear predictive models. We acknowledge that the latter approaches are less directly interpretable than conventional Cox regression. Table 1 provides a detailed comparison of the characteristics of these four models.

Stepwise Cox regression model

This method is based on the traditional CoxPHs model and offers good interpretability. It constructs a survival prediction model by selecting significant covariates through stepwise variable selection (including forward, backward, or stepwise methods). This approach is suitable for data with a limited number of variables and where there is a linear relationship between the variables and risk. The model relies on the proportional hazards assumption.

LASSO Cox regression model

The LASSO Cox regression model introduces an L1 regularization penalty term into the CoxPHs model, which allows automatic feature selection during the modeling process and enhances the model’s stability in high-dimensional, small sample data. Although the interpretability of this model is slightly weaker than that of stepwise Cox regression, it is particularly advantageous when there is significant redundancy among the variables.

RSF

The RSF is a non-parametric survival analysis method based on the random forest algorithm. By constructing multiple survival decision trees and integrating their prediction results, RSF can capture complex nonlinear relationships between variables and survival outcomes. This model does not rely on proportional hazards or linear assumptions, making it suitable for high-dimensional, complex data. However, its predictive performance may be unstable with small sample sizes.

DeepSurv (deep neural network survival analysis model)

DeepSurv combines deep learning with the concepts of Cox regression, using deep neural networks to automatically learn the nonlinear relationship between features and survival risk. This method is particularly suited for large-scale, high-dimensional clinical or omics data, and it can improve prediction accuracy. However, due to its “black-box” structure, the model’s interpretability is weaker, and it is more dependent on sample size.

Model-related parameters were selected based on model characteristics and empirical performance within the study dataset. For stepwise Cox regression, bidirectional variable selection was guided by the Akaike information criterion. For LASSO Cox regression, the penalty parameter was selected using the default regularization path generated by the modeling algorithm, and the final model was chosen according to internal cross-validation performance. For RSF and DeepSurv, parameter settings were chosen empirically to balance predictive flexibility and overfitting risk given the limited sample size. Because an exhaustive grid search was not performed, the current parameter configuration should be regarded as exploratory rather than fully optimized.

Model training and validation

For clinical studies that prioritize model interpretability and robustness, the stepwise Cox and LASSO-Cox models are more advantageous. However, for high-dimensional, nonlinear, large-scale data, RSF and DeepSurv provide superior predictive capabilities. To better study the prognosis of LarynxSubglottic patients, all four models will be utilized.

To further assess the reliability of the models, data from non-LarynxSubglottic patients in the complete cohort dataset, subset 1, subset 2, and subset 3 were used as training sets (t), while the LarynxSubglottic patient data were used as the validation sets (v). As a result, each dataset had the same validation set but different training sets, which allows for a better comparison of the predictive performance of LarynxSubglottic patients using data from other patient subtypes and enables a more accurate prognostic analysis of LarynxSubglottic patients.

The R software package (version 4.2.3) was used to fine-tune the hyperparameters of the stepwise regression, LASSO regression, and RSF models using a grid search method. After selecting variables in the previous steps, the most favorable hyperparameters were chosen to construct the survival models. Finally, Python (version 3.11) was used to build the DeepSurv model, with additional optimization performed via grid search.

Due to the limited sample size of the LarynxSubglottic cohort, model performance was assessed using the available internal split strategy described in this study. We acknowledge that more robust internal validation approaches, such as bootstrap resampling or nested cross-validation, would provide optimism-corrected estimates and should be considered in future work.

Model evaluation and interpretation

To evaluate the predictive performance of the models on the test set, we selected the concordance index (C-index) and the area under the curve (AUC) of the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve at 5-year and 10-year time points as the main evaluation metrics. It is important to note that when the model outputs survival probabilities or risk scores, the AUC at a specific time point can reflect the model’s discriminative ability to some extent. This is similar in meaning to the C-index, but the two metrics are not completely equivalent.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using appropriate descriptive measures. Group comparisons between LarynxSubglottic and non-LarynxSubglottic cases were conducted using standard statistical methods according to variable type. Survival discrimination of the prognostic models was evaluated using the C-index and time-dependent AUCs at 5 and 10 years. Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.2.3, and DeepSurv was implemented in Python version 3.11.

Construction of the RSF model

We used a total of 1,000 trees in the model. For each tree, the number of randomly selected variables was set to the square root of the total number of variables. The minimum number of samples per terminal node was 5, and the sample size per tree was limited to no more than 70% of the full dataset, with a maximum of 50 nodes per tree. Sampling was performed with replacement.

Feature importance was calculated to identify which variables contributed most to the model’s predictive performance, allowing for reduced model complexity and improved computational efficiency. Additionally, a similarity matrix between samples was computed within the model. Dimensionality reduction techniques were applied to the similarity matrix to facilitate visualization of high-dimensional data, enabling researchers to more intuitively observe the data distribution and relationships between samples.

Construction of the DeepSurv model

The DeepSurv model does not require feature selection; therefore, all feature variables in the dataset were used during model development. The model was built with a three-layer neural network: the input layer contained 18 neurons corresponding to the input features of each sample, the hidden layer contained 32 neurons, and the output layer had 1 neuron. The DeepSurv model was trained on right-censored survival data using the negative Cox partial log-likelihood loss, consistent with the standard DeepSurv framework. Training was conducted for 500 epochs. The model output represented an individualized risk score rather than a direct survival probability.

Discussion

Early-stage laryngeal cancer is generally associated with favorable clinical outcomes, with a 5-year survival rate of approximately 70% to 90%. However, survival declines substantially once the disease progresses to an advanced stage, particularly in the presence of lymph node involvement or distant metastasis (1,2). According to anatomical location and developmental origin, laryngeal cancer can be classified into glottic, supraglottic, subglottic, and other subtypes. Prognosis is closely associated with subtype. Patients with glottic cancer often develop early symptoms, such as hoarseness, which facilitates earlier diagnosis and treatment. In contrast, supraglottic and subglottic cancers are more likely to remain clinically occult in the early stage and are therefore often diagnosed later, contributing to poorer outcomes (6).

Previous prognostic studies in laryngeal cancer have mainly focused on glottic and supraglottic tumors, and the CoxPHs model has been widely used in this field (10-13). With the development of machine learning methods, RSFs and DeepSurv have also been increasingly applied in survival analysis for head and neck malignancies, including some studies involving laryngeal cancer (14-26). In general, conventional Cox-based models offer the advantage of relatively straightforward interpretation, whereas RSF and DeepSurv may better capture complex nonlinear relationships when sufficient training data are available. However, these more flexible approaches are also more sensitive to sample size and model specification.

Because subglottic laryngeal cancer is relatively rare, prognostic studies specifically targeting this subtype remain limited (6). In the present study, we attempted to address this gap by constructing multiple prognostic models using different training datasets that combined subglottic cases with other laryngeal cancer subtypes. This strategy was motivated by the limited number of subglottic cases and by the clinical assumption that shared prognostic patterns may exist across laryngeal cancer subtypes. The results suggested that acceptable predictive performance could be achieved across the complete cohort and the three subset-based training strategies. Overall, the AUC and C-index point estimates indicated that models trained with mixed-subtype data retained potential utility for prognostic prediction in subglottic laryngeal cancer.

When comparing the four training datasets, the complete cohort showed the highest point estimate in the stepwise Cox model, subset 2 showed the highest point estimates in the LASSO Cox and DeepSurv models, and subset 3 showed the highest point estimate in the RSF model. These findings suggest that the composition of the training cohort may influence prognostic performance, possibly because different laryngeal subtypes contribute distinct clinicopathological patterns to the model-learning process. However, because confidence intervals and formal pairwise statistical comparisons were not performed, these observed differences should be interpreted cautiously as descriptive rather than inferential findings.

In the present study, the traditional stepwise Cox and LASSO Cox models showed numerically better performance than the RSF and DeepSurv models under several experimental settings. One possible explanation is the limited sample size, particularly for the subglottic cohort. Cox-based models are generally more stable in small datasets, whereas RSF and DeepSurv require more data to fully exploit their capacity to model complex nonlinear relationships. Therefore, the comparatively weaker performance of RSF and DeepSurv in this study should not be interpreted as evidence that these approaches are intrinsically inferior, but rather as an indication that their potential may not have been fully realized under the current sample conditions.

The DeepSurv architecture used in this study was intentionally kept compact in order to reduce the risk of overfitting. A relatively simple network structure was selected as a pragmatic compromise between model flexibility and stability in a small dataset. Accordingly, the chosen hidden-layer size should not be regarded as a uniquely optimal architecture. It is possible that alternative network structures or more extensive hyperparameter optimization may yield different results, and this should be explored in future studies with larger cohorts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, subglottic laryngeal cancer is a relatively rare subtype, and the limited sample size may have increased the risk of model instability and overfitting, especially for more flexible machine learning methods. Second, although internal data splitting was performed, more rigorous validation strategies, such as bootstrap-based optimism correction or nested cross-validation, were not conducted. As a result, the reported model performance may still be optimistic to some extent.

Third, confidence intervals and formal pairwise statistical comparisons among models were not performed. Therefore, differences in discrimination should be interpreted descriptively rather than as statistically confirmed superiority. Fourth, calibration analysis was not comprehensively presented, and complete supplementary model specifications were not provided. This limits a full assessment of calibration, reproducibility, and direct clinical implementation.

Fifth, complete model information for bedside risk calculation, including baseline survival estimates at fixed time points, was not fully reported in the current manuscript. Consequently, the Cox-based models are currently more interpretable than the machine learning models in practical terms. Sixth, detailed missing-data pattern analysis and more advanced imputation strategies were not performed, which may also have affected model stability.

Finally, the DeepSurv training procedure was selected pragmatically rather than through exhaustive optimization, and validation loss trajectories were not systematically reported. Therefore, the neural-network findings should be considered preliminary. External validation in larger, independent, multicenter cohorts will be essential before these models can be considered for broader clinical application.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides an initial exploration of prognostic modeling for subglottic laryngeal cancer, a relatively understudied subtype. By comparing multiple survival modeling approaches across different training cohort compositions, this study offers preliminary evidence that structured clinicopathological data may support risk stratification in this rare population. Future work should focus on larger datasets, more rigorous validation, improved model transparency, and external clinical verification.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study developed and compared four prognostic models for patients with subglottic laryngeal cancer using SEER-based clinicopathological data and different training-cohort compositions. The findings suggest that incorporating cases from other laryngeal subsites, particularly supraglottic tumors, may improve prognostic prediction for this rare subtype. Among the four approaches evaluated, the Stepwise Cox model demonstrated the most stable overall performance under the current sample conditions. These results provide preliminary evidence supporting the feasibility of survival prediction modeling for subglottic laryngeal cancer, although larger datasets and external validation are still required before clinical application.
