3D and 4D Free‐Breathing Abdominal T1‐Weighted MRI in Clinical Practice Using Deep Learning Auto‐Navigation and Reconstruction
Authors: Victor Murray, Yan Wen, Subin Erattakulangara, Oguz Akin, Richard Do, Gerald Behr, Zhigang Zhang, Arnaud Guidon, Ricardo Otazo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo develop and evaluate an automated clinical prototype for a 1‐min free‐breathing T1‐weighted 3D MRI and a 2.25‐min 4D MRI utilizing radial k‐space acquisition and deep learning (DL) auto‐navigation and reconstruction.MethodsThe clinical prototype was deployed on 3 T GE Healthcare scanners, using the GE DISCO‐Star (radial golden‐angle stack‐of‐stars) pulse sequence, DL auto‐navigation (RANGR), DL reconstruction (Movienet), and vendor‐specific image processing to efficiently generate DICOM files. The system automatically collects raw data, transmits the data to an external high‐performance computer, performs image reconstruction, and generates DICOM files. A customized version of the Movienet network was trained, using compressed sensing references, to achieve 2.25‐fold and 2‐fold acquisition acceleration for 3D and 4D MRI, respectively. The prototype was evaluated on 50 patients (ages: 8–87) with TE = 1.46–1.6 ms, TR = 3.2–3.4 ms, flip angle = 12°, in‐plane resolution = 1.17–1.64 mm, and slice thickness = 4 mm. Image quality was assessed qualitatively by three expert radiologists, who compared Movienet to conventional vendor methods, followed by a statistical analysis using Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests.ResultsThe Movienet prototype demonstrated remarkable efficiency, requiring only 90 s of GPU and 4 min on a CPU computation for 3D reconstruction, while exhibiting better performance, characterized by reduced streaking artifacts and about one‐point improvement in image quality on a five‐point scale compared to vendor technology. For 4D reconstructions, reconstruction time increased by 20 s (GPU, +1 min CPU) while maintaining comparable quality metrics. For all metrics, the differences were statistically significant (all p‐values < 0.0001).ConclusionThe Movienet prototype significantly enhances motion robustness and acquisition speed in abdominal MRI, offering a transformative approach for clinical applications.Keywords: abdominal MRI, clinical MRI translation, deep learning, dynamic MRI, fast MRI, motion, radial k‐space

Introduction

Motion remains one of the main issues in abdominal MRI, causing artifacts, scan repetitions, and prolonged acquisition times [1, 2]. Conventional approaches, such as breath‐holds and respiratory gating, offer only partial solutions that are patient‐dependent and less efficient [1, 3]. Motion correction techniques [4, 5] can use all the acquired data to combine different motion states into a single motion‐combined static image, with the caveat of registration errors. Non‐Cartesian acquisitions, such as golden‐angle radial [6], can enable free‐breathing imaging by averaging motion, and extensions, such as eXtra‐Dimensional GRASP (XD‐GRASP) [7], can further improve image quality by reconstructing different motion states at the expense of longer acquisition and reconstruction times. DL dynamic reconstruction techniques [2], such as Movienet [8], can improve acquisition and reconstruction efficiency compared to compressed sensing (CS) reconstructions [7, 9]. Moreover, deep learning can also improve auto‐navigation performance, which is another major challenge in motion‐resolved MRI. For example, RANGR (Respiratory Auto‐Navigator for Golden‐angle Radial free‐breathing abdominal MRI) [10] has emerged as a robust alternative to conventional principal component analysis (PCA)‐based auto‐navigation. The combination of RANGR and Movienet has been demonstrated to produce a fast‐motion‐robust T1‐weighted abdominal MRI system with scan times of only 1 min [11]. However, deployment and validation in a clinical setting are still needed.

DL‐based motion correction techniques, including architectures such as CINENet [12], Dracula [13], MoDL [14], and CNNs used for registration [15], markedly improve image quality and have been successfully applied not only to conventional T1‐weighted and T2‐weighted anatomical imaging but also to diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI) [16] and magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) [17]. In addition to improving image quality, DL techniques have reduced scan time, which is essential for patients who are too ill to lie in MRI scanners for extended periods or for whom breath holds are not feasible, such as frail patients or pediatric populations. Additionally, DL's ability to manage highly undersampled radial data helps eliminate specific artifacts and facilitates real‐time 4D imaging [18], providing crucial motion‐resolved data for radiotherapy planning, consistent with the initial promises of CS‐DL work [19]. Nevertheless, there are ongoing challenges and considerations. A primary concern is diagnostic confidence, for example, with methods that rely heavily on image‐domain learning [20, 21]. Some architectures were explicitly designed to address this issue by maintaining alignment with the raw data through unrolled optimization [22, 23]; however, they tend to be slower than purely image‐domain methods. Furthermore, developing and implementing these systems requires extensive, high‐quality reference datasets for supervised training, a significant hurdle. There is an unmet need to develop and implement motion‐tolerant and motion‐resolved dynamic MRI in a clinical setting.

This work presents the development and deployment of an automated prototype that integrates Movienet into clinical scanners and generates DICOM images for evaluation using a clinical visualization system. The aim is to test the combination of advanced deep learning methods that have been proven in research for auto‐navigation and reconstruction for clinical translation. The proposed work targets two types of applications. First, fast motion‐tolerant 3D MRI for diagnostic imaging purposes to improve efficiency and avoid repeated exams due to poor breath‐holds or gating. Second, dynamic motion imaging to guide radiotherapy of mobile tumors [24] or to obtain functional information related to motion, such as tissue volume per respiratory phase. The Movienet prototype was tested on 50 cancer patient exams and evaluated by three expert body radiologists.

Methods

Movienet Prototype

The Movienet clinical prototype comprises the clinical GE DISCO‐Star (Differential Subsampling with Cartesian Ordering—Stack‐of‐Stars) pulse sequence, which uses a golden‐angle stack‐of‐stars k‐space trajectory with Cartesian kz and radial ky‐kx, RANGR [9] auto‐navigation, Movienet [8, 11] dynamic reconstruction, and phased‐array uniformity enhancement (PURE) coil sensitivity correction [25], as shown in Figure 1. RANGR replaces the traditional PCA‐based navigation method used in XD‐GRASP [7] with an encoder trained on the same z‐t matrix used in PCA. RANGR has been shown to produce stable results when PCA fails, for example, with limited liver coverage or in the presence of high‐intensity regions outside the liver dome. Movienet removes aliasing artifacts from 5D aliased images (x, y, z, time, coil) obtained via non‐uniform fast Fourier transform (NUFFT) using a DL‐based dynamic 4D MRI reconstruction that leverages spatio‐temporal information. The DISCO‐STAR raw data was preprocessed on the scanner computer. This preprocessing included ARC [26] reconstruction along the kz dimension, gradient delay correction, and coil compression [27] to eight virtual coils. The k‐space dataset was then transferred to a Linux computer equipped with Intel Xeon Platinum 8380 processors and NVIDIA A‐100 GPU boards for RANGR auto‐navigation and Movienet reconstruction. RANGR estimated a 1D respiratory motion signal by generating a matrix of z‐projections over time, achieved through an inverse Fourier transform at the central k‐space position, followed by a sum‐of‐squares coil combination. The k‐space data were organized into N motion states (N = 4 for 3D MRI applications and N = 10 for 4D applications), ranging from expiration to inspiration. 3D NUFFT was then performed to obtain 5D aliased images (x, y, z, time, coil). The original Movienet network reconstruction [11] was used to remove aliasing artifacts and produce a motion‐resolved 4D output. During this work, the Movienet network was adapted and retrained to produce 320 × 320 images, ensuring alignment with the matrix size used in clinical practice and the subsequent PURE correction [25] step. Additionally, a fixed normalization of the input and reference at the 99.9th percentile was applied to eliminate potential pixel outliers. This retraining used data from 46 patients (40 prior cases [11] and 6 new cases) with a reference given by CS motion‐resolved reconstructions of data acquired using more spokes, resulting in effective acquisition acceleration. For 3D imaging, the scan times for Movienet and the CS reference were 1 min and 2.25 min, representing a 2.25‐fold acceleration. The final motion‐tolerant 3D reconstruction is given by the end‐expiration volume out of the four motion states. The 4D output is the series of 10 3D motion states from expiration to inspiration.

Movienet prototype including the DISCO‐Star sequence with 1‐min acquisition for 3D imaging and 2.25 min for 4D imaging, RANGR auto‐navigation to extract a respiratory motion signal directly from the data, Movienet network for motion‐resolved reconstruction (4 and 10 motion states for 3D and 4D imaging, respectively), PURE coil sensitivity intensity correction, and DICOM generation. GE Product Reconstruction

For comparison purposes, the same 1‐min data used for Movienet was reconstructed with the clinical GE non‐deep learning (non‐DL) product, which is based on soft gating [28]. This non‐DL reconstruction produces a 3D T1‐weighted image that uses all 1‐min acquisition spokes, weighted by their motion state.

Prototype Configuration

The implementation of the prototype on clinical scanners involves a sequential process that can be defined using three distinct layers, as shown in Figure 2:

Patient Layer: An internal script runs continually within the scanners, monitoring the clinical acquisition of patient imaging data. The script is programmed to identify specific keywords in the series names. When it detects the term “Movienet” within a series name, it triggers the scanner to preprocess the raw data and generate a TAR (Tape Archive) file. This file captures not only the k‐space data associated with the series but also copies of the relevant clinical DICOM files from the session for future comparisons.

Patient Studies

Patient imaging was performed using a retrospective study approved by the institutional review board (IRB) with a clinical sequence (DISCO‐Star, GE Healthcare) and the proposed Movienet reconstruction pipeline. 50 patients (ages 8–87 years; 31 female and 19 male) with a history of abdominal cancer were scanned on three 3 T scanners (Signa Premier and Signa PET/MR, GE Healthcare). The Movienet acquisition was performed after the standard clinical protocol using the DISCO‐Star sequence with 1794 spokes (scan time of 260–272 s) and 1496 spokes (scan time of 219–225 s). The FOV was selected to cover 2–3 cm above the liver dome for auto‐navigation purposes. Relevant acquisition parameters include TE = 1.46–1.6 ms, TR = 3.2–3.4 ms, flip angle = 12°, in‐plane resolution = 1.17–1.64 mm, and slice thickness = 4 mm. For 3D MRI, the first 400 spokes were considered, which represent a scan time of 1 min. For 4D MRI with 10 motion states, the first 900 spokes were considered, which represent a scan time of 2.25 min.

Image Quality Evaluation

A comprehensive blinded qualitative assessment was conducted involving the 50 cases, evaluated by three experienced body radiologists. One radiologist has over 20 years of experience in advanced cancer diagnosis and treatment through imaging. The second radiologist has more than 16 years of experience in body imaging, with a specialization in liver cancer, bile duct cancer, and pancreatic cancer. The third radiologist also has over 20 years of experience and specializes in pediatric patients.

The primary objective was to compare the performance of Movienet with the conventional reconstruction methods used in the non‐DL GE Healthcare product currently used for DISCO‐Star. Table 1 shows the scoring criteria [8], which were developed by the three readers using four metrics: (1) overall image quality, (2) lesion conspicuity, (3) ability to discern anatomical structure, and (4) artifacts, using a 5‐point grade, where 1 represents the lowest value and 5 the maximum. To ensure the rigor of the findings, statistical analysis was conducted using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank tests to determine whether Movienet outperforms the current standard non‐DL reconstruction approach.

Results

Patient Population

According to the specifications outlined in Subsection 2.4, the in‐scanner script was set up on three 3 T scanners (Signa Premier and Signa PET/MR, GE Healthcare). Two stages were planned during the year 2025: (1) from June 20 to July 25, which focused on testing the original implementation and tuning parameters and models (12 cases), and (2) from July 26 to November 25, which centered on a continuous image reconstruction and evaluation (38 cases).

During the first stage, it was determined that the previous version of Movienet produced images at 256 × 256 pixels, rendering the PURE correction in the final step is inconsistent. Consequently, a new Movienet network, as described in Subsection 2.1, was trained. In the second stage, automatic scans continued until November 25, 2025. The updated prototype, featuring Movienet at 320 × 320 pixels, was retrospectively applied to all scans from the first stage. Overall, 50 scans were completed and sent for radiologist clinical evaluation. Figure 3 summarizes the data collected throughout the study.

Data distribution of the 50 patients' scans: (a) ages, and (b) weight. The scans were obtained from June 20 to November 25, 2025.

Reconstruction Times

The raw data preprocessing time on the scanner was approximately 1 min. In contrast, reconstruction (which includes RANGR, Movienet, PURE, and DICOM generation) required 22–30 s on a GPU and about 4 min on a CPU (see computer details in Subsection 2.1). For 4D MRI applications, the time needed increased by 20 s (GPU, +1 min CPU). This is because the Movienet network's differences between 3D applications (which involve 4 motion states) and 4D applications (which involve 10 motion states) are limited to the first and last layers corresponding to the different motion states [8]. The non‐DL product reconstruction time is approximately 12 s, measured from the end of the scan to when all DICOM files appear. This process runs inside the scanner using optimized code, reading the raw data during the scan to save significant time.

The results presented in Table 2 highlight the significant advantages of Movienet over vendor non‐DL reconstruction methods. Movienet presented higher scores for all four metrics, and the differences in scores compared to the vendor non‐DL reconstruction are statistically significant (all p‐values < 0.0001). Even after multiple testing adjustments, all results are still significant. However, this analysis ignores reader variability and runs multiple tests. Moreover, the 1–5 rating, which is essentially an ordinal categorical outcome, is treated as a numeric variable for simplicity. Although taking the mean makes the values approximately continuous, the nature of the original measurement should be acknowledged. It would be helpful to analyze the data using a single comprehensive model that incorporates all features of the dataset. Therefore, an ordinal cumulative logit mixed model could be conducted, in which the metric and method are specified as fixed effects, their interactions are considered, and a random patient intercept is included. The reader effect is also treated as a fixed effect (since there are only three readers, a random slope by reader is not considered, as the estimates may not be stable). In other words, this model: (1) accounts for within‐patient correlation; (2) adjusts for reader variability; and (3) estimates the proposed Movienet implementation versus the non‐DL product reconstruction across different metrics, accounting for potential interactions. Thus, this model can be summarized as: (1) the main effect of the proposed Movienet implementation versus the non‐DL reconstruction is statistically significant, and it is true for all four metrics (all odds ratios are significantly higher than 1 with p‐values < 0.0001); (2) in terms of the difference between the proposed Movienet implementation versus the non‐DL reconstruction, the metric “artifacts” has a lower change than the other three metrics, being smaller than others; and (3) reader 2 tends to give somewhat higher ratings than reader 1, but reader 3 tends to give clearly higher ratings than reader 1. Overall, these results indicate that the Movienet prototype demonstrates the highest overall image quality. During evaluation of the 50 cases, 8 cases did not present lesions; thus, the metric “lesion conspicuity” was evaluated using 42 cases. The lesions presented in these cases included liver cysts, lesions, and metastases, renal cysts and lesions, and a case with peritoneal lesions.

Discussion

The prototype uses a combination of radial k‐space and deep learning‐based auto‐navigation and reconstruction methods, specifically RANGR [9] and Movienet [8], to enable motion sorting and image reconstruction with improved respiratory motion compensation in clinical abdominal MRI. For 3D MRI applications, only 4 motion states are required to achieve a motion‐robust static 3D reconstruction with only a 1‐min acquisition. In a 40‐year‐old male patient (see Figure 4 and Video S1), significant differences in the shape and position of the organs are observed between the extreme end‐inspiration and end‐expiration phases. For example, the liver vessels and kidneys demonstrate how their appearances can shift between open and closed forms (in terms of image morphology), depending on the respiratory state (as indicated by the yellow arrows). Furthermore, the importance of the PURE correction is evident. Movienet processes raw data directly, which can result in uneven intensity (highlighted by yellow arrows). However, applying the PURE correction helps to mitigate these issues. It is important to note that the prototype applies the same postprocessing correction to the Movienet output as to the non‐DL product reconstruction.

While the proposed Movienet prototype has been successfully tested in a clinical setting at a cancer hospital, several challenges remain. For instance, integrating the entire reconstruction pipeline into the clinical scanner will require strong vendor support. This work still uses a dedicated external high‐performance computer (HPC) for reconstruction, enabling general‐purpose programming languages such as Python that do not require∖figbotskip4pt working with the vendor's reconstruction code, and providing access to the higher computational power of GPUs on the external HPC. Also, since these proposed reconstructions are not used for clinical diagnosis, it is a priority to avoid any risk of altering the hospital's clinical workflow if an in‐house implementation inside the scanner is not in a final distribution and a fully tested version. Even with strong academic‐industrial partnerships, full testing and validation require additional steps, such as increasing the number of scanners and test locations, and implementing the tests locally inside the scanners. The current implementation was developed by combining components written in various programming languages and includes additional processing, such as saving files to different disks and transferring data. Future work will aim to implement the Movienet pipeline in a single, optimized, and efficient programming language, so it can use the internal power computation inside the scanner, and real‐time and parallel features, such as reading the raw data during the scan, as is done for the non‐DL product reconstruction currently (see Subsection 3.2).

A noticeable difference between the proposed Movienet prototype and the non‐DL product reconstruction is observed in Figure 5. The results from the Movienet prototype show sharper details in abdominal images, particularly in the liver and kidneys, and improved vessel visibility in the liver. While both methods capture the essential structures, the main distinction lies in the quality of the finer details. A liver metastasis with rim enhancement is better seen with the proposed Movienet reconstruction (left column, orange arrows). The major advantage of Movienet is improved visualization of small, low‐contrast features, which are degraded in non‐DL product images due to inferior motion compensation. While Movienet is a motion‐resolved reconstruction, the regular non‐DL product still combines all motion states into a single state, even though they are weighted by soft gating, leading to residual motion blurring. A similar effect is observed in a cyst (middle column, orange arrows), where the motion‐tolerant images produced by the Movienet implementations show improved detail for its detection. The improved vessel visibility in the liver is particularly evident, for example, in the vessel indicated by the green arrows in the middle column. It is important to highlight that a blurred reconstruction can obscure fine patterns, whereas the motion‐resolved reconstruction from the Movienet prototype reveals even these fine vessel details. Similar findings are observed in the case of a 15‐year‐old male patient, presented in Figure 6, where the Movienet prototype again delivers sharper images of the liver and kidneys, along with enhanced vascular visibility in the liver and parenchyma. This pediatric case highlights the significant potential of deep learning‐based systems in pediatric applications, particularly in reducing the need for anesthesia in younger patients. However, it is important to recognize that the organ structures in very young patients appear different, suggesting that retraining the Movienet model to include mostly pediatric cases would be necessary. Additionally, optimizing the RANGR algorithm to account for additional structures, such as arms in projections or even bulk motion, would be important. In the current work, the youngest case was 8 years old, which is closer to the preadolescent period.

The advancement of dynamic 4D MRI imaging presents an important opportunity for applications such as MR‐guided radiotherapy. Using 10 motion states, as supported by the relevant literature [29, 30], enables a refined analysis of patient‐specific anatomy during treatment planning [24]. Unlike previous methodologies that lacked postprocessing corrections, incorporating the PURE correction step ensures a more uniform intensity across the 4D MRI dataset. This detail is important, as even minor variations in image quality can significantly impact clinical outcomes. Minor modifications to the DL architecture result in only a marginal increase in processing time, ensuring the clinical applicability of this advanced imaging technique remains robust and efficient (see Figure 7 and Video S2). The implications of this technology extend beyond imaging enhancements; they hold promise for improving the precision of tumor localization and treatment planning in radiotherapy. By revealing apparent differences in organ shape and position across respiratory phases, continuous 4D MRI monitoring could lead to better‐targeted therapies and reduced side effects. Moreover, as discussed in pediatric cases (see Figure 8 and Video S3), where patient cooperation can be challenging, the feasibility of obtaining high‐quality images with only a minute to 2.25 min of acquisition time is particularly meaningful. This capability not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also mitigates the need for sedation or other interventions that may pose risks in younger patients [31]. The fundamentals of this prototype can also be extended to applications that require measuring and defining volumes over time, such as in lung examinations [32, 33].

The successful evaluation of the Movienet prototype in clinical studies demonstrated favorable performance compared to existing product reconstruction technologies, highlighting its potential for clinical implementation. The proposed method was tested on 50 cases and showed statistically significant improvements (p‐values < 0.0001) using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, suggesting generalizability to the presented comparison with the non‐DL product reconstruction. In this work, the focus is on presenting the results of the clinical prototype, leveraging the fact that a comparison of the Movienet results with other state‐of‐the‐art CS methods, such as XD‐GRASP, has already been published [8]. This advancement could significantly expand the use of MRI in areas heavily affected by motion, particularly in the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. There is still an opportunity to improve outcomes and achieve a comprehensive, robust free‐breathing abdominal MRI in clinical practice. For example, the methods outlined in this document are currently limited to T1‐weighted stack‐of‐stars imaging only. Future efforts will aim to adapt Movienet for T2‐weighted and DWI acquisitions to establish a fast, motion‐robust, complete abdominal MRI protocol. These sequences present new challenges directly related to acquisition time, which are reflected in motion problems and potential distortions. T2‐weighting using stack‐of‐stars would be very inefficient, since the TR would have to be long, resulting in dead time. The Propeller sequence could be a better candidate to implement a T2‐weighted version of Movienet, with the requirement to navigate the sequence and then sort the data into motion states. However, even if the current Movienet architecture does not work directly, the knowledge and contributions presented in the first publications to date can be transferred to address those problems. For example, while the current T1‐weighted Movienet implementation uses dynamic information from the combination of temporal motion states and coils, in DWI, that dynamic information can be obtained from different directions, b‐values, shots, and repetitions, thereby enabling the application of the Movienet concept. Other potential applications of Movienet include calculating fat fraction, using stack‐of‐stars acquisitions with multiple echo times. The data from each time point can be reconstructed separately using Movienet, and the end‐expiration images can be used to compute the fat‐fraction map. Also, Movienet could be potentially applicable to elastography as well, to exploit correlations between phase‐encoded images. However, the acquisition would need to be modified to have different undersampling artifacts for each time frame, which can be more challenging for EPI. Additionally, while this work compares Movienet against a non‐DL product technique using the same specific acquisition sequence (see Subsection 2.4), future work will explore the potential of using the proposed T1‐weighted Movienet images instead of the clinical standard LAVA, which uses Cartesian acquisition and is usually performed during a breath‐hold. In this case, the study will be completely blinded and randomized to compare diagnostic information, such as lesion conspicuity.

Conclusions

The development and implementation of a clinical prototype that harnesses the power of DL auto‐navigation and motion‐resolved reconstruction represents a leap forward for fast, free‐breathing abdominal MRI. The Movienet prototype enables 1‐min acquisition for motion‐compensated 3D imaging and 2.25‐min acquisition for motion‐resolved 4D imaging, with significant improvements in image quality compared to clinical standards, as shown by p‐values < 0.0001 using the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, and an automated image reconstruction pipeline for seamless implementation in clinical practice. The Movienet prototype offers a more efficient and reproducible method for abdominal MRI, promising to improve patient diagnosis and streamline clinical workflow ultimately.
