Understanding the impact of gender during gastroenterology postgraduate medical training
Authors: Amber Cintosun, Noor Jawaid, Chandni Pattni, Jordan LoMonaco, Natasha Bollegala
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLiterature from procedural specialties reveals gender differences amongst trainees regarding work-life balance, career goals, and workplace discrimination.PurposeTo determine the impact of gender on gastroenterology resident experiences.MethodsA 70-question cross-sectional survey was electronically disseminated to gastroenterology trainees across Canada. Survey questions addressed personal, professional, financial, and training characteristics. Comparisons were made between self-identified men and women. Wilcoxon test was used for continuous variables. Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were applied for categorical variables. Qualitative thematic analysis was applied for short answer responses with 2 independent reviewers.ResultsForty-four trainees were surveyed, with 41% women. At work, women were more likely to have their clinical competency challenged than men (35% vs 8%, P = .024) and report difficult relationships with support staff (35% vs 0%, P = .001). Most trainees planned to complete further gastroenterology training (86%), but males were more likely to choose therapeutics (36% vs 0%, P = .033). Women more commonly were the predominant caregiver for children (67% vs 8%, P = .012) and felt that having children would slow their career advancement (77% vs 33%, P = .046). Men were more likely to prefer having children during residency (50% vs 7%, P = .036). No statistical difference was found between genders regarding desire for advanced subspecialty training, leadership aspirations, or academic pursuits.ConclusionGender-related differences exist amongst Canadian gastroenterology trainees regarding work-life balance and career advancement. Women may benefit from supports to allow them to meet career goals which are similar to their male peers.

Introduction

Gender disparities amongst practicing gastroenterologists are an ongoing concern. These disparities are pervasive, affecting clinical practice, remuneration, and personal life even 10 years into practice.1 The overall trajectory and satisfaction in a gastroenterology (GI) career has also been shown to be impacted by gendered experiences, with women being more likely to report lower career satisfaction. Women also felt that their gender negatively affected their career advancement and led to greater difficulty in achieving promotion and tenure in academia.2 The Canadian context is similar, with female gastroenterologists reporting underrepresentation in leadership, unequal pay, and challenging work-life balance due to gender-related barriers.3,4

What is less clear is what perceived gender disparities exist in the career pathways of gastroenterologists and how this affects the lived experience of GI trainees. The surgical literature has shown similar trends of gender disparities for women including lack of mentorship, difficulty balancing family obligations, and disparities in promotion.5 During training, female surgical residents experienced more pregnancy complications, maternity leave-associated stigma, and overall professional dissatisfaction.6,7 Female GI trainees were less likely to have children during training and more likely to alter their family planning because of program restrictions. Females also experienced more gender discrimination and sexual harassment than their male peers.8 The paucity of data on GI trainees’ gender-based experiences in the last 20 years, especially within Canada, leaves an untouched area worth exploring further.

The aim of this study is to determine the impact of gender on adult GI-related career preferences and experiences during residency and fellowship in Canada.

Methods

Survey

A cross-sectional, 70-question survey was electronically disseminated to postgraduate year (PGY) 4-6 adult GI residents and fellows across Canada. The survey was administered through SurveyMonkey and distributed by email through each GI program director’s office. A second reminder email was sent to the same population 2 weeks later. SurveyMonkey’s software prevented multiple participation and ensured survey completion and submission in order to be included in the analysis. Respondents were guided to skip certain questions when not relevant (eg, “Skip this question if you do not have a mentor.”). Participants could not skip a question that did not have this option listed without resulting in an incomplete survey. No personal identifiers were documented and results were password-protected. Nonresponse error was addressed by recirculating the survey.

Formulation of the survey content was based on a literature review and focused on barriers to career advancement for physicians in general as well as factors previously reported to affect gender disparities at the staff level (eg, lack of mentorship, difficulty balancing family obligations, gender discrimination). The survey consisted of 8 groups of questions aimed at understanding current and future personal, professional, financial, and training characteristics (see Supplementary Material). Survey question topics were developed to (1) understand the demographic, social, and financial backgrounds that may be impacting participants’ training experiences; (2) describe trainees’ lived experiences during GI training; (3) explore career aspirations; and (4) understand barriers to achieving those aspirations.

The questions were original and created based on guidelines for graduate medical education research survey design.9 The original questions were pretested for feedback among the co-authors (A.C., N.J., C.P.) who were trainees to test for accurate interpretation. Multiple choice options were provided for closed-ended questions with the option to add “other” information if necessary to minimize item nonresponse. Open-ended qualitative questions were included to more deeply understand the respondent’s unique experiences.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons were made between self-identified male and female participants. Wilcoxon (Mann-Whitney U test) was used for continuous variables. Chi-squared test and Fisher’s exact test were applied for categorical variables using SAS software.

Qualitative thematic analysis was applied for short answer responses with 2 independent reviewers (N.J. and C.P., see Supplementary Material for positionality statements).10 This process involved familiarization of short answer responses to elicit recurring themes for each question set. These were then used to create a thematic framework to apply the cutting and sorting technique of specific response quotations. If significant differences between the 2 independent reviewers arose, consensus was reached by group approval including the lead physician investigator (N.B.). This method allowed for thematic saturation to be achieved.

Research ethics

This study was formally reviewed by institutional authorities at Women’s College Hospital, University of Toronto and was deemed not to require full research ethics board approval. Informed consent was considered implicit with completion and submission of the survey. No identifying information was obtained.

Results

Demographics

In total, 122 residents were approached for inclusion in the study between May and June 2020 with a response rate of 36% (44/122; Table 1); 41% were female. There were 17 PGY4s, 15 PGY5s, and 12 PGY6s with similar representation by gender for each training level. The average age (±SD) was similar between males and females (34 ± 5.6 vs 31 ± 2.9 years). The majority was completing GI training in Ontario (64%). All 6 provinces with GI training programs within Canada were represented: 42% of men and 28% of women had completed additional training such as a Masters or Diploma program; 92% men and 72% of women were in a relationship or married. The majority did not have children (57%).

GI training

Participants’ reasons for choosing GI included clinical area of interest (88%), procedures (78%), and patient population (63%). The least common reasons were work hours (13%) and prestige (13%). The top barriers to advancing through a GI career were maintaining work-life balance (63%), job availability after training (60%), and negative impact on childbearing and rearing (32%). These did not significantly differ by gender.

Most trainees planned on completing advanced GI subspecialty training (86%; Table 2). Hepatology was the most popular subspecialty choice (31%). Men were more likely to want therapeutics as a specialty (37% vs 0%, P = .033). The most common reasons for choosing an advanced subspecialty fellowship were for clinical interest and improving job prospects. Barriers to pursuing further advanced GI subspecialty training included competitiveness and family constraints.

Mentorship

Eighty-five percent of male and 61% of female participants had mentors during GI training. Although most trainees had both male and female mentors, women were less likely to have male mentors than men (35% vs 65%, P = .042; Table 3). Mentor acquisition occurred via informal means (56%), formal assignment (31%), recommendation (9%), or by choosing from a provided pool (4%). Most participants had a faculty mentor (72%) and 16% had a resident mentor. The focus of mentorship varied widely including clinical (26%), subspecialty (25%), informal (25%), and research (23%).

Training experiences

When asked whether trainees perceived that they had different expectations placed on them during training due to intrinsic factors regarding who they are such as marital status, gender, etc, most said no (65%). For those who answered yes, perceived family obligations were the most common reason (46%). Qualitative analysis showed parent trainees appreciated tempered expectations and reduced pressure due to child-rearing responsibilities. Conversely, trainees without children felt that they were being treated unfairly working more hours and holidays: “Being a male with no dependents likely resulted in an expectation of doing more work and not complaining.”

Challenging relationships with senior colleagues were experienced by the minority (14%) and non-Caucasian ethnicity was perceived to be the most common reason (36%). Although the minority experienced challenging relationships with support staff (14%), women were more likely to have experienced this (35% vs 0%, P = .001) and to feel that that gender was the main reason (48% vs 0%, P < .001). Qualitative analysis showed multiple responses related to male trainees being treated better than female: “As a woman, I find that especially endo[scopy] nurses … will ask me to do tasks that the[y] would not ask my male colleagues and call me Dr [first name] and not Dr [last name] like my colleagues. Sometimes they just address me as my first name. In addition, if I … [try] to book [a procedure] they will give me a harder time than my male colleagues.”

A similar trend was seen in trainees having their clinical competency challenged. Most had no issues (81%), but women were more likely to perceive having their competency challenged than men (35% vs 8%, P = .024) and to feel that their gender was the reason (37% vs 0%, P = .007).

Family life

Most participants stated their partner’s preference (71%) and occupation (70%) played a role in their career choices. Men more often reported that their partner’s preference mattered in their choices (42% vs 11%, P = .009). Qualitative analysis brought forward 2 main themes: location and physician-spouses. Trainees did not wish to uproot their families or become long-distance to complete training. There was also a large proportion of dual-physician couples (35%) which brought further limitations by having to compromise if 1 partner was in a more competitive specialty.

Most trainees felt that they had or would have to compromise their career for marriage (55%). Qualitative analysis of 23 comments showed the major reason to be location again followed by difficulties with work-life balance, childcare, and needing a supportive spouse. Disruption to family life was evident in an analysis of 8 comments due to the time commitment a GI career requires: “Call, weekend obligations, etc have placed more stress/responsibility on my wife.”

Half of respondents (50%) had their first child during residency, but only 29% felt that residency was the best time to have children. Men were statistically more likely to state a preference for having children in residency than women (50% vs 7%, P = .036). Women were statistically more likely to be the predominant caregiver to their children (67% vs 8%, P = .004). Likewise, men were significantly more likely to state their spouse took on the predominant care of their children (66% vs 0%, P = .012; Table 4). Most trainees felt that their training was disrupted by children (61%). Forty-four percent felt that children slowed their career advancement compared to enhancing (22%), significantly slowing (6%), or no effect (28%).

For trainees who did not have children, 72% planned to have children in the future. In this group, half (53%) felt that having children would slow down their career advancement and women were more likely to feel this way (77% vs 33%, P = .046). Qualitative analysis of 7 comments showed that issues around children and maternity leave were paramount to trainees: “Children will delay my plans to pursue GI subspecialty training. I also worry that it will interfere with getting a job if I am visibly pregnant at the time I am job hunting.”

Remuneration

Most respondents earned between $50 000 and 100 000 (44%) or $100 000 and 200 000 (47%). Personal and family incomes did not significantly differ by gender. Desired future personal income was most commonly between $400 000 and $500 000 (26%), closely followed by 21% at $300 000-$400 000 and 18% at $500 000-$600 000. Desired personal and family income did not significantly differ by gender.

Future career

Most survey participants planned to practise full time (93%) in Ontario (55%) in an urban setting (64%). Most aimed for a subspecialty-focused (69%) and academic practice (62%). The approach on how to acquire a job varied considerably, with applying for nonadvertised positions being most common at 63%, followed by applying for advertised positions such as on the Canadian Association of Gastroenterology website at 47% (15/32), and cold calling or emailing institutions at 44% (14/32). There was no gender difference in this respect. Qualitative analysis of 20 comments showed that job availability was the main concern for a future career in GI.

Beyond their clinical practices, 60% of trainees overall and 53% of female trainees aimed to hold an administrative or leadership position. The most popular leadership positions desired were within a clinical program (44%) or hospital administration (28%). Most trainees were either currently involved or planning to be involved in academic pursuits such as research (65%). There were no gender differences in leadership aspirations or academic pursuits. A minority of trainees felt barriers existed to following academic pursuits (41%). Among these, the most common reason was family obligations (50%). Qualitative analysis centred on work-life balance as a barrier to academic pursuits but there were also mentions of noninclusivity within academia: “Publishing with peer review in a field still dominated by male figures”; “I do qualitative research which isn’t taken as seriously in the academic sphere. I don’t have as many mentors to choose from as my colleagues who do more “real” research do.”

Discussion

This is the first comprehensive study focused on the gender-related experiences of Canadian trainees during GI fellowship. Despite an increase in the number of women graduating from medical school and internal medicine, GI continues to have poorer gender representation compared to other specialties.11 Gender-based differences in career experiences do exist amongst GI trainees. These perceived differences were evident in the following major areas: gender discrimination in the workplace, mentorship, work-life balance, career planning, and leadership. The qualitative results provided a more in-depth understanding of GI trainees’ lived experiences regarding barriers to a successful career in GI and potential foci for improvement.

Gender discrimination in the workplace

Sexism in the medical workforce is prevalent. In a review of 6237 trainees, 53.6% reported gender discrimination, and this disproportionately affected women. The most common sources of discrimination were consultants, patients, and nurses.12 In our study, female fellows more often had their clinical competency challenged and faced challenging relationships with hospital staff. The literature illustrates that nurses may treat female and male physicians differently—deferring more to men, while being more direct or even hostile with female physicians who they see more as equals, especially regarding medical decision-making.13 Gender discrimination in medicine leads to barriers to promotion, unequal salaries, and burnout.2-4 Potential strategies to mitigate gender bias at the GI training program level include implicit bias training for healthcare personnel; policies on equitable and transparent processes in hiring, promotion, and remuneration; sponsoring female leadership; and empowering trainees to report discriminatory encounters.

Mentorship

Mentorship is necessary for GI career advancement and satisfaction,14 especially for underrepresented groups such as women. Targeted mentorship programs can help promote and retain women in academic medicine.15 We found that almost a quarter (23%) of trainees lacked a mentor altogether, most of whom were women although this was statistically nonsignificant. Women were less likely to have male mentors. Another GI study supported our findings, showing that women have more difficulty finding mentors and fewer women consider themselves to be effective mentors, possibly adding to the gap.16 Most trainees in our study found their mentors through informal means, leaving the onus on the trainee. This is an opportunity for GI training programs to create more formalized and equitable mentoring programs. GI programs can survey their incoming trainees and pair them with a senior fellow for less formal guidance and a faculty member who shares their career interests.17

Work-life balance

Balancing career and personal life is a constant battle within medicine, especially in GI where call requirements often endure throughout one’s career. These pressures can impact life decisions such as marriage and childbirth.18 In our study, 50% of trainees with children had their first child during residency. Men more often preferred to have children during residency, while women more often felt that having children would slow their career advancement. Women were predominantly the primary caregivers, even though 35% of married respondents in our study were in a dual-physician marriage. The disproportionate burden of family obligations shouldered by female trainees is well-documented.19 Previous studies of dual physician couples show that the male partner’s career took priority, leading the female physician to reduce work hours for family obligations.20,21 Although parental leave is mandated by Canadian law,22 GI training programs should actively support a positive culture around pregnancy and parental leave.

Subspecialty choices

Most respondents planned to or were already pursuing advanced GI training. Men were more likely to pursue therapeutic endoscopy, which in line with previous research.23,24 Women noted potential reasons including childcare obligations, lack of self-advocacy to seize opportunities to learn advanced therapeutic techniques, and a discouraging work culture.25 Our study found that women more often chose their subspecialty based on patient population (60% vs 25%, P = .045), which may reflect a greater focus on face-to-face care over procedures. This aligns with the literature showing that female physicians spend more time with patients doing nonprocedural activities.26 Trainees should be exposed to the noninterventional and procedural sides of GI with equal enthusiasm, while encouraging equitable opportunities to learn advanced endoscopy skills.

Leadership aspirations

In our survey, 60% of trainees and 53% of female trainees aimed to hold a leadership position, representing similar goals across genders. However, women face challenges such as the “glass ceiling” theory of invisible barriers to advancement as well as the “leaky pipeline” theory where there is a loss of women faculty along the path to advancement.27,28 Female leadership in GI remains limited within hospitals, academia, and professional associations.29,30 The literature provides ample analyses highlighting mentorship, sponsorship, networking, and research productivity as key elements to leadership success.14,31 GI training programs can support their trainees through formal mentorship programs, equitable sponsorship of trainees for career advancement, structured networking opportunities, and protected research time.

Limitations

Our study had a small sample size and response rate given the small population of Canadian GI trainees and voluntary nature of survey studies. There was representation of both genders across all 6 Canadian provinces with GI training programs. However, most of the respondents were from Ontario where there are the highest number of Canadian GI trainees, which may affect generalizability to other provinces. The survey was also quite long with 70 questions which may have precluded some participants from completing the survey, especially during busy GI training schedules. This may have led to a reduced sample size. Creating a modified, shortened survey may promote a higher response rate among trainees.

Additionally, free answer responses were subjective. However, our aim was to understand trainees’ perceptions and lived experiences. Another limitation is that some questions also required commitment to a “yes” or “no” without the option to select “unsure.” The option was provided for these questions to “elaborate further” in the participant’s own words.

Next, there were no prior validated questionnaires available in our Canadian GI trainee population addressing our specific study topics, thus questions were developed de novo. Pilot testing was not performed given the small population of GI trainees, confidentiality concerns, and the potential for introducing bias within the small sample. More rigorous pretesting of survey components by participants from multiple training centres may strengthen the rigor of the survey questions. Providing multiple pilot surveys of shorter durations may make the pretesting process more feasible. This limitation was partially mitigated by involving Canadian trainees in the study design process.

Conclusion

This study is the first to focus on GI residents’ and fellows’ lived experiences within Canadian training programs. Trainees do perceive gender-related differences regarding work-life balance and career advancement. Gastroenterology training programs can help mitigate gender disparities by providing equitable opportunities for mentorship, exposure to subspecialties, and career guidance. Gastroenterology residency training programs should also aim to foster a supportive environment to achieve work-life balance and deter gender discrimination.
