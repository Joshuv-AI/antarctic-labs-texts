Potential of patient portals to enhance adolescent asthma care engagement
Authors: Emma J Clark, Whitney Chan, Emily Gao, Ilana Radparvar, Rong Guo, Ana Gomez, Nashica Hamm, Dariush Kafashzadeh, Gery W Ryan, Mindy K Ross
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceElectronic health record-based patient portals hold promise for health care transition (HCT), particularly for chronic conditions like asthma.ObjectiveTo understand the potential of a portal-based asthma care management module in adolescent patients with asthma and evaluate its performance and usability.MethodsWe completed semi-structured interviews to determine adolescent perceptions of a portal-based asthma care module and its potential for HCT. We prototype tested a portal-based asthma management module with adolescent patients (12–17 years old) with asthma for their upcoming pediatric pulmonary clinic visits and measured its performance and usability. We reported descriptive statistics for survey data, and content analysis for interviews.ResultsSixteen adolescents completed interviews. Most interviewees favored the idea of using a module before visits and believed it could help them with HCT. We identified five themes: adolescent familiarity with the patient portal, perceived benefits, perceived barriers, suggestions for usability, and perspectives on a portal module’s potential role in supporting their asthma and HCT readiness for asthma management. Eleven adolescents tested the module. The percentage of asthma data captured from the physician visit notes with the module was higher compared to notes without (93.1 % [95 % CI: 90.3, 95.3] vs. 74.6 % [95 % CI: 71.0,77.6], p < 0.005). The average System Usability Scale score of the module was 71 ± 9.6 (considered above average).ConclusionsOur findings support the potential of portal-based based management modules to be accepted by adolescents with asthma and to engage adolescents more in their own care. We will continue to iterate the module for this population.Keywords: Health care transition (HCT), Adolescent asthma, Patient portal

Introduction

Asthma is one of the most common chronic conditions of childhood. For those with asthma, the adolescent and young adult (AYA) period presents the highest risk of asthma exacerbations.1, 2 There are several factors that contribute to this risk, including suboptimal preparation for health care transition (HCT) from pediatric to adult care for asthma.3, 4 Despite the rising rates of pediatric onset of chronic conditions in the U.S., including asthma,5 the rates of HCT preparation for adolescents with asthma is approximately 19 %,6 and in general for adolescents with and without chronic health care conditions rates are estimated to be less than 25 %.7, 8, 9, 10 Therefore, it is imperative to address HCT preparation in patients with asthma to improve outcomes. Rates of HCT can increase through adolescent self-efficacy, involvement, and knowledge about their own care.1, 2, 11, 12

To address this need to increase rates of HCT preparedness among AYAs with asthma, the European Academy of Allergy and Clinical Immunology (EAACI) published guideline recommendations focused on HCT for AYAs with asthma and allergies.13 Their guidance highlighted technology as a high priority area to conduct further research to advance asthma HCT management. While there are many potential digital approaches to study, we consider the electronic patient portal to hold the most potential for HCT because of the portal’s many functions that facilitate direct, active involvement in one’s own care (e.g., provider communication, medication management, appointment making, etc.). The portal also involves all relevant healthcare management parties including the patient, primary caregiver (henceforth, “parent”) and healthcare practitioner. Because patient portals have demonstrated improvement in general outcomes for various pediatric chronic conditions including asthma,14, 15, 16, 17 they are also starting to be explored for HCT in children with chronic medical conditions.18, 19 Yet to our knowledge, portal-based tools in the EHR have not been studied specifically for HCT of adolescents with asthma.

In addition, despite the clinical benefit of technologic tools for care management in controlled settings, one challenge has been attaining widespread adoption of these tools.15, 20 To address this, our group is working toward adapting a module for pediatric patients with asthma through a user-experience (UX) design-informed approach to increase usability and adoption of technological tools for asthma management.21, 22, 23 We previously developed a general portal-based asthma management module for parents of children under 12-years-old with asthma, which had high usability scores.24 In this study, we interviewed adolescents with asthma with the goal of understanding their perceptions of using a patient portal for asthma and HCT management. We also piloted the portal module among adolescents with asthma to obtain usability feedback as we continue to adjust the module for AYAs.

Materials and methods

Participants and setting

Starting in April 2023, we conducted both the interviews (completed in September) and prototype testing (completed in July) with adolescent patients ages 12–17 years old with an asthma diagnosis (based on ICD code and patient confirmation) who had an upcoming outpatient pediatric pulmonary clinic visit at the University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) Health System. The payor mix in these clinics is ∼85 % private or employer-issued insurance. The EHR is Epic Systems® with an integrated patient portal, MyChart.®

Enrollment in both components was based on a convenience sample of adolescent patients with upcoming visits for asthma who met eligibility criteria. To be included in the study, participants were not required to have prior portal knowledge or experience, however, if prototype participants did not already have a portal account, they were required to activate the portal. In addition, they needed to be proficient in English because the EHR and portal were only patient-facing in English at the time of the study. Participants could not be neurocognitively impaired to a degree that would interfere with their caregiver’s expectation of independent management of their health care in the future. We stratified the enrolled adolescents by age (12–13, 14–15, 16–17 years); and we continued enrollment until we reached thematic saturation of feedback.25 Participants could enroll in both components of the study. Prototype participants received $25 gift cards and interviewees received $50 gift cards for their participation.

The asthma management module

Our previously developed asthma management module for parents of children with asthma was based on our paper intake forms, which are designed at a fifth-grade reading level. In addition, the wording is already designed for patients or parents to complete (e.g., my/my child’s asthma), so we were readily able to apply this module in the adolescent population.

The purpose of this previously developed asthma management module was to more actively engage parents in their child’s care as well as help physicians be more involved in the child’s care by spending less visit time data gathering and more time focusing on the clinical management.24 This module includes a pre-visit portal-based intake questionnaire, a physician note template that automatically captures the participant’s portal response data, a physician-facing summary of relevant asthma data to help inform clinical decisions, an asthma order set, and an asthma action plan module.24 The pre-visit intake questionnaire is guideline-based26, 27, 28 and was designed to collect clinically relevant information about the patient’s asthma prior to the visit. For new patients, the questionnaire obtains the patient’s reason for visit, asthma history, co-morbidities, environmental exposures, adherence, symptoms (impairment and risk), and barriers to care. The return patient questionnaire is shorter and focused on the patient’s reason for visit, symptoms (impairment and risk), adherence, and barriers to care. Impairment, risk, and adherence data was captured by the Pediatric Asthma Control and Communication Instrument (PACCI). The PACCI is a 12-item validated, cross-cultural questionnaire to measure asthma control in children.29 It has concurrent validity with other common tools to measure asthma impairment, yet is more robust in that it measures adherence and risk of asthma exacerbation.

Data collection

Interviews

Our masters-level qualitative research coordinator (EC) and undergraduate research assistant (WC) conducted semi-structured interviews. Our interview guide was adapted to the adolescent population from our previously published interview guide for parents of children with asthma.21 The guide questions focused on six areas including adolescent participants’ experience with their asthma care (e.g., physician, family involvement), health information gathering (e.g. questionnaires, portals), opinions about the concept of the portal and its use to collect information related to asthma, facilitators and barriers of the portal for asthma management, the potential role of the portal asthma management module in their HCT, and an opportunity to share any information that was not covered in the interview. See Supplemental Table 1 for the full interview guide.

Interviews were conducted via Zoom with individual participants and scheduled for an hour. Interviews were conducted without verbalizing identifying information and were audio-recorded. The interviews were led by EC who also took notes. They were transcribed by a HIPAA-compliant secure translation service, Datagain Inc. The transcripts were manually reviewed to ensure no personal identifying information was included. Interviewees were assigned electronic surveys to collect demographics data via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) hosted by UCLA.30

Prototype testing

Once participants enrolled, they were manually assigned the pre-visit intake questionnaire to complete via the patient portal before an upcoming visit. Our research coordinator provided instruction on how to navigate within the portal to find the assigned questionnaire. After the visit, participants were assigned electronic surveys via REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture [REDCap] hosted by UCLA)30 to collect feedback about usability and other characteristics. Usability was measured through the System Usability Scale (SUS), a 10-question validated survey about new technologic tools.31 The SUS survey includes 10 Likert Scale questions (1 =strongly disagree; 5 =strongly agree) which comprise a total score. Based on the literature, an average score is considered 68 and scores above 78 are considered “excellent.”31, 32 We also included open-ended questions to obtain participant perspective of the module through questions of, “What was easy?” “What was hard?” and “What would you change?” about both the portal questionnaire and process system.33

The other characteristics surrounding asthma management collected were adherence, and transition readiness. Adherence was measured using the Medication Adherence Report Scale for Asthma [MARS-A], which is a 10-question, validated self-report instrument to assess adherence to daily controller medications (inhaled corticosteroids, typically).34 Adolescent transition readiness was measured using the Transition Readiness Assessment Questionnaire (TRAQ).35, 36 The TRAQ has evolved over time and we used the most recent version (TRAQ 6.0), a 20-question 5-point Likert type scale with four main categories of managing medication, appointment keeping, tracking health issues, and talking with providers.37

For both components of this study, we obtained informed assent from the adolescents, and permission from one of their parents for this UCLA IRB approved study (IRB #20–000347).

Data analysis

Interviews

Interview transcripts were uploaded to Dedoose 9.0.62 for data management and analyzed for themes and sub-themes using thematic analysis.25 A codebook, including both inductive and deductive codes, was iteratively revised by the research team [MR, EC, WC, EG, IR] by independently reading and coding transcripts, reconciling the coded transcripts with the team, and refining the codebook, Supplemental Table 2. This process was repeated until code saturation was reached.38 Using the established codebook, EC, WC, EG and IR coded transcripts. All transcripts were double coded. Finally, the research team analyzed the coded data by writing analytic memos to describe observations for each code (or group of codes). These memos were used to identify connections between codes and major themes. While our interview encompassed the entire adolescent experience surrounding their asthma management, we focused our reporting on themes that elucidated our research goal of understanding adolescent perspectives surrounding use of the patient portal for their asthma and HCT management. We presented our data from the semi-structured interviews in a standard fashion typical of qualitative research in exploratory stages, using a 5-point ordinal scale (none, few, many, most, and all). We used the ordinal approach because while interviewees are asked the same questions based on the interview guide, the exact wording and responses vary so we use ordinal scales to convey the prevalence of responses in datasets such as this, rather than percentages which give a false sense of precision.38, 39 In addition, as guided by the authors’ informed judgment, we integrated illustrative quotes to convey authenticity and insights into the participant lived experience.39, 40

Prototype testing

We assessed the module’s ability to capture key asthma management data elements. Similar to our previous work, we manually reviewed the most recent visit before (if a return visit) or after (if a new visit) the intervention and documented the presence of 13 pertinent elements of asthma management: triggers, symptoms, severity classification, risk, relevant labs/imaging, missed school or workdays, medication, control classification, co-morbidities, barriers to care, asthma goals, asthma action plan, and adherence.24 We used Wilcoxon signed rank testing to evaluate if there was a significant difference in the presence of pertinent asthma data with or without the module. Our team used SAS Version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC) for statistical analysis.

Demographics, SUS scores, and characteristics (MARS-A and TRAQ scores) were reported using descriptive statistics. We used the constant comparative method to identify themes from the open-ended questions about the module to understand facilitators and barriers to adoption.41 We ran a Spearman rank-order correlation to determine the relationship between adolescent age with the SUS, TRAQ, and MARS-A scores.

Results

Interviews

Sixteen adolescents were interviewed. Thirty-one percent were 12–13-year-olds, 38 % were 14–15-year-olds, and 31 % were 16–17-year-olds. Through our thematic analysis of the interviews, we identified five overarching themes related to perceptions of using an asthma management module: 1) adolescent familiarity with and access to the patient portal, 2) perceived benefits, such as improved efficiency and communication with practitioners, 3) perceived barriers, including confusing language and technological limitations, 4) suggestions to improve the usability of a module such as adding text-based reminders, and 5) perspectives on the portal module’s potential role in supporting their health care transition readiness for asthma management. These themes are described in more detail below.

Portal familiarity

Most interviewees had familiarity with the concept of health intake questionnaires in general and reported that their parents were usually the ones to complete these on their behalf. In regard to the patient portal, most interviewees were also familiar but there was variation in how they managed their portal account. For example, “my parent usually uses [the portal], but occasionally I check [it]. If my parents say [I] have an appointment scheduled, I go check it really quick (14–15 year-old). Most interviewees noted they shared usage of the portal with their parents, with the rest divided between having their parent(s) manage their portal account entirely for them or managing the portal entirely by themselves. Even interviewees who reported that they managed their account independently noted that they still shared their login information with their parents in case their parent needed to access it.

There was no apparent trend between patient age and how they managed their portal account. An interviewee noted that even though they were not familiar with how to navigate their portal account, they were able to gain confidence and become more independent with it over time: “My dad set [my portal account] up because I'm not very tech savvy when it comes to those things but I figured out how to use it eventually” (12–13 year-old). When asked about what they considered to be beneficial about the portal in general, most interviewees noted that being able to communicate with their health practitioners electronically was the greatest benefit.

Perceived benefits of the module

When queried about the concept of the asthma portal module, interviewees mostly had overall positive attitudes and stated they would be open to using a portal module if offered. A primary benefit noted about the module was the perception that completing the form ahead of time could save them time during their visits because “you don't have to waste time out of your appointment to fill out a paper form when you can fill it out online” (12–13 year-old) so patients could spend more of the visit focused on discussing asthma concerns and asking follow-up questions. It was also noted that “[the portal module] could be of value, because not only would it save time for me, but it would save time on the appointment overall” (14–15 year-old). In addition, interviewees noted that completing the questionnaire via the patient portal could help doctors be more prepared for their patients’ visits if their doctor reviewed the information before the visit because the doctor would already know their patients’ answers ahead of time and “give them an idea of what symptoms I have without the appointment even starting” (14–15 year-old).

Many interviewees also perceived that the module would allow them more flexibility to complete the questionnaire on their own schedule, rather than being time-constrained in their doctor’s office. A few interviewees reported that they have felt stressed by intake forms in the office or answering questions on the spot. They conveyed that using the portal module in their home environment would allow them to work collaboratively with their parents if needed to provide a proper amount of detail, as well as think more about any follow up questions they might have. They imagined the module would alleviate these stressors and ultimately collect better data because “when you're filling out a questionnaire in the safety of your own home you're free to ask your parents, ‘hey, do I do this sometimes?’ instead of shaking in your boots [in the waiting room or in front of the doctor] because you're like, ‘oh my God, what do I say?’” (12–13 year-old).

Another benefit the interviewees imagined was that the module could allow adolescents to feel more comfortable because they could alert their healthcare practitioner to issues ahead of the visit they would like to discuss during without their parents in the room: “…so you don't have to make it weird, like you want your parent to go out, but then [the doctor] knows [you're] trying to talk about something [privately]” (16–17 year-old).

Interviewees who were familiar with the portal conveyed that the module could ease asthma management since all their care would be centralized in the portal. Regarding the specific asthma intake questionnaires, many interviewees perceived that the questions relating to medical history, asthma symptoms and medication as important. They noted, for example, that the portal questionnaire could serve as a useful reminder for adolescents about their own asthma management before their visit: “Doing [the questionnaire] online is a lot easier. I would know in advance about all this medical stuff I need to know for the appointment” (12–13 year-old).

Perceived barriers of the module

When asked about willingness to adopt a portal module in practice, all interviewees were affirmative except for one who noted “maybe, but I think I would just let my mom use it” (14–15 year-old). Most interviewees mentioned a potential barrier was that they are less familiar with their medical care compared with their parents. And even if they might be familiar, they defer to their parent(s) to manage their care. For example: “Well, I can take care of myself. It’s just easier to have my mom do it” (14–15 year-old). Many also noted concerns the content of the portal questionnaires in that “unless the teen is like Albert Einstein or something, they won’t be able to understand a lot of the or the scientific wording that doctors and nurses use” (12–13 year-old).

Other potential barriers noted were that people might “not have a lot of free time, so would much rather get to the appointment early and just fill it out there” (12–13 year-old). In addition, technology-related barriers were noted such as “sometimes it is harder to use a computer or a phone to fill certain forms – like the internet isn’t always completely reliable” (14–15 year-old). Additionally, potential barriers mentioned were forgetting portal login information, technical issues within the portal itself, and long wait times for information technology (IT) support if there were to be issue.

Suggestions for the module

Most interviewees suggested that reminders to complete the portal questionnaires before the visit would be important. In terms of timing, an initial reminder was thought to be helpful, so they are aware they need to do it; however, a reminder to complete the portal questionnaire closer to the visit was key. The ideal timing among the interviewees was one to three days before their actual visit, for example, “because if I’m alerted too early then I’m just gonna forget about it” (16–17 year-old). In addition, they proposed the best modality to receive reminders to complete the questionnaires would be through text message rather than e-mail “because I text my friends and family every day [and] don’t check my [non-school] email all the time. But definitely a text would be easier for me to be like; I'm texting my friend [and] look -- I have to go do that right now” (12–13 year-old).

Many interviewees noted it was important to ensure adolescents are able to understand the language within the questionnaires was because as one interviewee noted, “a lot of time I’ll be in the appointment and [the doctor, nurse, or parent] will be, like, her lungs are blah, blah, blah, blah and I’m just like what the heck did you just say? So probably simpler wording that will be easy to understand so you’ll just be like, two plus two is four” (12–13 year-old).

Other recommendations from the interviewees related to improving efficiency as much as possible including to ensure the questionnaire was easy to find (e.g., to have a link to the questionnaire appear on the home screen instead of a drop-down menu). They would also like for as much information as possible to be auto filled into the portal questionnaire to reduce the time people would have to spend filling it out. In addition, they were interested in technological innovation to help efficiency: “I think it'd be great if there was a way I could put how I'm feeling [in the portal] and then [it] could recommend what to do. If I'm out of breath, they could say ‘use your asthma medication’ or ‘drink some water.’ I think that would help a lot” (12–13 year-old).

In regard to technological innovations as applied to asthma management using the patient portal, a participant answered that if the portal were to remind me [by sending] me a text message [saying] "take your inhaler right now" I feel it would allow me to remember to do that every single day (15–16 year-old).

Health care transition readiness for asthma management

Despite most interviewees reporting that they typically deferred to their parents for managing their health care, including completing intake forms and communicating with their practitioners. However, interviewees endorsed they would have agency over this if they were called upon: “Usually my parents fill out the forms. But if my doctor would ask me to fill out a form, I would (12–13 year-old).

Most interviewees acknowledged they would need to be more responsible for their care in the future and the module could be valuable as part of their HCT of their asthma management into adulthood to improve their overall health competency: “I know that I need to start taking care of it at some point and I think the portal is a good way to ease me into that” (12–13 year-old).

In addition to navigating the portal, interviewees also felt that completing questionnaires in the portal earlier in life would help them with their HCT: “If you start using the portal now, it prepares you for when you're older and the responsibility” (12–13 year-old). They realized that while one could talk to their doctor and say “hey, I’m 18-years-old now and I still don’t know how to use this thing. But [instead] if you talked to your doctor when younger, then you could end up being like, yeah I really know how to use this thing…so when I’m in immediate danger, I’m just going to use it because I know [when and how] to use it…Again, it really helps you be a lot more independent than you would be if you just didn’t use the app at all” (12–13 year-old).

Furthermore, an interviewee endorsed proof of concept that they succeeded in becoming more involved through use the portal: “I didn't really use [the portal] much, but then [my] doctor said we are able to message her through the app. So, I just downloaded it on my phone and now I'm using it any time I need to refill a prescription or have a question I need to ask my doctors” (16–17 year-old).

Prototype testing of the module

Eleven participants used the prototype asthma management module, 10 completed usability surveys, and 9 completed demographic and baseline characteristics surveys. Forty percent were female, 50 % were 16–17 years old, and 50 % were Hispanic/Latine. All participants had access to a stable internet connection. Half of the participants had 1–4 years of experience using the portal, 20 % had less than 1 year of experience, and 20 % had over 5 years of experience. The most preferred method to access the portal was through a mobile phone application (Table 1).

Findings from the prototype testing focused on four main areas: 1) the module’s impact on asthma data capture and clinical documentation based on manual chart review; 2) overall usability of the module, assessed through SUS scores and open-ended survey feedback; 3) adolescent suggestions for improving the module and user experience, drawn from open-ended survey responses; and 4) the perceived relevance of the module to adolescents’ health care transition readiness, using TRAQ and MARS-A survey data. The findings both from both structured surveys and open-ended responses from the surveys are presented below and in more detail in Supplemental Table 3.

Impact on asthma management documentation

Physician progress notes with the asthma module had a significantly higher percent of key asthma management data captured compared to physician progress notes without the module (93.1 % [95 % CI: 90.3, 95.3] vs. 74.6 % [95 % CI: 71.0,77.6], p < 0.005), Fig. 1. The module particularly increased the documentation of the patient goals, barriers to care, and missed school days elements (See Supplemental Table 4).

Percent relevant asthma-related data captured for adolescents (n = 11) with and without the asthma management module, p < 0.005.

Usability of the module

The average SUS score for the PAC management module was 71 ± 9.65 (scores over 78 are considered “excellent” for this measure), Supplemental Table 5. There was a weak positive trend related to age that did not meet significance thresholds, Supplement Table 6. From the open-ended survey questions, all participants reported the module was overall easy to use and straightforward. Examples of feedback included that the questions were “easy,” “could be answered with confidence,” and were “straightforward.” Some participants also reported that they appreciate the “convenience” of an online module instead of, for example, manually downloading and uploading an intake form before or completing a paper form at the visit. A participant also implied the portal questionnaire could improve data quality because it “allowed me to have time to answer my questions truthfully and in depth instead of just answering the questions to get it done with” (14–15 year-old).

Participants also shared aspects they disliked or found challenging about the module. Many participants commented was that the questions themselves were “confusing” (14–15 year-old) and another noted that “I wish that the questionnaire was a little bit easier to understand. Sometimes when I was answering the questions I did not know what they meant so I had to ask my family” (14–15 year-old). While many did not report any barriers, other feedback included that the questionnaires were long, the interface was not displaying the entire sentence of some answer choices, and they did not like electronic questionnaires in general. For example, one participant reported that “I like paper copies plus sometimes I have dead spots, and internet is not available” (12–13 year-old).

Suggestions for the portal module

The participant suggestions for optimization of the module mirrored the feedback they provided about barriers and facilitators. Primarily, many participants recommended either fewer questions or clarification of questions, so they were easier to understand. However, on the other hand, some suggestions included that the questions should be more detailed, have additional questions about their personal life, and be more personalized. For example, a participant suggested “the questions [to be] more specific to the person depending on their age or severity of [their] asthma” (14–15 year-old).

In addition, related to the technical build, one participant recommended to optimize the interface, so the questionnaires are displayed in their entirety rather than needing to scroll. A participant suggested for it to be easier to find the questionnaire itself once assigned. Of note, once the portal module is beyond the research phase, it will be readily accessible for patients during their pre-check in workflow.

Health care transition readiness

The average transition readiness assessment questionnaire (TRAQ) score was 2.92 ± 1.78 out of 5 (with above 4 as transition ready); and the average TRAQ sub-scores for managing medications, appointment keeping, tracking health issues, and talking with providers ranged from 1.75 ± 1.46–4.22 ± 1.22 (Table 2, Supplemental Table 7). The total average MARS-A score was 36 ± 4.97 (scores 46–50 considered adherent), Supplemental Table 8. There were weak positive trends between transition readiness scores with age, and a weak negative trend between adherence score and age, Supplemental Table 6.

Discussion

Overall, we received mostly positive feedback about the portal module concept for asthma management from adolescents during semi-structured interviews. We found they would be willing to use the portal asthma management module even though most did not manage their portal account by themselves. They also identified that the portal module concept has potential to facilitate their HCT from pediatric to adult care. For example, by allowing them to mention issues before the visit that they would like to discuss, their comfort level to discuss issues with their physician would increase and contribute to increase collaboration, engagement, and shared decision-making with their physician. The prototype testing of the asthma management portal module increased capture of key asthma management data in clinical notes and had above average usability scores from adolescent participants in this study. We also found that adolescents are receptive to technologic interventions to assist with asthma management.

However, despite enthusiasm for the module, high activation rates of the portal in our health system,42 and general technological savviness of adolescents, the usability scores of the module were somewhat lower for the adolescents compared with the parents from our previous work.24 This may be due to the adolescents having less familiarity with the content (e.g., asthma care management) rather than their ability to use the patient portal itself. This unfamiliarity with their own health care and asthma management is suggested by baseline transition readiness and adherence scores in the prototype testing cohort.34, 35, 36, 37, 43, 44 And as discovered through our adolescent end-user interviews, it was apparent that at all ages, adolescents tend to defer to their parents for their asthma health care management despite acknowledging that, in theory, they are capable of performing the care.

Increased agency, sense of responsibility for one’s own health, and engaging in the transition planning process with their parents are identified as key factors for successful HCT.45, 46 Unfortunately, this transfer process from parent to adolescent is very complex with several barriers including differing neurocognitive development rates of individual adolescents, parental over- or under- estimation of the adolescents’ ability to manage their own healthcare, fear by adolescents and parents of transferring to adult medication, lack of parent engagement with the adolescent about HCT, and variable practitioner involvement discussing HCT with patients and families.47, 48, 49, 50

Parental involvement in their adolescent’s portal management is estimated to be above 50 %, but the exact extent is unkown.51 This is noteworthy because asthma guidelines recommend patients start becoming involved in their own care as young as 10 years old26, 28; and in the U.S., adolescents as young as 12-years-old are granted independent access to their patient portal.52 The lead up to this shift in portal responsibility could be an opportunity for parents, healthcare practitioners, and institutions to begin the HCT discussion. With parents, for example, portal ownership can be used as an access point to provide parental education about HCT and encouragement to sit with their adolescent and teach them about the portal, and for parents to begin a more supervisory – rather than direct – role as able. With adolescents, pediatric healthcare institutions have recognized this need and do encourage adolescents to enroll, but there is still a need to increase their interest and engage in the portal beyond just enrollment.53, 54 In addition, it appears important for adolescent engagement to know their healthcare practitioners will use the information they enter into the portal. By increasing adolescent interest and engagement in the portal, this may lead to more asthma management discussions with their parents, active learning on the adolescent’s part, and more agency in their care.

Limitations

While we found indications that our module could be accepted by adolescent populations with asthma, our study has limitations. First, for both study components, the small sample sizes as well as a study population that had access to reliable internet, was less publicly insured, and had only English speakers increased bias and likely limited the generalizability of our results.55 In addition, we did not confirm (digitally or otherwise) if prototype participants actually completed the portal questionnaire independently from their parents or if the physicians reviewed the patient-reported information before the visit started; however, we can assess this in the future. Also, some responses appeared to address the post-module use questions (surveys) rather than the portal questionnaires themselves and we did not interview individual users, which is something we can do in the future. For the qualitative interviews, some interviewees also participated in the prototype testing phase of the study, which could have informed their perceptions and affected our findings. Additionally, many interviewees provided brief answers even with interviewer probing, potentially constraining the depth of insights gathered; however, this limited degree of elucidation is not unexpected for this age group. For the prototype testing, we did not have a true control group for the asthma key element comparison. Also, we only had participants use the module once and therefore cannot make conclusions about the impact of the intervention over time. Additionally, the TRAQ and MARS-A surveys were administered to participants after testing the prototype which could have affected their responses.

Given these limitations, our results still show early promise of portal-based interventions for improving HCT for adolescents with asthma. Through our mixed-methods approach, we were able to gain insight into how to optimize the design of the module itself. Features mentioned by our participants that may enhance adolescents’ engagement with portal asthma management modules include sending text-based reminders, providing training and technical support to aid with portal navigation, and tailoring portal material to patients based on their age and the severity of their asthma. In regard to tailoring the design, our first priority will be to shorten the length of the portal questionnaire and simplify the language as much as possible. Ideally, we would work toward a process in which questionnaires had incremental complexity based on age until adult competency levels are reached. In addition, features such as increased personalization, automated integration of pre-existing EHR data into notes, and ability to “hover” over terms with a displayed definition are not available within the current EHR system but we will be mindful of this for future portal modification plans.

Clinical implications

In the meantime, as mentioned above, it is important for stakeholders to have as much portal and HCT exposure, training and education as possible to support adolescents gain independence managing their health care. Not only should this apply to patients and parents, but also to healthcare practitioners. As identified in our interviews, healthcare practitioners’ influence surrounding adolescent health behavior may be underappreciated and a potential area of future study into the domain of portal for asthma management and HCT. Healthcare practitioners can also use the patient portal to tailor information to the adolescent’s needs (such as the after-visit instructions) and can reach the adolescents directly through portal messaging.

Harnessing portal-based strategies to aid with adolescent HCT could ultimately improve asthma health outcomes by facilitating practices that help achieve transitional care milestones, such as keeping appointments and managing medication. In addition, it could increase engagement with care, health literacy and medication adherence. Future studies should explore evaluating the acceptability of enhancements to portal-based tools among adolescent patients, and the impact of portal-based tools on adolescent health outcomes over time.

Conclusion

The portal-based asthma management module shows early potential as an EHR-integrated module to collect relevant patient information. Early findings show promise, and we will iterate the asthma management module and incorporate into HCT for adolescents with asthma. There is a need for ongoing research to investigate further through large-scale studies with comparison groups. This approach would also be scalable and applicable to other medical conditions.
