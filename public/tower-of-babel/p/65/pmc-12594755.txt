In vitro evaluation of biocompatibility of Ti–20Zr–3Mo–3Sn β-alloy for oral implantology
Authors: Etienne Lefrançois, Doina-Margareta Gordin, Pascal Pellen, Sylvie Tricot, Thierry Gloriant, Yvan Bédouin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of new titanium alloys for dental implants aims to eliminate potentially cytotoxic elements while achieving mechanical properties compatible with bone, particularly a low elastic modulus. This study evaluates the in vitro biocompatibility and mechanical performance of a newly developed β-type Ti-20Zr-3Mo-3Sn (Ti2033) alloy, in comparison with Ti-14Zr and Ti-6Al-4V, two reference alloys used in oral implantology. Mechanical properties were assessed by tensile testing and hardness measurements, while biocompatibility was evaluated using MTT and wound healing assays on osteoblastic cells (Saos-2). Ti2033 exhibits a significantly reduced Young’s modulus (52 GPa), nearly 50% that of the reference alloys, thereby improving mechanical compatibility with bone. Although its ultimate tensile strength (825 MPa) and hardness (300 HV) are slightly lower, Ti2033 shows good ductility (elongation at rupture: 10%). No cytotoxic effects were observed, and cellular viability and migration were comparable among the three alloys. These findings suggest that Ti2033 combines mechanical performance and biocompatibility, making it a promising candidate for endosseous dental implant applications.Keywords: Alloys, Titanium, Elastic modulus, Biocompatibility, Dental implantsSubject terms: Dental materials, Dental implants

Introduction

Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are essential in medicine thanks to their biological and mechanical properties: low density, non-magnetic, good mechanical strength and excellent corrosion resistance, remarkable biocompatibility and osseointegration properties1. Despite its widespread use in the biomedical field, the Ti-6Al-4V (wt%) alloy, known as grade 5 or grade 23 in its Extra Low Interstitial form, is a source of potential adverse effects. Vanadium (V) is considered toxic1–3 and, more particularly, neurotoxic4. Aluminium (Al), listed as an element that can cause encapsulation reaction, is also neurotoxic and may be associated with other pathologies such as osteomalacia1,3. Many biocompatible Ti-alloys have, therefore, been developed without deleterious chemical elements using biocompatible elements, such as Zirconium (Zr), Niobium (Nb), Tantalum (Ta), Molybdenum (Mo) and Tin (Sn)3,5–7.

A binary Ti–Zr alloy (13–17 wt% Zr) was developed 15 years ago to improve the properties of implantable medical devices8. This alloy, commercialized by Straumann as Roxolid®, has demonstrated efficacy for dental implant manufacturing9–11; however, its elastic modulus remained high and was comparable to that of grade 4 or 5 Ti alloys. For medical devices implanted in bone tissue, the challenge of the new generations of Ti alloys is to obtain not only a potentially free-toxic elements material but also a material with appropriate mechanical properties: low density, low modulus of elasticity (closest to bone), good wear resistance and sufficiently high strength for perform sustainably12. A low Young’s modulus is required to prevent damaging the bone, both through the accumulation of stress in the surrounding tissues and through the absence of stimulation, which leads to resorption3,12–15. β-Ti alloys have received particular attention in recent years, as their moduli of elasticity are generally lower than those of α and (α + β) alloys due to their body-centered cubic crystalline structure3.

Many low-modulus β-Ti alloys have been developed over the last decade for implantable medical devices. In addition, they can exhibit high strength and improved biocompatibility6,16,17. In a recent review by Neto et al. on β-Ti alloys for dental implant applications, 81 studies were analyzed, covering compositions from binary to quinary systems18. Some alloys exhibited particularly low elastic moduli, such as Ti-19Co-14Zr (46.74 GPa)19 and Ti-15Mo-5Zr-3Al (43 GPa)20. However, optimizing the elastic modulus to better match bone tissue must be accompanied by the use of highly biocompatible alloying elements. In this regard, other alloys such as Ti-25Ta-25Nb (55 GPa)21 and Ti-30Zr-6Mo (70 GPa)22 have been proposed for their favorable combination of mechanical properties and biocompatibility. In addition to mechanical optimization, biological performance must also be demonstrated. Liu et al. recently reported, through in vivo experiments, that the Ti-B12 alloy (Ti-10Mo-6Zr-4Sn-3Nb, wt%), with an elastic modulus of 84.7 GPa in its fully β-phase state23, shows low cytotoxicity and superior osseointegration performance compared to the conventional Ti-6Al-4V alloy24. The newly developed β Ti-20Zr-3Mo-3Sn25, studied in this work, aligns with the ongoing development of Ti-based materials for implant applications, as it appears to offer a favorable balance between a low elastic modulus, high tensile strength, and adequate elongation, while being composed exclusively of elements regarded as highly biocompatible.

This study aims to compare the biocompatibility of the β Ti-20Zr-3Mo-3Sn alloy (Ti2033, at%) with other Ti alloys such as Ti-14Zr (wt%) and Ti-6Al-4V ELI (Grade 23), which are widely used in oral implantology and often serve as controls in studies18. The in vitro biological evaluation was carried out under the design conditions of an endosseous implantation device using human osteoblast cells. Mechanical properties were evaluated by hardness measurements and tensile strength tests.

Materials and methods

Samples preparation

The Ti–20Zr–3Mo–3Sn (Ti2033, at%) and the Ti-14Zr (14 wt%, is equivalent to 7.87 at%) were synthesized by cold crucible levitation melting (CCLM) technique under an argon atmosphere. For this synthesis, pure raw metals, including 99.6% pure Ti, 99.2% pure Zr, 99.9% pure Mo and 99.99% pure Sn, were used. The ingots were cold rolled until a thickness of 1 mm (cold rolling reduction of 90%). Then, the sheets of Ti2033 alloy as well as commercial sheet (1 mm thick) of Ti-6Al-4V ELI (Grade 23) and Ti-14Zr alloys were punched as disks (13 mm diameter) for biocompatibility tests. For the tensile tests, specimens of standard shape and size were also cut from the three alloy sheets. They were prepared by machining into a dog-bone shape: 3 mm in width, 1 mm in thickness and a gauge length of 15 mm. To restore a fully recrystallized microstructure from the cold-rolled state, Ti-14Zr and Ti2033 specimens were finally solution-treated under a high vacuum (10−7 mbar) at 900 °C for 30 min followed by water quenching.

Materials characterization

Microstructural observations were performed on longitudinal sections (parallel to the rolling direction) using optical microscopy (DMi8 Inverted Microscope; Leica) on specimens mechanically mirror-polished with SiC papers of decreasing grit size, followed by colloidal silica suspension polishing and chemical etching (HF 5%, HNO3 5%, H2O 90%). Phase identification was carried out by X-ray Diffraction (XRD). The lattice parameters were calculated, with a Rietveld refinement approach, from the XRD data using the software (CelRef; LMGP) with the space group No. 194 of the hexagonal system and No. 229 of the cubic system of Ti. The Vickers hardness of the alloys was evaluated using a hardness testing machine (Duramin; Struers) with a 98.07 N load. Six measurements were performed for each alloy sample at random locations.

Tensile tests were carried out until rupture with a tensile machine (Instron 3345, Instron) with a strain rate of 10−4 s−1 to evaluate the mechanical properties of the different studied alloys. The tensile direction of the samples was chosen parallel to the cold rolling direction.

The samples used for cell viability and migration assays were mechanically polished (Tegramin-20; Struers) for 1 min at 15 N with SiC grinding paper (grade 80). They were ultrasonic cleaned with acetone for 15 min, rinsed twice for 15 min with distilled water, before being sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C for 20 min. The roughness was determined by confocal microscopy (Conscan; CSM instruments). Three values were obtained from 5 × 5 mm scans taken in different areas of each surface. The contact angle analysis (DSA20E Easy Drop Goniometer; Kruss) was performed by deposing 3 µl ultrapure water droplets at room temperature on the metallic material discs to assess wettability. Static contact angles were measured for three samples of each alloy (two measurements per sample).

Cell culture

The Saos-2 cell line (human osteosarcoma cell line, ATCC® HTB-85™) was obtained from ATCC (Manassas, USA). The cells were cultured with basic culture medium composed of DMEM medium, supplemented with 10% foetal calf serum, 15mM HEPES buffer, 2 mM L-Glutamine, 100 UI.mL−1 penicillin and 100 µg.mL−1 streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. After reaching approximately 80% confluency, cells were harvested and subcultured.

Cell viability

Three discs of each alloy were sealed individually with agarose into the wells of the 24-well culture plates. Surfaces were seeded with cells in 1 mL of basic culture medium at initial concentrations of 8 × 104, 4 × 104 and 1 × 104 cells.mL−1 for one, three and seven days assays, respectively. The medium was changed every third day up to 7 days. Seeded surfaces were incubated with a 1 mg·mL−1 MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl) 2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] solution according to tetrazolium-based colorimetric assay26. The solutions’ optical densities (OD) were measured at 570 nm with a spectrophotometer (Sunrise; Tecan). The OD was proportional to the number of viable, metabolically active cells. The results were compared to the Tissue Culture Plastic (TCP) as control. The experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Cellular colonization

In vitro wound healing assay using silicone insert (Culture-Inserts 2 Well; Ibidi) was performed on each raw alloy as classically described27. Silicone wells were seeded by Saos-2 cell suspension at 4 × 105 cells. mL−1. The silicones were removed 32 h after seeding. The experiment was blocked at different time (0 h, 16 h, 24 h, 40 h and 48 h) with formaldehyde. The cells were permeabilised with 0.5% Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich) in Phosphate-Buffered Saline and then stained with Phalloidin-FITC and Hoechst (Sigma-Aldrich). Samples were visualized by fluorescence microscopy (Inverted microscope Axio observer Z1; Zeiss). Gap closure was measured using the image software Fiji and Ilastik with a semi-automated method of quantification of the surface area not covered by cells at each time.

Statistical analysis

Statistical software (PRISM; GraphPad) was used for all the analysis. Data were compared between groups using analysis of variance (ANOVA). The probability of p-value < 0,05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Materials characterization

The Ti-6Al-4V alloy exhibits a classical bimodal microstructure, predominantly consisting of fine globular α-grains with a dispersed β-Ti phase. The Ti-14Zr alloy is characterized by a martensitic α′ phase (needle-like) microstructure. The microstructure of the Ti2033 alloy is composed entirely of β-phase, with grain sizes ranging from 50 to 300 μm, evenly distributed throughout the alloy.

Cell viability

The ratios (%) of cell proliferation and viability measured from cells seeded on the three alloys were compared to the cell viability on TCPs. The Saos-2 viability after one day (D1), three days (D3) and one week (D7) of contact are shown in Fig. 4. The cell viability of the three alloys was nearly 50% compared to TCPs at each contact time. No cytotoxicity was observed for all three alloys. At all time points, there was no significant difference in Saos-2 viability between the three alloys.

Discussion

The Ti2033 revealed a low modulus of elasticity (52 GPa) while maintaining a quite high ultimate tensile strength (825 MPa). These mechanical characteristics are explained by the fully β phase crystallographic structure of this alloy. The specific amounts of Zr, Mo, and Sn added to the alloy avoid the formation of both the α″ martensite and the ω phase when the alloy is water-cooled at room temperature25,28,29. These phases can be beneficial for certain applications requiring high alloy strength, but they tend to reduce the material’s ductility, which can be a valuable characteristic for use in oral implantology. The slight shift in the XRD profile of the alloys that can be noted in this study is attributed to the different lattice parameters modified by the chemical elements of the different alloys. In the Ti2033 alloy, both Zr and Sn contribute to the increase in the β-phase lattice parameter, consistent with the findings of Hao et al. on a Ti–Nb–Zr–Sn system28. The shift of the diffraction peaks toward lower angles in the XRD patterns for Ti-14Zr, indicates lattice distortion of the phase, likely caused by the incorporation of Zr atoms with a larger atomic radius. The higher the Zr concentration in the Ti alloy, the more pronounced the leftward shift31. The increased peak broadening observed for Ti2033 and Ti-14Zr compared to Ti-6Al-4V can also be explained by the fact that these alloys underwent quenching, which induces thermal stresses, unlike Ti-6Al-4V, which was purchased in a rolled, non-quenched condition.

From a mechanical point of view, implant materials with low modulus reduce stress shielding on the bone3,12–15. Koguchi et al. have recently reported that using a Ti-Nb-Sn alloy with a low modulus of elasticity promoted faster bone healing than Cp-Ti after osteotomy on rabbits32. Another β Ti-10Mo-6Zr-4Sn-3Nb alloy has also demonstrated low toxicity, and better osseointegration performance than the Ti-6Al-4 V alloy24. Hence, the Ti2033 exhibits promising properties that are potentially suitable for the fabrication of dental implants25,33. In this study, this alloy was compared to the Ti-6Al-4V and the Ti-14Zr alloys. The Ti-6Al-4V alloy is the most commonly used for dental implants and the Ti-14Zr alloy was synthesized in the laboratory to match the characteristics of Straumann’s Roxolid34. This binary alloy, currently the only one on the market for dental implants, has shown an elastic modulus of 103.7 GPa11 and an UTS of 968 MPa35. The synthesized Ti-14Zr exhibited a slightly lower UTS, an identical elastic modulus, and an HCP crystalline structure, similar to Roxolid. The α′ phase has the same HCP crystal structure as the α phase. It is typically formed by rapid quenching from the temperature range of β phase stability36.

Identifying the ideal composition of a Ti alloy that meets the requirements of the target application involves a compromise between optimized mechanical properties and enhanced biocompatibility. The Zr content of Ti2033 (20 at%) appears to be a promising target for this purpose. In 2016, Lee et al. demonstrated that in Ti-Zr alloys, mechanical strength increases with Zr content, reaching a maximum at Ti-30Zr, without compromising the biological response31. This specific Ti2033 composition, combined with a tailored thermomechanical treatment (cold rolling and solution treatment at 900 °C for 30 min followed by water quenching), was therefore selected for implant applications. This solution-treated alloy yields a β-metastable alloy with properties that closely meet the design specifications. Variation of the annealing parameters of this alloy has been shown to reduce its strength while inducing superelastic properties, thereby making it suitable for other applications25. In addition, the Ti2033 alloy has a much higher elongation at rupture than the other two alloys. This property would appear to be of interest for the manufacture of dental implants. It could be used to detect deformation of the implant structure in clinical situations. Deformation would lead to untimely unscrewing before the implant potentially failed.

Based on these results and the current trends in the literature, β-type Ti alloys are receiving particular attention due to their lower elastic modulus compared to commercially available alloys. The choice and proportion of alloying elements play an important role in determining the mechanical properties of the material, along with the thermomechanical treatments it undergoes. The Ti2033 alloy composition appears to be a promising candidate, as it meets key mechanical requirements. It performs better than Cp-Ti and, although slightly lower in strength than Ti-6Al-4V, it offers a lower elastic modulus that is closer to that of bone, along with relatively high elongation. Additional parameters must also be considered, such as wear and corrosion resistance. Corrosion testing, which is the focus of a separate study, has already yielded very promising preliminary results. Furthermore, other factors such as the alloy’s machinability may be limiting, especially when considering industrial-scale production. While the in vitro biocompatibility results are comparable to those of the reference alloys, further in vivo biocompatibility testing is still required.

The biocompatibility of Ti2033 is in agreement with existing literature indicating that Ti, Zr, Mo and Sn, rank among the chemical elements known for their high biocompatibility3,5–7. The Ti2033 alloy does not contain V and Al, which can be criticized due to their potential toxicity1,3,4. It contains only elements considered to be the most biocompatible. Only the biocompatibility of Mo remains a subject of debate2,6. This chemical element is used to produce many novel biomedical alloys as β stabilizer24 and some authors considered Mo as a promising material for osteosynthesis40,41.

Surface parameters (roughness and wettability) are major in regulating the biological activity of osteoblastic cells on alloys42–44. These parameters were comparable between the three alloys to obtain similar surface conditions that vary only in their chemical composition. There is no difference in relative proliferation between the three alloys on viability assay in this study. Osteoblastic cells proliferate on samples with similar surfaces to the same extent without any positive or negative effect of the chemical composition. The Ti2033 alloy, therefore, showed no cytotoxicity towards Saos-2. This is in line with previous studies and the high biocompatibility of the elements constituting this alloy7,44,45.

The three alloys studied have three different crystallographic structures (α’, α/β and β). Straumal et al. have recently shown, for the first time, that the crystallographic modification of Ti (in particular with the omega phase compared to α and β phases) may has positive impact for the colonization of the alloy by human multipotent mesenchymal stromal cells46. The different crystallographic structures of the Ti alloys used in this study did not affect cell viability.

The wound healing results reveals the cellular proliferation and potential migratory behavior across the various alloys. The culture insert method offers a more reproducible assay than the pipette tip scratch technique47.This assay offers valuable insights into the colonization kinetics of a bone implant, thereby assessing the material’s attractiveness under scrutiny. Improving the osseointegration potential of implant devices involves enhancing osteoblast kinetics, i.e. optimizing differentiation while minimizing its impact on proliferation43. The Ti2033 alloy showed initial proliferation results comparable to the reference alloys. Although a significant difference in colonization was observed at 16 h, it remained limited at this time point and was likely without any real biological effect. In fact, gap closure became identical between the alloys after 24 h. Previous studies have described the ability of different alloys to promote the differentiation of osteoblastic cells48–50, particularly for Ti alloys containing a high percentage of Zr51. Modifying Ti surfaces with osteogenic components is also a strategy widely developed to improve the biological activity of implants52. Further analysis of migration times and cell differentiation are still required to clarify the initial promising results of the newly developed Ti2033.

Conclusion

In this in vitro study, the properties of the newly developed β-type Ti-20Zr-3Mo-3Sn alloy make it particularly interesting for endosseous implant application. This alloy contains only biocompatible elements. A low modulus of elasticity (52 GPa), high ultimate tensile strength (825 MPa) and high elongation at rupture (10%) are its defining mechanical characteristics. Biocompatibility results are comparable to the Ti-6Al-4V and Ti-14Zr alloys, making it a promising alloy for dental implant applications.
