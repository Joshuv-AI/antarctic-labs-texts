Metabolic Markers as Potential Risk Factors in Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Sarah McGarrity, Pär I Johansson, Jørn Carlsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a condition characterized by elevated mean pulmonary arterial pressure confirmed by right heart catheterization. PH involves both structural and functional changes in the pulmonary vascular bed. Since 2022, a four‐level risk stratification has been used to predict 1‐year mortality at follow‐up. This system is based on the World Health Organization Functional Classification (WHO‐FC), 6‐min walk distance, and the pro‐Brain Natriuretic Peptide level combined.This study included 47 patients with suspected PH. Of these, 40 were diagnosed with PH while 7 was not, and 6 were included as similarly presenting controls. Untargeted metabolomics using ultra‐high pressure liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐MS), and gas chromatography mass spectrometry (GC‐MS) showed correlations with both risk strata and their component measurements: N‐terminal pro‐brain natriuretic peptide (NT‐proBNP), World Health Organization Functional Class (WHO‐FC), and the 6‐min walk distance (6MWD). There was a greater overlap in metabolites associated with WHO‐FC or 6MWD than between either of them and NT‐proBNP. Several of these metabolites showed the same pattern in a verification data set of 35 PH patients.In conclusion, metabolic patterns in pulmonary hypertension (PH) are related to the predicted risk of disease progression. The clinical variables that contribute to prognostic stratification have overlapping but distinct metabolic associations. Further research is needed to verify how these patterns relate to disease progression in a longitudinal cohort. Metabolomics provides a potential new tool for defining risk in PH.

Introduction

Pulmonary hypertension (PH) is a progressive disease associated with a persistently low 5‐year survival rate despite recent advances in therapeutic options [1, 2, 3]. PH is defined by altered hemodynamics, however there is no consensus as to the exact prognostic implications of hemodynamic changes at diagnosis [2]. There are several different standardized risk stratification tools in use across the world, both etiology‐based, PH class‐specific, and more general. Most include a combination of functional assessments (6‐min walk distance (6MWD) and World Health Organization Functional Class (WHO‐FC)), hemodynamic (right heart catheterization (RHC)), and imaging assessments (echocardiography), heart failure status, and N‐terminal pro‐bone natriuretic peptide [2, 4]. These stratification guidelines rely heavily on invasive and imaging parameters, therefore simpler indicators of prognosis would be valuable. These guidelines determine the necessary intensity of treatment; however, they are not able to predict the most effective treatment modality. This underscores a need for more informative biomarkers to enable more targeted therapeutic decision‐making. Plasma metabolite measurements are a minimally invasive way to monitor patients, for example, as therapeutic drug monitoring [5, 6]. Metabolomics has become a tool for studying and diagnosing diseases, as it has become increasingly possible to measure large numbers of metabolites simultaneously and process the resulting large datasets. In PH, metabolic patterns might, in the future, offer an easily measured diagnostic or disease risk‐stratifying tool [7, 8, 9]. Thus far, a number of small‐to‐medium scale metabolomics studies [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15] have been published, and a significantly larger study is also underway and has published some results [16, 17]. However, interpreting these results is challenging due to the large number of metabolites measured, difficulties in confidently identifying them and the complexity of metabolic networks across various tissues. A key goal is to identify metabolic markers of disease that distinguish clinically relevant patient groups, allowing both better clinical outcome prediction and enhanced understanding of the underlying biological mechanisms of PH.

Genome‐scale metabolic modeling [10] and network analysis tools [18] have been used to identify groups of patients with commonly altered metabolic pathways and to propose the cellular metabolic basis of disease mechanisms based on prior biological knowledge. Other studies have shown that variations in key metabolic pathways are associated with clinical measurements, including right ventricular (RV) dilation and N‐Terminal Pro Brain Natriuretic Peptide (NT‐pro‐BNP) [15, 19, 20]. Metabolites from these pathways may serve as promising markers for disease progression. Metabolic differences have also been observed between PH patients from different etiology‐based subgroups [21]. There are metabolic differences between patients who have connective tissue disease or general pulmonary disease who do and do not have PH [14, 22]. Further, there are studies showing metabolic responses to therapy [11, 23, 24]. Two studies have identified that sphingomyelin metabolites are promising markers for disease outcome [25, 26]. Rhodes et al further identified that patients who develop metabolic patterns more similar to healthy controls after treatment have better treatment outcomes [25]. However, no studies have yet considered the metabolomics of the 2022 ESC/ERS prognosis stratification grouping.

The present study utilized untargeted metabolomics measurements from a cohort of 47 cardiology department patients. The individual metabolic features measured were correlated with the European Framework simple prognostic strata [2] and its component measurements, NT‐proBNP, 6MWD, and WHO‐FC. Pathway analysis was also conducted to identify the metabolic functions that become dysregulated in a manner that is associated with clinical features that act as risk factors.

This analysis has demonstrated prognostic strata in pulmonary hypertension, and it's biochemical and functional components are related to metabolic changes in pulmonary hypertension.

Methods

Patient Selection

Forty‐eight patients were considered for inclusion in this study at the Copenhagen University Hospital Rigshospitalet, Denmark. The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 18 and being seen in the cardiology department for either prevalent PH or suspected PH. Several patients who attended the cardiology clinic with suspected PH but did not meet the diagnostic criteria after RHC were included as a control group (Figure 1). Patients were assigned to diagnostic sub‐groups that amalgamate similar diagnostic classes due to the relatively small size of the study population. Eight patients (not initially diagnosed with PH) had two samples analyzed. The Ethics Committees in the Capital Region of Denmark approved this study (H‐17021130 and Danish Data Protection Agency (2012‐58‐0004)). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects, and patients were enrolled after providing informed consent.

Showing the Distribution of the patients included in this study according to their pulmonary hypertension diagnosis.

There was also a final analysis made to compare the findings of this study with the untargeted metabolomics data that were collected but not used in a previous study [10]. The patients from that study were also adult (≥ 18 years) PH patients admitted to the Department of Cardiology, Copenhagen University Hospital, Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. The study was approved by the Ethics Committees in the Capital Region of Denmark (H‐17021130) and was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and informed consent was obtained from all patients. It included 12 patients with IPAH, 12 patients with CTD‐aPAH, and 11 patients with CTEPH. Clinical data from the patients were retrieved from a research database and electronic health records.

Data Collection and Electronic Health Record Processing

Plasma samples were analyzed using gas and liquid chromatography mass spectrometry metabolomics measurements by MSOmics. Clinical and biochemical data for this study were obtained from a dedicated study registry (DAN‐PH) extracted from patient data and provided on the basis of the EPIC chart. This unique feature includes the progressive registration of patient data, as detailed in the supplementary data. The data included standard clinical biochemistry measurements, hemodynamic measurements from the most recent right heart catheterization (RHC) and echocardiography, as well as information about the patients' characteristics and health history; generally these were taken from the appointments before the blood draw for research or the nearest available date.

Although this data was collected before the 2022 European Society of Cardiology Guidelines for risk stratification were implemented [2], the risk strata at the time of sampling were retrospectively calculated based on the reported World Health Organization Functional Class, NT‐proBNP, and 6‐min walk distance (6MWD) recorded in their medical records.

Metabolomics Mass‐Spectrometry Analysis

Ultra‐high‐performance liquid chromatography‐mass spectrometry (UHPLC‐MS) analysis and gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry (GCMS) analysis were performed by MSOmics (Vedbæk, Denmark), details of mass spectrometry settings in Supplementary Table 1. Peaks were identified and quantified for a number of metabolites, listed in Supplementary Table 2, which includes details on whether they were measured by UHPLC‐MS or GCMS and the level of annotation (from quantification and comparison to a standard down to unidentified). To reduce systematic variations introduced during sample preparation and analysis, MSOmics processed samples in a randomized order. Additionally, for quality control, a mixed pooled sample, consisting of small aliquots from each sample, was analyzed at regular intervals throughout the sequence.

The UPLC‐MS analysis was performed using a slightly modified version of the protocol described by Catalin et al. [27], see supplementary methods. An untargeted multiple reaction monitoring method was used to quantify these metabolites of interest by comparison to known standards. Other peaks were annotated as per the MSOmics library at three levels of certainty.

GC‐MS analysis was carried out by MS‐Omics as described in the supplementary methods section.

All quantifications were performed using external calibration rows. Compounds quantified using the GC‐MS platform were normalized to alanine‐4d, while selected compounds quantified using the UHPLC‐MS platform (both positive and negative mode) were normalized using one of five internal standards (Carnitine‐d9, Glucose‐13C6, Glutamic acid‐d5, Phenylalanine‐d8, or TMAO‐d9). The choice of internal standard was based on an evaluation of which option most effectively removed matrix effects in plasma based on spiking experiments.

Data Filtering and Quality Control

The data, including reported metabolite concentrations and various clinical features, were processed using R (version 4.3.2). An initial assessment of the data led to the removal of one patient due to incomplete records, resulting in a data set of 47 patients.

Metabolites measured at or below the limit of detection (LOD) were replaced with either the metabolite's LOD limit of detection or the median of all LODs if the metabolite did not have a specified LOD. To focus on biological changes and remove highly variable metabolites, filtering was applied. Metabolites with more than 10 outlying measurements. Metabolites were then range‐normalized.

Additional statistical tests were performed in R using tidyverse, rstatix, and tableone packages [28, 29, 30]. The chisq.test is used for categorical variables the one‐way analysis of variance test, equivalent of t‐test, was used for continuous variables in performing the initial clinical analysis.

Differential Metabolite Identification By Linear Regression With Limma

Linear regression of the range‐scaled metabolic data versus clinical features of interest was performed using the limma package [31]. The clinical features of interest were considered to be simple risk strata (as numeric data), and then a model for the three components thereof: NT‐proBNP (log adjusted), 6‐min walk, and WHO‐FC (as numeric values). Patients were excluded from this analysis if they lacked a recent (as defined by the clinician withdrawing data generally 12 months) measurement for a relevant component (for limma analysis n = 35). P values for each metabolite were corrected for multiple testing by Benjamin Hochberg correction (BH) adjustment. The additional multiple testing function was applied to the overall limma model for the three components. The significance of the interactions of the components was calculated and accounted for in the multiple testing correction but not considered in the main text. We have focused some discussion on uniquely identified metabolites – those identified as significantly related to one of the clinical features after multiple testing, but not to the other two.

Comparison of Significant Correlations Found By Limma Analysis with a Previous PH Cohort

A data set previously analyzed using genome‐scale metabolic modeling [10] had accompanying untargeted metabolomics data that was also performed by MSOmics. The reported peaks in this dataset were compared to those found in the current dataset. Those that had the same reported mass and formula and approximately the most similar retention time as reported in our data set were selected and normalized as above. After normalization, peaks with a significant difference on ANOVA by study were removed. Further, ANOVA of the model “Metabolite ~ Study + Strata” was performed for each comparison; the uncorrected p‐values were then filtered to find those metabolites where Strata was significant (p < 0.05), but Study was not (p > 0.05).

Lasso Analysis

To determine the most important metabolic features to prognostic strata and the component measures of prognostic strata, the glmnet package [32] was used to implement a LASSO regression of a multinomial function or linear function, depending on the variable of interest [33]. Further information on LASSO regression and the specific procedures used for parameter selection is in the supplementary methods. LASSO regression was performed on the subset of metabolites that were found to be significantly linked to prognostic strata by the limma analysis. LASSO was performed separately for each of the prognostic strata (multinomial fit), WHO‐FC (multinomial fit), NT‐proBNP (linear fit), and 6MWD (linear fit). The set of metabolites used for this analysis included only those identified as not overall significantly different from the test data set Carlsen et al. [10], metabolites annotated as related to key PH drugs such as sildenafil were also removed from this analysis in order to avoid treatment artifacts, this removal did not extend to all drugs some, such as sunitinib, atenolol, miglitol remained but are of less importance to prognosis. Drug metabolites were generally annotated with low or very low confidence.

Pathway Enrichment Analysis with DeCoupleR

The relationships between metabolites and clinical features identified in the limma analysis were further examined to identify metabolic pathways associated with these clinical features relevant to PH diagnosis. Metabolites from the limma analysis annotated by MSOmics with confidence levels “1a”, “1”, “2a”, “2b”, and “3” (see Supplementary Table 2) were converted to KEGG identifiers using the MetaboAnalyst online tool [34], with checking in the Kegg, HMDB, and PubChem datasets, with small variations in spelling and punctuation where appropriate. The Consensus Path Database [35], downloaded in January 2025, provided pathway data for the enrichment analysis, incorporating all metabolite pathways from the combined database. This was then filtered for KEGG IDs present in the dataset and unused pathways were removed and further duplicate pathways from different sources were removed, with the most comprehensive retained. The score was used as the measure of difference in the decoupleR [36] gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) analysis for metabolites with available KEGG identifiers.

Results

Comparison of Patient Characteristics between Prognostic Strata

The 47 dyspnea patients considered in this study were mostly diagnosed with PH, with only 6 not meeting the diagnostic criteria (one previously removed due to lack of data). These patients were compared retrospectively according to their 1‐year simple risk strata as defined by the 2022 guidelines (see Table 1). The distribution across prognostic strata in this study was fairly even across the lower end of the spectrum, with 21 patients classified as low risk, 16 as low‐intermediate risk, and 10 as high‐intermediate risk in the overall cohort. In the subgroup diagnosed with PH, the distribution was similar, comprising 16 low‐risk, 16 low‐intermediate‐risk, and 9 high‐intermediate‐risk patients. Notably, no patients fell into the high‐risk stratum. The age and sex distribution of patients did not differ across risk strata. Although no males were among the patients not diagnosed with PH, this difference was not statistically significant. As expected, males were in the minority across all risk strata. There was a statistically significant distribution shown between diagnostic classification only when non‐diagnosed controls were considered as a class. This difference disappeared when only PH patients were considered (p = 0.579 among only patients).

Correlations Among the Components of Prognostic Strata

We investigated the correlation between 2022 guideline‐based simple prognostic strata as well as the clinical features that comprise the prognostic strata (NT‐proBNP, 6MWD, WHO‐FC).

There were significant correlations between prognostic strata and all of its components. The least strongly associated feature was NT‐proBNP level with any of the others. The most strongly associated feature was 6‐min walk, which is negatively correlated with the prognostic strata. See Supplementary Table 4.

Using a cutoff of beta coefficient (B value in tables) > than 10, indicating a 10 times greater likelihood of significant association compared to not, and equivalent to an adjusted p‐value < 1e‐9, 376 out of 718 metabolic features were associated with prognostic strata, 669 at adjusted p‐value < 0.05 (see supplementary table 5). This cut‐off was chosen rather than p < 0.05 in order to obtain a more conservative cut‐off without using a fold change cut‐off. The metabolite most strongly associated (lowest p values) with prognostic strata was an unidentified pentose; this pentose was not identified with any of the tested clinical features that are components of the prognostic strata. There are a total of 272 metabolites significantly associated with prognostic strata but not any of the components, the top four of which are the unidentified pentose, cis‐aconitic acid, fumaric acid, and citric acid, see Figure 2a. Other associated metabolites included the carboxylic acids: citrate, malate, and 2‐oxoglutarate.

Metabolites associated with WHO‐FC also included amino acids, fatty acids, and their derivatives and some glucose metabolism‐associated metabolites, see supplementary table 6.

Metabolites that were associated with 6MWD included amino acids, fatty acids, and their derivatives, see supplementary table 7.

Two out of three metabolites that were associated uniquely with NT‐proBNP were bile acids, see Figure 2d. The metabolite most strongly associated only with NT‐proBNP was annotated as atenolol, a beta‐blocker not commonly used in PH. The most associated with NT‐proBNP and also with prognostic strata was a tentatively labeled betaine; there were only four metabolites in this overlapping group, the others were annotated as ethylbenzaldehyde, acetyltryptophan, and propionic acid.

There was a substantial overlap between metabolic features associated with WHO‐FC and 6MWD, with around 25% of the metabolites involved overall. In particular, over 60% of metabolites associated with WHO‐FC are also associated with 6MWD. In contrast, there was far less overlap between the metabolic features associated with NT‐proBNP and WHO‐FC (about 6% of the total). The overlap between NT‐proBNP and 6MWD was about 14% of the metabolites associated with either; a majority of metabolites associated with NT‐proBNP were also associated with 6MWD, while this set was a far smaller proportion of the 6MWD‐associated metabolites. There was a large overlap between metabolic features associated with prognostic strata and each of its components, see supplementary Figure 1.

Metabolic Pathways Associated with Prognostic Strata and Its Components

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed in order to gain some insight into the likely underlying biology of the observed changes in metabolites. The Kegg metabolite‐labeled section of the Consensus Pathway Database [35], which overlapped with our annotated metabolites, was used to incorporate pathways from multiple sources. Where there were pathways incorporated from multiple sources with the same name, the longest pathway was used in order to avoid duplication.

Various carbohydrate pathways and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar pathways are upregulated in association with Prognostic strata. Also, there is a negative association with various aspects of fatty acid metabolism, which seems counterintuitive given some previous associations; further verification of the underlying metabolic changes resulting in the plasma metabolites would need to be made. Changes in fatty acid metabolism have also previously been seen associated with PH diagnosis [37, 38, 39, 40]. This includes “sphingomyelin and ceramide metabolism,” which is implicated with various inflammatory conditions, Figure 4a.

The pathway “synthesis of UDP‐N‐acetyl‐glucosamine” is the most strongly associated pathway with both WHO‐FC and 6MWD, but is not in the top 15 pathways for NT‐proBNP, Figure 4b–d. Many metabolic pathways were shared between prognostic strata and WHO‐FC or 6MWD, whereas NT‐proBNP and prognostic strata did not share metabolic pathways, supplementary Figure 2. Pathways of glutamine metabolism, nucleotide and nucleotide sugar metabolism, especially purines, central energy metabolism, and glutathione‐related metabolic pathways are associated with both 6MWD and WHO‐FC. Two caffeine‐related metabolic pathways, aspects of malate and aspartate metabolism, “organic anion transport”, generic “nucleotide metabolism”, and “sphingomyelin metabolism/ceramide salvage” are shared pathways between WHO‐FC, 6MWD, and prognostic strata. However, it should be noted that two pathways of sphingolipid metabolism are associated with NT‐proBNP.

Metabolites Most Associated With Prognostic Strata and Its Components

To identify the metabolites most closely associated with prognostic strata and their components in this data set, LASSO regression [33] was performed on the features identified by LIMMA analysis associated with prognostic strata that were overall similarly observed in both datasets and not including metabolites identified as PH drugs.

Histidine was selected in all 4 models. It had a positive coefficient for the prognostic strata model, WHO‐FC model, and 6MWD model, but a much weaker negative coefficient in the NT‐proBNP model, see Figure 5.

Plots showing the LASSO coefficients for identified metabolites associated with (a) prognostic strata, (b) WHO‐FC, (c) 6MWD, (d) NT‐proBNP.

There were relatively few metabolites shared between any of the three component models. Each component model shared between a quarter and a half of their components with the prognostic strata model, see Figure 5.

The goodness of fit measures for the models in the whole original dataset are generally good (AUC 0.96 Strata, AUC 0.75 WHO‐FC, RSQ 0.87 NT‐proBNP, RSQ 0.73 6MWD). However, the goodness of fit measure for the same model with the independent Carlsen 2022 dataset [10], there is a substantial drop (AUC 0.57 Strata, AUC 0.68 WHO‐FC, RSQ −0.66 NT‐proBNP, RSQ −0.35 6MWD). The relationship between metabolism and 6MWD and NT‐proBNP is particularly unstable between datasets and WHO‐FC was the most stable between the two, possibly due to the compound nature of this data point.

Discussion

This study has shown that in a population of prevalent PH patients (groups 1–4), metabolism is related to prognostic strata as defined in the ESC/ERS guidelines (2022) [2]. Due to limitations of the dataset, we were unable to further investigate links with the actual long‐term outcome. The study also identified metabolites measured in an independent population [10] and verified that many have a similar relationship to PH clinical markers, suggesting they may be generalizable biomarkers. Additionally, the analysis demonstrated that functional changes of PH, as quantified by WHO‐FC and 6MWD, and cardiac dysfunction, defined by NT‐proBNP, have somewhat different metabolic consequences.

Many previous metabolomics studies focused on PAH and CTEPH (Class I and IV), or a comparison of those groups [10, 11, 12, 17]. This study, however, included patients from the four most common disease classes and all but the most severe patients were included, making the results more generalizable. However, it does lack the most severely ill patients with the highest risk prognostic strata. patients, limiting its scope. Many previous studies have concentrated on the metabolic distinction between the classes, or between patients with an underlying condition such as sclerotic disease, COPD, or congenital heart disease with and without PH [14, 17, 22]. This study is therefore unusual in focusing on the relationship of biomarkers to the assigned clinical prognostic strata. However, an important limitation is that this study does not include a long‐term follow‐up, therefore the prognostic strata act largely as a severity measure. Therefore, it will be important to repeat this study with a patient group with a longer‐term follow‐up to identify the real utility of these biomarkers.

This study has identified metabolites that are significantly related to the simple prognostic strata as defined in the ESC/ERS 2022 guidelines [2]. It has also identified metabolites that are independently related to each of the three components of this prognostic summary, WHO‐FC, 6MWD, and NT‐proBNP. It is interesting to note that more metabolites and metabolic pathways related to WHO‐FC and 6MWD are associated with overall prognostic strata than those related to NT‐proBNP.

This study has identified several metabolites of the Tricarboxylic acid cycle in association with prognostic indications. This group of metabolites has previously been associated with PH severity or PH diagnosis [13, 41, 42]. Histidine appeared in the LASSO models with positive coefficients for prognostic strata, 6MWD, and WHO‐FC. There have previously been other metabolomic and mechanistic links between PH and histidine proposed [26, 43].

Purine nucleotides were also associated with WHO‐FC and 6MWD. These metabolites have previously been linked to sildenafil response [23]; as these were not drug naïve patients, this may be linked to therapeutic response and therefore current state.

Interestingly, norepinephrine sulfate was associated with 6MWD. A previous study found that in patients with PAH or CTEPH and greater metabolic disarrangement, there were higher adrenaline levels [10]. Testosterone sulfate was also associated with 6MWD, which has previously been linked to cardiac changes in PH [42].

Proline, a metabolite important to collagen formation and implicated mechanistically in changes to pulmonary artery endothelial cells [44, 45], is significantly associated with both WHO‐FC and prognostic strata in this study. Metabolites that were associated with NT‐proBNP concentration prominently included bile acids. These metabolites have been previously shown to be associated with iPAH versus PAH, endothelial function in PH, and right ventricular dysfunction in PH [46, 47, 48, 49], see supplementary Table 8. Betaine was also prominent as a metabolite that was seen in association with NT‐proBNP concentration and prognostic strata that have previously been linked to PH [50].

Carnitine metabolites have been associated with PH severity and diagnosis in numerous studies [17, 20, 51, 52]. Furthermore, they have been previously associated with PAH outcomes [25, 26]. This study has once again identified these associations.

“Sphingomyelin metabolism/ceramide salvage”, a pathway identified with WHO‐FC, 6MWD, and prognostic strata, stood out as it has previously been associated with PAH and CTEPH, although it has also been associated with RV function [11, 12, 20]. This suggests that this is a general reflection of the dysfunction in PH and not solely associated with functional changes when considered in a larger population. Sphingomyelin metabolic pathways have previously been identified with outcomes in PAH patients; that this study found the similar associations in a broader population is interesting [25, 26].

A follow‐up to this study with more patients could consider the relationship between metabolism and therapy. It would be advantageous to consider these measurements in a drug‐naïve population.

Currently, NT‐proBNP is the only recognized blood‐borne biomarker for PH prognosis. Interestingly, this study found that NT‐proBNP is associated with a broadly different set of metabolites compared to the two functional characteristics examined. This may suggest that there are distinct pathological metabolic changes that are related to the cardiac effects of PH compared to the more general functional changes. Including both sets of characteristics likely makes the strata more generalizable, though it may come at the cost of not being able to identify patients at higher risk of heart failure. Other studies have correlated changes to the right ventricle with metabolic changes [41, 53, 54]. Developing a set of blood or plasma‐sampled biomarkers that provide detailed information on different aspects of PH pathology would be beneficial.

Perspectives

This study has demonstrated that in a population of prevalent PH patients, metabolism is related to prognostic strata. It is interesting to note that different metabolic pathways are associated with distinct metabolic alterations. This offers the potential for follow‐up investigations to identify metabolic fingerprints for populations with increased cardiac risk, separate from their functional status and vice versa. This study has also identified metabolites measured in an independent population and verified that the majority of them follow similar patterns, suggesting that they may be generalizable biomarkers. In the future, it will be necessary to study a treatment‐naïve population, as intended to be screened by the prognostic strata, and to include adequate clinical follow‐up to see if metabolic features can better classify patients than the current stratification system alone. The lack of clinical follow‐up and, therefore, a lack of time for event analysis is a weakness of this study. Furthermore, given the distinction between cardiac‐driven and pulmonary‐driven changes, metabolic screening may, in the future, better target therapy.

Conclusions

This dataset has shown that numerous metabolites are associated with prognostic strata and their components. Additionally, it suggests that the components of prognostic strata form two overlapping but different metabolic groups that complement their known functions. WHO‐FC and 6MWT are more pulmonary, while NT‐proBNP is more cardiac. Overall, the prognostic strata metabolically seem to be more influenced by the hypoxic and pulmonary aspects of the disease. This study has also identified metabolites measured in an independent population and verified that the majority of them follow similar patterns, suggesting that they may be generalizable biomarkers. However, the population in the test dataset was limited to only groups 1 and 4, thereby limiting the generalizability of our results. There were also no patients in the highest risk group, again limiting the conclusions to be drawn. Also, while the changes of individual metabolites were similar between test and training data, the overall metabolic patterns identified by LASSO analysis were not consistent between the two populations. This may be due to the small size of the initial population and exacerbated by the differences in which groups of PH patients are included in the two studies.

This study shows interesting data on a small scale, but it would be advantageous to repeat it with a treatment‐naïve population and longitudinal clinical follow‐up. Furthermore, given the distinction between cardiac‐driven and pulmonary‐driven changes, metabolic screening may, in the future, better target therapy.
