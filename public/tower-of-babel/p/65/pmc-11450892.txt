Artificial Intelligence–Enhanced Electrocardiography Identifies Patients With Normal Ejection Fraction at Risk of Worse Outcomes
Authors: Jwan A Naser, Eunjung Lee, Francisco Lopez-Jimenez, Peter A Noseworthy, Omar S Latif, Paul A Friedman, Grace Lin, Jae K Oh, Christopher G Scott, Sorin V Pislaru, Zachi I Attia, Patricia A Pellikka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAn artificial intelligence (AI)-based electrocardiogram (ECG) model identifies patients with a higher likelihood of low ejection fraction (EF). Patients with an abnormal AI-ECG score but normal EF (false positives; FP) more often developed future low EF.ObjectiveThe purpose of this study was to evaluate echocardiographic characteristics and all-cause mortality risk in FP patients.MethodsPatients with transthoracic echocardiography and ECG were classified retrospectively into FP, true negatives (TN) (EF ≥50%, normal AI-ECG), true positives (TP) (EF <50%, abnormal AI-ECG), or false negatives (FN) (EF <50%, normal AI-ECG). Echocardiographic abnormalities included systolic and diastolic left ventricular function, valve disease, estimated pulmonary pressures, and right heart parameters. Cox regression was used to assess factors associated with all-cause mortality.ResultsOf 100,586 patients (median age 63 years; 45.5% females), 79% were TN, 7% FP, 5% FN, and 8% TP. FPs had more echocardiographic abnormalities than TN but less than FN or TP patients. An echocardiographic abnormality was present in 97% of FPs. Over median 2.7 years, FPs had increased mortality risk (age and sex-adjusted HR: 1.64 [95% CI: 1.55-1.73]) vs TN. Age and sex-adjusted mortality was higher in FP with abnormal echocardiography than FP with normal echocardiography and to TN regardless of echocardiography result; FP with normal echocardiography had comparable mortality risk to TN with abnormal echocardiography.ConclusionsFP patients were more likely than TNs to have echocardiographic abnormalities with 97% of exams showing an abnormality. FP patients had higher mortality rates, especially when their echocardiograms also had an abnormality; the concomitant use of AI ECG and echocardiography helps in stratifying risk in patients with normal LVEF.Key words: artificial intelligence, echocardiography, electrocardiography, machine learning, ventricular function

Heart failure (HF) is a global pandemic affecting more than 64 million people worldwide.1,2 Despite advances in available therapy, HF hospitalizations and mortality rates continue to be high in these patients.3 Even in the absence of clinical HF, asymptomatic reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) (stage-B HF) was shown to be associated with a 60% increase in the risk of mortality despite the fact that only half of these patients experienced clinical HF on follow-up.4 Under these circumstances, early detection of patients at risk of HF and low LVEF who may benefit from preventive strategies becomes critically important.5

Several scoring systems consisting of clinical, laboratory, and/or imaging variables have been developed previously to predict the development of incident HF.6, 7, 8, 9 More recently, a novel artificial intelligence (AI) tool was developed to identify patients who have a reduced LVEF using a neural network trained on electrocardiograms (ECGs).10 Patients with normal LVEF ≥50% who had abnormal scores by the AI-ECG tool (ie, false positives [FPs]) were found to be at fourfold increased risk for the development of low LVEF ≤35% at follow-up.10 However, the clinical and echocardiographic characteristics of these patients and their outcomes remained unknown. Herein, we aimed to: 1) identify clinical and echocardiographic characteristics of patients with normal LVEF but abnormal AI-ECG score (ie, FPs); and 2) evaluate the clinical significance and outcomes of a FP AI-ECG.

Methods

Study sample

The data underlying this article will be shared upon a reasonable request to the corresponding author. The Institutional Review Board approved the protocol and waived the informed consent requirement. Only patients who had previously agreed to include their data in a retrospective cohort chart review research study were included. All adult patients who had a first transthoracic echocardiogram at any Mayo Clinic site (Rochester, Minnesota; Scottsdale, Arizona; Jacksonville, Florida; Mayo Clinic Health System in the upper Midwest) between 2014 and 2019 were identified. Exclusion criteria included congenital heart disease, malignancy other than nonmelanoma skin cancer, the absence of ECGs within 7 days of the echocardiogram, the absence of follow-up at Mayo Clinic sites after the echocardiogram, and patients who had ECGs that were included in training the original AI algorithm.

The AI-ECG data was obtained from the standard 12 lead ECG using the previously developed platform for the detection of low LVEF10; no additional training was performed for the purpose of this study. The AI-ECG model used a convolutional neural network that was trained on 12-lead ECGs obtained from 35,970 patients with ECG-TTE pairs within 2 weeks from each other from Mayo Clinic digital data between January 1994 and February 2017; the model outputs a probability between 0 to 100% of how likely a low LVEF is present. This previous platform was trained to identify patients with LVEF ≤35% with 86% sensitivity and 86%. However, the model output correlated with the continuous LVEF, and the model was able to detect a LVEF <50% with a sensitivity of 78%, specificity of 78%, positive predictive value of 63%, and negative predictive value of 93.1% (at a prevalence of low LVEF of 21%).10 In the current study, low LVEF was defined as <50%. Patients in our study were classified as FPs if they had a normal LVEF ≥50% but positive AI-ECG for low LVEF, true negatives (TN) if they had a normal LVEF ≥50% and a negative AI-ECG for low LVEF, true positives (TP) if they had a low LVEF <50% and positive AI-ECG, and false negatives (FN) if they had a low LVEF <50% but a negative AI-ECG for low LVEF.

Comorbidities including coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, atrial fibrillation, chronic lung disease, sleep apnea, diabetes mellitus, stroke, transient ischemic attack, malignancies, hypertension, amyloidosis, and sarcoidosis were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. ICD codes can be shared upon a reasonable request from the corresponding author.

Echocardiography

All transthoracic echocardiograms were clinically indicated, performed according to guidelines, and interpreted by level III board-certified echocardiologists.11, 12, 13, 14 The LVEF was determined by volume assessment (3D or biplane method of disks), if this was not feasible, by linear method, and if this was not possible, by visual estimation. The echocardiogram was considered within normal limits when all of the following criteria were met: LVEF ≥50%; LV end-diastolic dimension <59 mm in males and <53 mm in females; no regional wall motion abnormalities; no ≥ moderate valve regurgitation or ≥ mild valve stenosis; medial E/e′ <14; medial e′ >7 cm/s; left atrial volume index < 34 cm3/m2; LV mass index <115 g/m2 in males and <95 in females; tricuspid regurgitation velocity ≤2.80 m/s; and absence of RV enlargement or more than trivial pericardial effusion.

Electrocardiography

We identified patients who had a normal ECG as previously interpreted by board-certified cardiologists. To be interpreted as normal ECG, all the following criteria were met: 1) all the intervals (PR, QRS, QT) were normal; 2) the hazard ratio (HR) was normal (60-100 beats/min); 3) the rhythm was sinus during the ECG; 4) no ST-T changes; 5) no pathologic Q waves; and 6) no evidence of LV hypertrophy. Only the closest ECG within 7 days of the echocardiogram was considered for each patient.

Follow-up

The primary outcome of the study was all-cause mortality. The vital status was retrieved using the Mayo Clinic records and Accurint, a Web-based resource combining multiple national sources (queried on February 17, 2023). The outcome was compared between the FP, TN, FN, and TP groups until last available follow-up.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are reported as number (percentages) and continuous variables as median (IQR). The ANOVA and Student’s t-tests were used to compare continuous variables and Chi-square test to compare categorical variables between the FP, TN, FN, and TP groups. Kaplan Meier analysis was used to plot mortality. A conditional approach was used to create age- and sex-adjusted survival curves. Cox regression was used for adjusted and multivariable analysis of factors associated with all-cause mortality. In the multivariable model, all variables with P < 0.05 at the univariable level and known association with mortality based on a priori knowledge were included. Pairwise deletion (ie, available case analysis) was used, in which patients were only dropped from a particular analysis if the variables needed for that analysis contained missing data. Sensitivity analyses were performed in patients with normal ECG, echocardiograms, or both and depending on patients’ setting (inpatient, outpatient, or emergency department). Statistical analyses were performed with JMP Pro software version 16.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc.) and R version 4.2.2. Two-sided P values <0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Of 204,052 adult patients who had a first transthoracic echocardiogram at Mayo Clinic sites between 2014 and 2019, 100,586 patients met the eligibility criteria. Of those, 44% had their echocardiograms in Rochester, Minnesota, 19% in Scottsdale, Arizona, 19% in Jacksonville, Florida, and 17% in the Mayo Clinic Health System. Median age was 63 years and 45.5% were females. Of these patients, 79,688 (79%) were TNs; 7,458 (7%) were FPs; 5,275 (5%) were FNs; and 8,165 (8%) were TPs. The ECG was recorded within a median of 0.5 (IQR: 0.3-0.7) days from the echocardiograms; 77% of ECGs were within 1 day and 87% were within 2 days. Baseline characteristics in the overall cohort and in the FP, TN, FN, and TP groups are shown in Table 1. Compared to TN patients, FP patients were older, more likely males, and had more comorbidities. In addition, FP patients had lower LVEF and cardiac index, larger, heavier LVs, worse diastolic parameters, higher pulmonary pressures, and more valve disease than TN patients, Table 1 and Figure 1. Compared to FN patients, FP patients had similar or more prevalent comorbidities and worse echocardiographic parameters, except for LVEF and LV dimensions which, by definition, were worse in the FN group. Among all groups, comorbidities and echocardiographic abnormalities were most prevalent in TP patients, Table 1.

Over median follow-up of 2.7 (IQR: 0.2-5.0; mean 2.93) years, death occurred in 15,568 of 100,586 patients (rate 5.3% per year vs expected for the US general population of mean 2.6% per year in this cohort). Compared to TNs, FP (HR: 1.87 [95% CI: 1.77-1.97], P < 0.001), FN (HR: 2.42 [95% CI: 2.30-2.55], P < 0.001), and TP patients (HR: 3.54 [95% CI: 3.39-3.69], P < 0.001) had increased all-cause mortality, Figure 2 and Central Illustration. Other univariable factors associated with mortality are shown in Table 2. On multivariable analysis, FP patients remained at increased risk of mortality compared to TN patients (HR: 1.09; 95% CI: 1.02-1.18), independent of age, sex, comorbidities, echocardiographic, and other ECG parameters. Overall, 58,510 (58%) of patients had their ECG obtained in the outpatient setting, 20,982 (21%) in the inpatient setting, 20,635 (21%) in the emergency department, and 441 (0.4%) in the catheterization lab or in the pre-operative area. When stratifying by location of the ECG, the age- and sex-adjusted HR for all-cause mortality was 1.45 (95% CI: 1.32-1.59) for FP vs TN in the ER, 1.45 (95% CI: 1.33-1.58) in the inpatient setting, and 1.74 (95% CI: 1.59-1.91) in the outpatient setting.

When analyzed across race groups, FP remained associated with mortality in patients with White race (age- and sex-adjusted HR: 1.61 [95% CI: 1.51-1.70], P < 0.001), Black race (age- and sex-adjusted HR: 1.62 [95% CI: 1.26-2.08], P < 0.001), and in other remaining races (age- and sex-adjusted HR: 1.49 [95% CI: 1.02-2.20], P = 0.04).

When analyzing the association of a binary AI-ECG result (positive vs negative) adjusted to age, sex, and low LVEF <50%, a positive AI ECG was associated with increased mortality (HR: 1.57 [95% CI: 1.51-1.64], P < 0.001), (Supplemental Table 2).

Patients with normal ECGs

Of 36,904 patients with normal ECGs by clinical interpretation, 35,033 (95%) were TNs; 620 (2%) were FPs; 1,078 (3%) were FNs; and only 173 (0.5%) were TPs. Baseline characteristics of these patients are shown in Supplemental Table 3. FP patients were at increased risk of mortality both at the univariable and multivariable level compared to TN patients (multivariable HR: 1.26 [95% CI: 1.02-1.56], P = 0.04), (Supplemental Table 4, Figure 2). Analysis of baseline characteristics in patients without missing data included in the final multivariable model and in patients with missing data is shown in Supplemental Table 5.

Patients with normal echocardiograms

After excluding patients with prior diagnosis of heart failure or cardiomyopathy as well as patients with missing echocardiographic data (15,059 patients [15%] including 6% of FP patients and 16% of TN patients), 97% of FP patients and 79% of TN patients had one or more echocardiographic abnormality as stated above. On the other hand, 3% of FP and 21% of TN patients had normal echocardiograms. Of 13,416 patients who met the criteria for a normal echocardiogram, 13,212 (98%) were TNs and 204 (2%) were FPs. Baseline characteristics of these patients overall and across the TN and FP categories are shown in Table 3. FP patients were older, more likely males, and had more comorbidities. Despite being in the normal range, LVEF and LV stroke volume index were lower, and LV mass index, E/e’, RV systolic pressures, and resting heart rates were higher in the FP group. Over median 2.7 (IQR: 0.1-5.1) years of follow-up, 475 patients died. Mortality was higher in FP patients at the multivariable level (HR: 2.01 [95% CI: 1.26-3.21], P = 0.003) than TN patients (Figure 2, Table 4). Of patients with a normal echocardiogram, 7,964 patients also had normal ECG. The AI-ECG was TN in 7,911 (99%) patients and FP in 53 (1%). Age, sex, and most comorbidities were comparable between the 2 groups, Supplemental Table 6. In FP, AF was more frequent, LV mass index and heart rate were higher, and LV stroke volume index was lower; other echocardiographic parameters were similar. Over median 2.6 (IQR: 0.1-5.1) years, mortality occurred in 230 patients. Despite adjustment to baseline differences, FPs had higher mortality (HR: 3.65 [95% CI: 1.49-8.91], P = 0.005) than TNs; the age- and sex-adjusted mortality is shown in Figure 2.

Mortality by echocardiogram and AI-ECG result

When comparing the age- and sex-adjusted mortality between FP and TN patients according to the result of the echocardiogram (normal vs abnormal), FP patients with abnormal echocardiograms had the highest mortality (age- and sex-adjusted HR: 3.68 [95% CI: 3.30-4.09], P < 0.001 compared to TN patients with normal echocardiogram). FP patients who had normal echocardiograms and TN patients with abnormal echocardiograms had comparable age- and sex-adjusted mortality (P = 0.82); however, each of these groups of patients had a higher age- and sex-adjusted mortality than TN patients with normal echocardiogram (HR: 2.30 [95% CI: 1.45-3.64], P < 0.001 for FP with normal echocardiograms and HR: 2.42 [95% CI: 2.20-2.67], P < 0.001 for TN with an abnormal echocardiogram).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated clinical characteristics, echocardiographic parameters, and outcomes in patients with normal LVEF ≥50% who had abnormal AI-ECG scores (ie, FP AI-ECG) compared to patients with normal LVEF and AI-ECG (TN) and to patients with low LVEF with normal and abnormal AI ECG scores (TP and FN patients). A FP AI-ECG score reflected cardiovascular abnormalities found on echocardiographic exams and was independently associated with excess mortality compared to a TN AI-ECG score (Central Illustration). Routinely obtained echocardiographic parameters were normal in only 3% of FP patients. The age- and sex-adjusted mortality was higher for FP patients with abnormal echocardiograms than FP patients with normal echocardiograms or TN patients with normal or abnormal echocardiograms. Interestingly, however, the age- and sex-adjusted mortality for FP patients with normal echocardiograms and TN patients with abnormal echocardiograms was comparable. Even in patients with normal ECGs and echocardiograms, the AI-ECG tool identified a subgroup with relatively lower but normal values of stroke volume index and relatively higher heart rate and LV mass index; these patients were still at increased risk of mortality. Our data suggest that a FP AI-ECG has an additive value to conventional diagnostic testing including the ECG and echocardiogram in risk stratification of patients for worse outcome. Furthermore, since most FP patients had cardiac abnormalities on echocardiographic exams, a positive AI-ECG score may justify a subsequent transthoracic echocardiographic study for further evaluation of cardiac structure and function.

The AI-ECG tool for the identification of low LVEF was developed to detect patients with an LVEF ≤35%. However, this model demonstrated the capacity to generalize its learned understanding to other thresholds such as 40%, 45%, and 50% as previously validated.10,15, 16, 17, 18, 19 This successful generalization indicates the possibility of a continuous influence of LVEF and cardiomyopathies on the ECG in more nuanced ways than previously understood. Furthermore, in the original development study, around half of patients with a higher LVEF identified as positives by the AI-ECG still had abnormal LVEF values between 36%-49%, and those with LVEF ≥50% had a fourfold increased risk of developing low LVEF over the next 5 years of follow-up.10 The current study shows that in an unselected population, patients with normal LVEF ≥50% but positive AI-ECG had comorbidities and echocardiographic values intermediate between those with a normal LVEF ≥50% and normal AI-ECG score and those with low LVEF <50%. Echocardiographic differences included systolic and diastolic function of the left ventricle, valve disease, estimated pulmonary pressures, and right heart parameters. Importantly, these FP patients had worse outcomes beyond what can be explained by age, sex, comorbidities, or echocardiographic parameters. This was evident both at multivariable analysis and in subanalyses involving patients with normal ECG, echocardiogram, or both.

Only 3% of the FP patients had normal echocardiographic exams in the absence of missing data and 97% had abnormalities on echocardiography. In contrast, 21% patients of TN patients had normal echocardiograms. FP patients with abnormalities on the echocardiogram had increased age- and sex-adjusted mortality compared to FP patients with normal echocardiogram. At the same time, FP patients with normal echocardiogram had an age- and sex-adjusted mortality rate similar to that in TN patients with abnormal echocardiograms. This finding suggests that combining AI ECG and transthoracic echocardiography results may help further stratify patients’ risk compared to one test alone.

One possible explanation for these findings is that the AI-ECG tool, which utilizes convolutional neural networks trained on raw ECG data, recognizes patterns in the ECGs that reflect metabolic, structural, or functional derangements that are commonly, but not exclusively, present in patients with low LVEF. These derangements may be present and detected by the AI ECG at an earlier disease stage, when the LVEF is still within normal limits. In some patients (3% in this study), these abnormalities may only manifest as subtle differences in routine echocardiographic testing. Specifically, patients with normal ECGs and echocardiograms who were FP had higher heart rates and LV mass index and lower stroke volume index than TNs, but when looking at the individual patient level, these parameters were in the normal range and therefore can easily go unnoticed. Because deep learning was used to develop the AI-ECG algorithm, the exact patterns in the ECG identified by the neural network remain unknown, however.

Clinical implications

Taken together, patients with normal LVEF but a positive AI-ECG may represent a high-risk group with cardiac abnormalities, and further evaluation with an echocardiographic study can reveal abnormalities of cardiac structure or function in most cases and help further stratify patients’ risk. Given its association with future low LVEF and worse survival, a FP AI-ECG score for low LVEF may be considered to reflect stage B heart failure. These patients may benefit from aggressive risk factor modification and closer follow-up. Future studies investigating the potential role of certain medications such as sodium glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors, beta blockers, renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, and aldosterone receptor blockers to improve outcomes in these patients are needed.

Study Limitations

This was a retrospective study with variable follow-up intervals between patients. All patients had echocardiography performed for a clinical indication, limiting generalizability of results. Some of the Mayo Clinic sites are tertiary centers with referral bias of likely sicker patients than a community setting. Furthermore, as these patients had an indication for a clinical echocardiogram, they are sicker than the general population, as noted with excess mortality. ECGs and echocardiograms were not obtained simultaneously, and cardiac events occurring between the 2 tests could not be completely excluded. However, 77% of the ECGs were performed within 1 day of the echocardiograms and 87% were within 2 days. Manual confirmation of ECGs described as normal was not practically feasible given the large number of patients in the study. Only routinely obtained echocardiographic parameters were analyzed, and future studies are needed to evaluate the association of abnormal AI-ECG with abnormal global longitudinal strain values and other echocardiographic results. ICD codes were used to identify comorbidities; therefore, it is possible that the burden of comorbidities may have been underestimated. Our analysis focused on all-cause mortality as the outcome of interest given the inability to accurately ascertain cause of death retrospectively.20 Notably, the goal of the current analysis was to evaluate the implications of a positive AI ECG for low LVEF despite a normal LVEF on echocardiography with the finding that these patients had excess mortality risk compared to patients with negative AI ECG and normal LVEF. Not surprisingly, there are AI ECG models that were specifically trained to identify patients at highest risk of worse outcomes with stronger performance in this task.21 Finally, the AI ECG model for low LVEF utilized in this study was developed at Mayo Clinic. The performance outside the Mayo Clinic Health System needs further evaluation.

Conclusions

Patients with normal LVEF by echocardiography but FP AI-ECG for low LVEF had more echocardiographic abnormalities than patients with normal LVEF and TN AI-ECG. Most FP patients (∼97%) had abnormal echocardiographic findings. Importantly, a FP AI-ECG was independently associated with increased mortality, and the concomitant presence of echocardiographic abnormalities indicated an even higher mortality risk than if the echocardiogram was normal. Our findings indicate that the concomitant use of AI ECG and echocardiography helps in stratifying risk in patients with normal LVEF. Whether these patients may benefit from preventive strategies and aggressive risk factor modification needs further investigation.

COMPETENCY IN MEDICAL KNOWLEDGE: Around 97% patients with normal EF but false positive AI ECG for low EF had other echocardiographic abnormalities. Patients with false positive AI ECG had increased mortality risk compared to true negative patients with normal EF and AI ECG. An abnormal echocardiogram in false positive patients conferred additional mortality risk compared to false positive patients with normal echocardiogram. Notably, however, false positive patients who had normal echocardiograms had comparable mortality risk to true negative patients with abnormal echocardiograms, and true negative patients with normal echocardiography had the lowest mortality risk.

TRANSLATIONAL OUTLOOK: The concomitant use of AI ECG and echocardiography helps in stratifying risk in patients with normal LVEF. Abnormal echocardiography, even when the LVEF is normal, is associated with increased mortality risk in false positive patients as compared to normal echocardiography. However, false positive AI ECG still confers additional mortality risk even in patients with normal echocardiography compared to true negatives. Whether false positive patients may benefit from preventive strategies needs further investigation.
