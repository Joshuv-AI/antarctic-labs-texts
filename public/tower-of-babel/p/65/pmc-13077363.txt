Association between glycemic variability and all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis-associated acute respiratory distress syndrome: a retrospective study based on the MIMIC database
Authors: Qihuan Ma, Juan Guo, Youjing Yang, Yanmei Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBoth sepsis and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) are related to high mortality. Previous studies have demonstrated that glycemic variability (GV) is significantly associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill patients, including those with sepsis. However, no study has specifically examined the impact of GV on mortality in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. Our study retrospectively analyzed the relation of GV to all-cause mortality (ACM) in this patient population.MethodsClinical data of the patients with sepsis-associated ARDS were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV database. GV was calculated, and the link of GV to ACM was examined through Kaplan-Meier (KM) survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression (CPHR) models. The nonlinear association between GV and mortality risk, along with the identification of a potential risk threshold, was assessed via restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves.ResultsThere were 4,203 patients with sepsis-associated ARDS included in this study. KM analysis revealed significantly higher mortality in the high-GV group than in the low-GV group. CPHR analysis showed that increasing GV was independently related to a higher mortality risk in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, and the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) of 28-day ACM was 1.009 (95% confidence interval (CI): 1.006–1.012). RCS modeling demonstrated a marked nonlinear relation of GV to death, with mortality risk increasing markedly when GV exceeded 21%. Subgroup analyses indicated significant interactions between GV and both age and furosemide use on 28-day ACM. The effect of GV on death was more prominent in patients who did not receive furosemide and in those younger than 60, and the HRs were 1.017 (95% CI: 1.011–1.023) and 1.012 (95% CI: 1.007–1.018).ConclusionsElevated GV is significantly related to increased ACM in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. GV may serve as a potential prognostic indicator reflecting disease severity and metabolic instability in this population, thereby facilitating risk stratification.

Introduction

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection (1,2), represents one of the major global health threats. A study estimating the global burden of sepsis reported 48.9 million incident sepsis cases and 11 million sepsis-related deaths worldwide (3). A systematic review and meta-analysis estimated an annual incidence of 189 per 100,000 people, with a death rate of 26.7%. Among patients with sepsis admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), mortality was reported to be as high as 41.9% (4). Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is an acute, diffuse, inflammatory lung injury precipitated by a predisposing risk factor, such as pneumonia, nonpulmonary infection, trauma, transfusion, burn, aspiration, or shock, excluding cardiogenic pulmonary edema (5). ARDS is characterized by both high morbidity and mortality, accounting for approximately 10.4% of ICU admissions, with mortality in severe cases reaching 45% (6-9). In comparison to patients with sepsis or ARDS alone, those with sepsis-associated ARDS exhibit substantially higher mortality (10-12). Thus, the early identification of prognostic markers and the development of preventive strategies are of critical importance for patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.

Disorders of glucose metabolism are common pathophysiological conditions in critically ill patients (13-15). Hyperglycemia has been shown to exert adverse effects on outcomes in this population (16-18). However, strict glycemic control regimens carry a considerable risk of hypoglycemia, which itself is related to poor prognosis (19,20). Glycemic variability (GV), the degree of fluctuation in blood glucose levels over a period of time, is an important indicator for assessing the quality of glycemic control (21,22). In recent years, with advances in continuous glucose monitoring and the concept of refined glycemic management, increasing attention has been directed toward GV. Some studies have proposed that optimization of insulin infusion and nutritional support strategies—such as continuous feeding insulin injection (CFII), may help reduce glycemic fluctuations in ICU patients and thereby improve glucose stability (23). Recent studies have demonstrated that GV is related to adverse outcomes across varied conditions like sepsis, cerebrovascular disease, cardiovascular disease, acute kidney injury (AKI), and acute severe pancreatitis (22,24-27). Chao et al. demonstrated that early GV in patients with sepsis was significantly associated with mortality risk, suggesting that GV may possess potential prognostic value (28). However, evidence regarding the prognostic role of GV in sepsis-associated ARDS remains limited. Accordingly, a retrospective study was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC) database to unravel the relation of GV to all-cause mortality (ACM) in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. These findings may provide important evidence for the early identification of unfavorable prognostic indicators in this high-risk population and contribute to more effective risk stratification. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-aw-2414/rc).

Methods

Data source and patient selection

This retrospective study leveraged MIMIC-IV v3.1 (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1/). The database includes records for 94,458 ICU stays involving 65,366 patients receiving treatment at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston from 2008 to 2022. It provides extensive data on patient demographics, vital signs, laboratory results, and diagnostic information. One investigator completed the required training course of the collaborative institution, obtained access, and extracted pertinent data. As MIMIC-IV is an open, de-identified database, institutional review board approval was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Patients with sepsis-associated ARDS were identified according to the Sepsis-3.0 criteria and the Berlin definition. Sepsis was diagnosed based on the Sepsis-3.0 criteria: Organ dysfunction can be identified as an acute change in total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score ≥2 points consequent to the infection. ARDS was defined according to the Berlin criteria: (I) acute onset of respiratory symptoms; (II) bilateral opacities on chest imaging; (III) ratio of arterial oxygen partial pressure to fractional inspired oxygen (PaO2/FiO2) <300 mmHg with a minimum positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) ≥5 cmH2O; and (IV) no cardiac failure.

The exclusion criteria were: (I) ICU length of stay <24 hours; (II) age <18 years; (III) fewer than three blood glucose measurements in the ICU; (IV) missing data exceeding 20%; (V) extreme outlier values in the dataset; and (VI) only the initial ICU admission was analyzed for patients with multiple stays. Data extraction

Data were extracted via PostgreSQL 17.2-3 with structured query language. The baseline variables included: (I) demographics: gender, age, and race; (II) vital signs: respiratory rate (RR), heart rate, and PaO2/FiO2; (III) laboratory parameters: glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (HB), hematocrit (Hct), platelet count (PLT), bicarbonate (HCO3−), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), prothrombin time (PT), international normalized ratio (INR), potassium, and sodium; (IV) clinical severity scores: SOFA, Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Acute Physiology Score III (APS III), and Oxford Acute Severity of Illness Score (OASIS); (V) comorbidities diagnosed according to ICD-9 or ICD-10, including septic shock, myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF), malignant cancer, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and chronic pulmonary disease; (VI) therapeutic interventions: enteral nutrition, parenteral nutrition, corticosteroid, furosemide administration, mechanical ventilation (MV), renal replacement therapy (RRT) and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT). All vital signs, lab parameters, and clinical severity scores were obtained from the first measurements within the first 24 hours after ICU admission.

In addition, all blood glucose values in the ICU were extracted. GV is calculated as the coefficient of variation (CV) using the formula: [standard deviation (SD) of glucose/mean glucose]×100%. Patients were then stratified into tertiles according to GV: T1 (GV <17.267%), T2 (GV 17.267–27.218%), and T3 (GV >27.218%).

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome was 28-day ACM during ICU stay. Secondary outcomes encompassed 7-day and 14-day ACM during ICU stay.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as mean ± SD for normally distributed continuous variables and median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables. Between-group comparisons were conducted through analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal-Wallis test based on the data distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages) and compared via Fisher’s exact or Pearson’s chi-squared test. HbA1c was excluded due to a missing rate exceeding 20%. The proportion of patients receiving parenteral nutrition was very low (<1%). Therefore, this variable was excluded from the analysis. Variables with a missing rate below 20% were imputed after multiple imputation via the mice package. Multicollinearity among variables was assessed, and covariates with severe multicollinearity were excluded.

Patients were stratified according to tertiles of GV. Temporal trends in survival and differences among the GV groups were examined via Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves. The relation of GV to 7-day, 14-day, and 28-day ACM among patients with sepsis-associated ARDS was explored through Cox proportional hazards regression (CPHR) models. GV was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable according to tertiles. Three sequential models were created: Model 1 was crude; Model 2 involved adjustment of demographic characteristics (age, gender, and race); Model 3 was further adjusted for demographics, vital signs (RR, heart rate), laboratory parameters (WBC, HB, PLT, HCO3−, Scr, BUN, PT, potassium, sodium), comorbidities (MI, AF, malignant cancer, diabetes, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic pulmonary disease), and therapeutic interventions (furosemide, MV, RRT, and CRRT). Potential non-linear relation of GV to outcomes was assessed via restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses. Stratified analyses were carried out across subgroups by gender, age, race, comorbidities, and therapeutic interventions to evaluate the robustness of the findings and potential interactions between these covariates and GV. The hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the subgroup analyses were presented in a forest plot. In addition, sensitivity analyses were performed. On the basis of Model 3, further adjustments were made for septic shock, enteral nutrition, corticosteroid use and PaO2/FiO2 to construct Model 4, to examine the robustness of the association between GV and clinical outcomes.

All data cleaning and statistical analyses were conducted via R (RStudio v4.4.1). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value <0.05.

Patients were stratified into tertiles according to GV (Table 1). In contrast to the low GV group (T1), the high GV group (T3) had an older median age, a lower proportion of White patients, and higher proportions of females as well as patients of Asian, Black, and other races. T3 tended to receive furosemide, MV, and RRT and exhibited higher rates of comorbid diabetes and hyperlipidemia. Clinical severity scores, like SOFA, OASIS, and APS III, were markedly higher in T3 (P<0.05).

Subgroup analysis

Stratified analyses were conducted across subgroups defined by gender, age, race, comorbidities, and therapeutic interventions to evaluate the robustness of the study findings and to explore possible interactions between these covariates and GV (Figure 4). In the subgroups of male and female patients, White individuals and other races excluding Asian and Black populations, and patients without MI, chronic pulmonary disease, or diabetes with complication and comorbidity, GV was significantly positively related to 28-day ACM (P<0.05), consistent with the overall group. Conversely, in the subgroups of Asian and Black patients, those with MI, chronic pulmonary disease, diabetes with complication or comorbidity, this association was statistically insignificant (P>0.05). Significant interactions were not noted between these covariates and GV (P for interaction >0.05).

Forest plots for GV with 28-day ACM. ACM, all-cause mortality; CC, complication and comorbidity; CI, confidence interval; GV, glycemic variability; HR, hazard ratio.

Among patients who did not receive furosemide, GV was significantly positively related to 28-day ACM (HR =1.017, 95% CI: 1.011–1.023, P<0.001), but among those receiving furosemide, the association between GV and 28-day ACM was not significant (P>0.05). A significant interaction between furosemide use and GV on 28-day ACM was observed (P for interaction <0.001).

In patients aged <60 years, GV was significantly positively related to 28-day ACM (HR =1.012, 95% CI: 1.007–1.018, P<0.001). In patients aged ≥60 years, GV remained significantly related to 28-day ACM (HR =1.009, 95% CI: 1.005–1.012, P<0.001), though the HR was higher in the younger subgroup. A significant interaction between age and GV on 28-day ACM was identified (P for interaction =0.01).

Sensitivity analysis

Based on Model 3, Model 4 was additionally adjusted for septic shock, enteral nutrition, corticosteroid use, and PaO2/FiO2 to test the robustness of the association between GV and outcomes. In Model 4 (HR =1.007, 95% CI: 1.003–1.010, P<0.001), an increase in GV was associated with a significantly higher risk of 28-day ACM in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. When GV was analyzed by tertiles (T1: GV <17.267%; T2: GV 17.267–27.218%; T3: GV >27.218%), with T1 as the reference, the risk of 28-day ACM in T3 was markedly higher in Model 4 (T2: HR =1.083, 95% CI: 0.918–1.278, P=0.35; T3: HR =1.468, 95% CI: 1.246–1.729, P<0.001) (Table S1).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between GV and ACM in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. The results indicate that higher GV levels are significantly associated with adverse outcomes in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, with patients exhibiting high GV having a substantially increased risk of mortality. This association remained robust after adjustment for multiple potential confounding factors. A nonlinear relation was noted between GV and mortality, with risk rising when GV exceeded 21%. Subgroup analyses revealed significant interactions between furosemide use, age, and GV on 28-day ACM. The association between GV and mortality was more pronounced among patients who did not receive furosemide and among those younger than 60 years.

In comparison to patients with non-sepsis-associated ARDS, those with sepsis-associated ARDS exhibit distinct demographic characteristics, laboratory profiles, and comorbidities, with higher disease severity, lower extubation success rates, and higher mortality (29). Early identification of poor prognostic indicators in sepsis-associated ARDS may facilitate risk stratification and individualized management. Prior studies have identified several potential prognostic markers in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. For instance, elevated levels of renin are related to increased mortality (30), and plasma presepsin is linked to higher mortality (31). Wang et al. demonstrated that the high-lactylation group exhibited higher 28-day and 1-year mortality than the low-lactylation group, identifying six potential prognostic biomarkers (ALDOB, CCT5, EP300, PFKP, PPIA, and SIRT1) (32). Additionally, both lactate/albumin ratio (LAR) and procalcitonin/albumin ratio have been shown to predict mortality risk in sepsis-associated ARDS, with increasing values related to higher risk (33,34). However, renin and presepsin are not routinely available in outpatient or low-tier clinical settings; biomarkers like ALDOB remain under validation, and frequent venous sampling is required for lactate, albumin, and procalcitonin measurement. Blood glucose, in contrast, is commonly measured via fingertip sampling, allowing for straightforward calculation of GV. Previous studies have demonstrated the prognostic relevance of GV in various diseases (15,22,24-27,35,36). In sepsis, GV has been related to ACM (28,37), with elevated risk in patients with comorbid diabetes, septic shock, MI, or congestive heart failure (24). Admission hyperglycemia is also an independent ARDS mortality risk factor (29), and high GV is related to increased ACM in COVID-19 patients with ARDS (38). Prior research has predominantly focused on GV in isolated sepsis or ARDS populations, with limited investigation into its association with mortality in sepsis-associated ARDS. This study may provide useful references for the clinical characterization and risk stratification of patients with sepsis-associated ARDS.

This study demonstrated a positive relation of GV to ACM in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. With increasing GV, the risk of 28-day, 14-day, and 7-day ACM rose accordingly. After adjusting for potential confounders, the impact of GV on mortality was attenuated but remained positively related, which suggests that GV is an indicator reflecting disease severity in this population, and that higher GV levels are significantly associated with adverse outcomes, with patients exhibiting high GV having an increased risk of death. The pathogenesis of ARDS involves oxidative stress and injury to alveolar epithelium and capillary endothelium (39), whereas the pathophysiology of sepsis also involves oxidative stress (1,2). Previous studies have shown that experimentally induced high GV causes more pronounced oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction than sustained hyperglycemia (40), which implies that elevated GV may reflect more severe oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, thereby indicating an unfavorable prognosis.

Our findings further indicate that the relation of GV to mortality was more pronounced in those who did not receive furosemide. It should be noted that the use of furosemide itself may reflect differences in volume status, organ function, or overall disease severity. Therefore, the observed interaction may be influenced by confounding by indication rather than indicating a true modifying effect of furosemide on the association between GV and mortality risk. Prior research has shown that early implementation of conservative fluid management strategies with diuretics like furosemide is related to lower mortality (41). In ARDS patients primarily caused by sepsis, mortality in the conservative fluid management group was lower than in the liberal fluid management group (42). However, since fluid management strategies were not directly evaluated in this study, these findings are exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature and require confirmation in future prospective interventional studies. Our study also identified a statistically significant interaction between age and GV with respect to mortality in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. Specifically, the strength of the association between GV and mortality risk was weaker in older patients (≥60 years) than in younger patients (<60 years). This finding suggests that the magnitude of the association between GV and mortality may differ across age strata in critically ill populations. Previous studies involving sepsis and critically ill patient groups similarly reported an interaction between age and GV, wherein high GV had a smaller effect on mortality among patients aged ≥65 years in comparison to those <65 years. These studies suggested that this may be due to higher prevalence of severe comorbidities like cardiovascular disease and diabetes and associated medication use in older patients, which partially obscures the impact of GV on mortality (24,43). Some studies have proposed explanations based on adaptive responses to oxidative stress (44); however, these mechanistic interpretations remain speculative, and the subgroup analyses presented here are exploratory and hypothesis-generating, requiring validation through prospective interventional studies.

Using an RCS curve, our study revealed that when GV exceeded 21%, the risk of mortality increased progressively with rising GV in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. These findings indicate that higher GV levels are associated with poorer prognosis in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. Importantly, GV cutoff values may vary across populations and clinical contexts. Current international consensus recommends a GV cutoff value of 36% (45), whereas other studies have reported a cutoff of approximately 33% in Chinese patients with diabetes based on continuous glucose monitoring data (46). These observations suggest that GV thresholds may be population- and disease-context dependent and should be interpreted with caution when applied to specific populations such as patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. Notably, these cutoff values are derived from retrospective observational studies and are exploratory and hypothesis-generating; they should not be interpreted as definitive clinical intervention thresholds. Whether interventions targeting GV can improve patient outcomes remains to be determined and requires further validation in prospective interventional studies. In clinical practice, glycemic management should continue to focus on minimizing marked glucose fluctuations while integrating individualized considerations of overall disease severity, nutritional support, and therapeutic strategies.

There are limitations in this study. First, it is single-center and retrospective, and the external validity of the findings is limited. Further prospective, multicenter studies are necessary to confirm our observations. Second, as a retrospective study, selection bias is inevitable. Although possible confounders were adjusted, unmeasured confounding factors may still exist due to limitations inherent in the MIMIC database. For example, detailed insulin treatment information (including dosage, administration routes, and adjustment strategies) and intermediate outcomes (including organ dysfunction trajectories, ventilator-free days, and progression of AKI) could not be comprehensively obtained in this study. In addition, data on parenteral nutrition and HbA1c were largely missing, precluding robust statistical analyses. These limitations restrict a comprehensive assessment of insulin therapy, intermediate outcomes, nutritional status, and recent glycemic control, potentially introducing bias and affecting the interpretation of the association between GV and prognosis. Furthermore, GV in this study was calculated based on intermittent glucose measurements rather than continuous glucose monitoring. The frequency of glucose measurements was not standardized across patients, and those with more severe illness were more likely to undergo frequent monitoring. This may introduce monitoring intensity bias, whereby higher GV partially reflects differences in monitoring practices related to disease severity rather than true biological glucose fluctuations. Although indicators of disease severity, such as oxygenation index and septic shock, were included in multivariable models, residual confounding related to illness severity cannot be entirely excluded. Therefore, the observed association between GV and mortality should be interpreted primarily as an independent association rather than a causal relationship. Future prospective studies incorporating standardized glucose monitoring protocols or continuous glucose monitoring, along with systematic inclusion of key treatment-related variables and clinically relevant intermediate outcomes, are warranted to further validate the robustness of these findings and clarify their potential clinical implications.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that high GV is significantly related to ACM in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS. GV may serve as a potential prognostically relevant indicator reflecting disease severity and metabolic instability in patients with sepsis-associated ARDS, and may aid in risk stratification within this population.
