Spatial Distribution and Determinants of Fertility in India
Authors: Pradeep Deshmukh, Nishaant Ramasamy, Priyanka Yadav, Yogesh Bahurupi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:India has witnessed a fall in fertility rates, but there are regional variations. Spatial planning requires an understanding of the spatial pattern of fertility rates and the factors affecting them. This research was undertaken with the objective of understanding the spatial pattern of total fertility rates and socioeconomic factors in India.Materials and Methods:This ecological study used state-level data from the National Family Health Survey-5 (2019–2021). TFR was the outcome variable. Explanatory variables included early marriage, female literacy, women’s workforce participation, urbanization, poverty, and the multidimensional poverty index (MPI). Exploratory spatial data analysis assessed global spatial autocorrelation using Moran’s I and identified clusters using Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA). Spatial regression models—ordinary least squares (OLS), spatial lag (SLM), and spatial error (SEM)—examined associations while accounting for spatial dependence.Results:TFR ranged from 1.1 to 3.0 across states (mean 1.81). Global spatial autocorrelation was weak (Moran’s I = 0.020). LISA analysis identified high-fertility clusters in Bihar, Jharkhand, and Assam and low-fertility clusters in Tamil Nadu and Kerala. MPI was the only significant predictor of TFR. In OLS, a one-unit increase in MPI was associated with a 9.443 increase in TFR (P < 0.001); similar associations were observed in SLM (β = 9.309, P < 0.001) and SEM (β = 9.174, P < 0.001).Conclusion:India has shown declining fertility rates with localized and regional variations. The need for addressing poverty reduction and reproductive health together has been emphasized by the fact that multidimensional poverty still remains the largest predictor of increasing fertility.

INTRODUCTION

Fertility is a key fundamental driver of population dynamics, significantly affecting socioeconomic development, public health, and policy planning globally.[1] Many countries have experienced fertility decline due to improved education, access to contraception, and overall socioeconomic advancement.[2] Currently, India is the most populous country experiencing demographic transition. Though the overall total fertility rate (TFR) has declined to 2.0, however, fertility rates remain uneven across its various regions.[3] Understanding the spatial and contextual variations is essential for effective planning and implementation of demographic policies at national and subnational levels.

Fertility differences across various states in India impact the population growth, demographic structure, and the likely demographic dividend.[4] These differences are also closely associated with maternal and child health, literacy level, women’s empowerment, and household well-being.[5] To achieve the desired Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) for India, especially those related to health (SDG 3) and gender equality (SDG 5), it is mandatory to have an in-depth understanding of fertility disparities across these states.

There is a noticeable regional variation in fertility rates across India, such as below-replacement levels in parts of the Southern states, in contrast with persistently high rates in the Northern and Northeastern states.[6] For instance, states, like Kerala, Maharashtra, and Tamil Nadu, have demonstrated a consistent reduction in fertility rate. The variations across states often reflect a complex web of socioeconomic, cultural, and programmatic differences and suggest the need for geographically targeted interventions. Fertility outcomes are influenced by a range of social determinants of health, including education, wealth, age at marriage, son preference, and media exposure, often operating in a context-specific manner.[78]

The National Family Health Survey-5 (NFHS-5, 2019–2021) offers comprehensive, nationally representative data on fertility patterns and their determinants.[6] We performed spatial analysis for geographical variations and spatial regression modelling to generate evidence to guide targeted, equity-focused reproductive health policies across India.

METHODOLOGY

Study area

Data for this article were obtained from NFHS 5, which was conducted across 28 states and eight Union Territories of India. Considering India’s federal structure and implementation of health governance at the state level, the NFHS-5 data provide an appropriate framework for analyzing spatial patterns and determinants of fertility at the subnational level.

Variables and data sources

This study used nationally representative, government-verified datasets for reliable and comparable results.

Total fertility rate (TFR)

TFR is defined as “The average number of children a woman would have by the end of her childbearing years if she bore children at the current age-specific fertility rates. Age-specific fertility rates are calculated for the three years before the survey, based on detailed birth histories provided by women.” This indicator was obtained from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5), 2019–21, conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW).[69]

Married before age 18 years (women age 20–24 years)

This variable represents the percentage of women aged between 20 and 24 years who were first married or in union before their 18th birthday, as reported in the NFHS-5 (2019–21). It reflects social norms and the timing of family formation at the population level.[69]

Female literacy

“Respondents who have completed standard nine or higher are assumed to be literate. All other respondents were given a sentence to read, and they were considered to be literate if they could read all or part of the sentence”, as reported in NFHS-5. This measure is critical for assessing women’s access to education and empowerment.[69]

Women in the workforce—worker population ratio

“Worker Population Ratio (WPR) is the percentage of persons employed among the persons in the population.” It is calculated for the 15 years and above population. The data were sourced from the Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS) Annual Report 2020–21, as cited in the Press Information Bureau (PIB) release by the Ministry of Labour and Employment.[1011]

Urbanization

Urbanization is measured as the percentage of a state’s population residing in urban areas, based on definitions used by the Census of India. The data were extracted from the Open Government Data (OGD) Platform India—Urban proportion, which compiles state-level urbanization rates using Census 2011 data.[12]

Below poverty level (BPL)

“The poverty line has been expressed in terms of Monthly Per Capita Consumption Expenditure (MPCE) based on Mixed Reference Period” based on Tendulkar Methodology.[13]

Multidimensional poverty index (MPI)

The MPI captures simultaneous deprivations across three dimensions—health, education, and standard of living—using 12 indicators, such as “nutrition, child mortality, maternal health, years of schooling, school attendance, cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, housing, electricity, assets and bank account.” A household is considered multidimensionally poor if it is deprived in at least one-third of the weighted indicators. The MPI data were sourced from NITI Aayog’s National Multidimensional Poverty Index: A Progress Review 2023, developed in collaboration with Oxford Poverty and Human Development Initiative (OPHI) and UNDP India.[1415]

Conceptual framework

The conceptual framework for this analysis is grounded in demographic transition theory, Bongaarts’ model of proximate determinants of fertility, and the social determinants of health framework [Figure 1].

Conceptual framework of fertility analysis

Fertility, measured by the total fertility rate (TFR), is influenced by structural factors, such as education, employment, urbanization, and multidimensional poverty, which shape proximate determinants like marriage before 18 years and contraceptive behavior.[816]

Women married before 18 years directly indicate the increased proportion of women who are exposed early to childbearing, while female literacy enhances reproductive autonomy and decision-making. Women’s workforce participation introduces opportunity costs to childbearing, often leading to lower fertility. Urbanization tends to reduce fertility through improved access to health services and evolving family norms. The multidimensional poverty index (MPI) reflects overlapping deprivations in health, education, and living standards, which restrict access to reproductive choices and contribute to higher fertility levels. Spatial factors are also relevant, as fertility behaviors often cluster geographically due to diffusion of norms or programmatic similarities. These interlinked influences justify the use of spatial econometric models to capture both socioeconomic and geographic determinants of fertility.

Statistical analysis

To explore the spatial distribution and clustering of fertility rates across India, we used state-level fertility data from the NFHS-5.

Exploratory spatial data analysis (ESDA)

First, we carried out ESDA. This involved mapping the geographical distribution of fertility rates to identify spatial patterns using quintile distributions visually. To quantify global spatial autocorrelation, we calculated Moran’s I statistic[17] to determine whether the observed spatial pattern of fertility was clustered, dispersed, or random across the study area.

A Moran’s scatter plot was used to visualize the relationship between fertility rates in a given state and the average fertility rates of its neighboring states.

Local indicators of spatial association (LISA)

Second, to identify the precise locations of statistically significant spatial clusters of high or low fertility rates, we used Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA), specifically Anselin’s Local Moran’s I.[18] LISA delineated hotspot areas (High–High clusters, where other high fertility regions surround high fertility regions) and coldspot areas (Low–Low clusters, where other low fertility regions surround low fertility regions). It also identified spatial outliers (High–Low or Low–High clusters).

LISA cluster maps were generated to visually represent these significant local spatial patterns, at a significance level of P < 0.05. We used a distance-based spatial weights matrix, which defined “neighbourhood” relationships.

Spatial regression models

Third, to understand the relationship between fertility and its determinants while accounting for spatial dependencies, spatial regression models were considered.

Standard ordinary least-squares (OLS) regression assumes independence of observations, an assumption often violated in spatial data due to autocorrelation. Therefore, models like the spatial lag model (SLM), which incorporates a spatially lagged dependent variable, or the spatial error model (SEM), which accounts for spatial autocorrelation in the error terms, were evaluated.[19]

Lagrange multiplier test for spatial lag and spatial error dependence was used to determine the most appropriate spatial regression model. Model fit was assessed using Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC), alongside R-squared values.

These models helped in identifying significant socioeconomic (proportion of people below poverty line, MPI), demographic (percentage of women married before 18 years, percentage of female literacy, proportion of people living in urban areas), and programmatic determinants (proportion of women formally engaged in workforce) of fertility while controlling for spatial effects.

The GeoDa software package was used for these spatial analyses.[20]

RESULTS

Exploratory spatial data analysis

The boxplot analysis of total fertility rate (TFR) across Indian states shows a range from 1.1 to 3.0, with a median of 1.8 and a mean of 1.807, indicating a nearly symmetric distribution. The interquartile range is narrow (Q1: 1.7, Q3: 1.9; IQR: 0.2), suggesting low variability among most states. However, several high outliers (TFR > 2.3) introduce mild positive skewness, representing states, such as Bihar and Uttar Pradesh, which have high fertility rates.

A few states also fall below 1.4, indicating early demographic transition. These findings highlight regional disparities, with most states experiencing fertility rates near or below replacement level, warranting targeted interventions in high-TFR regions and informed demographic planning in low-TFR states [Figure 2].

Boxplot showing the distribution of TFR across the states of India

The quintile choropleth of total fertility rate (TFR) across Indian states highlights distinct regional disparities. Seven states, mainly in the south and west (e.g. Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Goa), have very low TFRs (1.1–1.5), indicating an advanced demographic transition. Three states report a TFR of 1.6, while the majority (17 states) fall in the 1.7–1.8 range, reflecting national averages. Seven states each fall into the 1.9 and 2.0–3.0 categories, with the highest TFRs concentrated in the north, central, and northeast regions, including Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, and Meghalaya [Figure 3].

Quintile distribution of TFR across the states of India

The Moran’s I value for total fertility rate (TFR) across Indian states is 0.020, indicating negligible global spatial autocorrelation. This suggests that TFR values are randomly distributed across the states, with no significant clustering of similar fertility levels among neighboring regions. The scatterplot confirms this with a near-flat trend line and dispersed points, implying that local rather than regional factors likely influence fertility patterns [Figure 4].

Moran’s I scatter plot showing global clustering of TFR

The LISA cluster map for total fertility rate (TFR) reveals spatial clustering in specific regions of India. Five states—Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Assam, and Arunachal Pradesh—form significant High–High clusters, indicating high TFR values surrounded by similarly high TFR neighbors.

In contrast, Low–Low clusters are observed in Tamil Nadu and Kerala, suggesting spatial concentration of low fertility. Additionally, seven states exhibit Low–High outlier patterns—low TFR surrounded by high TFR neighbors—indicating spatial heterogeneity. No states fall under the High–Low category. Most states (27) show no significant local spatial association [Figure 5].

LISA showing cluster map

The LISA significance map for total fertility rate (TFR) shows that 14 Indian states exhibit significant local spatial autocorrelation—11 at P < 0.05 and 3 at P < 0.01—primarily in the east, northeast, and south, such as West Bengal, Odisha, and Tamil Nadu.

The remaining 27 states show no significant clustering. This indicates the presence of localized spatial patterns in fertility, despite an overall weak national spatial autocorrelation [Figure 6].

Across all models, the multidimensional poverty index (MPI) emerged as a statistically significant and positive predictor of TFR. In the OLS model, a one-unit increase in MPI was associated with a 9.443-point increase in TFR (SE = 1.497, P<0.001). This relationship remained robust in the spatial models: 9.309 (SE = 1.279, P<0.001) in the SLM and 9.174 (SE = 0.514, P<0.001) in the SEM.

Other independent variables (percentage married before 18 years, female literacy, women workforce participation, urban population, and proportion below poverty line (BPL)) did not show statistically significant associations with TFR in any of the models. However, the direction and magnitude of coefficients remained generally consistent across specifications.

In the SLM, the spatial lag parameter (ρ) was statistically significant and negative (ρ = –0.735, SE = 0.259, P < 0.05), indicating significant spatial dependence in TFR across neighboring states. In contrast, the spatial error coefficient (λ) in the SEM was negative but not statistically significant (λ = –0.610, SE = 0.514), suggesting weaker spatial autocorrelation in the error terms.

Model fit statistics support the use of spatial regression approaches. The spatial error model had the lowest AIC (–8.108) and BIC (3.887) values, compared to the OLS (AIC = –2.767, BIC = 9.227) and the SLM (AIC = –4.986, BIC = 8.721), indicating a better fit with a lower penalty for model complexity. All three models explained a substantial proportion of the variance in TFR, with R² values of 0.701 (OLS), 0.736 (SLM), and 0.732 (SEM).

Overall, the results highlight the central role of multidimensional poverty in shaping fertility levels in Indian states, with some evidence of spatial dependence that is better captured by spatial models, particularly the spatial error model, which demonstrates a superior model fit.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative spatial weights (distance-based, Queen contiguity, and Rook contiguity). Across all models (OLS, spatial lag, and spatial error), the direction and magnitude of the key covariates were largely stable. MPI consistently showed a strong positive association with TFR across all specifications (P < 0.001). Other sociodemographic variables remained nonsignificant across all weights, indicating that their effects are not sensitive to the choice of spatial structure. Spatial dependence (ρ/λ) changed in sign and magnitude across weights, but this did not alter the substantive conclusions. These results show that the main findings of the study are robust to alternative spatial weighting types [Supplementary Tables 1 and 2].

DISCUSSION

This study examined the spatial variation and socioeconomic determinants of fertility across Indian states and Union Territories. The multidimensional poverty index (MPI) is the only consistent and significant predictor of total fertility rate across all models. Other variables, like married before 18 years, female literacy, women in the workforce, urbanization, and below poverty line, did not show significant associations. Spatial models demonstrated an improvement over the ordinary least-squares (OLS) approach, providing evidence of interstate diffusion in fertility behaviors. Although variables, like female literacy, early marriage, and urbanization, were conceptually relevant, they did not emerge as statistically significant in any model. This suggests that their effects may be indirectly mediated through broader structural poverty, as captured by the MPI. Moreover, at the state level, such variables may lack the resolution necessary to capture intrastate variations or cultural nuances influencing fertility behaviors. Future analyses using substate or district-level data could better capture these localized effects.

Our findings confirm the observations that regional disparities in fertility in India are deeply rooted in structural and programmatic inequities.[45] Earlier studies indicate that fertility rates have dropped unevenly: southern and western states are below replacement level, while parts of the north and east still have high fertility rates.[13] Our findings confirm spatially localized patterns, with high-fertility clusters in Bihar, Jharkhand, and Assam, and low-fertility clusters in Tamil Nadu and Kerala.

The importance of MPI is supported by Bongaarts[8] and Cleland,[21] who suggested that fertility is more sensitive to overall socioeconomic deprivation than specific single factors like education or urban residence. Unlike past nonspatial studies,[22] literacy and urbanization are not significant, possibly because state-level data and spatial autocorrelation adjustment reveal their effects mainly reflect broader poverty.

The uneven success of fertility reduction across states can be attributed to variations in health system capacity, governance, and community-level demand generation. Southern states, such as Kerala and Tamil Nadu, succeeded due to early investments in female education, functional primary health systems, and sustained political commitment to reproductive health.[2324] Tamil Nadu’s decentralized sterilization services and reliable contraceptive supply through trained nurses contributed to high contraceptive prevalence and rapid fertility decline.[25] Contrary to this, states, like Bihar, Uttar Pradesh, and Jharkhand, often experience consistent shortages of human resources, inadequate contraceptive supply chains, and limited outreach. Facility assessment in Bihar documented recurrent stock-outs and low readiness to deliver family planning services.[26] Certain sociocultural barriers, like early marriage, preference for male child, and low female autonomy, further hinder contraceptive uptake, even where services are available.[27] In high-fertility states, front-line workers often prioritize maternal incentive schemes over family planning counselling, leading to a reduction in demand-generation efforts.[28]

The findings have several important programmatic implications. First, the consistent association between multidimensional poverty and fertility underscores the need to integrate family planning with poverty alleviation strategies. Programs must address determinants such as education, nutrition, and economic insecurity, especially in high-fertility states.[121]

Second, the spatial clustering of high fertility in states, such as Bihar, Jharkhand, and Assam, calls for region-specific, decentralized focused interventions. National programs, like Mission Parivar Vikas, must be tailored to local contexts and needs since uniform strategies have demonstrated limited effect in socioculturally diverse and disadvantaged settings.[2829] Localized micro-planning, supported by geospatial dashboards, can help identify fertility hotspots and optimize resource allocation.[30]

Third, robust health system readiness in high fertility rates states is essential. This includes uninterrupted contraceptive supplies, a full method mix, and adequate human resources. Tamil Nadu’s decentralized distribution and health worker engagement offer a replicable model.[2325]

Spatial diffusion effects indicate that fertility rates in one state can affect neighboring regions. Coordinating and collaborating efforts and adopting successful approaches from states, like Kerala and Tamil Nadu, may assist fertility trends more rapidly.[25]

Finally, behavior change interventions must be culturally tailored and gender sensitive. In contexts characterized by a preference for male children, early marriage, and restricted female autonomy, community-based strategies, male engagement, and normative change are crucial. Interventions, like My Health, My Choice in Bihar, have shown promise in increasing contraceptive uptake when these social norms are explicitly addressed.[27]

Collectively, these measures have the capacity to improve both the equity and effectiveness of reproductive health strategy, thereby advancing demographic stabilization efforts and facilitating progress toward achieving the Sustainable Development Goals.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is that we used spatial models, which provide a more accurate estimation of geographic dependencies and improve upon conventional regression approaches, which assume independence of observations. The inclusion of both global and local spatial diagnostics increases the robustness of the findings and supports model choice based on empirical evidence.[1718]

However, the analysis also has a few limitations. First, the ecological design limits inferences at the individual or household level and may hide within-state heterogeneity. Second, the cross-sectional nature of NFHS-5 limits the ability to assess fertility transitions over time. To overcome this limitation, future studies to be conducted on longitudinal data if available. Third, while MPI is a comprehensive indicator, it may obscure the differential contribution of its subcomponents, such as education, nutrition, sanitation, or health. Lastly, source limitations, potential biases cannot be excluded due to self-reporting of data during NFHS, which obscures the validity of reported results.

CONCLUSION

This study reiterates the spatially uneven nature of India’s fertility transition, with the multidimensional poverty index emerging as the most consistent determinant of higher fertility at the state level. Spatial analysis provides new insights into regional clustering and diffusion patterns of fertility, which have practical implications for targeted, equity-focused population policies. Integrating reproductive health into broader socioeconomic development programs remains central to achieving sustainable fertility decline and the demographic goals outlined in India’s national and subnational strategies.
