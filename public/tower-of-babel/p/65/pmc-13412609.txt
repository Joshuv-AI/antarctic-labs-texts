To Be or Not to Be: Amiodarone as Both Savior and Foe—A Therapeutic Paradox in Atrial Fibrillation Management and Ventricular Arrhythmia Associated with Amiodarone-Induced Thyrotoxicosis
Authors: Monika Amielan, Małgorzata Zalewska-Adamiec, Hanna Bachórzewska-Gajewska, Sławomir Dobrzycki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT) is a serious thyroid dysfunction that represents a significant complication of pharmacological therapy in cardiology patients. The development of AIT can substantially complicate the management of cardiac arrhythmias, posing a major clinical challenge. We present the case of a patient in whom amiodarone was initially effective in the treatment of atrial fibrillation but subsequently led to type II amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis and exacerbation of the atrial arrhythmia. This case highlights the need for regular monitoring of patients treated with amiodarone, the challenges of managing arrhythmias in the setting of AIT, and the importance of close collaboration between cardiologists and endocrinologists in the diagnosis and treatment of AIT.

1. Introduction

Amiodarone is a widely used antiarrhythmic drug. It is a lipophilic benzofuran derivative that contains approximately 37% iodine and belongs to the Vaughan Williams class III antiarrhythmic agents [1]. It is primarily indicated in patients in whom other antiarrhythmic drugs are contraindicated, such as those with structural heart disease, heart failure, or cardiomyopathy [2].

Long-term amiodarone therapy is associated with a high incidence of adverse effects, particularly thyroid dysfunction. Although thyroid dysfunction of any type occurs in approximately 15–20% of patients treated with amiodarone, amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT) develops in 7–15% of these patients, posing a significant clinical management challenge [3,4,5].

Three clinical types of amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis are distinguished: type 1 (AIT1), type 2 (AIT2), and mixed forms. AIT1 more often occurs in patients with pre-existing structural thyroid abnormalities, such as multinodular goiter, thyroid nodules or autoimmune thyroid disease (e.g., Graves’ disease). It involves excessive cell activation due to iodine overload, which leads to increased synthesis and release of thyroid hormones. In contrast, AIT2 is more common in areas without iodine deficiency and occurs in structurally normal thyroid glands. Its pathophysiological mechanism involves inflammatory destruction of thyrocytes, which contributes to release of previously synthesized thyroid hormones. Mixed forms are less clearly defined and often complicate therapeutic management [6]. Appropriate treatment of AIT1 consists of suppressing thyrotoxicosis with antithyroid drugs such as methimazole, whereas treatment of AIT2 aims to inhibit the inflammatory cascade through the use of glucocorticoids [7].

Hypothyroidism usually occurs after 6–12 months of amiodarone therapy, whereas hyperthyroidism may develop during the early stages of treatment or even several months after discontinuation due to the accumulation of amiodarone in adipose tissue [5,8].

Thyrotoxicosis is a serious complication that may be life-threatening, as it is associated with a 2.7-fold increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) [9]. This may be related to the effects of the thyrotoxic state on the cardiovascular system, including an increased heart rate, shortened action potential duration, increased systemic vascular resistance, and increased preload, leading to disorganized atrial activity. Furthermore, subclinical hyperthyroidism is associated with a risk of atrial fibrillation (AF) comparable to that observed in overt hyperthyroidism [10].

Amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis is an important clinical issue because it can significantly complicate the management of patients with atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmias. We present the case of a patient with heart failure, paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, and treatment-resistant ventricular arrhythmias in whom the development of amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis considerably complicated further therapeutic management.

2. Case Presentation

A 64-year-old man was admitted to the clinic due to three inappropriate implantable cardioverter–defibrillator (ICD) shocks. Two years earlier, he had experienced an anterior myocardial infarction complicated by cardiogenic shock, which was treated with percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) of the circumflex artery (Cx) and obtuse marginal branch (OM) with implantation of two stents. One month later, the patient underwent revascularization of the right coronary artery (RCA) with implantation of two additional stents. Three months after the myocardial infarction, the patient experienced sudden cardiac arrest (SCA), and following repeat coronary angiography, an implantable cardioverter–defibrillator (ICD) was implanted for secondary prevention of SCA. Additionally, the patient’s medical history included arterial hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, for which he had been treated with amiodarone and apixaban for several years.

At admission to the clinic, the patient was hemodynamically stable, with a blood pressure of 101/82 mmHg, and reported dyspnea corresponding to NYHA functional class III. Physical examination revealed no significant abnormalities. Electrocardiography (ECG) showed sinus rhythm at 84 bpm with ventricular bigeminy. ICD interrogation demonstrated that the inappropriate shocks were triggered by episodes of atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response of approximately 160 bpm. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level of 3060 pg/mL and hormonal findings consistent with hyperthyroidism (hormone levels are presented in Table 1). Echocardiography demonstrated enlarged atria, severe tricuspid regurgitation, moderate mitral regurgitation, and severely impaired left ventricular systolic function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) estimated at 32%. The patient underwent an endocrinology consultation. Thyroid autoantibodies were measured and were within the reference range: anti-thyroglobulin antibodies (TgAb) 0.61 IU/mL (reference range: 0.00–4.11), anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb) 0.42 IU/mL (reference range: 0.00–5.61), and TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb) 0.80 IU/mL (reference range: 0.00–1.75). Thyroid ultrasonography showed a non-enlarged thyroid gland with a heterogeneous, hypoechoic appearance, sparse vascularity, and no visible focal lesions (Figure 1). Given the absence of a previous history of thyroid disease and the prolonged duration of amiodarone therapy, a diagnosis of type II amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis was made. Amiodarone was discontinued, and treatment with prednisone was initiated (40 mg/day). During hospitalization, the patient experienced recurrence of atrial fibrillation (Figure 2). Holter ECG monitoring recorded atrial fibrillation with a ventricular rate ranging from 71 to 151 bpm, as well as frequent ventricular ectopic beats accounting for 19% of the recording. Pharmacological treatment was adjusted by increasing the dose of metoprolol and adding digoxin (0.1 mg/day). In addition, the patient’s previous treatment was continued (apixaban, sacubitril/valsartan, empagliflozin, eplerenone, torasemide, and atorvastatin). At discharge, electrical cardioversion was planned after achieving euthyroid status.

Two weeks later, the patient was again transferred from the emergency department to the clinic due to four inappropriate ICD shocks occurring during episodes of atrial fibrillation with a rapid ventricular response. Echocardiography revealed worsening left ventricular systolic function, with an LVEF of 25%. The case was reviewed by the Heart Team, and the patient was qualified for pulmonary vein cryoballoon ablation, which was subsequently performed. Due to worsening ventricular arrhythmia observed on Holter ECG monitoring (Figure 3), the patient was also qualified for coronary angiography. However, one month later, laboratory findings still showed hormonal features of hyperthyroidism. Therefore, following endocrinology consultation, coronary angiography was postponed until euthyroid status was achieved. Previous pharmacotherapy, including prednisone, was continued.

One month later, improvement in thyroid hormone parameters was observed. Coronary angiography was performed and demonstrated a sustained effect of the previous PCI and only non-significant atherosclerotic lesions in the remaining coronary arteries (Figure 4).

Several months later, the patient was hospitalized again for electrical cardioversion. At that time, he was in good general condition, with dyspnea corresponding to NYHA functional class II. ECG continued to show atrial fibrillation. Laboratory tests demonstrated euthyroid status and elevated NT-proBNP levels (2849 pg/mL). Electrical cardioversion was successfully performed, restoring sinus rhythm. Prednisone was then gradually tapered and discontinued.

Six months later, follow-up echocardiography showed improvement in left ventricular systolic function, with LVEF increasing to 38%, although severe tricuspid regurgitation persisted. Holter ECG monitoring continued to demonstrate persistent ventricular arrhythmia accounting for 23% of the recording; therefore, amiodarone was reintroduced. At a later stage, the patient was qualified for upgrading the ICD to cardiac resynchronization therapy with defibrillator (CRT-D) and for catheter ablation of the ventricular arrhythmia substrate.

3. Discussion

For many years, amiodarone has remained the most effective pharmacological agent used in the treatment of supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias [11,12]. Unfortunately, its use is also associated with a high risk of adverse effects, particularly thyroid dysfunction.

In our patient, amiodarone had been prescribed to prevent recurrence of atrial fibrillation. After several years of therapy, the drug induced type II amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis, which exacerbated the supraventricular arrhythmia. Atrial fibrillation with a very rapid ventricular response was the direct cause of the inappropriate ICD shocks. Due to the necessity of discontinuing amiodarone and the exhaustion of other pharmacological treatment options (maximum-dose metoprolol plus digoxin), the patient underwent pulmonary vein cryoablation. Electrical cardioversion was not attempted at that time because of its very limited efficacy in the setting of thyrotoxicosis. The cryoballoon ablation effectively eliminated the cause of the ICD shocks. At the same time, euthyroid status was achieved after 2.5 months of prednisone therapy. Unfortunately, despite normalization of thyroid function, ventricular arrhythmia persisted in our patient for several months thereafter (accounting for approximately 20% of beats on Holter ECG monitoring). Therefore, amiodarone was reintroduced.

An important aspect is the treatment of AIT2. Although antithyroid drugs are commonly used to treat hyperthyroidism, they are recommended for AIT1 and mixed forms of AIT. In contrast, because AIT2 is an inflammatory condition, the 2018 European Thyroid Association (ETA) guidelines recommend an initial prednisone dose of 40–60 mg/day for 1–3 months, followed by gradual tapering of the dose by 5 mg every 2 weeks [13]. In our patient, prednisone was initiated at a dose of 40 mg/day (administered once daily), and after 1 month, the dose was gradually tapered by 5 mg every 2 weeks. Euthyroidism was achieved after only 2.5 months of prednisone therapy.

The most controversial issue remains whether to discontinue or continue amiodarone during AIT and whether the drug can be safely reintroduced afterward. The European Thyroid Association (ETA) guidelines allow continuation of amiodarone during the thyrotoxic phase in patients with life-threatening arrhythmias that are responsive to the drug. However, continuing amiodarone may prolong the time required to achieve stable euthyroidism and extend the period of thyroid hormone-mediated toxicity. In patients with a history of AIT, restoration of euthyroidism may allow amiodarone to be reintroduced, although close endocrinological follow-up is essential [13,14]. In our patient, achieving euthyroidism was the initial priority; therefore, amiodarone was discontinued, and thyroid function normalized after 2.5 months of prednisone therapy. Subsequently, because ventricular arrhythmia persisted, amiodarone was reintroduced. Reintroduction of amiodarone carries a risk of recurrent AIT; however, studies have shown that this risk is significantly higher in patients with type 1 AIT than in those with type 2 [15]. This suggests that re-exposure to amiodarone may be better tolerated in cases such as ours. The patient was advised to undergo regular clinical follow-up and thyroid function testing every 3 months.

Another therapeutic challenge in the presented case was the decision whether to perform or postpone coronary angiography due to thyroid dysfunction. Numerous studies have confirmed the significant impact of contrast agents used in coronary diagnostics on thyroid function. During routine coronary angiography, approximately 15–20 mg of iodine is delivered into the circulation, which may induce iodine-related hyperthyroidism. Studies have reported an incidence of subclinical hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism after contrast administration of approximately 3.5% among individuals with normal thyroid hormone levels before the procedure, while the risk of contrast-induced hyperthyroidism itself has been estimated to range from approximately 0.2% to 9% [16,17]. Considering the lack of urgency for invasive diagnostics and the potentially high risk of further exacerbating amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis, the decision was made to postpone the procedure until normalization of thyroid function.

A limitation of our case report is that thyroid scintigraphy was not performed. The diagnosis of type 2 amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis (AIT2) and its differentiation from AIT1 and mixed forms were made clinically, in accordance with the ETA guidelines, based on the absence of a previous history of thyroid disease, the prolonged duration of amiodarone therapy, biochemical evidence of thyrotoxicosis, normal thyroid autoantibody levels, and thyroid ultrasonography findings supporting AIT2 rather than AIT1 or mixed AIT.

In summary, this case highlights the complexity of managing patients who develop AIT after previously successful treatment with amiodarone. By worsening the control of atrial fibrillation, AIT may contribute to inappropriate ICD shocks. When pharmacological treatment options for atrial fibrillation have been exhausted, catheter ablation can effectively eliminate the underlying cause of these ICD shocks. In patients with type 2 AIT, glucocorticoid therapy can successfully restore euthyroidism. Although discontinuation of amiodarone is not always mandatory in AIT, it may shorten the time required to achieve euthyroidism. A history of AIT does not preclude the subsequent reintroduction of amiodarone. Effective management of endocrine complications in cardiology patients requires close collaboration between cardiologists and endocrinologists.

4. Conclusions

The presented case highlights the importance of monitoring thyroid hormone levels in patients treated with amiodarone and the need for collaboration between cardiologists and endocrinologists when endocrine complications occur. The development of amiodarone-induced thyrotoxicosis impairs the control of cardiac arrhythmias, significantly complicating subsequent clinical management and treatment.
