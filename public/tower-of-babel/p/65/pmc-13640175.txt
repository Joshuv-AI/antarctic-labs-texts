A novel pH-responsive nanoreactor for pyroptosis induction: multifaceted ROS amplification drives potent immunotherapy against triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Wenjia Liu, Ying Wang, Xiangyu Hao, Yangxun Wang, Xiaofan Zhang, Jinchao Zhang, Kun Ge, Guoqiang Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is associated with rapid progression and poor sensitivity to monotherapies, necessitating combination strategies to enhance tumor immunogenicity, reprogram the tumor microenvironment (TME), and convert immunologically “cold” tumors into “hot” ones. In this study, we develop a pH-responsive, pyroptosis-inducing nanoreactor, ZnS/ZIF-90:Fe/Vk3 (ZZFV), designed to elicit potent antitumor immunity. Under acidic conditions, ZZFV rapidly degrades, releasing Zn2+, Fe2+, H2S, and Vk3. H2S promotes glucose uptake and consumption while downregulating cytochrome c oxidase subunit IV (COX IV) expression, whereas Zn2+ suppresses lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) activity, collectively disrupting glucose metabolism and exacerbating intracellular acidification. Meanwhile, NAD(P)H quinone oxidoreductase 1 (NQO1), highly expressed in tumor cells, catalyzes the conversion of Vk3 to H2O2, which is subsequently converted to highly toxic •OH through Fe2+-mediated Fenton reactions. Additionally, Zn2+ and H2S synergistically disrupt mitochondrial function, thereby amplifying reactive oxygen species (ROS) production. Excessive ROS triggers Caspase-1–dependent pyroptosis, leading to the liberation of damage-associated molecular patterns. These events promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation, robust T-cell activation, M1 macrophage polarization, and expansion of central memory T-cell populations, thereby conferring durable immune protection. Consequently, ZZFV demonstrates potent antitumor efficacy, effectively overcoming immune tolerance in TNBC and achieving robust, sustained antitumor immunity.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04795-3.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a heterogeneous subtype of breast cancer [1], associated with high rates of metastasis, recurrence, and increased mortality [2, 3]. Chemotherapy remains the primary treatment for TNBC, but patient prognosis is still poor, with a median overall survival of only approximately 13.3 months [4, 5]. Immunotherapy has achieved considerable clinical success in several malignancies [6, 7], but its efficacy in TNBC remains limited, largely due to the immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), which suppresses antitumor immune responses [8, 9]. Consequently, transforming “cold” tumors into “hot” ones by enhancing tumor immunogenicity and remodeling the immunosuppressive TME represents an attractive approach for TNBC treatment.

Pyroptosis is a highly immunogenic form of programmed cell death characterized by membrane pore formation, the release of proinflammatory cytokines, and the extrusion of danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), triggering strong immune responses [10]. Unlike conventional forms of cell death, pyroptosis induces robust immunogenic cell death (ICD), promotes tumor antigen exposure, and enhances immune cell recruitment into the TME, ultimately improving immunotherapeutic efficacy [11, 12]. Reactive oxygen species (ROS), as key intracellular redox signaling molecules, can induce pyroptosis through inflammasome activation and Caspase-1-mediated cleavage of gasdermin D (GSDMD), followed by the generation of the pore-forming N-terminal fragment (GSDMD-N) [13, 14]. Accordingly, ROS-based therapeutic strategies, including photodynamic [15], chemodynamic [16], and sonodynamic therapies [17], as well as nanozyme-based systems [18], have been widely explored for pyroptosis-mediated antitumor immunotherapy. However, most of these strategies rely on a single ROS generation pathway, which limits ROS yield. Thus, strategies that amplify ROS production through multiple mechanisms are highly desirable for antitumor immunotherapy involving pyroptosis. In nanomedicine, Vk3 and Fe2+ have been widely used to amplify oxidative stress, as Vk3 can undergo NQO1-mediated redox cycling to promote ROS generation, whereas Fe2+ catalyzes Fenton reactions to produce highly toxic •OH [19, 20]. The integration of these ROS-generating components offers a rational strategy for achieving robust pyroptosis induction.

Increasing attention has focused on the roles of gaseous signaling molecules and metal ions in tumor redox homeostasis. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S), an endogenous gasotransmitter with dose-dependent biological activity, regulates cellular metabolism within the TME, but endogenous H2S levels are generally insufficient to exert significant antitumor effects [21, 22]. Elevated H2S levels suppress COX IV activity, thereby blocking electron transport [23]. This disruption results in ROS accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), ultimately reducing ATP production [24]. Meanwhile, H2S can promote glucose uptake and glycolysis, leading to abnormal pyruvate accumulation and intracellular acidification [25]. Similarly, excessive intracellular Zn2+ can disrupt mitochondrial electron flow, promote electron leakage to O2, and increase superoxide anion (•O2−) production, thereby amplifying ROS accumulation [26]. In addition, Zn2+ can inhibit LDH activity, restricting pyruvate-to-lactate conversion and contributing to intracellular acidification and energy depletion [27, 28]. Thus, H2S and Zn2+ may cooperatively regulate tumor oxidative stress and glucose metabolism by promoting ROS accumulation, disrupting pyruvate conversion, enhancing intracellular acidification, and impairing ATP supply. To achieve the delivery and responsive release of these functional molecules, ZIF-90 serves as an ideal nanocarrier owing to its pH-responsive degradation behavior, and the Zn2+ released during degradation may also contribute to tumor metabolic disruption [29]. In addition, ZnS can act as an acid-sensitive donor of Zn2+ and H2S, further disturbing tumor energy metabolism [30]. Thus, developing a single pH-responsive nanoreactor that integrates ROS cascade amplification with metabolic disruption to induce pyroptosis, enhance ICD, and reshape the immunosuppressive TME remains highly desirable.

In this study, we developed a pH-responsive ZnS/ZIF-Fe/Vk3 (ZZFV) nanoreactor to investigate whether integrating metabolic regulation, ROS amplification, pyroptosis, and ICD induction could remodel the immunosuppressive TME and enhance tumor cell immunogenicity (Fig. 1). ZZFV was constructed to release H2S, Zn2+, Fe2+, and Vk3 under acidic conditions, providing a potential strategy to disrupt glucose metabolism, amplify oxidative stress, and induce ICD. We hypothesized that H2S- and Zn2+-mediated metabolic interference may disrupt glucose metabolism by altering mitochondrial OXPHOS, the conversion of pyruvate to lactate, and intracellular pH homeostasis. Meanwhile, Fe2+ and Vk3 may promote ROS generation through Fenton reactions and NQO1-related redox processes, respectively, thereby providing an oxidative stress basis for subsequent pyroptosis and ICD induction. To test this hypothesis, we systematically evaluated the pH-responsive degradation behavior, cellular uptake, intracellular release, metabolic regulation, ROS generation, pyroptosis, and ICD-associated responses of ZZFV in 4T1 cells. Furthermore, the in vivo antitumor efficacy, immune activation, TME remodeling, immunological memory response, and lung metastasis inhibition of ZZFV were investigated in an orthotopic 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model. This study aimed to determine whether a multifunctional pH-responsive nanoreactor could enhance tumor immunogenicity and reshape the immunosuppressive TME for TNBC immunotherapy.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization

The ZnS/ZIF-90:Fe/Vk3 (ZZFV) nanoreactor was synthesized following the procedure illustrated in Fig. 2A, and the morphology at each synthesis stage was examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Initially, ethylenediamine-modified ZnS nanoparticles were synthesized using a liquid-phase method [31, 32]. TEM images revealed a slight agglomeration of ZnS quantum dots with a uniform particle size of approximately 3.5 nm (Fig. 2B), consistent with previously reported values. ZIF-90:Fe (ZIF-Fe) was subsequently synthesized at room temperature using Zn(CH3COO)2, Fe(CH3COO)2, and imidazolidine-2-carbaldehyde (2-ICA) as precursors. TEM images showed that both ZIF-90 (Fig. S1) and ZIF-Fe (Fig. 2C) nanoparticles exhibited well-defined polyhedral morphologies with average diameters of approximately 200 nm, indicating that Fe2+ doping did not affect ZIF-90 morphology. This observation was further corroborated by powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis, which showed that the diffraction patterns of ZIF-90 and ZIF-Fe nanoparticles matched the simulated ZIF-90 pattern without detectable impurity peaks (Fig. S2), confirming that Fe2+ incorporation did not disrupt the native crystal architecture of ZIF-90. The successful formation of ZIF-Fe was confirmed using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, which exhibited characteristic peaks of ZIF-Fe (Fig. S3). Subsequently, Zn2+ and Fe2+ from the precursors Zn(CH3COO)2 and Fe(CH3COO)2 were coordinated to the ethylenediamine groups on the ZnS surface for immobilization before the final addition of 2-ICA to synthesize the ZnS/ZIF-Fe (ZZF). TEM images revealed that the resulting ZZF nanoparticles retained a distinct polyhedral structure with an average diameter of approximately 200 nm (Fig. 2D). Subsequent loading of Vk3 produced the final ZZFV nanoplatform, which retained a comparable particle size and morphology, indicating that Vk3 incorporation did not induce significant morphological changes (Fig. 2E). The XRD patterns of ZnS, ZZF, and ZZFV nanoparticles displayed diffraction peaks corresponding to both ZnS and ZIF-90, indicating retention of both crystalline phases throughout the synthesis process (Fig. 2F). The successful fabrication of ZZFV was verified by its absorbance spectrum (Fig. 2G), which exhibited two characteristic absorption bands at 251 nm for Vk3 and 290 nm for ZIF-Fe. Functional group analysis using FT-IR spectroscopy showed that ZIF-Fe and ZZFV shared nearly identical peak positions, including the characteristic aldehyde stretching vibration of the ICA ligand at 1666 cm⁻¹ (Fig. 2H). Furthermore, ZZFV exhibited an additional absorption peak at 1593 cm⁻¹ that was absent in the pure Vk3 spectrum, confirming the successful loading of Vk3. The drug-loading capacity of the nanocarriers is closely correlated with their BET-specific surface area, measured via N2 adsorption isotherms. ZIF-Fe and ZZF exhibited surface areas of 811.34 and 507.14 m2 g⁻1, respectively, whereas the ZZFV surface area decreased to 319.41 m2 g⁻1 after Vk3 loading (Fig. 2I). Correspondingly, changes in solution absorbance before and after Vk3 loading were observed (Fig. S4), and the loading efficiency was calculated to be 85.11% based on the standard curve and the corresponding absorbance changes (Fig. S5). Wide-scan X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) showed six peaks corresponding to S 2p, C 1s, N 1s, O 1s, Fe 2p, and Zn 2p (Fig. 2J). The Zn 2p spectrum showed two main peaks at 1021.6 eV and 1044.6 eV, corresponding to the Zn 2p3/2 and Zn 2p1/2 peaks, respectively (Fig. 2K). The S 2p spectrum showed two significant peaks at 161.2 eV and 162.4 eV, corresponding to the S 2p3/2 and S 2p1/2 states, respectively (Fig. 2L). The Fe 2p XPS spectrum showed two sets of doublets corresponding to Fe2+ and Fe3+ (Fig. 2M), with Fe 2p3/2 peaks at 710.6 eV (Fe2+) and 713.8 eV (Fe3+) and Fe 2p1/2 peaks at 724.4 eV (Fe2+) and 727.6 eV (Fe3+). Satellite peaks observed at approximately 717.5 eV and 721.9 eV were attributed to Fe2+ and Fe3+, respectively [33–35]. The Fe2+/Fe3+ ratio was determined to be 1.53 through semi-quantitative integration of the Fe 2p XPS peak area. The XPS profiles of the C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s regions for ZZFV were further examined, as shown in Fig. S6. Collectively, these results demonstrate the successful construction of the ZZFV nanoreactor.

pH-Responsive degradation and Fenton activity

The pH-responsive degradation behavior of the nanoreactor governs the release kinetics of encapsulated drugs and ions and ultimately determines their therapeutic outcomes in vivo. Figure. 3A illustrates the pH-responsive structural collapse of the nanoreactor and its corresponding drug release mechanism. To investigate this behavior, XRD patterns were recorded at pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4. The structural stability of ZIF-Fe decreases with decreasing pH, culminating in framework collapse (Fig. 3B). At pH 5.0, the near disappearance of the characteristic ZIF-Fe diffraction peak, along with a diminished ZnS peak intensity, confirmed the effective degradation of the delivery system under acidic conditions. The pH-responsive behavior of ZZFV was further assessed by monitoring its ion release profile under different pH conditions using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES). Zn2+ and Fe2+ release was minimal and almost negligible at pH 7.4, consistent with high stability under physiological conditions (Fig. 3C, D). In contrast, acidic conditions markedly promoted ion release. At pH 5.0, cumulative release over 24 h reached approximately 79.97% for Zn2+ and 62.57% for Fe2+. Simultaneously, ZnS degradation in ZZFV led to the release of H2S. H2S was assessed using Pb(CH3COO)2 indicator paper, which changes from a light brown to a deep brown color with an increase in H2S concentration. To monitor H2S release, ZZFV nanoparticles were incubated in buffers at different pH values, placed in sealed 6 cm culture dishes with moistened Pb(CH3COO)2 paper affixed to the lid, and the color changes were photographed (Fig. 3E). When ZZFV nanoparticles were incubated at pH 7.4, no noticeable color change or characteristic odor was observed, indicating negligible degradation under neutral conditions. In contrast, the browning time of the indicator paper decreased with increasing acidity, demonstrating accelerated ZZFV degradation under acidic conditions. Simultaneously, H2S released from ZZFV in buffers at different pH values was quantified using an H2S assay kit (Fig. 3F), yielding results consistent with those of the Pb(CH3COO)2 paper assay. At pH 5.0 and 500 µg mL⁻1 ZZFV, H2S production reached 276.32 µmol L⁻1 at 24 h. Finally, Vk3 release was quantified and exhibited a pH-dependent profile similar to that of Zn2+, Fe2+, and H2S (Fig. 3G), with a cumulative release of 70.35% at pH 5.0 after 24 h. Collectively, these results indicate that ZZFV exhibits strong pH responsiveness, undergoing structural degradation in acidic environments with the concomitant release of Zn2+, Fe2+, H2S, and Vk3, while maintaining excellent stability under neutral conditions.

The released Fe2+ ions serve as catalytic centers for the Fenton reaction, converting H2O2 into cytotoxic •OH radicals [36]. •OH generated from H2O2 oxidizes TMB to blue oxidized TMB (oxTMB), with a characteristic absorbance at 652 nm [37]. As shown in Fig. 3H, the Fenton reaction activity, indicated by oxTMB blue intensity, exhibited a clear pH dependence and was maximal at pH 5.0. This optimal activity is attributed to the Fe2+ released from acid-triggered ZZFV degradation. The Fenton reaction activities of ZIF-Fe (Fig. S7) and ZZFV were evaluated under identical conditions. Additionally, TMB oxidation increased with ZZFV concentration, attributed to higher •OH generation resulting from enhanced Fenton activity at elevated ZZFV levels (Fig. 3I). A similar trend was observed when the ZZFV concentration was held constant, and the H2O2 concentration varied (Fig. 3J). Furthermore, electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscopy was used to confirm •OH generation, revealing the characteristic 1:2:2:1 signal pattern and demonstrating time-dependent production (Fig. 3K). Together, these findings confirm that ZZFV exhibits strong Fenton activity under acidic conditions.

Cellular uptake and cytotoxicity evaluation

Considering that ZZFV can release multiple therapeutic components, including Zn2+, Fe2+, H2S, and Vk3, under acidic conditions, we systematically evaluated its in vitro antitumor activity to assess its therapeutic potential. The 4T1 cell line was selected as a representative model of TNBC because of its aggressive growth, strong metastatic potential, and syngeneic nature with immunocompetent BALB/c mice. This model enables subsequent evaluation of tumor growth inhibition, immune activation, and spontaneous lung metastasis, which are key aspects for assessing the therapeutic mechanism of ZZFV. Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled ZZFV was first used to evaluate nanoreactor uptake by 4T1 cells, and intracellular fluorescence intensity was quantified using flow cytometry. FITC-labeled ZZFV exhibited time-dependent internalization, as reflected by increasing intracellular fluorescence (Fig. 4A). After 8 h of incubation, 88.3% of cells were FITC-positive, indicating efficient cellular uptake of ZZFV. The strong uptake of ZZFV may facilitate its subsequent intracellular activation and metabolic regulation, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy. This is consistent with previous reports that enhanced cellular uptake is often associated with stronger cytotoxic or antitumor effects in nanoparticle-based delivery systems [38, 39]. To determine whether efficient cellular uptake was associated with enhanced cytotoxicity, 4T1 cell viability was determined by CCK-8 assay. The pH-dependent cytotoxicity of ZZFV was first examined by exposing 4T1 cells to the ZZFV nanoreactor under varying pH conditions. At pH 7.4, cell viability at 100 µg mL⁻1 was 30.51% after 24 h and 16.13% after 48 h (Fig. 4B), whereas at the same material concentration and pH 6.0, cell viability further decreased to 17.86% and 9.38% (Fig. 4C), respectively. These results indicate that ZZFV induces time- and concentration-dependent cytotoxicity, which is further enhanced under acidic conditions. This enhanced cytotoxicity under acidic conditions is consistent with the acid-responsive degradation behavior of ZZFV, suggesting that acidic environments facilitate ZZFV activation, thereby strengthening its therapeutic efficacy. To further assess ZZFV selectivity, HUVEC viability was examined under the same treatment conditions. HUVEC viability remained approximately 80% after 48 h of incubation with 100 µg mL⁻1 ZZFV, whereas 4T1 cells were much more sensitive to the same treatment (Fig. S8). This difference suggests that ZZFV exhibits relatively low toxicity toward normal endothelial cells and a certain degree of tumor cell selectivity, which may be related to the higher metabolic vulnerability and acidic intracellular environment of tumor cells. Cell viability assays revealed that 4T1 cell viability gradually decreased with increasing material concentrations in all treatment groups (Fig. 4D). At 100 µg mL⁻1, ZnS and ZIF-Fe treatments resulted in cell viabilities of 67.47% and 59.91%, respectively, whereas their combination in ZZF further decreased viability to 45.67%. This enhanced cytotoxicity suggests that the released Zn2+, Fe2+, and H2S collectively contribute to the improved cytotoxic effect. Notably, Vk3-loaded ZZFV produced the strongest growth inhibition, reducing viability to 30.51%. This result indicates that Vk3 further amplifies the antitumor effect, probably by participating in redox cycling and ROS generation in the presence of Fe2+, thereby strengthening the overall cytotoxic response of ZZFV.

Because mitochondrial integrity is closely associated with cell survival, JC-1 staining was performed to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. In the present assay, damaged mitochondria with dissipated membrane potential emit green fluorescence due to JC-1 monomers, whereas intact, polarized mitochondria emit red fluorescence from JC-1 aggregates [40]. As shown in Fig. 4E, control cells displayed intense red fluorescence, consistent with a high mitochondrial membrane potential. Conversely, ZZFV-treated 4T1 cells exhibited pronounced green fluorescence, indicating severe mitochondrial depolarization and dysfunction. This result indicates that ZZFV-induced cytotoxicity is accompanied by mitochondrial damage, which may contribute to the reduced viability of 4T1 cells. Furthermore, Calcein-AM/PI staining was used to assess cell viability and membrane integrity following different treatments. As shown in Fig. 4F, minimal cell death was observed in the Vk3 group, whereas substantial cell death occurred in the ZZF and ZZFV groups. Among all treatments, the ZZFV-treated cells exhibited the strongest red fluorescence and minimal green fluorescence, consistent with the CCK-8 assay results. Cell death was further evaluated by flow cytometry following Annexin V–FITC/PI staining (Fig. 4G, H). The total Annexin V-positive populations (Q2 + Q3) in the control, Vk3, ZnS, ZIF-Fe, ZZF, and ZZFV groups were 3.30%, 12.17%, 32.02%, 39.37%, 53.96%, and 66.70%, respectively. The markedly increased Annexin V/PI-positive population in the ZZFV group indicates substantial treatment-induced cell death and membrane damage. Collectively, cell viability assays, mitochondrial membrane potential analyses, Calcein-AM/PI staining, and Annexin V-FITC/PI quantification confirm the potent in vitro antitumor activity of the ZZFV nanoreactor.

Scratch wound healing assays

Local invasion into the surrounding extracellular matrix represents a critical early step in tumor metastasis, enabling malignant cells to disseminate from the primary tumor. To evaluate the effect of ZZFV on this invasive capability, a standardized in vitro scratch-wound healing assay was performed. This assay assesses cell migration by creating a defined gap in a confluent monolayer and quantifying wound closure over time. As shown in Fig. 4I, control cells exhibited strong migratory ability, with wound closure reaching 52.63% at 12 h (Fig. S9) and 73.57% at 24 h (Fig. 4J), consistent with the high motility of 4T1 cells. In contrast, ZZFV treatment markedly inhibited cell migration, yielding wound closure rates of only 16.89% and 30.40% at the corresponding time points. This pronounced reduction in wound closure provides clear qualitative and quantitative evidence that the ZZFV nanoreactor effectively impairs cell motility and invasive potential. Together, these findings indicate that ZZFV substantially interferes with a key cellular process underlying local invasion, supporting its potential to inhibit metastatic progression.

In vitro antitumor mechanism

Building on the demonstrated antitumor efficacy of ZZFV, subsequent investigations were conducted to elucidate its underlying mechanisms of action. Following cellular internalization, ZZFV may undergo degradation primarily within acidic endo/lysosomal compartments, leading to the release of Zn2+ and H2S. Intracellular Zn2+ levels in 4T1 cells were assessed using the TSQ probe. Strong blue fluorescence signals were observed in the ZnS, ZIF-Fe, ZZF, and ZZFV groups (Fig. 5A), confirming intracellular Zn2+ release, which is consistent with the acid-responsive degradation behavior of these materials. The intracellular H2S release behavior of ZZFV was then evaluated using the WSP-1 probe. Green fluorescence signals were clearly observed in the ZnS, ZZF, and ZZFV groups (Fig. 5B), indicating H2S generation from ZnS-containing formulations. Consistent results were further confirmed using flow cytometry (Fig. S10). Given that the therapeutic efficacy of H2S is concentration dependent [41], flow cytometry showed that ZZFV exposure for 12 h dose-dependently increased WSP-1-positive 4T1 cells, supporting the contribution of H2S generation to the antitumor activity of ZZFV (Fig. 5C). Glucose, the primary substrate for ATP production, is essential for maintaining cellular energy homeostasis. Due to the Warburg effect, tumor cells exhibit markedly elevated glucose consumption [42], which can be further enhanced by H2S [43, 44]. Cellular glucose uptake was subsequently evaluated using the 2-NBDG probe via fluorescence imaging and flow cytometry. The ZnS, ZZF, and ZZFV groups exhibited significantly stronger green fluorescence than the control group, indicating enhanced intracellular glucose accumulation and confirming that H2S released from these formulations promotes glucose uptake (Fig. 5D; Fig. S11). Flow cytometry analysis further confirmed this trend (Fig. 5E; Fig. S12). Together, these results suggest that ZZFV can release Zn2+ and H2S after cellular uptake and that H2S generation may enhance glucose uptake, thereby providing an important metabolic basis for subsequent ZZFV-induced metabolic disturbance.

After confirming the responsive degradation of ZZFV upon Zn2+ and H2S release, as well as its ability to promote glucose uptake in 4T1 cells, we further investigated its regulatory effects on cellular energy metabolism. Enhanced glucose uptake does not necessarily indicate complete glycolytic flux, as its downstream metabolic fate is jointly regulated by mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and lactate production. In this context, COX IV contributes to mitochondrial electron transport and OXPHOS, whereas LDH catalyzes the conversion of pyruvate into lactate. Previous studies have shown that excess Zn2+ can inhibit LDH activity, thereby blocking the conversion of pyruvate to lactate and promoting pyruvate accumulation [45]. Meanwhile, H2S may affect COX IV function, impair mitochondrial electron transport and OXPHOS, and limit the mitochondrial oxidative utilization of pyruvate [46]. Therefore, COX IV protein expression and LDH activity were examined to elucidate the mechanism by which ZZFV induces glucose metabolic disturbance. Western blot analysis showed that Vk3 had only a minor effect on COX IV expression, whereas ZnS and the formulations containing ZnS, including ZZF and ZZFV, markedly reduced COX IV protein levels (Fig. 5F). This result suggests that H2S released upon material degradation may disrupt COX IV-related mitochondrial function and OXPHOS. Meanwhile, LDH activity was reduced to varying degrees across all treatment groups except the Vk3 group, with the most substantial decreases observed in the ZZF and ZZFV groups (Fig. 5G), suggesting that Zn2+ released from the materials may suppress LDH activity and limit the conversion of pyruvate to lactate. Further analysis of intracellular lactate levels revealed a gradual decrease in lactate content in 4T1 cells as ZZFV concentration increased (Fig. 5H). In contrast, intracellular pyruvate levels increased to varying degrees in all treatment groups except the Vk3 group, with the most pronounced increases observed in the ZZF and ZZFV groups (Fig. 5I). Together, these results indicate that ZZFV disrupts both mitochondrial OXPHOS involving COX IV and lactate production mediated by LDH, thereby blocking downstream pyruvate metabolism and causing a metabolic imbalance characterized by reduced lactate levels and pyruvate accumulation.

ZZFV-induced disruption of glucose metabolism may further compromise intracellular acid–base homeostasis and energy supply. Therefore, intracellular pH and ATP levels in 4T1 cells were further examined to determine whether ZZFV could induce intracellular acidification and energy depletion by interfering with glucose metabolism and mitochondrial energy metabolism. Intracellular pH was monitored using BCECF. Green fluorescence intensity was significantly reduced in the ZnS, ZIF-Fe, ZZF, and ZZFV groups compared with the control group, indicating increased intracellular acidity (Fig. 5J; Fig. S13). Notably, the ZZFV group exhibited the weakest fluorescence signal, indicating the most acidic intracellular environment among all groups. Moreover, BCECF fluorescence gradually decreased with prolonged ZZFV incubation (Fig. S14), indicating time-dependent intracellular acidification. This acidified intracellular environment may further accelerate ZZFV degradation and active component release, forming a potential positive feedback loop. As shown in Fig. 5K, ATP levels decreased by 29.91%, 24.32%, 51.56%, and 67.01% in the ZnS, ZIF-Fe, ZZF, and ZZFV groups, respectively, while remaining largely unchanged in the Vk3 group. Notably, ATP depletion in the ZZFV group exceeded that observed in the individual component groups, supporting a synergistic inhibitory effect. Mechanistically, Zn2+ mediated LDH inhibition may restrict pyruvate to lactate conversion and disrupt glycolytic flux, whereas H2S mediated impairment of COX IV related function may suppress mitochondrial OXPHOS and ATP synthesis. In addition, reduced lactate formation may decrease H+ consumption during pyruvate conversion, while ATP depletion may weaken ATP-dependent proton extrusion. These combined effects promote intracellular H+ accumulation and aggravate intracellular acidification. Together, these findings indicate that ZZFV disrupts downstream glucose metabolism through H2S and Zn2+-mediated regulation of COX IV and LDH, ultimately leading to reduced lactate production, pyruvate accumulation, ATP depletion, and enhanced intracellular acidification.

Following internalization, ZZFV undergoes intracellular degradation, releasing Fe2+ and Vk3. Within 4T1 cells, Fe2+ catalyzes the conversion of accumulated H2O2 into cytotoxic •OH radicals, whereas Vk3 undergoes NQO1-mediated redox cycling to generate additional H2O2 [47], collectively intensifying intracellular oxidative stress. Intracellular H2O2 levels were quantified using an H2O2 assay kit, and the results showed significantly increased H2O2 accumulation following Vk3 or ZZFV treatment relative to untreated controls. Among these, the ZZFV group showed a 1.92-fold increase in H2O2 content (Fig. S15). ROS act as signaling molecules to maintain cell proliferation and homeostasis; however, excessive ROS accumulation surpasses cellular antioxidant capacity, leading to oxidative damage and apoptosis [48]. DCFH-DA staining was performed to monitor intracellular ROS accumulation. As shown in Fig. 5L, minimal fluorescence was detected in the control and Vk3 groups, whereas the ZnS and ZIF-Fe groups displayed increased fluorescence due to Zn2+- and H2S-induced mitochondrial dysfunction and Fe2+-catalyzed conversion of endogenous H2O2. Notably, the ZZFV group exhibited the strongest fluorescence signal, attributable to the combined effects of mitochondrial dysfunction, elevated H2O2 production, and acidosis-enhanced Fenton reactions, which collectively result in markedly increased ROS accumulation and enhanced antitumor efficacy. Flow cytometry produced results consistent with the fluorescence imaging findings (Fig. 5M; Fig. S16). Elevated ROS levels may further promote oxidative damage and provide an important upstream stimulus for subsequent pyroptosis.

Excessive ROS has been reported to trigger pyroptosis, an inflammatory form of regulated cell death characterized by cell swelling, membrane disruption, and the release of proinflammatory intracellular contents [49, 50]. Subsequently, the morphology of control and ZZFV-treated groups was examined under a microscope (Fig. 5N). Control cells maintained normal morphology, whereas ZZFV-treated cells exhibited prominent swelling and vesicle-like membrane structures, which are typical morphological features of pyroptosis. Consistently, ZZFV treatment markedly increased IL-1β and LDH release, with levels reaching 3.12- and 3.71-fold those of the control group, respectively (Fig. S17, 18). These changes reflect membrane disruption and the release of inflammatory mediators, providing functional evidence for pyroptosis. Western blot analysis further showed that cleaved Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N were markedly upregulated in the ZZFV group, supporting activation of the Caspase-1/GSDMD pyroptotic pathway (Fig. 5O; Fig. S19). Meanwhile, GAPDH protein levels were significantly reduced after ZZFV treatment (Fig. S20). Since GAPDH has been identified as a Caspase-1 substrate and Caspase-1-mediated cleavage can reduce GAPDH abundance [51], the decreased GAPDH level may be partially associated with enhanced Caspase-1 activation. Collectively, the morphological changes, IL-1β and LDH release, and upregulation of cleaved Caspase-1 and GSDMD-N provide strong evidence that ZZFV induces pyroptosis in 4T1 cells.

In vitro ICD induction

ICD refers to a regulated cell death process that enables dying tumor cells to expose or release tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) and key DAMP signals such as calreticulin (CRT), high-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1), and ATP [52, 53], thereby enhancing the immunogenicity of dying tumor cells. These signals facilitate the phagocytosis of tumor cells by immature DCs, promote DC maturation, and enhance tumor-specific T-cell responses [54]. Based on the pronounced ROS amplification induced by ZZFV, we further investigated whether ZZFV could trigger ICD-related immune activation in 4T1 cells. Accordingly, CRT exposure on the cell surface, HMGB1 release, and ATP secretion into the extracellular space were examined following different treatments. Surface-exposed CRT serves as an “eat-me” signal that promotes the engulfment of damaged tumor cells by phagocytes [55]. Immunofluorescence staining revealed markedly enhanced membrane-associated CRT fluorescence in ZZFV-treated cells, in sharp contrast to the undetectable or faint signals observed in the control group (Fig. 6A, B). The enhanced CRT exposure indicates that ZZFV facilitates CRT translocation to the cell surface, suggesting improved immunogenic recognition of damaged tumor cells. Cellular injury drives the extracellular translocation of nuclear HMGB1, thereby stimulating immune activation by promoting neutrophil recruitment and inducing macrophages to secrete proinflammatory cytokines [56]. HMGB1 was mainly localized in the nucleus of untreated cells, whereas ZZFV markedly decreased nuclear HMGB1 fluorescence, suggesting its translocation from the nucleus and subsequent extracellular release (Fig. 6C, D). In parallel, extracellular ATP released from damaged tumor cells facilitates DC maturation, recruits inflammatory cells, and supports cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses [39]. Quantitative analysis of extracellular ATP levels showed that the ZZFV group had the highest extracellular ATP level among all treatment groups, suggesting enhanced ATP release associated with ICD (Fig. 6E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ZZFV-induced ROS elevation triggers ICD, as evidenced by CRT surface exposure, HMGB1 nuclear release, and extracellular ATP efflux, thereby potentially alleviating tumor-associated immunosuppression and promoting antitumor immune responses.

During ICD, released DAMPs can be sensed by DCs through pattern recognition receptors, including Toll-like receptor 4, thereby promoting DC recruitment, activation, and maturation [57]. Mature DCs are characterized by increased co-expression of CD80 and CD86. Given the critical role of DC maturation in T-cell activation in vivo, ZZFV-induced DC maturation was evaluated using a Transwell co-culture model. Bone marrow DCs (BMDCs) were maintained in the presence of ZZFV-treated 4T1 cells for 24 h, followed by quantitative flow cytometry of CD80⁺ and CD86+ cell populations. The proportion of CD80⁺CD86⁺ double-positive DCs increased from 2.40% to 9.89% following ZZFV treatment, significantly exceeding that observed in the control group (Fig. 6F). The increase suggests that ZZFV-treated tumor cells can effectively promote DC maturation, likely owing to enhanced DAMP release during ICD. This effect provides an important cellular basis for subsequent antitumor immune activation.

In vivo therapeutic efficacy

Given the biological relevance of the 4T1 model for evaluating aggressive TNBC tumor growth, immune responses, and spontaneous lung metastasis, ZZFV-mediated tumor inhibition was further evaluated in orthotopic 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. Encouraged by these promising in vitro results, ZZFV was further evaluated for its in vivo antitumor efficacy and anti-metastatic potential in orthotopic 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice (Fig. 7A). Before in vivo antitumor evaluation, the hemocompatibility of ZZFV was assessed by incubating erythrocytes with varying concentrations of the nanomaterial, with distilled water and 0.9% saline serving as positive and negative control samples (Fig. S21). Whereas the positive control produced a bright red supernatant indicative of complete hemolysis, ZZFV-treated samples exhibited hemolysis rates < 5% even at 100 µg mL⁻1, demonstrating excellent hemocompatibility and suitability for intravenous administration. Subsequently, major organs were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging to evaluate the biodistribution and tumor accumulation of ZZFV in mice (Fig. S22). At 24 h post-administration, fluorescence signals in the kidney and liver were markedly reduced, whereas strong fluorescence remained in tumor tissues, suggesting relatively rapid systemic elimination and effective tumor accumulation of ZZFV. Given its effective accumulation at tumor sites, ZZFV may initially undergo partial degradation in the mildly acidic extracellular environment, followed by further disassembly within acidic endosomal/lysosomal compartments after cellular uptake. In parallel, Zn2+- and H2S-mediated intracellular acidification may further enhance the pH-responsive degradation of ZZFV, thereby amplifying the acidification effect and promoting sustained ZZFV disassembly and active component release.

Dynamic changes in tumor volume provide a direct, quantitative measure of therapeutic efficacy in tumor growth inhibition. As shown in Fig. 7B, C, both individual and mean tumor growth curves were monitored throughout the 18-day treatment period. Tumors in the control group grew rapidly, whereas the Vk3, ZnS, and ZIF-Fe monotherapy groups showed only moderate tumor inhibition, likely due to insufficient ROS generation to effectively induce pyroptosis. In contrast, ZZFV treatment resulted in a pronounced 60.92% tumor inhibition (Fig. S23), attributable to synergistic ROS amplification pathways that promote apoptosis and restrict tumor growth. Throughout the treatment period, body weights remained stable across all groups, indicating no obvious systemic toxicity associated with ZZFV administration (Fig. 7D). Consistent with these findings, the excised tumor weights and corresponding digital images further confirmed the superior antitumor efficacy of ZZFV. Tumor weight trends closely matched changes in tumor volume (Fig. 7E), and representative digital images clearly demonstrated the pronounced antitumor efficacy of ZZFV treatment (Fig. 7F). These in vivo results demonstrate that ZZFV effectively suppresses orthotopic 4T1 tumor growth while maintaining good preliminary safety. To further evaluate the in vivo antitumor efficacy and treatment-associated mechanisms, tumor sections were subjected to H&E staining and immunofluorescence (IF) analysis (Fig. 7G). H&E staining revealed densely packed tumor cells with relatively intact tumor architecture in the control group, whereas the ZZFV-treated tumors exhibited pronounced pathological injury characterized by extensive tissue disruption, nuclear pyknosis, karyorrhexis, and nuclear loss. TUNEL staining, which detects apoptotic DNA fragmentation, showed markedly enhanced green fluorescence in the ZZFV-treated tumors (Fig. 7H), indicating extensive DNA damage and tumor cell death rather than merely a reduction in tumor volume. In addition, Ki67 IF staining revealed dramatically reduced red fluorescence in the ZZFV group in stark contrast to all other treatments, and semiquantitative analysis confirmed a significant decrease in tumor cell proliferation (Fig. 7I). These histological and immunofluorescence results indicate that ZZFV suppresses tumor growth by simultaneously promoting tumor cell death and inhibiting tumor cell proliferation. Consistent with the ROS-amplifying mechanism observed in vitro, intratumoral ROS staining showed a pronounced red fluorescence signal in the ZZFV-treated group, indicating substantial ROS elevation in tumor tissues (Fig. S24). Since excessive ROS can promote pyroptosis-related signaling, GSDMD-N expression was further examined. Compared with the control group, ZZFV-treated tumors exhibited markedly increased GSDMD-N levels (Fig. S25), supporting effective pyroptosis induction in vivo. Moreover, enhanced CRT green fluorescence together with reduced nuclear HMGB1 red fluorescence in ZZFV-treated tumors indicated the activation of ICD-associated DAMP signaling (Fig. S26). These results suggest that ZZFV-induced ROS amplification not only promotes tumor cell death but also induces pyroptosis and ICD, thereby providing a mechanistic basis for enhanced antitumor immune activation in vivo. Furthermore, H&E of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys, revealed no evident pathological abnormalities, structural damage, or inflammatory infiltration, suggesting that ZZFV caused no obvious systemic toxicity and exhibited good preliminary in vivo biocompatibility (Fig. S27). Moreover, serum biochemical markers associated with cardiac, hepatic, and renal function remained within normal physiological ranges across all groups (Fig. S28). Collectively, these findings suggest that ZZFV has favorable preliminary in vivo biocompatibility and causes no obvious systemic toxicity under the tested treatment conditions.

Metastatic progression accounts for most breast cancer-related deaths, and patients with distant metastatic lesions continue to have poor five-year survival outcomes [58]. Numerous studies have shown that the lung is among the most common and clinically significant sites of distant metastasis, presenting a major challenge to effective clinical management and long-term patient survival [59]. Based on the observed ability of ZZFV to impede cell migration and activate antitumor immunity, its inhibitory effect on pulmonary metastasis was further evaluated in vivo using animal models. On day 26, all animals were sacrificed, and lung specimens were harvested for histological examination (Fig. 7J). Digital images of lung tissues revealed numerous metastatic nodules in the control group, characterized by rough and irregular surface protrusions typical of the highly aggressive 4T1 metastatic phenotype. In contrast, ZZFV-treated mice displayed only a few scattered nodules, with lung surfaces appearing smooth and morphologically intact, indicating a significant inhibitory effect on metastatic progression. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the ZZFV group harbored significantly fewer metastatic nodules than the other treatment groups (Fig. 7K). These findings were further corroborated via H&E staining. The metastatic nodules appeared as dark purple tumor masses within the lung sections. In the control group, lungs exhibited densely infiltrated clusters of tumor cells, whereas only minimal metastatic infiltration was observed in the ZZFV-treated mice. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ZZFV effectively suppresses metastasis in vivo, likely through the integrated effects of inhibiting tumor growth, suppressing migratory potential, and promoting ICD-associated antitumor immune activation. Overall, these findings indicate that the ions and drug molecules released upon ZZFV degradation collectively elevate intracellular ROS levels, induce ICD, and activate systemic antitumor immunity, ultimately leading to effective suppression of breast cancer progression and metastasis.

In vivo immune activation

Following internalization, pH-triggered degradation of ZZFV liberates Zn2+, Fe2+, H2S, and Vk3, which synergistically increase intracellular ROS levels and induce pyroptosis. This inflammatory form of pyroptosis triggers robust ICD by promoting the release of intracellular contents and DAMPs, thereby activating immune responses and reprogramming the immunosuppressive TME (Fig. 8A). Accordingly, immune activation in an orthotopic 4T1 tumor–bearing mouse model was systematically evaluated following different therapeutic interventions to elucidate the in vivo immunomodulatory mechanisms of the nanoplatform and its potential to reverse the immunosuppressive TME. Flow cytometric analysis was performed to assess changes in immune cell phenotypes and their relative abundances across treatment groups. As potent antigen-presenting cells, mature DCs efficiently capture, process, and present antigens, thereby driving T-cell activation and sustaining adaptive immune responses [60]. Similar to the in vitro observations, the ZZFV-treated group exhibited a markedly elevated proportion of mature DCs in the spleen, reaching 42.27%, the highest level among all groups (Fig. S29; Fig. 8B, C). This result is consistent with ZZFV-induced ICD and suggests that DAMP release from tumor cells may contribute to DC maturation. T cell-mediated cytotoxicity is a central effector mechanism in antitumor immunity, and effective tumor control relies heavily on T-cell activation and intratumoral infiltration [61, 62]. Subsequently, the intratumoral infiltration of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells was quantitatively assessed. The ZZFV group exhibited the highest level of CD3⁺CD4⁺ T-cell infiltration, reaching 11.53%, an 8.96-fold increase compared with the control group (Fig. 8D, E). Similarly, CD3⁺CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration increased to 14.27%, representing a 6.15-fold enhancement compared with the control (Fig. 8F, G). These marked increases in T-cell infiltration underscore the potent antitumor immune activation mediated by ZZFV and contribute to its enhanced antitumor efficacy. Together with the observed DC maturation and intratumoral T-cell infiltration, these findings support the notion that ZZFV induces potent ICD in tumor cells, thereby effectively activating antitumor immunity and ultimately augmenting its antitumor efficacy.

Extensive infiltration of M2-polarized macrophages contributes to an immunosuppressive TME by inhibiting T-cell function and promoting tumor progression, a hallmark of immunologically “cold” tumors. Reprogramming these macrophages toward the proinflammatory M1 phenotype restores inflammatory signaling and antigen-presenting capacity, thereby converting tumors into an immunologically “hot” state conducive to effective antitumor immunity [63, 64]. Given that pyroptosis-mediated ICD can facilitate M2-to-M1 macrophage repolarization, the effect of ZZFV on macrophage phenotypes was further evaluated. Flow cytometry revealed a marked reduction in M2 macrophages in the ZZFV group, decreasing to 3.70% compared with 7.67% in the control group (Fig. S30, 31; Fig. 8H). In contrast, the frequency of M1 macrophages was markedly increased to 20.17% in the ZZFV group compared with 7.97% in the control group, resulting in an increase in the M1/M2 ratio from 1.04 to 5.46 (Fig. 8I). These findings indicate that ZZFV promotes macrophage repolarization toward the M1 phenotype, thereby alleviating intratumoral immunosuppression and strengthening antitumor immune activation within the TME.

Building on the potent immune activation observed with ZZFV, immunological memory activation was further assessed by quantifying effector memory T cells (TEM, CD62L⁻CD44⁺) and central memory T cells (TCM, CD62L⁺CD44⁺) in the spleen. This analysis provides critical insight into ZZFV-driven memory T cell responses associated with long-lasting antitumor immunity (Fig. S32; Fig. 8J). In the ZZFV-treated group, the proportion of TEM cells increased to 15.2%, which was 2.5-fold higher than that in the control group, indicating robust immunological memory activation (Fig. 8K). Similarly, the TCM population increased to 5.21%, representing a 2.87-fold increase relative to the control group (Fig. S33). Collectively, these results indicate that ZZFV effectively remodels the immunosuppressive TME and elicits a strong immune response, which may contribute to sustained antitumor immune surveillance. The flow cytometry results for CD4+ and CD8+ T cells were further corroborated via IF staining. IF imaging revealed markedly increased intratumoral infiltration of CD4 (Fig. 8L) and CD8 (Fig. S34) T cells in the ZZFV group, consistent with the flow cytometry results, further validating the potent immune-activating capability of ZZFV. The relative stability of ZZFV at physiological pH, together with its preferential activation in tumor tissues, may help minimize nonspecific effects on normal immune cells. Enhanced intratumoral CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, together with no apparent systemic toxicity in vivo, supports the ability of ZZFV to maintain antitumor immune activation under the tested conditions. Furthermore, IF staining revealed increased iNOS expression in the ZZFV-treated group (Fig. 8M), indicating increased infiltration of M1-polarized macrophages, whereas Arg-1 fluorescence was markedly reduced (Fig. S35), indicating decreased M2-polarized macrophages. Collectively, these results are consistent with the flow cytometry analysis and further support that ZZFV promotes TAM repolarization toward a proinflammatory M1-like phenotype, thereby contributing to the reversal of the immunosuppressive TME. Although the 4T1 model is widely used to evaluate TNBC-associated immune activation and lung metastasis, the reliance on a single cell line represents a limitation of this study. Future validation in additional TNBC models is needed to further assess the generalizability and translational potential of ZZFV.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed a pH-responsive ZZFV nanoreactor as a multifunctional strategy for TNBC therapy. The main novelty of this work is the integration of ROS amplification, pyroptosis-mediated ICD, and immune microenvironment remodeling into a single nanoreactor. Following cellular internalization, the acidic intracellular environment promotes ZZFV disassembly, leading to the release of Zn2+, Fe2+, Vk3, and H2S. These events induce metabolic disruption and sustained ROS accumulation, ultimately activating the c-Caspase-1/GSDMD-N pathway and triggering pyroptosis-associated ICD. This process promotes the release of DAMPs, DC maturation, T-cell infiltration, and M1 polarization of TAMs, thereby reshaping the immunosuppressive TME and enhancing antitumor immunity. Thus, ZZFV suppresses TNBC progression by coupling direct oxidative damage to tumors with immune activation, thereby inhibiting primary tumor growth, metastasis, and recurrence. Although this study is limited by the use of a single TNBC model, the results suggest that pyroptosis-induced ICD may be a promising approach to enhance TNBC immunogenicity and provide new insights into the development of nanomedicine-based immunotherapies. In addition, this strategy may be effective in other tumors with similar acidic and immunosuppressive microenvironments, although its efficacy may vary depending on their metabolic and biochemical characteristics. Future studies are needed to validate its efficacy across additional models and to thoroughly evaluate its long-term safety and translational potential.

Preparation methods of ZnS/ZIF-Fe/Vk3

Preparation of ZnS: ZnS quantum dots were functionalized with ethylenediamine following previously reported procedures [31, 32]. Briefly, a colloidal solution was prepared by adding zinc acetate dihydrate (0.25 mmol) and ethylenediamine (500 µL) to 50 mL of deionized water under stirring, followed by the addition of sodium sulfide (0.25 mmol). The reaction was maintained under reflux at 120 °C for 2 h to obtain ZnS. After centrifugation and washing with distilled water and anhydrous ethanol, the precipitate was dispersed in 5 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF) for subsequent use.

Preparation of ZIF-90: In a typical synthesis, Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O (2 mL, 0.05 mol L⁻¹ in DMF) and 2-ICA (4 mL, 0.05 mol L⁻¹ in DMF) were combined and vigorously stirred for 1 h. The resulting solid was collected via centrifugation, washed, and dried under vacuum to yield ZIF-90.

Preparation of ZIF-90:Fe (ZIF-Fe): In a typical synthesis, Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O (2 mL, 0.05 mol L⁻¹ in DMF), Fe(CH3COO)2·4H2O (0.01 mmol), and 2-ICA (4 mL, 0.05 mol L⁻¹ in DMF) were mixed and vigorously stirred for 1 h. The resulting solid was collected via centrifugation, washed, and dried under vacuum to obtain ZIF-90:Fe (ZIF-Fe).

Preparation of ZnS/ZIF-Fe (ZZF): ZnS solution (1 mL), Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O (1 mL, 0.1 mol L⁻¹ in DMF), Fe(CH3COO)2·4H2O (0.01 mmol), and 2-ICA (4 mL, 0.05 mol L⁻¹ in DMF) were sequentially added to a reaction vessel and stirred for 1 h. After centrifugation, the obtained solid was washed and vacuum-dried to yield ZnS/ZIF-Fe (ZZF).

Preparation of ZnS/ZIF-Fe/Vk3 (ZZFV): The final ZnS/ZIF-Fe/Vk3 (ZZFV) composite was prepared by sonicating the as-prepared ZZF powder (5 mg) in anhydrous ethanol (5 mL), followed by the addition of Vk3 (0.1 mg). After 12 h of stirring at room temperature, the product was separated by centrifugation and thoroughly rinsed with ethanol.

Material characterization

Nanoparticle morphology was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HT7800, Hitachi). Crystal structures were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm) over 2θ = 5°–90°. BET surface areas were measured using an Autosorb-iQ analyzer (Anton Paar). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher) with a monochromatic Al Kα source was used to analyze elemental composition and valence states. Zinc and iron content were quantified by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES, Agilent 5800 VDV). Electron spin resonance (ESR) spectra were collected on an EMXmicro spectrometer (Bruker).

pH-Dependent Zn2+, Fe2+, and Vk3 release from ZZFV

To evaluate the pH-dependent release behavior, 30 mg ZZFV was dispersed in 15 mL PBS buffers at pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4 under stirring. At predetermined intervals (0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h), 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn and centrifuged for supernatant analysis. The pellets were then redispersed in fresh PBS (1 mL) by sonication and returned to the original suspension. Zn2+ and Fe2+ concentrations in the supernatants were determined using ICP-OES, whereas Vk3 concentrations were analyzed using a microplate reader.

pH-Dependent H2S release from ZZFV

H2S production was assessed qualitatively using Pb(CH3COO)2 test paper and quantitatively using an H2S assay kit. ZZFV was dispersed in PBS at pH 5.0, 6.5, and 7.4 at 2 mg mL⁻¹ and kept at room temperature. Pb(CH3COO)2 test paper was affixed to the inner surface of the petri dish lid and photographed at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 12 h to document color changes. For quantitative analysis, ZZFV was prepared at 500 µg mL⁻¹ in PBS under identical conditions, and H2S concentrations were measured at the same time points using an H2S assay kit.

Determination of •OH generation

The generation of •OH was quantified using TMB as the substrate in the presence of H2O2. Reaction mixtures containing TMB, H2O2, and ZZFV were prepared in buffers of varying pH. The effects of H2O2 (25, 50, 75, 100, and 150 µM) and ZZFV (25, 50, 75, 100, and 150 µg mL⁻¹) were examined in a pH 5.0 buffer. In parallel, •OH generation was determined via ESR spectroscopy. DMPO was employed as a spin trap for •OH, dissolved in ultrapure water, and added to the ZZFV dispersion. The ESR spectra were then recorded at room temperature after reaction times of 0, 5, and 10 min.

Cell line and culture conditions

The 4T1 mouse breast cancer cell line was acquired from the Shanghai Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified environment containing 5% CO2.

Cell viability and apoptosis assays

CCK-8 assays were performed to assess metabolic activity, while Calcein-AM/PI and Annexin V-FITC/PI staining were used to assess Live/Dead status and apoptosis, respectively, to evaluate cell viability in vitro. 4T1 cells were plated in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and exposed to different concentrations of ZZFV for 24–48 h at pH 7.4 or 6.0. Cell viability was evaluated after CCK-8 incubation by measuring absorbance. For live/dead staining, 4T1 cells were plated in 6-well plates, treated for 24 h, and stained with Calcein-AM/PI. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, BX53, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 10× objective lens and a digital camera.

Fluorometric assay

4T1 cells were plated in a 6-well plate, subjected to various treatments, and incubated for 12 h after adherence. Intracellular levels of H2S, glucose, Zn2+, pH, and ROS were assessed using WSP-1, 2-NBDG, TSQ, BCECF-AM, and DCFH-DA, respectively. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 min, fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ts2, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 20× objective lens and a digital camera. For confocal analysis, 4T1 cells were seeded in confocal dishes, treated for 12 h, and then stained with JC-1 and Hoechst 33,342. Confocal fluorescence images were obtained using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, LSM 880, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil-immersion objective lens.

Flow cytometric assay

4T1 cells were seeded in a 6-well plate and treated under different conditions upon reaching 80% confluence. FITC-labeled ZZFV was added at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 h to evaluate cellular uptake, which was subsequently quantified using flow cytometry. Following attachment, cells were subjected to different treatments and incubated for 12 h. To assess intracellular H2S, glucose, and ROS levels, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA, WSP-1, and 2-NBDG for 30 min at 37 °C, followed by flow cytometric analysis.

Determination of intracellular H2O2, pyruvate, and ATP

4T1 cells were seeded in 6-cm cell culture dishes and treated under different conditions after reaching 80% confluence. The cells were then subjected to various treatments and incubated for 12 h. After incubation, the cells were collected, disrupted using an ultrasonic disruptor, and centrifuged to collect the supernatants. Intracellular levels of H2O2, pyruvate, and ATP were quantified using the corresponding assay kits.

Determination of extracellular IL-1β, LDH, and ATP

4T1 cells were plated in a 6-well plate, allowed to adhere, subjected to various treatments, and incubated for 12 h. Extracellular ATP and LDH levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using commercial kits, while IL-1β concentrations were determined by ELISA. All assays were conducted according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Western blotting

4T1 cells were grown in 6-cm culture dishes until approximately 80% confluent and then subjected to the indicated treatments. Cells were washed with PBS and lysed on ice in RIPA buffer containing 1% protease and phosphatase inhibitors. After protein quantification by BCA assay, equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and wet-transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk, incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against cleaved Caspase-1 (1:1000), GSDMD-N (1:1000), COX IV (1:1000), GAPDH (1:5000), and β-actin (1:5000), and then the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL detection system and analyzed with ImageJ software.

Immunofluorescence of CRT and HMGB1

After adhesion in confocal dishes, 4T1 cells were exposed to the indicated treatments for 12 h. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilization with Triton X-100, and blocking with 5% BSA, samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-CRT (1:200) and anti-HMGB1 (1:250) primary antibodies. Secondary staining was performed using CoraLite488- or CoraLite594-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG antibodies (1:200) for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using a confocal laser scanning microscope (ZEISS, LSM 880, Oberkochen, Germany) equipped with a 63× oil-immersion objective lens.

Wound healing assay

4T1 cells were grown to confluence in 6-well plates and serum-starved in medium containing 1% serum for 24 h. The cell monolayer was scratched with a 200 µL pipette tip and gently rinsed with PBS to remove detached cells. After the experimental treatments, cells were maintained at 37 °C, and wound closure was monitored at 0, 12, and 24 h using optical microscopy. The wound area was imaged in bright-field mode using an inverted microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ts2, Tokyo, Japan) equipped with a 4× objective and a digital camera.

DC maturation

Bone marrow cells (BMCs) were isolated from the femurs of 6–8-week-old female BALB/c mice by flushing the marrow cavity with a syringe. After filtration through a cell strainer, cells were collected by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. BMCs were differentiated into BMDCs in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with GM-CSF (20 ng mL− 1) and IL-4 (10 ng mL− 1) in 6-well plates at 37 °C under 5% CO2. On day 3, half of the medium was replaced with fresh cytokine-containing medium. Suspended cells harvested from days 5 to 8 were used as immature BMDCs. For co-culture, immature BMDCs were seeded in the lower chamber of a Corning Transwell system equipped with 6-well polycarbonate (PC) membrane inserts (24 mm diameter, 0.4 μm pore size), while 4T1 cells were added to the upper chamber. Following different treatments, 4T1 cells were co-cultured with immature DCs for 24 h. Suspended cells were collected and stained with anti-CD11c–APC, anti-CD80–PE, and anti-CD86–FITC antibodies, followed by flow cytometric analysis of DC maturation.

Hemolysis assay

Blood was collected from the posterior orbital plexus of BALB/c mice and centrifuged to obtain red blood cells (RBCs). After being washed with saline until the supernatant was nearly colorless, the RBCs were resuspended in saline to reach a 10% volume fraction. Aliquots of the RBC suspension were mixed with ZZFV to achieve final ZZFV concentrations of 0, 3.1, 6.2, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg mL–1. Hemolysis was evaluated visually after incubation at 37 °C for 3 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant absorbance at 570 nm was recorded with a UV-vis spectrophotometer, and the hemolysis rate (%) was calculated using the following equation:

Hemolysis rate (%) = (Asample – Anegative)/(Apositive – Anegative) × 100%.

Tumor model establishment

Six-week-old female BALB/c mice obtained from Beijing SPF Biotechnology (Beijing, China) were used to establish an orthotopic mammary tumor model by injecting 4T1 cells (50 µL, 5 × 106 cells mL⁻¹) into the right fourth mammary fat pad.

Treatment of mice

At 7 days post-inoculation, tumor volumes were measured, and tumor-bearing mice with comparable baseline tumor volumes of approximately 100 mm3 were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 5): Control, Vk3, ZnS, ZIF-Fe, ZZF, and ZZFV. Controls were given an equal volume of saline, while mice in the treatment groups received the corresponding saline-dispersed nanomaterials via tail vein injection at 2.5 mg kg⁻¹ on days 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12. This regimen enabled sustained treatment and helped assess the tolerability and safety of the material after multiple administrations, thereby supporting its further in vivo application. The tumor volume was calculated using the formula V = L × W2/2, with L and W representing the major and minor diameters, respectively. Following the 14-day treatment period, mice were monitored for an additional four days before euthanasia for subsequent analyses. Tumor volume and body weight were recorded every other day throughout the study.

Immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues

Tumor tissues were harvested and processed for immunofluorescence staining of TUNEL, Ki67 (1:200), ROS, GSDMD-N (1:200), CRT (1:200), and HMGB1 (1:200). Tissue samples used for ROS fluorescence detection were processed as frozen sections, whereas tissue samples used for other immunofluorescence staining were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, subjected to antigen retrieval, and blocked with 5% BSA. The sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and then labeled with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cy3, 1:300; FITC, 1:200) at room temperature. Finally, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, followed by PBS washing. Stained sections were imaged with a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, ECLIPSE Ts2, Tokyo, Japan) fitted with a 20× objective lens and a digital camera. Semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence signals was performed with ImageJ using uniform parameters.

Antitumor immunity activation evaluation

On day 18, tumors and spleens were harvested to assess the in vivo antitumor immune responses. Tumor and splenic tissues were mechanically dissociated in 1640 medium to obtain single-cell suspensions. Tumor tissues were washed with pre-chilled PBS, digested at 37 °C, and filtered through a nylon mesh to remove residual debris. After centrifugation and PBS washing, cells were treated with erythrocyte lysis buffer for 5 min at room temperature, then terminated with pre-chilled saline. The cells were finally collected and resuspended in flow cytometry buffer. Spleens were aseptically collected and mechanically passed through a 70 μm cell strainer to generate single-cell suspensions. After centrifugation, red blood cells were lysed, and the remaining cells were washed with PBS and resuspended in staining buffer. To assess DC maturation, splenic mononuclear cells were labeled with anti-CD11c–APC, anti-CD80–PE, and anti-CD86–FITC antibodies. Memory T-cell subsets were analyzed by staining splenic mononuclear cells with anti-CD3–FITC, anti-CD8a–PE, anti-CD44–APC, and anti-CD62L–PerCP antibodies. T-cell activation was assessed by staining tumor-derived mononuclear cells with anti-CD3–FITC, anti-CD4–PE, and anti-CD8a–PE antibodies. Macrophage polarization was evaluated by staining tumor-infiltrating mononuclear cells with anti-F4/80–PE, anti-CD16/32, anti-CD86–FITC, and anti-CD206–APC antibodies. For TAM analysis by flow cytometry, Fc receptors were blocked with anti-CD16/32 prior to antibody labeling of single-cell suspensions. Dead cell exclusion staining was not included in the flow cytometry protocol. All samples underwent flow cytometric evaluation. Concurrently, immunofluorescence staining was performed to assess the distribution of M1 (iNOS, 1:1000) and M2 (Arg-1, 1:500) macrophages, and CD4 (1:2000) and CD8 (1:5000) T cells within the TME. A fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Eclipse Ti2, Tokyo, Japan) fitted with a 20× objective lens and a digital camera was used to capture fluorescence images.

Biosafety assessment

At the experimental endpoint, blood samples and major organs were collected from each mouse for further analysis. Major organ samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, then paraffin-embedded and sectioned. Serum biochemical parameters were quantitatively evaluated using an automated biochemical analyzer to assess hepatic (ALP, ALT, and AST), renal (UA, UREA, and CREA), and cardiac (CK, LDH, and HBDH) function. Tissue sections from major organs were prepared and stained with H&E for histopathological analysis. A quantitative assessment of visible metastatic nodules on the lung surface was also performed.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative results are expressed as means ± standard deviation (SD). Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.0, and intergroup differences were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Before parametric analyses, the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were examined in GraphPad Prism 10.0, using the Shapiro–Wilk test for normality and the Brown–Forsythe test for homogeneity of variance. Unless otherwise stated, n represents the number of independent biological replicates. Statistical significance is indicated as ns, not significant; *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; and ***p < 0.001.
