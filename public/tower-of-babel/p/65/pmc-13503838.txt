Learning-driven multi-timescale operation simulation and hierarchical boundary optimization for renewable-dominated energy systems under temporal and scenario uncertainties
Authors: Qian Ma, Jin Zou, He Huang, Jinbing Liang, Siyu Lu, Jialu Li, Qiang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper addresses the challenge of coordinating power system operation across multiple temporal horizons under high renewable penetration and scenario uncertainty, where conventional decoupled scheduling frameworks fail to maintain operational consistency and adaptability. To overcome this limitation, we propose a learning-driven multi-timescale operation simulation and hierarchical boundary optimization framework that integrates deep sequence forecasting, reinforcement learning, and rolling model predictive control within a unified optimization structure. The framework dynamically constructs and updates operational boundaries across long-term, short-term, and rolling horizons, enabling continuous coordination between planning and real-time control. Case studies on a renewable-dominated IEEE 118-bus system demonstrate that the proposed approach significantly improves system performance. Compared with conventional decoupled scheduling, the framework reduces total operating cost by approximately 9.6%, increases system reliability by 15.8%, and lowers renewable curtailment from 9.5% to 5.7%. In addition, boundary deviations during rolling operation are constrained within a narrow band below 2%, confirming the effectiveness of the adaptive boundary-learning mechanism. These results indicate that the proposed framework not only enhances economic efficiency and operational robustness, but also establishes a new paradigm for integrating learning and optimization in multi-timescale energy system management.Subject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In modern power systems, the increasing integration of renewable energy sources, the proliferation of distributed generation, and the electrification of transportation and industry are fundamentally altering the operational landscape1,2. The conventional paradigm of static, deterministic scheduling–where planning horizons are separated and optimized independently–is no longer sufficient to ensure secure and efficient operation. Renewable energy, characterized by high temporal and spatial variability, introduces nonlinear and non-stationary behaviors into the system. These dynamics propagate across multiple timescales, from monthly fuel scheduling and maintenance planning to daily boundary setting and real-time dispatch control3,4. As renewable penetration deepens, operational consistency across timescales becomes a critical requirement, as mismatches between planning assumptions and real-time conditions can lead to cost inefficiency, energy curtailment, or even system instability. Consequently, the challenge facing contemporary research is to develop a unified, adaptive, and data-driven operational framework that bridges the gap between long-term strategic planning and real-time responsive control5,6.

The evolution of multi-timescale operation modeling in the literature has followed a logical but limited trajectory. Early efforts relied on deterministic optimization frameworks–primarily linear or mixed-integer formulations–to coordinate between long-term maintenance scheduling and short-term dispatch decisions7. These models, while mathematically elegant, were constructed under rigid assumptions of predictability, treating uncertainties as bounded perturbations rather than as endogenous dynamics that evolve with time. As renewable variability became more pronounced, researchers sought to incorporate uncertainty through stochastic programming, where scenario trees and probability distributions attempted to capture future randomness. Although this class of models improved realism, its computational complexity grew exponentially with the number of scenarios and time stages, rendering it impractical for real systems with high-dimensional uncertainties. Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) frameworks emerged to relax dependence on precise probability distributions by defining ambiguity sets, but despite their theoretical appeal, these approaches remained computationally expensive and insufficiently adaptive to online data streams8–10.

Parallel to this line of development, the concept of hierarchical or multi-layered coordination gained traction as a way to manage the intrinsic coupling among different operational horizons. Researchers decomposed system operation into three layers–long-term, short-term, and real-time–and employed coordination strategies such as bilevel optimization, iterative boundary exchange, and rolling-horizon schemes. In these models, upper-level layers set boundary conditions that guided the optimization of lower layers9,11. For instance, long-term layers determined maintenance plans, fuel allocations, and regional exchange limits, while short-term layers optimized daily unit commitment and dispatch decisions. Real-time layers, in turn, fine-tuned control actions based on actual system feedback. Although such frameworks improved structural coherence, they still relied on predefined parameters, static forecasts, and fixed model assumptions, limiting their ability to adapt to evolving conditions. They could coordinate across timescales but could not learn from the data they generated.

The rise of data-driven learning methods has begun to transform this landscape. The growing availability of operational data–renewable generation profiles, load curves, weather conditions, and market transactions–has catalyzed the use of machine learning for forecasting and decision-making in energy systems. Deep learning architectures such as Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCNs) have proven exceptionally capable of extracting temporal patterns and long-term dependencies from complex time series12–14. LSTM networks, by design, capture both short- and long-term dependencies, making them ideal for predicting multi-day or multi-week operation boundaries, while TCNs, with their dilated convolutional structure, are adept at identifying short-term fluctuations within high-frequency data streams. Studies using these architectures have demonstrated improvements in renewable forecasting accuracy, which in turn enhances reliability and reduces reserve costs. However, most applications have treated deep learning solely as a forecasting tool, leaving a gap between prediction and optimization.

Reinforcement learning (RL) represents another major advance in decision-oriented data-driven methods. Unlike supervised learning, which learns static mappings, RL learns through interaction with the environment, optimizing a control policy that maximizes long-term reward. In power system contexts, RL has been successfully applied to voltage regulation, frequency control, and energy storage scheduling. Algorithms such as Deep Q-Networks (DQN), Policy Gradient methods, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) have shown strong performance in high-dimensional and uncertain operational environments15–17. Among these, PPO has emerged as a particularly stable and scalable choice, as it constrains policy updates within trust regions, ensuring steady convergence. By combining function approximation with continuous feedback, PPO can learn adaptive operational strategies that generalize across changing system states. Nevertheless, most RL applications in the literature focus on isolated timescales–typically real-time control–without embedding reinforcement learning into hierarchical, multi-horizon structures. Bridging this gap requires a paradigm that synthesizes predictive modeling, decision optimization, and adaptive control within a unified multi-timescale framework18,19. This paper contributes to that emerging paradigm by proposing a learning-driven hierarchical operation simulation framework that integrates LSTM–TCN-based forecasting, PPO-based reinforcement learning, and online adaptive learning with model predictive control (MPC). The core idea is to couple forecasting, decision-making, and continuous adaptation into a closed-loop process. At the medium-to-long-term level, deep learning models generate monthly and ten-day operational boundaries that reflect maintenance requirements, inter-regional power exchange capacities, and renewable availability. These boundaries then serve as strategic guidelines for lower layers. In the short-term layer, a reinforcement learning agent trained via PPO interacts with a simulation environment to learn optimal daily dispatch strategies that minimize operating costs and maintain system stability. Finally, the rolling operation layer employs online learning and MPC to continuously correct boundaries based on observed deviations and forecast updates. Together, these components form a dynamic and coherent learning architecture capable of both anticipatory planning and reactive adaptation.

Recent studies have also explored deep learning ensemble-based optimization strategies in prognostics and predictive maintenance20. For example, recent work on dynamic burn-in optimization integrates deep learning ensembles with precise remaining useful life prediction for lithium-ion batteries, while wrapper cascade-stacking frameworks have been developed for joint health-state and lifetime prediction21. In addition, deep learning–stochastic ensemble models have been proposed to support predictive maintenance and operational decision policies under uncertainty. These studies highlight the growing importance of ensemble-based learning in reliability-oriented optimization problems and further motivate the integration of learning and decision mechanisms adopted in this work22.

From a modeling perspective, the framework is anchored in a hierarchical optimization formulation that captures the cost structure and operational coupling across all temporal layers. The objective function aggregates economic costs–fuel consumption, maintenance, and penalties for renewable curtailment and boundary violations–while the constraints enforce power balance, generator and storage limits, ramping rates, reserve adequacy, and temporal boundary continuity. By embedding learning modules directly into this optimization structure, the framework enables parameters such as forecasted boundaries and operational policies to evolve dynamically, guided by data rather than static assumptions. This fusion of learning and optimization not only enhances accuracy but also improves computational tractability, since learned models replace repeated scenario generation with efficient functional approximations. The methodological synergy among LSTM, TCN, PPO, and MPC marks a departure from previous research. Whereas earlier studies typically employed one of these techniques in isolation, the proposed integration creates a hierarchical intelligence structure where information flows both top-down and bottom-up. Predictions from the upper-level LSTM–TCN models inform the PPO agent’s policy learning, while feedback from the rolling MPC layer updates the upper-level predictions in real time. This bidirectional flow of information ensures that long-term plans remain responsive to short-term realities and that short-term decisions remain consistent with strategic objectives. Moreover, the learning-based formulation eliminates the need for explicit probability distributions or manually designed scenario sets, allowing the system to learn directly from empirical data streams and adapt continuously as new patterns emerge.

Despite extensive research on multi-timescale operation, several critical gaps remain. First, most existing studies treat different temporal horizons as loosely coupled layers, where long-term planning provides static boundary parameters that are not adaptively updated based on short-term operational feedback. This separation limits the ability of the system to respond coherently to evolving renewable uncertainty. Second, while machine learning and reinforcement learning methods have been introduced into power system operation, they are typically applied to isolated tasks such as forecasting or real-time control, rather than being embedded within a unified hierarchical optimization structure. As a result, current approaches lack a closed-loop mechanism through which prediction, decision-making, and boundary adaptation can interact dynamically across timescales. Third, existing coordination frameworks rarely provide an explicit mathematical formulation for boundary evolution itself, leaving the inter-temporal consistency problem only partially addressed.

To fill these gaps, this paper proposes a learning-driven multi-timescale operation simulation and hierarchical boundary optimization framework that fundamentally redefines how operational limits are generated and updated. The key contributions of this work are threefold. First, we introduce a dynamic boundary-learning mechanism in which operational boundaries are not predefined but continuously inferred from historical patterns, forecast information, and rolling operational feedback. Second, we develop a unified hierarchical optimization structure that integrates LSTM–TCN-based boundary prediction, PPO-based decision learning, and MPC-based rolling correction into a single closed-loop architecture, enabling consistent coordination from monthly planning to real-time operation. Third, we formulate a mathematically explicit boundary-coupled optimization model that captures cross-horizon consistency and enables adaptive boundary evolution without requiring scenario convergence assumptions. Through these innovations, the proposed framework establishes a new paradigm for learning-enabled coordination in renewable-dominated power systems.

To clearly position the technical novelty of this work, we emphasize that the central contribution of the proposed framework lies in a boundary-learning coupling mechanism that dynamically links prediction, decision-making, and rolling correction across multiple temporal horizons. Unlike conventional approaches where operational boundaries are fixed parameters derived from planning models, this study introduces an adaptive boundary update law in which the operational limits evolve as a function of historical patterns, forecast outputs, and real-time deviations. Formally, the boundary evolution is governed by a recursive update relation that continuously adjusts the boundary state based on the discrepancy between predicted and observed operational conditions, thereby embedding temporal learning directly into the optimization structure.

This mechanism differs fundamentally from several closely related state-of-the-art approaches. First, compared with stochastic or distributionally robust optimization methods, which represent uncertainty through scenario sets or ambiguity regions, the proposed framework does not rely on predefined probabilistic structures. Instead, boundary adaptation is learned directly from data and updated online, allowing the system to respond to evolving uncertainty without increasing scenario dimensionality. Second, in contrast to hierarchical scheduling models that exchange static boundary parameters between layers, the proposed method establishes a bidirectional coupling mechanism in which boundaries are continuously revised through feedback from lower-layer operations, forming a closed-loop coordination structure rather than a one-way planning cascade. Third, compared with recent reinforcement-learning-based dispatch frameworks that learn control policies while keeping system constraints fixed, this study allows the constraint set itself to evolve through learning, effectively transforming operational boundaries into adaptive state variables.

Through this formulation, the proposed approach introduces a new paradigm in which boundary evolution becomes an endogenous component of the optimization problem rather than an exogenous input. This dynamic coupling mechanism provides a formal bridge between forecasting, learning, and control, enabling consistent multi-timescale coordination that cannot be achieved by existing scenario-based, hierarchical, or policy-learning methods alone.

To evaluate the scalability of the proposed framework, we conducted an additional analysis of computational complexity and runtime behavior under increasing system size. The framework consists of three main computational components: the forecasting module, the reinforcement learning training stage, and the online optimization stage. The forecasting and learning components are trained offline, and their computational burden grows mainly with the length of historical data and the size of the neural network, rather than directly with the number of system buses or generators. As a result, once trained, these components can be reused without significantly increasing runtime for larger systems.

For the operational stage, the short-term dispatch optimization and rolling control were tested on systems of different sizes by progressively increasing the number of generators, renewable units, and interregional links. The results show that runtime increases approximately proportionally with the number of controllable units, indicating near-linear scaling behavior. Even when the system size was doubled, the total runtime increased by less than two times, demonstrating that the proposed hierarchical structure avoids exponential growth in computational effort.

This favorable scaling behavior arises from two structural features of the method. First, the hierarchical decomposition separates long-term planning, short-term scheduling, and rolling correction, preventing the optimization from being solved as a single large problem. Second, the learning-based boundary mechanism reduces the need for repeated scenario enumeration, which typically dominates the computational cost in traditional stochastic approaches. These properties together ensure that the proposed framework remains computationally feasible for large-scale renewable-dominated power systems.

In contrast to existing multi-timescale operation frameworks, the proposed method introduces a fundamentally different perspective by treating operational boundaries as adaptive decision variables rather than fixed parameters derived from planning models. This conceptual shift enables the system to continuously refine its feasible operating region based on both historical patterns and real-time feedback, thereby establishing a dynamic coupling mechanism across temporal horizons. Furthermore, while previous studies typically apply forecasting, reinforcement learning, or optimization techniques in isolation, this work integrates LSTM–TCN-based boundary prediction, PPO-based decision learning, and MPC-based rolling correction into a unified closed-loop architecture. This integration allows prediction, decision-making, and boundary adaptation to interact iteratively, ensuring consistency between long-term planning and real-time operation. Additionally, unlike stochastic or distributionally robust approaches that rely on predefined scenarios or ambiguity sets, the proposed framework learns uncertainty directly from data and updates operational limits online, improving both adaptability and computational efficiency. These features collectively distinguish the proposed method as a learning-driven, boundary-adaptive, and fully integrated multi-timescale operation paradigm.

The growing literature on hybrid deep learning and RL architectures in energy systems provides a foundation for this integration. Several studies have explored using deep learning to forecast renewable power and RL to determine energy management strategies, but most of these works focus on isolated or small-scale systems, such as microgrids or building-level demand response. The present framework extends this idea to the system-wide level, addressing multi-timescale coordination from months to minutes. It embeds learning components within a mathematically rigorous optimization model that governs power flow, reserve margins, and inter-regional exchanges. The result is a theoretically grounded yet practically adaptive framework capable of large-scale implementation. Through this fusion, the proposed framework transcends the limitations of both traditional optimization and pure learning methods. It replaces static boundary definitions with dynamic, learning-derived ones; substitutes rigid scenario sets with continuously updated data-driven forecasts; and unites strategic and operational layers through a shared learning backbone. The approach thus transforms the power system from a reactive operator-controlled structure into an intelligent, self-correcting cyber-physical entity capable of learning from its own operational history. Such integration aligns with the emerging vision of intelligent energy systems–autonomous infrastructures that anticipate uncertainty, adapt policies in real time, and maintain robustness without human intervention. The overarching aim of this study is to provide a mathematically rigorous and algorithmically efficient approach for coordinated operation under uncertainty. By designing a hierarchical model that learns across timescales, the paper contributes a novel method that unites long-term foresight with short-term adaptability. The simulation results, although not detailed here, are designed to illustrate the efficacy of the approach in reducing operational cost and enhancing renewable integration efficiency. Beyond immediate applications, the proposed framework represents a conceptual advancement in how learning and optimization interact within energy systems, offering a blueprint for future research into self-learning, multi-scale coordination under complex, data-rich conditions. It suggests that the boundary between predictive intelligence and operational optimization can be dissolved, yielding a single, cohesive system that plans, acts, and learns simultaneously across time.

Problem formulation

The mathematical modeling framework forms the analytical core of this study, establishing a unified representation of system operation across three temporal levels: long-term, short-term, and rolling horizons. Each level represents a different layer of decision granularity yet remains dynamically linked through boundary coordination that ensures operational consistency. The objective is to capture both the economic and physical essence of the system while accommodating variability introduced by renewable generation and fluctuating demand. The modeling design structures the problem as a hierarchy of cost minimization and reliability enhancement. The upper layer defines planning boundaries covering monthly and decadal operation, where maintenance, transmission capability, and primary energy generation are determined. The intermediate layer refines these constraints for daily scheduling, guided by forecast updates and network conditions. The lowest layer, operating in a rolling fashion, continuously adjusts operational limits based on real-time information and scenario realizations. This hierarchical coupling between layers ensures that planning remains flexible while short-term decisions remain physically feasible and economically optimal.

Methodology

To clarify the core idea of the proposed method, this study develops a learning-driven multi-timescale operation framework in which operational boundaries are treated as adaptive decision variables rather than fixed planning parameters. The method integrates three tightly coupled components within a unified hierarchical structure. At the upper level, a hybrid LSTM–TCN model learns temporal correlations from historical data to generate predictive operational boundaries across long-term and short-term horizons. At the intermediate level, a reinforcement-learning agent trained via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) determines optimal daily dispatch decisions while interacting with a simulation environment constrained by these learned boundaries. At the rolling level, a model predictive control (MPC) mechanism continuously updates the operational limits based on real-time deviations and forecast corrections.

Through this closed-loop interaction, prediction, decision-making, and boundary adjustment are performed simultaneously rather than sequentially. In contrast to conventional approaches where boundaries are predefined and remain static, the proposed method enables operational limits to evolve dynamically according to both historical patterns and real-time system feedback. This adaptive boundary-learning mechanism constitutes the central innovation of the framework and allows consistent coordination across multiple temporal horizons.

To clarify the roles of learning and optimization in the proposed framework, we explicitly distinguish which decisions are produced by data-driven models and which are determined through optimization procedures. In the proposed architecture, the learning modules are responsible for generating predictive information and adaptive operational limits, while the optimization modules determine concrete dispatch actions subject to these learned constraints.

Specifically, the learning components are used to infer operational boundaries, forecast renewable availability, and capture temporal patterns in system behavior. These outputs provide adaptive guidance for system operation but do not directly determine generator schedules or control actions. Instead, the optimization layer uses this learned information to compute dispatch decisions that minimize operational cost while satisfying physical and security constraints. The reinforcement learning agent operates at an intermediate level, where it learns decision policies for daily scheduling by interacting with the simulation environment, but the resulting actions are still evaluated within the optimization-based feasibility structure.

In the rolling stage, the model predictive control mechanism adjusts short-term operational limits based on observed deviations, while the optimization solver continues to determine the final control actions. Therefore, learning in the proposed framework is primarily responsible for generating predictive structure and adaptive constraints, whereas optimization is responsible for computing the final operational decisions. This separation ensures both adaptability and physical feasibility, allowing the framework to combine data-driven intelligence with rigorous system-level optimization.

The methodology integrates learning mechanisms with optimization principles to achieve adaptive coordination among multiple temporal horizons. Rather than treating prediction and optimization as separate processes, the framework fuses them within a single iterative structure. The learning component extracts temporal correlations from historical and simulated data, while the optimization component embeds these learned relationships into the decision process through adaptive boundary updates.

The methodological workflow proceeds in three coordinated phases. First, the learning module identifies how past operation patterns influence boundary evolution, using sequence-based models that interpret temporal dependencies. Second, the simulation module executes multi-horizon optimization under the constraints derived from the learned parameters, updating boundaries according to the most recent observations. Third, a rolling coordination mechanism links the two modules by feeding back operational results into the learning process, thus refining predictions and boundary settings for subsequent cycles. Through this closed-loop design, the framework evolves toward higher accuracy, faster convergence, and greater resilience against forecast and scenario uncertainty.

The proposed framework addresses uncertainty through a multi-layer learning–adaptation mechanism that operates across prediction, decision-making, and real-time correction stages. Uncertainty in the system arises primarily from renewable generation variability, load fluctuations, and forecast errors that propagate across temporal horizons. Rather than representing these uncertainties through predefined scenario sets or probability distributions, the proposed method incorporates them directly into the learning and control process.

At the forecasting level, the hybrid LSTM–TCN architecture captures both long-term temporal correlations and short-term fluctuations from historical operational data. This allows the predicted operational boundaries to implicitly encode uncertainty patterns without relying on explicit stochastic modeling. At the decision level, the PPO-based reinforcement learning agent is trained through repeated interaction with a simulation environment containing variable renewable outputs, demand profiles, and disturbance realizations. This exposure enables the learned policy to internalize uncertainty and select robust dispatch actions that perform well across diverse operating conditions.

At the rolling level, the MPC-based correction mechanism provides an additional safeguard by continuously adjusting operational limits based on observed deviations between predicted and realized system states. This real-time feedback loop ensures that prediction errors or unexpected disturbances do not accumulate across timescales but are progressively corrected.

Through this three-stage mechanism–learning-based forecasting, uncertainty-aware policy training, and real-time adaptive correction–the framework transforms uncertainty from an external disturbance into an endogenous component of the operational learning process. This enables the system to maintain stability and efficiency without requiring explicit probabilistic scenario enumeration, thereby improving both robustness and computational tractability.

To evaluate the robustness of the proposed framework with respect to computational parameters, we conducted a sensitivity analysis covering key learning and optimization settings, including neural network size, reinforcement learning learning rate, and MPC weighting coefficients. The objective of this analysis was to examine whether the performance improvements observed in the case study remain stable under reasonable parameter variations.

First, the LSTM–TCN architecture was tested under different hidden-layer sizes (64–192 units) and dilation depths for the TCN component. The resulting variation in forecasting error remained within ±3.2%, and the corresponding operational cost changes did not exceed 1.4%, indicating that the framework is not highly sensitive to moderate adjustments in network scale. Second, the PPO learning rate and clipping parameter were varied within commonly used ranges (learning rate from 1e-4 to 5e-4, clipping factor from 0.1 to 0.3). The resulting policy performance showed only limited variation, with reliability indices fluctuating within ±0.7% and renewable utilization within ±1.1%. This confirms that the reinforcement learning component converges to similar operational policies across a reasonable hyperparameter domain. Third, the MPC weighting coefficients controlling state deviation, control effort, and smoothing penalties were scaled within ±20% of their nominal values. The resulting dispatch solutions exhibited minor shifts in short-term control actions but produced negligible differences in long-term performance indicators, with total operational cost varying by less than 1.8%. Overall, the sensitivity analysis demonstrates that the proposed framework maintains stable performance under moderate variations in computational parameters. This indicates that the method is not overly dependent on fine-tuned settings and can be reliably implemented across different system configurations without requiring extensive parameter recalibration.

Simulation results

The case study is designed to validate the proposed learning-driven multi-timescale operation simulation framework under realistic system conditions. A modified IEEE 118-bus power system is adopted as the benchmark network, augmented with high renewable energy penetration and multi-region exchange interfaces to reflect real-world complexities. The system consists of 54 thermal generators, 35 wind farms, and 21 solar photovoltaic (PV) plants, collectively accounting for 6.45 GW of installed capacity. Total system load varies between 3.2 GW and 5.7 GW, with a daily average of 4.5 GW. To test inter-regional coordination, the system is divided into three interconnected control zones–North, Central, and South–linked by six tie-lines with transfer capacities ranging from 120 MW to 450 MW. Renewable penetration is approximately 46% of total generation, ensuring a challenging environment for uncertainty handling and adaptive boundary formulation. Each generator’s technical parameters, including ramp rates (3–9 MW/min), minimum up/down times (2–6 h), are defined based on the IEEE Reliability Test System database and adjusted to modern efficiency standards. Renewable generation profiles are derived from one-year measured wind and solar datasets provided by NREL’s Western Wind and Solar Integration Study (WWSIS), downsampled to 10-minute resolution and normalized by installed capacity.

The temporal configuration of the case study spans three scales consistent with the hierarchical learning structure. The medium-to-long-term horizon covers 12 months, divided into 36 ten-day subperiods for maintenance planning, primary energy scheduling, and interregional power exchange target setting. The short-term horizon simulates 30 consecutive days with hourly resolution to capture day-ahead dispatch and daily boundary optimization via reinforcement learning. Finally, the rolling simulation horizon operates on a 15-minute basis over 72 hours, allowing online learning and model predictive control (MPC) adjustments under forecast updates and real-time deviations. For the learning-based modules, the LSTM and TCN networks are trained on two years of historical data (17,520 hours) including load, wind speed, solar irradiance, and tie-line flow measurements. Each input vector comprises 24 lagged features and 8 exogenous variables such as temperature, humidity, and regional weather indices. The LSTM uses two hidden layers with 128 and 64 units respectively, while the TCN uses 6 convolutional layers with dilation factors (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) and kernel size = 3. Both networks are trained with the Adam optimizer, batch size = 256, and early stopping at validation loss convergence. The reinforcement learning (RL) module, based on the Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) algorithm, interacts with a digital twin of the IEEE 118-bus environment over 40,000 episodes. Each episode contains 96 steps (representing 15-minute intervals) and returns a composite reward function reflecting cost, renewable utilization, and frequency deviation.

To improve reproducibility and clarify the implementation details of the optimization layer, we explicitly describe the solver configuration and computational settings used in this study. The operational scheduling problems in all case studies were formulated as mixed-integer linear programs and solved using a commercial optimization solver. The solver was executed in deterministic mode with default presolve enabled, and solution tolerances were set to standard engineering values to balance accuracy and runtime.

Relative optimality gaps were restricted to a small percentage to ensure near-optimal solutions, while feasibility tolerances were maintained at standard levels to avoid artificial constraint violations. Runtime limits were applied to prevent excessive computation in large instances, and the solver was allowed to terminate early once acceptable near-optimal solutions were obtained. These settings ensure that the optimization process reflects realistic operational conditions rather than unlimited computational assumptions.

Warm-start initialization was used in the rolling optimization stage, where the solution from the previous time step was provided as an initial guess to accelerate convergence. This significantly reduced runtime and improved solution stability during sequential dispatch updates. In addition, the hierarchical structure of the proposed framework naturally decomposes the problem into planning, scheduling, and rolling layers, which prevents the solver from handling a single large monolithic problem and improves computational efficiency.

All solver settings were kept consistent across the baseline methods and the proposed framework to ensure fair comparison. These implementation choices confirm that the optimization component of the proposed method is practical for real-world deployment and does not rely on unrealistic computational assumptions.

To improve reproducibility and provide a clear understanding of the computational requirements, we report the hardware configuration and runtime statistics used in the experiments. All simulations were performed on a workstation equipped with an Intel i7-class processor operating at approximately 3.6 GHz, 32 GB of system memory, and an NVIDIA RTX-series graphics card used for neural network training.

The reinforcement learning policy was trained for forty thousand episodes, and the full training process required approximately five hours. This stage was executed offline and performed only once before deployment of the operational framework. After training, the learned policy was reused in all subsequent case studies.

For operational simulations, the runtime per experiment depended on the length of the scheduling horizon and system size. In the IEEE-scale test systems considered in this study, a full multi-day rolling simulation required roughly twenty minutes, while individual dispatch updates typically required less than half a minute. These results confirm that the proposed framework is computationally practical and can be applied to realistic system studies without excessive computational demand.

To further demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed framework, we conducted additional comparisons with three representative classes of existing approaches: stochastic programming-based scheduling, distributionally robust optimization methods, and recent learning-based dispatch strategies. These approaches represent widely used solutions for handling uncertainty and multi-timescale coordination in modern power systems.

For the stochastic programming approach, we implemented a scenario-based scheduling model using multiple renewable and load scenarios. While this method improves robustness relative to deterministic scheduling, it requires extensive scenario enumeration and leads to increased computational burden. In our case study, the stochastic model achieved slightly improved reliability compared with deterministic scheduling but produced higher operational costs than the proposed framework due to conservative reserve allocation and scenario averaging effects.

For the distributionally robust optimization approach, we tested a model that considers uncertainty sets instead of fixed scenarios. This method showed improved worst-case reliability performance but resulted in noticeably higher cost levels and lower renewable utilization because of its inherently conservative decision structure. Compared with this approach, the proposed method maintained comparable reliability while achieving better economic efficiency and reduced renewable curtailment, demonstrating that learning-based boundary adaptation can replace conservative uncertainty margins.

We also compared the proposed framework with a learning-based dispatch strategy in which reinforcement learning directly determines operational actions without adaptive boundary modeling. While this method improved dispatch flexibility relative to optimization-only approaches, it exhibited less stable performance under forecast errors and showed higher variance in reliability outcomes. The proposed framework achieved more consistent results because the optimization layer ensures physical feasibility while the learning components guide adaptive boundary evolution.

To evaluate the computational practicality of the proposed framework, we analyzed the execution time of each module across the case study. All simulations were conducted on a workstation equipped with an Intel i7 CPU (3.6 GHz), 32 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX-series GPU. The training of the LSTM–TCN forecasting module required approximately 3.4 hours for the full historical dataset, which is performed offline and therefore does not affect real-time operation. The reinforcement learning component based on PPO required around 5.2 hours for policy convergence over 40,000 training episodes; similarly, this training stage is completed offline and reused during operational deployment. During online simulation, the short-term dispatch optimization required on average 18–26 seconds per hourly scheduling step, while the rolling MPC correction at 15-minute resolution required approximately 3–5 seconds per update. The overall runtime for a full 72-hour rolling simulation was approximately 21 minutes, which demonstrates that the proposed framework is computationally feasible for operational planning and real-time decision support. These results indicate that although the proposed method integrates multiple learning and optimization components, most computationally intensive tasks are performed offline. The remaining online computation remains within practical operational limits, confirming the scalability and applicability of the framework for large-scale renewable-dominated systems.

To further demonstrate the necessity of each component in the proposed framework, we conducted an ablation study in which individual modules were removed while keeping the remaining structure unchanged. Three reduced variants were tested: one without the reinforcement learning module, one without the forecasting module, and one without the rolling correction mechanism. These tests allow us to evaluate how each component contributes to overall system performance.

When the reinforcement learning module was removed and replaced with a conventional short-term optimization strategy, the system remained feasible but exhibited noticeably higher operational costs and less effective renewable utilization. In particular, average system cost increased by about 5–6 percent compared with the full framework, indicating that the reinforcement learning policy plays an important role in improving scheduling efficiency under changing operating conditions.

When the forecasting module was removed and replaced with static boundary settings derived from historical averages, the system showed increased boundary mismatch and more frequent renewable curtailment. Curtailment levels rose by roughly 3–4 percentage points, and reliability indicators displayed larger fluctuations. This confirms that the learning-based boundary prediction is essential for maintaining cross-timescale consistency.

When the rolling correction mechanism was removed, the system became more sensitive to forecast errors and short-term disturbances. Although the average performance remained acceptable, reliability variance increased significantly and occasional constraint violations appeared in high-uncertainty periods. This demonstrates that the rolling control layer is crucial for stabilizing system operation and correcting prediction deviations.

Overall, the ablation results show that each module contributes to a different aspect of system performance: the forecasting layer improves boundary accuracy, the reinforcement learning layer enhances dispatch efficiency, and the rolling correction layer ensures operational robustness. The full framework achieves the best combined performance because it integrates these complementary functions into a unified structure.

Concluding remarks

The proposed learning-driven multi-timescale operation simulation and hierarchical boundary optimization framework presents a transformative approach to coordinating energy system operation across long-, short-, and rolling horizons under high renewable penetration and uncertainty. By merging data-driven learning with physically constrained optimization, the framework establishes a dynamic mechanism in which operational boundaries evolve continuously rather than being pre-defined. This shift from static to adaptive coordination enables the system to anticipate uncertainty, assimilate forecast updates, and refine scheduling decisions in real time while preserving long-term consistency. The unified formulation bridges the structural gap between planning and operation, providing a continuous feedback loop that integrates prediction, optimization, and execution. Simulation results from the renewable-dominated 118-bus case study verify the efficacy of the proposed approach. The framework achieves substantial improvements in both economic and reliability performance, reducing total operating cost by nearly 10% and enhancing system reliability by more than 15%. Renewable curtailment decreases by approximately 40%, demonstrating superior integration capability for intermittent sources. Moreover, rolling adaptation effectively suppresses boundary deviation from 14% to below 2%, validating the self-correcting property of the hierarchical design. The interpretability analysis further reveals that dominant physical drivers such as load, wind, and solar generation are consistently recognized by the learning module, confirming the model’s alignment with real-world dynamics. Beyond numerical gains, this work introduces a new operational philosophy for renewable-dominated energy systems–one that emphasizes temporal intelligence and boundary plasticity. The integration of deep learning architectures (LSTM and TCN) into a constrained optimization environment illustrates that artificial intelligence can coexist with power system physics, yielding solutions that are both accurate and explainable. The hierarchical architecture’s recursive updating structure demonstrates scalability, allowing the approach to extend to larger regional or national grids with diverse uncertainty profiles. In essence, the study provides both a theoretical and computational foundation for next-generation operational intelligence in complex energy networks. It reveals that true system resilience arises not solely from redundancy or reserve capacity but from the capability to learn and adapt across temporal scales. As future power systems evolve toward deep renewable integration and tighter spatiotemporal coupling, the proposed multi-timescale learning framework offers a promising direction for realizing real-time, data-informed, and self-optimizing operation. The methodology’s generalizability suggests potential applications beyond electricity–such as integrated power-gas-heat networks, multi-energy microgrids, and carbon-neutral dispatch planning–thereby positioning this research as a foundational step toward intelligent and adaptive energy systems of the future.

“The proposed framework handles temporal and scenario uncertainties in an implicit, data-driven manner rather than through explicit probabilistic modeling. While the LSTM–TCN forecasting module captures temporal variability and the reinforcement learning component is trained under diverse simulated conditions, the approach does not construct formal probability distributions, scenario trees, or ambiguity sets as in stochastic or distributionally robust optimization frameworks. As a result, the method does not provide theoretical guarantees on risk measures such as worst-case performance or tail-event resilience. In particular, the learned models may exhibit reduced robustness under rare or out-of-distribution events that are insufficiently represented in the training data. Furthermore, temporal uncertainty is addressed through pattern learning rather than explicit inter-temporal uncertainty propagation constraints, which limits the interpretability of how forecast errors evolve across time horizons. Future work will focus on integrating explicit uncertainty quantification mechanisms, such as scenario generation, probabilistic forecasting, or risk-aware optimization, to enhance the theoretical robustness of the proposed framework.
