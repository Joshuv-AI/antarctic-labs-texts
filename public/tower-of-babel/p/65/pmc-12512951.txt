Geospatial variation and determinants of incomplete antenatal care follow-up in ethiopia: a spatial and geographically weighted regression analysis
Authors: Habtamu Wagnew Abuhay, Muluken Chanie Agimas, Getaneh Awoke Yismaw, Tigabu Kidie Tesfie, Nebiyu Mekonnen Derseh, Meron Asmamaw Alemayehu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIncomplete antenatal care (ANC) follow-up remains a significant public health issue, especially in low- and middle-income countries, where it poses serious risks to both maternal and fetal health. Although ANC plays a critical role in improving maternal and child health outcomes, data on regional disparities and high rates of incomplete ANC follow-up in Ethiopia are limited. Understanding the local factors contributing to these geographic variations is essential for targeted public health interventions. This study aimed to assess the spatial variation and determinants of incomplete ANC follow-up in Ethiopia.MethodsThis study utilized data from the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS), employing a stratified, two-stage cluster sampling design. A total of 3,926 women gave their consent and were included in the study. Spatial analysis, including hotspot analysis, interpolation, and spatial statistics (SaTScan), was conducted using ArcGIS 10.8, SaTScan 9.6, and STATA 17. Additionally, spatial regression analyses, including ordinary least squares regression and geographically weighted regression (GWR), were performed to identify hotspot areas and geographical predictor variables.ResultsThe study found that 56.75% (95% CI: 54.32%, 59.14%) of pregnant women had incomplete ANC follow-up. Spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed a Global Moran’s I value of 0.660 (p < 0.001), indicating significant spatial clustering of incomplete ANC follow-up across Ethiopia. Hotspot areas were identified in the Southern, Somali, Gambela, Afar, and Oromia regions. GWR analysis indicated that being from rural areas, in the poorest wealth index, and lacking formal education were significant positive determinants of spatial variation in incomplete ANC follow-up.ConclusionThis study revealed that over half of pregnant women in Ethiopia had incomplete ANC follow-up, with significant spatial clustering observed across the country. The GWR analysis also indicated that rural residence, low household wealth, and lack of formal education were spatially varying determinants associated with incomplete ANC follow-up. These findings suggest that targeted interventions in the identified hotspot areas and addressing the spatially varying determinants may help improve ANC follow-up.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08245-0.Keywords: Incomplete ANC, Antenatal care follow-up, Spatial analysis, Geographical weighted regression, Ethiopia

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) refers to the care provided by skilled healthcare professionals to pregnant women to ensure the best possible health outcomes for both the mother and the fetus during pregnancy [1, 2]. In 2020, approximately 800 women died each day worldwide due to preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth [3]. Of these deaths, Sub-Saharan Africa and Southern Asia accounted for around 87% (253,000) of the estimated global maternal deaths, with Sub-Saharan Africa alone contributing approximately 70% (202,000) [4]. The majority of these deaths result from complications of pregnancy and childbirth, most of which are preventable and treatable [5].

Pregnant women during ANC follow-up receive a wide range of services, such as risk assessment, prevention and management of pregnancy-related complications, health education, and health promotion [6]. Previously, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended that pregnant women receive at least four ANC visits [7], and the most recent WHO model of 2016 suggests eight contacts [8]. Ethiopia still recommends four ANC follow-ups [9, 10], and a significant number of pregnant women do not attend the full range of ANC follow-ups [11].

Incomplete antenatal care becomes a major public health problem, especially in low- and middle-income countries [12]. Incomplete ANC can have serious consequences for both the mother and her fetus. It increases the risk of developing maternal and fetal complications such as preeclampsia, eclampsia, and premature and low-birthweight babies [13, 14].

Utilization of ANC care services varies across different sociodemographic and geographic contexts, both globally and within Ethiopia. Studies in Bangladesh showed that 51.8% of pregnant women had incomplete ANC follow-ups [15], while in sub-Saharan Africa and Ethiopia, 35% and 41.37% of women had incomplete ANC follow-ups [16, 17]. Besides, factors such as maternal age, educational status, wealth index, residence, husband’s occupation, marital status, and distance to ANC health facilities are associated with the number of ANC care follow-ups [18–21].

Despite the WHO recommendation that pregnant women receive at least four ANC visits [7], a substantial proportion of women in Ethiopia didn’t complete the recommended ANC follow-up. Previous studies have focused on the prevalence and factors associated with ANC utilization. However, they have not adequately examined the variation in ANC utilization across different sociodemographic and geographic contexts within Ethiopia.

This study addresses the existing knowledge gap by employing spatial and GWR methods to identify hotspot areas and explore local-level factors that contribute to incomplete ANC follow-up. By focusing on these spatial variations, the study provides valuable insights that can guide targeted interventions for policymakers and stakeholders. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the geospatial variation of Incomplete Antenatal care follow-up and its predictors among pregnant women in Ethiopia: A Spatial and geographical weighted regression analysis.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

This study utilized the most recent data from the 2019 Ethiopian Mini Demographic and Health Survey (EMDHS), a nationally representative cross-sectional survey [22]. Ethiopia, located in the Horn of Africa, is divided into nine administrative regions: Tigray, Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Benishangul-Gumuz, Southern Nations, Nationalities, and Peoples’, Gambella, and Harari. In addition, Addis Ababa and Dire-Dawa are two self-governing city administrations.

Data source

The analysis utilized the 2019 EMDHS women’s dataset (IR file), which included data from 3,926 women who had given birth in the past two years. The EMDHS was conducted using a nationwide questionnaire administered across the country from March 21 to June 28, 2019. Data was obtained from the Measure DHS website (http://www.measuredhs.com) after justifying the purpose of the request, and subsequently downloaded for analysis.

Variables of study

Dependent variable

In this study, the outcome variable was antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, categorized as either complete or incomplete. Pregnant women who attended four or more ANC visits were classified as having complete ANC follow-up, while those who attended fewer than four visits were considered to have incomplete ANC follow-up [23, 24].

Independent variables

A set of determinant variables was used, including women’s age, women’s educational status (not educated, primary, secondary, and higher), husband’s educational level (not educated, primary, secondary, and higher), wealth index (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest), Marital status (single, married or cohabitating, divorced, and widowed), place of residence (urban and rural), regions, media exposure (no, yes), sex of household heads (male or female), and type of facility ANC follow-up (at a private health facility or a government health facility). Furthermore, variables were selected based on evidence from previous peer-reviewed literature on determinants of ANC utilization, as well as their availability within the EMDHS dataset, to ensure both scientific relevance and feasibility of analysis.

Data collection tools and procedures

A stratified, two-stage cluster sampling was used to select the study sample. A total of 305 enumeration areas (EA) (93 in urban areas and 212 in rural areas) were selected with a probability proportional to the EA size. In the second stage, a fixed number of 30 households per cluster were selected with an equal probability of systematic selection from the newly created household listing. The Women’s Questionnaire was administered to all eligible women aged 15–49 years. Data were collected electronically using a digital data collection system during the 2019 EMDHS [24].

Data management and analysis

The data were extracted from the women’s data (IR) dataset of the EMDHS 2019. After cleaning and recoding, the data were weighted to account for differences in sampling variability. Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize the overall characteristics of the study participants using text, tables, and figures. Categorical variables were analyzed and presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were analyzed and summarized using means and standard deviations. Differences in proportions were assessed using the chi-square test, and mean differences between groups were compared using independent-samples t-tests. STATA version 17 and Microsoft Excel 2016 were used for data management. ArcGIS version 10.8 was used to map incomplete antenatal care follow-ups in Ethiopia. Regarding missing variables, cases with incomplete outcome data were excluded from the analysis using listwise deletion. Similarly, records without geographic coordinates were excluded to ensure the accuracy of the spatial analysis.

Spatial analysis

Spatial autocorrelation was determined through global Moran’s, which is an indicator for spatial autocorrelation and measures whether the spatial pattern of incomplete antenatal follow-up was clustered, dispersed, or random. The Moran I value ranges from − 1 to + 1, where − 1 indicates incomplete antenatal follow-up is dispersed and + 1 indicates incomplete antenatal follow-up is clustered [25]. If Moran’s I is close to 0, it indicates that there is no spatial autocorrelation. A statistically significant Moran’s I value (p < 0.05) can lead to rejection of the null hypothesis (incomplete ANC follow-up is randomly distributed) and indicates the presence of spatial dependence.

Getis-OrdGi* statistics were used to identify significant hotspot and cold spot areas of incomplete ANC follow-up. Getis-OrdGi* statistics were computed to measure how spatial autocorrelation varies over the study location, and a Z-score was computed to determine the statistical significance of clustering, and the p-value was computed for the significance [26]. Statistical output with high GI* indicates a “hotspot”, whereas low GI* means a “cold spot”. Kriging interpolation techniques were employed to predict the values to unsampled areas [27], for incomplete ANC follow-up based on the observed values.

Spatial scan statistical analysis was done using SaTscan version 9.6 software to identify significant primary and secondary clusters [28]. Bernoulli’s model was fitted since the outcome variable was binary. A woman who had incomplete ANC follow-up was considered as a case, whereas those who completed the ANC follow-up were considered as control.

Spatial regression analysis

In this study, both local and global analysis techniques were used to identify determinants of spatial variation in incomplete antenatal care follow-up within the study areas [29, 30]. We employed Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression as the global statistical model to examine the relationship between dependent and independent variables [31]. The analysis began with exploratory regression using OLS, followed by testing model assumptions.

Geographically Weighted Regression (GWR) was utilized to explore spatial variations in incomplete antenatal care follow-up. GWR allows for local examination of predictor effects, which can vary across locations. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF), with a threshold of VIF < 7.5 [32]. Model bias was checked with the Jarque-Bera statistic, and spatial variations were confirmed using the Koenker test (p-value < 0.05). Significant Koenker statistics indicated spatially varying relationships. Finally, the model with the lowest AICc score and the highest adjusted R-squared value was found to be the best match for the data, and variables that have a p-value less than 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval were selected and described based on their coefficients.

Results

In this current study, the weighted proportion of pregnant women who did not complete the recommended antenatal care follow-ups across the nine regions and two administrative cities was 56.75% (95% CI 54.32%, 59.14). Besides, rural residence and southern nation nationality regions had the highest proportion of incomplete antenatal care follow-up (Table 1.).

Cold spot (low-risk) areas of incomplete antenatal care follow-up were identified in Addis Ababa, central Oromia, Benishangul-Gumuz, and Tigray regions. These areas were represented by blue clusters on the spatial analysis map (Fig. 2).

Kriging interpolation analysis

In the spatial kriging interpolation analysis, areas shaded in red indicated a higher prevalence of incomplete antenatal care (ANC) follow-up, particularly in the Somali, Afar, Gambella, Oromia, and Southern regions. In contrast, areas shaded in green, such as Addis Ababa, Dire Dawa, Tigray, Benishangul-Gumuz, and parts of the Amhara Region, showed a lower extent of incomplete ANC follow-up (Fig. 3).

Spatial scan statistical analysis

Spatial scan statistics identified two significant clusters of incomplete antenatal care follow-up. The primary cluster was centered at t 5.479641 N, 42.196835 E, covering most parts of Somali and Oromia regions, containing 58 clusters with a 428.49 km radius, LLR 81.31, p-value < 0.001. Women within this cluster had a 1.45 times higher risk of incomplete ANC follow-up compared to those outside the cluster. The second scanning window for the secondary clusters was found in the southern region, which contained 19 clusters located at 6.892369 N, 37.116364 E, with a 128.84 km radius, LLR 16.49, and a p-value < 0.000. Women inside this cluster had a 1.26 times higher risk of incomplete antenatal care follow-up compared to women outside the area (Fig. 4)(Table 2).

Although OLS analysis identifies determinants of hotspot areas for incomplete antenatal care follow-up, it assumes that the relationship between each independent variable and incomplete antenatal care follow-up is consistent across the study area. However, this assumption was violated, as evidenced by significant Koenker (BP) statistics (p-value < 0.001). Additionally, the spatial autocorrelation test (Moran’s I) showed that the residuals were spatially auto-correlated (p-value > 0.1), indicating that the model residuals were clustered (Fig. 5). As a result, the issue was addressed using geographically weighted regression, which was fitted to produce reliable estimates. Following that, being rural residents, having the poorest wealth index, and not being educated women were identified as the spatial determinants of hotspot areas for incomplete antenatal care follow-up in Ethiopia (Table 3).

Geographic weighted regression analysis

The local spatial regression (GWR) was conducted because one of the six assumptions of OLS regression was violated, as indicated by significant Koenker (BP) statistics. The GWR analysis demonstrated improved model performance compared to the OLS model. Specifically, the AIC value decreased from 2716.67 in the OLS model to 2647.04 in the GWR model. Additionally, the adjusted R² increased from 0.47 in the OLS model to 0.60 in the GWR model, indicating that GWR enhanced the model’s ability to predict incomplete antenatal care follow-up. Overall, this study shows that the GWR analysis performed better than the OLS model (Table 4).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine incomplete ANC follow-up and its determinants among pregnant women in Ethiopia using Spatial and geographically weighted regression models based on EMDHS 2019. It highlights the importance of addressing incomplete ANC follow-up, as ANC plays a critical role in detecting and managing pregnancy-related risks to improve maternal and neonatal outcomes. Incomplete ANC follow-up was spatially clustered (Moran’s I = 0.66 p-value < 0.001) in Ethiopia. This finding was supported by a study in Tanzania [33], and India [34] shows incomplete antenatal care follow-up was spatially clustered.

The hotspot areas of incomplete antenatal care follow-up were observed in the southern, Somali, Gambela, some parts of Afar, and Oromia regions of Ethiopia. Besides, the Spatial scan statistics of incomplete ANC follow-up had primarily clustered in the Somali and Oromia regions, and secondary clusters were found in the Southern Region. This pattern aligns with the findings of previous studies in Ethiopia, which have highlighted regional disparities in ANC utilization driven by factors such as geographical inaccessibility, socio-economic inequality, and cultural influences [35, 36].

For instance, studies in pastoralist and semi-pastoralist regions, including Somali and Afar, have reported a lower ANC attendance, primarily due to mobility patterns, limited health infrastructure, and lower awareness of maternal healthcare services [37–39]. Additionally, research in Oromia and the Southern Region has identified economic constraints, long travel distances to health facilities, and cultural practices as key barriers to completion of ANC [40].

The GWR analysis identifies significant determinants of hotspots for incomplete ANC follow-up. The analysis reveals that women from rural areas, those belonging to the poorest wealth index families, and not educated women were more likely to be located in hotspots for incomplete ANC follow-up. This suggests that these factors are strongly associated with the likelihood of women not completing the recommended ANC visits, particularly in certain geographic areas.

The current study identified that a proportion of women from rural residences had a positive relationship with incomplete ANC follow-up. A higher GWR coefficient of rural residence was detected in the East Harergie and Shoa zones of Oromia, as well as in the Somali and Southern regions. This finding is also supported by other studies in Ethiopia, which showed that rural residents tend to have fewer ANC visits and higher dropout rates [17, 41]. This could be because rural areas in Ethiopia often face limited transportation infrastructure and long travel distances to healthcare facilities [42]. This can make it challenging for pregnant women, especially those in remote rural areas, to attend regular antenatal care follow-ups.

Similarly, the findings of this study showed that women who were from the poorest wealth index were positively related to incomplete antenatal care follow-up. Hot spot areas with higher coefficients for the poorest wealth index were observed in the Benishangul-Gumz, the northwest part of Oromia, Gambela, the Southern parts of Amhara, and the Southern region. This finding is supported by studies from Ethiopia [43], and Rwanda [44], which showed that ANC services are utilized more by the richest women than by the poorest.

The possible reason could be that Pregnant women from lower-income households might not be able to pay the expense of transportation to come to healthcare facilities for antenatal sessions [45]. Furthermore, antenatal care follow-up often requires time away from work, which can result in a loss of income for pregnant women from the poorest households [46]. As a result, pregnant women miss the required number of antenatal care follow-ups.

Furthermore, not-educated women were strong determinants of incomplete ANC follow-up and the GWR coefficients varied between 0.01 and 0.56 from region to region. Significant hotspot areas were the Harergie zones of the Oromia region, the Somali region, the western part of Tigray, the northern parts of Amhara, and the Southern parts of the Afar region. This finding is supported by other studies, which revealed that Somali, Afar, and Oromia regions have the lowest female literacy rates [47], and also a higher number of nomadic people founded [48]. Women with lower education levels may have limited awareness about the importance of antenatal care and the potential risks of incomplete follow-up. Furthermore, pregnant women from nomadic communities may face difficulties in accessing regular ANC follow-ups.

Strengths and limitations: For data confidentiality reasons, location data values were shifted by up to 2 km for urban and 5 km for rural enumeration areas to prevent identification of respondents or communities. This could affect estimated cluster effects in the spatial analysis. Additionally, this study used secondary data from the EMDHS, which lacked key variables such as behavioral factors, health facility-related characteristics, and husband/partner-related factors. Which could have provided a more comprehensive understanding of incomplete ANC follow-up. Therefore, we recommend future research to incorporate these variables to strengthen the findings in this area.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. It uses data from the most recent, nationally representative, and largest population-based survey sample, covering all regions and administrative cities of the country. In addition, the study employed a robust methodology to analyze the data and assess the non-stationary nature of determinants for incomplete ANC follow-up.

Conclusions

This study revealed that over half (56.75%) of pregnant women in Ethiopia had incomplete ANC follow-up, with significant spatial clustering observed across the country. Hotspot areas were identified in the Southern, Somali, Gambela, Afar, and Oromia regions. Moreover, the GWR analysis indicated that rural residence, low household wealth, and lack of formal education were spatially varying determinants of incomplete ANC follow-up. These findings suggest that targeted interventions in the identified hotspot areas and addressing the spatially varying determinants may help improve ANC completion rates and contribute to better maternal and child health outcomes.
