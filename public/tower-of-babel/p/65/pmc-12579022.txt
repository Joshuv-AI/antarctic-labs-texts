AI-Based Quantitative Assessment of Retinal Vascular Morphology in Circumscribed Choroidal Hemangioma
Authors: Zhen-Yu Liu, Hai-Long He, Xuan-Yu Chen, Zhi-Han Zhang, Xiao-Yan Peng, Zi-Bing Jin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to apply a deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) system for quantitative analysis of retinal vascular morphology in patients with circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH), and to explore differences based on lesion location.MethodsThis retrospective case–control study included 45 treatment-naive CCH eyes and their contralateral healthy eyes as controls, recruited from 45 patients (mean age: 44.91 ± 11.98 years; 10 female patients). Retinal photographs were analyzed using AI software to extract vascular parameters, including vessel density, caliber, tortuosity, and fractal dimension. Inter-group comparisons and conditional logistic regression were conducted. Subgroup analysis was performed based on the lesion's position relative to the optic disc.ResultsCompared with controls, CCH eyes showed significantly reduced vascular density (p < 0.005) within 3 mm and 5 mm of the fovea. Venular caliber was significantly increased across multiple concentric zones (1.0–2.5 papillary diameter, PD), while arteriole-to-venule ratio (AVR) was decreased (p < 0.001). Tortuosity and fractal dimension of both arterioles and venules were significantly reduced (all p < 0.05). Logistic regression confirmed vessel caliber (p = 0.001), AVR (p = 0.001), tortuosity (p = 0.006), and fractal dimension (p = 0.004) as significant parameters associated with CCH. Lesions within 1.0 PD of the optic disc were linked to lower arteriolar caliber and AVR (p < 0.05).ConclusionRetinal vascular morphological alterations are evident in CCH and vary with lesion location. Key parameters, such as vascular density, venular caliber, AVR, and fractal dimension, may serve as potential imaging biomarkers for evaluating and monitoring CCH-related retinal changes.Keywords: Circumscribed choroidal hemangioma, Retinal vascular morphology, Artificial intelligence, Fundus photography

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study? Circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) poses a significant risk to vision due to retinal complications; however, its impact on retinal vasculature remains poorly characterized.This study aimed to determine whether AI-based analysis could identify differences in retinal vascular parameters between CCH-affected and healthy eyes and assess their correlation with lesion location. What was learned from this study? Eyes with CCH demonstrated significant retinal vascular alterations, including reduced vascular density, tortuosity, and fractal dimension, alongside increased venular caliber and a decreased arteriole-to-venule ratio (AVR).These changes may serve as valuable noninvasive biomarkers for CCH assessment and monitoring.

Introduction

Choroidal hemangiomas comprise two subtypes: circumscribed choroidal hemangioma (CCH) and diffuse choroidal hemangioma (DCH). CCH represents a rare, benign choroidal tumor classified as a congenital vascular developmental anomaly with unclear pathogenesis [1, 2]. CCH typically presents as a well-defined, solitary lesion predominantly located in the macular region, with possible extension to the edge of the optic disc. Its predilection for the posterior pole suggests a potential association with the short posterior ciliary artery [1, 2]. The lesion of CCH may involve the full choroidal thickness and is often accompanied by secondary alterations in the overlying retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) and neurosensory retina. Histologically, it is mostly composed of cavernous vascular tissue, including thin-walled vascular lumens and loose connective tissue. CCH can lead to significant vision-impairing complications, including exudative retinal detachment and macular edema [1, 2]. In contrast, DCH predominantly occurs in patients with Sturge–Weber syndrome, with diffuse involvement from the ora serrata to the posterior pole and anomalies in the structure and function of blood vessels affecting the facial skin and brain [3, 4].

The retinal vascular network can be directly visualized in vivo. Fundus imaging allows for noninvasive evaluation of retinal vessel morphology, which facilitates the diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring of various ocular disorders [5]. In diabetic retinopathy (DR), alterations in retinal vascular caliber and fractal dimension have been observed, and studies have shown that retinal vascular morphological parameters were associated with microvascular complications of diabetes [6]. In patients with high myopia, the retinal vascular density, fractal dimension, and number of vascular branches were all significantly reduced [7, 8]. Retinal vascular morphological changes can also represent a robust biomarker for early detection and monitoring of systemic conditions [5].

Although the retinal and choroidal vasculatures originate from distinct embryological precursors and develop through different mechanisms, retinal microcirculation remains physiologically interdependent with choroidal vascular status [9]. In various ocular and systemic diseases, both the choroidal and retinal circulations are simultaneously affected, with interrelated changes [10]. The choroidal vasculature plays a pivotal role in retinal homeostasis, particularly through the choriocapillaris—a specialized vascular layer situated between the RPE and choroidal stroma. This structure serves as the principal vascular supply for the outer retina, nourishing both the RPE and photoreceptors [9, 11]. In previous studies, patients with choroidal diseases, such as choroidal melanoma and choroiditis, have shown significant changes in the morphology and function of retinal vessels [12, 13]. CCH leads to a variety of retinal complications, with the most common being subretinal fluid, macular edema, and cystoid macular degeneration, accompanied by changes in the neurosensory retina and RPE [1, 14]. The local hemodynamic disturbances, inflammatory responses, and ischemia-hypoxia caused by CCH all indicate that retinal blood circulation may be affected. Previous studies have reported morphological changes in retinal vessels in patients with choroidal hemangioma, such as decreased vascular density in the deep capillary plexus (DCP) [14]. There have also been reports of CCH combined with superior temporal retinal vein occlusion [15]. However, current evidence remains largely limited to case reports, and the majority focus on the impact on deeper retinal vessels, without fully considering the influence of hemangioma location on retinal vessels.

Deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) technologies has enabled us to efficiently detect morphological parameters of retinal vessels. Therefore, the present study aims to assess the retinal vascular morphological features in patients with CCH using AI, to identify morphological parameters significantly associated with CCH, and to further investigate the impact of hemangioma location on these parameters. This will provide a deeper understanding of the effects of choroidal hemangioma on retinal blood circulation and offer valuable references for clinical diagnosis and treatment.

Methods

This study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Beijing Tongren Hospital, and all procedures followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (Ethics Approval Number: TREC2022-KY045). Given that this is a retrospective study employing noninvasive methods and the collected data have been de-identified, informed consent from the study participants was not required.

Study Participants

In this retrospective observational study, patients diagnosed with unilateral CCH by ophthalmologists (XY P) at Beijing Tongren Hospital, Capital Medical University, between August 2024 and March 2025 were included. We carefully reviewed the complete ophthalmic medical records and examinations of the patients. All patients underwent routine ophthalmic examinations, including visual acuity, intraocular pressure, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and fundus photography.

Patients with complications associated with CCH, such as retinal detachment or subretinal fluid, were excluded. Additionally, those with other ocular diseases including glaucoma, DR, age-related macular degeneration, uveitis, various types of cataracts, vitreous hemorrhage, or corneal opacity were also not eligible. Exclusion also applied to patients with ocular or systemic conditions that could interfere with quantitative assessment or examination cooperation, such as refractive media opacities, high myopia, severe hypertension or diabetes, and Alzheimer's disease. Patients who had previously received any treatment for CCH or other related ocular conditions, including retinal laser therapy or vitreoretinal surgery, were excluded. Finally, cases with large hemangiomas or significant elevation that resulted in poor image clarity or incomplete scanning were also excluded. It should be noted that the exclusion of eyes with large or highly elevated tumors may result in a study population biased toward less severe CCH cases. However, this was necessary to ensure image quality sufficient for reliable AI-based vascular analysis.

Image Acquisition

All participants were informed about the retinal imaging process. Following a 5-min dark adaptation period in a dimly lit room to allow for natural dilation of their pupils, 45° color fundus photographs of both eyes were captured by experienced ophthalmic photographers using a Topcon TRC-NW400 (Topcon, Japan) or Canon CR-DGi (Canon, Japan) non-mydriatic fundus camera. Each image captured the posterior pole encompassing the macular region and optic disc region.

Extraction of Morphological Parameters

We utilized the EVisionAI software (EVision Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) to process color fundus photographs. This software integrates advanced computer vision and deep learning techniques for image processing, enabling accurate identification of retinal vessel morphology in fundus photographs and quantitative assessment of regional features. The image processing workflow encompasses several key steps [16–18]: (1) Preprocessing: Images underwent normalization, denoising, and contrast-limited adaptive histogram equalization to minimize variability in illumination and quality, ensuring standardized analysis. (2) Vessel segmentation: Retinal vessels were segmented by a deep neural network (ResNet101-UNet), which precisely delineated the vascular tree from the background with high accuracy and segmentation quality. (3) Artery/vein classification: Segmented vessels were subsequently classified as arteries or veins based on a combination of color, brightness, and topological structural features. (4) Morphometric parameter extraction: The software skeletonized the segmented vessel map to obtain center lines. Vessel density was computed as the ratio of the total vessel area to the area of the region of interest. Vessel caliber was measured as the mean width at perpendicular cross-sections. Tortuosity was calculated as the integral of the squared curvature along the vessel path, divided by the total length. Fractal dimension was estimated using the box-counting method. The deep learning model for vessel extraction within the EVisionAI software, which was utilized in our study, has been rigorously validated and demonstrated high performance, with accuracy of 0.970, sensitivity of 0.859, specificity of 0.982, and an intersection over union (IoU) of 0.739 [16]. Our primary focus was on medium to large retinal vessels, specifically the retinal central arteries and veins and their branches, as these vessels play a pivotal role in retinal blood supply and drainage. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 for Windows (IBM SPSS Statistics, USA), while data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5.1 and Origin 2024. The normality of the distribution of quantitative data was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Variables with normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those that did not conform to a normal distribution were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). Based on the normality of the data, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were employed to compare the differences in parameters between the CCH group and control group. Conditional logistic regression analysis was used to investigate the strength of the association between morphological parameters and CCH and to identify independent influencing factors, with odds ratios (OR) quantifying the impact of exposure factors on the outcomes. For comparisons between groups with hemangioma at different locations, independent-samples t-tests or nonparametric tests were selected according to the normality of the data. All p-values were two-sided, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Discussion

AI-assisted image processing has emerged as an efficient modality for quantitative ophthalmic image analysis across diverse ocular pathologies. In contrast to conventional manual assessment, AI-based quantification demonstrates superior throughput and reproducibility while minimizing human-induced measurement variability. Previous studies have demonstrated significant alterations in retinal vascular morphological parameters in patients with myopia and DR [6–8, 19, 20]. In patients with Sturge–Weber syndrome, characteristic retinal vascular abnormalities predominantly coexist with diffuse choroidal hemangioma [21]. Nevertheless, the influence of CCH on retinal vascular architecture remains largely unexplored in the existing literature. Through quantitative analysis of retinal vascular parameters in patients with CCH, we revealed the impact of CCH on retinal blood circulation and focused on the differences between lesions at different locations.

We found that patients with treatment-naive CCH exhibited significant alterations in retinal vascular morphological parameters. The CCH group demonstrated larger vascular caliber and venular caliber, while vascular density, vascular density within a 3-mm radius of the macular fovea, vascular density within a 5-mm radius of the macular fovea, AVR, vascular tortuosity (2.0–2.5 PD), arteriolar tortuosity (1.5–2.0 PD), arteriolar tortuosity (2.0–2.5 PD), venular tortuosity (2.0–2.5 PD), fractal dimension, arteriolar fractal dimension, and venular fractal dimension were significantly smaller than in the control group. In the conditional logistic regression model, vascular tortuosity, fractal dimension, AVR, and vascular caliber were significantly associated with CCH status. Additionally, we found that when the tumor involved the peripapillary region within 1 PD, the arteriolar caliber (1.0–1.5 PD) and AVR were reduced. This indicates that the impact of CCH on retinal vessels is associated with the location of the lesion.

The retinal vasculature derives from the ophthalmic artery, with the central retinal artery entering the inner retina through the optic nerve head to form a plexiform capillary network. Existing investigations regarding CCH and retinal vasculature have predominantly examined vascular alterations at the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) and DCP levels [1, 14, 22]. Optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA) analysis demonstrated that CCH-affected eyes exhibited no significant differences in superficial foveal avascular zone (FAZ), deep FAZ, and superficial plexus capillary density, but a significant decrease in deep plexus capillary density [22].

The CCH group exhibited a significant reduction in vascular density, which may be associated with the mechanical compression of the hemangioma and local ischemia due to impaired choroidal vascular perfusion. As the choriocapillaris serves as the principal nutritional source for the outer retina, studies have documented thinning of the RPE–photoreceptor complex secondary to choroidal attenuation [23]. However, it has been reported that in cases of choroidal thickening, the outer retina thins in patients with Sturge–Weber syndrome due to impaired choroidal perfusion [23]. CCH eyes with current or historical evidence of cystoid macular edema (CME) or subfoveal subretinal fluid (SRF) exhibited significantly reduced deep plexus capillary density compared with unaffected eyes [22]. The decrease in deep plexus capillary density may be associated with increased intraretinal pressure caused by CME and SRF, which may compress the deep capillaries and lead to reduced blood flow [14, 22]. Vascular density reduction is commonly seen in high myopia. The AL elongation in patients with high myopia leads to alterations in retinal structure and subsequent small artery remodeling [7]. This stretching may have a greater impact on DCP and choroidal capillaries because these vessels are more vulnerable than the larger retinal vessels in SCP [8]. Considering the high-flow characteristic of choroidal circulation and the high oxygen extraction demands of retinal tissue, evaluating the changes in retinal and choroidal vasculature is crucial for early identification of fundus vascular disorders. It should be noted that the retinal ischemia caused by CCH is chronic, accompanied by progressive changes in retinal structure. Therefore, we should consider that although none of the patients in our study had CCH-related complications, these structural changes of the retina may be related to a potential tendency for retinal vascular sparsity.

Fractal dimension is used to quantify the complexity of retinal vascular branching and serves as an indicator of vascular irregularity [20, 24]. The significantly reduced fractal dimension observed in patients with CCH indicates a tendency of vascular occlusion and dropout of the retina. Various conditions leading to inadequate retinal perfusion could result in decreased fractal dimension as well [7, 24–26]. Fractal dimension was also related to cognitive dysfunction in previous studies, reflecting similar changes in cerebral microvasculature [24]. Multiple studies have revealed that alterations in fractal dimension may antedate the emergence of clinical symptoms, thereby serving as an early indicator of microvascular anomalies. This underscores the clinical significance of monitoring fractal dimension as a proactive measure in the detection and management of microvascular pathologies [11].

The CCH group exhibited significantly larger venular caliber than the control group. Researchers have documented associations between venular caliber variability and a range of phenotypic characteristics, and the increase in retinal venular caliber may reflect systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier abnormalities [5, 24]. In the context of choroidal tumors, retinal venous alterations are more frequently observed in the setting of choroidal melanoma, with reports of retinal vein drainage formation and venous occlusion [27]. A study showed that CCH exceeding 3 mm in thickness induced pressure-related vascular changes in the deep retina, exhibiting pathophysiological similarities to choroidal melanoma-associated changes [28]. The venular caliber changes associated with CCH may be linked to local hemodynamic alterations induced by the hemangioma. CCH could elevate intraocular venous pressure (EVP), potentially affect retinal venous outflow and resulting in compensatory venular dilatation [29, 30]. Furthermore, direct mechanical compression by the hemangioma may impede retinal venous drainage, thereby aggravating venous congestion.

Another potential mechanism is that retinal veins may serve as potential drainage pathways for blood originating from choroidal hemangiomas. In patients with retinal vein occlusion, the formation of collateral circulation suggests the presence of venous hypertension and compromised blood outflow. Under such conditions, retinal vessels may undergo adaptive remodeling to establish alternative drainage pathways to mitigate pressure [30]. Notably, these findings in patients with CCH could also be attributed to undiagnosed congenital vascular anomalies [5].

AVR reflects the relative relationship between the diameters of arteries and veins. In this study, we found that while the arteriolar caliber remained unchanged in the CCH group, the venular caliber was significantly increased, leading to a decreased AVR. A similar change has been observed in patients with choroidal melanoma [13]. The underlying pathophysiology of AVR alterations involves multiple interrelated mechanisms. The higher metabolic demands of the choroidal melanoma lesion may lead to an increase in retinal venular caliber to accommodate the augmented blood flow. Additionally, patients with choroidal melanoma have higher concentrations of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and other inflammatory cytokines, which could potentially alter the structure and function of retinal vessels. The inflammatory response may also lead to venous dilation, consequently affecting AVR. Furthermore, the growth of choroidal melanoma may be accompanied by neovascularization and remodeling of existing vessels, which could alter the diameter and distribution of retinal vessels, with the increase in venular caliber being part of this vascular remodeling [13].

The interaction between retinal and choroidal vessels is crucial for the process of visual formation. Although anatomically the retinal and choroidal vascular networks are distinct entities, they are functionally interconnected through mechanisms such as vascular covariation and compensatory interactions [11, 31]. Through mechanisms such as autoregulation and functional hyperemia, retinal vasculature modulates blood flow to satisfy metabolic demands [32]. Unlike retinal vasculature, the choroidal circulation is highly dependent on the autonomic nervous system and lacks an autoregulatory mechanism [9]. A typical example is the different mechanisms of retinal and choroidal blood flow changes in patients with pathological myopia. [19]. Changes in the local microenvironment can lead to adaptive alterations in the retinal central vascular system, which may result in dynamic changes in the retinal vascular caliber and AVR [33].

We found that both arteriolar and venular tortuosity were significantly decreased in the CCH group, which to some extent reflects the overall dysregulation of retinal microvasculature [34]. Previous studies have shown that vascular tortuosity is associated with various ocular and systemic diseases [5, 6, 35]. In our regression model, vascular tortuosity remained significantly associated with CCH when other factors were taken into account simultaneously. The decreased vascular tortuosity in patients with CCH may be the result of the combined effects of multiple factors, including local hemodynamic changes, endothelial dysfunction, systemic factors, and genetic background [34]. In addition, previous studies have found that there is an increased tortuosity of retinal vessels in most cases of DCH. This discrepancy highlights the distinct effects on the retinal vasculature between two subtypes of choroidal hemangioma [4]. The diffuse lesion in Sturge–Weber syndrome might impose a different mechanical and hemodynamic stress on the retinal vessels, leading to the contrasting morphological alterations.

The impact of CCH on the ocular microenvironment remains incompletely understood. Previous studies have revealed that the levels of VEGF-A and interferon-inducible protein (IP)-10 in the aqueous humor of patients with CCH were elevated [36]. The altered concentration of these cytokines reflects a dynamic imbalance between angiogenesis and anti-angiogenesis within the eyes of CCH [1, 37]. Elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the aqueous humor of patients with CCH suggest that the eye is in a state of chronic inflammation. The formation of neovascularization in the eyes of CCH has been reported previously, and was associated with the chronic inflammation and ischemia caused by CCH [38]. In addition, retinal detachment secondary to CCH is one of the causes of retinal neovascularization. [39].

The central retinal artery passes through the optic disc and subsequently branches into a capillary network which extends throughout the retina. Therefore, tumors near the optic disc are more likely to interfere with the central retinal vascular system. In light of this, we categorized patients based on the spatial relationship of CCH and the optic disc. Our findings revealed that lesions in close proximity to the optic disc were associated with a reduction in arteriolar caliber (1.0–1.5 PD) and a decreased AVR. In the research conducted by Krohn and colleagues [40], the presence of SRF was significantly associated with CCH near the optic disc. These findings suggest that lesions near the optic disc may have a greater impact on retinal vessels. Yu and colleagues [41] found a connection between the central retinal artery and the short posterior ciliary arteries, but it is unclear whether choroidal lesions near the optic disc can affect retinal vessels through this relationship.

Our research has several limitations. First, although our sample size is comparable to those in previous studies of this rare condition, it may still limit the statistical power of our findings, particularly in subgroup analyses based on lesion location [42–44]. Second, to ensure the accuracy of AI-based quantitative analysis, we excluded eyes with large or highly elevated tumors that resulted in poor image quality. While necessary for reliable parameter extraction, this criterion may limit the generalizability of our results to patients with more advanced or severe CCH. Third, we did not categorize CCH into different stages of disease progression or conduct follow-up studies to examine dynamic changes in vascular morphology over time. Such stratification and longitudinal assessment could provide valuable insights into the evolution of CCH-related retinal vascular changes. Additionally, the cross-sectional design of our study precludes the establishment of a temporal relationship between retinal vascular alterations and CCH, thereby limiting causal inference. Future multicenter studies incorporating larger, more diverse cohorts, advanced imaging techniques, and longitudinal designs are warranted to validate our findings and extend them across the full spectrum of CCH severity.

Conclusion

In summary, this study is the first to utilize AI to uncover the morphological alterations in retinal vasculature among patients with CCH, thereby providing substantial evidence of the impairment of retinal circulation in these patients. Our findings further indicate that the impact of CCH on retinal vessels is related to the location of the hemangioma. The retinal consequences of CCH have been overlooked in the past. Therefore, this study fills a significant gap in the literature, enhancing our understanding of the pathogenesis of CCH and the influence of the choroidal vascular pathology on retinal vasculature. Currently, no standardized treatment protocol exists for CCH. Photodynamic therapy (PDT) and laser photocoagulation, common treatments for symptomatic CCH, are often limited by their invasiveness, lack of precision, and associated complications [1, 2]. Our discoveries suggest that retinal vascular morphological parameters may serve as a valuable tool for the assessment and monitoring of CCH, potentially offering new insights and guidance for therapeutic and management strategies. However, the mechanisms underlying this association warrant further investigation.
