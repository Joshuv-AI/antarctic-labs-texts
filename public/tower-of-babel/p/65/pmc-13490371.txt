Mechanistic insight into signal bias by the agonist-dependent conformational dynamics of GPR84
Authors: Shota Suzuki, Duy Phuoc Tran, Kouki Nishikawa, Akio Kitao, Yoshinori Fujiyoshi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGPR84 is an orphan class A GPCR primarily expressed in immune cells, where it plays key roles in inflammation and metabolism. Here, we present the cryo-electron microscopy structures of the GPR84-Gi complex bound to the G protein–biased agonist DL-175, and the inactive state of GPR84 bound to the antagonist GLPG1205. Combined with signaling assays and molecular dynamics simulations, these structures elucidate the conformational landscape spanning the inactive and G protein-biased active states of GPR84, providing a mechanistic basis for biased agonism. Notably, steric interactions between DL-175 and L3366.52 selectively preclude the conformational changes required for efficient β-arrestin recruitment without compromising G protein activation. These structural insights provide a structural context for the rational design of GPR84-targeted therapeutics with precisely tuned signaling profiles.Subject terms: Cryoelectron microscopy, G protein-coupled receptors, Computational biophysics

Introduction

G protein–coupled receptors (GPCRs) constitute the largest and most diverse family of cell-surface receptors in the human genome, transducing a variety of extracellular signals into intracellular responses1–3. Owing to their physiological importance, GPCRs are primary drug targets, accounting for approximately one-third of all FDA-approved medicines4,5. GPR84, a class A (rhodopsin-like) GPCR, is predominantly expressed in innate immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, and microglia6–8. GPR84 is an inducible receptor; its expression is typically maintained at low levels but is rapidly upregulated in response to inflammatory stimuli such as lipopolysaccharide and tumor necrosis factor-α9. This upregulation is closely associated with a wide range of inflammatory and fibrotic diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease10,11, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis12, and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis13. GPR84 recognizes medium-chain fatty acids with carbon chain lengths of 9 to 14 as putative endogenous ligands and inhibits adenylyl cyclase activity through coupling to inhibitory G proteins (Gi/o)9,14. This signaling cascade triggers cytokine production and immune cell chemotaxis, forming a positive feedback loop that amplifies inflammatory responses. Conversely, GPR84 activation also potently enhances macrophage phagocytosis, contributing to pathogen clearance and anti-tumor immunity14,15.

This functional dichotomy presents a therapeutic dilemma. Conventional antagonists (GLPG1205) block both the detrimental pro-inflammatory signals and the beneficial pro-phagocytic activity, while agonists potentiate both pathways, risking excessive inflammation16,17. A more precise pharmacological approach to selectively modulate these downstream pathways is therefore highly desirable. GPCRs are now understood to be dynamic molecules that can adopt multiple distinct active conformations stabilized by different ligands. Agonist binding not only leads to G protein coupling but also triggers receptor phosphorylation by GPCR kinases (GRKs), followed by the recruitment of β-arrestins. β-Arrestins terminate G protein signaling while also functioning as independent signaling molecules3,18. Biased agonism describes a phenomenon in which different ligands stabilize distinct receptor conformations, resulting in differential efficacy for one signaling pathway over another relative to a reference ligand19. This paradigm enables the design of safer and more effective drugs by targeting only the pathways responsible for the desired therapeutic effects while avoiding those leading to side effects20,21.

Functional dissociation of signaling pathways has also been demonstrated for GPR84 using pharmacological tools. The reference agonist 6-n-octylaminouracil (6-OAU) potently activates both the Gi-mediated and β-arrestin recruitment pathways, enhancing both phagocytosis and chemotaxis22,23. In contrast, DL-175 is a highly G protein-biased agonist that potently activates Gi but exhibits significantly reduced efficacy for β-arrestin recruitment23. Functional studies have shown that DL-175 enhances phagocytosis to a similar extent as 6-OAU but does not induce chemotaxis23,24, suggesting that Gi signaling primarily mediates phagocytosis at GPR84. In contrast, β-arrestin signaling is responsible for chemotaxis, providing a rationale for G protein–biased agonism as a promising therapeutic strategy.

In an effort to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying GPR84 activities, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structures of the active-state GPR84 in complex with Gi and reference agonists such as 6-OAU and LY237 were recently reported22,25. These structures revealed similar features to those of other lipid GPCRs, including a unique “occluded” ligand-binding pocket completely shielded from the extracellular environment and the presence of extracellular loop 2 (ECL2) acting as a “lid” covering the pocket. The structure of an antagonist-bound inactive state has remained unknown, however, and the complete conformational transition of receptor activation has not been clarified. Furthermore, the structural basis underlying how a biased agonist like DL-175 potently activates Gi while limiting the conformational changes required for β-arrestin recruitment remains unclear.

In this study, we used single-particle cryo-EM to address these fundamental questions by determining the high-resolution structures of DL-175–bound GPR84 in complex with the Gi protein and GLPG1205-bound inactive state GPR84. By combining mutagenesis studies and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations, we characterize the conformational change from receptor inactivation to activation and identify the structural determinants that govern G protein–biased signaling. These findings provide a structural overview for the mechanism of biased agonism at GPR84 and may contribute to the rational design of a next generation of GPR84-targeted therapeutics with finely tuned signaling profiles.

Results

Structural determination of active and inactive GPR84

DL-175 is a G protein-biased agonist that potently activates Gi signaling while exhibiting markedly reduced efficacy for β-arrestin recruitment (Fig. 1a). By contrast, reference agonists such as 6-OAU, which mimic medium-chain fatty acids, activate both the G protein and β-arrestin pathways (Fig. 1a). First, to characterize the fundamental properties of the agonists used in this study, we profiled the signaling of the known G protein–biased agonist DL-175 and the reference agonist 6-OAU in GPR84-expressing cells. In a cAMP inhibition assay using HEK293T cells, both agonists exhibited comparable potency (Fig. 1b). However, DL-175 displayed a lower maximal inhibition of Forskolin-stimulated cAMP than 6-OAU (48% versus 59%), suggesting partial agonism. In the NanoBiT G protein recruitment assay, DL-175 and 6-OAU showed equivalent maximal G protein recruitment responses (Fig. 1c). While 6-OAU showed similar potency for β-arrestin recruitment as for the G protein pathway, DL-175 exhibited markedly reduced β-arrestin recruitment (Fig. 1d). Since the cAMP inhibition assay measures G protein-dependent signaling through unmodified endogenous G proteins, unlike the NanoBiT G protein recruitment assay, which employs a modified Gαi subunit, we used the cAMP assay as the primary method for quantifying signaling bias. Quantification of signaling bias using the relative activity (RA) method yielded a bias factor of 3.37 (log BF = 0.527 ± 0.139) toward the G protein pathway (Supplementary Table 3). These findings reaffirm the previously reported pronounced G protein–biased profile of DL-17523.

To understand the ligand binding and mechanism underlying bias activation on GPR84, we purified the GPR84-Gi complex in the presence of DL-175. To obtain a stable DL-175–bound heterotrimeric Gi-coupled GPR84 complex (GPR84-Gi), we introduced a thermostabilized BRIL at the N-terminus of the full-length wild-type (WT) GPR84. We applied the NanoBiT tethering strategy to improve the stability and homogeneity of the complex. GPR84-Gi was further stabilized by scFv16, which was introduced to bind to the interface between Gi and Gβ. Despite these efforts, however, the DL-175-bound GPR84-Gi complex was unstable, and most of the complex dissociated during the grid preparation process. We obtained a large data set and sorted out good-quality particles by iterative heterogeneous refinement, finally determined to an overall resolution of 3.3 Å (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 1). Most EM density maps of the complex were well resolved, enabling unambiguous modeling of the complex, including the ligand DL-175 (Supplementary Fig. 1e,f). In contrast, the α-helical domain of Gi and the LgBiT-HiBiT complex were absent from the map, likely due to high flexibility, which is a common observation in other GPCR structure determinations26,27.

To the best of our knowledge, no experimentally determined inactive structure of GPR84 has been reported. Despite remarkable progress in the structural analysis of GPCRs, analysis of inactive structures remains challenging, partly due to the small size of the receptor, which hampers particle alignment in cryo-EM. To facilitate alignment, we employed the BRIL strategy previously used to determine the inactivated conformation28,29. First, the Escherichia coli apocytochrome b562RIL was tightly fused between transmembrane segments (TM) 5 and 6, removing the intracellular loop (ICL) 3. Because GPR84 in the active conformation displays a significantly longer cytoplasmic extension of TM5, we reasoned that the C-terminus of BRIL alone would not be sufficient to form a continuous helix with TM6. We therefore inserted a segment of the A2AR sequence to bridge this gap; the resulting chimeric linker was predicted by AlphaFold2 to form a continuous helix connecting TM5 and TM6 to BRIL. The construct was stable during the purification process, and the obtained purified sample formed a complex with anti-BRIL Fab (BAG2) and anti-kappa-Nb (FabNb) for single-particle analysis. The structure of the GLPG1205-bound GPR84-BAG2-FabNb complex was successfully determined at a 2.7-Å resolution. Further refinement focusing on the receptors resulted in a map that enabled modeling of the ligands and most side chains (Fig. 1f and Supplementary Fig. 2). In both the active and inactive states, the overall architecture is stabilized by the conserved disulfide bond between C933.25 and C168ECL2, connecting TM3 to ECL2 and a second disulfide bridge between C11Nt and C166ECL2 (Supplementary Fig. 3). These disulfide bonds constrict the extracellular entrance, effectively occluding the ligand-binding pocket from the external environment. Alanine substitutions of these cysteine residues reduced receptor activity25.

Molecular architecture of DL-175 receptor recognition

The cryo-EM structure reveals that the ligand-binding pocket of GPR84 is amphipathic, comprising a polar region at the extracellular vestibule and a hydrophobic cavity within the receptor core. DL-175 occupies the same orthosteric pocket as the previously described agonists 6-OAU, LY237, and 3-OH-C12 (Supplementary Fig. 4). Consistent with the pocket’s amphipathic nature, the oxidopyridine moiety of DL-175 is situated in the polar region, whereas its bulky chloronaphthalene group is buried deep within the hydrophobic cavity. Notably, polar interactions between DL-175 and GPR84 are limited and involve only a single hydrogen bond between the oxygen of the oxidopyridine ring and Ser169ECL2 (Fig. 2a). As anticipated, the S169A mutant exhibited diminished receptor activation (Fig. 2b, upper). Furthermore, although the oxidopyridine moiety lacks extensive interactions, it forms van der Waals contacts with several residues within a 4-Å radius, including Y692.53 at TM2, L1003.32 and N1043.36 at TM3, F3356.51 at TM6; and T3597.42, and W3607.43 at TM7 (Ballesteros–Weinstein numbering in superscript30). In contrast, the chloronaphthalene group establishes extensive hydrophobic interactions with surrounding residues such as F1013.33, M1835.43, Y1865.46, F1875.47, Y3326.48, F3356.51, and L3366.52 (Fig. 2a). Consistent with these structural observations, alanine substitution of these hydrophobic residues, except for F1875.47 and L3366.52, markedly decreases receptor activation by DL-175 (Fig. 2b). Intriguingly, the Y69A, F187A, and W360A mutations markedly enhanced receptor activation, although F187A and W360A decrease cell surface expression level (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Fig. 5a). Y69 and W360 form hydrogen bonds with the polar moiety of 6-OAU, which is critical for receptor binding and activity. However, DL-175 is highly hydrophobic in nature, and the polar parts of the phenyl oxygen at Y69 and the nitrogen at W360 may be disadvantageous for binding, potentially allowing the alanine variants to significantly increase potency. The distinct mechanism underlying the F187A phenotype is discussed below.

R172ECL2, located at the polar head of the ligand-binding pocket of GPR84, is considered essential for the recognition of medium-chain fatty acids, as suggested by the structures of GPR84 bound to 6-OAU and LY23722,25. The distances between 6-OAU and R172ECL2 and between LY237 and R172ECL2 are 3.2 Å and 2.9 Å, respectively, forming a polar interaction. Consistent with this structural observation, the activity of the fatty acid-like agonist 6-OAU was abolished in the R172 mutant (Supplementary Fig. 6). The distance between DL-175 and R172, by contrast, is approximately 4.3 Å (Fig. 2c). Molecular dynamics simulations further revealed a striking divergence in the dynamic behavior of R172ECL2 between the two ligands. While the distance between R172ECL2 and 6-OAU fluctuates broadly across trajectories, the distance between R172ECL2 and DL-175 converges to approximately 2.5 Å and remains remarkably stable throughout all simulations, indicating a persistent hydrogen bond that is not fully captured in the static cryo-EM structure (Supplementary Fig. 7). Intriguingly, the R172A mutation significantly enhanced the G protein activation efficacy of DL-175, suggesting that the interaction between DL-175 and R172ECL2 may modulate receptor activity. The broader functional implications of this observation are discussed below.

Molecular architecture of GLPG-1205-receptor recognition

GLPG1205 is a compound that has progressed to clinical trials as a potential therapeutic agent for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis12 and ulcerative colitis31 (Fig. 3a). Although GLPG1205 is known to inhibit GPR84, the structural basis of its antagonism has remained elusive.

While GLPG1205 occupies the same orthosteric binding site as DL-175, its binding pose is remarkably different (Fig. 3b, c). Whereas agonists such as DL-175 and 6-OAU are embedded parallel to the membrane plane, GLPG1205 binds in an orientation perpendicular to it (Fig. 3d). This perpendicular orientation allows GLPG1205 to penetrate substantially deeper into the receptor core, reaching a depth of 17 Å, compared to 10.6 Å for DL-175 and 9.5 Å for 6-OAU (Fig. 3d). The central pyrimidoisoquinoline core occupies a position analogous to the polar moieties of the other agonists and fills a substantial portion of the GPR84 ligand pocket. The pyrimidoisoquinoline group forms a hydrogen bond with the amide of W3607.43, and the methoxy group forms hydrogen bonds with N1043.36 and Y3326.48. Furthermore, it establishes extensive hydrophobic interactions with residues including Leu1003.32, F1013.33, V1063.38, V165ECL2, and F3356.51 (Fig. 3e). The dioxane group extends deep into the pocket, where it is surrounded by an amphipathic region comprising residues such as Y692.53, N1043.36, F3286.44, Y3326.48, and T3597.42 (Fig. 3e, middle). This deep engagement creates a steric clash with T3597.42, effectively impeding the inward shift of TM7 required for receptor activation. Furthermore, the rigid positioning of the dioxane group sterically restricts the rotameric switch of N1043.36, locking it in an inactive conformation (Supplementary Fig. 8a). Finally, the cyclopropylacetyl group extends toward the extracellular space, positioning itself near the crevice between TM3 and TM6, where it forms hydrophobic interactions with F1013.33, F170ECL2 and M1835.43 (Fig. 3e, right). Notably, this extracellular extension creates a direct steric clash with F3356.51 on TM6, preventing the inward closure of TM6 observed in agonist-bound structures (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Collectively, the perpendicular binding orientation and the dual steric engagement of both the deep pocket and extracellular vestibule distinguish GLPG1205 from membrane-parallel agonists and underlie its antagonistic mechanism at GPR84.

Activation and inactivation mechanisms of GPR84

Our structures of GPR84 in an inactive state bound to GLPG1205 and in an active state bound to DL-175 allowed us to characterize the receptor’s activation and inactivation mechanisms in detail (Fig. 4). Relative to the inactive state, the extracellular sides of TM6 and TM7 shift inward toward the receptor core in the active state (Fig. 4b). Concurrently, the intracellular segment of TM6 undergoes a significant outward displacement, accompanied by an inward movement of TM7, a hallmark conformational change associated with the activation of class A GPCRs32 (Fig. 4c). This movement of TM6 prevents steric clashes with the C-terminal helix of the Gαi subunit, thereby facilitating G protein coupling and activation.

The toggle-switch residue Y3326.48 transitions to a downward conformation, leading to the rearrangement of the hydrogen bond with N1043.36. This rearrangement triggers a rotameric change in F3286.44, which facilitates the outward swing of the intracellular segment of TM6, a hallmark of class A GPCR activation. e In the inactive state, Y3326.48 and F1875.47 form a π-π stacking interaction that stabilizes the receptor’s inactive conformation. To accommodate the bulky moiety of DL-175, F1875.47 rotates outward, thereby disrupting this stabilizing interaction and enabling the activation cascade. f Intracellular conformational changes leading to G protein coupling. Following activation, the hydroxyl group of Y3326.48 forms a new hydrogen bond with N3627.45 on TM7. This interaction propagates the conformational signal to the conserved D(G)RY and NPxxY motifs, ultimately stabilizing the receptor in a fully active state competent for G protein engagement.

To identify the molecular events underlying these helix movements, we examined the side-chain conformational changes in detail. In the inactive structure, steric hindrance from the more extended structure of GLPG1205 prevents F3356.51 from shifting toward the receptor’s center, thereby stabilizing TM6 in an outward conformation. Downstream, N1043.36 forms a hydrogen bond with the phenolic oxygen of the toggle switch residue, Y3326.48, locking it in an upward conformation (Fig. 4d). Furthermore, Y3326.48 forms a π-π stacking interaction with F1875.47 at TM5, which further stabilizes the inactive state (Fig. 4e). The binding of DL-175 induces substantial conformational changes. An induced-fit interaction with F3356.51 leads to an inward shift of the extracellular segment of TM6 (Fig. 4d). Concurrently, the parallel orientation of DL-175 relative to the membrane plane pushes Y3326.48, forcing its side chain into a downward conformation. This shift results in the formation of a new hydrogen-bond network among N1043.36, Y3326.48, and N3627.45 (Fig. 4d, f). Consequently, F3286.44 rotates to avoid a steric clash, initiating the outward displacement of TM6 (Fig. 4e). The altered conformation of N3627.45 then triggers the inward movement of TM7 (Fig. 4f). In parallel, F1875.47, which forms a π-π stacking interaction with Y3326.48, undergoes an outward rotation upon DL-175 binding to accommodate the chloronaphthalene group, a conformational rearrangement that is required for ligand-induced receptor activation (Fig. 4e). These concerted movements propagate to induce conformational changes in the N3667.49xxPY3707.53 motif and the D(G)R1173.50119Y3.51 motif, allowing the receptor to adopt a fully active conformation (Fig. 4f).

Steric interactions create signal bias

To elucidate how ligand-receptor interactions generate signaling bias, we compared the structures of GPR84 bound to the reference agonist 6-OAU and the G protein-biased agonist DL-175. The key structural distinction between the two agonists lies in the hydrophobic tail: the flexible saturated octyl chain of 6-OAU is replaced by a bulky, rigid chloronaphthalene group in DL-175 (Fig. 5a). Other previously reported reference agonists, such as LY237 and 3-OH-C12, similarly possess a flexible alkyl tail (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Superposition of the receptor moieties from the DL-175- and 6-OAU-bound structures reveals a high degree of overall similarity, with a root-mean-square deviation of 0.994 Å; however, significant differences within the ligand-binding pocket were identified. In terms of polar interactions, the carbonyl group of 6-OAU forms hydrogen bonds with R172ECL2 and W3607.43. In contrast, in the DL-175-bound structure, R172ECL2 is located 4.3 Å away from the ligand, although MD simulations revealed that this distance dynamically converges to approximately 2.5 Å, while W3607.43 interacts primarily through van der Waals forces (Fig. 5b). In the hydrophobic region, to accommodate the chloronaphthalene group of DL-175, the extracellular end of TM6, including L3366.52, shifts outward relative to its position in the 6-OAU-bound structure (Fig. 5b). This movement is coupled with an inward displacement of F1875.47 on TM5. Furthermore, superposition with an inactive-state structure suggests that the TM6 conformation in the DL-175-bound complex is intermediate between the GLPG1205-bound inactive state and the 6-OAU-bound active state (Fig. 5b). For other receptors with well-characterized biased signaling, such as AT1R and μOR, interactions with the residue at position 7.43 are critical33,34. In GPR84, although 6-OAU forms a hydrogen bond with the amide of W3607.43 while DL-175 does not, the position of W3607.43 remains unchanged across all three structures (Fig. 5b), suggesting that the structural basis of bias at GPR84 is distinct from that of AT1R and μOR. Rather, the outward displacement of TM6 driven by the bulky chloronaphthalene group of DL-175, and the resulting intermediate conformation of L3366.52, emerged as key structural features distinguishing the biased from the reference agonist. To functionally validate these structural observations, we generated four alanine mutants (R172A, F187A, L336A, and W360A) and profiled their signaling across cAMP inhibition, β-arrestin recruitment, and GRK2/3 recruitment assays (Fig. 5c–f, Supplementary Table 2).

The L336A mutation exhibited the most pathway-selective phenotype. In the cAMP inhibition assay, L336A showed profiles comparable to the wild-type receptor for both ligands. However, in the β-arrestin and GRK recruitment assays, L336A selectively enhanced the efficacy of DL-175 by approximately 3-fold for β-arrestin and 1.7-fold for GRK2 and GRK3, whereas the efficacy of 6-OAU was either reduced or unaffected. These results are consistent with our steric clash model. L3366.52 selectively hinders the conformational transition required for GRK/β-arrestin engagement by DL-175 without compromising G protein coupling, establishing L3366.52 as the primary structural determinant of signaling bias. Consistent with this interpretation, bias factor analysis revealed that the L336A mutation reduced the bias factor of DL-175 from 3.37 to 1.21 (log BF = 0.083 ± 0.197), a value indistinguishable from unity, indicating effective abolition of G protein bias (Supplementary Table 3). The F187A mutation produced a universal gain-of-function phenotype irrespective of the ligand or signaling pathway. F187A dramatically increased the potency of both agonists (approximately 1000-fold for DL-175) across all assays, with moderate enhancements in efficacy. This is consistent with the structural observation that F1875.47 occupies a rotameric position in the inactive state that would sterically clash with an incoming ligand. Its removal lowers the energetic barrier for ligand entry into the pocket, enhancing signaling uniformly rather than selectively. The W360A mutation displayed ligand-dependent effects. For 6-OAU, W360A reduced efficacy across β-arrestin and GRK pathways, consistent with the loss of a key hydrogen bond between 6-OAU and W3607.43. For DL-175, which does not form this hydrogen bond, W360A instead drastically increased potency by over 1000-fold across all pathways without substantially affecting efficacy. One possibility is that removal of the bulky indole side chain permits an alternative binding pose that more favorably positions the chloronaphthalene group within the hydrophobic cavity. The R172A mutation produced the most contrasting effects between the two ligands. For 6-OAU, R172A completely abolished activity across all assays, consistent with the essential role of R172ECL2 in accommodating fatty acid-like agonists. For DL-175, R172A paradoxically enhanced efficacy in the β-arrestin and GRK pathways by approximately 3.5-fold and 1.5-fold, respectively, while increasing cAMP efficacy by 1.6-fold. These contrasting outcomes are discussed below.

To directly test whether GRK-mediated phosphorylation acts as the upstream determinant of signaling bias, we measured β-arrestin recruitment in the presence of the GRK2/3 inhibitor cmpd101. Treatment with cmpd101 resulted in only a partial reduction of β-arrestin recruitment across all mutants examined, except for R172A, which showed a more pronounced inhibition (Supplementary Fig. 9). Although the partial suppression observed in most mutants suggests that GRK activity contributes to β-arrestin recruitment at GPR84, the incomplete inhibition indicates that GRK-mediated phosphorylation alone is insufficient to fully account for the signaling bias. The structural determinants governing the differential coupling to downstream transducers, therefore, likely involve additional mechanisms beyond GRK accessibility, which need further investigation.

Distinct conformational dynamics underlie biased agonism

Intracellular loop 3 (ICL3) is intrinsically disordered and thus less susceptible to direct ligand-induced effects. We hypothesized that the observed conformational differences in TM5, TM6, and TM7 allosterically influence GRK and β-arrestin binding and, consequently, ICL3 phosphorylation. Indeed, 6-OAU stimulation reportedly leads to the phosphorylation of T263 and T264 in ICL3, whereas DL-175 stimulation does not35. In our G protein–bound structures, the intracellular sides of the receptor structures and G-protein binding mode are nearly identical, making it difficult to determine the structural basis for signaling bias from these static snapshots (Supplementary Fig. 10). Therefore, we performed MD simulations on the G protein–free receptor, using the 6-OAU–bound structure (PDB: 8G05), our DL-175–bound structure, and the apo state as starting models. Our MD simulations revealed that the G protein–biased agonist DL-175 exhibited markedly distinct dynamics compared to both the reference agonist 6-OAU and the apo state (Supplementary Fig. 11). First, we used principal component analysis to compare differences in overall structures between four models (Supplementary Fig. 11a-f). The G-protein-free receptor structure significantly shifted along the most dominant principal component (PC1). The DL-175-bound receptor exhibited significant fluctuation, displaying multiple conformational states along both PC1 and PC2 (the second most dominant principal component) (Supplementary Fig. 11d). In contrast, 6-OAU and apo state are significantly confined in a narrower conformational space (Supplementary Fig. 11e, f). To elucidate the specific structural changes underlying this difference in global dynamics, we performed a detailed analysis of the regions critical for activation. We evaluated the stability of the hydrogen bond network surrounding the toggle switch residue Y3326.48, which is crucial for the activation of class A GPCRs32. Simulations using both 6-OAU and apo state showed that the distance distributions for the N1043.36-Y3326.48 and Y3326.48-N3627.45 pairs formed a single dense cluster (Supplementary Fig. 11h,i). In contrast, simulations using DL-175 revealed a bimodal distribution for these distances, confirming two distinct clusters (Supplementary Fig. 11g). This bimodal distribution suggests that DL-175 destabilizes the toggle switch hydrogen bond network, promoting dynamic fluctuations between multiple conformations. Next, we investigated how these differences in the receptor core propagate to the cytoplasmic side. Measuring the distance between the cytoplasmic ends of the helices revealed a significant difference between the two agonists. The conformational change of TM6 was calculated as the distance between R1183.50 and V3176.34. The biased agonist DL-175 tends to fix TM6 in a single, stable open state at approximately 20.5 Å (Supplementary Fig. 11j). In contrast, both 6-OAU and apo state samples multiple states, transitioning between shorter and longer distances, suggesting greater structural fluctuations compared to DL-175 (Supplementary Fig. 11j). Furthermore, for the distance between TM2 (D662.50) and TM7 (P3677.50), 6-OAU and apo state stabilizes a single structure characterized by a short distance, corresponding to an inward shift of TM7 (Supplementary Fig. 11k). In contrast, DL-175 induces a bimodal distribution, sampling both short- and long-range conformations (Supplementary Fig. 11k). While DL-175 stabilizes TM6 in a single open state, it induces a bimodal distribution of TM7 positions, suggesting that TM6 and TM7 respond differently to biased agonist binding. Taken together, these simulations reveal that DL-175 induces a conformational landscape that is fundamentally distinct from both the 6-OAU-bound and apo states, which closely resemble each other across all metrics examined.

Discussion

In this study, we report the cryo-EM structures of GPR84-Gi in complex with the G protein–biased agonist DL-175 and in its inactive state complexed with the antagonist GLPG-1205. By integrating structural data with functional analyses of site-directed mutants and MD simulations, we provide a structural overview for understanding GPR84 activation and signaling bias, revealing how distinct ligand-receptor interactions translate into specific signaling outcomes.

To elucidate the molecular mechanism of biased signaling in GPR84, we compared our DL-175–bound structure with previously reported structures complexed with reference agonists such as 6-OAU and LY237. This comparison revealed that the bulky chloronaphthalene moiety of DL-175 creates a steric clash with L3366.52 on TM6, preventing the helix from fully engaging the receptor core. The TM6 conformation induced by DL-175 can therefore be considered an “intermediate active state,” positioned between the inactive and fully active, reference agonist-bound conformations (Fig. 5b). Our functional data strongly support this structural insight. Remarkably, the L336A mutant, while preserving G protein activation by DL-175, dramatically rescues the recruitment of GRK and β-arrestin. We hypothesize that the smaller alanine side chain alleviates the steric hindrance, allowing TM6 to shift closer to the receptor core and adopt a conformation analogous to that induced by reference agonists. These findings provide evidence that the steric interaction between the ligand and L3366.52 acts as a critical molecular switch that determines signaling bias at GPR84. MD simulations further reveal that this intermediate conformation has distinct dynamic consequences. DL-175 destabilizes the toggle switch hydrogen bond network surrounding Y3326.48, producing bimodal distance distributions, whereas both 6-OAU and the ligand-free apo state maintain unimodal clusters (Supplementary Fig. 11g–i). This divergence propagates to the cytoplasmic side, where DL-175, 6-OAU, and the apo state each exhibit distinct conformational signatures for TM6 and TM7 (Supplementary Fig. 11j, k). The close resemblance between the apo and 6-OAU-bound dynamics suggests that the reference agonist stabilizes a conformation already accessible to the unliganded receptor, whereas DL-175 populates a qualitatively distinct set of conformations. This divergence provides a mechanistic rationale for how ligands with comparable G protein efficacy can nonetheless produce markedly different β-arrestin recruitment profiles.

Beyond the bias-determining L3366.52, our mutagenesis screen revealed that several orthosteric pocket residues, W3607.43, F1875.47, and R172ECL2, all produce gain-of-function phenotypes for DL-175 upon alanine substitution, yet none of these mutations alter signaling selectivity in the manner observed for L336A (Supplementary Table 2). W360A is particularly noteworthy because it enhances all signaling pathways for DL-175 uniformly without altering selectivity, while reducing the efficacy of 6-OAU across β-arrestin and GRK pathways (Fig. 5c–f). W360 occupies position 7.43, which plays a central role in dictating signaling bias in multiple GPCRs, including AT1R, μOR, and CCR133,36,37. GPR84 unusually possesses a tryptophan rather than the tyrosine conserved at this position in over 90% of class A GPCRs. These contrasting effects are consistent with the distinct binding modes of the two ligands: 6-OAU forms a direct hydrogen bond with W3607.43 that is critical for full efficacy, whereas DL-175 interacts primarily through van der Waals contacts, and removal of the bulky indole side chain likely creates a more favorable hydrophobic environment for the chloronaphthalene group. These findings indicate that position 7.43 in GPR84 modulates ligand binding affinity rather than signaling bias, distinguishing GPR84 from the AT1R/μOR paradigm. F1875.47 forms a π-π stacking interaction with the toggle switch residue Y3326.48 in the inactive state (Fig. 4e), and its outward rotation is required to accommodate the incoming ligand. The F187A mutation produces a universal gain-of-function phenotype, enhancing both potency and efficacy for all ligands across all pathways, consistent with the interpretation that F1875.47 imposes a steric barrier that must be overcome during ligand-induced activation. The F1875.47-Y3326.48 interface thus represents a kinetic checkpoint gating receptor activation, a feature that may be exploitable for the design of ligands with enhanced potency. In contrast, R172ECL2 presents a more complex case. MD simulations reveal that R172ECL2 forms a persistent hydrogen bond with DL-175 that is not fully captured in the static cryo-EM structure (Fig. 2c, Supplementary Fig. 7), and R172A selectively enhances β-arrestin and GRK recruitment by DL-175 while completely abolishing all signaling by 6-OAU. We propose that this stable interaction constrains ECL2 in a conformation that limits the receptor rearrangements required for transducer engagement. Given that R172ECL2 is located near a putative ligand entrance between TM5, TM6, and ECL2 (Supplementary Fig. 12), it may additionally modulate the dynamics of ligand entry and ECL2 opening, consistent with reports that ECL2 residues allosterically regulate transducer coupling in other GPCRs37–39. The diversity of these phenotypes highlights the remarkable plasticity of the GPR84 binding pocket in accommodating hydrophobic ligands and reveals that multiple energetic barriers, namely the F1875.47–Y3326.48 kinetic checkpoint, the R172ECL2-mediated ECL2 constraint, and the steric environment surrounding W3607.43, collectively shape the activation energy landscape. Among these, L3366.52 is the sole position that selectively gates the bias outcome. The precise interplay among these barriers, and how they are differentially engaged by chemically diverse ligands, warrants further investigation through mutant MD simulations and structural analyses of GPR84–β-arrestin complexes.

Collectively, we propose that the steric interaction between the bulky chloronaphthalene group of DL-175 and L3366.52 on TM6 is the primary structural determinant of G protein-biased signaling at GPR84. A rational design strategy for G protein-biased agonists targeting GPR84 should therefore incorporate a bulky substituent that intentionally introduces steric hindrance with L3366.52 deep within the orthosteric pocket. This design principle may extend beyond GPR84 to other class A GPCRs where an analogous steric gate exists at the equivalent TM6 position. Future structural analyses of GPR84 in complex with GRK2/3 or β-arrestin, and with ligands possessing diverse chemical scaffolds or allosteric modulators such as PSB-1667140,41, will provide further mechanistic insights into the precise control of GPR84 signaling and enable the rational design of transformative therapeutics targeting this receptor.

Methods

Constructs

Human GPR84 (residues 1–396) was cloned into a pFastBac1 vector with an N-terminal hemagglutinin (HA) signal peptide and a FLAG-tag, followed by thermostabilized apocytochrome b562RIL (BRIL). The LgBiT subunit was fused to the C terminus to stabilize the complex. Gαi1 (Gi) with two dominant-negative mutations (G203A, A326S), Gβ-SmBiT, Gγ2, and a single-chain antibody scFv16 (a kind gift from Dr. Brian Kobilka at Stanford University) were individually cloned into pFastBac1 vectors. To generate mutants for the signaling assay, the site-directed mutation was introduced in GPR84 using the primers shown in Supplementary Table 2.

The expression construct for human GPR84 in its inactive conformation was designed with an HA signal peptide at the N-terminus, insertion of BRIL in intracellular loop 3, and a C-terminal TEV cleavage site followed by mEGFP and a 10x His tag. The BRIL residues were inserted between WT GPR84 ICL3 loop residues Q219 and D309 using two short, modified linkers derived from the A2A adenosine receptor (ERARSTLQKEV between the C terminus of BRIL and residue D309).

Protein expression and purification

GPR84, Gi, Gβ1, Gγ2, and scFv16 were co-expressed in Sf9 insect cells using the Bac-to-Bac Baculovirus Expression System (Invitrogen). Cell cultures were grown in Sf900-II SFM medium (Gibco) to a density of 2–3 × 106 cell/mL and then infected with the viruses. Cell cultures were collected by centrifugation 48 h post-infection and stored at -80 °C.

For purification of the GPR84-Gi complex, cell pellets were lysed by homogenization in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 50 mM NaCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 10% glycerol, 25 mU/mL apyrase (New England Biolabs), and Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). After 1-h incubation at room temperature, the membranes were solubilized for 2 h at 4 °C in 0.5% (w/v) lauryl maltose neopentylglycol (LMNG, Anatrace), and 0.03% (w/v) cholesteryl hemisuccinate Tris salt (CHS, Anatrace). The supernatant was cleared by centrifugation and incubated for 1 h with M2 FLAG resin (Sigma-Aldrich). After binding, the resin was washed with 10 column volumes of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.05% LMNG, 0.05% glyco-diosgenin (GDN, Anatrace), and 0.003% (w/v) CHS. The complex was then washed with 10 column volumes of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.01% LMNG, 0.01% GDN, and 0.0006% (w/v) CHS. The complex was eluted in the second wash buffer containing 200 μg/mL FLAG peptide (Sigma-Aldrich). The eluted complex was supplemented with 100 µM TCEP (Fujifilm, Wako) to maintain reducing conditions. The complex was purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 10/300 column (GE) in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.00075% LMNG, and 0.00025% GDN with 0.00004% CHS and 100 μM TCEP. Peak fractions were concentrated to ~15 mg/mL for cryo-EM. We added DL175 for stable complex formation throughout the purification procedure.

To purify inactive GPR84, cell pellets were lysed by homogenization in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 10 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EDTA, and EDTA-free cOmplete Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Roche). Membrane pellets were collected by centrifugation at 50,000 xg for 20 min, followed by the addition of 10 μM of GLPG1205 and incubation for 30 min at room temperature. Membranes were solubilized for 2 h with 1% LMNG and 0.2% CHS in 500 mM NaCl and 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 10% glycerol, and 100 μM TCEP supplemented with EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (cOmplete, Sigma-Aldrich). The supernatant was cleared by centrifugation and incubated for 1 h with M2 FLAG resin (Sigma-Aldrich). After binding, the resin was washed with 10 column volumes of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol, 0.05% LMNG, 0.05% glyco-diosgenin (GDN, Anatrace), and 0.003% (w/v) CHS. The resin was further washed with 10 column volumes of 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG, 0.01% GDN, and 0.0006% (w/v) CHS. The protein was eluted using wash buffer containing 200 μg/mL FLAG peptide (Sigma-Aldrich). To remove the GFP tag, the eluted protein was incubated overnight with TEV protease (in-house product). After RV-IMAC, an excess of anti-BRIL Fab and anti-Fab nanobody was added, and the mixture was incubated on ice for 2 h. The complex was concentrated and purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superose 6 10/300 column (GE) in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 0.00075% LMNG, and 0.00025% GDN, with 0.00004% CHS, 100 μM TCEP, and 10 µM GLPG1205. Peak fractions were concentrated to ~15 mg/mL for cryo-EM.

Cryo-EM grid preparation and data collection

Purified GPR84-Gi and GPR84-BRIL-BAG2-FabNb complexes (3.5 μL) were applied to a glow-discharged 300 mesh R1.2/1.3 grid (Quantifoil). Support films were plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher) with a 10-s hold period, blot force varying between 0 ~ 10, and a blotting time of 4 s, while maintaining 100% humidity and a temperature of 4 °C. For GPR84-Gi with DL175, cryo-EM data were collected at 300 kV using a JEM-Z320FHC electron microscope (JEOL) equipped with a K2 Summit direct electron detector in the electron counting mode using SerialEM42. The calibrated pixel size at the specimen level was 1.005 Å, and 8-s exposures were dose-fractionated into 40 frames with an electron flux of 8 e-/pix/s. For GPR84-BAG2-FabNb with GLPG1205, cryo-EM data were collected using a CryoARM3000 (JEOL) with a BioQuantum energy filter (Gatan) and a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan), operated using serialEM software (Thermo Fisher) running a 3 ×3 image shift pattern at a 0° stage tilt. A nominal magnification of 60 K was used in CDS mode with a calibrated pixel size of 0.878 Å, and 3.35-s exposures were dose-fractionated into 60 frames with an electron flux of 14.2 e-/pix/s.

Cryo-EM data processing

Datasets for GPR84-Gi and GPR84-BRIL-BAG2-FabNb complexes were processed using CryoSPARC43 and Relion44. For GPR84-Gi with DL175, GPR84-BAG2-FabNb with GLPG1205, a total of 12,884 and 6846 image stacks, respectively, were subjected to beam-induced motion correction using MotionCor2.144 in Relion-4.0. Contrast transfer function parameters for each micrograph were estimated by the CryoSPARC patch CTF algorithm. Particles were autopicked using reference-based picking, extracted, and subjected to several rounds of 2D classification to remove contaminants. Initial maps were generated using stochastic gradient descent-based multi-ab initio reconstruction in CryoSPARC. Iterative heterogeneous refinement was performed using the generated initial model as a reference. A good particle set was subjected to 3D non-uniform refinement45, and particles were re-imported to RELION-4.0. Particles were then subjected to 3D refinement in RELION-4.0, followed by Bayesian polishing. Particles were then imported back to CryoSPARC for 3D non-uniform refinement. The final dataset contained 159,829 and 220,809 particles, which generated maps with 3.24-Å and 2.72-Å resolutions, respectively. To improve the density of the GPR84-Gi map with DL-175, we created a mask covering the receptor and G protein regions for local refinement, yielding resolutions of 3.4 Å for the receptor and 3.1 Å for the G protein, respectively; the resulting maps were then combined. For the GLPG1205-bound inactive GPR84, local refinement focused on the receptor region yielded a map at 3.05-Å resolution. The resolution of these maps was estimated internally in CryoSPARC by gold-standard Fourier shell correlation using the 0.143 criterion. Local resolution estimation was performed with the CryoSPARC local resolution estimation algorithms using half maps.

Model building and refinement

Model building and refinement were carried out using an Alphafold246 predicted structure as a starting model, which was fitted into our EM-map using UCSF ChimeraX47. A draft model was refined by iterations of real-space refinement in Phenix48 and manual refinement in Coot49. The ligand model was generated with the Grade web server (https://grade.globalphasing.org), docked using Coot, and refined in Phenix. Final map model validations were carried out using Molprobity in Phenix.

cAMP inhibition assay

The inhibitory effects of different GPR84 constructs or mutants on forskolin-induced cAMP accumulation were measured using the GloSensor cAMP assay (Promega). HEK293T cells were transiently co-transfected with the GloSensor and various mutants of GPR84 plasmids using polyethylenimine in six-well plates. After 24-h incubation at 37 °C, the transfected cells were harvested and washed with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), centrifuged at 190 × g for 5 min, and suspended in Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 0.01% bovine serum albumin (BSA, Fujifilm Wako) and 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). The cells were then resuspended for 2 h at room temperature in 2% GloSensor cAMP reagent (Promega). The cell suspension was seeded into a 96-well white plate (Corning) at a volume of 80 μL per well. A 10 μL volume of 10x drug buffer diluted in HBSS containing HEPES and 0.01% BSA was added to each well; the plates were incubated for 10 min. Then, a 10 μL volume of 10 µM forskolin (Sigma-Aldrich; final 1 μM) was added and the plates were incubated for 20 min at room temperature. The luminescence of the cells was measured using a SpectraMax i3x multi-plate reader. Data were analyzed using “Sigmodal, 4PL, X is concentration” in GraphPad Prism 10.0.

NanoBiT assay

Plasmids for the NanoBiT–G protein–recruitment assay50, NanoBiT–β-arrestin–recruitment assay, and NanoBiT-GRK recruitment assay were previously described51,52.

HEK293T cells were seeded in a six-well culture plate at a cell density of 2 × 105 cells /mL in 2 mL of complete DMEM and cultured for 24 h in a humidified 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. Plasmid transfection was performed in six-well plates (per well, hereafter) with the following plasmid mixtures. For the Gi protein recruitment assay, 500 ng LgBiT-containing Gi, 500 ng Gβ1, 500 ng Gγ2, and 500 ng of the GPR84-SmbiT were transfected. For the β-arrestin recruitment assay, 200 ng SmBiT-β-arrestin 1, and 1000 ng GPR84-LgBiT were transfected. For the NanoBiT-GRK recruitment assay, 600 ng GRK2-SmBiT or GRK3-SmBiT and 600 ng GPR84-Lg were transfected. After 24 h of culture, the transfected cells were harvested with 1 mL of 0.53 mM EDTA-containing Dulbecco’s PBS, followed by the addition of 1 mL of HBSS containing HEPES. The cells were pelleted by centrifugation at 190 × g for 5 min and resuspended in 2 mL HBSS containing 0.01% BSA and 5 mM HEPES (pH 7.4;assay buffer). The cell suspension was seeded in a 96-well culture white plate (SPL Life Science) at a volume of 80 μL (per well hereafter) and loaded with 20 μL of 5× Nano-Glo Luciferase Assay Substrate (Promega) solution diluted in the assay buffer. After a 1-h incubation at room temperature, luminescent background signals were measured using a SpectraMax i3x multi-plate reader (Molecular Devices). Test compound (6×, diluted in the assay buffer) was manually added to the cells (20 μL). Luminescent signals were measured 3–5 min after ligand addition and divided by the initial count. The ligand-induced signal ratio was normalized to that treated with the vehicle. The G protein dissociation signals were fitted to a four-parameter sigmoidal concentration–response curve, from which the pEC50 values (negative logarithmic values of half-maximum effective concentration values) and Emax were used to calculate the mean and standard error of the mean.

For β-arrestin recruitment assay in the presence of the GRK2/3 inhibitor cmpd101, cmpd101 was added to a final concentration of 30 μM.

MD simulation

We performed molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for the three systems, GPR84/DL-175, GPR84/6-OAU, and GPR84/DL-175/Gi. We prepared the initial structures of the GPR84/DL-175 and GPR84/6-OAU systems by deleting Gi from the GPR84/DL-175/Gi (this work. PDB ID: 9WIK) and GPR84/6-OAU/Gi complexes (PDB ID: 8G0422. For GPR84/DL-175/Gi, the structure of the Gi missing region was carefully modeled using the same procedure as described previously53. Residues 218-318 of the ICL3 loop were replaced with an (Ala)₁₃ linker, and the protonation states of all proteins were chosen to mimic physiological conditions at pH 7.0. The systems were embedded in a POPC lipid bilayer membrane within a 14.01 × 14.01 × 14.01 nm3 simulation box, then solvated using the TIP3P(CHARMM) water model and 0.15 M ionic concentration of NaCl to yield a final simulation box of 14.01 × 14.01 × 19.56 nm3. We employed the CHARMM36m force field54 to model the proteins, lipids, waters, and ions. The models were first energy-minimized using 50,000 steps of steepest descent followed by 50,000 steps of conjugate gradient algorithm to eliminate high-energy interactions. Subsequently, the systems were heated from 50 K to 300 K in the NVT ensemble using a v-rescale thermostat55 over 1.25 ns. The systems were equilibrated with the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar using v-rescale thermostat and c-rescale barostat56 with a progressive protocol of reducing force constants (unit: KJ/mol·nm²) for positional restraints in five steps: (1) protein backbone (2000), protein sidechains (1000), and lipid head groups (400) for 1.25 ns; (2) protein backbone (1000), protein sidechains (500), and lipid head groups (400) for 5.0 ns; (3) protein backbone (500), protein sidechains (200), and lipid head groups (200) for 5.0 ns; (4) protein backbone (200), protein sidechains (50), and lipid head groups (40) for 5.0 ns; and (5) protein backbone only (200) for 100.0 ns. Production MD simulations were carried out without positional restraints for 1 μs using the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1 bar with a Nosé-Hoover thermostat and a Parrinello-Rahman barostat. For each system, 10 independent MD trials were conducted to enhance sampling statistics. All covalent bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm57. The final 500 ns of all simulation trials were analyzed, and principal component analysis was performed using the backbone atoms of the GPR84 receptor.

Statistics and reproducibility

All functional study data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software) and are presented as means ± SEM of at least three independent experiments.
