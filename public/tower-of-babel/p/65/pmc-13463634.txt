Conformational epitope engagement by anti-MSLN ADC RC88 confers resistance to soluble mesothelin
Authors: Yidan Xu, Mingyang Li, Lili Wang, Kailin Wang, Xiao Wang, Xiaoping Zhang, Zhanjiao Yu, Yinghao Xin, Chenglin He, Xiangyi He, Jing Zhou, Xiaoshan Min, Hang Chen, Zhulun Wang, Dong Li, Jianmin Fang, Yuanhao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMesothelin (MSLN) is a therapeutic target for antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) due to its tumor-selective overexpression. However, soluble MSLN (sMSLN) in circulation compromises efficacy by acting as a decoy. RC88 is a clinical-stage ADC composed of a humanized anti-MSLN antibody conjugated to a monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) payload. This study elucidates the unique binding mechanism that allows RC88 to maintain superior efficacy despite sMSLN interference.MethodsWe characterized the RC88–MSLN interaction via structural analysis and mutagenesis. The impact of sMSLN on binding was assessed using competitive cellular assays. In vitro internalization kinetics and cytotoxicity were evaluated to determine the therapeutic potential.ResultsRC88 targets a high-affinity N-terminal epitope overlapping the MUC16/CA125-binding site, competitively inhibiting this interaction. Notably, due to the structural flexibility of MSLN, RC88 also engages a secondary, low affinity juxtamembrane epitope at the C-terminus. This conformational binding confers resistance to sMSLN interference and enhanced tumor cell retention. In vitro, RC88-ADC maintained near-complete cytotoxic potency even in the presence of physiologically relevant sMSLN concentrations. In vivo, RC88 suppressed tumor growth in ovarian cancer xenografts.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that RC88 utilizes a novel conformational binding strategy to overcome the limitations of soluble antigen interference. These findings provide a mechanistic rationale for the encouraging clinical activity of RC88 and suggest that conformational epitope targeting represents a promising strategy for overcoming sMSLN interference in MSLN-positive cancers.

Introduction

Mesothelin, a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored cell surface glycoprotein encoded by the mesothelin (MSLN) gene on chromosome 16p13.3, exhibits limited expression in normal tissues but is aberrantly overexpressed in various solid tumors, including ovarian cancer, gastric cancer, mesothelioma, and colorectal cancer [1–4]. This tumor-restricted expression profile, combined with its limited distribution in normal tissues, has made MSLN an attractive target for antibody-based therapeutics such as CAR-T cells, immunotoxins, and antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) [5–9]. Despite decades of investigation, no MSLN-targeted therapy has yet gained approval from the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), underscoring the persistent challenges in effectively engaging this target [10–12].

The biological role of MSLN has been closely linked to tumor progression and metastasis [10]. It interacts with CA125, a glycoprotein abundantly expressed on the surface of ovarian and other cancer cells, through high-affinity binding [13, 14]. This interaction facilitates heterotypic cell adhesion, promoting intraperitoneal and pleural dissemination, which is a hallmark of advanced ovarian cancer and mesothelioma [3, 14]. In addition, MSLN has been implicated in the regulation of cell survival, proliferation, and resistance to apoptosis via downstream signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK [15, 16]. Preclinical models have shown that MSLN overexpression enhances tumor invasiveness and chemoresistance, while its knockdown suppresses tumor growth, further validating its functional relevance in oncogenesis [17, 18].

A major obstacle in the clinical development of MSLN-directed therapies is the presence of soluble MSLN (sMSLN), which is generated through proteolytic cleavage of membrane bound MSLN by sheddases such as ADAM10, ADAM17, BACE1, BACE2, and MMP15 [19]. Shed MSLN accumulates at high concentrations in patient serum, ascites, and pleural effusions, where it acts as a decoy antigen, binding therapeutic antibodies and reducing their availability to target tumor cells [20, 21]. This neutralization can diminish drug efficacy and, for ADCs, increase off-target toxicity risk. Elevated sMSLN has been shown to bind and neutralize anti-MSLN antibodies, directly compromising treatment and leading to worse prognosis [22–24]. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of targeting membrane-proximal epitopes of MSLN to circumvent sMSLN interference [10, 25].

In earlier phase I/IIa study (NCT04175847), RC88 showed encouraging antitumor activity and a manageable safety profile in patients with advanced MSLN-positive solid tumors [26]. In this study, we characterize the RC88–MSLN interaction to understand its pharmacological profile. Through structural and functional studies, we demonstrate that RC88 not only occupies the CA125 binding site but also binds the MSLN C-terminus via an induced conformational change. This conformational epitope engagement enhances sMSLN resistance and tumor anchoring, suggesting that the binding profile of RC88 contributes to its favorable clinical activity.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HEK293, OVCAR3-MSLN, and NCI-H226 cells were cultured at 37°C under 5% CO₂. HEK293 cells were maintained in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Cat. 11995065, Thermo Fisher), while OVCAR3-MSLN and NCI-H226 cells were in RPMI-1640 (Cat. A1049101, Thermo Fisher). Media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Cat. A5669701, Thermo Fisher) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. OVCAR-3 medium was supplemented with an additional 10% FBS and 0.01 mg/ml bovine insulin (Cat. I8040, Solarbio). HEK293, OVCAR3, and NCI-H226 cell lines were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The OVCAR3-MSLN cell line was generated from OVCAR3 cells via lentiviral transfection of MSLN.

Plasmid construction

For GPI-anchored MSLN, the coding sequence encoded, from N- to C-terminus, the MSLN NSP, a GS linker, thermostable GFP (TGP), and MSLN residues 501–622 [including the C-terminal signal peptide (CSP) 599–622], cloned into pcDNA3.1. Upon expression, proMSLN is processed by GPI transamidase, replacing the CSP with a GPI anchor. MSLN residues 501–598 are anchored on the surface, with TGP as a reporter.

For cell surface display, constructs in pcDNA3.1 encoded, from N- to C-terminus: a 6× His tag, an MSLN fragment (residues 488–598, 580–598, or 488–580), a GS linker, and the Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Receptors β (PDGFRβ) transmembrane domain. The His tag enables transfection efficiency assessment. All plasmids were synthesized by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China).

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by high-pressure heating in the 1× citrate buffer (Cat. MVS-0100, Xinmai). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide at 37°C for 10 min. For the detection of MSLN, sections were incubated at 37°C for 1 h with a home-made monoclonal anti-mesothelin antibody (clone 3-2G6, RemeGen) at 0.3 μg/ml. After washing, a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated secondary antibody was applied, followed by detection using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB, Cat. KIT-0016, Xinmai) substrate. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin.

HDX-MS

Twenty-seven micromoles of MSLN (His tagged MSLN 296–580, Cat. 13128-H08H1, SinoBiological) and 13.3 μM RC88 were incubated in PBS at room temperature (RT, 22-25°C) for 1 h, diluted with deuterated buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate in D₂O, pD 6.6), and labeled on an automated HDX platform (Waters). Aliquots were quenched at 0.5°C with equal volume of quench buffer (100 mM potassium phosphate, 4 M Guanidine HCl, 0.5M TCEP, pH 2.0), then loaded at 40 μl/min onto a Enzymate Protein Pepsin Column (Cat. 186007233, Waters). Peptides were trapped on a C18 VanGuard pre-column (2.1 × 5 mm) and separated on a C18 column (1.0 × 50 mm; both Waters BEH, 1.7 μm) using a 2%–40% acetonitrile gradient (70 μl/min), followed by a 95% wash step; online digestion was at 4°C and separation at 0.5°C.

Mass spectrometric analysis was performed on a Synapt XS QTOF system (Waters) operating in positive ion ESI mode. The mass spectrometer was set to data-independent acquisition (MSE) mode. The source conditions were as follows: capillary voltage, 2.8 kV; sampling cone, 40 V; source temperature, 120°C; desolvation temperature, 250°C; and desolvation gas flow, 800 L/h. The quadrupole profile was set to transmit ions between m/z 400 and 600. MS data were acquired over a mass range of m/z 50–2000 with a scan time of 0.5 s. For MSE collision energy, a low energy of 6 eV was used for precursor ions, and a high-energy ramp from 15 to 45 eV was applied in the transfer cell for fragment ion generation.

Crosslinking mass spectrometry

The buffer of RC88 was changed into digestion buffer (50 mM Tris–HCl, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.6) by desalting column (Cat. HY-Z0330, Thermo) and concentrated to 2 mg/ml. 500U FabCOUPER protease (Cat. QIP-009-A, RhinoBio) was added to 0.5 mg RC88 antibody, and the mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 h. Zero-point one milliliters of Protein A Magnetic beads (Cat. L-1004A, Bio-linkedin) were added, incubated at RT for 30 min. After the separation of magnetic frame, the supernatant that containing fab of RC88 was collected and changed into 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES, pH 7.5) buffer. MSLN (His tagged MSLN 296–580, Cat. 13128-H08H1, SinoBiological) and fab were incubated as 1:1 (w/w) ratio, and the final concentration was >1 mg/ml. Two millimoles of DSS was added, and the mixture was shaken at 37°C for 30 min. The reaction was quenched with 1/10 volume of 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0) for another 30 min.

Each 100 μg protein was reduced with DTT (56°C, 40 min) and alkylated with iodoacetamide (dark, 30 min), then exchanged into digestion buffer. Trypsin/GluC was added to sample as 1:40 (wt/wt) ratio and digested at 37°C for 4 h. The reaction was stopped with 1 μl formic acid, and samples were analyzed using a Thermo Q Exactive Plus mass spectrometer.

Biolayer interferometry assay

Binding kinetics were measured on an Octet HTX system (Sartorius). For MSLN mutants and RC88 binding, RC88 (5 μg/ml) was immobilized on ProA biosensors in Kinetic Buffer (0.002% (v/v) Tween 20, 1 × PBS) at 30°C. The sensor was equilibrated (baseline) for 60 s, before incubating with MSLN at various concentrations (association) for 90 s. The concentrations for MSLN are 25, 50, 100, and 200 nM. The sensor was then transferred into Kinetic Buffer for dissociation. For the blocking of RC88 antibody to CA125, an HIS1K biosensors (Sartorius) was coated with MSLN. The sensor was equilibrated in a Kinetic Buffer for 30 s, before bathing in RC88 or PBS for 250 s. The block was monitored for 160 s after transferring the sensor to the Kinetic Buffer. Data were fitted for a 1:1 stoichiometry for dissociation constant (KD), association rate (Kon), and dissociation rate (Koff) calculations using the built-in software Data Analysis 12.2.

Surface plasmon resonance analysis

Protein binding was measured on a Biacore T200 system. The antibody was immobilized onto a CM5 chip (Cat. BR100530, Cytiva) prepared with anti-Fc capture kit (Cat. BR1008-39, Cytiva). Serial diluted ligands were injected in a multiple-cycle kinetics experiment. The temperature of the analysis is at 25°C, and the injection rate is 30 μl/min. The association and dissociation times were set at 2 min, respectively. Kinetic data were globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir binding model to determine the Kon, Koff, and KD.

Flow cytometry

To map the C-terminal-binding epitope of RC88, HEK293 cells were transiently transfected with plasmids encoding MSLN fragments: MSLN 501–598 (GPI-anchored) or MSLN 488–598, 580–598, and 488–580 (transmembrane-anchored). Four micrograms of plasmid in 250 μl Opti-MEM was mixed with 12 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Cat. 11668027, Thermo Fisher) in 250 μl Opti-MEM and added to cells. After 48 h, cells were trypsinized and incubated with primary antibodies for 1 h at RT, washed, and incubated with anti-Human IgG (H + L) secondary antibody (Cat. A-21445, Thermo Fisher) for 1 h. Cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (C6 Plus, BD Accuri) by allophycocyanin (APC) wavelength. For GPI-anchored MSLN, APC-positive cells within the fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) positive (TGP-expressing) population were analyzed.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

OVCAR3-MSLN cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates. After overnight adhesion, cells were treated with serial dilutions of RC88-ADC in the presence or absence of sMSLN (296–580; 0, 6, 25, 50, or 100 nM) and incubated for 72 h at 37°C, 5% CO₂. Viability was assessed using the CellTiter-Meiluncell 2.0 Luminescent Kit (Cat. PWL111-2, MeilunBio) on a plate reader (VARIOSKAN LUX, Thermo Fisher). IC50 values were determined by four-parameter logistic curve fitting using GraphPad Prism 10. Each concentration was tested in duplicate, and each experiment was independently repeated three times.

Internalization assay

OVCAR3-MSLN cells (1 × 105 per well) were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated overnight. RC88, Anetumab (BAY94-9343), HBM9033, and RG7600 were labeled with pHAb Amine Reactive Dye (Cat. G9831, Promega) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. pHAb-labeled antibodies were added at 50 nM and co-incubated with cells at 37°C for 0.5, 1.5, 3, 5, 7, or 24 h. At each time point, cells were washed with cold PBS to remove unbound antibody, and surface-remaining antibody was detected with APC-conjugated anti-human IgG secondary antibody by flow cytometry (BD Accuri C6 Plus).

Computational structure prediction

The structures of MSLN (residues 296–598) alone and in complex with the RC88 Fab were predicted using Boltz-2. The RC88 Fab heavy- and light-chain sequences were used as binder inputs. Five independent prediction runs were performed per complex using different random seeds. The top-ranked model was selected based on the interface predicted TM-score (ipTM). Model confidence was assessed using per-residue predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) scores; regions with pLDDT <70 were designated as low-confidence.

Animal study

Human ovarian carcinoma xenograft models were established via subcutaneous inoculation of OVCAR3-MSLN cells (~2.5 × 106 cells per mouse) into female Balb/c nude mice that aged ~8 weeks old. When the mean tumor volume reached ~140–150 mm3, the mice were randomly allocated to four treatment groups (n = 6 per group) with matched initial tumor volumes to ensure group homogeneity. Each mouse was administered a single intravenous injection of either the ADC candidate or PBS at a dose of 3 mg/kg. Tumor dimensions and body weights of the mice were measured twice weekly, and tumor volume (mm3) was calculated using the formula: Volume = (Length × Width2)/2. All the animal study procedures and protocols were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of RemeGen.

Results

sMSLN is present in tumor tissues

To establish the clinical relevance of MSLN shedding as a barrier to antibody-based therapies, we first characterized the post-translational processing of MSLN and quantified sMSLN levels in patient samples. This was essential to contextualize the therapeutic hurdles, as previous studies have shown that MSLN shedding varies across tumor types and contributes to heterogeneous antigen presentation, potentially explaining variable responses in clinical trials of anti-MSLN agents [19, 27]. The mature of MSLN undergoes a variety of post-translation modifications. The precursor protein of MSLN (prepro-MSLN) is cleaved on the endoplasmic reticulum by GPI-Transamidase (GPI-T) at the ω-site [28, 29]. The CSP from 599 to 622 is removed and anchored on the cell membrane via GPI (Fig. 1a). The ~71 kDa GPI-anchored form MSLN is then processed by furin in the Golgi, releasing the 31 kDa megakaryocyte potentiating factor (MPF) and retaining the 40 kDa mesothelin on the cell surface [30]. In this study, the terms N- and C-termini refer specifically to the 40 kDa membrane-bound form of MSLN. Mesothelin can also be shed to release sMSLN by the action of tumor necrosis factor-alpha–converting enzyme (TACE/ADAM17), leaving a small truncated protein on the cell surface via GPI [10, 19]. The existence of multiple cleavage sites for diverse sheddases, which vary across tumor cell types, results in a heterogeneous mixture of sMSLN fragments, complicating the therapeutic landscape [10]. This variability in shedding, as reported in ovarian and mesothelioma models, results in diverse soluble fragments that can act as decoys.

Patients’ plasma analysis revealed elevated sMSLN concentrations in ovarian cancer (0.42–7.4 nM) and pleural mesothelioma (0.23–6.78 nM) compared to other malignancies (Fig. 1b). The sMSLN levels in different cancer patients may vary, but they are all higher than those of the healthy individuals at normally below 1 nM [31]. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) with clone 3-2G6, validated to specifically recognize recombinant MSLN 296–580 (Supplementary Fig. 1), confirmed broad MSLN expression across lung adenocarcinoma, ovarian cancer, pleural mesothelioma, and triple-negative breast cancer specimens (Fig. 1c) [32, 33]. While conventional IHC cannot discriminate membrane-bound from intratumoral soluble antigen, the diffuse cytoplasmic staining observed in addition to membrane localization is consistent with published evidence of shed MSLN accumulating in the tumor microenvironment [27]. The functional relevance of circulating sMSLN as a decoy antigen is directly supported by plasma quantification data (Fig. 1b) and others [34].

RC88, evaluated in a phase I/II trial (NCT04175847) for advanced solid tumors [26, 35, 36], comprises a humanized IgG1 anti-mesothelin antibody conjugated via a valine–citrulline (VCit) dipeptide linker to monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE) (Fig. 1d). This investigation was motivated by the need to address gaps in existing anti-MSLN ADCs, such as Anetumab ravtansine, where sMSLN interference contributed to modest responses [23]. At 2024 ASCO, RC88 was reported to achieve objective response rates (ORRs) of 37.2% (16/43) in ovarian cancer, 21.7% (5/23) in nonsquamous non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), and 35.3% (6/17) in cervical cancer, prompting further mechanistic characterization.

Conformational binding of RC88: strong N-term and weak C-term affinities

To characterize the epitope of RC88, we employed integrated structural methods. Given the importance of the N-terminal domain for neutralizing MSLN-mediated tumor progression, hydrogen–deuterium exchange mass spectrometry (HDX-MS) and crosslinking mass spectrometry (XL-MS) were used to map the binding interface, while biolayer interferometry (BLI) and surface plasmon resonance (SPR) quantified binding kinetics and affinity [37–39].

HDX-MS revealed a significant reduction in deuterium exchange within regions 315–323, 337–349, and 378–383 of MSLN upon RC88 binding, indicating a protected primary binding interface (Fig. 2a–c, Supplementary Fig. 2A). Region 315–323 is rich in aromatic residues, including F317, likely contributing to hydrophobic stability. Protection of region 337–349, encompassing residues critical for CA125 binding, underscores its functional importance [40, 41]. Furthermore, the decreased exchange in the flexible loop region 378–383 suggests that RC88 binding may induce local conformational rigidity, thereby enhancing binding specificity and stability. XL-MS analysis identified an interprotein crosslinked peptide corresponding to MSLN residues 309–320 (EIDESLIFYKK) (Fig. 2d, Supplementary Fig. 2A). When interpreted together with the HDX-MS protection data and structural modeling, the combined results indicated that the RC88 epitope located on three N-terminal regions of MSLN: residues 309–323, 337–349, and 374–383. To further resolve the critical contact residues, we performed alanine scanning mutagenesis across these regions, which pinpointed F317, F344, T345, and Q348 as key determinants for RC88 binding (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Fig. 3). Among these, F317 and F344 are likely involved in forming π-π stacking or hydrophobic pockets, while T345 and Q348 may stabilize the interface through hydrogen bonds. Collectively, these structural and biophysical data demonstrate RC88 engages a high-affinity N-terminal epitope overlapping the CA125 interaction domain. Consistent with this, competitive enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) showed RC88 inhibited CA125 binding to MSLN with an IC50 of 0.82 nM (Supplementary Fig. 4A), and BLI assays confirmed that RC88 pre-treatment blocked subsequent CA125 binding by 87.4% (Supplementary Fig. 4B), providing a structural rationale for its efficacy.

SPR analysis revealed RC88 binds with distinct affinities depending on MSLN fragments: an overall KD of 12.1 nM for MSLN 296–572, with high affinity (2.3 nM) for the N-terminus (296–430) and low affinity (639 nM) for the C-terminus (418–592) (Fig. 2f–h, Supplementary Fig. 2A). The rapid association rate (3.23 × 106 1/Ms) for the N-terminus suggests efficient initial capture, while the weaker C-terminal interaction may provide stabilization. To further establish the C-terminal binding, we performed ELISA studies using the synthetic MSLN KLH-577-598 peptide (Supplementary Fig. 5). RC88 bound the peptide with an EC50 of 1.3 μg/ml, compared to 0.4 μg/ml for the full ectodomain, suggesting that this minimal juxtamembrane segment is sufficient for recognition, though its disordered nature as a free linear peptide contributes to a weaker apparent affinity.

Biphasic binding of an antibody to its antigen is consistent with antigen complexity. Our results indicate that RC88 engages a dominant high-affinity N-terminal epitope and a lower-affinity C-terminal epitope on MSLN, suggesting its binding is conformational and may explain its superior tumor engagement.

RC88 exhibits enhanced cellular binding to C-terminal MSLN

To validate the functional relevance of C-terminal binding at the cellular level, we evaluated the binding of RC88 to MSLN-expressing cells, aiming to correlate the epitope with tumor-targeting efficiency, as cellular assays have predicted clinical performance in prior ADC studies [42, 43]. On MSLN-positive NCI-H226 cells, which predominantly express the 296–598 of MSLN, RC88, BAY94–9343, HBM9033, and RG7600 showed comparable binding (Fig. 3a). This is consistent with the shared recognition of the accessible N-terminal epitope present in the intact antigen. In contrast, RC88 demonstrates superior cellular binding in cells expressing the C-terminal MSLN (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 2A). RC88 exhibited a significant shift compared to all other antibodies, highlighting its unique ability to engage the membrane-proximal region. To mimic endogenous anchoring, we expressed MSLN 501–622 in HEK293 cells, where it is GPI-anchored as MSLN 501–598 (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) confirmed the specific binding of RC88 to these cells, showing a clear shift compared with the IgG1 control. This enhanced binding, particularly toward C-terminal truncations, suggests that the conformational epitope recognized by RC88 promotes more effective tethering to tumor cells.

RC88 engages specific juxtamembrane binding in the C-terminal of cellular MSLN

As sMSLN compromises ADC efficacy, antibodies with membrane-bound preference can help overcome this [10, 25]. We used cell-surface display to investigate C-terminal binding. HEK293 cells were transfected with truncated MSLN fragments (488–598, 580–598, and 488–580) fused to the PDGFRβ transmembrane domain (Fig. 4a, Supplementary Fig. 2A and C). FACS analysis revealed that RC88 selectively bound to cells expressing the 580–598 fragment but not to 488–580, localizing the epitope to residues 580–598. Alanine scanning mutagenesis was performed across the juxtamembrane region of MSLN 488–598 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Compared with wild type, the mutants L589A, L591A, and M593A exhibited the most pronounced reduction in binding, identifying these as critical C-terminal-binding interface residues (Fig. 4b).

Using Boltz-2 [44, 45], we predicted the structures of MSLN alone and in complex with the RC88 Fab. The top-ranked model (confidence score = 0.8506, complex pLDDT = 0.8789) was selected for structural analysis. The C-terminal-binding region of MSLN exhibits high structural flexibility and low per-residue pLDDT scores, consistent with its disordered nature (Fig. 4c). Structural alignment revealed that upon binding to RC88, the juxtamembrane helix of MSLN undergoes displacement with an root mean square deviation (RMSD) of 2.64 Å (Supplementary Fig. 7). Given the reported flexibility of the MSLN C-terminus, we propose that conformational changes mediate this binding [41, 46, 47]. This suggests that RC88 may act as a conformational effector, ultimately leading to structural rearrangements in MSLN. This induced-fit mechanism, akin to conformational changes observed in other anti-MSLN antibodies, likely exposes cryptic epitopes, facilitating stable membrane anchoring and differentiating RC88 from antibodies like 15B6, which target proximal regions but without induction [10].

RC88 maintains cytotoxic activity against sMSLN interference and suppresses tumor growth in vivo

To comprehensively evaluate the pharmacological properties of RC88, we performed internalization, in vitro cytotoxicity, and in vivo efficacy studies.

Internalization kinetics were compared for RC88, Anetumab, HBM9033, and RG7600 on OVCAR3-MSLN cells (Fig. 5a). All four antibodies demonstrated progressive, time-dependent internalization with comparable kinetics. This shows the conformational epitope binding mode does not impair internalization. Given RC88, Anetumab, and RG7600 all use the same vc-PABC-MMAE payload with comparable DAR (~4), observed efficacy differences are primarily attributable to target engagement rather than payload properties. In competitive cell-binding assays with sMSLN, RC88 showed stronger resistance than Anetumab and RG7600 in OVCAR3-MSLN cells (Fig. 5b) and NCI-H226 cells (Fig. 5c). The superior binding retention of RC88 under these conditions directly correlates with its unique conformational epitope, which engages a membrane-proximal C-terminal region less susceptible to blockade by shed antigen. RC88-ADC demonstrated potent in vitro cytotoxicity in OVCAR3-MSLN cells with an IC50 of 0.23 nM (Fig. 5d). To directly test whether sMSLN-resistant binding translates into maintained cytotoxic potency, we performed assays with increasing sMSLN concentrations (Fig. 5e). At 6 and 25 nM sMSLN, within patient plasma ranges, IC50 values were 0.20 nM, showing no meaningful attenuation. At 50 nM sMSLN, the IC50 increased modestly to 0.35 nM (1.5-fold). Even at 100 nM sMSLN, the IC50 was 0.65 nM (2.8-fold shift). These data directly link RC88’s sMSLN-resistant binding to preserved therapeutic efficacy under physiologically relevant conditions.

To translate this cellular advantage into in vivo therapeutic efficacy, we evaluated RC88 in Balb/c nude mice bearing OVCAR3-MSLN xenografts (Fig. 5f). A single 3 mg/kg dose of RC88 produced significant tumor growth suppression compared to PBS and superior inhibition compared to RG7600 (P < .05). These results underscore the clinical potential of RC88 as a mechanistically differentiated MSLN-targeting ADC [6, 48, 49]. This unique binding property establishes RC88 as an ADC capable of overcoming the validated resistance mechanism of soluble antigen interference.

Discussion

The development of MSLN-targeted therapies is challenged by sMSLN shedding as a decoy antigen and the difficulty of selecting epitopes that maintain high-affinity engagement in antigen-rich environments. This study provides a mechanistic explanation for the favorable clinical performance of RC88 by demonstrating that its conformational epitope binding strategy confers two complementary advantages: high-affinity N-terminal engagement with functional blockade of the pro-metastatic MSLN-CA125 interaction and auxiliary membrane-proximal C-terminal anchoring that mitigates sMSLN interference. We emphasize that C-terminal engagement does not imply universal superiority over other MSLN-targeting agents; efficacy may be governed by different payloads, internalization routes, or epitopes. Rather, the specific advantage of C-terminal binding in RC88 demonstrated here is resistance to sMSLN-mediated competitive displacement.

A central finding is that the primary RC88 epitope directly overlaps the CA125 interaction domain. RC88 blocked CA125 binding with an IC50 of 0.82 nM and 87.4% efficiency. This has implications beyond direct tumor cell killing; disruption of the MSLN-CA125 axis, a driver of peritoneal dissemination, may exert an antimetastatic effect independent of ADC cytotoxicity. This potential dual mechanism, cytotoxic payload delivery, and inhibition of peritoneal metastasis via CA125 blockade, warrants dedicated investigation in preclinical metastasis models.

It is important to acknowledge the methodological asymmetry in evidence for the two binding interfaces. The N-terminal epitope is supported by three orthogonal structural methods (HDX-MS, XL-MS, and alanine-scanning BLI). The C-terminal interaction, by contrast, was not detected by these methods. This is mechanistically expected: the C-terminal region is inherently flexible and disordered, reflected by low pLDDT scores and corroborated by crystallographic studies. The KD for C-terminal binding (639 nM) is over 270-fold weaker than the N-terminal interaction (2.3 nM). HDX-MS requires stably protected amide hydrogens, and DSS-based XL-MS requires sustained lysine proximity, conditions not met by transient, low-affinity interactions. C-terminal evidence therefore relies on functional cell-based assays and direct peptide ELISA, methods better suited for detecting weak or transient interactions. We consider this asymmetry a reflection of the biophysical reality of a conformational binding mechanism.

The approach of RC88 to sMSLN resistance can be contrasted with those of other MSLN-targeting modalities. In the CAR-T cell field, Liu et al. demonstrated that antibody 15B6, which binds exclusively the juxtamembrane region of MSLN, can confer sMSLN resistance by bypassing the shed epitope entirely [10]. RC88 achieves resistance through a different mechanism, its primary interaction is with the N-terminal region, while C-terminal engagement provides an auxiliary stabilizing anchor that membrane-bound MSLN retains but shed MSLN does not. This integrated approach may provide advantages in tumor microenvironments with heterogeneous MSLN-shedding profiles, where high-affinity N-terminal capture ensures efficient target engagement at low antigen density, and C-terminal anchoring provides stability under high sMSLN competition. In the immunotoxin field, SS1P was significantly impaired by sMSLN [21], underscoring the broad clinical relevance of the sMSLN resistance mechanism embodied by RC88.

Internalization assays demonstrated the conformational epitope-binding mode does not impair internalization efficiency compared to comparators. Since RC88, Anetumab, and RG7600 all employ the vc-PABC-MMAE payload with comparable DAR (~4), differences in efficacy are primarily attributable to target engagement differences. Comprehensive characterization of intracellular trafficking, including endosomal/lysosomal routing and cathepsin B–mediated linker cleavage, represents an important future direction for understanding how epitope engagement influences ADC processing.

Several limitations merit explicit acknowledgment. First, the structural model of C-terminal conformational rearrangement is based on computational predictions; cryo-EM or X-ray crystallography is needed to confirm the induced-fit mechanism. Second, the in vivo evaluation employed a single OVCAR3-MSLN xenograft model; prior publications have demonstrated consistent RC88 efficacy across multiple MSLN-expressing tumor types [35, 36], but formal multi-model evaluation within a single comparative study would strengthen generalizability. Third, the association between RC88’s sMSLN-resistant binding and its observed clinical ORRs is correlative; prospective biomarker studies correlating pre-treatment sMSLN levels with clinical outcomes are needed.

RC88 employs a conformational epitope binding strategy combining high-affinity N-terminal CA125 site engagement with low-affinity membrane proximal C-terminal anchoring. This binding mode confers remarkable resistance to sMSLN interference, maintains nanomolar cytotoxic potency across physiologically relevant sMSLN concentrations, and provides a structural rationale for the encouraging clinical responses observed in MSLN-positive tumors. These findings illustrate how rational target engagement design can yield antibody therapeutics with refined pharmacological properties suited to challenging clinical contexts and suggest that conformational epitope strategies may be broadly applicable to other targets subjected to antigen shedding.
