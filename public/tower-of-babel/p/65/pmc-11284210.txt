Naringenin ameliorates amyloid-β pathology and neuroinflammation in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Yueli Zhu, Xiaoming Guo, Shumin Li, Yue Wu, Feng Zhu, Chengfan Qin, Qin Zhang, Yunmei Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia characterized by amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, and neuroinflammation. Naringenin (NRG), a natural flavonoid widely present in citrus fruits, has been reported can penetrate the blood-brain barrier and exert anti-inflammatory effects in the central nervous system. Here, we investigate the protective effects of long-term NRG treatment on AD. The novel object recognition test and Morris water maze test reveal that NRG treatment can improve the learning and memory ability of APP/PS1 mice. Besides, we find that NRG can significantly reduce Aβ deposition, microglial and astrocytic activation, and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in APP/PS1 mice. Results further show that NRG effectively decreases pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells. Lastly, the molecular mechanistic study reveals that NRG attenuates neuroinflammatory responses via inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway in vivo and in vitro. Overall, NRG may emerge as a promising compound for the prevention and treatment of AD.Subject terms: Alzheimer's disease, Alzheimer's disease

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disorder presenting with progressive cognitive decline and behavioral impairment. The hallmark histopathological features of AD are characterized by the extracellular deposition of amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein1–3. In recent decades, extensive studies have been conducted on AD, but its pathogenesis still remains unclear and there is currently no proven effective treatment, which prompts researchers to focus on other aspects beyond Aβ and tau4,5. As we know, neuroinflammation is regarded as an important player in the pathophysiology of AD6–8. Some epidemiological evidence have shown that anti-inflammatory treatment can reduce the risk of AD9–11. And neuroinflammation is thought to be driven mainly by microglia and astrocytes in AD, which can produce and secrete pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-1β (IL-1β)12.

Naringenin (NRG) is a natural flavonoid compound with many biological activities and is widely found in citrus fruits13. In addition to anti-oxidative, anti-inflammatory, anti-viral, anti-tumor, and heart-protective properties, NRG can also exert neuroprotective effects14. Besides, it has been proven that NRG can penetrate the blood-brain barrier, suggesting the significant potential of NRG in the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases15,16.

Recently, some in vitro and in vivo experiments have explored the potential neuroprotective role of NRG in AD. NRG can effectively inhibit Aβ25-35-caused nerve damage in PC12 cells by modulating the ER and PI3K/Akt pathways17. Ghofrani et al. proposed that NRG pretreatment can attenuate Aβ1-40-induced impairment of learning and memory in rats through mitigation of lipid peroxidation and apoptosis18. Yang et al. found that NRG can improve cognitive function and reverse tau hyperphosphorylation in the streptozotocin-induced AD rats through downregulating the expression of glycogen synthase kinase-3β19. Besides, the potent anti-inflammatory effects of NRG have been found in the central nervous system. NRG had the ability to attenuate lipopolysaccharides (LPS)/IFN-γ-induced TNF-α and NO production, as well as inhibit iNOS expression in glial cells20. In addition, NRG can produce neuroprotection and reduce the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS-induced Parkinson’s disease rat model via suppressing the microglial NLRP3 inflammasome activation21. However, whether long-term treatment with NRG can attenuate neuroinflammation and exert neuroprotective effects in familial AD still remains unknown.

Thus, in the present study, we mainly investigated the effects of long-term NRG treatment on cognitive function, Aβ accumulation, and neuroinflammatory responses, as well as underlying anti-inflammatory mechanisms in APPswe/PSEN1dE9 (APP/PS1) transgenic mice and LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells.

Results

NRG attenuated learning and memory deficits in APP/PS1 mice

The timeline for NRG treatment and the behavioral tests are indicated in Fig. 1a. To investigate whether NRG treatment can ameliorate cognitive deficits in APP/PS1 mice, we conducted novel object recognition test (NORT) and Morris water maze (MWM) test to assess the learning and memory ability of these mice. In the NORT, APP/PS1 mice exhibited significantly lower recognition index (RI) compared with wild‐type (WT) mice, while NRG treatment significantly reversed these changes (Fig. 1b). After that, the mice were subjected to MWM test for consecutive 5 days to evaluate their spatial learning and memory. During the acquisition phase, APP/PS1 mice had a notably longer escape latency than WT mice, whereas the escape latency in APP/PS1 + NRG group was significantly decreased on day 2 and day 3 (Fig. 1c). During the probe trial on day 5, the obviously decreased frequency of crossing the former platform occurred in APP/PS1 mice when compared with WT mice. And the number of platform crossings increased in APP/PS1 mice treated with NRG, although the difference was not significant (Fig. 1d, e). Overall, these results indicated that NRG can mitigate cognitive impairment in APP/PS1 mice.

All data are presented as the mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was performed. NRG reduced Aβ deposition in APP/PS1 mice

To determine whether NRG can reduce Aβ deposition in the brains of APP/PS1 mice, we performed immunohistochemistry staining and enzyme linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA). Compared with APP/PS1 mice, less Aβ plaques were observed in the hippocampus and cortex of the mice in APP/PS1 + NRG group using immunohistochemistry staining with antibody 6E10 (Fig. 2a, b). Moreover, the levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 were measured in the hippocampal tissues using ELISA. The results revealed that NRG treatment can markedly decrease the levels of soluble Aβ42 but not Aβ40 in APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 2c).

Scale bar = 200 μm. b The quantitative results of the percentage of Aβ plaque area in the hippocampus and cerebral cortex. n = 4–5/group. c Levels of soluble Aβ42 and Aβ40 in the hippocampus measured using ELISA. n = 4/group. d Representative Western blots and densitometry analysis of APP and BACE1 in the hippocampus. n = 4/group. All data are presented as the mean ± SD. The t test was performed to examine the differences between two groups.

Aβ is a proteolytic product of amyloid protein precursor (APP) and thus we used Western blot to examine the expression of APP in the hippocampal tissues of APP/PS1 mice. The result revealed that there was no significant difference in APP expression between the APP/PS1 and APP/PS1 + NRG groups (Fig. 2d). In the amyloidogenic pathway, beta-secretase 1 (BACE1) cleaves APP and leads to Aβ peptides. Therefore, we detected BACE1 by Western blot, and it showed that NRG treatment markedly down-regulated the expression of BACE1 in the hippocampal tissues of APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 2d). Thus, NRG can reduce Aβ deposition in the brains of APP/PS1 mice, which may be related to the suppression of β-secretase activity.

NRG inhibited reactive gliosis and decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in APP/PS1 mice

The presence of activated microglia and astrocytes surrounding Aβ plaques is a typical feature in AD brains. And these activated glial cells can mediate neuroinflammation via producing pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α and IL-1β. To investigate the effects of NRG on neuroinflammation in APP/PS1 mice, we conducted immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blot to assess the expressions of Iba-1 and GFAP. The results revealed that Iba-1 and GFAP-positive cells were markedly increased in the hippocampus and cortex of APP/PS1 mice compared with WT mice, whereas NRG treatment obviously reduced the number of microglia and astrocytes (Fig. 3 and Fig. 4a, b). What’s more, double immunofluorescence staining showed that Aβ plaques were surrounded by reactive Iba‐1‐positive microglia and GFAP-positive astrocytes in the brains of APP/PS1 mice, whereas NRG treatment decreased the number of activated microglia and astrocytes around less Aβ plaques (Fig. 4c, d).

Next, we investigated whether NRG decreased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice. The real-time fluorescence quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) results revealed that the mRNA levels of Tnf-α and Il‐1β were remarkably upregulated in APP/PS1 mice compared with WT mice, while NRG treatment significantly reduced the expression level of Il‐1β mRNA (Fig. 5a, b). We further performed ELISA to confirm the above results and it showed that NRG can significantly decrease the protein levels of TNF-α and IL‐1β in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice (Fig. 5c, d). Taken together, NRG can effectively inhibit reactive gliosis and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines in APP/PS1 mice.

NRG inhibited the activation of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling pathway in APP/PS1 mice

It is well known that the activation of MAPK signaling pathway has been widely involved in the regulation of neuroinflammation22,23. To investigate whether NRG can ameliorate neuroinflammatory responses by inhibiting MAPK signaling pathway, the related proteins were determined by Western blot. Our results revealed that in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, p-p38 and p-JNK were markedly increased compared with the WT mice (Fig. 6), suggesting that the MAPK signaling pathway was activated. By contrast, NRG treatment can obviously decrease the expression of p-p38 and p-JNK (Fig. 6). Therefore, our findings indicated that NRG can inhibit the activation of MAPK signaling pathway in APP/PS1 mice.

NRG (50 mg/kg/d) inhibited the activation of MAPK signaling pathway in APP/PS1 mice.

All data are presented as the mean ± SD. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was performed.

NRG reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS/Aβ‐stimulated BV2 cells

To further investigate the role of NRG in microglia-mediated inflammatory responses, BV2 cells were employed and exposed to LPS and Aβ1-42. First, the CCK-8 assay results showed that NRG (1–100 μM) had no significant cytotoxicity (Fig. 7a). We further examined the potential regulatory effects of NRG on pro-inflammatory cytokines in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells. The qPCR results showed that NRG markedly inhibited LPS/Aβ-stimulated mRNA expression of Tnf-α, Il-1β, iNos, and Cox-2 in BV2 cells, especially at the concentration of 100 μM (Fig. 7b). Besides, ELISA and Western blot results revealed that 100 μM NRG can significantly decrease the protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS, and COX-2 in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells (Fig. 7c, d). In summary, these findings indicated that NRG can exert anti-inflammatory effects in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells by reducing the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Besides, the 100 μM concentration of NRG was selected in further experiments.

One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was performed.

NRG inhibited the activation of MAPK signaling pathway in LPS/Aβ‐stimulated BV2 cells

In order to further investigate the potential mechanism underlying the regulation of microglial activation by NRG, the expression levels of MAPK pathway-related proteins were identified by Western blot. It showed that NRG can significantly suppress the expression of p-p38, p-JNK, and p-ERK1/2 proteins in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells (Fig. 8).

NRG and selective MAPK inhibitors decreased the mRNA levels of Il-1β and iNos in LPS/Aβ‐stimulated BV2 cells

To further clarify the molecular mechanism of NRG in affecting the inflammatory responses, BV2 cells were cultured in the presence or absence of 100 μM NRG, 10 μM SB203580 (p38 MAPK inhibitor), 10 μM SP600125 (JNK inhibitor), and 10 μM U0126-EtOH (ERK1/2 inhibitor) for 1 h, followed by LPS and Aβ1–42 for another 24 h. As shown in Fig. 9, we found NRG and the three selective MAPK inhibitors can all significantly down-regulate the expression of Il-1β and iNos mRNA in LPS/Aβ‐stimulated BV2 cells. And it further revealed that NRG can attenuate neuroinflammatory responses via inhibition of the MAPK signaling pathway.

Discussion

In the present study, our findings demonstrated that long-term treatment with NRG can ameliorate cognitive impairment, Aβ accumulation, and neuroinflammation in APP/PS1 mice. Subsequent mechanistic investigation revealed that NRG reduced the neuroinflammatory responses by inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway, as the phosphorylation levels of MAPK pathway-related proteins were significantly decreased in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice. What’s more, the results suggested that NRG reduced the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines via suppressing the MAPK signaling pathway in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells.

AD is the most prevalent type of dementia in the elderly. The two pathological hallmarks of AD include senile plaques composed of Aβ and NFTs consisting of hyperphosphorylated tau. As we know, APP is cleaved by β-secretase into two fragments in the amyloidogenic pathway: secreted APPβ (sAPPβ) and C‐terminal fragment β (CTFβ), which is further processed by γ-secretase to generate and release the pathological Aβ with a length ranging from 36 to 43 amino acids and can aggregate to form senile plaques24. And Aβ40 and Aβ42 are the two predominant forms of Aβ in the brains. Interestingly, we found NRG can markedly reduce Aβ deposition in the brains of APP/PS1 mice. Further, the levels of soluble Aβ42 were significantly decreased in hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice after NRG treatment. Several reasons may explain this phenomenon. As we know, the only difference between Aβ42 and Aβ40 is that the former has two extra residues at the C-terminus, resulting in the fact that Aβ42 is more prone to aggregating and toxicity than Aβ40. Besides, Aβ42 is more abundant within plaques than Aβ40, with some plaques containing only Aβ4225,26. BACE1 is the major β-secretase and it has been reported that the expression of BACE1 was significantly increased in the brains of AD patients27–29. Our results have shown that long-term NRG treatment decreased the expression of BACE1 in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, indicating that the beneficial effects of NRG treatment on reducing Aβ deposition may be involved in inhibiting BACE1 activity.

Alongside the Aβ accumulation, neuroinflammation is thought to play a critical role in the disease progression and is even considered to be the third core pathological characteristic of AD30. And the neuroinflammatory process in AD is mainly driven by activated microglia and astrocytes, which are the innate immune cells in the brain31. Neuroinflammation can result in both beneficial and detrimental effects, which depends on the severity of the disease32,33. The activation of microglia and astrocytes can contribute to Aβ clearance at the early stage of AD. However, as the disease progresses, persistent activated microglia and astrocytes may elicit detrimental effects by producing excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines, especially TNF-α and IL-1β, and impairing Aβ clearance7. In this study, we examined whether long-term treatment with NRG can inhibit the activation of glial cells and alleviate neuroinflammation in vivo, which still remains to be explored. Our results showed that activated microglia and astrocytes were obviously reduced in the brains of APP/PS1 mice treated with NRG. Besides, fewer microglia and astrocytes were found around less Aβ plaques. Furthermore, we found that NRG can significantly reduce the release of TNF-α and IL‐1β. Based on these results, we concluded that long-term NRG treatment had protective effects through reducing microgliosis and astrogliosis, as wells as decreasing pro-inflammatory cytokines in the brains of APP/PS1 mice. Similar results were obtained from in vitro experiments. It revealed that NRG can obviously suppress the mRNA and protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, iNOS, and COX-2 in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells.

MAPKs are serine-threonine protein kinases that regulate a diverse range of cellular processes involving proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, inflammation, and innate immunity34,35. Previous studies have reported that the activation of MAPK signaling pathway can impact the onset and progression of AD by many ways, including promoting neuroinflammatory responses and increasing the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines36. Many years ago, the activation of p38 MAPK and JNK signaling pathways was observed in the brains of AD patients and was considered an important factor in the pathological changes of AD37–41. It has been reported that inhibiting p38 and JNK pathways can reduce the expression levels of Iba-1 and GFAP in the brains of Aβ1-42-induced mouse model of AD, suggesting the effects of p38 and JNK pathways on inflammatory responses42. Thus in the present study, we conducted Western blot to determine whether NRG can affect MAPK signaling pathway both in vivo and in vitro. We found that in the hippocampus of APP/PS1 mice, the expressions of p-p38 and p-JNK were significantly increased. In contrast, NRG can effectively suppress the expressions of p-p38 and p-JNK. Besides, as expected, it revealed that NRG can significantly inhibit the expressions of p-p38, p-JNK, and p-ERK1/2 in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells. And the mechanisms underlying the anti-neuroinflammation effects of NRG may involve the inhibition of MAPK signaling pathway. Additionally, NRG, selective p38 MAPK inhibitor (SB203580), selective JNK inhibitor (SP600125), and selective ERK1/2 inhibitor (U0126-EtOH) can all decrease the mRNA levels of Il-1β and iNos in LPS/Aβ-stimulated BV2 cells. Taken together, these findings demonstrated that NRG can ameliorate neuroinflammation via inhibition of MAPK signaling pathway and provide an important basis for the neuroprotective effects of NRG on AD.

The present study had several limitations. First, we used APP/PS1 transgenic mice which is a widely applied animal model of AD; however, this animal model may not fully imitate the pathological changes of this disease. Therefore, other AD animal models should be assessed to further confirm the effects of NRG. Second, we used the dose of 50 mg/kg/d by referring to published literature. We believe using different concentrations of NRG in APP/PS1 mice will provide more convincing evidence. Third, further investigation of the mechanisms underlying the neuroprotective effects of NRG on AD is warranted.

Methods

Animals and drug treatment

Male APP/PS1 transgenic mice on a C57BL/6 background were purchased from Changzhou Cavens Lab Animal Co. Ltd. APP/PS1 males were bred with WT females with the same genetic background. Their offspring were genotyped by performing PCR using DNA extracted from toe tissues. And the specific primer sequences were as follows: APP (forward, 5′-GAC TGA CCA CTC GAC CAG GTT CTG-3′; reverse, 5′-CTT GTA AGT TGG ATT CTC ATA TCC G-3′)43; PS1 (forward, 5′-AAT AGA GAA CGG CAG GAG CA-3′; reverse, 5′-GCC ATG AGG GCA CTA ATC AT-3′)43. After genotyping, four-month-old male APP/PS1 mice were randomly divided into APP/PS1 group (n = 9) and NRG-treated APP/PS1 group (APP/PS1 + NRG group, n = 8). Age-matched WT male littermates were used as the control group (WT group, n = 10). Mice were group-housed in filtered cages in a temperature-controlled room with a 12-h light/dark cycle and received food and water ad libitum. We have complied with all relevant ethical regulations for animal use, and all experiments were approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. 2022-1622).

NRG (98% purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Saint Louis, USA). And it was suspended in 0.5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC-Na). Mice in APP/PS1 + NRG group were intragastrically administrated with NRG at a dose of 50 mg/kg once a day for 3 months, while mice in the APP/PS1 and WT groups were treated with the same volume of 0.5% CMC-Na each day for 3 months.

NORT

The NORT was carried out to assess recognition memory in mice which relied on the innate preference of rodents to explore novelty. And the NORT was performed for five consecutive days in our study. For consecutive four days, the mice were allowed to individually habituate to an empty box for 10 min. At the fifth day, two identical objects were put on the two opposite sides of the box and mice were gently placed into the box to explore the objects for 5 min. Then, the mice were returned to the initial cage. After 10 min, one of the objects was changed to a novel one, with different color and shape. And the cumulative time spent exploring each object was recorded for 5 min. Between every trial, the box and objects were cleaned with 75% ethanol. The RI was calculated as the percentage of time spent exploring the novel object over the total time spent exploring two objects.

MWM test

After the NORT, the mice were subjected to MWM test for five consecutive days to investigate the spatial learning and reference memory ability. In brief, the test was performed in a circular pool (diameter 120 cm, height 50 cm) filled with opaque water at a temperature of 22 ± 1 °C. And the pool area was divided into four quadrants with a circular platform submerged 1 cm under the water surface. All mice were given four training sessions per day for four consecutive days. For each daily trial, mice were randomly placed at one of the four starting locations within the pool and were allowed 60 s to find the hidden platform. If the mice failed to find the platform within 60 s, the experimenter can manually lead them to the platform to remain for 15 s. The escape latency in each trial, defined as the time required to find the hidden platform, was collected to analyze the performance of these mice. On day 5, the platform was removed, and the mice were placed in the pool on the opposite side of the platform and were allowed to swim freely for 60 s. The number of crossing the platform was recorded.

Brain tissue collection

After completing the behavioral tests, the mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 0.9% saline. The brains were removed immediately and divided into left and right hemispheres. The left hemispheres were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 4 °C for the purposes of immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence. As for immunofluorescence, the tissues were further transferred to 30% sucrose solution until they sank to the bottoms of the centrifuge tubes, followed by being embedded in optimum cutting temperature compound and cut into frozen serial coronal sections at 30 µm. The right hemispheres were carefully dissected into cerebral cortices and hippocampi, which were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until assaying.

Preparation of oligomeric Aβ1–42, LPS, and NRG

Lyophilized Aβ1-42 powder was purchased from ChinaPeptides Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and firstly dissolved in cold hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP), and the solution obtained was evaporated to dryness to remove HFIP. Next, the peptide was resuspended in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain 5 mM concentration and then diluted with sterile phosphate buffer saline (PBS) to 100 μM concentration and incubated at 4 °C for 24 h to prepare Aβ oligomers, which were further centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 rpm. The final solution was allowed to incubate at 37 °C for 16 h before use. LPS (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) was dissolved in sterile PBS at 1 mg/mL and further diluted to a final concentration of 1 μg/mL with DMEM. NRG was dissolved in DMSO and further diluted in DMEM to the required concentrations.

Cell culture and treatment

The murine BV2 microglia cell line44 was obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China) and incubated in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. BV2 cells were presented in the presence or absence of NRG (50 and 100 μM) for 1 h followed by Aβ1-42 (10 μM) and LPS (1 μg/mL) treatment for an additional 24 h. Finally, the cells and culture medium were collected for detecting subsequent indices.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

For immunohistochemistry staining, the paraffin‐embedded sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min and then blocked for nonspecific antigen binding using immunostaining blocking solution (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) at room temperature for 15 min. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies against 6E10 (1:500; #803004; BioLegend, San Diego, USA), Iba-1 (1:500; #019-19741; Wako, Osaka, Japan), and GFAP (1:1000; #AB5541; Merck Millipore, Billerica, USA) at 4 °C overnight and then 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, sections were incubated for 1 h with appropriate biotinylated secondary antibodies at 37 °C. At last, the staining was developed using a DAB kit (Abcam, Cambridge, USA). For immunofluorescence staining, the frozen sections were permeabilized by 0.3% Triton X-100 for 20 min at room temperature, followed by being incubated in the blocking solution. Afterwards, sections were incubated with primary antibodies against 6E10 (1:500; #803004; BioLegend, San Diego, USA), Iba-1(1:500; #019-19741; Wako, Osaka, Japan), and GFAP (1:1000; #AB5541; Merck Millipore, Billerica, USA) at 4 °C overnight and then 37 °C for 1 h. After washing, sections were incubated in fluorescent-conjugated secondary antibodies at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained by incubation in DAPI solution for 10 min. The slices were subsequently examined using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Carl Zeiss AG, Oberkochen, Germany). For quantification, the percentage of positive staining area was measured by Image J software.

ELISA

The hippocampal tissues were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors, and then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The protein concentration was measured using BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and adjusted to a concentration of 5 mg/mL.

The concentrations of soluble Aβ40 and Aβ42 were measured using ELISA kits (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, USA)45 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Besides, TNF-α and IL-1β ELISA kits were purchased from Proteintech Group (Wuhan, China)46. Absorbance was measured using a microplate absorbance reader (Bio Tek, Vermont, USA).

qPCR

Total mRNA was isolated and extracted from brains and BV2 cells using Trizol (Takara, Tokoyo, Japan). Reverse transcription was performed with HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and real-time PCR analysis was conducted using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The PCR primer sequences were as follows: mouse Tnf-α (forward, 5′-CTG AAC TTC GGG GTG ATC GG-3′; reverse, 5′-GGC TTG TCA CTC GAA TTT TGA GA-3′)47; mouse Il-1β (forward, 5′-GCA ACT GTT CCT GAA CTC AAC T-3′; reverse, 5′-ATC TTT TGG GGT CCG TCA ACT-3′)48; mouse iNos (forward, 5′-GTT CTC AGC CCA ACA ATA CAA GA-3′; reverse, 5′-GTG GAC GGG TCG ATG TCA C-3′)48; mouse Cox-2 (forward, 5′-TTC CAA TCC ATG TCA AAA CCG T-3′; reverse, 5′-AGT CCG GGT ACA GTC ACA CTT-3′)49; mouse Gapdh (forward, 5′-AGG TCG GTG TGA ACG GAT TTG-3′; reverse, 5′-TGT AGA CCA TGT AGT TGA GGT CA-3′)50.

The relative gene expression was normalized to the mean expression level of GAPDH which was used as the endogenous control. And the relative levels of mRNA were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Western blot

The protein samples were heated at 100 °C for 10 min in loading buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China). Then, equal amounts of proteins (20 μg per lane) were loaded onto 4–20% SDS‐PAGE gels. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Merck Millipore, Billerica, USA), followed by being incubated with protein free rapid blocking buffer (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) for 15 min. Subsequently, the protein bands were incubated at 4 °C overnight with corresponding primary antibodies against 6E10 (1:1000; #803004; BioLegend, San Diego, USA)51, BACE1 (1:1000; #5606; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52, Iba-1 (1:1000, #ab178847; Abcam, Cambridge, USA)53, GFAP (1:1000; #AB5541; Merck Millipore, Billerica, USA)54, p-p38 (1:1000; #4511; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, p38 (1:1000; #8690; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, p-JNK (1:1000; #4668; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, JNK (1:1000; #9252; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, p-ERK1/2 (1:1000; #4370; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, ERK1/2 (1:1000; #4695; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)52,55, iNOS (1:1000; #68186; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA), COX-2 (1:1000; #12282; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, USA)56, GAPDH (1:1000; AF0006; Beyotime, Shanghai, China)57 or α-tubulin (1:1000; AF2827; Beyotime, Shanghai, China)58. After incubation with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, the protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (Epizyme, Shanghai, China) and analyzed by the Image J software.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analysis was performed using Graphpad Prism 8 software. Data are presented as mean and standard deviation (SD) and sample sizes are indicated in the figure legends. The escape latency in MWM test was analyzed by two-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test. The t test was used to examine the differences between two groups. One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni’s post-hoc test was applied for comparisons among multiple groups. P < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.
