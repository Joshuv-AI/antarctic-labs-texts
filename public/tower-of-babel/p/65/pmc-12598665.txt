Demise of the Milwaukee Protocol for Rabies
Authors: Alan C Jackson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman rabies has a very high fatality rate and there have only been about 34 well-documented survivors, defined as survival at 6 months after onset of clinical rabies. Many have had serious neurological sequelae. After a young patient survived rabies in Milwaukee in 2004, the approach dubbed the “Milwaukee protocol” has been aggressively promoted as an effective therapy. The protocol has included therapeutic (induced) coma, ketamine, ribavirin, and amantadine and details of the protocol have changed over time. Over the past 2 decades, no subsequent detailed reports have documented evidence of efficacy. There have been at least 64 cases with failure of the protocol. Likely critical care, which has been used for more than 50 years, is an important component of an aggressive approach. The time has now come to abandon the failed Milwaukee protocol for the therapy of rabies and consider new approaches based our current knowledge of rabies pathogenesis.Keywords: amantadine, ketamine, Milwaukee protocol, ribavirin, therapeutic coma

More than 20 years ago, colleagues and I published a viewpoint article in this journal outlining potential approaches to the treatment of patients with rabies [1]. In 2005, a case report was published in the New England Journal of Medicine about a 15-year-old girl who was bitten by a bat in church, received no postexposure rabies prophylaxis, developed clinical rabies with the presence of serum and cerebrospinal fluid neutralizing antirabies virus antibodies, received aggressive therapy, and survived with mild neurological sequelae [2,3]. The patient received therapy that included therapeutic (induced) coma with benzodiazepines (midazolam) and barbiturates to achieve a burst suppression pattern on the electroencephalogram, ketamine, and antiviral therapy with ribavirin and amantadine [2]. There have been numerous reports of this case in the lay press and also in Scientific American [4]. This therapeutic approach has been dubbed the “Milwaukee protocol” and has been relentlessly promoted by its main proponent for about 20 years despite accumulating evidence of its lack of efficacy. There are claims of numerous survivors on a registry website, https://www.mcw.edu/departments/pediatrics/divisions/infectious-diseases/rabies-registry-website, and at an international conference [5] despite a paucity of documentation of credible reports in peer-reviewed journals. The website does not provide specific information about the cases or the actual number of alleged rabies survivors. The data are not available for review in any publication, including in supplementary data. The good results with the protocol have to be accepted on faith rather than on review of the evidence. There have been a number of subsequent versions of the Milwaukee protocol posted online since the initial report. This article will focus on the therapy used on the initial published report in which a single patient had a good outcome [2,3]. A detailed analysis of the Milwaukee protocol was previously published and serious deficiencies were noted [6]. However, the protocol is still used by inexperienced clinicians seeking an aggressive therapeutic approach.

THERAPEUTIC (INDUCED) COMA

Therapeutic (induced) coma with administration of continuous intravenous anesthetic drugs is a generally accepted therapy for the treatment of refractory status epilepticus [7]. This therapy works by terminating the firing of synchronous bursts of neurons that occur in epileptic seizure activity. The initial goal of therapy is to maintain electrographic seizure cessation for a minimum of 24 to 48 hours [7]. Achieving a burst suppression pattern or a isoelectric suppression pattern on the electroencephalogram is not necessarily needed for efficacy. In addition to refractory status epilepticus, therapeutic coma has also been used in traumatic brain injury with increased intracranial pressure to protect brain function and improve oxygen delivery to brain neurons. The use of therapeutic coma for an infectious disease of the nervous system such as rabies is not based on a solid scientific rationale or on our current knowledge of mechanisms involved in rabies pathogenesis [8]. There was speculation that excitotoxicity might be important in the pathogenesis of rabies, and, hence, the rationale for therapeutic coma with N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonism with ketamine and amantadine and with γ-aminobutyric acid receptor agonism with benzodiazepines and barbiturates [2]. Subsequently, experimental evidence in vitro and in a mouse model of rabies did not support an excitotoxicity hypothesis in the pathogenesis of rabies and has also shown a lack of efficacy of therapy with ketamine [9].

Autonomic dysfunction with hyperactivity and/or hypoactivity of the sympathetic and/or parasympathetic systems frequently occurs in human rabies [10], and NMDA receptor antagonist therapy was indicated as a therapy for autonomic dysfunction [2], but this is not a generally accepted effective therapy [11]. NMDA-mediated neuroprotection has failed in many clinical trials in humans both in traumatic brain injury [12] and in stroke [13].

KETAMINE

Ketamine is a dissociative anesthetic agent and a noncompetitive antagonist of NMDA receptor. At high concentrations (1-2 mM) ketamine has been reported to inhibit the in vitro replication of rabies virus by inhibiting rabies virus genome transcription [14]. In another report after stereotaxic inoculation of a strain of fixed rabies virus in the neostriatum of rats, administration of high-dosage ketamine (60 mg/kg administered every 12 hours intraperitoneally) led to reduced infection in multiple brain regions including the hippocampus, cerebral cortex, and thalamus [15]. On the basis of these 2 reports from the Pasteur Institute [14, 15], experts speculated that ketamine might be an efficacious therapeutic agent for rabies [1] and hence it was also included in the Milwaukee protocol. However, subsequent detailed in vitro and mouse model studies failed to demonstrate efficacy [9]. Hence, ketamine should no longer be routinely used for the treatment of human rabies until efficacy is demonstrated in confirmatory studies.

ANTIVIRAL THERAPY

Intravenous ribavirin was included in the Milwaukee protocol. A preceding [16] and many subsequent reports of ribavirin therapy have failed in human cases [17]. A detailed experimental study in a mouse model also showed a lack of efficacy despite in vitro activity [18]. Ribavirin is known to shift the immune response toward a Th1-type inflammatory response [19]. Because of the importance of a balanced Th1/Th2 immune response for the clearance of rabies virus infection, ribavirin may suppress antibody production that is essential for recovery from rabies and, hence, have detrimental effects [20].

Amantadine is an NMDA receptor channel blocker and there is in vitro evidence of inhibition of rabies virus production [21]. For these reasons, amantadine was also included in the Milwaukee protocol. However, amantadine failed to show efficacy in mice when inoculated daily into the site of intramuscular inoculation of wild-type rabies virus [21]. There are no reports of in vivo studies of therapy with amantadine in experimental rabies. Furthermore, to date, no antiviral agent has been demonstrated to have efficacy for the therapy of rabies [17, 20].

MULTIPLE FAILURES OF THE MILWAUKEE PROTOCOL

In 2018 Willoughby claimed to have 18 survivors of human rabies with the Milwaukee protocol [5], but there are no high-quality reports of survivors beyond the index case in Milwaukee. Interestingly, he has not claimed any additional survivors through the 6-year period ending in October 2024 [22]. There are at least 64 reports of failures of the Milwaukee protocol [17]. There are occasional rabies survivors treated with the Milwaukee protocol with severe neurological sequelae who received rabies vaccine before the onset of clinical rabies. However, there are now at least 34 rabies survivors who received critical care management without the main components of the Milwaukee protocol, including many from India [17, 23] (Supplementary Table 1). This is likely a result of many more recent attempts at aggressive care and improved care in critical care units in India, where canine rabies is endemic and more than 20,000 human deaths occur yearly [24]. Critical care is probably the only efficacious component of the Milwaukee protocol, which has been used for many years, including for the first rabies survivor reported in 1972 [25]. Most survivors from rabies have serious neurological sequelae and are unable to return to an independent life and some succumb within several months to a few years due to medical complications.

The main proponent of the Milwaukee protocol includes the claim of a survivor who likely never had rabies. This girl from California made a rapid recovery and never developed neutralizing antirabies virus antibodies [26]. Hence, she should not be considered a rabies survivor. Another, similar patient quickly recovered without aggressive care (and without use of the Milwaukee protocol) and did not develop neutralizing antirabies virus antibodies and, hence, also likely did not actually have rabies [27]. In this case, a low level of rabies virus antibodies was probably the result of cross reactivity with another virus. Patients who recover from rabies have neutralizing antirabies virus antibodies in their serum and cerebrospinal fluid [17]. The development of early neutralizing antibodies is important for potential recovery from rabies. The patient from Milwaukee developed early neutralizing antibodies [2], which played an important role in her recovery. Of course, this was unrelated to the therapy that she was given.

Patients who die early in their clinical course of medical complications have also been deemed to be survivors of rabies, including a girl from Colombia [28]. A man from Peru with rabies also died of medical complications resulting from urosepsis (Rodney E. Willoughby, Jr., personal communication) after transfer from an intensive care unit and was also claimed to be a rabies survivor [29]. Willoughby has deemed that any rabies patient who has been discharged from a critical care unit is a survivor, which is inappropriate. However, deaths from medical complications of rabies should not be considered “accidental” deaths and be deemed survivors as pointed out in a letter to the editor about the published report about the case from Colombia [30]. A much more useful and robust definition proposed for a survivor is survival for a period of 6 months after the onset of initial rabies symptoms/clinical disease [17]. Most patients with rabies are not candidates for an aggressive approach to their care, and palliative care is very important for their management [23, 31].

Our current understanding of rabies pathogenesis indicates that degeneration of neuronal processes plays an important role in producing the clinical disease [32]. This occurs as a result of oxidative stress [33] induced by mitochondrial dysfunction [34] because of interaction with rabies virus and mitochondrial complex [8, 35]. Although this knowledge was unknown at the time that the Milwaukee protocol was conceived, no components of the Milwaukee protocol address these mechanisms (e.g., with antioxidant therapy) important in rabies pathogenesis. The protocol also does not target autophagy pathways [8].

CONCLUSIONS

Continued repetition of the Milwaukee protocol in multiple versions over about 20 years has failed to show efficacy and has actually served to impede progress for the development of effective therapy for human rabies. Most cases of human rabies occurring in resource-rich countries are encountered by physicians without expertise in rabies who are aware of the poor prognosis in rabies and reach out to or are directly contacted by the main proponent of the Milwaukee protocol, who claims to have had multiple successful cases using this approach. There is little credible evidence of benefit beyond the index report and it is now time that the Milwaukee protocol be abandoned. New approaches need to be taken based on scientific advances in the field rather than endless repetition of this flawed and unsuccessful approach. Palliative care is important for the majority of patients with rabies who are not candidates for an aggressive approach for their care.
