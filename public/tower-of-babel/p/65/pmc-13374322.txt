Antimicrobial resistance and plasmidome of carbapenemase-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae causing bloodstream infections between 2014 and 2024
Authors: Wentao Zhu, Qian Liu, Hong Shen, Xi Chen, Chunxia Yang, Ming Wei, Li Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The WHO has designated carbapenemase-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae (CRKP) as a priority pathogen due to the potential for nosocomial infection outbreaks. In this study, a total of 724 non-duplicated K. pneumoniae bloodstream infection isolates were collected in Beijing between 2014 and 2024, of which 14.5% were identified as CRKP (n = 105). The incidence of CRKP isolates remained at a relatively high level since 2018, with a significant increase observed following the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic. The most common sequence type was ST11 (84.8%), followed by ST16 (5.7%) and ST15 (5.7%). Sixteen potential clonal transmissions were identified, including four clonal clusters transmitted at the ward-level. Antimicrobial resistance was severe, with almost all isolates showing resistance to 15 antimicrobials tested. The blaKPC-2 (93.3%) was the dominated carbapenemase gene, followed by blaOXA-181 (5.7%). The blaCTX-M-65 (61.0%) was the most common ESBL gene, followed by blaCTX-M-15 (14.3%) and blaSHV-12 (12.4%). Phenotype-genotype concordance was 100% for cephalosporins and carbapenemases. The correlation of plasmids with antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes were determined, with the IncFII(pHN7A8)_1_pHN7A8 plasmid showing a significant correlation with blaCTX-M-65 and blaKPC-2, and the IncHI1B_1_pNDM-MAR and IncR_1 plasmids significantly correlated with iucABC-iutA (P < 0.05). The trend of type KL64 was increasing and surpassed that of type KL47 since 2019, similar to the trend of O2a gradually surpassing O13. Our study provides insights into the epidemiology, dissemination, AMR genes, and plasmids of CRKP bloodstream infections in Beijing, China, over the long term.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05395-9.

Introduction

The WHO has identified carbapenemase-resistant and third-generation cephalosparin-resistant Enterobacterales as the two critical groups on the priority bacterial pathogen list for 2024, due to limited treatment options and the rapid acquisition of novel resistance mechanisms [1]. Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), a member of the order Enterobacterales, is a major cause of hospital- and community-acquired infections, including respiratory tract infections and bloodstream infections [2]. The multidrug-resistance and hypervirulence in K. pneumoniae has made it one of the six leading bacterial pathogens associated with mortality [3]. Bloodstream infections (BSIs) are a life-threatening condition globally, with an estimated 250,000 cases reported in hospitals each year and increasing rate [4]. K. pneumoniae bloodstream infections (KP-BSI) are now the third leading cause of Gram-negative bloodstream infection, leading to a significant burden of morbidity and mortality [5].

The population structure of K. pneumoniae showed genetic diversity and high complexity [6]. Understanding long-term population trends of clinically relevant lineages is crucial for guiding treatment, infection prevention strategies, and vaccine development [7]. Whole genome sequencing (WGS) has emerged as a vital tool for epidemiology and surveillance of this pathogen [6], revealing the global dominance of a few clonal groups (CGs), such as CG15, CG29, CG231, and CG258 [8, 9]. Notably, the predominant sequence type (ST) varies at both a local and global scales, even among different types of infections. For all Klebsiella infections, ST147 and ST395 are prominent in the United Kingdom and Russia, respectively [10]. Meanwhile, K. pneumoniae ST258 has played a significant role in the spread of carbapenemase resistance in the USA and European countries [11]. In the UK, most bloodstream infections of Klebsiella spp. are caused by sporadic STs [5]. However, there have been few longitudinal studies published on the K. pneumoniae population in Beijing to date.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become one of the most serious public health problems [3]. K. pneumoniae has the ability to rapidly acquire resistance genes, particularly in hospital settings where antibiotics are frequently used [12]. Although susceptible isolates cause the majority of K. pneumoniae infections, the spread of AMR genes on mobile genetic elements between different lineages is a significant concern [5, 13]. K. pneumoniae strains harboring carbapenemase and extended spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBLs) genes are particularly notable, as they render last-line antibiotics for treating infections ineffective [14]. Plasmids in K. pneumoniae play an important role in the dissemination and evolution of AMR genes within and between species [15, 16]. Previous studies on plasmids showed a wide variety of plasmids contributing to AMR, but historically focused on a single K. pneumoniae isolate [17, 18]. Thus, we still lack comprehensive knowledge about the plasmidome dynamics in K. pneumoniae causing bloodstream infections in China.

While K. pneumoniae is distributed worldwide, its dominant lineages and resistance genes show marked geographic variation, likely reflecting local population immunity and antimicrobial pressure [7]. LPS O-antigens and capsular polysaccharide antigens are the most important factors that activate host’s innate immune system [19]. A better understanding of the epidemiology of these antigens in K. pneumoniae could help identify more representative trends and aid in the development of vaccines targeting locally dominant lineages.

In this study, our objective is to analyze the genomic diversity of clinical carbapenemase-resistant K. pneumoniae (CRKP) causing bloodstream infections between 2014 and 2024. All non-duplicate CRKP isolates from a single Chinese medical center are collected for WGS to identify their STs and patterns of dissemination. Additionally, phenotypic assays and comparative genomics are performed to characterize the AMR profiles of the CRKP isolates and provide insights into the dynamics of AMR. Moreover, our work aims to elucidate the plasmidome to better understand the spread of AMR in bloodstream infections.

Materials and methods

Study design and isolates enrolled

We conducted a retrospective cohort study on preserved K. pneumoniae isolates causing bloodstream infections. The study population consisted of all patients admitted to a tertiary-level hospital and treated for K. pneumoniae bloodstream infections within the capital of China between Jan 1, 2014, and Dec 31, 2024. The hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, has 2500 beds and serves a population of over 100 000 inpatients and approximately 4 million outpatients annually [20]. These isolates were recovered using China Blue agar plates at 37 °C for 24 h, and the corresponding single colony was further identified by using MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) with the Bruker Biotyper Library version 12.0. To avoid duplication, only one isolate per patient was selected for further study, even if they experienced several bloodstream infections during the period. The K. pneumoniae isolates used in the study were bacterial isolates that had been archived as part of routine clinical laboratory operations. Isolates exhibiting phenotypic resistance to carbapenem antibiotics (meropenem or imipenem), defined as a minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) ≥ 4 µg/L, were classified as CRKP according to CLSI criteria. All CRKP isolates subsequently underwent whole-genome sequencing, and carbapenemase genes were detected in all isolates included in the study. Patient protected health information was not collected since no patient samples were used. The study was divided into four periods, including period A (2014–2016) defined as early years, period B (2017–2019) and C (2020–2022) representing before and during the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic, and period D (2023–2024) indicative of after the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic.

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records. Variables included demographic characteristics (age and sex), clinical manifestations (fever, cough, sputum production, chills, and shivering), healthcare-related factors (tracheal cannulation and ICU admission), laboratory findings (white blood cell count, neutrophil percentage, and hemoglobin level), and clinical outcomes. The primary outcome was 14-day all-cause mortality following the onset of bloodstream infection.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted on all non-duplicated isolates as described in a previous study [21, 22], utilizing both the VITEK®2 AST-GN67 systems (bioMérieux, France) and Kirby–Bauer (K-B) disk diffusion method (Oxoid, UK). The resistance breakpoint was interpreted based on Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines (CLSI M100-Ed34, 2024), with E. coli ATCC 25922 serving as the quality control. A total of twenty-two antimicrobials or combinations were tested to determine the phenotypes of K. pneumoniae isolates, including, trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole (SXT), amikacin (AK), gentamicin (CN), tobramycin (TOB), cefotetan, cefepime (FEP), ciprofloxacin (CIP), levofloxacin (LEV), meropenem (MEM), aztreonam (ATM), ampicillin/sulbactam (SAM), Cefperazone-Sulbactam (SCF), ceftriaxone (CRO), cefazolin (CFZ), imipenem (IPM), piperacillin (PRL), cefuroxime axetil (CA), ceftazidime (CAZ), and piperacillin-tazobactam (TZP). Tigecycline (TGC) and minocycline (MH) susceptibility testing was performed using the disk diffusion method. Freshly prepared Mueller–Hinton agar plates were used for testing in accordance with CLSI/EUCAST recommendations and laboratory quality-control procedures. The carbapenem resistance of the isolates was confirmed using both MIC and K-B disk diffusion methods, with IPM and MEM as the antibiotics of choice. The concordance between antimicrobial resistance genotypes and phenotypic susceptibility profiles was evaluated using phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing as the reference standard. For each antimicrobial class, isolates were classified as follows: (i) True Positive, isolates phenotypically resistant and carrying at least one corresponding resistance gene; (ii) True Negative, isolates phenotypically susceptible and lacking the corresponding resistance gene(s); (iii) False Positive, isolates carrying resistance gene(s) but exhibiting a susceptible phenotype; and (iv) False Negative, isolates exhibiting a resistant phenotype but lacking the corresponding resistance gene(s) included in this study. Concordance metrics were calculated based on these classifications.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

Liquid cultures of isolates in LB broth were washed twice using PBS buffer, and the pellet was used for genomic DNA extraction and purification by the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Promega, USA). The DNA was then randomly fragmented using an ultrasonic crusher (Covaris, USA), and used to construct a sequencing library with the NEBNext®Ultra™ DNA Library Prep Kit (NEB, USA). Sequencing was performed by Magigene Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using the Illumina Novaseq 6000 platform (San Diego, USA) to generate 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. Quality control of the obtained raw reads was conducted using FastQC v.0.11.5 (https://github.com/s-andrews/FastQC), and the adapters were trimmed using Trimmomatic v0.39 [23]. Adapter sequences were removed using the ILLUMINACLIP function, and low-quality reads were filtered using the parameters LEADING:3, TRAILING:3, SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20, and MINLEN:50. Only high-quality reads were retained for downstream analyses. The average coverage depth was 100 ×. The number of contigs per genome ranged from 57 to 187 (mean ± SD: 113.5 ± 19.7). The total assembly size ranged from 5.42 to 9.61 Mb (mean ± SD: 5.69 ± 2.03 Mb), and the N50 values ranged from 117,261 to 812,045 bp (mean ± SD: 199,687 ± 66,402 bp), indicating overall good assembly quality suitable for comparative genomic analyses.

Genome assembly and annotation

Genomes were included only if they achieved an average genome coverage of 100x. Contamination screening was performed using CheckM v2 (https://github.com/ecogenomics/checkm). Genomes were considered contaminated and excluded if they showed > 5% contamination or evidence of mixed species assignment based on taxonomic classification and genome completeness assessment. De novo assembly of paired-end clean reads was conducted using SPAdes v4.0.0 with default parameters, as previously described (spades.py −1 left.fastq.gz −2 right.fastq.gz -o output_folder) [22, 24, 25]. Assemblies with sizes less than 5 Mb and greater than 7 Mb were excluded from further analysis [26]. Annotation of assembled genomes was performed using the Bakta pipeline v1.9.3 with default parameters [27]. The KL type and O antigen (LPS) serotype of each isolate was determined from the de novo assembly using Kleborate v3.1.3 [28]. The clonal group (CG) and sublineage (SL) of each isolate were determined by uploading the genomes to a web platform, which identifies them using multilocus sequence typing (MLST) based on the ‘Klebsiella pneumoniae complex’ database (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/klebsiella/) [29].

Phylogenetic analysis

To perform the phylogenetic analysis, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in genomes were identified using Snippy v4.6.0 (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), with the first identified isolate (KP30820) as the reference. The core SNP alignment was obtained from full-length whole-genome alignment using the snippy-clean function [30], and this alignment was subsequently processed with Gubbins v3.4 [30] to filter regions of homologous recombination. The SNPs were extracted from the core full alignment to build a phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE v2.0.6 with the default parameters [31], and visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) [32]. The SNP distance matrix among isolates was calculated using SNP-dists v0.8.2 (https://github.com/tseemann/snp-dists). Clonal clusters were defined as isolates differing by fewer than 21 core-genome SNPs (cgSNPs), as previously reported [26], while singleton isolates were strains that did not form any clusters. A minimum spanning tree was constructed and visualized using GrapeTree v1.5.0 [33] based on the SNP distance matrix.

Identification of AMR and virulence genes

The AMR genes of each isolate, such as ESBL and carbapenemase genes, were predicted using ABRicate v1.0.0 [34] against the NCBI AMRFinderPlus database with default parameters (≥ 80% identity and ≥ 80% coverage) [35]. The drug classes of identified AMR genes were summarized based on the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) [36]. The presence of virulence genes (iuc, iro, and rmpA2) was determined using both the virulence factor database in ABRicate v1.0.0 [34] and Kleborate v3.1.3 [28]. The virulence score (ranging from 0 to 5) of each isolate was estimated based on the presence of ybt, clb, and iuc using Kleborate v3.1.3 [28]. Hypervirulent KP was defined as isolates possessing a virulence score ≥ 3.

Plasmid reconstruction and clustering

The plasmid replicons of each isolate were screened using the PlasmidFinder database in ABRicate v1.0.0 [34]. The plasmid sequences in each genome were reconstructed, clustered, and typed using the MOB-suite software v3.1.9 [37] based on over 30,000 plasmids deposited in PLSDB [38]. The MOB-clusters of plasmid sequences were assigned based on sequence similarity, which was estimated using Mash v2.3 (https://github.com/marbl/Mash). If two plasmids were clustered into the same secondary cluster, they shared nearly identical sequences, indicating that they were sufficiently related to be strong candidates for plasmid spread [39, 40]. The individual plasmid sequences were reconstructed using MOB-recon. Plasmid sequences were annotated by Bakta v1.9.3 [27], and then used as input for Roary v3.12.0 [41], which could identify the core and accessory genes. The obtained core genes were used to construct a plasmid phylogenetic tree using IQ-TREE v2.0.6 as mentioned above.

Statistical analysis

Network analysis and visualization were performed in R (version 4.3.3) using the igraph package (version 2.3.2) for network construction and the ggraph package (version 2.2.2) for graphical visualization. Correlation of AMR genes with plasmid replicons were quantified using Spearman correlation coefficient, with a P value < 0.05 indicating statistical significance. The clinical data, including sex, age, symptoms, and blood routine indices, were retrieved from the respective patient’s medical records. These data were calculated as the medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or percentages of patients in each category. The Fisher’s exact test and Cochran-Armitage trend test were used for comparisons of continuous and categorical variables.

Results

Temporal distribution of bloodstream CRKP isolates

From Jan 2014 to Dec 2024, a total of 724 nonduplicated K. pneumoniae isolates were collected from bloodstream infections in inpatients across 25 departments of a tertiary hospital in Beijing, with the highest number observed in 2021 (Figure S1). The comprehensive sampling approach provided a unique opportunity to longitudinally analyze the prevalence and distribution of CRKP isolates. Among these K. pneumoniae isolates, 105 CRKP isolates were identified (14.5%, 95%CI: 11.9–17.6), exhibiting phenotypic resistance to both meropenem and imipenem (MIC ≥ 4 µg/L). During the study period, the prevalence of CRKP isolates in the early years was considerably different from the most recent year (Figure S1). The crude frequency of CRKP isolates in periods B and C showed an upward trend. Although the positive rate dropped in 2022, there was a new significant increase from 2023 to 2024.

Population structure of bloodstream CRKP isolates

The maximum likelihood phylogeny tree was built based on 103,608 SNPs extracted from 3,393 core genes of the 105 CRKP isolates, which comprised a genetically diverse population (Fig. 1). Eight known STs were identified, with ST11 being the most common at 84.8% (89/105), followed by ST16 at 5.7% (6/105) and ST15 at 5.7% (5/105). The remaining five STs contained only one genome and were sporadically observed in different years. ST11 clearly dominated the population throughout the study period, except for 2016 when only one ST15 isolate was identified. The 105 genomes were further classified into 8 known SLs and 9 known CGs. The most frequently identified SL258 contained 89 genomes (84.8%), followed by SL15 and SL17, each at 5.7%. The most frequently detected CGs was CG11 (83.8%, 88/105), with CG15 and CG16 being followed. Interestingly, the proportion of ST11 CRKP isolates decreased after 2022, indicating an increase in less common STs, CGs, and SLs in the annual population of bloodstream CRKP isolates. In addition, sixty-one isolates (52.4%, 55/105) were classified as carbapenemase-resistant hypervirulent K. pneumoniae (CR-hvKP), with the majority belonging to ST11 (96.7%, 59/61), followed by ST16 (1.6%, 1/61), and ST23 (1.6%, 1/61) (Fig. 1). Transmission of CRKP multiple clones

Given that nosocomial transmission and clonal dissemination of CRKP have been widely reported in healthcare settings worldwide, particularly among epidemic lineages such as ST11, we further investigated the population structure of CRKP isolates in our hospital to determine whether similar dissemination patterns were present [42, 43]. The number of pairwise SNPs among the analyzed CRKP isolates ranged from 0 to 19802, with all comparisons between different STs exceeding 11,835 SNPs (Fig. 2A). Based on a threshold of 21 SNPs [26], a total of 16 clonal clusters were identified, containing 68 isolates (64.8%, 69/105). The majority of clonal clusters consisted of 2–3 isolates (median: 3, IQR: 2–5). The largest clonal cluster was cluster 1, which comprised thirteen isolates collected between 2017 and 2024. Among these clonal clusters, isolates from nine clusters (56.3%, 9/16) were collected from at least two different years (Fig. 2A). Additionally, isolates from four clonal clusters were collected from only one department, indicating inter-departmental transmission of isolates from the other 12 clonal clusters (75.0%, 12/16) (Fig. 2B). Apart from isolates of cluster 16 belonging to ST16, all other clustered isolates were classified as ST11. Notably, only five clonal clusters originated before 2019. Antimicrobial resistance profiles of CRKP isolates

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) was performed on CRKP isolates against 22 antimicrobial agents belonging to seven drug classes. The result revealed that the CRKP isolates displayed resistance to 12 to 22 antimicrobial agents per isolate, with a median of 19 (IQR 17–20). All CRKP isolates were found to be phenotypically resistant to ten out of the twenty-two antimicrobials tested, including SAM, SCF, CXM, CRO, CFZ, IPM, PRL, cefuroxime axetil, CAZ, and TZP (Fig. 3A). Additionally, almost all isolates demonstrated resistance to ATM (99.0%, 104/105), CIP (98.1%, 103/105), LEV (98.1%, 103/105), MEM (98.1%, 103/105), and FEP (96.2%, 101/105). Resistance to the remaining antimicrobials was relatively low, particularly towards MH (24.0%, 26/105) and TGC (9.5%, 10/105). In total, twenty-nine unique resistant profiles were identified, each characterized by different combinations of resistance phenotypes (Fig. 3A). The majority of these profiles consisted of only one to two isolates. The most prevalent resistant profile among these isolates was susceptible only to TGC and MH (19.0%, 20/105). Concordance of AMR genotype and phenotype

The genome of each isolate was screened using in silico analysis to investigate the presence of genes encoding AMR determinants, which suggested that the K. pneumoniae population harbored diverse AMR genes. A total of 103 unique AMR genes belonging to fourteen drug classes were identified (Fig. 3B). All 105 genomes harbored at least one AMR gene that conferred resistance to carbapenemases (Figure S3), with 93.3% of them containing blaKPC-2 (93.3%, 98/105), followed by blaOXA-181 (5.7%, 6/105), blaNDM-1 (1.9%, 2/105), and blaNDM-5 (1.9%, 2/105). Notably, three isolates contained two carbapenemase genes (blaKPC-2-blaOXA-181, blaKPC-2-blaNDM-1, and blaKPC-2-blaNDM-5). The five ESBL genes were detected in 83 (79.0%) isolates, with blaCTX-M-65 (64/105) being the most common, followed by blaCTX-M-15 (15/105). We found that two ESBL genes were present simultaneously in one isolate (blaCTX-M-65-blaCTX-M-3). Additionally, thirteen other beta-lactamase genes were identified, with blaTEM-1 (68.6%, 72/105) and blaLAP-2 (36.2%, 38/105) being the most prevalent. Furthermore, the AMR genes that conferred resistance to aminoglycosides were classified into different groups [e.g., aac(6'), aadA, aph, armA, and rmt*] based on the CARD database, with 98 isolates harboring at least one (median: 2, range: 1–5) AMR gene belonging to the five groups. A total of four genes associated with resistance to phosphonic acid antibiotics were detected in 104 (99.0%) isolates. Other AMR determinants, including genes against fluoroquinolone, macrolide, phenicol, and tetracycline, were also detected, but at low frequencies (Fig. 3B).

The study investigated the level of concordance between the results of the AST and the in silico AMR genes conferring resistance to different antimicrobials (Fig. 3C and supplementary Excel tables). A 100% concordance between the resistant phenotypes and the presence of the corresponding AMR genes were observed for cephalosporin, ESBLs, and carbapenemase. The concordance between aminoglycoside resistance phenotype and genotype exhibited 78.1% agreement, with all of them being true positives (phenotypic and genotypic concordance). The phenotype-genotype agreement of sulfonamide resistance was 77.1%, with 44.8% being true positives and 32.3% true negatives. The phenotype-genotype concordance for fluoroquinolone and tetracycline was 59.1% (58.1% true positive and 1.0% true negative) and 54.3% (16.2% true positive and 38.1% true negative), respectively.

Correlation analysis of AMR and virulence genes with plasmids

A total of 468 plasmids were identified from these CRKP isolates using MOB-suite, with a median of four plasmids per isolate (IQR 3–5, range 2–9). Plasmids were predicted to be conjugative (22.2%, 104/468), mobilizable (28.4%, 133/468), and non-mobilizable (49.4%, 231/468). The plasmids were found to have originated from 16 different hosts, predominantly in K. pneumoniae (82.7%), followed by Escherichia coli (10.3%), Citrobacter freundii (1.7%), and Acinetobacter baumannii (0.9%). Of the 468 plasmids, 70 primary clusters were observed, with AA406 (n = 57), AA942 (n = 55), and AA032 (n = 52) being the most dominant. It is noted that blaKPC-2 was mainly presented in plasmids within AA032 cluster (Fig. 4A), most of which contained blaTEM-1 (85.1%, 40/47) and rmtB1 (80.9%, 38/47) genes simultaneously. Antigenic diversity

The capsular and surface polysaccharide antigens of K. pneumoniae are receiving increasing attention as potential vaccine targets. Twelve K-antigens were detected in these CRKP isolates, with KL47 being the most prevalent (41.0%, 43/105), followed by type KL64 (35.2%, 37/105), and type KL25 (7.6%, 8/105). There had been an upward trend in type KL64, surpassing that of type KL47 since 2019 (Fig. 6A). The proportion of KL64 among KL47/KL64 isolates increased significantly over time (Cochran-Armitage trend test, P < 0.05). Additionally, seven different O-antigens were identified, with type O2a (41.9%, 44/105) being the most dominant, followed by type O13 (41.0%, 43/105). O2a showed a gradual increase, while O13 showed a gradual decrease during the study period (Fig. 6B). The increase in O2a prevalence over time was statistically significant (Cochran-Armitage trend test, P < 0.05). As the primary K-antigens, all isolates with type KL64 and KL47 were ST11 K. pneumoniae (Fig. 6C). The dominant O2a and O13 isolates all belonged to ST11, with other O-antigen types being sporadically presented in other STs (Fig. 6D). Unsurprisingly, we observed that all isolates with type O13 had KL47 antigens. While most isolates with type O2a contained KL64 antigens, several isolates with type O2a belonged to KL25 and KL43 clusters (Fig. 6E). Comparison of clinical manifestations

The median age of all cases infected with ST11 isolates was 65 years (IQR 51–74), with 75.4% of them being male (49/65) (Table S1). In comparison, 84.6% (11/13) of patients infected with non-ST11 isolates were male, with a median age of 63 years (IQR 50–74). Fever was the most common clinical symptom, with no significant difference observed between the two groups. The proportion of patients with cough, sputum, chill, and shiver were higher in the ST11 group than in non-ST11 group. Moreover, although the rates of ICU admission and 14-day mortality were not significantly different between the groups, both were higher in the ST11 group. Abnormalities in clinical laboratory indicators were most frequently observed in hemoglobin levels in both groups, followed by neutrophil count.

Discussion

KP-BSI is one of the most fatal infections, leading to a high mortality rate ranging from 20 to 40%, and even higher at 65% in ICU patients [44, 45]. The emergence of CRKP isolates has gained increasing attention, resulting in even higher mortality rates [46]. In the current study, the longitudinal collection of routine diagnostics for bloodstream infections caused by CRKP isolates from 2014 to 2024 provided a unique opportunity to analyze their epidemiology and antimicrobial resistance in Beijing, China, which drastically increased over time. The incidence of CRKP-BSI in KP-BSI remained below 10% before 2017, but sharply increased thereafter, nearly doubling since 2018. Furthermore, there was a notable increase in occurrences of CRKP-BSI following the SARS-CoV-2 epidemic. The overall mortality rate caused by CRKP-BSI in our hospital was approximately 30%, which was considered moderate compared to global rates [44], and similar to other hospitals in China [47]. In addition, the time span of our sample collection (2014–2024) is longer than those in existing studies, such as Zhou et al. (2014–2019) [48] and Hu et al. (2016–2020) [49], and is comparable to that of Wang et al. (2011–2021) [50]. Notably, our sample collection extends up to 2024, allowing us to reflect the latest trends of CRKP-BSI in Beijing. Although clinical data were available, the present study was not specifically designed to investigate independent risk factors for infection with particular CRKP lineages or clinical outcomes. Future studies integrating detailed clinical and genomic data are warranted.

Analysis of the population structure of CRKP-BSI isolates revealed an overall low level of diversification, with ST11 occupying the overwhelmingly dominant position at 84.8%. While it was well-documented that ST11 isolates contributed significantly to the spread of carbapenemase resistance in BSI in China [51, 52], this study offered a real-world perspective on the prevalence of ST11 in CRKP-BSI. The predominance of ST11 observed in this study is consistent with the molecular epidemiology of CRKP in China, where ST11 has long been recognized as the dominant epidemic lineage. However, the distribution of CRKP sequence types varies geographically. In North America, ST258 has historically been the predominant clone and is closely associated with KPC production [53]. In Europe, ST258, ST512, and related clonal groups are frequently reported [54], whereas ST147, ST307, and ST15 have emerged as important high-risk lineages in several regions worldwide [55]. The predominance of ST11 among CRKP bloodstream isolates suggests the potential circulation of a successful epidemic clone within the hospital. ST11 is the most prevalent CRKP lineage in China and has been frequently associated with nosocomial transmission and outbreaks. The sustained detection of ST11 isolates over the study period may reflect persistent clonal dissemination in the healthcare setting. Although our study was not designed to investigate transmission pathways, these findings highlight the importance of continuous genomic surveillance and strengthened infection prevention and control measures to limit the spread of high-risk CRKP clones. The long-term persistence of ST11 over the 10-year study period further suggests endemic circulation of this lineage rather than sporadic introductions. In addition to the predominant ST11 lineage, ST15, ST16, NDM-producing, and OXA-181-producing isolates were identified. Although detected at low frequencies, these emerging high-risk clones and resistance mechanisms have been increasingly reported worldwide and warrant continued surveillance due to their potential for further dissemination.

Interestingly, while the primary ST of CRKP isolates in China remained unchanged, there was a major transformation in both the K-type and O-type antigens. The K-antigens exhibited a very high level of diversity, with twelve types identified in this study. The KL47 accounted for an absolute majority before 2017, differing from previous reports in which KL64 was the most common type between 2015 and 2017 [56]. However, the KL64 had emerged in 2018, and gradually surpassed that of type KL47 since 2019. On the other hand, there were fewer O-antigen types in CRKP isolates, with the initially dominate O13 isolates being surpassed by O2a over the study period, mirroring the trend in K-antigens. Overall, this study provided valuable insights for surveillance and the design of vaccines targeting CRKP-BSI isolates, highlighting the importance of focusing on O2a-KL64-ST11 isolates in China. The serotype distribution identified in this study may have important implications for the development of K. pneumoniae vaccines. Currently, no licensed vaccine against K. pneumoniae is available; however, several capsule polysaccharide (CPS)-based and O-antigen-based vaccine candidates are under development [57]. Notably, O-antigen-targeted vaccine candidates have attracted considerable attention because a limited number of O serotypes account for the majority of clinical isolates worldwide [58]. Studies have shown that O1, O2, O3, and O5 collectively cover more than 80% of K. pneumoniae isolates globally.

To further investigate the ancestral relationships between the dispersed populations, pairwise SNP differences between these isolates were determined in this study. The 16 potential intrahospital transmission chains accounted for 64.8% of all isolates, using the same cut-off value (< 21 SNPs) reported in ST258/512 [26]. Isolates within four transmission chains were identified from the same department, suggesting they were linked at the ward level. The other twelve clonal clusters were collected from at least two departments, indicating the transmission between different wards. Since more than half of patients underwent operations, such as tracheal intubation, inter-ward dissemination could be caused by wounds from medical devices during treatment. However, a major limitation of this hypothesis was the lack of corresponding samples from the environment and medical equipment. Most clonal transmission events occurred during the COVID-19 pandemic period. Although infection prevention measures were strengthened during this time, increased healthcare burden, prolonged hospitalization, widespread antibiotic use, and reallocation of infection-control resources may have facilitated CRKP transmission. Additionally, successful epidemic clones such as ST11 may possess enhanced environmental persistence and dissemination capacity. Therefore, the observed clonal spread was likely driven by a combination of healthcare-system pressures and the biological advantages of epidemic CRKP lineages.

AMR in BSI was recognized as one of the leading threats to global human health [59]. The carbapenemase genes in clinical CRKP isolates collected between 2014 and 2024 were predominated by blaKPC-2 (93.3%), consistent with nationwide collections [50]. These results supported the notion that the blaKPC-2 gene was ST-specific in CRKP isolates in China. In contrast, the global epidemiology of carbapenemases varies considerably across regions. KPC-producing clones, particularly ST258, predominate in North America and parts of Europe, whereas NDM-producing isolates are more common in South Asia and have increasingly disseminated worldwide. OXA-48-like carbapenemases are frequently reported in the Mediterranean region, the Middle East, and parts of Europe, while VIM and IMP enzymes remain important in certain geographic areas. These regional differences highlight the distinct evolutionary and transmission dynamics of carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae and emphasize the importance of ongoing genomic surveillance to monitor the emergence and spread of high-risk resistance mechanisms.

In this study, there was 100% concordance between carbapenemase phenotype and genotype, with remaining isolates harboring blaOXA-181, blaNDM-1, or blaNDM-5. Ceftriaxone resistance was attributed almost exclusively to the ESBL-encoding blaCTX-M-65 (61.0%), followed by blaCTX-M-15 (14.3%), blaSHV-12 (12.4%). This pattern differs from previous reports, in which blaCTX-M-15 and blaCTX-M-14 waere identified as the most widespread ESBL-encoding genes [7]. The emergence of blaCTX-M-15 has been associated with successful epidemic clones and mobile genetic elements that facilitate its dissemination. Therefore, the predominance of CTX-M-65 observed in our collection may reflect the evolving molecular epidemiology of ESBLs in China rather than a hospital-specific phenomenon. Continued surveillance is warranted to monitor the dissemination of this increasingly important resistance determinant. In addition to thirteen isolates carrying two ESBL genes, it was found that only 84 isolates had an ESBL-encoding gene, suggesting that eight isolates lacked identifiable ESBL gene. This discrepancy may be due to the draft genome used in this study and warrants further investigation in the future.

Mobile genetic elements, especially plasmids, played an important role in the transmission of AMR genes and have been implicated in bacterial outbreaks [2, 60, 61]. Previous studies had reported that an IncHI1B/IncFIB plasmid could be transmitted between two K. pneumoniae ST11, and even horizontally to Escherichia coli isolates [62]. In this study, the IncFII(pHN7A8)_1_pHN7A8 plasmid contributed to the clonal transmission of blaKPC-2. In addition, plasmids encoding ESBLs that mediated the success of international and epidemic K. pneumoniae clones (ST307 and ST258) had been identified [6]. Here, we identified plasmids that contributed to the spread of ESBLs genes in K. pneumoniae ST11 isolates, such as the IncFII(pHN7A8)_1_pHN7A8 plasmid for blaCTX-M-65, ColKP3_1, IncX3_1, and IncFII(S)_1 plasmids for blaCTX-M-15. These results provided an important basis for plasmid surveillance in CRKP ST11 isolates in China.

Although high concordance was observed between genotypic predictions and phenotypic resistance profiles for most antimicrobial classes, false-negative results were detected for tetracyclines, sulfonamides, and quinolones. Several explanations may account for this discrepancy. First, the extremely high rates of ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin resistance observed in this study were likely associated with the widespread presence of QRDR mutations. Analysis of quinolone-resistant isolates revealed that 97.1% carried classical substitutions in GyrA (D87A, D87G, D87N, S83F, or S83I) and/or ParC (E84K or S80I), which are well-recognized mechanisms mediating high-level fluoroquinolone resistance in K. pneumoniae [63]. These findings suggest that chromosomal QRDR mutations, in addition to plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance determinants, contributed substantially to the fluoroquinolone-resistant phenotype of the CRKP isolates. Second, additional resistance determinants that were not investigated in this study may contribute to the observed phenotypes. For example, resistance phenotypes may be influenced by changes in gene expression, efflux pump activity, or other regulatory mechanisms that are not captured by targeted resistance gene detection.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study conducted in a tertiary-care hospital, and therefore the molecular epidemiological characteristics observed may not be fully representative of other institutions or regions. Second, information regarding the primary source of bloodstream infection was not systematically available in the medical records and therefore could not be included in the present analysis. Third, plasmid reconstruction was performed using Illumina short-read sequencing data and MOB-suite-based predictions. Although these approaches provide valuable insights into plasmid diversity and resistance gene dissemination, short-read assemblies may not fully resolve complex plasmid structures, particularly in repetitive regions. Fourth, the < 21 SNP criterion was originally proposed in studies involving ST258 isolates and may not fully reflect the evolutionary dynamics of ST11 strains circulating in China. Therefore, the inferred transmission clusters should be interpreted cautiously and regarded as indicative of potential clonal dissemination rather than definitive transmission events. Fifth, we classified CR-hvKP as isolates with a Kleborate virulence score ≥ 3, a widely used genome-based threshold largely driven by the presence of aerobactin [28]. As hypervirulence is a multifactorial phenotype, this classification should be interpreted as a genomic prediction rather than a phenotypic confirmation of hypervirulence. Finally, although clinical data were available for a subset of patients, detailed patient-level risk factor analyses were not performed.

In conclusion, this longitudinal study provided a unique insight into the prevalence of CRKP isolates in BSI, showing an overall upward trend in a clinical setting in China. We also identified that blaKPC-2 was predominantly associated with ST11 isolates in this cohort, with changes in O- and K- antigens observed during the study period. Furthermore, we investigated the plasmids that contributed to the spread of AMR and virulence genes. This work will be valuable in current efforts to design effective vaccines and interventions to control the transmission of CRKP clones, highlighting the importance of continuing genomic epidemiological studies.
