Immunoregulatory properties of the human acellular amniotic membrane for diabetic wound healing
Authors: Rui-Peng Cai, Zhen Xiang, Lin-Jun Huang, Zhen-Yu Huang, Chao-Jun Zhuo, Kai Liu, Ornella Parolini, Yong-Can Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRepair of chronic diabetic wounds remains a major clinical challenge, and the immunoregulatory mechanisms of the human acellular amniotic membrane (HAAM) are not fully understood. Using a db/db type 2 diabetic mouse model with full-thickness dorsal wounds, this study evaluated the therapeutic effects of the HAAM compared with conventional gauze in wound healing. Compared with the control group, the HAAM group exhibited significantly accelerated wound closure and enhanced collagen deposition. Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that the HAAM reshaped the immune microenvironment by promoting chemotactic, tissue-reparative macrophage phenotypes while reducing S100a9+ neutrophils linked to neutrophil extracellular traps. Intercellular communication analysis indicated enhanced macrophage-neutrophil interactions via Spp1-CD44 signaling. HAAM-treated wounds upregulated prodegenerative and angiogenic genes and exhibited balanced matrix remodeling. Overall, the findings indicated that the HAAM promoted diabetic wound healing by reducing excessive inflammation and activating regenerative pathways, highlighting its potential as a bioactive scaffold for next-generation immunomodulatory biomaterials.Keywords: acellular amniotic membrane, diabetic wound healing, single cell RNA-seq, neutrophil extracellular traps

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs) are among the most severe complications of diabetes, with a lifetime risk of approximately 15%–25%.1 These ulcers are stubborn, hard to treat, and prone to recurrence, significantly affecting patients’ quality of life and placing a burden on healthcare systems.2 Globally, approximately 18.6 million individuals are affected by DFUs annually, 12.5% of whom requiring amputation.3

DFUs are characterized by chronic inflammation, recurrent infections, and impaired blood circulation, which contribute to poor healing.4 In diabetic wounds, the early inflammatory response is often delayed compared with normal wounds, and the inflammatory phase is significantly prolonged.5 The recruitment of immune cells to the wound site is crucial for coordinating healing, as multiple cell types are involved in this process.6 However, the hyperglycemic local microenvironment in diabetes impairs chemotaxis and migration of peripheral neutrophils, delaying infiltration at the early inflammatory stage.7 Additionally, the diabetic milieu disrupts the phenotypic plasticity of macrophages, causing prolonged retention in a proinflammatory M1 state and reducing their transition to anti-inflammatory and reparative M2 phenotypes.8,9 Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs)—complex structures formed by decondensed chromatin, myeloperoxidase (MPO), and neutrophil elastase—play a critical role in neutrophil-mediated immune regulation.10 While NETs can facilitate wound healing, excessive activation due to dysregulated inflammation can impede the healing process.11 Therefore, regulating the balance between macrophages and neutrophils, as well as the timely initiation and resolution of the inflammatory phase, is essential for controlling the intensity and duration of the inflammatory response and promoting healing.12

Since the first successful use of fetal membranes (amnion + chorion) for skin grafting in 1910, the amniotic membrane has been widely used in clinical research and therapy.13,14,15 Amniotic membrane derivatives, which are available in fresh, cryopreserved, dehydrated, lyophilized, powder, extract, and composite hydrogel forms, act as low-immunogenic natural extracellular matrix (ECM) carriers.16,17 These derivatives exhibit multiple functions, including antibacterial and antibiofilm effects, immunomodulation, promotion of angiogenesis and reepithelialization, and inhibition of scar-forming fibrosis.18 Despite their promising therapeutic potential, the clinical use of the amniotic membrane faces challenges such as bacterial and viral contamination, risk of disease transmission (e.g., HIV and hepatitis B and C), and storage and preservation difficulties.19 To address these concerns, the human acellular amniotic membrane (HAAM) has emerged as a promising solution.20,21 The HAAM is produced through decellularization techniques using physical, chemical, or enzymatic methods; the epithelial cellular components of the amnion can be removed while preserving the native ECM and bioactive factors, thereby substantially reducing the risk of immune rejection.22,23 Theoretically, the HAAM can modulate chronic inflammation and address healing deficits in difficult diabetic foot wounds, providing anti-inflammatory and tissue-regenerative benefits and making it an ideal therapeutic approach.24

However, the mechanisms governing cellular interactions and tissue repair in the wound microenvironment of DFUs are not well understood.25 DFUs have complex cellular composition and diverse molecular networks.26,27 However, whether the HAAM can efficaciously improve the chronic inflammatory microenvironment in diabetic wounds remains unclear, as does its mechanism of action. Thus, further investigation into the regulatory effects of the HAAM on the inflammatory microenvironment of diabetic foot wounds, particularly the functional roles of immune cell subclusters, is essential. High-throughput single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was, therefore, employed to explore the similarities and differences in the wound-healing microenvironment, focusing on the phenotypes and potential interactions between neutrophils and macrophages.

Results

Characteristics of the HAAM

As illustrated in Figures 1A and 1B, the HAAM is a semi-transparent membrane approximately 10 μm thick, with no visibly discernible cellular components. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) and optical profilometry (Figures 1C–1E) indicated that the membrane exhibits an aggregated morphology characterized by pronounced undulations in the central region and relatively smoother edges. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed ECM components and a collagen scaffold within the HAAM, with fibril diameters ranging from 80 to 100 nm (Figures 1F–1H).

The HAAM accelerates wound healing

We used a db/db type 2 diabetes mouse model (Figure 2A). The db/db mouse, carrying a homozygous mutation in the leptin receptor with normal leptin production, develops an obesity-associated diabetic phenotype characterized by hyperphagia, polydipsia, and polyuria.28 By photographing the dorsal wounds, the wound-healing process in each group of mice was monitored. The HAAM group exhibited superior recovery compared with the control group, with accelerated repair observed by day 7 and a smaller final unhealed area (7.39% vs. 11.3%) (Figures 2B and 2C). Throughout the entire 14-day experimental observation period, there was no significant statistical difference in the body weight changes observed between the HAAM treatment and the control groups at any time point (Figure 2D). To assess the dynamics of wound healing, we assessed reepithelialization by performing hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of wound tissues on day 14. Both groups exhibited a continuous epithelial layer (Figure 2E). The HAAM group had a mean wound diameter of 3.15 mm, whereas the control group exhibited slower reepithelialization with a mean wound diameter of 3.48 mm (Figure 2F). Next, collagen deposition and arrangement were examined to elucidate the wound-healing effect. Masson’s trichrome staining revealed denser collagen deposition with a more regular pattern in the HAAM group, with a mean tissue collagen content of 45.05%, significantly higher than that of the control group (28.32%) (Figures 2G and 2H). These results indicate that the HAAM promoted wound healing by accelerating reepithelialization and collagen deposition.

Single-cell sequencing during the wound healing process

After quality control, 92,323 high-quality cells were analyzed. Unbiased clustering through uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analyses was used to categorize the cells into clusters (Figure 3A). On the basis of previous literature and automatic annotation,26,28,29 we identified 11 cell types: melanocytes (Dct, Tyr, and Pmel), T cells (Cd3d, Cd3e, Cd4, and Cd8a), Schwann cells (Mbp and Kcna1), pericytes/smooth muscle cells (Rgs5, Pdgfrb, and Rgs4), neutrophils (S100a9 and S100a8), macrophages (Lyz2, Cd68, and Csf1r), lymphatic endothelial cells (Reln, Prox1, and Tbx1), keratinocytes (Krt14, Krt5, and Krt15), fibroblasts (Col1a2, Dcn, and Lum), endothelial cells (Pecam1, Cd34, and Vwf), and dendritic/Langerhans cells (Cd86, Cd207, and Ltc4s) (Figure 3B). UMAP plots illustrated the spatial expression patterns of each cell’s marker genes in single-cell transcriptomic data (Figure 3E). Furthermore, we compared the composition of different cell populations at different time points between the HAAM and the control groups (Figures 3C and 3D).

The HAAM mitigates excess inflammation by downregulating S100a9+ neutrophils in diabetic wounds

Compared with the control group, the HAAM group exhibited a higher proportion of neutrophils on day 7 (42.01% vs. 77.53%) but a lower proportion on day 14 (9.75% vs. 1.08%). This indicates that the HAAM rapidly promoted neutrophil recruitment during the inflammatory phase and facilitated their reduction during the remodeling phase, thus achieving an anti-inflammatory effect. Next, we clustered neutrophils on the basis of gene expression profiles and known markers and then divided them into four functional subgroups. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis for each subgroup identified characteristic genes (Figure 4A). In particular, cluster C1 was enriched for NET-related genes, including S100a9, S100a8, and Mpo. On the basis of the enrichment of Cxcl2, Tnfaip2, and Saa3, we classified C2 as a proinflammatory subgroup. High expression of Rsad2, Ifitm3, and Rnf213 led us to classify C3 as a lipid-associated subgroup. C4 (Mt) was identified as the mitochondrial electron transport chain subgroup, with representative markers including mt-Nd2, mt-Nd4l, and mt-Atp6 (Figure 4B).

Compared with the control group, the HAAM group exhibited the greatest decrease in the proportion of S100a9+ neutrophils (C1) between days 7 and 14 (Figure 4C). Pseudotime analysis indicated that Neu-C1-Net progresses through key branching nodes into the Neu-C2-Pro, Neu-C3-Lipid, and Neu-C4-Mt lineages (Figures 4D and 4E). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis indicated that S100a9+ neutrophils (C1) were primarily enriched in processes related to vesicular and endosomal/lysosomal trafficking and organization; this indicates that Neu-C1-Net shows a vesicle/lysosome-centered program consistent with active neutrophil effector functions and intracellular trafficking dynamics (Figure 4F). We subsequently examined the dynamic expression patterns of NET marker genes in the HAAM group. We found that S100a8/a9 expression was preferentially distributed at the beginning of the inferred pseudotime trajectory and gradually decreased thereafter, whereas Spp1 density and expression level increased toward the end of the trajectory (Figure 4G; Figure S1). Collectively, these data suggest that the HAAM reduced NETs and attenuated dysregulated inflammation.

Heterogeneity of macrophages in diabetic wounds modulated by the HAAM

Unsupervized re-clustering of macrophages revealed six macrophage subclusters, each marked by unique marker genes displayed in a heatmap (Figures 5A and 5B). C1 (Mertk+) was enriched for clearance-related markers, with elevated expression of Mertk, Snx24, and Ccnd3. C2 (M1-like) exhibited high levels of pro-inflammatory factors, including Il1b, Il1a, and Tnf. C3 (M2-like) was enriched for chemotactic and repair-associated molecules, such as Ccl7, Ccl2, and Gas7. C4 (Fibro-like) represented fibroblast-like macrophages, expressing Col1a1, Col1a2, and Col5a1. C5 (Cd163l1+) indicated an immunoregulatory/M2-biased profile, characterized by high Cd163l1 expression, along with genes related to epithelial barrier and environmental sensing (Cldn18 and Olfr323). C6 (mt-Nd2+) was closely associated with mitochondrial function/oxidative phosphorylation, with high expression of mitochondrial-encoded genes mt-Nd2, mt-Nd4, and mt-Nd3. At day 14 post-wounding, the percentage of M2 macrophages in the HAAM group showed a notable recovery to 2.99%, which was significantly higher than that of the control group (Figure 5C). Although UMAP highlighted the heterogeneity of wound macrophages, we further examined their common differentiation trajectories. Pseudotime ordering, combined with RNA velocity, indicated that most macrophages were distributed along a primary trajectory with two secondary branches (Figures 5D–5H; Figure S2). The subclusters were broadly distributed across pseudotime, with Mertk+ and M1-like cells spanning nearly the entire trajectory, while M2-like cells primarily accumulated at the end of the trajectory (Figure 5G). Furthermore, as illustrated in Figure S3, the expression of SPP1 was primarily localized within the M2-polarized macrophage population, suggesting a specific association with the reparative phenotype.

The HAAM enhances macrophage–neutrophil interactions to accelerate diabetic wound healing

From days 7 to 14, we observed a smaller increase in the proportion of macrophages and a greater decrease in NET-forming neutrophils in the HAAM group, suggesting potential intercellular communication between these populations. We used CellChat to analyze intercellular communication patterns among immune cells, focusing on macrophages and neutrophils (Figure 6A). The four-circle plots depict the overall number and strength of ligand-receptor interactions in both the HAAM and the control groups (day 7). In the control group, S100a9+ neutrophils exhibited the largest interaction magnitude, consistent with Figure 4C. Comparative analysis of specific interactions between S100a9+ neutrophils and macrophage subclusters on day 7 revealed enhanced neutrophil-macrophage interactions in the HAAM group through Spp1-CD44 binding (Figure 6B). Notably, Spp1 has been recognized as a key gene involved in NET activation.30,31 The co-localization of citrullinated histone H3 (Cit-H3) and MPO is a classical marker of NETs.32 Immunofluorescence staining detected lower co-expression of Cit-H3 and MPO in HAAM-treated wounds on day 14, indicating reduced NET formation (Figure 6C). According to the violin and the dot plots in Figure S4, SPP1 was predominantly expressed in Macro-C1 and Macro-C5, whereas CD44 upregulation was specific to Neu-C3. These findings align with our scRNA-seq observations and further support the beneficial role of the HAAM in mitigating NET-driven impairment of chronic diabetic wound repair.

Gene expression data strongly support histological observations. Using a library of the most relevant genes associated with wound healing, we observed that the HAAM significantly activated regenerative and proliferative programs (upregulation of Yap1/Mki67/Egr1). Similarly, genes involved in tissue regeneration, including Egr1 and Mfge8, were elevated. The expression levels of several key pro-angiogenic and pro-healing growth factors (Vegfa, Fgf2, and Egf) indicate enhanced cell proliferation and recruitment. Additionally, matrix metalloproteinases (MMP2 and MMP9), which play pivotal roles in ECM remodeling and endothelial cell recruitment for neovascularization, were significantly upregulated in response to HAAM treatment. We also observed increased expression of tissue inhibitors of metalloproteinase (TIMP) in HAAM-treated wounds on day 14. Given that TIMPs are major inhibitors of keratinocyte-derived MMPs, these findings indicate a homeostatic balance between MMPs and their inhibitors, reflecting active remodeling at a more mature stage of wound healing (Figure 6D).

Discussion

DFUs represent a global clinical challenge and place a severe burden on healthcare systems worldwide. Despite advances in chronic wound management, DFUs remain difficult to treat. Chronic wounds, including DFUs, are characterized by persistent inflammation, which hinders the transition to the proliferative and remodeling phases of healing. This study is the first to investigate the cellular mechanisms by which the HAAM promotes diabetic wound healing. We observed that the HAAM enhanced macrophage-neutrophil interactions in diabetic wounds, restricted NET-prone neutrophil subsets, and accelerated reepithelialization and collagen deposition. The adjunctive healing effect of this graft shows broad clinical application in repairing diabetic wounds.

After decellularization, the amniotic membrane retained ECM components, such as collagen, laminin, and fibronectin, which provided a three-dimensional scaffold favorable for cell adhesion, proliferation, and migration.33,34 Recent studies have demonstrated that using the HAAM as a scaffold to load or encapsulate extracellular vesicles, mesenchymal stem cells, and growth factors leads to more effective tissue repair and regeneration.35,36,37,38 However, most existing studies on the amniotic membrane have focused on macroscopic therapeutic effects and conventional histology. In contrast, our study used scRNA-seq to analyze cell subpopulations and lineage trajectories, offering higher-resolution mechanistic insights. Our research provides valuable experimental evidence for the application of the HAAM in diabetic wound healing.

Clinical studies, including a multicenter randomized controlled trial and an open-label study, have confirmed the efficacy and safety of decellularized amniotic membrane for treating refractory DFUs.39 The randomized trial demonstrated that the membrane significantly outperformed standard care, achieving greater rates of wound closure and area reduction at 12 weeks, along with a markedly lower incidence of serious adverse events.40 Furthermore, we plan to initiate clinical trials to facilitate its translation into clinical practice.

A key factor contributing to delayed healing in diabetic wounds is hypoxia at and insufficient blood supply to the wound site,41 largely due to ischemia resulting from angiopathy.42 Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a crucial cytokine for angiogenesis and is primarily secreted by local cells.43 However, hyperglycaemia-induced oxidative stress in diabetes significantly impairs these cells’ ability to produce VEGF.44 Davoodi et al. reported that the human amniotic membrane contains VEGF, with levels remaining relatively stable after decellularization.45 This finding aligns with our observations: the HAAM group exhibited higher vascular density than the control group.

The tissue healing process involves four stages—hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling46,47—involving the activity of multiple cell types.48,49 Our analysis of the immune cell landscape revealed that the HAAM group exhibited significantly fewer neutrophils than the control group on day 14. Activated neutrophils can release NETs,50,51 and excessive NETs may activate NF-κB through TLR-4/TLR-9, upregulating NLRP3 and pro-IL-1β and inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) to promote TXNIP-NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and IL-1β maturation and release.52 This cascade can perpetuate chronic inflammation and delay healing by activating macrophage NLRP3 inflammasomes.53 The interaction between NETs and macrophages may contribute to the persistent inflammatory microenvironment observed in diabetic wounds. NETs induce macrophage polarization toward a proinflammatory M1-like phenotype by activating the inflammasome and glycolytic pathways.54 Wong et al. demonstrated that degradation of NETs with DNase 1 significantly accelerates wound healing.55 Consistent with these findings, our data suggest that reduced NETs formation, and thus, reduced NET-macrophage interaction, promotes faster tissue repair in diabetic wound models.

Our findings further indicate that macrophages exist along a highly dynamic and interdependent spectrum of activation states rather than as distinct M1 or M2 subpopulations. The classical M1/M2 dichotomy, therefore, oversimplifies macrophage plasticity. Bill et al. demonstrated that compared with traditional M1/M2 macrophage markers, the CXCL9/SPP1 expression ratio more effectively distinguishes patients with cancer on the basis of clinical outcomes.56 Furthermore, the anti-inflammatory effects of the HAAM on macrophages observed in our study are supported by emerging evidence on the responses of other immune cells to the HAAM.57 Another study reported that conditioned medium derived from human amnion-derived mesenchymal stem cells promotes an anti-inflammatory macrophage phenotype, even under proinflammatory conditions.58 Osteopontin (SPP1) is a matricellular protein encoded by the SPP1 gene that promotes monocytes/macrophages recruitment and regulates cytokine production in macrophages, dendritic cells, and T cells. As a Th1 cytokine, SPP1 is thought to enhance inflammation in several chronic inflammatory diseases, including atherosclerosis.59 Unlike structural matrix proteins, matricellular proteins do not play a primary role in tissue architecture. They are primarily induced following tissue injury and regulate cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions. In addition to potentially enhancing macrophage proliferation, SPP1 also regulates fibroblast migration, proliferation, and activation.60,61 Thus, HAAM intervention accelerated wound healing by enhancing the protective SPP1-mediated intercellular communication; however, this requires further experimental validation.

Limitations of the study

First, wound healing is a complex process requiring coordination between fibroblasts, endothelial cells, pericytes, and various immune cell subsets. The study lacks in-depth characterization of the specific interactions among these cell types. Future studies should analyze key cell populations to elucidate the cellular behavioral changes involved in diabetic wound healing. Second, rigorous quality control and standardization for the HAAM are essential to manage batch-to-batch and source-related variability, including differences in donor age, processing methods, and decellularization and sterilization procedures, to ensure reproducibility and clinical applicability. Third, reliance on a single diabetic mouse model may be insufficient to accurately mimic the complex clinical microenvironment of DFUs, which are influenced by frequently coexisting factors such as peripheral neuropathy, impaired distal perfusion, infection and biofilm formation, and repetitive mechanical stress. Therefore, improving and diversifying animal models is crucial for advancing our understanding. Finally, although our single-cell transcriptomic data provided robust mechanistic insights, these findings require validation through extensive biological assays. Future studies must incorporate comprehensive functional and histological evaluations, including both in vitro cellular experiments and targeted in vivo protein validations, to fully substantiate the sequencing conclusions.

Experimental model and study participant details

Eight-week-old male db/db mice were used as a type 2 diabetes mouse model. The animals were purchased from Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharma Tech(Jiangsu, China). Mice were maintained under standard specific pathogen-free laboratory conditions with controlled temperature and humidity, a 12 h light/dark cycle, and free access to standard chow and water.All animal procedures complied with the requirements of the Experimental Animal Ethics Review Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital/HKUST Medical Center (approval number: 2024-105). Only male db/db mice were used in this study. Therefore, the potential influence of biological sex on wound healing outcomes and the response to treatment could not be fully evaluated. This represents a limitation of the present study, and future studies including both male and female mice will be required to determine whether sex influences the observed therapeutic and immunomodulatory effects.

Method details

Preparation of the HAAM

The HAAM was supplied by Chengdu Qingshan Likang Pharmaceutical and approved by the China Food and Drug Administration. The preparation method has been detailed in our previous study.65

Materials characterization and measurement methods

Surface morphology was characterised using a multiscale approach. Samples were sequentially ultrasonically cleaned in absolute ethanol and deionised water for 10 min each and then dried with nitrogen. Nonconductive samples were sputter coated with gold for approximately 20 s prior to SEM imaging. SEM images were acquired using a TESCAN Mira3 XMH field-emission scanning electron microscope under a high vacuum in secondary electron mode, employing an accelerating voltage of 5–10 kV and a working distance of 8–10 mm. Images were captured at magnifications ranging from approximately 400× to 100,000×, with scale bars included. AFM measurements were conducted with a Bruker Dimension ICON scanning probe microscope in ScanAsyst in Air mode, using SCANASYST-AIR probes (k ≈ 0.4 N/m, tip radius <10 nm). Height images were collected in air with a scan size of 2.0 μm × 2.0 μm, supplemented with 5–10 μm scans, at a resolution of 256 pixels and a scan rate of 1.0 Hz per line. Minimal plane and stripe corrections were applied. Parameters such as Ra, Rq, Rmax, and surface area ratio were calculated using Nanoscope Analysis software. Macroscopic surface profiles and roughness were measured using a ZYGO NewView 7200 optical profiler, equipped with a 50× objective and 2× eyepiece. Each sample was scanned three times to obtain Ra, RMS, PV, and three-dimensional surface images.

Diabetic mouse wound-healing model

Eight-week-old db/db mice (Jiangsu Huachuang Sino Pharma Tech) were used as a type 2 diabetes model. The dorsal areas of the mice were shaved the day before the procedure. On the day of the experiment, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and the skin was disinfected with povidone-iodine. A circular region approximately 1 cm in diameter was marked.

Mice were randomly divided into the HAAM and control groups (n = 6 per group at each time point), with the latter receiving routine gauze coverage. On Days 7 and 14, newly healed full-thickness skin tissue was collected from the wound area for single-cell sequencing (n = 2) and subsequent experiments (n = 3). A biopsy punch and sterile scalpel were used to create a full-thickness skin defect. Wound areas were measured using ImageJ, and the relative wound area was calculated using the following equation: relative wound area = wound area on a given day/wound area on Day 0. The investigators performing wound measurements and histological analysis were blinded to the group allocation.

Histological analysis and immunofluorescence staining

Three sets of skin samples were taken on postoperative Day 14 and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 1 day, followed by embedding in paraffin and sectioning into 6-μm-thick slices for histological staining. Reepithelialisation and collagen deposition were evaluated using H&E and Masson’s trichrome dye solutions, respectively. Additionally, the sections were stained with antibodies targeting MPO (Servicebio, GB11224, 1:5000), CD44 (Servicebio, GB112054, 1:3000), and CITH3 (Abcam, ab281584, 1:5000). Histological staining images were captured using an optical microscope (Leica DM4B), whereas immunofluorescence images for each section were obtained using a confocal microscope (Leica STELLARIS 5).

Cell preparation

After harvesting, skin tissues were washed in ice-cold RPMI1640 and dissociated using collagenase II (Sigma, V900892-100 MG), collagenase IV (Sigma, C5138-500 MG), and DNase I (Sigma 9003-98-9). Cell count and viability were assessed using a fluorescence cell analyser (Countstar Rigel S2) with AO/PI reagent after erythrocyte removal (Solarbio R1010). Subsequently, debris and dead cells were removed (Miltenyi 130-109-398/130-090-101) as required. Fresh cells were washed twice in RPMI1640 and resuspended at 1 × 106 cells per mL in 1× PBS and 0.04% bovine serum albumin.

Single cell RNA-seq library construction and sequencing

Single-cell RNA-seq libraries were prepared using the SeekOne Digital Droplet Single-Cell 3′ library preparation kit (SeekGene Catalog No. K00202). Briefly, an appropriate number of cells were mixed with reverse transcription reagent and added to the sample well in SeekOne chip. Barcoded hydrogel beads and partitioning oil were then dispensed separately into the corresponding wells of the chip. After generating emulsion droplets, reverse transcription was performed at 42°C for 90 min and subsequently inactivated at 80°C for 15 min cDNA was purified from the broken droplet and amplified using PCR. The amplified cDNA product was cleaned, fragmented, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to a sequencing adaptor. Indexed PCR amplification was conducted to amplify the DNA representing the 3′ polyA region of expressed genes, which also contained Cell Barcode and Unique Molecular Index. Indexed sequencing libraries were cleaned with SPRI beads and analyzed by Qubit (Thermo Fisher Q33226) and Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent G2991BA). The libraries were then sequenced on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with a PE150 read length.

Processing and analysis of single-cell sequencing data

Raw sequencing data were preprocessed using Fastp (v0.20.1) for primer trimming and removal of low-quality bases, and to gather initial statistical metrics. Alignment to the mm10 reference genome and gene expression quantification were performed using SeekSoulTools. Quality control postalignment involved filtering out cells that expressed fewer than 200 genes or genes detected in fewer than three cells. Cells with high mitochondrial gene (more than 7% of expressed mitochondrial genes) expression were excluded, and potential doublets were identified and removed using DoubletFinder (v2.0.3) with a threshold of 10%. The filtered datasets were then normalized and variance-stabilized using the SCTransform algorithm. Dimensionality reduction was first performed using principal component analysis (PCA), retaining 30 components. To eliminate technical batch effects and integrate datasets across samples, the Harmony algorithm was then applied to these principal components. Finally, clustering and visualisation were performed using UMAP based on the Harmony-corrected principal components.

Cell clustering and visualization

Cell clustering was performed by constructing a K-nearest neighbor graph based on a Euclidean distance matrix within the PCA space. This graph was then converted into a shared nearest neighbor graph to detect densely connected cell communities. The Louvain algorithm was used to cluster the cells, aiming to maximise modularity. For data visualisation, we applied the UMAP technique to the cell loadings of selected principal components, integrating the cluster assignments obtained from the graph-based clustering process.

Cellular trajectory and RNA velocity analysis

Pseudotime trajectory analysis was conducted using the DDRTree algorithm implemented in the Monocle (v2.32.0) software package. The resulting trajectory was represented as a tree-like structure composed of tips and branches. Log-normalized data from the Seurat object were imported into Monocle, where gene expression profiles were analyzed to identify genes with distinct expression patterns across cell groups and to evaluate the statistical significance of these observations. The cells were ordered along the trajectory and visualised in a lower-dimensional space. Pseudotime analysis focused on genes correlated with similar expression trends. Branch points were identified to highlight genes driving developmental trajectory branches. Differential gene testing was employed to filter out genes dependent on pseudotime or branch points. Notably, branch-dependent genes were visualised using the plot-genes branched-heatmap function. For vector-based RNA velocity analysis, a newly developed R package called scTour (v1.0.0) was used to visualise the trajectory vector trend.

Analysis of cell–cell communication

The CellChat (v2.1.2) R package was used to analyze intercellular communication between identified cell types. Individual CellChat objects were generated for each condition and subsequently merged using the mergeCellChat function. Interaction counts and strengths were evaluated with the compareInteractions function, and differences in interaction number and strength among cell populations across the two datasets were visualised in a heatmap generated by the netVisual_heatmap function. Signaling pathway distances were calculated using the rankSimilarity function. Significant alterations in signal roles for each cell type were identified through the netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter function. Specific signaling changes in certain cell populations were further examined using the netAnalysis_signalingChanges_scatter function. Finally, the communication dynamics of individual signaling pathways were compared using the netVisual_aggregate function.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data processing was performed in R using the Seurat package’s corresponding functions with default parameters. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. p values were calculated using one-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post hoc multiple comparison testing. A single asterisk (∗) denotes p < 0.05, two asterisks (∗∗) denote p < 0.01, and three asterisks (∗∗∗) denote p < 0.001. All statistical analyses were conducted using Excel and GraphPad Prism. The statistical details of the experiments, including the statistical tests used, the exact values of n, and what n represents can be found described in detail in the corresponding subsections of the method details section.
