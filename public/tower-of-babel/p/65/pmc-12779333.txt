A critical review of high‐frequency activity for functional mapping in SEEG
Authors: Parveen Sagar, Matt Hudson, Genevieve Rayner, Terence J O'Brien, Joshua Laing, Andrew Neal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMapping functional brain networks is a critical component of stereo–electroencephalography (SEEG) evaluations. Although direct cortical stimulation (DCS) is the clinical gold standard, it has important limitations—particularly in mapping distributed, complex functions such as language and memory, where deficits may still occur despite preservation of DCS‐positive sites, impacting quality of life. More broadly, there is increasing emphasis on preserving cognitive function in epilepsy surgery. The growing use of minimally invasive treatments—such as laser interstitial thermal therapy and radiofrequency thermocoagulation—reflects this shift. This trend highlights a need for adjunctive mapping techniques that provide anatomically precise and clinically relevant information about cortical functions, complementing existing mapping methods. Task‐induced high‐frequency activity (HFA), broadly defined as neural activity above 30 Hz, reflects localized cortical processing and has emerged as a physiologically plausible signal for functional mapping. As an activation‐based technique, HFA may complement DCS by providing additional information about cortical function to inform surgical planning and outcome prediction. Despite growing interest, clinical uptake of HFA mapping in SEEG remains limited. This likely reflects methodological heterogeneity across published studies, lack of standardized analysis pipelines, and limited validation through outcome‐linked research. Together, these challenges create uncertainty around the clinical utility of HFA. In this narrative review, we describe the neurophysiological basis of HFA, review key mapping findings across functional domains, outline practical requirements for clinical implementation, and examine major barriers to clinical translation. In doing so, we aim to provide clarity on the current evidence base and identify opportunities for future research that may support the integration of HFA mapping into clinical workflows.Keywords: direct cortical stimulation, epilepsy surgery, functional mapping, high‐frequency activity, presurgical evaluation, stereo‐EEG

INTRODUCTION

Successful epilepsy surgery requires not only the identification and removal of the epileptogenic zone (EZ), but also the preservation of eloquent cortex to avoid post‐surgical neurological or cognitive deficits. Both the EZ and eloquent cortex can be understood as networks, the former comprising regions involved in seizure generation and propagation 1 and the latter encompassing nodes critical for functional integrity. 2

To map epileptic and functional networks, intracranial electroencephalography (iEEG) may be required. 3 There are two types of extra‐operative intracranial EEG techniques, stereo‐encephalography (SEEG) and subdural grid‐based electrocorticography (ECoG). SEEG is increasingly the favored approach due to its minimally invasive nature and ability to sample deep structures. 4 The current gold‐standard for functional mapping with both types of iEEG is direct cortical stimulation (DCS), an inactivation based “pseudo‐lesioning” technique. However, despite the use of DCS, the risk of unexpected post‐operative deficits is not negligible, in part due to false negatives. 5 , 6 Additional limitations, such as labor intensiveness and difficulties in pediatric populations, and its inherently localizationist (as opposed to network‐based) approach to functional mapping, highlight the need for adjunctive mapping techniques. 7

High‐frequency activity (HFA) mapping has emerged as a promising activation‐based alternative, 8 , 9 physiologically analogous to the blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal on functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI). It involves signal processing of raw SEEG to identify task‐related increases in spectral power, typically above 30 Hz, and in doing so infers a functional map. In the ECoG context, there have been attempts to clinically validate HFA mapping for language through comparison with stimulation mapping and post‐operative outcomes. 10 , 11 In SEEG, however, the clinical evidence remains limited. Early findings suggest that HFA may be best used to refine or triage DCS testing, rather than to replace it.

As interest in HFA mapping grows, it is important to distinguish HFA from high‐frequency oscillations (HFOs), as the two phenomena are often conflated in the epilepsy literature. HFA refers to broadband, task‐induced increases in spectral power, whereas HFOs are spontaneous, short‐duration, narrowband events that are often associated with epileptic cortex. 12 Clarifying this distinction is essential for interpreting HFA mapping data, particularly in epileptic tissue, where such mapping may help guide clinical decision‐making.

The growing adoption of minimally invasive treatments—such as laser interstitial thermal therapy and radiofrequency thermocoagulation—reflects a shift in surgical philosophy toward greater emphasis on functional preservation. This includes not only avoiding overt neurological deficits, but also mitigating higher‐order cognitive impairments that are not readily detected by existing mapping techniques, yet significantly impact post‐operative quality of life. 13 In this context, there is a demand for adjunctive mapping tools capable of providing spatially specific, clinically actionable, and non‐redundant information to supplement current mapping methods and guide surgical decision‐making.

In this narrative review, we critically evaluate the current state of HFA mapping in the context of SEEG, with a focus on its potential role in clinical functional mapping, particularly as an adjunct to DCS. We also identify key challenges that must be addressed to support clinical translation and highlight opportunities for future research. Although research applications are diverse, they are beyond the scope of this review.

The review is structured into four sections: (1) foundational principles of HFA mapping, (2) practical considerations for its use in SEEG, (3) synthesis of existing SEEG‐based HFA literature, and (4) barriers to implementation and future directions.

SEARCH STRATEGY AND SELECTION CRITERIA

We conducted a targeted literature search in PubMed (MEDLINE) to identify studies that examined task‐induced HFA during iEEG for functional mapping. Search terms included combinations of “SEEG”, “EEG”, “stereo‐EEG”, “stereoencephalography”, “ECoG”, “electrocorticography”, “gamma”, “high‐frequency activity”, “broadband”, “task”, and “functional mapping”, using Boolean operators. We included peer‐reviewed English‐language studies published between 1995 and 2025, that investigated task‐related HFA responses in the context of localizing neurologic or neurocognitive functions. Studies were excluded if they did not involve human recordings, focused solely on resting‐state activity, or lacked clear relevance to clinical functional mapping applications. Additional relevant studies were identified by reviewing the reference lists of included papers and relevant prior reviews. This review is intended as a focused overview rather than a systematic review.

SECTION 1: FOUNDATIONS OF HIGH‐FREQUENCY ACTIVITY MAPPING

Background

EEG waveforms have traditionally been categorized into delta, theta, alpha, and beta bands (.5–30 Hz). 14 Hans Berger first described the alpha rhythm and its attenuation with eye opening, linking EEG activity to brain state. 15 HFA, above 30 Hz, within the so‐called “gamma band,” was recognized in early EEG studies, but gained significance following the discovery of 40 Hz synchrony in the cat visual cortex during perceptual binding tasks. 16 This led to the formulation of the binding‐by‐synchrony hypothesis, 17 which led to human scalp EEG studies of gamma‐band responses during perception and attention 18 (see Ref 7 for details). 7

iEEG evidence subsequently provided compelling evidence that task‐related increases in HFA reflect localized cortical processing. Crone et al. 19 demonstrated task‐induced broadband HFA in the sensorimotor cortex, with their later studies showing similar activity during auditory perception and production. 20 , 21 Lachaux et al. 22 extended this work to the visual system. Combined, their work revealed that HFA responses were not limited to narrow‐band ~40 Hz synchrony, but spanned a higher and broader frequency range, without a spectral peak. 22 , 23 These broadband HFA responses were both anatomically specific and temporally aligned with task performance. Numerous groups have since demonstrated HFA across a wide range of cognitive domains including memory, attention, emotion, audition, and language. 7 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32

HFA is broadly defined as spectral activity above 30 Hz, and includes a range of signal types that differ both functionally and mechanistically, including narrowband rhythmic and broadband aperiodic components. 7 However, frequency boundaries for gamma and HFA are inconsistently defined across studies. Narrowband rhythmic activity—often observed in the 30–70 Hz range—has been linked to inter‐regional coordination and attentional binding. 33 , 34 In contrast, broadband aperiodic activity, typically observed above ~70 Hz, lacks a clear spectral peak and is associated with local neuronal processing and increased population spiking. 33 Increases in broadband HFA are associated with increased population spiking activity rates. 35 , 36 , 37

Functionally, HFA is considered a reliable marker of local neuronal activation and correlates closely with the BOLD signal in fMRI. 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 , 43 , 44 , 45 , 46 However, it offers higher spatial and temporal resolution, making it a valuable tool for intracranial functional mapping.

A common source of confusion in the iEEG literature is the overlapping and sometimes inconsistent use of the terms HFA and HFO. HFA typically refers to task‐induced, broadband power increases that are time locked to cognitive or sensorimotor tasks. In contrast, HFOs are brief, transient narrowband oscillatory events typically classified as ripples (80–250 Hz) or fast ripples (>250 Hz). HFOs can be further categorized as either physiological or pathological. Physiological HFOs, such as hippocampal ripples during memory consolidation, can be observed in healthy brain function. 47 In contrast, pathological HFOs—particularly fast ripples—are strongly associated with epileptogenic cortex and have been proposed as biomarkers of the EZ. 48 , 49 Of note, these categories have sometimes been blurred in the literature. In particular, what were historically termed “physiological HFOs” may more accurately correspond to broadband, non‐oscillatory HFA. Liu and Parvizi 12 directly addressed this ambiguity by showing that spontaneous epileptic HFOs can transiently impair cognitive function, whereas the so‐called “physiological HFOs” seen during task execution lack the spectral and temporal features of genuine oscillations. Their findings emphasize the need for spectral analysis and cautious interpretation when distinguishing between pathological HFOs and functionally relevant HFA.

For the purpose of this review, we define task‐related HFA as any transient increase in spectral power above 30 Hz, occurring in response to task execution. The lower bound of 30 Hz was selected pragmatically to include frequency ranges most commonly examined in studies that examine HFA for functional mapping. Here, “HFA mapping” refers to the identification of functionally relevant brain regions through task‐induced increases in HFA.

Functional mapping prior to epilepsy surgery

For functional mapping, diverse ontologies have been created that suit the purposes of the discipline studying the structure—function relationships of the brain. 50 In the context of epilepsy surgery, the functions that are mapped are driven pragmatically by the need to optimize neurological and neurocognitive outcomes after epilepsy surgery. For neurocognitive outcomes, the field of neuropsychology (NP) provides the ontological framework to map clinically relevant brain functions with methods such as DCS, intracarotid amobarbital procedure (IAP), fMRI and HFA. Examples of functional constructs in the NP ontology that may be targeted for mapping include ‘naming’, ‘attention’, ‘verbal memory’, and ‘visual memory’. Activation‐ vs inactivation‐based brain mapping

Functional mapping techniques can be classified broadly into inactivation‐based approaches—such as DCS and the intracarotid amobarbital procedure (IAP)—and activation‐based approaches, including fMRI and HFA mapping. Inactivation methods infer function by transiently disrupting local brain activity and observing behavioral impairments, thus providing causal evidence for the necessity of a given brain region. 52 These pseudo‐lesioning techniques anticipate the creation of a surgical or ablative lesion, and, for that reason, yield information that is irreplaceable by activation‐based methods in presurgical planning. In contrast, activation methods detect task‐related signal increases and reflect cortical engagement, not indispensability. 53 This distinction is central to interpreting functional mapping results: activation implies involvement, but not necessity. Crucially, these approaches are not mutually exclusive—when combined, they offer complementary insights on brain function.

HFA mapping vs direct cortical stimulation

DCS is the gold‐standard for functional mapping. 52 Identification of primary cortices with DCS is reliable due to their predictable anatomic location and simple and reproducible behavioral effects observed upon stimulation. 54 However, DCS mapping of complex functions like language and memory can be challenging, as brain regions supporting these functions are organized across widely distributed brain networks and are variable between patients. 54 Stimulating brain regions in these networks may trigger variable or non‐specific manifestations. 3 , 55 , 56 In a survey, 41% of epilepsy centers reported at least one case of an adverse language outcome despite preserving all DCS‐mapped language‐positive cortex. 57 This suggests that DCS may miss functionally critical areas not captured by focal inactivation, as it does not capture network‐level organization. 58 In addition, in dysplastic or irritable cortex, stimulation may provoke afteedischarges or seizures, which can prevent inference of eloquence, since both outcomes reflect a ceiling effect, thereby limiting further assessment.

HFA mapping offers several complementary advantages. Because it involves post hoc analysis of task‐related SEEG recordings, it avoids stimulation altogether and is inherently safer—eliminating the risks of seizures, afterdischarges, and patient discomfort associated with DCS. 59 It can be more time efficient and less fatiguing for patients and clinicians alike, addressing common criticisms of DCS workflows. 60 Crucially, HFA mapping enables simultaneous assessment of task‐related activation across all implanted sites, providing a more comprehensive view of functional network engagement than the site‐by‐site testing of DCS. This broader network perspective can help clinicians assess both the extent and topology of functional activation when planning resections or ablations. A recent study demonstrates that resection of HFA‐positive, DCS‐negative language sites results in post‐operative cognitive deficits—supporting the clinical value of HFA mapping in contributing non‐redundant, functionally meaningful information. 61

Interpreting HFA in epileptic cortex

Given that the purpose of presurgical functional mapping is to understand the relationship between functional networks and the EZ, an important question arises: can epileptic tissue generate HFA, and is thus amenable to mapping? A recent study demonstrated that non‐lesional epileptic tissue is indeed capable of generating normal physiological HFA responses to relevant cognitive stimuli, such as memory cues in the hippocampus and visual cues in the occipital lobe. However, these responses may be suppressed when pathological epileptic activity, specifically HFOs, occur near stimulus onset, inducing a transient “cognitive refractory state.” 12 These findings support the use of HFA to map non‐lesional epileptic tissue, while also indicating that highly epileptogenic tissue may produce attenuated task‐related HFA, potentially reflecting impaired functional capacity.

Overview of the HFA mapping workflow

Ideally, to minimize cortical irritability, HFA mapping should be performed while the patient is on their full antiseizure medication regimen and not sleep deprived. This reduces the likelihood of artifact contamination by interictal discharges and improves mapping quality. A quiet, low‐stimulation environment also helps limit noise and maintain task specificity.

During the admission, the patient performs task paradigms that target clinically relevant functional networks. The SEEG data are then extracted and analyzed using TFR techniques, revealing channels with task‐induced power increases (Figure 1). Artifacts are inevitable and must be addressed through manual review or automated rejection methods. Patterns of co‐activation are used to infer a functional network map, which may inform DCS, surgical, or ablative strategy.

Paradigm selection is critical when mapping functional networks. Because both task‐based fMRI and HFA mapping are activation‐based techniques, established fMRI paradigms—and the principles guiding their design—provide a valuable foundation for developing HFA protocols. Block‐designed paradigms—featuring alternating active and rest conditions—are typically more feasible than methodologically demanding event‐related paradigms, particularly in populations with neurologic or cognitive impairments, as may be the case in people with epilepsy. 63 Tasks should be optimized not only for functional localization but also for reproducibility and integration into routine workflows. 64

In the presurgical mapping context, neurocognitive paradigms are best adapted from neuropsychological assessments with established validity across key domains. These include, “content validity,” which ensures that the task adequately targets the intended functional domain (for instance, language, or memory); “construct validity,” which requires theoretical and empirical support that the task reliably engages the targeted functional network; and “criterion validity,” which reflects the task's demonstrated association with clinically meaningful outcomes, such as post‐operative deficits. 65 These principles of validity should guide task selection for SEEG‐HFA mapping. Tasks with strong evidence from both research and clinical practice, particularly those validated for pre‐ and post‐surgical use, should be prioritized. Examples include visual naming for language, verbal paired associates for memory, and finger tapping for motor mapping.

Signal analysis methods

The raw SEEG signal must be submitted to signal‐processing techniques to reveal HFA. Commonly employed methods include “wavelet analysis,” the “Hilbert transform,” and power spectral density estimation. 66 , 67 These approaches convert the raw time‐domain signal into a time–frequency representation, enabling detection of transient increases in power associated with task execution.

Of these, wavelet analysis is among the most frequently employed, and it is described here as an example. It decomposes the signal into a series of frequency bands using wavelet functions, producing a time–frequency spectrogram. Power fluctuations within target frequency ranges (for instance, 30–150 Hz) can then be visualized and typically compared to a pre‐task baseline to identify significant activation. 59 A threshold is generally applied to determine whether a given channel exhibits task‐related HFA and is thus considered functionally positive. This approach allows characterization of the spatiotemporal dynamics of task‐related responses across implanted brain regions.

Beyond identifying the presence of activation, additional characteristics of the HFA response—such as inactivation, timing, frequency profile, magnitude, and spatiotemporal dynamics—may offer further insight into clinical relevance of HFA. 68 However, like activation itself, these features require clinical validation before they can be confidently used to infer functional indispensability.

Clinical validation of HFA maps

HFA mapping is a correlative approach: it identifies regions that co‐activate with task performance but does not confirm their necessity. 53 Task‐related activation may include regions that are not essential for function, meaning their resection may not result in a clinically relevant deficit. 69 That is, activation does not distinguish causation, compensation, or incidental involvement. This limitation underscores the need to validate HFA‐derived maps before they are used to guide clinical decision‐making. SECTION 3: REVIEW OF THE SEEG‐HFA MAPPING LITERATURE

This section reviews SEEG‐based studies investigating task‐induced HFA for presurgical functional mapping. We include studies targeting clinically relevant networks and incorporating comparisons with established mapping techniques such as DCS and fMRI, where applicable. ECoG‐HFA mapping literature is included where it provides additional insights. We excluded studies that focused solely on the spatiotemporal or spectral properties of HFA, and those that lacked a clear functional mapping aim or clinical relevance.

Language mapping using HFA

A meta‐analysis of 15 ECoG‐based studies reported a pooled sensitivity of .61, specificity of .79, and a diagnostic odds ratio (OR) of 6.44 for HFA in identifying DCS‐positive language cortex. 10 These metrics serve as benchmarks for HFA mapping in the SEEG context. In contrast, only three SEEG‐based studies have evaluated HFA for presurgical language mapping with clinical comparators such as DCS or fMRI. 68 , 70 , 71

Reported diagnostic performance varied across the three SEEG studies. Cuisenier et al. found low sensitivity (8.9%) but high specificity (92.4%) for HFA in detecting DCS‐positive language sites. Ervin et al. used a region‐wise statistical model trained on HFA features and compared predictions to both DCS and population level fMRI‐derived language parcels. Their model showed high specificity (74%) but no better than chance sensitivity when predicting DCS sites. Both studies reported high negative predictive values—95.9% and 89%‐‐and suggested that HFA could assist in ruling out eloquent cortex. However, these conclusions are limited by imbalanced datasets with relatively few language‐positive regions. Moreover, despite the high negative predictive value, the low sensitivity substantially limits the reliability of HFA as a rule‐out tool, since many eloquent regions may not show HFA. Sensitivity improved to 66% when fMRI was used as the reference standard in the analysis by Ervin et al., likely reflecting greater physiological concordance between two activation‐based methods. Jensen et al. reported 96% concordance between HFA and DCS in pediatric patients but did not provide formal diagnostic metrics, and it was not stated whether concordance was calculated across all implanted sites or only those also tested with DCS.

Overall, in the SEEG context, the sensitivity of HFA for detecting language‐positive sites is not definitively established but is likely limited given the available evidence. This may be unexpected given that HFA is typically associated with distributed cortical engagement during language tasks, 72 so higher overlap with DCS‐positive language sites may be expected. In the ECoG context, sensitivity measures vary widely, .22–.99, but the pooled estimate is still only moderate. 60 Several factors may contribute to the observed discrepancy. First, methodological differences—such as conservative statistical thresholds, region‐wise rather than contact‐level analyses, limited trial numbers, and mismatched task paradigms between HFA mapping and DCS—may reduce sensitivity by suppressing true activations or by engaging non‐overlapping language sub‐networks. Second, DCS itself may elicit network‐level responses or false positives due to current spread, 56 which may not align with the spatially focal signal profile of HFA, typically observed at the millimeter scale. 7 , 19 , 73

All three studies demonstrate methodological differences. Each employed distinct preprocessing pipelines, frequency ranges, and activation criteria (see Table S1). The language tasks also varied—each likely recruiting different language sub‐networks. This diversity in analytic and experimental design reflects the absence of standardized protocols and makes it challenging to compare or generalize findings. In a meta‐analysis of ECoG‐based language mapping by Arya et al., 60 only 2 of 10 studies used the same task for both DCS and HFA mapping. Without consistent baselining, thresholding, or task structures, reported performance may reflect methodological artifacts as much as true language‐related activation, thereby limiting construct validity.

The clinical applicability of these findings is also constrained by the fact that two of the three studies derived diagnostic metrics solely from DCS‐stimulated sites. 68 , 70 This introduces selection bias: only regions already suspected to be eloquent are included, excluding HFA‐positive but unstimulated sites from analysis. This limitation is not unique to SEEG; ECoG‐HFA mapping studies provide precedent. 60 , 74 , 75 As a result, the diagnostic performance of HFA mapping has been assessed in only a subset of the implanted brain, and the validity of the full HFA map is untested.

Overall, the SEEG‐HFA language mapping literature suggests that the utility of HFA may lie in identifying language‐positive sites to help prioritize channels for DCS, potentially improving mapping efficiency. However, given the limited number of studies and the variability in methodologies, further validation is required before this approach can be broadly implemented.

Memory mapping with HFA

Memory deficits following epilepsy surgery, particularly after resection in the language‐dominant temporal lobe, are well documented. 76 Whereas the IAP was previously considered the gold standard for predicting memory risk, advances in structural and fMRI imaging have largely superseded it. 76 , 77 The use of memory fMRI to predict post‐operative changes in memory function remains primarily in the research domain, although it shows promise. 78 , 79 There is class B evidence for fMRI in predicting verbal memory outcomes using encoding or semantic decision tasks, and class C evidence for visuospatial memory using face recognition tasks. 80 Memory mapping with DCS is performed at some centers, but its clinical utility is limited by methodological inconsistencies and a sparse outcome‐based evidence base. 54 , 81

In contrast to language mapping, no SEEG studies to date have directly validated memory‐related HFA in a presurgical context. Specifically, no studies have compared HFA‐based memory mapping against gold‐standard measures such as the IAP or post‐operative neuropsychological outcomes. Nevertheless, several SEEG and ECoG studies have characterized the anatomic correlates of memory using HFA, with paradigms adapted from validated neuropsychological instruments targeting memory function.

Tan et al. 82 conducted an SEEG study in 100 patients performing a free‐recall memory task. Using Morlet wavelet analysis (44–100 Hz) across nine regions of interest, they identified a “core retrieval network” involving bilateral posterior hippocampi, right angular gyrus, bilateral precuneus, and right posterior lateral temporal cortex—regions broadly consistent with resting‐state memory networks identified via fMRI. 83 Erroneous retrieval was associated with decreased HFA in the left angular gyrus, whereas HFA in the lateral prefrontal cortex increased across retrieval conditions.

Another SEEG study examined spectral responses during the execution of two standard visuo‐spatial memory tasks—the Rey Complex Figure Test and the Corsi Block‐Tapping Test—in eight patients. 84 , 85 The authors observed greater HFA in anterior hippocampus during task performance, suggesting a spatial specialization within medial temporal lobe (MTL) subregions.

Several ECoG studies offer further insights. Greenberg et al. 86 reported increased HFA (45–95 Hz) in bilateral mesial temporal lobe (MTL), left frontal cortex, and visual areas during successful encoding of paired associates. Similarly, Burke et al. 87 demonstrated that during verbal free recall, HFA was observed in the left inferior frontal gyrus, posterior parietal cortex, ventrolateral temporal cortex, bilateral MTLs, fusiform, and visual cortex—regions that closely match fMRI BOLD activation patterns for memory. A related study found that left MTL HFA predicted recall performance during spontaneous episodic retrieval. 88

Collectively, these studies suggest that HFA reliably tracks memory‐related activity in biologically plausible brain regions. Although formal clinical validation is lacking, these findings offer empirical support that task‐induced HFA is both spatially specific and, in some cases, lateralized, particularly within the MTL. Its potential utility for localizing and lateralizing memory function in temporal lobe epilepsy is therefore plausible, although further validation is required. Given the clinical relevance of verbal memory lateralization in TLE, further research is warranted to determine whether HFA can assist in functional risk stratification.

Sensorimotor mapping with HFA

The first iEEG observations of HFA in humans were reported by Crone et al. 19 Since then, other ECoG studies confirmed HFA when subjects perform a motor task, 36 , 58 , 89 , 90 and some studies have validated these maps against DCS. 58 , 89 , 90 Compared with DCS, HFA was found to be sensitive, specific and time‐efficient in the mapping of the motor cortex. Leuthardt reported sensitivity of 73%–89%, and Qian reported sensitivity of 67%; specificity in the respective studies was higher at 92%–95% and 97%. 89 , 90 Given methodological similarities, these studies affirm the potential for using HFA in the SEEG setting for sensorimotor mapping. Two studies in the SEEG setting exist.

A study of eight patients provided early SEEG evidence for HFA mapping of the motor cortex. 91 HFA (30–60 Hz) was recorded during brief finger‐extension tasks, primarily in the hand motor area, but also in the premotor and supplementary motor regions. A high topographic concordance with DCS was reported. However, no formal diagnostic metrics were provided, and analyses relied on visual inspection without individualized statistical thresholds. The narrow frequency range and small sample size further limit generalizability.

A more recent study investigated HFA to map the motor cortex in 20 SEEG patients. 71 Testing paradigm involved brief hand and arm movements, and HFA was compared against DCS. HFA responses were identified in the primary motor cortex, premotor cortex, and supplementary motor area. The authors reported 99% concordance between HFA and DCS‐defined motor cortex. However, no formal diagnostic metrics such as sensitivity or specificity were provided, and it was unclear whether concordance was calculated across all implanted sites or restricted to DCS‐positive contacts, thereby limiting clinical interpretability.

SECTION 4: BARRIERS TO CLINICAL IMPLEMENTATION AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The clinical use of HFA mapping is limited by several methodological and logistical barriers. Addressing these barriers will help establish the clinical role and integration of this mapping method.

Thresholding and interpretation

The lack of consensus on activation thresholds is an important barrier to clinical translation. Both the SEEG and ECoG literature demonstrate wide variability in the determination of HFA positivity. Some studies apply contact‐wise statistical cutoffs such as Z‐scores or p‐values, 71 , 92 , 93 whereas others use percentage‐based increases above baseline. 75 Additional methods include requiring a minimum number of consecutive time–frequency bins within defined spectral and temporal windows, 94 or using non‐parametric tests corrected for multiple comparisons. In some cases, receiver‐operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis has been used to derive thresholds predictive of DCS outcomes, rather than applying fixed statistical criteria. 70 This methodological heterogeneity complicates reproducibility, hinders cross‐study comparisons, and creates uncertainty within the field—contributing to a lack of confidence in HFA mapping results for clinical purposes.

The clinical fMRI field faces analogous challenges. Despite its more mature analytic framework, standardization efforts have not led to convergence on a single thresholding strategy. 95 , 96 Indeed, threshold selection in fMRI remains variable across studies, despite field‐wide recommendations. 95 In the SEEG context, variability in baseline activity—whether due to pathology, such as focal cortical dysplasia, 97 or regional physiology—may influence the detection of task‐related HFA. Although baseline correction mitigates some of this variability, such differences may still affect threshold calibration, so region‐ or pathology‐specific thresholds may be required.

Although methodological standardization would be the ideal, a more realistic goal may be the promotion of methodological transparency, threshold calibration tailored to the specific clinical context (for instance, balancing sensitivity vs specificity), and the dual reporting of both effect size and statistical confidence—rather than the enforcement of a single analytic standard.

Signal processing and workflow complexity

Current HFA analysis pipelines are often bespoke, requiring custom implementation and substantial local expertise. This limits their scalability and makes routine clinical use difficult, where speed and automation are key. Although some centers have developed effective internal solutions, 98 no widely available, validated toolkits currently exist for clinical integration. Beyond thresholding, key signal processing steps—such as selection of frequency bands, baseline correction methods, and time–frequency decomposition techniques—also lack consensus, which creates uncertainty about how best to apply HFA mapping in clinical decision‐making.

An important step toward clinical translation will be the development of open‐source, user‐friendly toolkits that integrate standardized default pipelines with flexible, context‐sensitive parameter adjustments. Reducing complexity required for analysis will be essential to support implementation in settings with limited local expertise. Machine learning approaches are likely to play a key role—particularly for tasks like artifact rejection, which, if performed manually, are prohibitively time‐consuming in time‐constrained clinical environments.

Establishing the clinical relevance of HFA maps

Clinical validation of HFA maps in the SEEG context remains limited, with few studies comparing them against the gold standard of DCS 68 , 70 or fMRI. 68 Moreover, robust data linking HFA maps to post‐operative outcomes is sparse in the SEEG context, although what is emerging is promising. 61 ECoG HFA data provide additional support for post‐operative outcomes highlighting the clinical relevance of HFA activity. 11 , 99 , 100 Without robust clinical validation, the interpretation of SEEG HFA‐positive regions remains potentially speculative—particularly when used to guide or defer resection.

To support clinical translation, future studies must demonstrate that decisions informed by HFA mapping improve post‐operative outcomes. Prospective, multi‐center studies incorporating standardized paradigms and reproducible signal analysis pipelines will aid in this. Such studies should explicitly assess whether preserving or resecting HFA‐positive regions influences neurological and cognitive function, and whether HFA provides additive value when used alongside DCS, fMRI, and the IAP. Such data will be key to establishing whether HFA can be reliably incorporated into presurgical workflows.

Potential clinical integration of HFA mapping

A pragmatic approach to clinical integration may involve sequentially mapping relevant cortical functions within a single SEEG admission. The goal is to complement existing methods and reduce the risk of post‐operative neurological and cognitive deficits. HFA mapping can be applied selectively, based on clinical priorities, and in some cases even in real time, as demonstrated by Lachaux et al. 101

When used alongside DCS, HFA results may help prioritize which sites to stimulate, reducing unnecessary testing—a strategy already discussed in language‐mapping studies. 10 , 70 Even without DCS, HFA maps may support surgical planning and pre‐operative counseling by providing individualized functional information, but only if future studies confirm their clinical relevance. With appropriate integration into hospital workflows, technicians—not just neurologists—may be able to perform the mapping, improving efficiency and patient comfort while lowering costs.

CONCLUSION

HFA mapping during SEEG is an emerging technique that shows promise as an adjunct to established methods such as DCS. Although DCS remains the clinical gold standard, it has limitations—particularly for mapping distributed functions such as language and memory. HFA offers biologically plausible, task‐related signals that may help refine surgical risk stratification and guide resection or ablation strategies. However, the clinical significance of these signals is not yet fully established.

To become clinically useful, future research must prioritize the development of standardized task paradigms, reproducible analysis pipelines, and large‐scale validation studies linking HFA findings to functional outcomes. If these goals are met, HFA could become an important addition to the presurgical evaluation toolkit, informing surgical and ablative decisions and reducing the risk of functional deficits.
