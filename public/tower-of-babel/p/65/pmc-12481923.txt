Increased dose of adenosine and the relationship between the resting full-cycle ratio and fractional flow reserve
Authors: Christian A Christensen, Jens Trøan, Kirstine N Hansen, Manijeh Noori, Anders Junker, Karsten Veien, Martin K Christensen, Julia Ellert-Gregersen, Kristian Wachtell, Henrik S Hansen, Jens F Lassen, Diyako Qanie, Mikkel Hougaard, Lisette O Jensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Fractional flow reserve (FFR) and resting full-cycle ratio (RFR) have shown discordance.•FFR decreases with increased adenosine dose.•Increased adenosine doses results in a higher number FFR-positive lesions.•Microvascular function influence on FFR reduction with increased adenosine dose.•Area under the curve were similar for RFR compared to FFR140 and FFR200.Keywords: Adenosine, Fractional Flow Reserve, Microcirculation

Introduction

Angina pectoris can be caused by a stenosis in the coronary artery, microvascular dysfunction, or a combination of both [1]. In patients with intermediate epicardial stenosis, fractional flow reserve (FFR) can assess the severity of the stenosis. FFR is the ratio between the distal pressure to the stenosis and the aortic pressure during maximal hyperemia, and maximal hyperemia can be achieved through intravenous infusion of adenosine, although microvascular dysfunction may impair vasodilation [2].

Several non-hyperemic pressure ratios (NHPR) have been developed to circumvent the administration of adenosine, as adenosine increases procedure time, cost, and patient discomfort [3]. The instantaneous-wave-free ratio (iFR) is an NHPR measuring the pressure gradient across a given stenosis during the wave-free period of the diastole [4]. After it was shown that iFR was non-inferior to FFR, several other NHPRs have been developed and shown to be equal to iFR [5,6]. The resting full-cycle ratio (RFR) is a resting index measuring the absolute lowest distal pressure (Pd) to the aortic pressure (Pa) ratio (Pd/Pa) during the whole cardiac cycle, averaging over 5 heartbeats [7]. However, a 15–20 % diagnostic discrepancy between the NHPRs and FFR when assessing coronary lesions [8]. A recent meta-analysis showed an increase in five-year mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events when iFR-guided revascularization was compared to FFR [9].

The discrepancy between RFR and FFR may be related to the coronary microvascular dysfunction, and the current standard dose of 140 µg/kg/min of adenosine may not be sufficient to achieve true maximal hyperemia in patients with microvascular dysfunction [[10], [11], [12]]. A consequence of not achieving true maximal hyperemia could be that the variability caused by the microcirculation is not minimized when performing an FFR examination of the coronary vessels [[10], [11], [12]]. Thus, if true maximal hyperemia is not achieved, the measured FFR value could be overestimated. Previous studies have concluded that FFR values does not decrease when increasing adenosine dose however, these studies did not take into consideration the effect, the microcirculation might have had on FFR measurements and if the correlation between RFR and FFR consequently would improve when increasing the adenosine dose [[12], [13], [14]].

The microcirculation consists of arterioles with diameters of approximately less than 300 µm, which are subject to endothelial-dependent and independent vasodilators. Smoking, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes are all risk factors for endothelial dysfunction leading to microvascular disease [15,16]. The microvascular function can be assessed invasively as the index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR) [17].

This prospective study aimed to assess whether increasing the dose of adenosine during FFR measurement in patients with intermediate coronary stenosis would further reduce FFR, and if the discrepancy between RFR and FFR would improve.

Methods

The study was a single-center, prospective study conducted at the Department of Cardiology, Odense University Hospital. Patients were included if they had intermediate coronary artery stenosis identified during the coronary angiogram, as determined by a 50–70 % stenosis visually assessed by the operator. All patients gave written consent.

The assessment consisted of both physiological flow and pressure measurements with RFR without adenosine, FFR, IMR, and coronary flow reserve (CFR) at an infusion dose of 140 µg/kg/min of adenosine, followed by a repeat of FFR at an increased infusion dose of 200 µg/kg/min. Physiological measurements, such as RFR, were measured by the absolute lowest pressure distal to the stenosis (Pd) in relation to the aortic pressure (Pa, Pd/Pa) throughout the entire cardiac cycle, averaged over five consecutive heartbeats. A ratio ≤ 0.89 was defined as significant. FFR was calculated using the Pd/Pa ratio during a steady state of maximal hyperemia induced by intravenous adenosine infusion for two minutes. A partition value < 0.80 was deemed significant. IMR was the Pd during maximal hyperemia multiplied by the transient mean time (Tmn) during maximal hyperemia. IMR was calculated by Pd multiplied by Tmn during maximal hyperemia. An IMR ≥ 24 was deemed significant. Tmn was measured utilizing the thermodilution method described in detail below.

CFR was the ratio between non-hyperemic Tmn and hyperemic Tmn, where CFR = Tmnrest/Tmnhyperaemia. CFR < 2.0 was deemed significant.

Procedure

The procedure followed the hospital guidelines. Before the pressure and flow measurements, 5,000 IE of unfractionated heparin and nitroglycerine were administered. The Coroventis CoroFlow Cardiovascular System (Uppsala, Sweden) and the Pressurewire X from Abbott were used to perform the measurements. The pressure wire was equalized at the guiding catheter before advancing the wire distally to the stenosis.

RFR, along with the resting flow measurements, were completed. The infusion of adenosine 140 µg/kg/min was initiated via a peripheral vein, and FFR (FFR140) was determined. CFR and IMR were ascertained by applying the thermodilution method at a steady hyperaemic state during the 140 µg/kg/min adenosine infusion. The Tmn was utilized for the thermodilution method. Practically, 3 mL of saline water at room temperature was injected thrice into the coronary artery during resting and hyperemic settings. In both situations, 3 Tmns were averaged. IMR and CFR are then determined as mentioned above. The adenosine infusion dose was then increased to 200 µg/kg/min for 2 min, and FFR (FFR200) was re-evaluated. The patients were divided into those with microvascular dysfunction (IMR ≥ 24) and those without microvascular dysfunction (IMR < 24).

Statistical analysis

Categorical data were displayed as numbers and frequencies, and compared using Fisher's exact test or the Chi-squared test. Continuous data were presented as means and standard deviations (SD) and compared using Student's t-test. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, which showed a median of 25 % and a 75 % interquartile range (IQR). McNemar's test was used to determine whether the number of patients with an FFR-positive lesion increased significantly when the dose of adenosine was increased. Linear regression was used to assess correlation. Retriever operator curve (ROC) analysis was performed for the ability of RFR to predict a FFR of < 0.80 for adenosine doses of 140 µg/kg/min and 200 µg/kg/min, respectively. IBM SPSS version 29 was used to conduct the statistical analysis. The statistical program R (version 4.4.2) was utilized to calculate the confidence interval of the partition value for the ROC curves and to estimate the difference in the partition value. Net reclassification index (NRI) was used to quantify whether utilizing an adenosine dose of 200 µg/kg/min correctly classified more patients as having a significant or non-significant stenosis compared to a dose of 140 µg/kg/min. Integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) was used to assess if the increased dose of adenosine improved the discrimination ability of FFR. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Discussion

This study reports three novel observations. First, FFR decreases as the adenosine dose increases, resulting in a higher number of patients with FFR-positive lesions. Second, the FFR reduction is also observed in patients with both normal and impaired microvascular function, although the reduction in FFR with increased adenosine infusion was more pronounced in patients with microvascular dysfunction. Third, partition values and AUC were similar for RFR compared to FFR140 and FFR200, and were not influenced by microvascular function.

The fact that FFR was reduced further during increased doses of adenosine is consistent with previous studies where intracoronary boluses of adenosine infusion were utilized [11,13]. In the current study, approximately 12 % more patients would have a significant lesion if FFR200 were used instead of FFR140. In contrast to the current study, a previous study using intravenous adenosine showed that an increased dose of adenosine did not enhance hyperemia or reduce FFR values but was associated with increased patient discomfort [14]. The discrepancy between the two studies could be attributed to the smaller cohort of 75 patients in the previous study compared to the 200 patients in the current study, as well as a type II error. The difference between the two studies could also be due to differences in patient cohorts. The current study has a higher prevalence of patients with stable angina and a slightly lower prevalence of patients with non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina.

Role of microcirculation in FFR and RFR measurements

Microvascular dysfunction, characterized by impaired vasodilation due to endothelial dysfunction, increases microvascular resistance, potentially leading to overestimation of FFR measurements [16,18]. FFR measures the pressure across the stenosis during hyperemia when the resistance in the microcirculation is supposed to be constant due to sufficient vasodilation [10]. If the vascular bed is constricted due to insufficient vasodilation during adenosine infusion, hyperemia may be suboptimal. During suboptimal hyperemia, the microvascular resistance is not constant, thus affecting the pressure gradient across the coronary stenosis. As RFR measures the pressure drop during the cardiac cycle, microvascular dysfunction may have a reduced influence. One study found that microvascular dysfunction was an independent predictor of discordance between RFR and FFR, while another emphasized that although microcirculatory resistance does not primarily cause discordance, it remains an important element in flow and pressure measurements during hyperaemia [10,12]. If hyperemia is suboptimal, elevated microvascular resistance may result in an apparent reduction in the flow-limiting impact of a stenosis. The FFR values in our study were reduced more in the group with microvascular dysfunction, which could further indicate the importance of considering microvascular health when interpreting FFR values. Also, in patients with FFR negative lesions, the assessment of the microvascular function may find a treatable reason for the patients’ symptoms. An increased dose of adenosine may be needed to fully vasodilate the microcirculation, as a constricted vascular bed and limited dilation during hyperemia may lead to an underestimation of FFR values.

Relationship between RFR and FFR

Increased doses of adenosine infusion lowered FFR values yet did not alter the partition value of RFR to predict an FFR < 0.80, which in the presented study was 0.88 for both FFR140 and FFR200, close to the already established threshold of 0.89 [5,7]. However, more patients in the RFR-positive group had a previously FFR-negative lesion that became FFR-positive when the dose was increased. A hybrid approach has been proposed for borderline cases where NHPR values fall within a “grey zone” of 0.86–0.93. In such instances, FFR is preferred as a confirmatory guide for percutaneous coronary intervention. One study indicates that utilizing the hybrid approach increased the diagnostic accuracy of iFR to 95 % while decreasing the partition value of iFR to 0.86, which could reduce the need for confirmatory FFR assessments [19]. In the study presented, the 0.89 partitions remained within the confidence interval for IMR-separated groups. Altogether, the general consistency of FFR and RFR partition values across IMR variations supports previous study findings, strengthening the reliability of these measures and indicating that IMR does not substantially alter the diagnostic partition values for FFR or RFR [10].

The consistent AUC across IMR stratifications further validated the partition values, emphasizing the robustness of FFR and RFR, with studies showing minimal variability in diagnostic thresholds regardless of IMR differences [10]. This consistency supports standard partition values (FFR < 0.80 and RFR ≤ 0.89), even in patients with different microvascular profiles, simplifying decision-making in the clinical setting and standardizing diagnostic criteria across a broad patient population. The current study's correlation coefficients and partition values were similar to those of previous research and aligned across separation by IMR. Previous studies have shown that RFR and iFR maintain high correlations with FFR, demonstrating consistent diagnostic values [7,20].

Limitations

The study has limitations, including a small population size and the use of the thermodilution method for determining IMR and CFR, which is sensitive to the injection of saline into the coronary vessels. The study was not randomized and all patients had 140 μg/kg/min followed by 200 μg/kg/min. We did not do a washout, but the adenosine dose was increased after 2 min, when the IMR measurements were performed (after steady state).

Conclusion

An increased dose of adenosine significantly reduced FFR values in patients with and without microcirculation dysfunction. Although an increase in FFR positive lesions during increased adenosine dose, the correlation agreement between RFR and FFR remained.
