Improvement of heart rate variability after metabolic bariatric surgery in Korean subjects with obesity
Authors: Han Na Jang, Young Suk Park, Joon Ho Moon, Sung Hee Choi, Hak Chul Jang, Tae Jung Oh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionCardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN) is a chronic complication of diabetes. As obesity is a major risk factor for CAN, we hypothesized that metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) could improve CAN indices in Korean patients with obesity.Materials and MethodsPatients who underwent bariatric surgery between February 2020 and June 2022 were prospectively recruited. CAN was conducted once before surgery and again after surgery, using the Ewing method and heart rate variability (HRV) analysis (standard deviation of the NN interval [SDNN], root mean square of successive RR interval difference [RMSSD], and spectral analysis).ResultsA total of 47 patients were included. The mean age was 39.8 ± 8.7 years, 15 (31.9%) were male, and 26 (55.3%) had diabetes. Resting HR before surgery was 81.0 ± 12.3 bpm, which decreased significantly to 68.0 ± 9.3 bpm after surgery (P < 0.001). Changes in HR and BP according to the Valsalva maneuver, postural changes, and handgrip were not significantly different before and after surgery. However, SDNN significantly increased from 25.2 [15.1, 33.5] to 38.0 [25.4, 45.0] ms (P < 0.001), and RMSSD also significantly increased from 17.0 [9.2, 31.8] to 28.2 [15.3, 45.6] ms (P = 0.001). Both low‐frequency power (LF) and high‐frequency power (HF) increased significantly, and the LF/HF ratio significantly decreased from 2.1 ± 1.6 to 1.3 ± 1.3 (P = 0.010). Loss of weight, fat mass, and lean body mass were independently associated with improving the HRV variables.ConclusionsMBS improved HRV variables, and these changes were mainly associated with postoperative weight loss.Keywords: Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy, Heart rate variability, Metabolic bariatric surgery

INTRODUCTION

Cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy (CAN), a chronic complication of diabetes, is an abnormality in heart rate (HR) control and vascular dynamics caused by damage to the autonomic nerve fibers innervating the heart and blood vessels via various biochemical pathways caused by hyperglycemia 1 . In the early stages of clinical CAN, the loss of parasympathetic function causes a relatively increased sympathetic tone, resulting in resting tachycardia and exercise intolerance. In severe CAN, sympathetic denervation occurs, so HR does not change in response to sleep, stress, exercise, etc.; orthostatic hypotension also occurs and may result in nocturnal hypertension due to a paradoxical increase in sympathetic tone. Accordingly, CAN is associated with silent myocardial infarction, reduced left ventricular systolic and diastolic function, and fetal arrhythmias 2 , 3 . CAN also increased mortality independently of other cardiovascular risk factors, and a meta‐analysis including 19 studies revealed that the relative mortality risk of diabetic patients with CAN was significantly higher at 3.17 (95% confidence interval, 2.11–4.78) 4 , 5 . In this regard, early detection and prevention of CAN is crucial in the management of diabetes.

The Ewing test is the gold standard for diagnosing CAN. However, due to difficulties in performing the Ewing test, heart rate variability (HRV) and heart rate turbulence (HRT) can be used for assessing CAN 6 . HRV, which refers to variations in the period between consecutive heartbeats over time, is a measure of the balance of the autonomic nervous system that regulates cardiac activity (sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system). HRV tends to decrease not only in clinical CAN but also in subclinical CAN 7 .

Obesity has been identified as a strong risk factor for CAN in patients with type 1 diabetes and impaired glucose tolerance 8 , 9 . Metabolic bariatric surgery (MBS) not only reduces body weight, but also improves glycemic control in patients with type 2 diabetes with an improvement in insulin sensitivity 10 . A previous study showed that MBS, primarily including Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass, led to improvements in parasympathetic indicators of CAN among individuals with morbid obesity, whose BMI was 48 kg/m2 11 . In another study, it was reported that CAN‐related variables significantly improved 2 years after bariatric surgery in individuals with a BMI >35 kg/m2 12 . However, the impact of MBS on CAN remains unaddressed, particularly in subjects with less degree of obesity (BMI <35 kg/m2) who underwent other types of MBS such as sleeve gastrectomy, which is the most performed surgery in East Asia 13 . Therefore, in this study, we investigated the change of CAN using both Ewing test and HRV before and after MBS to identify the role of MBS in Korean subjects with a BMI not only ≥35 kg/m2 but also <35 kg/m2. We also discovered the clinical factors associated with changes of CAN‐related variables.

METHODS

Study participants

This study was conducted in a cohort of patients who underwent MBS at Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (SNUBH), a tertiary academic hospital (CRIS Registration No. KCT0005777). The cohort included patients aged ≥20 years, with a BMI ≥30.0 kg/m2 or ≥27.5 kg/m2 with poor glycemic control. The criterion for poor glycemic control was an HbA1c ≥7.0% despite standard medical care. Patients who had previously undergone MBS or did not meet the MBS indications in the Korean guidelines 13 were not included in the cohort. Among the included patients, those who underwent CAN evaluation before and after surgery were analyzed. Subjects were further analyzed by dividing them into baseline obesity severity (BMI <35 and ≥35 kg/m2) and diabetes status.

CAN evaluation

The Ewing test was performed, and HRV was measured (AFT‐800; MEDICORE Co., Ltd, Gyeonggi‐do, Korea). The guideline recommends that subjects remain fasted and avoid alcohol, caffeine, and nicotine for 12 h before the examination and perform the test after a 10‐min rest between 8:00 AM and 12:00 PM 14 . We followed this general recommendation, but the test was conducted at the time of visiting an outpatient clinic, so it was generally performed in the afternoon (preop 39 [83.0%], postop 33 [70.2%]). During the examination, electrocardiogram (ECG) leads were attached to the anterior wrists and ankles of the subject, and blood pressure cuff was placed on the left upper arm. HRV was measured with the subjects in a supine position, with eyes open and fixed at the ceiling, without moving or talking.

During the Ewing test, (1) HR response to deep breathing (E/I ratio): HR variation was measured by ECG, while the subject took six deep breaths per minute for 1 min; (2) HR response to standing up (30:15 ratio): after the subject sat up from a supine position, the beat‐to‐beat (R‐R) intervals of the 15th beat and the 30th beat were measured; (3) blood pressure (BP) response to standing up: the BP change in response to standing from a supine position was measured immediately after waking up, 1 and 3 min after waking up; (4) HR response to the Valsalva maneuver (Valsalva ratio): after inserting the mouthpiece into the Valsalva hose, the participant took a deep breath and then exhaled continuously for 15 s to maintain a pressure of 40 mmHg, and then, HR after maintaining a stable state for 20 s was measured; and (5) BP response to sustained handgrip: the systolic BP variation was recorded during grip strength maintained at 70% of maximum weak force. The test results were classified as normal, borderline, or abnormal as follows: (1) E/I ratio: the mean difference between the maximum and minimum HR during one breath is classified as normal when it is ≥15, borderline when it is 11–14, and abnormal when it is ≤10; (2) 30:15 ratio: the ratio of the R‐R interval between the 30th beat and the 15th beat is classified as normal when it is ≥1.04, borderline when it is 1.01–1.03, and abnormal when it is ≤1.00; (3) BP response to standing up: the difference between supine systolic BP and the lowest value among systolic BP measured immediately after waking up, 1 and 3 min after waking up is classified as normal when it is ≤10 mmHg, borderline when it is 11–19 mmHg, and abnormal when it is ≥20 mmHg; (4) Valsalva ratio: the ratio between the longest RR interval after discontinuation of the Valsalva maneuver and the shortest RR interval during the Valsalva maneuver is classified as normal when it is ≥1.21, borderline when it is 1.11–1.20, and abnormal when it is ≤1.10; and (5) BP response to sustained handgrip: the difference in diastolic BP before maintaining grip strength and while maintaining grip strength is classified as normal when it is ≥16 mmHg, borderline when it is 11–15 mmHg, and abnormal when it is ≤10 mmHg. One abnormal parasympathetic nerve test result was classified as early CAN, two or more abnormal parasympathetic nerve test results were classified as definite CAN, and orthostatic hypotension with parasympathetic abnormalities was classified as severe CAN 15 .

HRV was evaluated as a variable obtained through time‐domain analysis and frequency‐domain analysis (spectral analysis) of the R‐R interval obtained from continuous 5‐min ECG. The time‐domain parameters included the standard deviation of the NN intervals (SDNN) and the root mean square successive difference of the intervals (RMSSD). Spectral analysis involves decomposing a series of sequential R‐R intervals into a sum of sinusoidal functions of different amplitudes and frequencies through mathematical approaches such as fast Fourier transformation or autoregressive models. The results of the spectral analysis (power spectrum) can be expressed as the magnitude of variability as a function of frequency. The power spectrum of HRV consists of 3 major peaks: (1) very low‐frequency power (VLF, 0.01–0.04 Hz); (2) low‐frequency power (LF, 0.04–0.15 Hz); and (3) high‐frequency power (HF, 0.15–0.4 Hz). LF and HF were also expressed in normalized units (normalized LF and HF) to express the relative of each value to the total power (TP) 7 .

Anthropometric, body composition, and biochemical measurements

Trained research nurses measured the anthropometric indices. BMI was calculated by dividing the weight (kg) by height (m) squared. Waist circumference was measured at the midpoint of the margin of the lowest rib and iliac crest. BP was measured using an electronic BP monitor after resting for 10 min in a sitting position. Fat mass (FM), visceral fat area (VFA), and lean body mass (LBM) were measured using bioimpedance analysis (InBody770; InBody, Seoul, Korea).

Blood tests were performed after an overnight fast. HbA1c levels were measured using high‐performance liquid chromatography (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA), and fasting plasma glucose (FPG) levels were measured using the hexokinase method. Insulin levels were measured using an immunoradiometric assay (DIAsource, Nivelles, Belgium). Total cholesterol, triglyceride, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) levels were measured using enzymatic colorimetric assays. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation 16 . Homeostatic model assessment insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) was calculated by dividing the product of fasting insulin (μIU/mL) and FPG (mg/dL) by 405 17 .

Diabetic peripheral neuropathy was assessed with the Michigan Neuropathy Screening Instrument (MNSI), which consists of a 15‐item self‐administered questionnaire and comprehensive physical examination of appearance, presence of ulceration, ankle reflex, and vibration perception 18 .

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were changes in CAN and HRV variables after MBS. Secondary outcomes were clinical factors associated with improvements in these variables after MBS.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution are expressed as mean and standard deviation, and continuous variables not normally distributed are expressed as median [interquartile range]. Kolmogorov–Smirnov and Shapiro–Wilk tests were used to check the normal distribution of variables. Changes in continuous variables before and after MBS were compared using a paired t‐test for variables that followed a normal distribution and a Wilcoxon signed‐rank test for variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using the chi‐square test. Correlation analyses between improvements in CAN variables and clinical variables were performed using simple and multiple regression analyses. Covariates were age, sex, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, diabetes mellitus, HbA1c, body mass index, and insulin level. P values <0.05 were considered to indicate statistical significance. SPSS for Windows (version 27.0; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for the statistical analyses.

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The trial protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of SNUBH (No. B‐1812‐513‐302). All participants provided written informed consent.

RESULTS

Baseline and post‐MBS participant characteristics and clinical features

A total of 47 patients underwent MBS from February 2020 to June 2022 and were evaluated for CAN before and after MBS, with an average follow‐up duration of 312.1 ± 128.2 days (Table 1). Fifteen (31.9%) of the patients were male, and the average age was 39.8 ± 8.7 years. Twenty‐eight (59.6%) patients had hypertension, 26 (55.3%) had diabetes, and in patients with diabetes, the median duration of diabetes was 3.5 [1.0, 5.0] years. Eighteen patients (38.3%) had a MNSI‐Q score ≥3 points, and 4 (8.5%) had an MNSI‐PE score >2 points. Twenty‐eight (59.6%) patients underwent sleeve gastrectomy, ten (21.3%) underwent sleeve duodeno‐jejunostomy, and two (4.3%) underwent resectional gastric bypass.

Lipid profiles improved after surgery, with a decrease in triglyceride and LDL‐C levels and an increase in HDL‐C. AST and ALT levels decreased significantly, but there was no significant change in eGFR after surgery.

Regression analyses were conducted by dividing subjects into baseline obesity severity and diabetes status (Tables S5–S8). DISCUSSION

HRV variables significantly improved after MBS, along with improvements in obesity and metabolic parameters. Improvements in HRV variables were mostly associated with weight loss, and the benefit of MBS in terms of HRV was more prominently observed in subgroups with a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 or those without diabetes. However, there was no significant change in CAN, as evaluated by the Ewing test.

Obesity has been shown to be associated with sympathetic activation, particularly related to visceral adiposity 19 , 20 . Our study also demonstrated a strong positive correlation between reduction in weight or FM and decrease in HR, indicative of sympathetic tone 20 . As obesity is a major contributor to CAN, MBS, the most effective modality for controlling weight, could improve CAN and HRV variables. In addition, the improvement in HRV variables was significantly correlated with decreased body weight and FM. However, previous study has reported that when structured lifestyle interventions were applied to patients with metabolic syndrome, BMI decreased significantly, but CAN variables such as LF, HF, and E/I ratio did not change significantly 21 . In addition, in a study comparing dapagliflozin to sulfonylurea in patients with type 2 diabetes taking metformin, dapagliflozin significantly reduced body weight compared to sulfonylurea, but there were no differences in CAN indicators such as LF/HF ratio, SDNN, RMSSD, E/I ratio, average Valsalva ratio, and 30:15 ratio between groups 22 . In a study comparing exenatide and glargine in patients with type 2 diabetes, exenatide induced significant weight loss at 12 months, but there was no significant difference in HR, SDNN, RMSSD, LF/HF ratio, Valsalva ratio, or E/I ratio between the two drugs 23 . The reason why there was no significant improvement in HRV variables despite weight loss in previous studies is thought to be due to older age, shorter follow‐up duration, and longer duration of diabetes in participants of the previous studies than in those of our study. In addition, differences in the degree of weight loss appeared to have had an effect. In our study, there was a correlation between the degree of weight loss and improvement in the CAN variables. In other words, the greater the degree of weight loss, the greater the degree of improvement in CAN variables. The average body weight decreased of our study was around 26.2 [23.4, 29.0] kg approximately 1 year postoperatively. In contrast, non‐surgical interventions, such as lifestyle modifications or pharmacologic therapy, decrease body weight by as little as approximately 4 kg. This indicates that the strong weight loss effect of MBS may affect the improvement in CAN. In particular, the degree of weight loss at 3 months (19.0 ± 6.6 kg) after surgery was significantly correlated with HR, RMSSD, normalized LF and HF, and the LF/HF ratio changes. This suggests that weight loss in the early stage after surgery is an important factor in improving CAN.

Meanwhile, previous studies have reported that a loss of muscle mass is associated with low HRV 24 , and lower muscle mass is linked with poorer parasympathetic activity 25 . In the current study, however, the relationship between change of LBM and the changes of HRV was somewhat contrary to these earlier findings. We speculated that the weight loss itself might have a more significant impact on autonomic nerve function than changes in muscle mass. Further larger‐scale studies are needed to compare the effects of muscle preservation during weight loss on HRV.

In addition, we observed higher number of parameters improved after surgery in subgroups who did not have diabetes or with a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 compared to their counterparts. One possible reason can be inferred to be that the extent of weight loss is greater in subjects without diabetes or with a BMI ≥35 kg/m2 compared to their counterparts. Another reason to consider could be the reversibility of CAN might be expected in a shorter duration of diabetes or in non‐diabetic status, because the presence of diabetes (46.0% vs 68.4%) and HbA1c levels (6.7% vs 7.6%) were lower in subjects with BMI ≥35 kg/m2 compared to those with BMI <35 kg/m2. This explanation can be supported by a previous Danish study that enrolled newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, which showed preventing the progression of CAN under multifactorial treatment. Therefore, early intervention in subjects with relatively early stage of diabetes, and even prior to overt diabetes, can be crucial in managing the risk of CAN.

In this study, HRV variables showed significant improvement after MBS; however, there was no significant change in the results of the Ewing test or in the number of patients with abnormal CAN determined by the Ewing test. This may be due to the unique nature of each test. It has been reported that certain variables of the Ewing test are not suitable for evaluating autonomic dysfunction and that it has poor sensitivity for diagnosing CAN 26 , 27 , 28 . On the other hand, HRV analysis only evaluates a few cardiac cycles and may not reflect the actual overall conditions of patients 29 . In a study analyzing the impact of MBS on diabetes complications in patients with obesity, there was no change in the E/I ratio after surgery, but improved results were reported for HRV variables 12 . Recently, HRT analysis, a relatively new noninvasive cardiac electrophysiological method that is considered a reliable indicator of baroreceptor sensitivity, has been reported to have a similar sensitivity and higher specificity than HRV 30 . Considering these results, additional efforts are required to establish a standard method for diagnosing CAN.

This study had several limitations. First, the subjects of this study had a relatively short duration of diabetes and were young, and the number of subjects was small. Therefore, we cannot generalize our result. However, the population in our study appears to be representative of typical candidates for MBS in East Asia. Second, some patients reduced or discontinued anti‐hypertensives, which may affect HR. Therefore, the results must be interpreted with consideration for the effects of these drugs. Additionally, CAN was performed without fasting and in the afternoon for certain participants, potentially impacting the study results. There are individual differences in follow‐up duration due to the schedule of individual patients' outpatient visits. Regardless, this study is valuable in that MBS, which is reported to be effective not only in improving diabetes but also in improving various complications of diabetes, is also effective in improving CAN. Lastly, we do not know whether the effects of MBS on CAN have improved apart from weight loss. It would be necessary to conduct comparison studies with recently released potent anti‐obesity medications. It is also necessary to analyze the threshold of weight loss that can significantly enhance CAN variables through additional research.

MBS improved HRV variables associated with postoperative weight loss. MBS can be a promising candidate for treating CAN in subjects with obesity with or without diabetes.
