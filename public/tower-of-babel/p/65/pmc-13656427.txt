Three-order interactions in the alpha band of default mode network EEGs based on O-information rate: insights from aging and Alzheimer's disease
Authors: Hamed Azami, Yuri Antonacci, Daniel M Blumberger, Heather Brooks, Luca Faes, Corinne E Fischer, Alastair Flint, Nathan Herrmann, Sanjeev Kumar, Damien Gallagher, Linda Mah, Benoit H Mulsant, Nicole Schoer, Bruce G Pollock, Tarek K Rajji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlpha-band default mode network (DMN) connectivity declines with aging and Alzheimer's disease (AD), yet most electroencephalography (EEG) connectivity studies used pairwise (two-order) measures, such as mutual information rate (MIR). We leveraged O-information rate (OIR) to quantify three-order interactions and to separate redundant from synergistic information processing across frontal, temporal, and parietal DMN regions. We hypothesized that, extending established findings of reduced pairwise connectivity, (i) OIR (and its components) would be reduced in older versus younger adults and in AD versus healthy controls (HC); (ii) combining MIR with OIR would improve classification compared with MIR alone; and (iii) OIR measures would correlate positively with global cognition (as assessed by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)).MethodsResting-state EEG from two samples—healthy adult lifespan aging (95 younger; 93 older) and AD spectrum (44 HC; 84 amnestic mild cognitive impairment [aMCI]; 41 AD)—was source-localized using eLORETA to DMN regions. Alpha band (8–13 Hz) MIR and OIR were computed through multivariate spectral analysis. Group differences were tested using t-tests or analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with multiple comparison correction. Classification (OIR, MIR, demographic, and combined feature sets) used cross-validated logistic-regression, linear-SVM, and random-forest models, with bootstrap 95% confidence intervals and DeLong tests for AUC comparisons.ResultsOIR and its redundancy component were significantly reduced in older versus younger adults and in AD versus HC/aMCI, with no HC–aMCI differences. The synergy component showed no group differences. MIR decreased broadly with aging and fronto-parietally in AD. Regarding classification (AD spectrum dataset, cross-validated AUC), OIR-based metrics outperformed MIR (AD vs. HC: 0.93 vs. 0.73; AD vs. aMCI: 0.93 vs. 0.71; aMCI vs. HC: 0.48 vs. 0.46). Combining demographics with all information-theoretic features improved AD vs. HC (0.98; DeLong p = 0.023) but not AD vs. aMCI (0.93), where OIR alone was already at ceiling; no feature set exceeded chance for aMCI vs. HC. After correction, OIR and its redundancy component correlated positively with MoCA scores; MIR and the synergy component did not.ConclusionsAlpha-band three-order DMN interactions—particularly redundant information processing—decline with aging and AD and provide additional information beyond two-order connectivity for diagnostic classification. OIR offers complementary measures to traditional metrics, and future studies should examine its value when combined with more established AD biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13195-026-02131-3.Keywords: High-order Interactions, O-information rate, Default mode network, EEG, Aging, Alzheimer’s disease

Introduction

The default mode network (DMN) comprises interconnected brain regions that are typically more active at rest and are associated with cognitive functions [1, 2]. The DMN consists of broadly distributed brain regions located in the parietal, temporal, and frontal cortex [3]. The most widely recognized approach for studying the DMN is functional connectivity, which investigates correlations between pairs of interacting brain regions [4]. Reduced functional connectivity within the DMN, as measured by electroencephalography (EEG) or magnetoencephalography (MEG), has been linked to normal aging and cognitive impairment, including memory deficits associated with Alzheimer’s disease (AD) and amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) [5–10]. This functional connectivity reduction is prominent in long-range connections linking frontal, temporal, and parietal regions in the alpha band [5–10]. Resting-state alpha rhythms are closely linked to default-mode-network function and are among the most consistently altered EEG features in AD [11, 12]. Attenuation and slowing of posterior alpha activity have repeatedly been associated with cognitive decline [13, 14], and recent review indicates that reduced alpha-band connectivity is the most reliable EEG signature across MCI and AD [5].

To date, functional connectivity studies of the DMN have been limited by being based on pairwise analyses (two-order interactions), overlooking more intricate, higher-order interactions (HOIs) [15–18]. For example, modelling neuronal systems with HOIs has significant implications for accurately capturing true interactions in binary time series [19]. Moreover, HOIs can streamline the analysis of neuronal functional networks by reducing the complexity of interactions beyond the two-order [19].

HOIs denote the complex relationships or dependencies among three or more units, elements, or variables in a system simultaneously [20]. Studying HOIs allows for distinguishing between two qualitatively different types of interactions: (1) redundant interactions, where information is duplicated across multiple elements, leading to the resolution of uncertainty in one element when observing another; and (2) synergistic interactions, where uncertainty can only be resolved by considering the joint state of two or more variables [15]. Such a distinction is not possible using a bivariate functional connectivity network analysis [15].

To estimate synergy- and redundancy-dominated interactions in a network of multiple elements or variables, O-information (OI) based on mutual information (MI) has been recently introduced [21]. While MI, as a functional connectivity measure, quantifies the shared information between pairs of variables, OI—representing higher-order interactions—captures how information is distributed among multiple variables [21]. Building on the idea of the MI rate (MIR), O-information rate (OIR) extends OI to leverage the spectral representation of vector autoregressive and state-space models to quantify how groups of processes jointly contribute redundant or synergistic effects within specific frequency bands, and in the time domain after full-band integration [20–22]. This metric provides a dynamic formulation that better matches the time-evolving nature of EEG and enables frequency-domain assessment of higher-order interactions [20–22]. Building on previous applications of higher-order information-theoretic measures to brain dynamics [22–24], we apply the spectral block-OIR formulation to source-reconstructed EEG within the default mode network across the adult lifespan and in clinically diagnosed AD. Our contribution lies in the source-space application to a canonical functional network in DMN, the focus on the clinically motivated alpha band, and the integration of OIR with mutual information rate and the redundancy/synergy decomposition. We used two resting-state EEG datasets: 1) a healthy adult lifespan aging dataset with EEGs from 95 healthy younger participants and 93 healthy older participants [25]; and 2) an AD spectrum dataset including 44 older healthy controls (HC), 84 participants with aMCI [26], and 41 participants with AD [27]. We focused solely on aMCI because in previous resting-state EEG studies, participants with aMCI exhibited reduced alpha power and disrupted memory-network connectivity, whereas participants with non-amnestic MCI exhibited more variable patterns [28].

Given the loss of neural network connectivity observed in AD relative to HC and in healthy older compared to younger individuals, we hypothesized that OIR, redundancy, and synergy in the alpha band of the EEG DMN will be lower in older healthy subjects than in younger healthy adults and in AD than in HC participants. We also hypothesized that combining pairwise functional connectivity (MIR, representing two-order interactions) with three-order interactions based on OIR would yield higher classification accuracy than MIR alone when distinguishing younger and older individuals, or AD and HC. However, because resting-state DMN EEG connectivity findings in aMCI are complex and exhibit small effect sizes [10, 29], we did not make a specific hypothesis regarding the impact of aMCI on three-order interactions; instead, we explored OIR differences in aMCI and its classification vs the other diagnostic groups. Finally, we explored whether OIR measures correlated with cognitive performance measured by the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) [30] in the AD spectrum dataset.

Methods

Healthy adult lifespan aging dataset

Participants

The study includes 95 younger participants (20–35 years) and 93 older participants (60–80 years) [25]. EEG was recorded under an eyes-closed resting-state condition. The younger group comprised 56 females (mean age [SD]: 23.9 [3.2] years) and 39 males (mean age [SD]: 23.6 [2.6] years). The older group comprised 52 females (mean age [SD]: 68.71 [5.18] years) and 41 males (mean age [SD]: 69.85 [4.32] years). We included both right-handed and left-handed participants but excluded those with psychiatric disorders, severe neurological disorders (e.g., epilepsy), prior head injuries, stroke, transient circulatory brain disorders, Huntington's disease, Parkinson's disease, sensory or motor issues that could affect computer tasks, current use of psychotropic drugs, or the use of recreational synthetic or natural drugs. Additionally, participants with mini-mental state examination (MMSE) scores below 27, indicating higher risk of MCI and dementia, were also excluded. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Zurich's ethics committee in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent [25].

EEG data collection, pre-processing, and source localization based on eLORETA

A 128-channel EEG system was used to record EEG data with a sampling rate of 500 Hz, employing a band-pass filter of 0.1–100 Hz and Cz as the recording reference [25]. Electrode impedances were maintained below 40 kΩ. EEG data preprocessing was performed using the Automagic toolbox for MATLAB (https://github.com/methlabUZH/automagic). This included identifying and interpolating bad electrodes based on criteria such as correlation, line noise, and flatline activity. Subsequent steps involved band-pass filtering, line noise artifact removal, exclusion of high-amplitude data segments, and the application of Independent Component Analysis (ICA) to identify and remove components reflecting artifacts. Residual bad channels were excluded, and missing electrodes were interpolated using spherical spline interpolation [25].

In summary, data files were deemed to be of poor EEG quality and excluded from the analysis if they exceeded predefined thresholds for high-amplitude data points (> 30 μV), high variance across time points (> 15 μV), high variance across channels (> 15 μV), or a high ratio of bad channels (> 30%). After preprocessing with the Automagic toolbox, 105 EEG channels were retained for further analysis, while the remaining channels (primarily located on the neck and face) were discarded. The EEG data were re-referenced to the common average reference. For scalp- and source-level frequency analyses, the preprocessed continuous data were segmented into epochs, and epochs exceeding an amplitude threshold of ± 90 μV were rejected. These standardized preprocessing steps and quality metrics ensured consistent data quality throughout the analysis. Preprocessing scripts were made available before data acquisition at https://osf.io/a759y/ [25].

For source localization of EEG data to different brain regions, we employed exact Low-Resolution Electric Tomography (eLORETA) [31] that linearly estimates cortical source activity responsible for generating scalp-recorded EEG rhythms. The eLORETA freeware addresses the EEG inverse problem by estimating 'neural' current density values at each cortical voxel within the specified head volume conductor model. Solutions are computed on a frequency-bin-by-frequency-bin basis. The input for this estimation involves EEG data recorded from scalp electrodes, and the output provides estimates of neural current density at equivalent current dipoles. These dipoles are localized within one of the 6239 voxels (5 mm resolution) that constitute the cortical source space, limited to the cortical gray matter of the head volume conductor model. Specifically, eLORETA estimates local neural ionic currents at three axes—'z,' 'x,' and 'y'—of a dipolar source within each voxel of the cortical source space. Following this procedure, eLORETA source activities from EEG rhythms were estimated in specific Regions of Interest (ROIs). The selection of DMN nodes for network ROIs and Brodmann Areas is detailed in Table 1 [32, 33]. The brain model relies on a realistic cerebral shape derived from the Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI152 template) [34], a template commonly used in neuroimaging studies.

The default mode network was represented by 14 source ROIs grouped into three anatomical blocks: a Frontal block (bilateral Brodmann areas 9 and 10), a Temporal block (bilateral Brodmann areas 21 and 36 and bilateral hippocampus), and a Parietal block (bilateral Brodmann areas 39 and 40), comprising 4, 6, and 4 ROIs respectively. These regions correspond to major DMN-related nodes reported in the resting-state literature [35–37]. Each ROI was a multi-vertex source-space region (mean 43 vertices; range 10–82), and its representative time series was taken as the first principal component across all vertices in that region. The selection was fixed a priori from the canonical DMN definition, and the alpha-band source-power estimates show measurable source power within the selected DMN ROIs (Appendix, Supplementary Table S6).

Alzheimer's disease spectrum dataset

Participants

HC and aMCI individuals were included from a trial (Prevention of AD with Cognitive Remediation plus transcranial Direct Current Stimulation in Mild Cognitive Impairment and Depression (PACt-MD; NCT02386670). The trial was conducted in five academic hospitals in Toronto, Canada, and its methods were reported previously [38]. All participants gave written informed consent before any procedures. The Centre for Addiction and Mental Health Research Ethics Board approved the study as the single board of record for all sites through Clinical Trials Ontario. Eligibility criteria included: (1) absence of lifetime diagnosis of schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, or obsessive–compulsive disorder based on the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders Fifth Edition (DSM-5) [39]; (2) absence of significant neurological conditions affecting cognition or unstable medical illnesses; (3) absence of DSM-5 diagnosis of alcohol or other substance use disorder within the past 12 months; (4) no use of cognitive enhancers in the six weeks preceding study entry; and (5) a Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [40] score of 10 or below. Additional criteria for the Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) group involved age 60 or older and meeting DSM-5 criteria for a diagnosis of Mild Neurocognitive Disorder. For the HC group, participants also had to be 60 years old or older, not meeting DSM-5 criteria for diagnoses of Mild or Major Neurocognitive Disorder, or Major Depressive Disorder. Diagnoses were confirmed during a consensus conference, including the participant's clinical psychiatrist, one or two principal investigators, the study neuropsychologist, and research staff overseeing clinical, functional, and neuropsychological assessments. The EEG study was an optional component of PACt-MD, with 114 MCI and 44 HC participants ultimately included in the final analyses (refer to CONSORT charts, and Figures S1 and S2 in Supplementary Appendix.). In alignment with the findings in [9, 28], which highlight awake resting-state EEG studies predominantly reveal reduced alpha power and impaired memory-network connectivity in aMCI, we focused on aMCI, defined as having impairment in a memory domain with or without impairment in another domain, as non-amnestic MCI tends to exhibit more inconsistent patterns. This reduced the number of participants with MCI to 84. AD participants were included from two separate intervention studies (NCT01847586 and NCT02537496). The eligibility criteria for participants with AD included: (1) a diagnosis of probable AD based on the criteria of the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) [41] in one study or the National Institute on Aging – Alzheimer’s Association (NIA-AA) Research Framework for Alzheimer's Disease [42] in the other study; (2) either not taking an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor or having been on a stable dosage for at least 3 months; and (3) no DSM-5 diagnosis within the past 12 months other than Major Neurocognitive Disorder due to AD. Additionally, in one study, participants were 65 years old or older with a MMSE [43] score ≥ 17; in the other study, they were 55 years old or older with a MoCA [30] score ≥ 10 (refer to CONSORT chart, Figure S3 in Supplementary Appendix). Participants in these studies provided written informed consent following approval from the Research Ethics Board at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, Canada.

Cognitive assessments

Clinical assessments for aMCI and HC participants included both the MoCA and MMSE. Among AD participants, 26 completed the MMSE, and 14 completed the MoCA. One participant had no scores for either test. Equivalent MoCA scores were generated for the 26 participants who completed the MMSE, utilizing a published conversion table [44].

EEG data collection, pre-processing, and source localization based on eLORETA

EEG recordings were conducted using a 64-channel Synamps 2 EEG device and the 10–10 montage system. Electrodes were referenced to a posterior electrode behind Cz. The EEG signals were recorded for 10 min at a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz, with participants seated in a relaxed state with eyes closed, avoiding head or eye movements, and remaining awake. Subsequently, a band-pass filter with cut-off frequencies of 1 and 45 Hz was applied, removing bad channels. The data was then re-referenced to a standard average reference.

Offline EEG data processing was carried out using MATLAB (The MathWorks, Inc.) and the EEGLAB toolbox. Initially, a visual inspection of the EEG data was performed to ensure the absence of prominent delta and theta waves, indicative of sleep EEG patterns, to exclude any potential drowsiness during recording. Subsequent visual examination aimed to remove segments with noticeable noise and channels heavily impacted by various artifacts, including motion artifacts, eye movements, and blinks. To further minimize remaining noise, the processed continuous EEG data was divided into 4-s epochs, and ICA was applied to eliminate components associated with eye movements and muscle activity [45]. Source localization for the AD spectrum dataset followed the same eLORETA procedure described above.

When the OIR (or its gradient ) is positive, it indicates that the added process shares overlapping information with the existing set, resulting in redundancy-dominated interactions. In this scenario, the incremental information contributed by the additional process is less than the sum of its pairwise interactions with each of the processes in . Conversely, a negative OIR signifies that the combined observation of the processes yields more information than the sum of their pairwise contributions, reflecting synergy-dominated interactions. Essentially, redundancy implies that multiple processes are conveying similar information, whereas synergy indicates that the joint effect of the processes reveals unique information not present in any individual or pairwise interaction.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 23.0. For the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset, independent-samples Student’s t-tests and χ2 tests were used to assess group differences. The healthy adult lifespan dataset and the AD-spectrum dataset were analyzed independently and were not pooled into a single statistical model.

All within-cohort comparisons—young versus older adults in the lifespan dataset, and HC, aMCI, and AD participants in the AD-spectrum dataset—were performed using identical acquisition, preprocessing, source-reconstruction, and ROI-extraction pipelines within each cohort. Therefore, between-cohort acquisition differences could not confound the reported within-cohort effects. In addition, all EEG data were downsampled to 125 Hz before analysis. The reported O-information measures were restricted to the alpha band (8–13 Hz), which lies well below the Nyquist frequency after downsampling. Therefore, this preprocessing step does not affect the alpha-band results.

For the AD spectrum dataset, to assess differences among the three groups of demographic, clinical, and MoCA measures, we employed one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni post-hoc correction and χ2 tests. A significance level of α = 0.05 was set. Given the observed group differences in the AD spectrum dataset in age and education, we incorporated these variables as covariates in the subsequent group-difference (ANCOVA) and cognition (partial regression) analyses.

To test Hypothesis 1, we evaluated the effect of aging on higher-order interactions in the DMN in the alpha band (8–13 Hz) using the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset. We quantified higher-order interactions using the OIR and derived redundancy- and synergy-dominated components based on the sign of OIR computed at the segment level: segments with OIR > 0 were classified as redundancy-dominated, whereas segments with OIR < 0 were classified as synergy-dominated. For each participant, we summarized redundancy and synergy by aggregating OIR over segments separately within the positive and negative regimes (e.g., averaging OIR values across segments with OIR > 0 to obtain a redundancy index, and averaging OIR values across segments with OIR < 0 to obtain a synergy index; the latter is negative by definition, or can be reported as its absolute magnitude for interpretability). Group comparisons for OIR, redundancy, and synergy were Bonferroni-corrected for three tests. We performed the same analyses for the MIR to contrast higher-order interaction results with pairwise connectivity.

The O-information rate was computed using the block formulation applied to the 14 individual DMN ROI source signals. The 14 ROIs were organized into three multivariate blocks — Frontal (4 ROIs), Temporal (6 ROIs), and Parietal (4 ROIs) — specified by the partition Mv = [4, 6, 4]. The block-OIR quantifies the three-order interaction among these three blocks, with each block treated as a multivariate process retaining its constituent ROIs as separate variables. ROIs within a region were not averaged, and no single lobe-level aggregate signal was formed; each ROI signal was the first principal component across all vertices in that region (nPCA = 1; mean 43 vertices, range 10–82).

Similarly, for our second hypothesis, we calculated OIR, redundant and synergistic information for the AD spectrum dataset (HC, aMCI, and AD). All group analyses for the AD spectrum dataset were conducted using ANCOVA with the EEG measure as the dependent variable, group as the independent variable, and age and education as the covariates. Post-hoc analyses with Bonferroni correction followed each ANCOVA for 9 comparisons (3 (HC vs. aMCI, HC vs. AD, and aMCI vs AD) × 3 (OIR, redundancy, and synergy)). We also conducted the same analyses for MIR to contrast the results.

To assess whether the group effects were specific to the alpha band, the OIR, redundancy, and synergy ANCOVA pipeline was repeated for each canonical band (delta 1–4, theta 4–8, alpha 8–13, beta 13–30, and gamma 30–45 Hz), using the same dependent measures, group factor, age and education covariates, and Bonferroni post-hoc correction. In the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset, the corresponding per-band younger-versus-older comparisons used independent-samples t-tests with Bonferroni correction across the five bands.

For our third hypothesis, we used machine-learning classification to distinguish diagnostic groups in the AD spectrum dataset (AD vs. HC, AD vs. aMCI, and aMCI vs. HC). Each participant was represented by up to twelve features organized into nested sets: demographics (age, sex, and education), the OIR family (O-information rate and its redundancy and synergy components), the MIR family (the three inter-region mutual-information-rate terms), the OI family (the static O-information and its redundancy and synergy components), a nine-feature brain model combining the OI, OIR, and MIR families, and a full combined model adding demographics to the nine brain features. Each model was evaluated with stratified tenfold cross-validation repeated 20 times, with feature standardization fit within each training fold to avoid leakage and out-of-fold predicted scores averaged per participant. Performance was summarized by the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with a bootstrap 95% confidence interval (1000 participant-level resamples), together with balanced accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity at the Youden-optimal threshold. Pairwise differences between feature sets were tested with the DeLong method [51] on aligned per-participant scores, and significance against chance was assessed by label permutation (1000 permutations). To confirm results were not specific to a single algorithm, the entire procedure was repeated with logistic-regression, linear-SVM, and random-forest classifiers.

To assess whether three-order interactions quantified by OIR provide information beyond pairwise connectivity, we computed the mutual information rate (MIR) between the frontal–temporal, temporal–parietal, and frontal–parietal regions in both datasets (Fig. 2). For each region pair, MIR was computed for all inter-regional ROI pairs (i.e., every ROI in region A paired with every ROI in region B), and the resulting MIR values were averaged to yield a single connectivity estimate per region pair for each participant. In the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset, group differences were tested using an independent-samples t-test, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests for three comparisons (frontal–temporal, temporal–parietal, and frontal–parietal). In the AD spectrum dataset, group differences were assessed using ANCOVA with age and education as covariates, followed by Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc tests for nine comparisons (HC vs. aMCI, HC vs. AD, and aMCI vs. AD, each across the three region pairs).

Correlation with cognition

In the AD spectrum dataset, we examined the associations between cognitive performance (measured by MoCA scores) and alpha-band OIR and MIR metrics. We conducted linear regression analyses using the connectivity metrics as independent variables—specifically OIR, OIR-based redundancy and synergy, and MIR (frontal–temporal, frontal-parietal, and temporal-parietal)—and the MoCA score as the dependent variable. Age and education were included as covariates to control for group differences, and a Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Discussion

In this study we evaluated the alterations in three-order interaction metrics (OIR, redundancy, and synergy) between the frontal, temporal, and parietal regions in the alpha band of EEG DMN. The DMN is involved in the pathological processes of AD—potentially due to its connection with amyloid plaque accumulation [1, 7, 52, 53]. EEG/MEG studies have consistently reported alpha band DMN activity as particularly affected in both aging and AD [5, 6, 8, 9, 54, 55]. We found significant reductions in OIR and its corresponding redundancy in both older participants and those with AD, compared to their respective control groups. No significant differences were found between the HC and aMCI groups for OIR, redundancy, and synergy, nor were there significant differences in synergy among any groups in either dataset. Finally, we found that cross-validated classifiers using OIR-based metrics as input features showed notably higher performance in distinguishing AD vs. HC and AD vs. aMCI than those using MIR-based metrics. Overall, these results indicate that alpha-band DMN dysfunction in aging and AD is better captured by a loss of redundancy-dominated three-order interactions than by pairwise connectivity alone, supporting OIR as a sensitive network-level marker with potential diagnostic relevance.

The decline in OIR and redundancy observed in AD compared to HC may signify disruptions in the intricate connectivity of neural networks. AD is characterized by progressive neurodegeneration, particularly affecting regions crucial for memory and cognitive functions [56]. This degradation diminishes communication efficiency between frontal, temporal, and parietal regions, reflected in lower OIR and redundancy values, potentially contributing to cognitive decline. A similar decrease in OIR and redundancy in healthy older individuals compared to the young suggests age-related changes in neural connectivity due to structural and functional alterations, including reduced synaptic density and altered neural plasticity [57]. This age-related decline impacts information transfer efficiency between brain regions, highlighting a less adaptable neural network in older individuals, influencing cognitive processes. Recognizing these connectivity alterations is crucial for understanding cognitive aging complexities and developing interventions.

Given the definition of redundancy as repeated information in a network (here, across brain regions), the observed decrease in connectivity between frontal, temporal, and parietal regions contributes to reduced redundancy in AD compared to HC or aMCI, and in healthy older individuals compared to the young participants [29]. This diminished connectivity hinders the effective transmission of information, leading to reduced redundancy in both AD and healthy aging populations. On the other hand, the absence of significant differences in synergistic information may be influenced by the intricate dynamics of synergy vs. redundancy in neural networks. Synergistic information that can be obtained when considering multiple sources of information together and that could not be obtained when the sources are considered in isolation can be disrupted by the failure of even a single element of a network, possibly leading to a subtle or undetectable impact on overall synergy [58].

The selective reduction of redundancy alongside preserved synergy can be interpreted within recent information-decomposition accounts of brain organization [18]. In these accounts, functional connectivity and shared information across regions are carried predominantly by redundant interactions, whereas synergy reflects the integrative combination of information that cannot be recovered from individual regions; within-module communication tends to be more redundant, and cross-module integration comparatively more synergistic. The redundancy decline we observe across frontal–temporal–parietal DMN nodes is consistent with degradation of shared information supported by projection systems, including thalamocortical and long-range cortico-cortical pathways implicated in Alzheimer's disease, while the preservation of synergy suggests that AD-related disconnection preferentially affects shared (redundant) information rather than emergent synergy.

The decrease in MIR observed between the frontal and temporal, frontal and parietal, and temporal and parietal regions in the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset aligns with the known decline in functional connectivity among these regions in the alpha band, indicative of advanced aging [59]. This reduction may signify alterations in attentional focus, awareness, and arousal levels associated with the aging process [59, 60]. In the AD spectrum dataset, the reduction in MIR in fronto-parietal alpha, consistent with prior research [61, 62], implies a gradual decline in attentional systems from HC to AD. This diminished connectivity also suggests potential cholinergic dysfunction, impacting cognitive processes, particularly in visual and spatial domains [63]. Interestingly, the fronto-parietal connectivity in alpha correlated with cognitive performance based on MMSE score [63].

No significant differences were found between HC and aMCI in the alpha band, a finding that aligns with the broader challenges in detecting early cognitive changes using electrophysiological measures. This null result can be interpreted through several complementary perspectives. First, meta-analytic evidence indicates a consistently small effect size (Cohen's d = 0.25) for alpha synchrony decreases in aMCI compared to HC in fronto-parietal regions [10], suggesting that detectable changes in alpha-band connectivity may emerge only after substantial pathological progression. Second, the heterogeneity inherent in aMCI populations may contribute to these null findings. aMCI represents a transitional state between normal aging and dementia, with considerable variability in underlying pathophysiology, progression rates, and cognitive profiles across individuals [28, 29].

Gatica et al. [24] reported increased O-information in AD using whole-brain BOLD fMRI, whereas we report reduced alpha-band O-information in the DMN. Three differences account for this: modality (BOLD captures slow hemodynamic fluctuations whereas EEG alpha captures sub-second neural coordination), the absence of canonical-band decomposition in BOLD, and spatial scope (whole-brain vs DMN-specific). Camino-Pontes et al. [64] reported preserved or increased static O-information in fMRI, consistent for the same modality reasons. Herzog et al. [65] is directionally consistent with our findings, reporting alpha-band reductions in higher-order coordination in AD EEG. The dependence of higher-order signatures on modality and analytic framework is itself a methodologically important observation.

Our findings should not be interpreted as supporting a strictly graded HC → aMCI → AD continuum on the alpha-band block-OIR metric. Rather, the data show a categorical separation: HC and aMCI are statistically indistinguishable (Hedges' g ≈ 0; cross-validated AUC = 0.48), whereas AD shows a large reduction (d = 1.14; AUC = 0.93). This is consistent with the wider EEG literature, in which resting-state spectral and connectivity differences in the DMN are robust for AD versus controls but weak or inconsistent for MCI versus controls [10, 29], with the most reliable effects in the alpha band (e.g., recent systematic reviews of EEG connectivity in MCI and AD) [5–10]. Beyond the aMCI heterogeneity noted above, two factors may contribute: the alpha-OIR reduction may emerge relatively late in the disease course, after the aMCI stage, and the magnitude of the AD effect cannot be explained by clinical severity alone. Future work with biomarker-stratified subgroups will be needed to determine whether alpha-OIR changes abruptly at the AD stage.

Classification analyses reinforced the group-difference findings. OIR-based features discriminated AD from both HC and aMCI with high accuracy (AUC ≈ 0.93 for each), and the OIR family alone matched the full nine-feature brain model, indicating that the higher-order O-information carries most of the discriminative signal. The value of combining feature types was contrast-dependent: for AD vs HC, adding the remaining features to OIR yielded a significant improvement (combined AUC = 0.98; DeLong p = 0.023), whereas for AD vs aMCI OIR alone was already at ceiling and the combination added nothing. The HC-vs-aMCI contrast did not exceed chance for any brain-feature set, consistent with a categorical separation between AD and non-AD groups rather than a graded continuum. Pairwise MIR features classified less well than OIR in every contrast, supporting the added value of higher-order over two-order interactions. These results were stable across logistic-regression, linear-SVM, and random-forest classifiers.

OIR-based measures show promising results, but clinical translation will require defining acquisition/processing standards suitable for clinical workflows, demonstrating incremental value beyond established biomarkers and neuropsychological testing, and establishing longitudinal trajectories and prognostic utility. Mechanistically, the observed reductions in net redundancy across aging and AD suggest altered large-scale network coordination; clarifying their biological basis will require multimodal studies linking OIR metrics to amyloid burden, tau pathology, neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and structural/functional disconnection.

Our study is limited by the size of the age and diagnostic subgroups, which precluded robust subgroup analyses; by the secondary nature of these analyses relative to the original study aims; and by diagnoses based on clinical criteria without biomarker confirmation, although NINCDS-ADRDA clinical diagnoses are widely used and generally reliable [66, 67]. The Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) was not available for the AD group in this sub-study, so CDR-based staging could not be reported. Our analyses were confined to the default mode network, which was selected a priori for its established relevance to AD rather than chosen from among competing networks; the alpha-band source-power estimates (Supplementary Table S6) show the group differences expressed across the DMN nodes, with the largest reductions in temporal and medial-temporal regions central to early AD pathology. We did not, however, evaluate specificity by recomputing OIR for alternative or random within-DMN ROI sets, nor for alternative or random whole-cortex networks: at the model order used here (momax = 12), each such replicate would require re-estimating the multivariate OIR for every participant, which was computationally prohibitive. Our conclusions are therefore restricted to the selected DMN ROIs, and both a formal random-ROI null and a between-network specificity comparison remain important directions for future work. We also used the canonical 8–13 Hz alpha band rather than an individualized alpha frequency; given the large effect sizes and the broadband nature of the group differences, the results are unlikely to be driven by individual alpha-peak shifts [68], although individualized-alpha-frequency analyses remain a useful direction for future work. Overall, the findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and motivate preregistered, biomarker-anchored longitudinal studies in larger and more diverse samples to determine the clinical validity and usefulness of OIR-based measures.

Conclusions

Using a novel OIR method to investigate dynamic DMN EEG interactions, we found that both OIR and redundancy values were significantly lower in older participants compared to younger ones in the healthy adult lifespan aging dataset and similarly reduced in AD participants relative to HCs and participants with aMCI in the AD spectrum dataset. However, there were no significant differences between the older HC and the aMCI group. These reductions suggest disrupted three-order interactions among frontal, temporal, and parietal regions with aging and cognitive impairment. Notably, decreased MIR values further support these findings. Additionally, classification analyses demonstrated that OIR-based metrics outperformed MIR-based measures for distinguishing AD from HC and from aMCI; combining demographics with the information-theoretic features further improved AD-versus-HC discrimination, whereas OIR alone was sufficient for AD versus aMCI, and no feature set distinguished aMCI from HC. The observed decline in redundancy across comparisons—healthy older versus healthy younger, AD versus aMCI, and AD versus healthy controls—indicates damage to DMN connections associated with both physiological aging and pathological neurodegeneration. The association between diminished OIR and cognitive impairment underscores the clinical relevance of higher-order interactions as potential biomarkers for early detection and monitoring of AD and healthy aging. Overall, this study highlights the importance of complex brain interactions in understanding neurodegeneration and paves the way for future diagnostic and therapeutic strategies targeting these disorders.
