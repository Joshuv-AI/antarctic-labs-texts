Development and validation of the Thoughts on Eating Scale: a domain-specific measure of belief orientations toward eating
Authors: Rika Naito, Takatsune Kumada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding individual differences in eating behavior is a central challenge in psychological research. Because broad traits show only weak-to-moderate links to eating, and most measures focus on behavioral outcomes, belief-level, domain-specific constructs connecting global traits to situation-specific behaviors have not been a primary focus of existing measurement approaches. This study introduces and empirically validates “Thoughts on Eating” (ToE) as a domain-specific belief system positioned between stable personality traits and specific eating behaviors.MethodsWe conducted two sequential studies with Japanese adults. In Phase 1, we collected free-text responses from 100 participants to generate candidate items, then administered the resulting scale to a stratified sample of 552 adults to identify the dimensional structure of ToE through exploratory and confirmatory factor analyses. In Phase 2, we examined test–retest reliability and concurrent validity using the Expanded Mindful Eating Scale (EMES) and the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) in a follow-up sample of 434 participants.ResultsExploratory and confirmatory analyses supported seven factors: “Enriching One’s Heart,” “Encouraging Social Communication,” “Realizing Oneself,” “Sustaining Oneself,” “Respecting Food,” “Indifference to Eating,” and “Disliking Eating.” The latter two represent fundamental evaluative orientations that are not well captured by most existing eating-related measures. The scale demonstrated adequate temporal stability and theoretically coherent associations with established measures. Associations with demographic variables and eating behaviors indicated that ToE is shaped by the intersection of individual characteristics and sociocultural contexts, supporting its characterization as an intermediate-level construct that is more stable than situational attitudes yet more context-sensitive than global traits.ConclusionsOur findings establish that ToE is a theoretically sound construct that reflects belief orientations that have received limited systematic attention in individual-differences research on eating. This study proposes a multi-level structure as a foundation for integrating personality, social, cultural, and clinical psychology perspectives, which is expected to advance our understanding of individual differences in eating behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05026-y.Keywords: Thoughts on eating, Eating behavior, Individual differences, Belief system, Scale development

Background

Eating serves more than biological survival. For many people, eating is closely related to well-being, aesthetics, identity, and social connections. People differ in how they value these aspects of eating, and they place different emphasis on pleasure, health, self-realization, and social meaning. Eating is also a daily behavior with significant consequences for health and environmental sustainability through food production, processing, and consumption [1, 2]. Therefore, it is essential to understand why people differ in their eating habits. Individuals tend to show relatively stable within-person patterns in whether, what, and how much they eat, even in routine situations [3, 4].

Explaining individual differences in eating behavior is a persistent challenge in psychological research. Broad personality traits predict eating patterns; however, the associations between these traits and specific eating behaviors are often weak and inconsistent [5, 6]. In contrast, behavioral measures such as dietary restraint or emotional eating capture specific manifestations of eating, instead of the underlying psychological orientations that cause them. This suggests that a domain-specific, belief-level description of how individuals orient themselves toward eating, spanning the full range from engagement to indifference and aversion, has not been a primary focus of existing measurement approaches.

People’s thoughts are known to influence their behavioral choices (e.g [7, 8]), . The Theory of Planned Behavior (TPB; [7]) proposes that specific behaviors are shaped by behavioral intentions, which in turn are influenced by attitudes toward the behavior, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control. These attitudes are rooted in beliefs about the likely outcomes of a behavior and evaluations of those outcomes. The TPB functions effectively as a predictive model for specific actions. However, it is not designed to explain stable individual differences in the belief structures underlying specific actions.

To better understand personal “food policies” that explicitly or implicitly guide daily eating behavior, we propose “Thoughts on Eating” (ToE) as a domain-specific belief orientation concerning what eating means and does for the individual. ToE is conceptually related to the belief component of the TPB. However, the TPB focuses on mechanisms that generate behavioral intentions, whereas ToE seeks to describe the diverse ways individuals conceptualize food and eating more broadly. More specifically, the TPB distinguishes three classes of beliefs, each tied to a particular behavioral referent: behavioral beliefs (expected outcomes of a specific behavior), normative beliefs (perceived expectations of significant others regarding that behavior), and control beliefs (perceived factors facilitating or impeding that behavior). All three function as inputs to intention formation for a designated act (e.g., “eating two servings of fruits and vegetables”). ToE, in contrast, is not anchored to a specific behavioral referent. It captures overarching belief orientations about what eating is and what role it plays in one’s life, including evaluative stances (e.g., indifference, aversion) that need not give rise to a behavioral intention regarding any particular act. As a domain-specific belief system, ToE may encompass multiple dimensions, ranging from basic evaluative orientations toward eating itself to more elaborate personal and cultural meanings. This multidimensional structure allows ToE to capture variation across different levels of psychological abstraction, from fundamental attitudes to socially embedded values. Accordingly, the present study aims to establish ToE as an individual-difference construct and to provide initial empirical support for its proposed structure.

Within this conceptualization, a belief system refers to relatively enduring cognitive representations of what eating is and what role it plays in one’s life [9]. Crucially, such representations function as an interpretive scaffold within which attitudes and motivations operate: they define the meaning of eating before any specific evaluative judgment or goal-directed drive comes into play. This distinguishes ToE from attitudes in the TPB [7], which presuppose a specific behavioral referent and are defined by their proximity to intention formation. It also distinguishes ToE from eating motivations, which address why individuals engage in eating behavior (e.g [10]), , rather than how they fundamentally conceptualize eating itself. Although some ToE dimensions inevitably contain evaluative or motivational content—and may therefore appear conceptually close to value-based attitudes or goal-orientations—their defining feature is that they describe how individuals fundamentally conceptualize eating itself, rather than how they evaluate a specific act or seek a specific outcome. These orientations are expected to show relative temporal stability, consistent with their conceptualization as belief-level rather than state-level phenomena.

Cognitive–affective perspectives suggest that behavior is shaped by the interplay of cognitive and emotional processes [11]. ToE primarily reflects a cognitive dimension, as it involves individuals’ conscious awareness of their own views about eating rather than transient emotional reactions. At the same time, eating-related beliefs are often shaped by affective experiences [12]. In this sense, ToE provides an integrative construct that accommodates both cognitive and affective influences on eating behavior. While existing psychological models have clarified mechanisms that regulate behavior, they have paid less attention to the origin and structure of variability in eating-related belief systems across individuals.

We conceptualize ToE as a trait-like construct because it reflects individuals’ core beliefs about eating and is expected to show relative stability over time. However, unlike broad personality traits which show considerable rank-order consistency across the lifespan [13], ToE is not assumed to be fixed or resistant to change. Life experiences, cultural influences, and environmental contexts may gradually shape an individual’s ToE. In this sense, ToE occupies an intermediate position. It captures relatively stable orientations that consistently influence how people think about and engage with food, yet these orientations are more flexible than global personality traits. This positioning—more stable than situational states but more context-sensitive than broad traits—represents a level of psychological organization that has received limited attention in research on individual differences, particularly in the study of eating behavior.

We propose that multiple influences, including personal characteristics and environmental contexts such as cultural, social, and familial factors, shape ToE. ToE may contribute to broader eating attitudes through the interplay of these influences [7, 14]. In turn, these attitudes shape eating intentions and behaviors. In addition, eating attitudes and behaviors may, in turn, feed back to reshape an individual’s ToE over time. Such reciprocal influence is consistent with self-perception accounts in which individuals infer their own beliefs partly from observing their own behavior [15], and with research showing that repeated behavior can update underlying beliefs through automaticity and habit formation [8].

Psychological conditions may also influence both ToE and eating behavior. Individuals experiencing depressive symptoms, for example, are more likely to skip meals or adopt unhealthy eating patterns [16, 17]. Studies have examined associations between personal or environmental factors and eating attitudes or behaviors. ToE provides a construct for describing belief-level individual differences that may help clarify these relationships.

Several scales have been developed to assess eating-related constructs (Table 1). They can be broadly divided into two groups: measures of general motivations or attitudes toward eating (e.g., the Eating Motivation Survey [TEMS]; [10]; the Meaning of Food in Life Questionnaire [MFLQ]; [18]) and measures of specific eating patterns or symptoms (e.g., the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire [DEBQ]; [19]; the Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire [EDE-Q]; [20]). However, the item content of motivation- and meaning-focused scales generally presupposes active or positive engagement with food (e.g., “I eat what I eat, because I have an appetite for it” in TEMS [10] and “I eat in a way that expresses care for the world” in MFLQ [18]) and does not explicitly capture fundamental evaluative orientations such as indifference or aversion toward eating. Behavioral measures, on the other hand, document patterns of eating in response to specific triggers but do not assess the belief systems that may underlie these patterns. Additionally, clinical instruments (e.g., the Eating Disorder Inventory; [21]) focus on pathological eating attitudes and behaviors. They are primarily designed for populations with disordered eating. Although these scales provide essential insights into clinically relevant aspects of eating psychology, these instruments were not primarily designed to address the broader spectrum of belief orientations about eating in the general population, or to assess how individuals fundamentally conceptualize eating itself.

Cultural considerations further compound this theoretical difference. Most existing measures have been developed in Western countries. As a result, these measures may not adequately capture culturally specific orientations toward eating in Asian and Japanese populations, where beliefs about food are often embedded in distinct social norms, traditions, and value systems. For example, the East Asian principle that “medicine and food share the same source” holds that everyday food intake supports bodily balance and prevents illness. This orientation frames eating as inseparable from health and nature in ways that are not directly represented in measures developed in contexts where food and medicine are typically conceptualized as separate domains.

ToE addresses these limitations by proposing a domain-specific belief, encompassing the full range from engagement to disengagement and from personal meaning to culturally embedded values. Accordingly, the present study has three objectives: (1) to explore the dimensional structure of ToE through qualitative item generation and exploratory/confirmatory factor analyses; (2) to develop a reliable and valid self-report instrument, named the Thoughts on Eating Scale (ToES), to assess individual differences in ToE; and (3) to provide initial evidence of construct validity through associations with sociodemographic characteristics, eating behaviors, and established measures of mindful eating and eating behavior. Throughout this article, “ToE” refers to the theoretical construct, whereas “ToES” refers to the self-report instrument developed to assess it.

Methods

Phase 1 aimed to explore the scope and dimensionality of ToE through qualitative item generation and to identify its factor structure empirically through scale development.

Phase 1

Item generation

On 27 July 2022, we recruited 100 participants through a crowdsourcing service (Yahoo Crowdsourcing Inc.), which is a practical and efficient recruitment method [43, 44]. Participants were asked two open-ended questions: Q1, “What is mealtime for you?” and Q2, “What is food for you?” They were instructed to provide approximately three responses to each question in free-text format. Those who completed the survey received 2 JPY. In this and all subsequent studies, all procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Institutional Review Board of Kyoto University (Ref: KUIS-EAR-2019-005) and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. All participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

After clearly inappropriate responses consisting only of specific food names (e.g., “noodle,” “sushi”) were excluded by one of the authors, data from the remaining 94 (2.91 responses per respondent) respondents for Q1 and 91 respondents (2.86 responses per respondent) for Q2 were analyzed using a natural language processing tool (KH Coder; [45]). A morphological analysis was conducted on the written words and phrases, and collocations of analyzed content words (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adjectival verbs, and adverbs) within each participant’s responses were calculated separately for each question. Words appearing once in the corpus were excluded in the analysis, yielding 40 words for Q1 and 42 words for Q2. Based on the resulting collocation matrix, a hierarchical cluster analysis was performed. Clusters were identified using Ward’s method with Jaccard distance. Because some adjacent words in the dendrogram appeared to reflect a shared underlying concept, we merged such word pairs into a single conceptual label. For example, the conceptual cluster comprising “nutrition” and “replenishment” was developed into the item “I eat to take nutrition.” Overlapping expressions between questions were merged to reduce redundancy. Because the analysis focused on content words, negation terms (e.g., “not”) were not retained in words used in this analysis. Therefore, we additionally created and included two reverse-worded items based on a subset of the retained item statements. Item wording was refined through discussion among the authors to ensure adequate coverage of the major content domains identified while maintaining clarity and conciseness. The resulting initial item pool comprised 28 items (Appendix 1).

Supplementary content coverage check

To assess whether relevant viewpoints were missing from the initial pool, an additional sample of 200 participants who were recruited separately from the initial 100 through the same crowdsourcing service were asked an open-ended question: “If you have any additional thoughts about eating that were not covered by the 28 items, please describe them.” Responses were reviewed to identify potentially missing content domains and to generate two additional candidate items (“I eat to communicate with family and friends” and “Eating is a way to feel nature”), which were administered together with the initial pool in the subsequent quantitative study. “Eating is a way to feel nature” did not load substantively on any factor. “I eat to communicate with family and friends” loaded on the Encouraging Social Communication factor (loading = 0.8), but its content substantially overlapped with an existing item from the initial pool, “I eat to have a good time with others,” which loaded more strongly on the same factor (loading = 0.9); the original item was therefore retained to avoid redundancy.

Scale validation

Participants

Three independent samples were involved in Phase 1: (a) 100 participants for item generation (Sect. "Item generation"), (b) 200 participants for supplementary content coverage check (Sect. "Supplementary content coverage check"), and (c) a primary validation sample of 552 participants and an independent replication sample of 527 participants, both described below.

We used a crowdsourcing service to recruit participants (Yahoo Crowdsourcing Inc.) Between 5 November 2022 and 7 November 2022, we recruited 100 participants in each of six strata defined by sex (men or women) × age group (20–39, 40–59, or ≥ 60 years) using stratified sampling, and recruited a sample of 600 participants. Participants who completed the survey received 5 JPY as compensation.

Although 600 individuals participated in the survey, respondents were excluded if they selected the same response option for eight or more consecutive ToES items (straight-lining), which was treated as an indicator of low-effort responding. We included data from 552 participants in the analyses after excluding 48 respondents.

To test whether the factor structure observed in the primary sample would replicate in a non-overlapping sample, an independent replication sample of N = 527 Japanese adults was recruited separately through the same crowdsourcing service. The replication sample completed the same questionnaire and procedure as the primary sample.

Questionnaires

Participants rated each item on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), indicating the extent to which the item reflected their thoughts on eating. The questionnaire included the following instruction at the beginning to reduce potential social desirability bias: “Please respond based on your own thoughts rather than what you believe is generally accepted by society.” In addition to the ToES items, participants reported sociodemographic characteristics and eating behaviors. Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, and living style (living alone or living with others). Eating behaviors included the number of meals per day (1 = less than twice to 5 = more than four times), regularity of dinner time (1 = very regular to 4 = very irregular), dinner with others (times per week), eating out for dinner (times per week), and cooking dinner (1 = almost every day to 4 = rarely).

Data analysis

We conducted all the analyses in R (version 4.2.1). Psychological attitudes and values are typically interrelated [46], and factors in existing eating-related scales are often correlated [10, 18, 46]. Therefore, we conducted an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) using oblimin rotation, which also yields more interpretable solutions than other oblique rotation methods. We determined the number of factors to retain using multiple complementary criteria: parallel analysis [47], the Minimum Average Partial test, the Kaiser–Guttman criterion, and the Bayesian Information Criterion. Convergence across these indices was treated as primary evidence for factor retention, with interpretability also considered.

We conducted confirmatory factor analyses (CFA) to evaluate competing measurement models. We compared (a) a correlated seven-factor model, (b) a higher-order hierarchical model, and (c) a bifactor model. Model fit was evaluated using standard global fit indices: Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Given the limitations of relying solely on global fit indices when evaluating bifactor models, Omega Hierarchical (ωH) and Explained Common Variance (ECV) were examined to assess the reliability and substantive contribution of the general factor [48]. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α for each factor. Mean factor scores were calculated across sex, age group, and living style, followed by analyses of variance (ANOVA).

We conducted ordinal logistic regression analyses to examine associations between ToES scores and eating behaviors. A backward selection procedure was applied to determine the final regression model, and Nagelkerke R² was calculated to estimate explained variance.

To examine the replicability of the factor structure, the ToES was additionally administered to an independent sample of 527 participants recruited through the same crowdsourcing service. The identical EFA procedure was applied to this replication sample, and the results are reported in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2.

Phase 2

Phase 2 aimed to examine the temporal stability and construct validity of the ToES through test–retest reliability assessment and concurrent validity analyses.

Participants

We administered the ToES online to the 552 participants who had completed Phase 1, approximately one month later, between 1 December 2022 and 7 December 2022. The follow-up survey was completed by 434 participants.

The one-month interval was selected to balance reductions in memory effects with the expectation of temporal stability, consistent with prior research using similar intervals [49, 50]. As described earlier, ToE is conceptualized as reflecting relatively stable belief orientations rather than transient attitudes or emotions. Therefore, we considered a one-month interval appropriate to ensure that the test–retest assessment captured genuine stability rather than short-term fluctuations or long-term changes due to external influences. Participants who completed the follow-up survey received 8 JPY as compensation.

Questionnaires and data analysis

The participants again completed the ToES to assess test–retest reliability. We calculated correlations between the seven factor scores obtained in Phase 2 and those from Phase 1.

We examined concurrent validity using the Expanded Mindful Eating Scale (EMES) [42] and the Dutch Eating Behavior Questionnaire (DEBQ) [19, 51]. The EMES is a 20-item scale rated on a 4-point scale (1 = disagree to 4 = agree), comprising five subscales: Health of the Planet (4 items), Awareness and Appreciation for Food (5 items), Nonreactivity (5 items), Nonjudgmental Awareness (3 items), and Hunger and Satiety Cues (3 items). Kawasaki et al. [42] reported Cronbach’s α values of 0.79, 0.75, 0.74, 0.62, and 0.60 for each subscale, respectively, and confirmed the five-factor structure via confirmatory factor analysis in a sample of Japanese female university students (GFI = 0.914, CFI = 0.870, RMSEA = 0.061). The revised DEBQ comprises 33 items rated on a 5-point scale (1 = never to 5 = always), organized into three subscales: Restrained Eating (10 items), Emotional Eating (13 items), and External Eating (10 items). Kato and Roth [51] reported Cronbach’s α values of 0.91, 0.95, and 0.73 for these subscales, respectively, in a Japanese sample. Correlations between ToES factors and subscales of the EMES and DEBQ were computed to evaluate concurrent validity.

We expected the EMES to show positive associations with ToE dimensions reflecting meaning and value, and the DEBQ to demonstrate differentiated associations with specific ToE dimensions, allowing us to evaluate whether ToE exhibits theoretically consistent yet conceptually distinct relationships with established measures.

We examined multiple criteria to determine the appropriate number of factors. All indices, except the MAP test [52], which suggested five factors, consistently supported a seven-factor solution. The EFA with an oblimin rotation confirmed this structure, yielding seven interpretable factors (Fig. 2; Table 3). These factors were labeled “Enriching One’s Heart,” “Encouraging Social Communication,” “Realizing Oneself,” “Sustaining Oneself,” “Respecting Food,” “Indifference to Eating,” and “Disliking Eating.” Based on factor loadings (> 0.50) and conceptual interpretability, 20 items were retained in the final scale. To assess the replicability of this structure, the identical analysis was conducted in an independent replication sample (N = 527). The factor structure was largely consistent with the primary solution, although items for Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating loaded onto a single factor in the replication sample (Supplementary Tables S1 and S2). Such partial merging is not uncommon when conceptually adjacent dimensions share a common valence and are represented by a limited number of items. In the present scale, both dimensions reflect negative or disengaged orientations toward eating, which may increase their statistical proximity in certain samples. Nevertheless, we retained them as separate subscales because they may reflect conceptually distinguishable orientations, low engagement or salience versus active aversion or burden, which may have different theoretical and practical implications.

CFA compared three models. These included a correlated seven-factor model, a higher-order hierarchical model, and a bifactor model. The hierarchical model resulted in the poorest fit (CFI = 0.746, TLI = 0.704, RMSEA = 0.110, SRMR = 0.121) and was discarded.

The bifactor model showed the best overall global fit (CFI = 0.900, TLI = 0.853, RMSEA = 0.077, SRMR = 0.058), suggesting that both a general factor (G) and specific group factors (S) contributed to explaining responses. However, examination of Omega Hierarchical (ωH) and Explained Common Variance (ECV) indicated that the general factor accounted for only 15.1% of the total variance (ECV.G = 0.151), and its unique reliability was modest (ωH.G = 0.339). These results suggest that the general factor alone does not adequately capture the structure of ToE.

The correlated seven-factor model showed slightly lower global fit indices (CFI = 0.839, TLI = 0.795, RMSEA = 0.091, SRMR = 0.081) compared with the bifactor model. However, Omega Hierarchical values (ωH) for the group factors were consistently above 0.64, indicating that each factor retained sufficient reliability without relying on a dominant general factor. Taken together, we judged that the correlated seven-factor model provided the most theoretically and empirically appropriate representation of the data. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α (Table 4): Enriching One’s Heart α = 0.75, Encouraging Social Communication α = 0.84, Realizing Oneself α = 0.72, Sustaining Oneself α = 0.65, Respecting Food α = 0.79, Indifference to Eating α = 0.70, Disliking Eating α = 0.78, indicating moderate to high reliability across factors.

Discussion

ToE as a theoretically positioned domain-specific belief construct

This research introduces ToE as a domain-specific belief construct theoretically located at an intermediate level in the study of individual differences in eating behavior. Although many behavioral theories acknowledge that beliefs precede attitudes, eating research has paid comparatively less attention to the systematic description of individual differences in domain-specific belief orientations, particularly those spanning the full range from positive engagement to indifference and aversion. The present findings empirically support the ToES as a measure of this construct, and the intermediate-level positioning of ToE is offered theoretically.

Across Japanese adults from diverse demographic groups (men and women; ages 20–39, 40–59, and 60+), we identified seven dimensions of ToE: Enriching One’s Heart, Encouraging Social Communication, Realizing Oneself, Sustaining Oneself, Respecting Food, Indifference to Eating, and Disliking Eating. Importantly, the last two may represent evaluative stances that are not well captured in many existing measures of eating (e.g., TEMS [10]; MFLQ [18]; EMES [42]). Much prior research has implicitly assumed that individuals engage with food in purposeful or meaningful ways, emphasizing motivations, values, or goals related to eating. Such approaches tend to focus less on individuals who feel indifferent toward eating or who experience it as aversive. These orientations reflect a more basic evaluative dimension, paralleling, at the belief level, the fundamental evaluative component of attitudes described in attitude theory than the meanings or motivations typically studied [14]. In doing so, ToE helps account for patterns of eating behavior that motivation-based or meaning-based models may not fully explain.

The associations observed between ToE dimensions, demographic characteristics, and eating behaviors are consistent with this theoretical positioning. ToE appears to be shaped not only by relatively stable personal dispositions but also by age-related biological changes, social roles, living arrangements, and broader cultural contexts. This combination of stability, which was reflected in the moderate-to-high test–retest reliability, and contextual sensitivity aligns with our conceptualization of ToE as a domain-specific belief system shaped through ongoing interaction between individual and sociocultural factors. The clearest example of contextual sensitivity emerged for Encouraging Social Communication, on which living style produced the largest mean difference (Table 6A). This result can be interpreted that shared living contexts provide more opportunities for shared meals and may thereby reinforce beliefs that emphasize communication during eating. By contrast, Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating showed no significant demographic main effects (Table 6B), suggesting that these orientations may be shaped more strongly by individual or situational factors not captured by basic sociodemographic variables.

Construct validity and distinctiveness of ToES

The concurrent validity analyses using EMES [42] and the DEBQ [51] revealed a pattern of selective associations that supports the construct validity of the ToES (Table 8). Theoretically expected associations—such as those between Respecting Food on the ToES and Awareness and Appreciation for Food on the EMES, between Enriching One’s Heart and Emotional Eating/External Eating on the DEBQ, and between Enriching One’s Heart and Nonreactivity on the EMES are conceptually understandable, as these pairs reflect related underlying ideas.

In contrast, the associations between Encouraging Social Communication or Realizing Oneself and Health of the Planet on the EMES were less straightforward. Drawing on Schwartz’s theory of basic human values [46], Encouraging Social Communication, Realizing Oneself, and Health of the Planet can be broadly aligned with benevolence, self-direction, and universalism, respectively. In Schwartz’s circular model, these value domains are positioned adjacent to one another, indicating conceptual compatibility. From this broader value-based perspective, the observed associations appear theoretically coherent.

The relatively strong correlation between Realizing Oneself and Restrained Eating on the DEBQ is also noteworthy. Previous studies have associated restrained eating with body dissatisfaction and low self-esteem [53–55], which in turn have been linked to disordered eating attitudes and a desire to attain an idealized body image [56]. One possible interpretation is that dissatisfaction with one’s body or self may foster a desire for self-improvement, which could be reflected in higher scores on Realizing Oneself and may manifest behaviorally as restrained eating. Consistent with this interpretation, prior research has shown that women tend to invest more time and effort in achieving their ideal appearance [57]. In this study, women scored higher on Realizing Oneself (Table 6). Future research incorporating direct measures of body image and self-esteem would help clarify these relationships. In addition, body image concerns have been linked to external influences, such as exposure to social media [56], which may affect self-esteem through mechanisms described in Self-Determination Theory [58, 59]. From an applied perspective, these findings suggest the need to consider approaches that support individuals in making autonomous and healthy eating choices [60].

The generally weaker and fewer associations observed for Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating with the EMES and DEBQ subscales are consistent with discriminant validity. The EMES and DEBQ primarily assess mindful or behaviorally engaged eating styles, whereas Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating capture more detached or aversive orientations that fall outside the scope of these instruments. The moderate negative correlation observed between Disliking Eating and EMES Awareness and Appreciation for Food (r = − 0.43) further supports this interpretation.

Overall, the ToES captures domain-level orientations toward eating that are related to, yet not fully overlapping with, the behavioral and mindfulness-focused constructs measured by existing instruments. The weaker associations observed for Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating suggest that the scale assesses orientations that overlap only partially with prior measures.

Associations between ToE and eating behaviors

We also examined associations between ToES dimensions and eating behaviors (Table 7). Although most regression models predicting specific eating behaviors showed modest coefficients of determination (R² ≈ 0.07–0.09; with R² = 0.43 for frequency of shared meals), the observed associations were theoretically coherent and consistent with the proposed positioning of ToE. This pattern supports the validity of the scale while underscoring that eating behaviors are jointly determined by belief orientations, sociodemographic context, and other factors not assessed in this study.

Both Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating showed meaningful relationships with behavioral patterns. For example, individuals who reported lower indifference and more positive engagement with eating tended to eat more regularly. In contrast, those who were indifferent to or disliked eating were more likely to report irregular meal patterns. In addition, participants with lower Indifference to Eating scores were more likely to share meals with others. These findings are notable because these two dimensions are key features of the ToES and were related to many of the eating behaviors assessed in this study. In this sense, the ToES offers a somewhat different perspective on eating behavior. Although eating disorders have been extensively studied and instruments such as the Eating Attitude Test [33] are well established, the ToES makes it possible to examine another form of atypical eating orientation, namely, individuals who are disengaged from or actively dislike eating, rather than those who display classic disorder-related symptoms.

Cultural specificity and cross-cultural implications

Another noteworthy contribution of this study is identifying the Respecting Food factor. This dimension appears to reflect a cultural background, particularly of Japan. Respect for food is often linked to animistic beliefs that have shaped Japanese culture for centuries. For example, rice, the traditional Japanese staple, has been symbolically associated with the soul of a deity in ancient Japan [61]. In everyday practice, many Japanese people say itadakimasu (いただきます) before a meal and gochisosama deshita (ごちそうさまでした) afterward to express gratitude for the spirits or life force of the food and for those who prepared it [62]. This ethos is also reflected in the Basic Act on Shokuiku (Food and Nutrition Education Act) (Act No. 63 of 2005). Such attitudes are commonly taught at home and in schools. Previous research has shown that educational experiences are related to the level of appreciation for food [63].

Although mealtime gratitude and respect for food are not unique to Japan, their cultural framing varies. In Christian traditions, mealtime gratitude is typically directed toward God rather than toward the spirits or life force of food itself, suggesting a distinct framing of food appreciation.

By contrast, within East Asia, food and eating are commonly embedded in collective and relational practices, consistent with the broader emphasis on interdependent self-construal in this cultural region [64]. In Chinese family meals, for example, elders are typically served first, and the sequence of eating retains historical connections to worship activities involving food offerings to ancestors and deities [65]. Similarly, Korean food culture documents extensive integration of food into ritual and seasonal practices [66]. These shared elements, however, primarily concern the social and ritual order surrounding food rather than gratitude toward the food itself. Table etiquette can also differ in ways that reveal distinct orientations even within East Asia: for example, in some regions of China, leaving a small amount of food uneaten signals to the host that one was sufficiently fed and serves as a gesture of politeness toward the host [67], in contrast to the Japanese norm where finishing one’s meal is itself an expression of appreciation for the food.

The present study was conducted in a single national context and did not test cross-cultural differences directly. Relatively few studies have examined gratitude toward food outside the Japanese context, and in this sense the Respecting Food factor may capture a culturally distinctive orientation. Future cross-cultural research using the ToES, particularly comparative studies across East Asian countries, and between East Asian and Western contexts, could shed light on similarities and differences in food-related beliefs and clarify which dimensions are broadly applicable and which require culture-specific adaptation. Where culturally specific meanings prove difficult to capture with a single instrument, locally adapted measures may complement, rather than replace, a common core scale.

Three-level organization of the ToES dimensions

Building on the seven factors identified in this study, we propose a conceptual organization of these dimensions into three conceptual levels based on the type of meaning individuals attach to eating (Fig. 3). This organization is intended as a heuristic for connecting the seven empirically identified dimensions to broader psychological literatures on attitudes, motivation, and culturally embedded meaning, and as a framework for generating future hypotheses.

Three-level conceptual organization of the Thoughts on Eating Scale (ToES) dimensions

Level 1: fundamental evaluative orientations toward eating

The first level captures basic evaluative stances toward eating itself, and it consists of two factors: Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating. This level captures the most basic dimension of how eating is conceptualized as a domain: as positively valenced, neutrally valenced, or aversively valenced. At the belief level, this dimension parallels what attitude theorists have identified as the basic evaluative component of attitudes [14]. This basic dimension has been comparatively underrepresented in existing eating-related measures, most of which presuppose engaged or positive orientation toward food.

Although both factors reflect non-positive views of eating, they differ in tone and implication. Indifference to Eating describes a neutral position in which eating is seen as neither enjoyable nor unpleasant, but simply necessary. In contrast, Disliking Eating involves clear negative affect. Indifference may arise from habit or a routine view of food as fuel, whereas Disliking Eating may point to psychological strain or problematic patterns. If these two orientations can be reliably distinguished, this would clarify that not all low engagement with eating reflects the same underlying process.

Level 2: personally meaningful orientations

The second level includes ways in which eating is personally meaningful. It comprises four factors: Enriching One’s Heart, Encouraging Social Communication, Realizing Oneself, and Sustaining Oneself. These orientations broadly correspond to motivational and meaning-focused constructs identified in prior eating research, such as the affective, social, and self-related motives captured by TEMS [10] and the personal meanings examined in the MFLQ [18], although the present factors emphasize stable belief orientations rather than situational motivations or meaning attributions.

Importantly, Sustaining Oneself does not necessarily imply a positive evaluation of eating. Individuals who feel indifferent toward, or even dislike, eating may still endorse this factor because they eat to maintain physical functioning. In such cases, eating is treated as a practical necessity rather than a valued experience. This sets Sustaining Oneself apart from the other factors at this level, which generally involve some degree of personal meaning or positive valuation.

Level 3: culturally embedded meanings

The third level consists solely of Respecting Food, which reflects socially and culturally shared meanings. This dimension aligns with Social Identity Theory [68], which proposes that individuals internalize group norms and values to maintain a coherent sense of self. It is also related to the subjective norm component of the Theory of Planned Behavior [7], which concerns perceived social expectations about behavior.

At this level, eating is shaped by collective beliefs, traditions, and moral frameworks. Cultural contexts influence how food is understood, whether through religious dietary practices, rituals of gratitude, or concerns about environmental and animal welfare. Thus, motives captured at this level are inseparable from the broader cultural setting in which individuals live.

This three-level organization may be useful in several ways from a practical perspective. First, it can guide hypothesis development. For example, it is possible that Level 1 factors are more strongly associated with disengagement-related behaviors such as skipping meals. Second, this structure can help intervention design, as optimal intervention strategies might differ depending on whether the target involves basic aversion, personal meaning, or cultural values. Third, the structure can assist in scale selection by clarifying whether a study aims to assess the fundamental stance, personal orientation, or culturally embedded meaning.

Empirical tests of this organization, for example, examining whether the three levels show distinct patterns of cross-cultural variation, represent a direction for future research.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, ToE was assessed using the self-report ToES, which necessarily captures only conscious, verbally expressible thoughts about eating. Eating-related beliefs may also operate at an implicit level. To better understand how implicit processes contribute to eating behavior and eating disorders, future research could incorporate indirect methods, such as the Implicit Association Test [69], to examine automatic associations with food and eating.

Second, all participants were recruited through a web-based crowdsourcing platform. Although we attempted to balance the sample by sex and age group, individuals who use such platforms may differ from the general population in their patterns of internet use, communication styles, and lifestyles [44]. Online environments and digital habits may also influence eating behaviors. Examining the relationship between ToES and internet use could therefore provide additional insight. In addition, having voluntary participants introduces the possibility of self-selection bias [70, 71]. It is possible that individuals with an interest in food or psychology are more likely to respond, potentially limiting the study’s generalizability. We recommend that future studies consider probability-based sampling or statistical weighting to address these concerns.

Also, the Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating factors showed a moderately high inter-factor correlation (r = 0.54) and, in the independent replication sample (N = 527), loaded onto a single factor rather than two separate ones. Although we retain them as conceptually distinct factors—reflecting disengagement versus active aversion toward eating—the current evidence does not unequivocally support their statistical independence. Accordingly, findings involving these subscales should be interpreted with caution. Future research should examine whether an expanded item pool or alternative factor representations can more clearly differentiate these two orientations.

A further step in clarifying the theoretical position of ToE is to examine its relationship with broad personality traits. The present research focused on establishing the dimensional structure of ToE and providing initial evidence of construct validity. Ongoing work is investigating associations between ToE dimensions and the Big Five traits. Preliminary results suggest moderate, theoretically consistent correlations, for example, between Encouraging Social Communication and Extraversion, and between Respecting Food and Agreeableness, with effect sizes typical of intermediate-level constructs (r = 0.26–0.37). These findings are consistent with the view that ToE is related to personality while remaining conceptually distinct. Full analyses of mediation and incremental validity will be reported separately.

Finally, the Respecting Food subscale comprises only two items, below the conventional psychometric minimum of three per factor, and findings involving this subscale should therefore be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, we retained the factor in the present scale on both empirical and theoretical grounds. Empirically, both items exhibited high factor loadings (0.859 and 0.787), the factor replicated as a distinct dimension in an independent sample of 527 participants (Supplementary Tables S1, S2), which indicates a direct test of structural stability beyond item count alone, and the factor showed the strongest convergent validity among all ToES factors (r = 0.57 with EMES Awareness and Appreciation for Food; Table 8). Theoretically, this factor was not pre-specified at the outset of the research; we did not design items to capture food-related reverence or animistic gratitude, and this orientation emerged inductively from participants’ free-text responses (Sect. "Item generation") and subsequently appeared consistently across samples. To our knowledge, no existing eating-related scale captures this dimension systematically. We therefore document this factor in its present form as a provisional but empirically grounded finding, and recommend expanding the subscale with additional culturally grounded items for future applications, particularly cross-cultural comparison. More broadly, the unanticipated emergence of this factor suggests that culturally and religiously embedded orientations toward eating, whether rooted in animistic traditions, religious dietary practices, or collective moral codes, may constitute a distinct class of ToE dimensions that existing Western-developed scales have not systematically addressed.

Implications and applications of ToES

The ToE construct offers a concise way to describe domain-specific belief orientations toward eating that are not captured by behavior-focused measures alone. In research contexts, the ToES may help connect trait psychology with eating-behavior research by providing a construct that sits between broad dispositions and specific actions. It can also be used to stratify samples and examine sociocultural patterns across groups. In applied health communication, the construct may support more tailored approaches by aligning recommendations with individuals’ predominant orientations, while recognizing that cross-sectional findings do not permit causal conclusions.

This study established the reliability and validity of the ToES in a general adult sample. However, its applicability to clinical populations has not been tested yet. ToE captures broad belief orientations about eating. Therefore, it may offer additional insight into disordered eating among individuals with eating disorders or obesity. We suggest that future research examine the scale’s validity in clinical samples and compare patterns with those observed in non-clinical groups. For example, it is possible that individuals with eating disorders score higher on Realizing Oneself, reflecting their heightened concerns about body image and weight control. Moreover, longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether ToES scores are associated with the onset or severity of eating disorders and to evaluate the scale’s potential role in clinical assessment and intervention planning.

Conclusion

This study developed and validated the Thoughts on Eating Scale (ToES), operationalizing Thoughts on Eating (ToE) as a domain-specific belief construct positioned between broad personality traits and specific eating behaviors. Across two independent quantitative samples of Japanese adults, the ToES yielded seven replicable dimensions: Enriching One’s Heart, Encouraging Social Communication, Realizing Oneself, Sustaining Oneself, Respecting Food, Indifference to Eating, and Disliking Eating, with adequate internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and theoretically coherent associations with established measures of mindful eating and eating behavior. Indifference to Eating and Disliking Eating, in particular, capture evaluative stances that are not well represented in existing eating-related measures.

We propose that the seven dimensions can be organized conceptually into three levels: fundamental evaluative orientations, personally meaningful orientations, and culturally embedded meanings, offering a structure that connects belief-level individual differences to broader traditions in attitude, values, and cultural psychology. Future work should examine the ToES in clinical samples and in cross-cultural settings, and should test its incremental and mediational value relative to broad personality traits.
