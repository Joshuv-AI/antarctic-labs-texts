Integrative genomic analysis and gene expression patterns reveal a cardio-neuroendocrine signaling network for heat adaptation in geographically diverse chickens
Authors: Ali Hassan Nawaz, Qiqian Cui, Jiqiang Ding, Shaokat Ali, Dong Leng, Nasir Mukhtar, Phatthawin Setthaya, Niraporn Chaiwang, Zheya Sheng, Diyan Li, Thobela Louis Tyasi, Yiqiang Zhao, Chungang Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndigenous chickens in tropical regions routinely survive high environmental temperatures (40–45 °C) that cause significant mortality and production loss in commercial breeds, yet the genetic mechanisms of thermotolerance remain poorly understood. This study integrated genome‐wide selective scans across 14 geographically and climatically diverse chicken breeds with multi-tissue expression data, gene expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) analysis, transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS), and cross-species phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) to validate candidate genes. We identified 25 high-confidence genes under selection, with ATP1A1, PLCB4, RYR2 and AKT3 forming a regulatory hub coordinating cardiovascular, calcium and survival signaling. These genes converge on interconnected adrenergic, calcium, and GnRH signaling pathways, with coordinated expression across heart, hypothalamus, and liver forming an integrated thermoregulatory axis. The eQTL integration analysis using ChickenGTEx data identified 359 tissue-specific cis-eQTLs in selected regions. Additionally, TWAS analysis linked ATP1A1 to 145 gene-trait associations across 13 tissues and 14 trait categories (hepatic regulation, β = -2.13, p = 4.21 × 10⁻¹²), and cross-species PheWAS validated conserved roles in cardiovascular function (RYR2, resting heart rate p = 4.9 × 10⁻¹²), and ionic homeostasis (ATP1A1, chloride p = 1.18 × 10⁻³). In parallel, we also identified robust genomic signatures of domestication in classic candidate genes (TSHR, TBC1D1, BDNF), highlighting how initial separation from Red Jungle Fowl and subsequent adaptation to diverse climates have shaped the genetic and physiological diversity of the domesticated chicken. Collectively, our results reveal an integrated cardio‐neuroendocrine calcium network driving heat adaptation, providing potential targets for breeding heat‐tolerant chickens.Keywords: Chicken, Heat adaptation, Selective sweep, ChickenGTEx, TWAS

Introduction

Climate change has emerged as one of the pressing issues around the globe that threatens global poultry production and food security. In particular, tropical and subtropical regions are most vulnerable to climate change where rising temperatures and heat waves are predominantly common (Nawaz et al., 2021; Camacho et al., 2024). Most significant impacts of heat stress on poultry production include growth impairment (Nawaz et al., 2023), immune suppression, reduced egg and meat production while significantly increasing mortality rates (Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). Chickens are most susceptible to heat stress as they lack sweat glands. Recent intensive breeding and selection focused on growth and production have further accelerated their vulnerability due to higher metabolic rates (Brugaletta et al., 2022). Consequently, heat stress negatively affects carcass traits (El-Tarabany et al., 2021) chicken well-being (Brugaletta et al., 2022) and severely damages reproductive (Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023), digestive and immune systems (Zmrhal et al., 2023; Ncho, 2025). With rapidly rising global temperatures by each year, the poultry industry faces an urgent challenge to develop effective strategies for heat adaptation. Understanding a population’s ability to tolerate high temperatures is critical for comprehending the boundaries of its geographical distribution and its susceptibility to climate-related challenges (Guo et al., 2022; Nawaz et al., 2024).

The chicken was domesticated approximately 4,000 to 6,000 years ago, since then chickens spread across the globe and developed into multiple breeds with a variety of phenotypic traits, physiology and behavior (Rubin et al., 2010; Wang et al., 2020; Peters et al., 2022) These chicken breeds have evolved according to their specific geographical regions, environmental pressures and human needs. In recent years, selective signature studies have been widely used to identify genetic variants associated with specific traits such as heat adaptation. Several studies have shown that chickens living in harsh environments possess specific genomic signatures that may contribute to their survival and adaptation (Gu et al., 2020). Using a comparative genomic strategy, a recent study identified various genes under selection including FABP2, RAMP3, SUGCT, and TSHR in indigenous chickens from tropical regions of China, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, and Thailand, which may be associated with heat adaptation (Guo et al., 2022). While another study reported that genes involved in DNA repair, cellular stress responses, apoptosis, and metabolic oxidative stress were under selection in indigenous chickens, suggesting the polygenic nature of thermotolerance (Zhuang et al., 2020). Moreover, a recently published study identified many positively selected genes involved in energy balance and metabolism, cell apoptosis, angiogenesis, skin protection to solar radiation and growth, confirming that heat adaptation is a polygenic trait involving various metabolic, physiological and genetic parameters (Assiri et al., 2025). However, most selection sweep studies investigate few breeds or single geographic region, making it difficult to distinguish local or breed-specific mechanisms from broadly shared mechanisms of heat adaptation (Gu et al., 2020; Rachman et al., 2024). Moreover, previous studies have largely been limited to detecting selection signatures, and only a few have integrated tissue-specific expression, regulatory variation, and trait-level associations to comprehensively investigate the physiological functions that enable chickens to adapt to high environmental temperatures.

In regions such as South Punjab, Pakistan, summer temperatures can reach up to 50 °C far exceeding the thermoneutral range for optimal poultry production (https://www.pmd.gov.pk/en/). Likewise, indigenous chicken populations in other hot regions including southern China (up to 42 °C) (https://www.cma.gov.cn/en/) and Thailand (up to 40 °C) (https://www.tmd.go.th/en/), commonly experience high temperatures for extended periods. Populations historically residing in these hot regions present an excellent opportunity to study genetic and physiological mechanisms involved in heat adaptation. Here, by employing these geographically diverse chicken populations, we conducted genome-wide selective sweep analysis and then validated the functional relevance of our sweep‐prioritized genes. We integrated tissue‐resolved RNA-seq (ChickenGTEx) to map candidate gene activity across key thermoregulatory organs including the heart, hypothalamus, and liver (Guan et al., 2025). We then integrated TWAS results from ChickenGTEx to link genetically predicted expression levels of these genes with heat-tolerance and production traits, thereby establishing direct gene phenotype relationships. Finally, we performed PheWAS in human GWAS cohorts to assess pleiotropy and cross-species conservation of key genes across cardiometabolic and stress-response traits. This integrated approach reveals that heat adaptation in chickens is regulated through coordinated networks linking cardiovascular control, reproductive performance, and cellular homeostasis, rather than through isolated genetic changes.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

We collected blood samples from four indigenous chicken breeds while for other 10 breeds we downloaded the publicly available whole genome data. Among these four breeds (sampled in this study), two from Southern Punjab, Pakistan including the Aseel (AS, n = 30) and Desi (DS, n = 10), while the other two from Thailand: Pradu Hang Dam (PD, n = 30) and Thai native (TH, n = 30) (Table S1). These regions experience some of the extreme weather, with peak summer temperatures regularly exceeding 40°C, as detailed in our environmental data (Table 1). Consequently, we followed standard protocols for blood collection and adhered to institutional animal care guidelines. DNA extraction was performed, checked for DNA quality using 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and NanoDrop 2000 (A260/280 ratio), and finally libraries were sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7 platform. Average sequencing coverage of newly sequenced samples is 26 × with Q20 and Q30 quality scores exceeding 99 % and 97% (Table S1).

Variant calling and data filtering

We used the commercial FPGA hardware acceleration platform GTX-One developed by Genetalks for sequence alignment and variant detection in all 272 chicken samples. GTX-One utilizes heterogeneous acceleration technology where CPUs and FPGAs work in tandem, enabling full replication of the optimal BWA-GATK variant detection workflow while reducing computational time (Bu et al., 2020). The GTX-One's ‘gtx wgs’ command was used to generate gVCF files for the resequenced samples based on the Gallus_gallus.bGalGal1.mat.broiler.GRCg7b reference genome. This corresponds to the workflow combining BWA and GATK's HaplotypeCaller (Poplin et al., 2017). Subsequently, the GTX-One's ‘gtx joint’ command was employed to perform joint variant detection across all gVCF samples, yielding genotype VCF files (corresponding to GATK's GenomicsDBImport and GenotypeGVCFs workflow).

Following joint genotyping, we retained only biallelic SNPs and applied stringent quality control filters. Genotype-level filtering was performed using PLINK v2.0, removing variants with genotype missingness >0.1 (–geno 0.1). To fill missing genotypes, we performed phasing and imputation using Beagle v5.5 (01Mar24.d36) (Browning et al., 2018, 2021). After imputation, we applied a minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold of 0.01 to remove rare variants, yielding a final dataset of 16,171,515 SNPs for downstream selective sweep analyses. SNP density and distribution across chromosomes are visualized in Fig. S2.

Principal component (PCA) and admixture analysis

To minimize the influence of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on population structure inference, we performed LD based SNP pruning using PLINK v1.9 with parameters –indep-pairwise 50 10 0.2, retaining SNPs with an r² < 0.2 using 50-SNP sliding windows (10-SNP step). The pruned data set was used as input for ADMIXTURE v1.3.0 to estimate ancestry proportions for each individual (Alexander et al., 2009). We tested ancestral clusters (K) from 2 to 9. Cross-validation (CV) errors were calculated for each K, and the optimal K was determined by the value with the lowest CV error.

Population structure was further characterized using PCA on a subset of 70,000 SNPs selected for high differentiation between hot-adapted and cold-adapted populations. SNPs were extracted using PLINK v1.9 and PCA was performed on the pruned dataset using PLINK (Purcell et al., 2007).

Genetic differentiation (FST) and linkage disequilibrium decay analysis

Based on quality-controlled data, we calculated the pairwise FST in all chicken breeds using VCFtools (v0.1.16). Generally, FST values range from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates that the population is not differentiated, and 1 indicates that the population is completely differentiated. Genetic differentiation was divided into four different degrees according to the FST value: low (<0.05), medium (0.05–0.15), high (0.15–0.25), and extreme (>0.25). To assess patterns of linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay within each breed, we calculated pairwise LD statistics using PopLDdecay (v1.0). Analyses were conducted separately for each population using quality-controlled data. Correlation coefficient r² was used as measure of LD in this study with SNP pairs up to a maximum distance of 4,000 kb (-MaxDist 4000) (Zhang et al., 2019).

Runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis

ROH analysis was conducted using PLINK v1.9 package. The –homozyg module makes ROH calls using a pre-defined sliding window to detect homozygous stretches. In this study we used a minimum ROH length of 1 Mb, at least 50 consecutive SNPs per segment, and allowed for a maximum of one heterozygous and five missing calls per window (Weng et al., 2022). Selective sweep analysis

To detect genomic regions under selection, we applied four complementary population genetic statistics including FST, nucleotide diversity ratio (π ratio), Tajima’s D difference, and cross-population extended haplotype homozygosity (XP-EHH) for all pairwise population comparisons. All analyses were conducted in sliding windows of 40 kb with a step size of 10 kb. For each method, we defined the top 2 % of windows as significant, thereby focusing on high-confidence regions and minimizing the influence of background noise.

The π ratio was used to highlight genomic regions where strong reductions in nucleotide diversity pointed to recent selective sweeps. Per-population nucleotide diversity (π) was first calculated using VCFtools, the π ratio was then defined as π_reference / π_selected. Windows with high π ratios were interpreted as lower diversity in the selected population than in the reference.

Tajima's D difference (ΔTajima's D) was computed to capture shifts in the site frequency spectrum indicative of selection (Johnsson et al., 2016). Positive ΔTajima’s D values indicate more negative Tajima’s D in the putatively selected population than in the reference population.

XP-EHH was calculated using Selscan v1.3 to detect ongoing or recent population specific selective sweeps based on haplotype structure (Fu et al., 2016; Nam et al., 2017; Hall, 2022). XP-EHH was computed with a maximum inter-SNP gap of 200 kb (–max-gap 200000). XP-EHH scores were then summarized in 40 kb sliding windows with 10 kb steps to maintain consistency with other methods. Positive values indicate evidence of selection in selected populations, while negative values indicate selection in the reference population. For ΔTajima’s D and XP-EHH, Z-normalized values were calculated to facilitate the identification of candidate selective sweeps.

After the completion of selective sweep analysis, we extracted coding genes in top 2% windows and annotated them using the GRCg7b genome assembly reference GTF file.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment was performed for candidate genes under selection to get biological insights. Both gene ontology (GO) and kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using KEGG orthology-based annotation system (KOBAS 3.0) (http://bioinfo.org/kobas/). Afterwards, protein-protein interaction (PPI) network for the final high confidence candidate genes were constructed using STRING v12.0.

Integrated ChickenGTEx data for functional validation

Following genome wide selective sweep analyses, we prioritized a core set of candidate genes enriched in five key pathways repeatedly identified across multiple contrasts. To validate and characterize the functional relevance of these genes, we implemented a comprehensive multi-layer integrative approach leveraging ChickenGTEx resource.

Expression profiling and correlation analyses. Gene expression profiles across thermoregulatory and relevant physiological tissues were retrieved from the ChickenGTEx database (Guan et al., 2025), which provided RNA-sequencing data across diverse tissues including hypothalamus, heart, muscle, liver, brain, adipose, retina, trachea, and oviduct. Gene-gene correlation was constructed using Pearson correlation coefficients across tissue expression profiles followed by hierarchical clustering with Euclidean distance and complete linkage to elucidate co-regulatory modules and functional connectivity among the prioritized genes (Oyelade et al., 2016; Liu et al., 2019).

eQTL and TWAS validation. To assess regulatory variation and gene-trait associations for the prioritized genes, we integrated existing eQTL (chicken_gtex_v0.significant_cis_eqtl.txt.tar.gz; accessed on July 23, 2025) and pre-computed transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) results derived from the ChickenGTEx project. We downloaded corresponding files from the ChickenGTEx database (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/chickengtex/) and extracted significant associations for top candidate genes identified in selective sweep analyses. We focused on the top four genes with strong selection and expression evidence for detailed assessment of eQTL distribution, effect sizes, and TWAS signals. ChickenGTEx eQTLs were used to annotate tissue-specific regulatory context for candidate genes. As these data provided independent qualitative evidence of regulatory potential rather than direct quantitative effect size correlations.

Cross-species phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) validation

To extend the functional insight to translational relevance, human GWAS summary data was downloaded and explored for our top candidate genes. This approach assessed pleiotropic effects and associations with a broad spectrum of complex traits and diseases, including cardiometabolic, immune, neurobehavioral, and reproductive phenotypes. Comparative PheWAS results provided cross-species support for candidate gene functions implicated in heat stress resilience.

Allele frequency differentiation

For the four top candidate genes (ATP1A1, PLCB4, RYR2, AKT3), we extracted all biallelic SNPs and per-gene allele frequencies (AF) were calculated separately for hot and cold region populations using VCFtools –freq. Population differentiation was quantified as ΔAF = AF-hot – AF-Cold for each SNP. Strong differentiation was defined as ΔAF > 0.15, representing the extreme tail of the genome-wide ΔAF distribution.

Results

Population structure, genetic differentiation and linkage disequilibrium patterns

Principal component analysis showed genetic differentiation among the four population groups including hot-dry, hot-humid, cold and RJF (Fig. 2A). Specially, hot region breeds and cold region breeds are clearly separated by PC1. The population structure analysis separated the chicken populations based on their climatic classification. This population structure pattern strengthens our confidence that chicken breeds from diverse genetic background may have universal genomic landscape when it comes to environmental adaptation. The heat map of pairwise FST analysis showed low to moderate genetic differentiation among the breeds with FST values ranging from 0.01 to 0.13 (Fig. 2B). Hot region breeds were found to have a lower pairwise FST among themselves (0.015 to 0.10) suggesting common ancestry or gene flow (Fig. 2B). Cold region breeds (BY, TB, DG, BN) exhibited somewhat higher FST values among themselves (0.057 to 0.102). Comparisons between hot and cold groups generally yielded higher FST values up to 0.13 (Fig. 2B). Moreover, the admixture analysis across K = 2 to K = 7 revealed hierarchical population structure that progressively resolved finer-scale genetic differentiation (Fig. 2C). At K = 4, most of the breeds began to separate, while K = 7 identified as most appropriate model (Fig. 2D).

Inbreeding and genetic diversity patterns

In addition, the results of inbreeding coefficients (FIS) in 14 chicken breeds showed a wide range of population genetic structure with outbred populations (PD, TH and NY) having negative values of FIS and most inbred breeds crossing the threshold of 0.08 (Fig. 2F). Runs of homozygosity (ROH) analysis in 14 chicken breeds identified variations in genomic inbreeding (Fig. S3A). The average and total lengths of ROHs in each chicken population are shown in Figure S3. Average length ranged from approximately 1.2 Mb in the most outbred populations to over 1.7 Mb in inbred chicken breeds (Fig. S3B). FROH analysis, representing the proportion of the genome in homozygous runs, demonstrated the substantial differentiation among breeds, with values ranging from approximately 0.001 to over 0.166 (Fig. S3C). The low FROH values in WN (0.003) and GY (0.008) suggest genetic diversity through crossbreeding, while the high FROH in HX, BY and DG indicate severe population bottlenecks or intensive inbreeding. The relatively low FROH in hot-region populations suggests larger effective population sizes, providing a conservative demographic background that strengthens the adaptive interpretation of the convergent selective signals detected. Consistently, we found relatively stable MAF across all breeds, ranging from 0.11 to 0.15 (Fig. S4), suggesting that allelic diversity has been largely preserved.

Genome-wide selective sweep analysis for domestication (hot vs RJF)

FST analysis between heat-adapted domestic chicken populations and Red Jungle Fowl (RJF) identified the top 2% genomic differentiation regions (FST > 0.11), with peak FST values ranging from 0.25 to 0.40 predominantly located on chromosomes 1, 3, 5, 14, and 24 (Fig. 3A). Within these significant regions, we annotated 392 genes (Table S3). Similarly, the π ratio analysis (π hot/π RJF) identified 408 genes within the top 2% of genomic regions (Fig. 3B). The Tajima's D difference analysis identified 360 candidate genes, primarily distributed across chromosomes 5, 14, 18, and 28 (Fig. 3C), while the XP-EHH analysis identified 512 genes (Fig. 3D and E). All the genes identified through the four methods are listed in Table S3. Cross-method validation revealed varying levels of overlap among different analyses (Fig. 3F), while nine genes were detected by all four selective sweep approaches (Table S4). Notably, well-known domestication genes, including thyroid stimulating hormone receptor (TSHR), brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and TBC1 domain family member 1 (TBC1D1), were identified across three methods (Karlsson et al., 2016; Fallahshahroudi et al., 2021; Chen et al., 2022). For each comparison, candidate genes identified by at least two of the four selection detection methods were retained for downstream functional enrichment analysis to ensure robustness and cross method consistency.

Although the primary comparisons focused on environmental adaptation, signals related to domestication can confound climate-associated sweeps. Therefore, we performed an additional selective sweep analysis comparing humid-region breeds with RJF, which is also native to humid climates. Our analysis consistently identified some classical domestication genes including TSHR, TBC1D1, IGF1, IGF2BP1 and FGFR1 (Fig. S5). All the genes identified in this group comparison are listed in Table S5 and S7.

Genome-wide selective sweep analysis (hot vs cold)

FST analysis between hot and cold-adapted chicken populations identified the top 2% genomic differentiation regions with peak FST values ranging from 0.23 to 0.25 on chromosomes 1, 3, 4, and 18 (Fig. 3G). This moderate differentiation in hot vs cold populations was significantly lower than the differentiation in hot vs RJF comparison. Within these significant regions, we annotated 437 genes as strong candidates for environmental temperature selection. Nucleotide diversity ratio analysis (π hot/π cold) identified 377 genes in the top 2% of genomic regions (Table S7), with extreme ratios exceeding 3.5 fold differences (Fig. 3H). Tajima's D difference analysis identified 445 candidate genes on chromosomes 1, 8, 14, and 18 (Fig. 3I), and XP-EHH analysis detected 499 genes (Fig. 3J). All the genes identified in this group comparison are listed in Table S7.

Cross-method validation found varying levels of overlap among the four approaches (Fig. 3L), with 69 genes detected by three methods, while three genes (AGMO, ARHGAP5 and BMPR2) were identified by all four approaches (Table S8).

Genome-wide selective sweep analysis (hot-dry vs hot-humid)

To understand adaptation to humidity variation within hot environments, we compared hot-dry and hot-humid populations. FST peaks reached 0.25 on chromosomes 1, 3 and 8 (Fig. S6A), while π ratio analysis highlighted reduced diversity in hot-dry populations across these same regions (Fig. S6B). Tajima's D differentiation revealed candidate loci on chromosomes 3, 5, and 24 (Fig. S6C). XP-EHH analysis detected significant extended haplotype homozygosity in hot-dry populations relative to hot-humid populations, with selective sweep signals enriched on chromosomes 4 and 27 (Fig. S6D and S6E). All the genes identified through the four methods are listed in Table S9.

Together, these methods identified a combined set of high-confidence candidate genes under selection associated with adaptation to differing hot and humid environments, retaining those detected by at least two methods for enrichment analysis. Cross-method comparison revealed substantial overlap, with 44 genes detected by three methods (Table S10), including phospholipase c beta 4 (PLCB4), and calcium voltage-gated channel auxiliary subunit alpha2d1 (CACNA2D1), which encode calcium signaling components, and receptor activity modifying protein 3 (RAMP3), a vasodilatory GPCR accessory protein (Fig. S6F). These findings indicate robust selective signatures involving cardiovascular and osmoregulatory pathways.

Functional enrichment analysis of candidate genes identified through selective sweep analysis

We performed comprehensive functional enrichment of selective sweep identified candidate genes using hot vs cold (354 genes, overlap in at-least two selective statistics) and hot vs RJF (370 genes, overlapped in at least two selective statistics) and hot-dry vs hot-humid chicken populations (351 genes, overlap in at least two selective statistics). The analysis of KEGG pathways of the three groups showed a consistent significant enrichment of gene networks involved in cellular signaling, metabolic regulation and stress response mechanisms (Fig. S7A, B, C). Specifically, 5 key pathways were repeatedly enriched in all 3 comparisons that comprise adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, GnRH signaling pathway, apelin-signaling pathway, calcium signaling pathway, and vascular smooth muscle contraction. These mechanisms are also known to play a part in physiological adaptation and thermal resilience.

Hot vs RJF group showed an enrichment of AGE-RAGE signaling, FoxO signaling, MAPK signaling pathways and cell adhesion molecules pointing out the metabolic, stress response and neuroendocrine remodeling (Table S11). Hot vs cold comparison results indicated that mRNA surveillance pathway and glycerophoshpolipid metabolism and linoleic acid metabolism among others were highly enriched to indicate metabolic adaptations that are necessitated by climate changes (Table S12). While hot-dry vs hot-humid group showed overlapping but unique enrichment patterns that were consistent with microclimatic adaptation, and included pathways related to synthesis of bile acids and melanogenesis (Table S13).

GO term enrichment provided the understanding of the cellular and molecular processes of adaptation (Table S11-S13). Cellular component analysis primarily linked candidate genes to basic cellular structure, cytoplasm, nucleus, cell surface and plasma membrane as the most enriched. The molecular function analysis indicated that binding of the calcium ions was the most enriched in all the groups, which supported substantial role of calcium signaling in thermotolerance. Besides, the activity of ATP binding and signaling receptors was also significantly enriched which shows that energy metabolism and cellular communication are the crucial requirements of climate adaptation and domestication (Table S11-S13).

Pathway convergence, tissue-resolved gene expression, and network connectivity reveal a coordinated axis

To identify core regulatory genes from our selective sweep results, we prioritized 25 candidate genes based on consistent selection signatures across multiple methods, enrichment in five heat-response pathways and tissue resolved gene expression (Fig. 4A; Table 2). PLCB4 showed the broad connectivity with all five core-signaling pathways. RYR2, ATP1A1 and AKT3 emerged as secondary hubs that have complementary multi-pathway profiles (Fig. 4B). The involvement of ATP1A1 in adrenergic, GnRH and calcium signaling pathways indicates its role in the maintenance of ionic gradients that are important for cellular functioning (Xiao et al., 2009). While AKT3 was enriched in both adrenergic signaling and the apelin signaling pathway suggesting its crucial involvement in cell survival and metabolic homeostasis during stress (Savova et al., 2023). Compared to these multi-pathway integrative genes, more specific components such as CACNA2D1, TACR3, and HRH1 were enriched in a focused way mostly within the calcium signaling and cardiac muscle contraction pathways (Fig. 4B), indicating strong evolutionary pressure on the calcium handling process. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) indicated that there was a tight interaction module containing PLCB4, MAPK, AKT3, and calcium channel constituents (CAMK2D, RYR2, CACNA2D1) and coherent sub- clusters made up of stress regulators (NOS2, BECN1) (Fig. 4C). These findings indicate hierarchical information flow from receptor signaling to calcium mobilization, and cytoprotective transcriptional activity, precisely the coordinated strategy predicted for heat adaptation (Srikanth et al., 2019; Sinclair-Black et al., 2023).

Expression patterns of the 25 candidate genes in nine tissues suggested functional differentiation (Fig. 4D). Genes linked to calcium regulation such as CACNA2D1 in addition to ATP1A1 and PLCB4 were highly expressed in the heart and skeletal muscle. We found that ADCY1 and ADCY8, ATP1A1 and PLCB4 are highly expressed in the brain and hypothalamus, indicating that they play vital roles in cardiovascular and hormonal responses. The expression of cellular stress response genes (AKT3, MAPK and EGFR) and antioxidant regulators (NRF1 and BECN1) was higher in the metabolically active tissues including the liver and trachea (Fig. 4D). The correlation of the genes showed that RYR2 was cardiac-specific and CACNA2D1 highly enriched in muscle and the heart (Fig. 4E, F), which is also aligned with the increased demand of Ca2+ cycling and membrane gradient homeostasis to maintain the cardiac output and thermoregulation (Eisner et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024). While signaling genes showed tissue specialized patterns with RAMP2 and RAMP3 exhibited neuroendocrine-cardiac expression, while ADCY1 and ADCY8 were highly expressed in hypothalamus and retina (Fig. 4E, F), supporting cAMP and GPCR-mediated coupling between central control and peripheral vascular tone that facilitates heat dissipation (Tian et al., 2020; Deng et al., 2021). Moreover, stress and survival mediators (AKT3, MAPK13, MAPK14, EGFR, PLA2G4A, and BECN1) demonstrated expression across the liver, brain, trachea, and adipose, demonstrating metabolic buffering and cellular protection (Goel et al., 2021; Pritchett et al., 2023).

Collectively, these expression patterns reveal an integrated cardio-neuroendocrine axis in which cardiac Ca2+ cycling (RYR2), membrane ion homeostasis (ATP1A1), tissue specialized calcium regulation (CACNA2D1, PLCB4), neuroendocrine-vascular signaling (RAMP2, RAMP3) and cellular stress responses (MAPK14, EGFR, BECN1) coordinate across heart, muscle, brain, liver and trachea to sustain thermotolerance (Mota-Rojas et al., 2021; An et al., 2025).

Gene correlation matrix and regulatory variants

Among these, four genes (ATP1A1, PLCB4, RYR2, AKT3) emerged as central hubs based on their pathway participation, network connectivity, gene expression and rich regulatory variants. We examined allele frequency differences (ΔAF) across all SNPs in ATP1A1, PLCB4, RYR2, and AKT3 between hot vs cold area chicken populations (Fig. 5A). In total, 1,450 SNPs displayed strong differentiation (ΔAF > 0.15), demonstrating strong selective pressure. ATP1A1 showed 249 (6.2 %; 220 in hot Population; 29 in cold population) highly differentiated SNPs, PLCB4 showed 812 (11 %; 171 in hot; 641 in cold) highly differentiated variants. RYR2 and AKT3 demonstrated 250 (3.5 %; 163 in hot; 87 in cold) and 139 (3.1 %; 43 in hot; 96 in cold) differentiated variants, respectively (Fig. 5A). Allele frequency results revealed bidirectional selection pattern which includes selection for beneficial alleles at ATP1A1 and RYR2 and, thus, increasing their frequency in hot populations, while selecting against maladaptive alleles at PLCB4 and AKT3 and, thereby, decreasing their frequency in hot populations. The higher number of differentiated SNPs in key genes with possible regulatory potential (as evidenced by ChickenGTEx data) is consistent with observations in African cattle, where positive correlations between allele frequency differences and cis-eQTL effect sizes suggested that positive selection acts directly on regulatory variants (Friedrich et al., 2024).

Using ChickenGTEx reference data, we annotated 359 cis-eQTLs across AKT3, ATP1A1, RYR2 and PLCB4, in multiple tissues, indicating expression-level regulatory architecture (Fig. 5B). Significance and effect-size distributions indicated that a substantial number of eQTLs (n = 359, FDR<0.05) achieved high statistical support with moderate to large effects (0.09–0.93 SD) (Fig. 5C and D). The leading eQTL associations clustered in metabolically and physiologically active tissues (Fig. 5E). ATP1A1 has significant regulatory association in liver (n = 35 eQTLs, top SNP: p = 2.1 × 10⁻¹⁰, β=0.38 SD), consistent with hepatic ATP demand and myocytic ion pumping under heat stress (Kang and Shim, 2021; Ahmed-Farid et al., 2021). AKT3 top eQTL localized to bursa (n = 6 eQTLs, top SNP: p = 2.5 × 10⁻⁸, β=0.47 SD), aligning with immune tissue regulation and stress-responsive signaling (Oni et al., 2024). PLCB4 strongest regulatory link in the trachea (n = 112 eQTLs, top SNP: p = 3.0 × 10⁻⁶, β=0.29 SD) reinforces its role in respiratory tissue adaptation to sustained panting-mediated evaporative cooling. It is crucial to note here, these eQTLs are from ChickenGTEx and they suggest regulatory potential of these genomic regions rather than direct evidence or variant discovery linked to heat response.

TWAS and PheWAS associations reveal ATP1A1, AKT3, RYR2 and PLCB4 as key regulator for thermal adaptation

Genomic scans using FST revealed strong selection signals at ATP1A1, AKT3, RYR2 and PLCB4, highlighting the importance of these genes in adaptation (hot vs cold) (Fig. 6A-D) as they were also detected by other selection methods. Integrative TWAS data from ChickenGTEx revealed that ATP1A1 exhibited the most extensive profile of significant trait associations, spanning 145 associations across 13 tissues and 14 trait categories (strongest: hepatic eggshell regulation, p = 4.21 × 10⁻¹², β = −2.13) (Fig. 6E). In contrast, AKT3 showed limited TWAS signals (4 associations across 2 tissues and 3 trait categories), while PLCB4 and RYR2 demonstrated minimal association (1 association in 1 tissue) with cardiovascular trait. Effect-size and significance further highlighted robust ATP1A1 associations in the liver, adipose tissue, retina, and macrophages (Fig. 6F). Additionally, TWAS trait associations showed ATP1A1 was associated with cardiovascular, metabolic, immune, and growth traits. In contrast, PLCB4 showed associations primarily with cardiovascular traits, whereas RYR2 was linked to both cardiovascular and egg production traits (Fig. 6G).

Cross-species phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) analyses using human GWAS datasets revealed significant pleiotropic effects for all four genes influencing various trait domains including cardiometabolic health, immune and inflammatory traits, cognitive-behavioral domains, growth, skeletal development, and reproduction (Fig. 6H; Table S14-S17). These multifunctional associations support the integrated physiological response to passive heat stress in humans, where cardiac output must double to maintain blood pressure despite systemic vasodilation, requiring coordinated cardiovascular, metabolic, ionic, and immune system adjustments (Crandall and Wilson, 2014). RYR2 showed significant cardiovascular associations, with resting heart rate (p = 4.9 × 10⁻¹²) and pulse rate (p = 4.1 × 10⁻⁸) representing the top signals (Fig. S8). ATP1A1 showed strongest association for white blood cell count (p = 5.3 × 10⁻¹⁷) along with various metabolic traits (chloride homeostasis, p = 1.18 × 10⁻³, chronic kidney disease) validating its role in ionic homeostasis and metabolic regulation. Body mass index (p = 4.6 × 10⁻¹²) and body composition traits directly related to adaptation were found to be significantly associated with AKT3 pheWAS. PLCB4 exhibited associations including metabolic traits (body mass index, p = 2.2 × 10⁻¹⁰, body fat percentage, waist circumference) and cardiovascular phenotypes (blood pressure medication use, p = 3.3 × 10⁻⁷), consistent with its role in vascular tone regulation and lipid metabolism (Table S14-S17).

Discussion

Our PC findings demonstrated visible separation between hot region, cold region and ancestral RJF populations. This finding reveals how chicken populations have undergone differentiation following domestication, consistent with previous studies that domestic chickens experienced significant population size decline during early domestication despite maintaining higher genetic diversity (Rubin et al., 2010; Xu et al., 2022, 2023). The observation of extended LD in domestic breeds as compared to RJF points to the demographic bottlenecks during the domestication of these breeds.The varying degree of inbreeding (FROH: 0.001-0.166) observed in this study is an indication of the intensive Selection which has led to diverse genomic backgrounds. On the contrary, the genetic diversity of domestic breeds has been largely preserved regardless of the varying levels of inbreeding, which suggests genetic material for ongoing local adaptation has been maintained (Elferink et al., 2012; Guo et al., 2022). The identification of consistent adaptation signatures among breeds further strengthens our confidence that the identified selection targets suggest genuine adaptive evolution. These findings are in line with recent genomic studies showing that chickens in various tropical climates have developed similar adaptations, demonstrating the polygenetic nature of environmental adaptation (Tian et al., 2020; Xu et al., 2022).

Selective sweep analysis comparing domestic breeds against ancestral RJF revealed classical signals of domestication that are widely known to be involved in endocrine regulation, neural plasticity, and cardiovascular remodeling (Rubin et al., 2010; Karlsson et al., 2016). Some of the high-confidence classic domestication genes that have been identified are TSHR, which regulates behavioral adaptations such as reduced fear, reproductive timing (Rubin et al., 2010; Karlsson et al., 2016); BDNF and TBC1D1, which involved in neural plasticity and metabolic responses (Katz and Meiri, 2006; Manjula et al., 2018); and EGFR, which is responsible for growth and production adaptation during modern breed development (Qanbari et al., 2019). We further demonstrated that subsequent selection under different environments has reshaped physiological networks to support adaptation. This study identified convergent evolutionary pressure on interconnected pathways controlling calcium homeostasis, adrenergic cardiovascular regulation, and neuro-endocrine signaling with ATP1A1, AKT3, RYR2 and PLCB4 as major regulators. The integration of calcium signaling, adrenergic signaling in cardiomyocytes, and GnRH neuroendocrine pathways emerges as a coherent and multifaceted adaptive network that underlies thermal adaptation in chickens (Fig. 7) (Tian et al., 2020).

Integrated molecular signaling pathways underlying heat adaptation in chickens. This diagram illustrates how coordinated cardio neuroendocrine system helps chicken in heat adaptation. High temperatures activate the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal (HPG) axis, which increases PLCB4 expression. This boosts intracellular Ca²⁺ signaling and maintains GnRH release. At the same time, high temperature causes cardiomyocytes and skeletal muscle fibers to adapt by using calcium-signaling components (ATP1A1, PLCB4, CACNA2D1) to keep the Na⁺ gradient and speed up Ca²⁺ cycling, increase contractile force. G-protein-coupled receptors (AGTR1, RAMP2, RAMP3), adenylate cyclases (ADCY1, ADCY8), protein kinase A (PKA), AKT, and MAPK14 pathways work together to activate hypothalamic neurons and control survival, metabolic adaptability, and relaxation of vascular smooth muscle.

Calcium signaling is essential in mediating muscle contraction, neurotransmission, and cellular stress responses. The negative influence of adverse environmental factors on the chickens’ productivity and well-being has been documented, requiring various physiological adjustments. Established results associate calcium signaling with neuro-endocrine and cardiovascular adaptations; therefore, a strong augmentation of calcium signaling pathway, jointly, adrenergic signaling and GnRH cascades, indicates a multi-layered, integrated approach to heat adaptation. In this perspective, our analysis revealed key players involved in Ca²⁺ binding and calcium homeostasis; CACNA2D1 and ATP1A1, which have been previously identified as being under evolutionary selection in hot regions (Tian et al., 2020; Kim et al., 2021). ATP1A1 was identified to be linked to the maintenance of ion gradients and osmotic equilibrium in different tissues, as well as contributing to thermotolerance in cattle (Glorioso et al., 2007; Kashyap et al., 2015). Remarkably, the continuous expression of ATP1A1 in the nine tissues examined ascertains its probable involvement in thermal stress adaptation. In comparison, PLCB4 and RYR2 displayed tissue-specific expression patterns, still expressing them in coexisting physiological reactions (Passamonti et al., 2021; Juiputta et al., 2023; Capone et al., 2025). Furthermore, the role of RYR2 was found to be crucial in adverse climatic patterns, as evidenced by a recent selective sweep study in the Arabian Peninsula chicken (Assiri et al., 2025).

The GnRH signaling pathway enrichment is vital to reproductive adaptations that help to maintain sustained fertility and egg production even in hot conditions. The key role of GnRH signaling is to coordinate the reproductive timing with the environment via the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis (HPG), which is an essential component of the endocrine network (Lu et al., 2021; Huang et al., 2024). It was found in previous studies that the secretion of GnRH from the hypothalamus was impaired at elevated temperatures, hindering reproductive performance in chickens (Lu et al., 2021). Interestingly, our TWAS analysis showed that there were significant interrelations between RYR2, ATP1A1 and AKT3 variants and traits associated with egg yield (ATP1A1: 145 hits including eggshell weight p < 10⁻⁸, strength p = 4.21 × 10⁻¹²), and thickness p = 3.55 × 10⁻¹¹), which indicated a direct genetic relationship between ionic homeostasis and cellular stress response to thermal-resilient egg reproduction. This connection is physiologically plausible, since thermal stress cuts egg production up to 13.6–36.4 %, by interfering with calcium-mediated mechanisms which are important in thermoregulation as well as shell deposition (Loengbudnark et al., 2023; Kim et al., 2024). The linkage of ATP1A1 to eggshell quality identified the mechanisms of ion transport as the sources of cellular thermal defense and the processes by which calcium-based shell calcification could be mechanistically coupled to the heat-adaptive reproductive output. This coordination, by GnRH action along with calcium signaling pathways, as a part of calcium homeostasis, promotes thermoregulation at both the cellular level and reproductive functioning, eggshell calcification and oviduct activity (Ebeid et al., 2012). Likewise, genetic selection has been shown to simultaneously enhance heat tolerance and egg yield as evidenced by a recent study of Thai indigenous chickens (Loengbudnark et al., 2023). Our results indicated genetic overlap between thermotolerance and fertility traits indicating that evolutionary selection favored variants that maintain reproductive fitness, even when exposed to heat stress which is a critical adaptation mechanism for the indigenous breeds in hot regions.

In addition, the cardiovascular response to heat stress is based on the adrenergic signaling pathway. Studies have reported that the integrated cardiovascular responses are integral for adaptation to elevated temperatures, as they trigger essential autonomic adjustments to stabilize blood pressure (Crandall and Wilson, 2014). In contrast, individuals with suboptimal cardiovascular status show heightened vulnerability to extreme climatic conditions, lacking effective adaptability traits (Liu et al., 2022). In the perspective of these physiological evidences, our TWAS findings revealed linkage between adrenergic pathway genes (RYR2, ATP1A1, AKT3) and cardiovascular parameters, with AKT3 exhibiting gene-trait associations spanning both heart and neural tissues (p = 2.51 × 10⁻⁸, β = 0.42), directly emphasizing its involvement in regulating cardiac stress responses (Thompson et al., 2016; McDougald et al., 2019). AKT3 mediates antioxidant defense response and corresponding metabolic adaptation (Zeng et al., 2014; Ke et al., 2017), reflecting selective pressure to maintain heart function during increased hemodynamic requirements of thermoregulation (Taniyama et al., 2005).

This interplay is further supported by gene expression data, with genes related to adrenergic responses such as ADCY1, ADCY8, AKT3, and MAPK14 showing coordinated patterns between the hypothalamus, heart, and muscle tissues, to facilitate the cardiovascular heat dissipation responses. Moreover, ADCY1 and ADCY8, highly expressed in the brain tissues, aid rapid vasodilation and boost cardiac output to better cope with thermal stress (Smit et al., 2016; Juiputta et al., 2023). Vasoconstriction and dilation play a significant role in dissipating excessive heat via blood pressure regulation, ensuring internal homeostasis in chickens (Lara and Rostagno, 2013). Moreover, MAPK14 is also a part of this network, given as a complementary stress-responsive signaling, and is involved in the coordination of cellular oxidative stress responses to chronic heat. (Liu et al., 2024). This synchronized adrenergic system shows clearly that heat adapted chickens are able to mobilize cardiovascular resources such as increased cardiac output, peripheral vasodilation and optimal distribution of blood flow rapidly.

Our comprehensive integrative genomic analyses provide strong correlative evidence, but have some limitations. First, as a population genomics study, the inferred cardio-neuroendocrine network and candidate gene-trait relationships are correlative rather than causative, and functional studies are needed to confirm these relationships. Second, although we accounted for population structure, the fact that geographically diverse breeds have distinct ancestry may lead to residual stratification across breeds that partially confounds climate adaptation signals. Third, our functional interpretation relies on baseline expression data from ChickenGTEx (Guan et al., 2025) rather than heat-stress experiments in our study breeds. So we cannot confirm or claim the exact causal variants under selection in our indigenous breeds, nor can we quantify temperature associated expression changes. However we used ChickenGTEx data to propose the broader regulatory architecture of heat tolerance candidate genes, an approach that has been scientifically validated in recent livestock genomic studies including chicken (Zhu et al., 2026, 2025), cattle (Niu et al., 2025) and pig (Zhou et al., 2025).

Conclusions

This study revealed an integrated network of signaling pathways coordinating cardiovascular function, reproductive capacity, and cellular homeostasis that drives heat adaptation in chickens. Independent selection statistics converged with gene expression and tissue-specific regulatory patterns across 359 eQTLs to identify four genes ATP1A1, PLCB4, RYR2, and AKT3 as regulatory hubs under sustained evolutionary pressure, with each playing distinct physiological roles. Here, most notably is the shared genetic architecture linking cardiovascular performance to egg production, where natural selection appears to favor regulatory pathways preserving reproductive function during thermal stress through calcium homeostasis, cardiac performance, and eggshell calcification. By combining population genomics with tissue-resolved expression, regulatory variation, and cross-species phenotypic validation, we demonstrate that adaptation to chronic environmental stress rewires existing signaling pathways rather than inventing new mechanisms offering concrete genomic targets for future studies and breeding programs addressing climate change challenges.
