The Lomb–Scargle periodogram-based differentially expressed gene detection along pseudotime
Authors: Hitoshi Iuchi, Michiaki Hamada
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-cell RNA sequencing has provided high-resolution snapshots of biological processes and has contributed to the understanding of cell dynamics. Trajectory inference has the potential to provide a quantitative representation of cell dynamics, and several trajectory inference algorithms have been developed. However, the downstream analysis of trajectory inference, such as the analysis of differentially expressed genes, remains challenging. Here, we present scLS, a Lomb–Scargle periodogram-based framework for two differential expression tests: a dynamic expression test for pseudotime-associated variation and a shifted expression test for condition-dependent differences in pseudotime-indexed expression trajectories. Because scLS operates in the frequency domain, it does not require specification of an explicit regression model and can be applied to inferred tree-structured trajectories without explicit branch assignment. We validated this approach using simulated data and real datasets, and our results showed that scLS achieved competitive performance and complementary sensitivity to transient or complex pseudotime-associated patterns. Our approach provides a computationally efficient first-pass screening framework that can be combined with lineage-aware analyses for detailed biological interpretation.

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) provides high-resolution snapshots of biological processes and allows the observation of cellular state transitions, such as cell differentiation, cell cycle, and stimulus-response, at the single-cell level [1]. Indeed, trajectory inference, which is a computational technique based on single-cell transcriptomes for predicting biological processes, has advanced our understanding of cell dynamics across multiple disciplines, such as neuronal development [2, 3], immune cell differentiation [4], and state transitions in pathogenic organisms [5]. Bioinformatics plays a central role in enabling such analyses and in translating these technical advances into biological insight.

To date, bioinformatic efforts have focused on developing trajectory inference algorithms [6]. Most trajectory inference approaches involve the following steps: (i) reducing the dimensionality of the gene cell matrix; (ii) assigning each cell to a lineage in the reduced space; and (iii) assigning a pseudotime based on the distance of each cell from the starting point. Many trajectory inference algorithms have been developed with variations in dimensionality reduction and lineage assignment [6]. Despite the active development of trajectory inference, methodologies for downstream analyses are lacking.

After trajectory inference, differentially expressed genes (DEGs) are typically identified via a downstream analysis. In time-series omics data analysis, there are two patterns of DEGs: genes whose expression changes dynamically along pseudotime, and genes whose pseudotime-dependent expression pattern is shifted between two conditions (e.g. wildtype versus knockout). In this paper, we refer to the corresponding hypothesis tests as the dynamic gene expression test and the shifted gene expression test, respectively. Although the dynamic gene expression test is the mainstream test developed for time-series omics data analysis, shifted gene expression is also important for characterizing changes in gene expression patterns by comparing wild type and knockout and by the presence or absence of stimuli. Notably, DEGs in trajectory analyses can be formulated in multiple ways, including changes localized to specific neighborhoods/transitions or differences in the fraction of cells occupying particular branches between conditions. Accordingly, downstream methods should be selected to match the biological definition of differential change of interest. In this study, we aimed to develop an algorithm that can perform both dynamic and shifted gene expression tests.

Additionally, the development of scRNA-seq experiments and analysis methods generates complex trajectories, such as tree structures with branches, and there is a need for novel downstream analyses to address these datasets. Typical downstream analyses include tradeSeq [7], PseudotimeDE [8], and Lamian [9], which fit explicit regression models (e.g. negative-binomial GAMs or hierarchical likelihood) for gene expression as a smooth function of pseudotime. Although such regression-based approaches are the mainstream algorithms, they do not always provide a convenient representation of complex gene expression patterns associated with tree-structured trajectories containing multiple branching points. In this work, we propose scLS, which uses the Lomb–Scargle (LS) periodogram to represent gene expression patterns in the frequency domain [10, 11]. The fast Fourier transform, which is often used in time-series analysis, requires uniformly sampled points. However, the pseudotime estimated from trajectory analyses is typically non-uniform; thus, fast Fourier transform-based approaches are not directly applicable. Here, the LS periodogram can transform time-series data with non-uniform sampling points into frequency-domain data. The key practical distinction from conventional approaches is that scLS operates in the frequency domain and does not require specifying a regression model or explicit branch assignments. This allows scLS to handle complex gene expression patterns, including branching structures, flexibly, and without the need for regression model selection. Here, scLS is designed to screen for genes exhibiting any pseudotime-associated dynamics in datasets whose inferred trajectories are tree-structured, without requiring explicit branch assignment. Branch-resolved interpretation is outside the scope of scLS itself and can be obtained by downstream lineage-aware analyses. Consequently, it is a suitable method for the downstream analysis of diverse trajectory inference results.

This study introduces an LS periodogram-based algorithm for identifying DEGs associated with pseudotime. Our approach involves transforming pseudotime-domain data from scRNA-seq and trajectory inference into frequency-domain data using LS. This versatile method can be applied as a gene-level screening test to inferred trajectories, including tree-structured trajectories with multiple branching points. We validated this approach using simulated and real datasets, demonstrating its efficacy as a robust, model-independent tool for trajectory analysis.

Materials and methods

A schematic diagram of the proposed method is shown in Fig. 1. In this scenario, the gene expression of the WT is branched, which is difficult to model as a function; however, the proposed method can still be applied as a global gene-level screening test, even when the inferred trajectory includes branching or a combination of branching and non-branching structures.

Top: Dynamic expression test. Gene expression values indexed by pseudotime (which can be non-uniformly sampled and may arise from branching trajectories) are transformed into a frequency-domain representation using the LS periodogram. For each gene, false-alarm probabilities (FAPs) are evaluated over a predefined frequency grid, and the gene-level P-value is defined as the minimum FAP across scanned frequencies (equivalently, the FAP associated with the maximum LS power). Gene-level P-values are then adjusted across genes using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to identify dynamically expressed genes. Bottom: Shifted expression test. LS periodograms are computed separately for two conditions (e.g. WT and KO), and the distance between their power spectra (e.g. Euclidean or cosine distance) is used as the observed test statistic. Statistical significance is assessed by pooling cells from both conditions and randomly reallocating complete cell-level pseudotime-expression pairs to two pseudo-groups of the original sizes (equivalently, permuting condition labels while preserving each cell’s paired pseudotime and expression values). This generates a null distribution of the same distance statistic, from which a one-sided P-value is computed using a normal approximation based on the permutation-derived mean and variance. This framework does not require explicit branch assignment.

The dynamic gene expression test

Pseudotime-domain gene expression data are transformed into the frequency domain using Equation (1). For each gene, we calculate the LS periodogram using a predefined frequency grid. We then define the gene-level P-value as the minimum FAP across the scanned frequencies (equivalently, the FAP associated with the maximum periodogram power). We control multiple testing across genes using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure, and a gene is considered dynamic if the q-value is significant. In other words, if a gene has a significant power spectrum, our method regards it as being associated with pseudotime. The FAPs for each frequency in the dynamic expression test were calculated via the approximate values given in Zechmeister and Kürster [14] using Astropy [13].

The shifted gene expression test

The shifted gene expression test evaluates whether the pseudotime-indexed expression trajectory of a gene differs between two conditions. For each gene, the LS periodogram is computed separately for the WT and KO groups, and the distance between their power spectra (e.g. Euclidean or cosine distance) is used as the observed test statistic. This full-spectrum comparison treats the periodogram as a representation of the overall expression trajectory along pseudotime rather than as a set of individually interpreted frequency components, enabling the detection of broadband differences such as transient or multi-peak shifts. To assess statistical significance, we pool cells from both conditions and randomly reallocate complete cell-level pseudotime-expression pairs to two pseudo-groups with the original group sizes (equivalently, we permute condition labels while preserving each cell’s paired pseudotime and expression values). Recomputing the same spectrum-distance statistic across these permutations yields a null distribution under the null hypothesis that condition labels are exchangeable, i.e. that the two conditions share the same joint distribution of pseudotime-expression pairs for that gene. The mean and variance of this permutation-derived null distribution are then used to fit a normal approximation, and the P-value is computed as the right-tailed probability of observing a distance at least as large as the observed value. Because inference is calibrated using the same full-spectrum distance statistic under group-label permutation, adding spectral components is not expected to inflate type I error, although it may reduce power.

Implementation and availability

scLS is distributed as an R package on GitHub (https://github.com/hiuchi/scLS), with comprehensive documentation, a vignette, and direct support for Seurat-object inputs. The package is released under the MIT license. The source code for all experiments conducted in this paper is also available on GitHub (https://github.com/hiuchi/Iuchi_and_Hamada_2026).

Simulation data synthesis and trajectory inference

In brief, simulation data for the performance tests were generated using the dynverse framework [6] to synthesize count data, after which the trajectory and pseudotime were estimated using Monocle3 version 1.3.1 [15] and Slingshot version 2.0.0 [16]. All count data were generated using dyno version 0.1.2 or dyntoy version 0.9.9. The count data for Fig. 2 were generated using the generate_dataset(model=”linear” or ”bifurcating”, num_cells=250 or 500 or 1000, num_features=1000, differentially_expressed_rate=0.5) function in the dyntoy package. The values generated by the rnorm(mean = 1, sd = 0.4) function, which outputs a normal distribution with a mean of 1 and standard deviation of 0.4, were multiplied by the count to generate noise. These data include the correct label indicating whether a gene is a DEG and the pseudotime assigned to each cell.

DEG detection by comparative methods

These analyses were performed using default parameters unless otherwise stated.

tradeSeq

tradeSeq version 1.7 uses a negative binomial generalized additive model framework to model gene expression for each lineage. Modeling was performed using the fitGAM function (nknots=6). For comparisons between two groups, each group was analyzed as a lineage. The created models were evaluated using the associationTest, startVsEndTest, diffEndTest, patternTest, and earlyDETest (knots = c(1, 3)) functions to obtain P-values. For the dynamic expression test, we used associationTest (tests whether gene expression is associated with pseudotime along a lineage) and startVsEndTest (tests for differential expression between the start and end of the lineage). For the shifted expression test, we used three tradeSeq tests: earlyDETest (tests whether gene expression differs in the early part of pseudotime), diffEndTest (tests whether gene expression differs near the end of pseudotime), and patternTest (tests whether gene expression differs in overall expression patterns along pseudotime).

Lamian

Lamian version 0.99.1 is a framework that implements statistical analysis for various pseudotime analyses. We used the lamian_test(permuiter = 100) function with test.type=”chisq” and test.type=”time” for determining whether gene expression was associated with pseudotime and for comparing the two groups, respectively.

Practical analysis with a Drosophila dataset

To verify the characteristics of the scLS dynamic expression test on real data and identify its differences from other methods, we reanalyzed Drosophila scRNA-seq data [17]. The dataset was downloaded from the URL provided in the original paper. We performed trajectory inference using Monocle3, setting the most undifferentiated cell population (the root cluster, as defined in the original paper) as the root cell and calculating pseudotime for each cell. We then performed differential expression analysis using both scLS and the tradeSeq::startVsEndTest function, the latter being used as a representative baseline to contrast endpoint-difference detection with transient-pattern detection. Finally, we visualized representative gene expression trends along the pseudotime axis.

Practical analysis with a Trypanosoma dataset

In order to verify the characteristics of the scLS shifted expression test on real data and to identify its differences from other methods, we reanalyzed Trypanosoma scRNA-seq data [5]. The analyzed dataset, including PHATE embedding and trajectory analysis using Slingshot, was kindly provided by Ross Laidlaw, University of Glasgow [18]. In this dataset, since the developmental regulator ZC3H20 is knocked out, ideally the expression pattern of ZC3H20 should differ significantly between WT and KO. DEG analysis of the dataset was performed using scLS and tradeSeq.

Comparison of computation time

We benchmarked the computational runtime of the dynamic and shifted expression tests. In the dynamic setting, single-cell RNA-seq data were generated using dyntoy with 1000 cells and 2000 genes along a linear trajectory. In the shifted setting, paired WT and KO datasets were generated using dyngen on the linear backbone, with transcription factor knockdown from time 0 in the KO condition; each condition was simulated with 1000 cells and the gene set comprised 2000 genes shared between WT and KO. In both cases, ground-truth pseudotime was derived directly from the simulator-provided milestone networks and cell progressions, without performing trajectory inference, and rescaled to the unit interval. We evaluated scLS, tradeSeq, and Lamian in both settings, selecting for each method the test(s) appropriate to the corresponding biological question (dynamic expression along pseudotime or condition-dependent shifts along pseudotime). Computational runtime was assessed by measuring wall-clock elapsed time for each complete analysis, repeated three times under identical single-core execution conditions.

Results

Simulation dataset

Simulation data were used for benchmarking under controlled conditions (Figs 2 and 3). Although these synthetic data were cleaner than actual experimental data, they were valuable for performance comparisons. These simulation data were generated for benchmarking the dynamic expression test (Fig. 2) and the shifted expression test (Fig. 3). We conducted benchmarking using not only the correct pseudotime based on the ground truth but also the pseudotime estimated by Monocle3 and Slingshot. Because accurate estimation of pseudotime remains difficult, we compared performance under imprecise pseudotime settings, which are closer to practical analyses (Supplementary Figs S1 and S2).

The dynamic gene expression test

Using the simulated datasets described above, we assessed the performance of each method in a dynamic expression test to determine whether each gene was dynamically expressed along pseudotime. Note that pseudotime estimated by Monocle3 and Slingshot is subject to variation depending on the trajectory-inference method (Supplementary Fig. S1). To clarify the effect of each trajectory inference method and cell number, the accuracies of the linear trajectory (Fig. 2A and Table 1) and bifurcating data (Fig. 2B and Table 2) were evaluated using ROC curves across these conditions. The low-noise synthetic data resulted in AUC values close to 1 for most conditions. The near-ceiling AUC values in this simulation largely reflect its low-noise, high-effect-size nature, under which several methods achieve almost perfect discrimination and small AUC differences should not be over-interpreted. In summary, methods that functioned successfully demonstrated high performance regardless of cell number or trajectory-inference method. Additionally, to conduct a fairer comparison, we performed a benchmark using the datasets employed in the PseudotimeDE study [8] (Supplementary Fig. S3). In this scenario, scLS, PseudotimeDE, and tradeSeq’s associationTest demonstrated high detection performance, with AUC values generally above 0.9. PseudotimeDE showed the highest performance across all conditions, while scLS generally demonstrated comparable performance. These results indicate that scLS is robust to pseudotime estimation methods and can detect genes with pseudotime-dependent expression with performance comparable to PseudotimeDE and tradeSeq (associationTest).

The shifted gene expression test

Next, we assessed the detection accuracy of shifted gene expression under two conditions (WT and KO) (Fig. 3). The accuracy of pseudotime estimation using Monocle3 and Slingshot is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. In this scenario, C1_TF1 is knocked out, so the expression of C1_TF1 and its downstream genes should be detected as DEGs. Therefore, we assessed whether C1_TF1 and its downstream genes could be detected as DEGs. The shifted test is intrinsically more challenging than the dynamic test, because it targets between-condition differences in the full pseudotime-dependent trajectory. In bifurcating settings, perturbations can induce branch-dependent and broadband changes, for which full-spectrum comparisons can be particularly informative without requiring explicit branch correspondence. In this comparison, scLS showed competitive performance under most conditions (Fig. 3A and B, and Tables 3 and 4). These comparative methods are not always well-matched to shifted-expression analysis in bifurcating settings, especially when explicit branch correspondence across conditions is unavailable. The strong performance of scLS in such settings suggests robustness to complex condition-dependent trajectory differences.

Practical analysis with a Drosophila dataset

Next, to confirm the characteristics of the scLS dynamic expression test and its applicability to real experimental data, we analyzed Drosophila scRNA-seq data with a bifurcating developmental trajectory [17]. Using publicly available data, we performed trajectory inference with Monocle3 and calculated pseudotime. The results yielded a gradient in which pseudotime continuously increased along the low-dimensional embedding, reproducing a structure in which state transitions progress (Fig. 4A). Next, to identify DEGs along pseudotime, we performed dynamic expression tests using scLS and tradeSeq::startVsEndTest and calculated q-values. Comparing the q-values from scLS with those from tradeSeq::startVsEndTest revealed that they did not always agree. Here, we focused on genes that had a relatively small q-value in one method but a relatively large q-value in the other (Fig. 4B). Representative examples include FBgn0040827, FBgn0031277, FBgn0030158, and FBgn0262009 (Fig. 4C, upper panel; genes shown in skyblue). These genes exhibited a pattern of steeply rising expression in the latter half of pseudotime and a monotonically increasing trend toward the endpoint. These represent the type of variation strongly detected by startVsEndTest, characterized by large expression differences between the start and end points. Conversely, FBgn0034659, FBgn0035569, FBgn0051406, FBgn0034739, FBgn0036488, FBgn0264344, FBgn0038978, and FBgn0035585 (lower panel of Fig. 4C; genes shown in magenta) exhibited peak-type (transient) behavior, reaching a maximum around the middle of pseudotime and then declining. These tended to show relatively lower significance when evaluated solely by the start–end difference using startVsEndTest. In summary, tradeSeq::startVsEndTest tends to pick up genes with large differences between the start and end points, whereas transiently rising genes are specifically detected by scLS.

Practical analysis with a Trypanosoma dataset

Next, to evaluate the performance of the scLS-shifted expression test using real experimental data, we compared each method on a Trypanosoma scRNA-seq dataset [5]. The authors knocked out ZC3H20 , which is a key factor in the cell-form transition between the replicative slender form and the transmissible stumpy bloodstream form, and performed scRNA-seq (Fig. 5A) [5]. Therefore, ZC3H20 expression was absent in the KO strain. We compared scLS with three tradeSeq tests (patternTest, earlyDETest, and diffEndTest). As expected for a knockout gene, ZC3H20 showed very low P-values in scLS and in patternTest/earlyDETest, whereas diffEndTest, which focuses on end-point differences, was less significant (Fig. 5B). This indicates that the proposed method can identify genes that are expected to be detected. In addition, the variation in P-values between methods indicated that the results of DEG analysis in trajectory analysis varied from method to method.

Evaluation of runtime

To evaluate the computational efficiency of scLS, we compared its computation time with existing methods under two settings: the dynamic expression test and the shifted expression test (Fig. 6A and B). For the dynamic expression test, each method was applied to linear trajectory data consisting of 1000 cells and 2000 genes. The results showed that scLS exhibited shorter execution times than tradeSeq (associationTest and startVsEndTest) and Lamian (Fig. 6A). Notably, whereas Lamian required the longest computation time among the methods, scLS completed the analysis in <1 s, demonstrating high computational efficiency. In the shifted expression test, scLS completed the analysis in computation times comparable to or shorter than tradeSeq’s various tests (diffEndTest, patternTest, and earlyDETest) and Lamian (Fig. 6B). Conversely, Lamian exhibited the longest computation time even in the shifted test, indicating a higher computational load than scLS and tradeSeq-based methods. These results demonstrate that scLS possesses high computational efficiency compared with existing pseudotemporal gene expression analysis methods under both dynamic-expression-test and shifted-expression-test settings.

Discussion

A central contribution of this study is introducing frequency-domain analysis into scRNA-seq trajectory analysis. Unlike many conventional omics time-course experiments, which often contain relatively few sampling points, trajectory inference provides large numbers of cells ordered along a pseudotime axis and therefore offers a setting in which LS-based spectral representations are practically useful under irregular sampling. In this context, scLS uses the periodogram not to interpret biological periodicity, but as a compact representation of pseudotime-dependent expression structure that can be compared across genes or conditions.

The advantage of our proposed method is that it is a generic approach that is not limited by the trajectory structure. In complex biological phenomena, the trajectory structure may differ between WT and KO; for example, the trajectory may diverge into multiple branches or branch multiple times. It is difficult to model such complex structured trajectories and compare them in two groups; however, by transforming each condition-specific pseudotime-indexed expression profile into a spectral representation, scLS reduces the comparison to a distance between power spectra without requiring explicit branch assignment. For the shifted test, the null distribution is constructed by pooling cells from both conditions and randomly reallocating complete cell-level pseudotime-expression pairs to two pseudo-groups of the original sizes, which is equivalent to permuting condition labels while preserving within-cell pairing. Accordingly, scLS.shift should be interpreted as a global test of whether the observed condition-specific trajectories differ at the level of cell-level pseudotime-expression pairs. In practice, this makes the test sensitive to broad condition-dependent trajectory differences without requiring explicit branch matching. On the other hand, existing methods such as tradeSeq also have their advantages. If gene expression in the early phase of pseudotime is not important but is important in the late phase, the diffEndTest of tradeSeq is a promising candidate. As previously stated, the critical issues in DEG analysis are defining the genes to be extracted and selecting the appropriate DEG analysis algorithm [19].

As practical guidance, scLS is most useful as a model-independent first-pass screening method when the goal is to prioritize genes with pseudotime-associated dynamics, especially transient or non-monotonic patterns, under irregular pseudotime sampling or when explicit branch correspondence is unclear. In contrast, regression-based or branch-aware methods may be more appropriate when the biological question requires lineage-specific localization of expression changes, testing predefined regions of pseudotime such as endpoints, modeling sample-level or replicate structure, or making branch-resolved interpretations. Thus, scLS should be viewed as complementary to these approaches rather than as a replacement for them.

The approach used in this study for detecting DEGs using frequency components helps reduce the need for arbitrary branch correspondence decisions. To test whether there is a difference in the proportion of cells in two branches of WT and KO, Fisher’s exact test or the chi-squared test may be used, as in Lamian [9]. Such approaches require the branches in WT and KO to be aligned before comparison; however, when the number or topology of branches differs, as shown in Fig. 1, this correspondence is not always obvious. In contrast, scLS can be applied without explicit branch assignment, although it should be interpreted as a global gene-level screening test rather than a branch-resolved analysis.

A distinctive feature of this study is the comparison using not only ground-truth pseudotime but also pseudotime estimated by Monocle3 and Slingshot (Figs 2 and 3). These experiments revealed the influence of pseudotime estimation on the results of DEG analysis. The combination of trajectory-inference and DEG-analysis algorithms is an important issue that needs to be optimized in the future through comprehensive and fair comparisons.

Although we used dyngen as a simulator, many other simulators have been developed, such as Splatter [20], PROSSTT [21], and SymSim [22]. Many of these simulators generate gene expression using differential equations or stochastic models. These simulators can adjust the number of genes, read depth, noise level, and other properties using model parameters. However, it is unclear to what extent these simulators can mimic realistic data. Therefore, in recent years, simulators using generative models trained on real data, such as scGAN [23] and scDesign2 [24], have also been developed. The development of such simulators will further stimulate the development of trajectory inference and downstream analysis.

A potential limitation of this study is that the benchmark was primarily conducted using a scRNA-seq data simulator. Although simulated data were used because experimental data do not have correct labels, they may contain less noise than real data or may fail to mimic real data fully. However, simulation data have the advantage that they can be used to compare performance under controlled conditions, since parameters such as noise level and cell number can be specified. Another limitation is that scLS currently does not explicitly account for biological replicates, which are increasingly common in scRNA-seq studies. Extending scLS to support replicate-aware inference is an important direction for future work. Additionally, the issue in this study is not only accuracy but also the introduction of frequency-domain analysis into trajectory analysis and its compatibility with diverse trajectory structures. In the future, to compare DEG-analysis algorithms under more realistic conditions, comprehensive comparative experiments will likely be conducted by varying conditions such as cell number, noise, and trajectory-inference method. Additionally, our proposed approach involves performing a hypothesis test on the same cells as those used for trajectory estimation; thus, it does not yet have sufficient statistical guarantees. This problem is known as the double-use or double-dipping problem [25], and solutions such as count splitting under a Poisson assumption have been proposed [26]. The double-dipping problem will likely be discussed in more detail in future single-cell analyses. In scLS, the LS periodogram is used as a representation of gene expression patterns along the pseudotime axis rather than for estimating a biologically meaningful period. Consequently, spectral windowing or aliasing is expected primarily to affect statistical power rather than alter qualitative biological interpretation. For the dynamic test, gene-level significance is defined by the minimum FAP across the scanned frequency grid (equivalently, the FAP associated with the maximum LS power), followed by Benjamini–Hochberg correction across genes. For the shifted test, significance is calibrated by pooling cells from the two conditions and randomly reallocating complete cell-level pseudotime-expression pairs to two pseudo-groups of the original sizes, which is equivalent to permuting condition labels while preserving within-cell pairing. The resulting null distribution of the spectrum-distance statistic is then summarized by its permutation-derived mean and variance to compute a right-tailed P-value under a normal approximation. Because scLS produces a global gene-level statistic by aggregating cells along pseudotime, it can detect pseudotime-dependent dynamics in branching trajectories but does not, by itself, localize those dynamics to specific branches or lineages; branch-resolved interpretation requires stratifying cells by lineage or combining scLS with lineage-aware downstream analyses. As future work, scLS could be extended to branch-resolved screening by computing LS spectra within each lineage when reliable branch labels are available and controlling multiple testing across branches and genes. In addition, time-frequency or band-aggregated spectral summaries (e.g. integrating power over predefined frequency ranges) may help characterize more complex dynamics whose spectral content changes along pseudotime.

From a software perspective, given the increasing availability of single-cell datasets comprising millions of cells, and the expectation that dataset sizes will continue to grow, further software-level optimization will be important for future versions of scLS. In particular, reducing Python-dependent runtime components and developing native R/C++ or GPU-enabled backends may improve scalability and facilitate application to increasingly large single-cell trajectory datasets.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced scLS, an LS periodogram-based method for identifying genes associated with pseudotime in single-cell trajectory analysis. scLS provides model-independent gene-level tests for both dynamic expression along pseudotime and condition-dependent shifts along pseudotime. Because it does not require explicit branch assignment, it can be applied to inferred tree-structured trajectories; however, branch-resolved interpretation lies outside the scope of scLS itself. Across simulated and real datasets, scLS showed competitive performance and complementary sensitivity to transient or condition-dependent trajectory differences. These properties make scLS a computationally efficient first-pass screening tool that can be combined with lineage-aware analyses for detailed biological interpretation.
