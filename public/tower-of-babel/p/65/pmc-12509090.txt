Impact of mental health distress on preconception health
Authors: Manasicha Wongpaiboon, Jimia Brown, Jackson Lee Shelton, Tammie M Johnson, Rima Tawk
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMental health distress (MHD) is broadly defined as an emotional state characterized by symptoms of stress, depression, and anxiety whereas preconception health embodies the physical and mental health prior to a pregnancy. MHD among women of reproductive age is associated with an increase in hypertension, diabetes, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity, and alcohol use. These risk factors have been identified as measurable indicators of preconception health. The purpose of the study is to examine the relationship between MHD and preconception health indicators after controlling for sociodemographic and healthcare access characteristics. The 2022 Florida Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System was used. Analysis was limited to women of reproductive age. The main exposure variable involved MHD. Women who reported their mental health not well for ≥ 14 days during the past month were characterized as enduring frequent MHD. Multiple logistic regression analyses were used to assess associations between MHD and each preconception health indicator in separate models. Among women of reproductive age, 22.2 % reported frequent MHD. Our findings showed that nonpregnant women of reproductive age who experienced frequent MHD had worse preconception health indicator outcomes than those with lower levels of MHD, with the greatest disparities detected in fair or poor self-rated health, and physical inactivity (AOR:3.31; p < 0.0001 and AOR:1.78; p = 0.0002, respectively). Older age, minority race, being uninsured, unemployed, divorced, having less than high school education, and financial barriers in healthcare access were associated with worse preconception health outcomes in most models. Increased screening and treatment for mental distress, counseling women about unhealthy behaviors, and adapting behavior change interventions may help optimize the preconception health of women with MHD. Personalizing interventions that would target these preconception modifiable risk factors could lead to improved short-term, long-term, and favorable pregnancy health outcomes among women with MHD.Keywords: Preconception health, Mental distress, Maternal health, Behavioral risk factor surveillance system

Introduction

Preconception health is defined by the overall health of nonpregnant women of reproductive age (18–44 years). It can be measured by multiple indicators such as the presence of chronic diseases like hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, as well as lifestyle factors such as smoking, physical inactivity, and heavy alcohol use. It has been well established that chronic medical conditions and behavioral risk factors are associated with adverse maternal and infant outcomes.1 Both chronic medical conditions and behavioral risk factors can contribute to complications including, but not limited to, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, preterm births, small-for-gestational-age births, maternal infections, cardiovascular complications, placental issues, poor prenatal care adherence, and increased risk of maternal self-harm that affects both mother and fetus.1 Many vital organs are formed by the first prenatal visit; therefore, the window to intervene by a healthcare provider is before conception.2

The effects of poor mental health on mothers and their children pose a detrimental health component. Disregarding the effects of stress, depression, and emotional well-being can increase the possibility of pregnancy complications and decrease overall maternal health in women of reproductive age.3 Poor preconception mental health has been implicated as the most significant risk factor for pregnancy complications, non-live births, and low birthweight.4 Approximately 30 % of women of reproductive age are diagnosed with a psychiatric disorder, suggesting that mental health should be a top variable when assessing preconception health.3 Untreated, poor mental health statuses during preconception can affect both maternal and fetal outcomes in a similar fashion to chronic medical conditions, including preterm birth, low birth weight, small-for-gestational-age, hypertension in pregnancy, gestational diabetes, maternal morbidity, cesarean section, and hospital readmissions.5,6

Preconception mental health is a vital modifiable risk factor for poor obstetric outcomes and a preconception health indicator.3 Pregnancy complications are 40 % more likely in women with poor preconception mental health.4 Pregnancy complications and poor birth outcomes can affect the survival and long-term health of children, making the preconception period an opportunity to intervene and improve these outcomes.4 Mental health distress (MHD) is a self-reported emotional state defined by symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression. MHD not only has implications for mental and physical health conditions, but also for lifestyle that predisposes women to risk factors that lead to suboptimal reproductive and perinatal consequences. These risk factors include chronic health conditions, stress, fatigue, and loneliness, and high-risk health behaviors such as substance use, physical inactivity, and poor eating habits.7 MHD is associated with low levels of engagement in health behaviors preconception such as poor adherence to folate supplementation, physical activity, and fruits and vegetable consumptions.8 Failure to adhere or engage in these health behaviors preconception can lead to deleterious effects if continued during pregnancy.

Currently, the CDC reports that 90 % of the United States 4.5 trillion dollars’ worth of healthcare expenses are spent on chronic mental health conditions. At least 1 in 8 women experience some form of depression or anxiety disorder from the beginning of their pregnancy to five years postpartum.9 If left untreated, this can become an economic financial burden. Untreated prenatal depression and anxiety are projected to cost an additional $2.2 billion dollars in maternal and child healthcare costs from the time of conception to five years postpartum.10 Another study also projected maternal morbidity to cost the United States an additional $32.2 billion that includes births from pregnancy to five years postpartum.11 Interestingly, Peterson et al. outlined a probabilistic model that examined how many adverse child outcomes can be avoided if provided universal preconception care in a sample of diabetic women.12 An estimated $4.3 billion in annual medical costs can be averted due to avoiding 8,397 preterm deliveries, 3,725 birth defects, and 1,872 perinatal deaths.12

To our knowledge, little is known about the relationship between mental health distress and preconception health among women of reproductive age in the state of Florida. The purpose of this study is to examine the relationship between MHD and preconception health indicators after controlling for sociodemographic and healthcare access characteristics among women of reproductive age in Florida.

Methods

Data were drawn from the 2022 Florida Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) to better understand the role of MHD on preconception health. The BRFSS is the nation’s premier system of health-related telephone surveys that collect state data targeting U.S. residents aged 18 and older regarding their health-related risk behaviors, health behavior, and health outcomes, and use of preventive services. Analysis was limited to women of reproductive age (18–44 years old).

Variables

The main exposure variable involved MHD which was assessed with the question: “Now thinking about your mental health, which includes stress, depression, and problems with emotions, for how many days during the past 30 days was your mental health not good?” Women who reported their mental health not well for ≥ 14 days during the past month were characterized as enduring frequent MHD. With respect to the preconception health indicators, we identified 7 outcome variables: 1) poor health status, 2) diabetes status, 3) asthma status, 4) heavy drinking status, 5) smoking status, 6) physical inactivity, and 7) excess weight.

Self-rated health was assessed with the question: “Would you say that in general your health is:” The response options were split into 2 categories: “poor” (poor, fair) or “not poor” (good, very good, excellent) health. The diabetes, and asthma statuses were assessed by asking the questions: “Ever told you had diabetes?”, and “Ever told you had asthma?” Heavy alcohol consumption was classified into two categories: heavy drinkers (adult men having more than 14 drinks per week/adult women having more than 7 drinks per week), or non-heavy drinkers (adult men having 14 or fewer drinks per week/adult women having 7 or fewer drinks per week). Smoking status was categorized into “smoker”, or “Non-smoker”. Leisure Time Physical Activity was assessed with the question: “Adults who reported doing physical activity or exercise during the past 30 days other than their regular job”. Excess weight was assessed by dichotomizing body mass index (BMI) into 2 categories: “normal weight” or “overweight and obese”. All responses to the outcome variables listed above were dichotomized.

We included: age, race, marital status, education, and employment as sociodemographic characteristics. Age was classified into 3 groups: “18–24”, “25–34”, and “35–44”. Race was reported as “Non-Hispanic White”, “Non-Hispanic Black”, and “Hispanic”. Marital status was classified as “single”, “married”, and “divorced”. Education level was coded as “below high school”, “high school graduate”, and “college” (technical school graduate or college graduate). Employment status was recoded as “employed” or “unemployed”. Health care characteristics included insurance, having a personal doctor, and barrier cost.

Healthcare access coverage was assessed with the question: “What is the current primary source of your health insurance?” The response options were “private” “medicaid” “self-pay” “other government insurance”. Having a personal doctor was assessed by asking the question: “Do you have one person you think of as your personal doctor or health care provider?” The response options were “yes” (≥ 1) or “no”. Medical cost concern was assessed with the question: “Was there a time in the past 12 months when you needed to see a doctor but could not because of cost?” The response options were “yes” or “no”.

Data analysis

Multiple logistic regression analysis was used to adjust for socio-demographic and healthcare access when associating mental distress with each preconceptual health indicator. Data were weighed to adjust for varying probabilities of selection and non-response. PROC SURVEYFREQ and PROC SURVEYLOGISTIC were used and adjusted odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals were reported. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS Version 9.4.

Results

Our sample population as summarized in Table. 1 consisted of a weighted total of 3,200,008 (n = 1,470) including Non-Hispanic Whites (42 %), Non-Hispanic Blacks (19.4 %), and Hispanics (38.6 %) women. Our study sample was evenly split with 38.2 % of women aged 25–34, and 38 % aged 35–44. Nearly half of the study population were reported as being married (49.1 %), with a high school education (31.1 %), and employed (67.5 %). Most had relatively good access to healthcare with private insurance (57.4 %), the presence of a personal doctor (70.8 %), and no cost barrier (78.3 %). Most of our respondents had infrequent mental distress with 77.8 % reported experiencing stress, depression, or emotional distress for less than 14 days. Regarding some of our outcome variables, 21.4 % of respondents were smokers, 56.7 % had excess weight, 6.8 % were heavy alcohol users, 22.9 % were physically inactive, 2.4 % were diabetics, and 18.5 % had asthma.

Poor health status

In the adjusted analysis of mental distress on poor health status, there were no significant associations among race/ethnicity, marital status, insurance, having a personal doctor as summarized in Table 2. Younger aged women from 18 to 24 and 25to 34 had significantly lower odds of poor health status as compared to the older women group with OR:0.487 (p = 0.0089) and OR:0.473 (p = 0.0004), respectively. Discussion

The current study predicted an association between mental distress and women’s preconception health. About 22 % reported frequent mental distress which may have implications for future pregnancy outcomes. Mental distress was not the only significant predictor for poor health status. We also found that younger aged women, less than complete college education, unemployment, the presence of cost barriers, were significantly associated with poor health status. Race failed to reach statistical significance with poor health status.

The literature has indicated that higher levels of psychological distress occur among younger women in the postpartum period and individuals living with HIV.13,14 Likewise, lower educational attainment and unemployment are also implicated in poor mental status as each is often a risk factor for the other.15

We found no statistically significant relationship between the presence of mental distress and diabetes. Diabetes type was not specified in the BRFSS questionnaire. However, we did find that younger women had lower odds of diabetes when adjusting for mental distress. This is inconsistent with current literature suggesting that age is not a protective factor for type 2 or gestational diabetes.16,17 Current literature has shown that several mental health disorders are associated with increased risks of developing these types of diabetes. However, this is not supported in type 1 diabetics. Although poor mental health status can worsen type 1 diabetic outcomes, the presence of this does not increase the risk for development.18 Future studies should include more detailed assessment and questionnaires surrounding types of diabetes, age of onset, and their relationship with mental distress. Early diagnosis, intervention, and control of diabetes in preconception lead to glycemic control in pregnancy; thereby, reducing congenital anomaly frequencies.19

As our study indicated, barriers to care, Medicaid, and frequent mental distress were implicated in asthma; severe asthma is a costly burden that requires chronic disease management by clinicians and pharmacologic therapy, all of which can be unattainable given barriers to care. Likewise, women with uncontrolled asthma are also at higher risks of spontaneous abortion.20 Chronic severe asthma in addition to uncontrolled asthma presents itself prior to conception and can have deleterious effects in utero. Uncontrolled asthma and the cost burden of it can lead to increased frequency of mental distress, as any medical condition warrants.

The finding that heavy alcohol use is lower in unemployed women was an unexpected finding and had mixed consensus with current literature. A study conducted by Masum and Sparks found higher mortality risks between unemployed women and alcohol consumption, in conjunction to higher rates of psychological distress.21 However, another study found a decrease in alcohol consumption among unemployed women, like our findings.22 Although we did not further investigate this complex interaction, this relationship may be attributed to reduced financial resources.

We found that younger women had lower odds of smoking as compared to older adults which is supported by several studies. Cross-generational analyses have found a generational decline in smoking rates in young women.23 In addition to having lower smoking rates, younger women were also shown to have a higher propensity for smoking cessation.24 Likewise, women from racial minority backgrounds including Non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic women also had lower odds of smoking compared to their White counterparts. This is supported by the American Lung Association that found 13.5 % of African American and 7.1 % of Hispanic women smoked as compared to 16 % of Non-Hispanic Whites.25 This can also be attributed to cultural differences in addition to socioeconomic influences. Women with higher educational attainment in our study was also found to smoke less. Numerous interrelated factors including health literacy, supportive social norms, socioeconomic status, and access to healthcare are predominant factors in women with higher educational attainment that lead to these lower smoking rates. Higher number of mental distress days had higher odds of smoking; smoking can be used to self-medicate but can further exacerbate existing mental distress.26 Smoking cessation should be conducted well in advance of conception.27

Associations between mental distress, absence of a personal doctor, and physical inactivity are often bidirectional and require interventions at both ends to improve outcomes. Having access to a personal doctor and simply receiving quality care can change the course of a woman’s overall health, including health literacy and engaging in physical activity. In addition, older age, divorce, having less than high school education, and being of a racial minority background were also significant predictors in poor preconception health outcomes for both physical inactivity and excess weight gain. Significant life transitions, such as divorce, have been a predictor in less physical activity especially among older adults.28 Though the underlying reasons behind this have not been thoroughly investigated, this may be due to lower levels of life satisfaction and mental distress. With several common variables being predictors for both outcomes, there may be a causal relationship that leads to a cycle. Physical inactivity leads to increased body mass index (BMI), an indicator of excess weight gain, in addition to higher BMIs increasing sedentary behavior.29 The association between mental distress and excess weight is multifactorial; poor psychological health often leads to poor lifestyle habits that lead to poor physical health. Lower educational attainment and racial minorities have interconnected pathways that can predispose them to this vicious cycle. Both are associated with socioeconomic disadvantages, higher levels of stress, environmental factors, and reduced health literacy. Disparities in preconception health, which disproportionally affect women of lower socioeconomic groups,30 can be potentially mitigated by implementing culturally tailored public health education campaigns.31

Clinical implications

Mental health should be assessed in women of reproductive age frequently and any medical or psychological disorders should be treated prior to preconception. Clinicians, especially primary care providers, caring for women of reproductive age should screen them for mental health conditions and ensure that appropriate treatment is provided. Screening can come in the form of bundled or several short questionnaires that can be administered at a single visit such as the web-based depression and anxiety test. Tobacco and alcohol education and cessation counseling should also be provided at this visit. Identification and treatment of modifiable risk factors may improve women’s mental health and impact preconception health indicators directly. Indirectly, this can change unhealthy lifestyle behaviors including heavy alcohol and smoking cessation. For successful behavior change, women with poor mental health may need counseling and interventions tailored to target specific preconception health indicators. Interventions should be conducted prior to conception and screened periodically throughout their reproductive years.

Preconception healthcare is imperative to reduce pregnancy complications and improve maternal and birth outcomes. Expanding Women, Infants, and Children (WIC) eligibility to nonpregnant women of reproductive age, rather than only to those who are pregnant or parenting, to include preconception counseling regarding nutrition, mental health, chronic disease management, and substance use prior to conception can improve outcomes as it allows women to make changes earlier. Furthermore, expanding healthcare access to via mobile health clinics, expanding on telehealth services, insurance coverage to cover preconception health preventative health screenings and counseling, encouraging public-private partnerships, and being proactive in sending referrals that connect women with preconception health specialists can improve outcomes. Incorporating curricula in graduate medical education helps emphasize the importance of preconception health early on in clinician training.

Limitations

BRFSS data is cross-sectional and self-reported. One of the main limitations of this study include the subjectivity of self-reported responses to the preconception health indicators questions. Our exposure variable- frequent mental distress- is based on a self-reported question rather than a validated screening instrument or clinical diagnosis which does not allow for standardization. Due to the cross-sectional nature of this data, we only captured data at one point in time. We were unable to track trends and obtain longitudinal results both pre- and post-conception. Underreporting unhealthy behaviors and adverse conditions may have also occurred. Due to our target population, that is, Florida inhabitants, our results may not be representative of the overall population and therefore not generalizable.

Conclusions

Our study found a significant association between frequent mental distress and poor preconception health outcomes among nonpregnant women of reproductive age. Increased screening and intervention in especially at-risk women of reproductive age prior to conception, can induce self-sustainability and healthy behavioral changes that improves preconception health, thereby improving long-term outcomes for the woman, her pregnancy, and her child. Behaviors and lifestyle that are established in the preconception period often translate into the perinatal and postpartum period. Designing successful approaches in improving both preconception health and lifestyle changes includes multifaceted interventions.
