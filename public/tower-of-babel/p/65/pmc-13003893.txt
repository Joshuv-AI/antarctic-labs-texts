Immunogenicity of neoantigens from hepatocellular carcinoma patients treated with a combined radioimmunotherapy
Authors: Belén Aparicio, Leyre Silva, Marta Ruiz, Diana Llópiz, David Repáraz, Patrick Theunissen, Ibon Tamayo, Josune Egea, Ana Igea, Cristian Smerdou, Maria Peñas, Cristina Perpiñá, Ana Matilla, María Varela, Maria Reig, Manuel de la Torre-Alaez, Josepmaria Argemí, Bruno Sangro, Sandra Hervás-Stubbs, Pablo Sarobe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn some cancers, patients responding to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) usually harbor tumors with a higher tumor mutational burden and increased lymphocyte infiltration targeting mutation-derived neoantigens (neoAgs). In the NASIR-HCC trial, patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) received selective internal radiation therapy and the anti-PD-1 antibody Nivolumab, but many of them did not achieve clinical benefit (B). Since neoAg-based vaccination could enhance tumor infiltration and ICI responses, we studied neoAg-specific immunity in these patients and assessed whether patients with no benefit (NB) harbored sufficient immunogenic neoAgs for therapeutic vaccination. Using predicted neoAgs from 6 B and 4 NB patients, we tested immune reactivity in vitro. NeoAg-specific responses were detected in 75% of B and 83% of NB patients, recognizing 37% and 63% of neoAgs tested, respectively. In vivo immunization experiments in HLA transgenic HHD-DR1 mice using HLA-A*02.01- or HLA-DRB1*01-predicted neoAgs from four NB patients revealed responses against most neoAgs (87% and 73%, respectively), expanding the repertoire of immunogenic epitopes. Finally, polyepitopic peptide vaccination containing neoAgs from an NB patient induced stronger and broader T-cell responses compared to polyepitopic DNA vaccine. Our results demonstrate the immunogenicity of neoAgs in our cohort, supporting their potential utility in therapeutic vaccines designed to boost antitumor immunity.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, neoantigens, immunotherapy, polyepitopic vaccines, HLA transgenic mice

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the dominant form of primary liver cancer, is the third cause of cancer-related death worldwide.1 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) are used for the first-line treatment of patients with advanced HCC. However, even with currently approved combinations targeting programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) or PD-L1/PD-1 and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4), only 20%–36% of patients have objective tumor responses.2-4 As in other malignancies, tumors containing a higher lymphocytic infiltrate have a higher probability to respond to ICI.5 Therefore, strategies directed to increasing tumor lymphocytic infiltration and promoting the response to ICI are needed. In the NASIR-HCC (CA209-992) clinical trial, HCC patients received selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT), which is known to generate cell death in the presence of inflammatory factors,6 and the anti-PD-1 antibody Nivolumab, resulting in an overall response rate of 41.5%.7

Resistance to ICI generally correlates with low tumor mutational burden (TMB),8 which reduces the number of mutation-derived neoantigens (neoAgs) and consequently weakens tumor antigenicity. Given the relevance of neoAgs in the response to ICI and some adoptive T-cell therapy strategies,9,10 therapeutic vaccination is a promising strategy to promote neoAg-specific immunity and improve ICI efficacy. While neoAg vaccines have demonstrated encouraging results in heavily mutated tumors,11,12 HCC usually shows moderate TMB levels,13 potentially explaining the low rate of complete responses (3−5%) attained in these individuals with ICI,2,3 as opposed to those (15%) obtained in heavily mutated tumors like melanoma or MSI-hi colorectal tumors.14,15 This makes the therapeutic potential of vaccination less certain. Nonetheless, HCC patients with higher levels of T cells specific for high-affinity neoAgs have a better prognosis,16 suggesting that neoAgs may indeed contribute to anti-HCC immunity. However, we ignore if neoAg-specific immune responses predict treatment outcomes and what therapeutic benefit neoAg vaccination could provide to HCC patients.

In this study, we investigated spontaneously generated neoAg-specific immune responses and assessed whether neoAgs identified in refractory patients could be used for vaccine preparation. Our analysis of the NASIR-HCC cohort revealed that spontaneous immune responses can be detected against an important proportion of computationally predicted neoAgs. Furthermore, to characterize the repertoire of immunogenic neoAgs, we also used a murine HLA transgenic model. We demonstrated that the majority of neoAgs predicted in patients who derived no clinical benefit could successfully induce immune responses in these mice, suggesting their potential utility in therapeutic vaccines designed to boost antitumor immunity.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

The design of the NASIR-HCC study and clinico-demographic variables of patients have been described previously.7 The study protocol (CA209-992) was approved by the local ethics committee in Navarra (Comité Ético de Investigación Clínica) (#20/17) and conducted between 2018 and 2019 following the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. In brief, immunotherapy-naïve patients received SIRT using SIR-Spheres followed 3 weeks later by nivolumab (240 mg IV every 14 d until tumor progression, unacceptable toxicity or a maximum of 24 doses). The main objectives were safety and efficacy. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee at each center and written informed consent was obtained from every patient. Tumor response was assessed by investigators using Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.1 every 6 weeks for the first year and every 12 weeks thereafter until progression. We have defined clinical benefit as a best overall response of complete response, partial response or stable disease, all of them lasting for at least 6 months from the date of SIRT. Patients with clinical benefit had significantly longer progression-free and overall survival.

Eleven patients with available blood samples were selected for in vitro studies of neoAg-specific immunity (Supplementary Table S1). Six patients showed clinical benefit (B), and 5 patients showed no clinical benefit (NB). Four NB patients expressing the HLA-A*02:01 (patients 17, 30, and 34) or HLA-DRB1*01 (patients 15 and 30) alleles were selected to test the immunogenicity of their neoAgs in the murine vaccination model. Tumor and blood samples obtained at baseline were used for sequencing studies, whereas blood samples obtained at baseline and 8 weeks after the first dose of Nivolumab were used for in vitro analysis of neoAg-specific responses. All samples were frozen and stored in liquid N2 until use.

Whole-exome and RNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was purified from tumor biopsy and blood samples for WES experiments, using the Maxwell 16 Tissue DNA Purification Kit (Promega). RNA was purified from tumor biopsy samples with the Maxwell 16 LEV simply RNA Tissue Kit (Promega). Samples were preserved at −80 °C and sent to Macrogen Inc (Korea) for sequencing. WES was performed by using SureSelect Human All Exon V6 library and NovaSeq6000 150PE 18Gb 100X on target sequencing. An in-house bioinformatics pipeline was used for WES analyzes. Data were aligned to the reference human genome build hg38 with the BWA-mem algorithm (0.7.17). Then, duplicates were marked using Picard’s MarkDuplicates tool. Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) best practices were used for insertion and deletion (indel) realignment and base recalibration. SAMtools was applied to create tumor and normal pileup files. Four mutation callers were used for the somatic nonsynonymous single nucleotide variants (SNVs) calling: Mutect3.8, Varscan2, SomaticSniper (version 1.0.5.0), and Strelka (version 2.9.2). Mutations were annotated using Annovar and only the exonic and nonsynonymous single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) in RefGene were considered. The following filters were used for the generation of a putative mutations list: a tumor and normal coverage greater than 6, a variant allele frequency (VAF) ≥5% and variant read counts ≥3. In addition to mutation identification, WES data were also used for HLA genotyping using the HLA-HD algorithm.

For RNA sequencing, high-quality RNA (1 μg) was used for the construction of complementary DNA libraries, applying the TruSeq stranded Total RNA Sample Prep Kit. Paired-end sequencing was applied to the library using the NovaSeq platform (10 Gb 100PE 100 M reads/sample). Alignments to the reference human genome hg38 were obtained using the STAR method and duplicates were identified using Picard's MarkDuplicates tool. Then, GATK SplitNTrim tool was used to split and trim the reads, whereas the GATK toolbox was applied for the reads' realignment and base recalibration. FeatureCount, Cufflinks and HTseq were used for gene counts calculation.

Selection of mutations and HLA binding prediction algorithms for neoAg selection

Mutations were filtered according to the following criteria: (1) selection of exonic missense SNVs, not considering gene fusions or INDEL variants; (2) only single SNVs identified by at least two different variant callers were considered as “true SNVs”; (3) mutations exclusively expressed in tumor but not in healthy control tissue (blood) using RNAseq data; (4) single-nucleotide polymorphisms were discarded using the ExAC database. Prediction of HLA binding capacity of mutated and wild type (WT) peptides was carried out using NetMHCpan 4.1 and NetMHCIIpan 4.0 algorithms for class I and II epitopes, respectively. In addition to confirmed expression by RNAseq (>3 reads), neoAgs were selected according to: (i) defined as “strong binder” by the algorithm, (ii) with a putative binding affinity <50 nM (class I) and <100 nM (class II), and (iii) with a differential aggretopicity index (DAI: affinity of WT peptide/affinity of mutated peptide) >2 (group A peptides). In some patients, an additional set (group B peptides) was selected, with the same “strong binder” definition and binding affinity thresholds, but with a DAI of 1–2, and containing the mutation at non-HLA anchoring positions.

Peptides

Eight- to twenty-mer peptides containing the predicted neoAgs were purchased from GeneCust (Boynes, France) with >70% for in vitro and >80% purity for in vivo assays. Peptides were solubilized in PBS with 10% of DMSO and were preserved at −20 °C.

In vitro stimulation assays

Patient PBMC were thawed and incubated overnight in T-cell medium (1:1 mix of RPMI-1640-glutamax and AIMV) (ThermoFisher) supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated human serum (Biowhittaker), 12.5 mM HEPES (Lonza), antibiotics (100 U/ml penicillin/100 μg/ml streptomycin, Gibco), at 106 cells/ml in 24 well plates. GM-CSF (500 UI/ml) and IL-4 (1000 UI/ml) (Immunotools) were also added to induce monocyte differentiation to dendritic cells. Next day lymphocytes were stimulated with peptide pools (3–10 peptides/pool; each peptide at 10 μg/ml) in the presence of poly(I:C) (Amersham) (20 μg/ml) and IL-7 (25 ng/ml) (Immunotools). Wells were re-fed with IL-7 and IL-15 (25 ng/ml) (Immunotools) every 4–5 d, and at day 14, they were harvested and counted for ELISPOT assays.

DNA immunogens

Nucleotide elongated sequences from selected peptides from patient 17 were included in tandem flanked by an N-terminal leader peptide (SP) and an MHC class I trafficking signal (MITD) attached to the C terminus (SP-P17.L.I.2-P17.L.I.1/3-P17.L.I.7-P17.L.I.8-MITD). This insert (NeoP17) was obtained from GenScript and cloned in the pBK-T-SFV-b12A DNA vector to generate pBK-T-SFV-b12A- NeoP17, as described.17

Cell lines

HLA-A*02.01+ T2 and HLA-DRB1*01+ HOM2 cells18 were used for HLA class I- and II-binding experiments, respectively. Both cell lines were grown in complete RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) containing 10% FBS (Sigma) and antibiotics. They were routinely tested for mycoplasma.

HLA binding assays

HLA peptide binding assays were performed as described.18 For binding to HLA-A*02.01, stabilization of HLA molecules in T2 cells was measured by flow cytometry. All peptides were tested at 100 μM and mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was determined. Binding was expressed as Fluorescence Index (FI) using the following formula: (MFI with peptide − MFI without peptide)/MFI without peptide.

For HLA-DRB1*01 binding assays, HOM2 cells were incubated with experimental peptides (100 µM) and the biotinylated control peptide APK (APKYVKQNTLKLATG) (10 µM). Peptide binding was indirectly measured as the loss of fluorescence in competition with the biotinylated control. The percentage of peptide inhibition was calculated using the following formula: 100 − (MFI with peptide − MFI without peptide)/(MFI biot APK − MFI no peptide). The binding score (BS) was calculated as follows: % inhibition with experimental peptide/% inhibition with unbiotinylated APK. Peptides were considered as binders with FI and BS values >0.5.

Mice and immunization experiments

Humanized 6–12-week-old male and female HHD-DR1 mice, which were transgenic for the HLA-A*02.01 and HLA-DRB1*01 alleles, were obtained from Dr. F. Lemonnier (Paris, France). They were maintained and bred in our facilities in pathogen-free conditions, according to procedures approved by Ethics Committee for Animal Research (Universidad de Navarra; ref# 045-19). This included a 12-hour light/dark cycle, 22 ± 1 °C and 30%–35% humidity, receiving food and water ad libitum. The mice were humanely euthanized via cervical dislocation according to the experimental endpoints. We have adhered to ARRIVE guidelines on reporting of animal in vivo experiments. The sample size for the experimental groups was calculated using the GPower software. No criteria were set for including and excluding animals, and allocation was carried out by the first author.

For peptide immunization experiments, the mice were immunized subcutaneously with pools of 3–4 peptides (100 nmoles/peptide/mouse) in combination with adjuvants anti-CD40 (FGK4.5; Bioxcell) and polyI:C (50 μg/mouse each).

For immunization with the pBK-T-SFV-b12A-NeoP17 plasmid, first, anesthesia (ketamine (Richter Pharma, 0.66 mg/kg) and Rompun (Bayer, 8 mg/kg)) was administered intraperitoneally. The plasmid (10 μg/mouse) was injected intradermally in 40 μL of PBS on the mouse's back followed by an electroporation consisting of two pulses of 1125 V/cm for 50 μs and 8 pulses of 275 V/cm for 10 ms. The electrode was placed around the injection site, using an ECM 830 square wave electroporation system (BTX). Mice received a booster dose at day 7 (for peptide immunization) or at day 14 (for plasmid immunization). In all cases, mice were sacrificed a week after the last immunization and their spleens were removed for T cell response evaluation.

ELISPOT assays

For human IFN-γ ELISPOT assays (Human IFN-γ Set; BD-Biosciences), 105 T cells were stimulated with 5–10 × 103 short-term DC, generated by GM-CSF and IL-4 stimulation of monocytes for 48 h, with poly(I:C) added during the last 24 h of culture. T cells and DC were cocultured with or without peptides (10 μg/ml) for 20 h in ELISPOT plates coated with IFN-γ-capturing antibodies. Next, the wells were washed, incubated with conjugate and the spots were developed. Murine T cell responses were determined also by an IFN-γ ELISPOT (ELISPOT Set; BD-Biosciences). Splenocytes (8 × 105 cells/well) were stimulated with different peptide concentrations (10–0.01 μM) for 24 h. In all cases, spot-forming cells (SFCs) were counted with the ImmunoSpot® Series 6 ELISPOT Analyzer counter using the ImmunoCaptureTM (version 7.0.16.1) and ImmunoSpot® (version 7.0.30.2) software. Human responses were considered positive if they were above 3 SD of the value obtained in the wells without peptide and contained at least 10 SFC in stimulated cultures. For murine studies, peptides were considered immunogenic when SFC (with peptide)–SFC (without peptide) >50.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyzes (normality tests, Student's t test and ANOVA) were performed with GraphPad Prism (GraphPad) software version 8. P < 0.05 was taken to represent statistical significance.

Results

NeoAg-specific responses were present in HCC patients

Eleven patients in the NASIR-HCC cohort (Supplementary Table S1), with available tumor and blood samples for whole exome sequencing (WES) and RNAseq studies and with viable peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) for in vitro stimulation experiments, were selected to study neoAg-specific responses. This included six patients with clinical benefit (B patients) and five patients without clinical benefit (NB patients), considering clinical benefit as a best overall response of complete response, partial response or stable disease, all of them lasting for at least 6 months from the date of SIRT. Using WES data, neoAgs were predicted in silico based on the superior HLA binding capacity of the mutated peptide vs. the wild-type (WT) peptide (Group A neoAgs). After neoAg prediction, peptides corresponding to HLA class I neoAgs (median 5.5 peptides/patient, range 3–8) or class II neoAgs (median 3 peptides/patient, range 1–3, usually containing several overlapping epitopes) were synthesized (Supplementary Table S2). They were used to stimulate PBMC obtained at baseline or eight weeks after initiating Nivolumab administration (postNivo). No specific recognition of neoAg peptides was observed after direct ex vivo stimulation (data not show). Thus, we used an in vitro 14-d stimulation protocol. After this expansion, viable cells were obtained from all patients except patient 17 (at any time point) and patient 32 (postNivo), who were classified as NB and B patients, respectively. Stimulated cells were then tested in ELISPOT assays. NeoAg-specific responses were observed in eight patients (80%), and were elicited by 54.6% of the peptides tested (60.7% for HLA class I peptides and 41.6% for HLA class II peptides) (Figure 1A). Positive responses were observed in 60% of patients at baseline and 40% of patients after Nivolumab. Patients with and without clinical benefit had similar proportions of neoAg-specific responses (75% vs 83%) and recognized peptides (37% vs 63%) (Figure 1B and details in Supplementary Figure S1). Overall, our results indicate that neoAg-specific responses were present in the majority of patients in this cohort; however, no distinct patterns emerged when responses were analyzed by HLA class I vs class II peptides, sampling time point, or treatment outcome.

The empty and full squares correspond to negative and positive responses, respectively. (B) Percentage of recognized peptides, calculated as the percentage of peptides showing positive responses in each patient with respect to the number of peptides tested.

Predicted neoAgs from patients with no clinical benefit effectively bind HLA molecules and are immunogenic in HHD-DR1 mice

More than half (58.5%) of this subgroup of patients in the NASIR-HCC cohort did not experience clinical benefit from treatment, despite recognition of 63% of their predicted neoAgs. Such responses could be enhanced through vaccination if neoAgs were immunogenic. In order to test in vivo immunogenicity of selected peptides in HHD-DR1 mice, which express the HLA-A*02.01 and HLA-DRB1*01 alleles, four patients with no clinical benefit who carry these same alleles were selected for analysis. Patients 15, 17, and 30 had been previously analyzed in in vitro experiments, while patient 34 had a sample available for tumor sequencing but lacked viable PBMCs for functional studies.

Since neoAg recognition can occur through mutations at either MHC anchoring positions (which enhance binding affinity) or TCR contact sites (which alter T-cell recognition),19 we expanded our neoAg repertoire beyond the previously tested candidates shown in Figure 1. Group A neoAgs were those evaluated earlier, while Group B was a new set of neoAgs where mutations occurred at non-HLA anchoring positions, regardless of WT peptide binding affinity (Supplementary Table S3).

The capacity of these peptides for HLA-A*02.01 binding was analyzed in vitro. Overall, 87.5% (7/8), 94% (17/18), and 75% (3/4) of the predicted neoAgs from patients 17, 30, and 34, respectively, bound to HLA-A*02.01. When their corresponding WT peptides were analyzed, 25%–50% of group A and 100% of group B neoAgs bound to HLA-A*02.01 (Figure 2A; upper panels). Thus, the majority of HLA class I-restricted predicted neoAgs were true binders, whereas the binding of WT versions depended on the selection criteria. With respect to binding to HLA-DRB1*01, 46% of the predicted neoAgs (66% and 41% in patients 15 and 30, respectively) bound above the selected threshold (Figure 2B; upper panels). Only 20% of WT peptides demonstrated binding capacity to HLA-DRB1*01.

After vaccination with neoAg peptides, screening of their immunogenicity was carried out using IFN-γ ELISPOT assays, since we had observed that this readout shows a good correlation with other T cell functions (e.g. TNF-α secretion, CD107 degranulation).18 These vaccination studies in HHD-DR1 mice showed high immunogenicity rates for HLA-A*02.01-restricted MUT peptides: 87.5% (7/8) from patient 17, 83.3% (15/18) from patient 30, and 100% (4/4) from patient 34 (Figure 2A; lower bar graphs). Different degrees of cross-recognition of WT peptides by T cells obtained from neoAg-immunized mice were observed when testing peptides at the screening concentration (10 μM). On the other side, 66% (2/3 and 8/12) of HLA-DRB1*01-restricted neoAgs from patients 15 and 30 were immunogenic (Figure 2B; lower bar graphs), a higher proportion than predicted by analyzing the number of peptides with the capacity to bind to these HLA molecules. Interestingly, between 66% and 100% of these immunogenic peptides specifically recognized the mutated version, indicating strong discrimination between WT and mutant variants.

Due to the cross-recognition of the WT peptide observed for some neoAg-induced T cells, we carried out titration experiments using lower peptide concentrations (10–1000 nM), presumably closer to those found in tumor cells. These experiments clearly discriminated between mutated and WT peptides for most neoAgs (Figure 2C).

Overall, combined data from screening and titration assays demonstrated that 100% and 93% of the immunogenic HLA class I neoepitopes from patients 17 and 30, respectively, induced responses specific for the mutated peptide. Regarding patient 34, only peptide P34.I.4 induced neoAg-specific responses. Thus, 83% (27/30) of predicted HLA-A*02.01-restricted neoAg peptides from these patients with no clinical benefit were immunogenic, and the majority of them (82%) induced T-cell responses that specifically recognize the mutated sequence.

Generation of polyepitopic vaccines containing class I-restricted neoAgs

To design potential vaccines for those patients with no clinical benefit, we tested two vaccination platforms using HLA class I-restricted neoAgs identified as immunogenic in previous experiments (Figure 3A). We focused on patients 17 and 30, since most neoAgs found in patient 34 were not specific for the mutated sequence. We first used long peptides (15-mers), and selected four peptides from patient 17 and nine peptides from patient 30, encompassing the minimal HLA class I epitopes flanked at both ends by the three amino acids naturally encoded in the protein sequence (Supplementary Table S4), a strategy that has demonstrated its immunogenicity in preclinical and clinical studies.11,20,21 This approach not only included overlapping epitopes bearing the same mutation at different positions (e.g. peptides P17.I.1/P17.I.3, P30.I.2/P30.I.3, P30.I.5/P30.I.8, and P30.I.10/P30.I.12) but also required antigen processing of these long peptides, thereby providing valuable insights into epitope generation after proteolytic trimming. Immunization with these peptides resulted in T-cell responses against 75% (3/4) and 66% (6/9) of the peptides tested in patients 17 and 30, respectively (Figure 3B). Interestingly, some long peptides induced responses that recognized the long peptide version but not the minimal epitope, suggesting the induction of T cells that recognized other immunodominant minimal epitope inside the 15-mer peptide.

For epitopes from patient 17 we also generated a second type of vaccine, a DNA/RNA layer vector encoding a Semliki Forest Virus (SFV) self-replicating RNA containing an expression cassette encoding the four 15-mer peptides in a string. This approach has been previously used with neoAgs identified in breast cancer patients.17 As opposed to long peptides, immunization with the DNA encoding this SFV vector only induced responses against one of the four encoded peptides (Figure 3C).

Discussion

We have shown that neoAgs can be effectively predicted from tumor exome sequencing in patients with HCC, that these patients have endogenous responses to tumor neoAgs, and that neoAgs can be used in cancer therapeutic vaccines to induce specific responses in humanized mouse models. An enriched tumor lymphocytic infiltrate22 and a higher TMB8 usually predict better response rates to ICIs, since many of those lymphocytes are specific for immunogenic neoAgs generated from tumor mutations.9 Therefore, different strategies, including neoAg-containing vaccines or in situ vaccines that foster neoAg release in an inflammatory context, are being used to promote induction of T cell responses against neoAgs. In HCC, a tumor type with intermediate TMB, the relationship between neoAgs and clinical outcomes is complex. While some studies showed no correlation between neoAg burden and survival,23 others have found that high-affinity neoAgs or TP53-derived neoAgs predicted longer overall survival, providing a rationale for promoting immunity against these specific antigens.16,24

In the NASIR-HCC trial,7 patients were treated with SIRT, a locoregional therapy that induces tumor cell death and antigen release under inflammatory conditions,6 followed by the anti-PD-1 antibody Nivolumab. Genomic analysis revealed a highly individualized repertoire of neoAg-encoding mutations, with only two genes (APOB and HMCN1) found to be mutated in two patients. However, the specific mutations within these genes remained patient-specific. These results are in agreement with our previous study18 and underscore the critical necessity for a personalized approach to neoAg vaccines. Analyzes of neoAg-specific immunity in baseline and posttreatment blood samples in a subgroup of these patients showed that 80% of patients had neoAg-specific responses covering more than half of the epitopes tested. However, no significant differences were observed between patients with or without clinical benefit. Interestingly, broader analysis of the NASIR-HCC cohort suggests that a higher burden of Class II neoAgs correlates with clinical benefit (manuscript in preparation), highlighting the potential value of therapeutic vaccines to artificially expand these responses in patients with lower spontaneous neoAg-specific immunity.

We acknowledge the limitations of a reduced sample size, and that circulating immunity may not fully reflect the intratumoral environment, which is characterized by profound immunosuppression. While the lack of TILs and posttreatment biopsies is a limitation, the detection of neoAg-specific T cells in the periphery remains a critical step in validating HCC as a candidate for personalized immunotherapy. A notable drawback of this study is that the scarcity of baseline tumor material precluded protein-level validation of neoAg expression and subsequent direct autologous recognition assays. However, the recognition of HepG2 cells expressing neoAgs in our previous studies18 provides strong proof of concept and supports the ability of neoAg-specific T cells to target cells endogenously expressing the predicted mutations. Overall, our results do not reveal a clear association between the magnitude of neoAg-specific responses and clinical outcomes. These preliminary data suggest that baseline neoAg-specific immunity does not predict clinical benefit from this combined treatment. Additional features of the TME, including immunosuppressive mechanisms,25 may contribute to the lack of clinical benefit in these patients. Indeed, several factors, including a hostile TME (with immunosuppressive cells and soluble factors), the metabolic microenvironment and even the microbial microenvironment, have been described to hinder the activity of antitumor T cells.26 In addition, the presence of a barrier composed of SPP1+ macrophages and fibroblasts has been associated with the poorer efficacy of ICI.27 Moreover, radiation delivered by SIRT28 may affect tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, modulating neoAg-specific immunity and its contribution to treatment outcome. Larger cohorts will be needed to establish the relevance of neoAg-specific immune responses and their impact on treatment efficacy in this setting.

Considering the relevance of neoAg-specific immunity in the response to ICIs, we wonder if patients who did not achieve clinical benefit had a sufficient amount of immunogenic neoAgs for vaccine preparation. These individuals represent a significant proportion of treated patients, and we hypothesized that vaccination could further reinforce neoAg-specific immunity. Using a set of computationally predicted neoAgs selected according to their HLA binding capacity from four patients expressing HLA-A*02.01 or HLA-DRB1*01 alleles, we demonstrated that most of them indeed bound to HLA molecules. In order to identify the repertoire of potential immunogenic neoAgs of some NB patients, we used a murine HLA transgenic model as a proof of concept, to demonstrate in vivo in mice the immunogenicity of the same neoAgs that we identified in patients. The HLA-transgenic model serves as a “clean” system to identify the full repertoire of potentially immunogenic neoAgs, free from the immunosuppressive influence of the HCC microenvironment. This is essential for vaccine design, where targeting the widest possible range of epitopes is preferred to prevent tumor escape. We found that 83% of HLA-A*02.01- and 66% of HLA-DRB1*01-restricted neoAgs were immunogenic in transgenic HHD-DR1 mice expressing these alleles.

Although there is a close association between binding to HLA class I molecules and immunogenicity,29 class II neoAgs remarkably showed a different pattern. Some peptides not reaching the binding threshold in in vitro assays nonetheless demonstrated immunogenicity in the in vivo immunization experiments, likely reflecting the lower sensitivity of the HLA class II binding assay. Overall, the majority of our predicted neoAgs proved immunogenic, suggesting that predicted neoAg load reflects the proportion capable of inducing an immune response.

Considering the high proportion of predicted neoAgs presented by HLA-A*02.01 and HLA-DRB1*01 alleles that proved immunogenic, and assuming similar rates for neoAgs predicted to be presented by other patient HLA alleles, our results suggest that patients with no clinical benefit might harbor sufficient immunogenic neoAgs for vaccine preparation. Indeed, neoAg-based vaccination clinical trials usually employ 15–20 neoAgs per patient, well within the range potentially available in our cohort.11,30 A recent clinical trial in patients with advanced HCC31 include a median of 30 neoAgs in their personalized neoAg-based vaccines (range 4–67), confirming the feasibility of neoAg identification in these patients. Importantly, that trial found a positive correlation between neoAg number included in the vaccine and clinical response, emphasizing the need for accurate neoAg identification pipelines.32 In this regard, our approach is to include neoAgs with differential binding capacity between the mutated and the WT versions (group A), alongside neoAgs with mutations at TCR contact position (group B).19 This strategy increases the pool of available immunogenic neoAgs, potentially facilitating clinical benefit.31

Beyond establishing that sufficient neoAgs can be identified to create patient-specific vaccines, we compared peptide-based vs. DNA-based vaccination platforms. Immunization with peptides coadministered with poly(I:C) and antiCD40 as adjuvants outperformed immunization with an SFV-based DNA vaccine. This approach aligns with the successful neoAg peptide vaccines that have demonstrated a good immunogenicity by combining long (20-mer) peptides with poly(I:C).11,30 In our screening study, we incorporated anti-CD40 alongside poly(I:C) to enhance the identification of less immunogenic epitopes by mimicking T helper cell functions. Nevertheless, we have previously demonstrated that vaccines containing CD8 and CD4 neoAg epitopes can achieve immunogenicity using only poly(I:C) as adjuvant, eliminating the need for anti-CD40.18 Thus, combining CD8 and CD4 neoAg T cell epitopes with poly(I:C) represents a suitable strategy for inducing potent antitumor responses.

The recent development of mRNA vaccines against SARS-CoV-2 has sparked the interest in this type of vaccines.33 We have proposed the use of a DNA vector encoding a self-amplifying RNA, which would improve antigen availability. This strategy induced meaningful responses, though they were limited to a single epitope. These findings mirror our previous results with neoAgs identified in breast cancer patients, where DNA vaccination generated clear responses although less efficiently than peptide vaccines.17 NeoAg-encoding mRNA vaccines have demonstrated a remarkable immune and clinical activity in cancer patients. However, despite encoding a self-amplifying RNA, our DNA vaccine resulted in a less potent activity. Suboptimal DNA delivery by electroporation vs efficient vehiculization of mRNA vaccines in lipid nanoparticles34 and lipoplexes35 may explain the differences in immunogenicity. However, DNA-based neoAg vaccines can be improved by combination with other immunostimulatory maneuvers, like the inclusion of a plasmid encoding IL-12, as reported by Yarchoan et al. in HCC.31 In this study, a median of 64% of those neoAgs included in the vaccines elicited T cell responses.

In summary, we have shown that neoAg-specific responses can be detected in HCC patients with or without clinical benefit from a combination of radiation and PD-1 inhibition. Interestingly, we have demonstrated in HLA transgenic murine models that an important proportion of neoAgs from patients with no benefit are immunogenic, and immune responses against them can be boosted by immunization, providing a strong rationale for the use of neoAg vaccines in this population.
