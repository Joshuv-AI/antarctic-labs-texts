Optimization of TIG welding process parameters using Taguchi technique for the joining of dissimilar metals of AA5083 and AA7075
Authors: Balram Yelamasetti, Manikyam Sandeep, Santhi Sree Narella, Vishnu Vardhan Tiruchanur, Tushar Sonar, Chander Prakash, Sagar Shelare, Nabisab Mujawar Mubarak, Sandeep Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research paper aims to optimize the TIG welding parameters to join dissimilar metals of AA5083 and AA7075 using the Taguchi technique. The TIG welding current and root gap of butt geometry configuration are considered input parameters, and welding characteristics such as tensile strength, 0.2% of yield strength, hardness, and impact energy are output responses. The base metals are joined according to the Taguchi L-09 design of experiments. The welded samples were inspected using the X-ray radiography method for internal defects. Tensile properties, hardness number, and impact energy of different welding coupons were evaluated by conducting the uni-axial testing, a Brinell hardness test, and an Izod impact test, respectively. A better weld strength of 224 MPa was observed at 210 A welding current, and the root gap of 1.5 mm was maintained. Better hardness and impact energy values were observed when the root gap of 1.5 mm and welding current of 220 A were maintained. The root gap is the primary factor influencing tensile strength enhancement, which accounts for 44.78% of the effect, followed by 40.5% welding current. Root gap is the parameter that affects the tensile properties, hardness number, and impact toughness the most. The findings of this paper suggest the optimal parameters for welding AA5083 and AA7075 dissimilar base metals, which are suitable for complex structures requiring both durability and resistance to harsh environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74458-6.Keywords: AA5083, AA7075, Root gap, Welding current, Taguchi technique, ANOVASubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

TIG welding, commonly known as Gas Tungsten Arc Welding, is utilized in many sectors1. Over the decades, power sources, control systems, and electrode materials have improved TIG welding efficiency, dependability, and versatility2,3. Pulsed current, high-frequency starters and automated control allow modern TIG welding machines to produce high-quality welds in varied materials and thicknesses3,4. TIG welding is utilized in aerospace, automotive, marine, petrochemical, nuclear, and fabrication5,6. Its uses are most common in precision, weld quality, and aesthetics7. TIG welding creates smooth, high-quality welds with little spatter, making it perfect for demanding applications8–10. It can weld many metals and manage heat input, weld pool, and filler material for accurate and uniform welds11,12.

Modern engineering and production require welding dissimilar metals to create advanced components and complicated structures that combine the best qualities of multiple materials13,14. Combining lightweight aluminum with high-strength alloys like steel or titanium enhances performance, affordability, and durability15,16. Aerospace, automobile, and construction sectors need this method to reduce weight and boost fuel efficiency17,18. However, melting points, thermal expansion coefficients, and brittle intermetallic complexes make welding different metals difficult19–21. These conditions might weaken connections and weld penetration22,23. Electrical and thermal conductivity variations can also make welding heat challenging24,25. Bonding dissimilar metals requires a compatible filler material26,27. To address these obstacles, improved welding processes and precise parameter control are needed28,29. Taguchi optimization can examine and optimize welding process parameters for high-quality, reliable welds30–32.

AA5083 is a non-heat treatable aluminum alloy with a high magnesium concentration and is renowned for its exceptional corrosion resistance, weldability, and fatigue strength. It is particularly used in marine applications requiring welding and shaping. AA5083 typically comprises aluminum, magnesium, manganese, and small quantities of other elements33. AA7075 is a high-strength aluminum alloy with exceptional mechanical characteristics, part of the 7xxx class34. It comprises aluminum, zinc, magnesium, and copper and has a high strength-to-weight ratio, excellent fatigue resistance, moderate corrosion resistance, and favorable machinability35. AA7075 is a strong, lightweight alloy in aerospace, defense, automotive, and motorsports. It is used for high-strength components, military-grade equipment, and high-performance vehicle components36,37. It is also used in top-tier sporting products like bicycle frames and climbing equipment, ensuring strength and weight.

Aluminum alloys are extensively utilized across industries because of their beneficial features, including a high strength-to-weight ratio, exceptional corrosion resistance, and outstanding capacity to be shaped38. Yaduwanshi et al.39 observed that welding aluminum alloys presents numerous difficulties because they have a high thermal conductivity. As a result, more energy is needed to ensure that the weld penetrates properly. Hvozdets et al.40 investigated that aluminum develops a strong oxide layer (Al2O3) on its surface, which possesses a significantly higher melting temperature (about 2050 °C) in comparison to the aluminum base metal (around 660 °C). To prevent contamination and flaws, Meng et al.41 suggested that it is necessary to eliminate or disturb the oxide layer during the welding process. The significant solubility of hydrogen in molten aluminum might result in the formation of pores in the weld, compromising the joint’s mechanical characteristics. Aluminum’s high thermal expansion coefficient can lead to substantial deformation when welding42. Moreover, specific aluminum alloys are susceptible to heat cracking because of their metallurgical characteristics43. Specialized welding procedures are required to achieve high-quality junctions due to these properties.

TIG welding is widely employed for joining aluminum alloys44 and uses a non-consumable electrode (tungsten) to generate the weld and an inert shielding gas, usually argon or helium, to safeguard the weld zone from ambient impurities. Austenitic 304 L stainless steel and Ferritic-martensitic P92 steel dissimilar weld joints were tested by Dak et al.45, for creep life and high-temperature tensile qualities. Early creep failure in P92 steel was caused by laves phase development and precipitation strengthening. During TIG welding of AA6061-T6 along with AA7075-T6, Bin Reyaz et al.46 demonstrated that the welds of aluminum were of superior quality, characterized by their cleanliness, outstanding visual appeal, and minimum splattering. This makes TIG welding particularly well-suited for tasks that demand accuracy. Maurya et al.47, compares sDSS 2507/IN625 dissimilar weld joints’ slurry-erosion performance to slurry concentration. Surface properties, microstructure, and material removal are examined. Results recommend optimising weld fabrication heat input for slurry erosion wear resistance. Understanding microstructure improves operational weld performance. Bendikiene et al.48 investigated the TIG welding of 5083 aluminum plates. They inferred that the exact regulation of the amount of heat supplied is essential in welding thin aluminum parts and minimizing the chances of distortion and cracking, rendering it well-suited for many applications.

Optimizing welding process parameters is crucial for guaranteeing the structural integrity, mechanical characteristics, and overall quality of welded joints49–51. Optimising groove angle, current, and voltage in MIG welding medium carbon steel joints using the Taguchi approach improves mechanical qualities52. Inadequate selection of parameters can result in flaws such as cracking, porosity, incomplete fusing, and severe distortion53. Hence, it is crucial to determine the optimal welding parameters to enhance the quality of welds, increase production efficiency, and minimize costs54,55. Thejasree et al.56 have focused on optimizing welding parameters using experimental procedures, statistical analysis, and computer models to understand the intricate relationships between welding factors and weld quality. Kasgari et al.57 established an efficient and effective Taguchi experimental design approach for aluminum/copper friction stir spot welded joints with the maximum shear strength. The independent welding factors were pin rotation speed, dwell time, and downward pressure. Different optimization methods were utilized to find the best welding parameter ranges. The best process parameters were the 1800 r/min rotation speed, 15 s dwell duration, and 0.2 mm downward pressure. Integrating TOPSIS, neural network, and genetic algorithm yielded the optimal parameter values for shear strength experiment verification. Reyaz et al.58 investigated the impact of welding parameters on AA6061-T6 and AA7075-T6 aluminum alloy butt welds using TIG and FSW. They found that tool rotation speed significantly influenced response variables while traverse speed and tool tilt angle had less influence on adapting RSM. Dey et al.59 examine laser beam welding aluminum alloys for automotive parts.

Poisson regression was used to examine the influence of explanatory variables on outcome variables in lap joint laser welding experiments with Al2024 alloys. MOGA was utilized to find optimal solutions. Response surface shows analyzed input process parameter effects on responses. The maximum load-bearing capacity was 1,263 N, and Poisson regression models were well-fitted. A neoteric view of laser welding lap joint Al2024 alloys and optimization of input process parameters brings manufacturing insights. Karumuri et al.60 studied the FSW for AA6082 and AA5456 aluminum alloys to analyze the impact of welding input variables using Taguchi and GRA approaches. They found that tool rotation speed significantly impacts ultimate tensile strength, Brinell hardness number, and shear weld resistance.

Genetic algorithms are a highly popular methodology widely utilized in recent studies. Sankaralingam et al.61 adapted the heterogeneous TIG welding of Al5083-H111 along with AA6061-T6 alloys with ER5356 and scandium. The optimized genetic algorithm was used to select the optimal combination of welding parameters. Das and Chakraborty62 used MCDM techniques to determine optimal parameter combinations for FSW processes. The hybrid approach was the most effective in enhancing performance metrics. The revised quantum genetic algorithm (RQGA) and hidden markov model are used to develop an RSW quality rating method by Wang63. Precision is achieved using HMM and expert knowledge. The RSW process of stainless steel sheets railway carriage roof uses the model, and parameter training in four quality states takes less than 15 s. The four quality states’ average classification accuracy is higher than other models, and the model’s log-likelihood probability value is -47.8418. Abdelhady et al.64 recently used FSW to weld AA5754 plates, evaluating their tensile strength, hardness, and impact toughness. They used a multi-objective optimization strategy and ANOVA to determine the most significant parameters. It was found that the rotational speed significantly impacted tensile strength, hardness, and impact toughness. Multiple recent studies have established the beneficial effects of various optimization strategies. Bousnina et al.65 used a hybrid PSO-ANN-genetic algorithm to evaluate 304 L stainless steel spot weld strength. Vibration and welding settings affected spot welding resistance. The biggest component in displacement was vibration, at 47.20%. PSO-ANN projected values matched experimental data well. Genetic algorithms found optimal input parameters, improving by 93.1% above the maximum value. Verma et al.66 have utilized the genetic neural optimization technique to enhance the welding parameters for dissimilar weldments, leading to enhanced mechanical qualities and diminished flaws. Similarly, research utilizing the Taguchi-based desirability function approach has successfully enhanced the quality of welds by optimizing key parameters67.

Although there have been notable improvements in welding different types of aluminum alloys, important areas still need to be addressed, specifically in finding the best TIG welding parameters for AA5083 and AA7075 with ER4043 filler wire. Previous studies have focused on welding parameters or combining comparable metals. Still, limited studies have addressed the problems and optimization methodologies needed to join these two aluminum alloys using TIG welding. Few studies have assessed the effects of welding current, feed rate and gas flow rate on the Al similar and dissimilar metal joining process and their welding characteristics. Also, many researchers have employed the FSW technique to join Al alloys to study their weldability and characteristics. The research examines how welding current and root gap affect welded joint mechanical parameters such tensile strength, hardness, and impact energy. A suitable parameter of TIG welding is chosen to join dissimilar metals, AA5083 and AA7075, using ER4043 as filament wire, as it is a suitable material for both parent metals in terms of structural properties, alloying elements, and thermal behavior. The root gap and welding current are input responses for designing and analyzing outcomes such as UTS, YS, impact toughness, and hardness. Taguchi’s L9 array was used to design and experiment with TIG welding. The welding strength of dissimilar joints is evaluated by conducting uni-axial (tension) and impact tests. Taguchi’s analysis was adopted to optimize the output responses while analyzing experimental data. The optimization methodology is presented in Fig. 1 in a flow chart format.

Characterization

After welding, X-ray radiography checked the weldments for microscopic and macroscopic defects. Weldments are evaluated for faults using non-destructive testing without affecting their functionality. Radiography is an NDT that uses X-rays or γ-rays to pierce weldments and record images on film. This X-ray picture is used to find discontinuities, porosity, and fractures. The welding inspection is carried out on the CEREM 235 machine with short-wavelength X-rays for 2 min. The source voltage of 150 keV and a current of 3.5 mA have been maintained. Following NDT testing, the wire-cut EDM machine is used to slice welding coupons to evaluate the welding characteristics per ASTM standards (at room temperature 27 oC and humidity level 62%)53. A 3D isometric view of welded samples along with the slicing of specimens as shown in Fig. 3. To measure the quality of the welding process and optimize parameters, uni-axial testing with ASTM E855,70 standards was conducted using a UTM machine with a constant strain rate of 2 mm/min. The test specimens after the wire cutting are shown in Fig. 4. The weld surface hardness number was measured using a Brinell hardness tester by taking the average of three different points at the weld zone. Finally, strain energy under sudden loads was measured by an Izod impact tester.

Results and discussions

RT films

The RT results demonstrate no surface or subsurface flaws, such as spatter, inclusions, porosities, etc., in the dissimilar welded structures joined by the TIG welding procedure. The bead profile was uniform along the bead line in all the weldments, as shown in Fig. 5. The filler wire distribution during filament pass was almost uniform towards the base meals. The wider root gap (2 mm) weldments have shown more extended bead width compared to the other two root gap weldments.

It is perceived from the test that the welding strength (UTS) is higher in samples 5, 7, and 8 when the root gap is maintained at 1.5 mm than the other root gaps. The welding surface hardness number is measured by indentation at the weld zone in three locations along the weld line. The hardness number has slightly decreased with an increase in the welding current to 230 A from 210 A. The better hardness number is noticed when the welding current is maintained at 220 A. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

A statistical technique, ANOVA, evaluates the potential distinctions among a nominal-level parameter with two or more types and a dependent scale-level variable. ANOVA was used to analyze the hardness, impact toughness, and tensile strength of the dissimilar weldments of AA5083 and AA7075. Minitab, an application for statistical analysis, is used in this approach. The Eq. (1) is used to get the overall mean of the replies. The total of squared deviations will be found using this equation, which will then be divided into two parts using Eq. 2. Equation 3 is used to determine the squared deviation total. Equation 4 is used to compute the design parameters and contribution percentages.

Tensile strength

Table 8 shows the results of the ANOVA, in which a percentage contribution of every variable to tensile strength was determined by dividing the adjusted mean square by the sum of all adjusted mean square values. The root gap is the primary factor influencing tensile strength enhancement, which accounts for 44.78% of the effect. The welding current is closely followed, contributing 40.5% to the overall outcome. The error percentage was approximately 14.7%, and it can be reduced by considering other influential elements, such as gas flow rate and welding speed, to optimize the tensile characteristics of weldments. The main effects and residual plots of the tensile test results are shown in Fig. 8. It is observed that a root gap at level 2 and welding current level 1 are the most effective parameters to enhance the tensile properties due to the proper root gap and adequate heat input rate during the welding process.

0.2% of YS

The results of the ANOVA for 0.2% YS, as shown in Table 9, offer a statistical assessment of the impact of welding parameters, notably the current and root gap. Table 9 shows that the root gap substantially impacts the 0.2% yield strength of the welded joints. This is evident in the high F-value of 59.46 and a P-value of 0.001, much lower than the usually accepted significance level of 0.05. These findings indicate that differences in the root gap are accountable for a significant amount of the variance in yield strength, contributing 94.28% to the overall variability. On the other hand, the welding current has a minimal effect on yield strength. Its F-value is 1.61, and its P-value is 0.307, suggesting its impact is not statistically significant at the 0.05 level. The welding current contributes to only 2.55% of the overall variability in yield strength. The error term has a sum of squares of 113.78, representing 3.17% of the total variation. The low error percentage suggests that most of the variation in yield strength may be related to the controlled components, specifically welding current and root gap, with the root gap being the primary cause. The main effects and residual plots of 0.2% of the YS results are shown in Fig. 9. It is observed that root gap level 3 and welding current level 1 are the most effective parameters to enhance the yield strength properties. The welding current taken at three levels exhibits almost similar yield strength properties.

Hardness (BHN)

The results of the ANOVA for hardness (BHN), as shown in Table 10, offer a comprehensive statistical study of the impact of welding parameters, notably welding current and root gap. The welding current has an F-value of 6.24 and a P-value of 0.059, indicating that its impact on hardness is close to statistical significance (often considered significant at a level of 0.05). The welding current accounts for 36.88% of the entire variation in hardness, suggesting a significant but not predominant impact. The root gap demonstrates a greater F-value of 9.67 and a P-value of 0.029, suggesting a statistically significant impact on hardness. The root gap contributes to 57.20% of the overall variation, establishing it as the most relevant element in determining the hardness of the welded joints. The error term, which signifies unexplained variability, has a sum of squares equal to 24.44 and explains 5.91% of the overall variation in hardness. The low error percentage suggests that most of the variation in hardness is caused by the controlled factors of root gap and welding current. The main effects and residual plots of the hardness results are shown in Fig. 10. It is observed that the root gap at level 3 and welding current level 1 are the most effective parameters to enhance the hardness number due to the proper root gap and adequate heat input rate during the welding process. The level 1 and 2 parameters of welding current similarly affect the hardness number.

Conclusions

This study aimed to optimize the welding parameters of AA5083 and AA7075 by utilizing TIG and ER4043 filler wire. The experiment centered on examining the impact of current and root gaps on major outcomes, specifically UTS, YS, impact toughness, and hardness. Taguchi’s L9 array was organized, and the outcomes were optimized using ANOVA to ascertain the impact of each parameter on the variability in the responses.
