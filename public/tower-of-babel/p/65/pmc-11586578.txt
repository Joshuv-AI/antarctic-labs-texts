Identification of feline erythrocyte antigen 6 and lack of alloimmunization to feline erythrocyte antigen 4 in cats
Authors: Thomas Ternisien, Florian Azoulay, Mohammad Y Bangash, Marie‐Claude Blais
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNew feline erythrocyte antigens (FEAs) have been described based on the presence of naturally occurring alloantibodies (NOAb), but their immunogenicity and clinical relevance are poorly understood.Hypothesis/ObjectivesDescribe the immunogenicity of FEA 4 after sensitizing FEA 4–negative cats lacking NOAb and characterize anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies produced, including their rate of appearance, agglutination titer, and immunoglobulin class.AnimalsNineteen healthy type A cats were blood typed for FEAs 1 to 5 to identify suitable donor‐recipient pairs for FEA 4 sensitization.MethodsFour FEA 4–negative cats were transfused with FEA 4–positive red blood cells. Using a gel column technique, posttransfusion samples were screened daily for a week, weekly for a month, and monthly thereafter for anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies.ResultsAlloantibodies were not detected in the first 3 recipients despite repeated transfusions (1 and 3 additional transfusions for 2 and 1 recipients, respectively). In the 4th recipient, alloantibodies against its donor red blood cells were detected 21 days postsensitization. However, they were not directed against FEA 4, but rather against a novel FEA not yet described. The alloantibodies, named anti–FEA 6, remained detectable for >4 months after sensitization and were determined to be mostly immunoglobulin M based on sulfhydryl treatment.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceFeline erythrocyte antigen 4 does not appear to be immunogenic because repeated sensitization of 4 cats failed to produce detectable anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies. A new immunogenic antigen, named FEA 6, has been discovered, but additional studies are needed to document its clinical importance.Keywords: blood transfusion, crossmatch, feline erythrocyte antigen, immunogenicity

INTRODUCTION

Based on the presence of naturally occurring alloantibodies (NOAb) directed against red blood cell (RBC) antigens outside of the AB system, a recent study identified 5 novel feline erythrocyte antigen(FEAs), designated FEAs 1 to 5 in order of their discovery. 1 The discovery of these 5 FEAs highlights the complexity of transfusion medicine in cats beyond the AB system, although the clinical importance of these FEAs remains largely unknown. A previous study identified an association between FEA 1–negative phenotype and the presence of NOAb, which in addition to FEA 1 prevalence, suggested that it might correspond to the lost Mik antigen. 1 Based on this finding, a study evaluating the immunogenicity of FEA 1 was conducted in which the transfusion of FEA 1–negative cats with FEA 1–positive blood resulted in the production of anti–FEA 1 alloantibodies as early as 5 days posttransfusion. 2 In addition, an acute hemolytic transfusion reaction was described in an FEA 1–negative transfusion‐naive anemic cat with an anti–FEA 1 NOAb titer of 1 : 256. 2 This observation emphasizes the importance of performing a crossmatch, in addition to AB blood typing, before RBC administration to transfusion‐naive cats to improve transfusion safety. However, the immunogenicity of other FEAs currently remains undetermined.

As FEAs previously described, 1 FEA 4 was discovered based on the presence of NOAb, but interestingly not in 1 but in 3 different cats. Indeed, NOAb 4, 6, and 7 were likely directed against the same antigen, eventually referred to as FEA 4 based on their good to perfect agreement. 1 Based on this finding, FEA 4 became a 2nd key blood group of interest after FEA 1. The purpose of our study was to investigate the alloimmunogenicity of other FEAs with FEA 4. Our 1st objective was to characterize the development of anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies in FEA 4–negative transfusion‐naive cats lacking NOAb, including their rate of appearance, agglutination titer over time and immunoglobulin class. If successful, the 2nd objective was to bank serum or plasma containing the produced anti–FEA 4 alloantibody. Our investigation resulted in the identification of a new FEA, which led to the additional objective of its characterization in terms of prevalence in the study population, as well as rate of appearance, agglutination titer over time, and immunoglobulin class of the alloantibody directed against it.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Nineteen blood type A cats from Centre Hospitalier Universitaire Vétérinaire (CHUV) of the Faculty of Veterinary Medicine of the Université de Montréal's blood bank (n = 4) and teaching colonies (n = 15) were enrolled in the study. All cats underwent extensive blood typing for FEAs 1 to 5 and were investigated for the presence of NOAb using a gel column technique (ID‐MTS Micro Typing system; Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics), as previously described and detailed below. 1 , 2 The aim was to identify NOAb‐free type A donor‐recipient pairs to allow specific sensitization to FEA 4, which is compatible with FEAs 1, 2, 3, and 5, but incompatible with FEA 4 (ie, donors being FEA 4‐positive and recipients FEA 4‐negative). A complete physical examination, blood testing (hematology, serum biochemical profile, and serum total thyroxine concentration), and an echocardiogram by a board‐certified cardiologist were performed on each cat to assess their eligibility as potential blood transfusion donors and recipients, and were found to be unremarkable. In addition, major and minor crossmatches were performed between the donor and recipient cats, as described below.

The study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Université de Montréal (Rech‐2050).

Extensive blood typing: AB and FEAs 1 to 5

Briefly, after centrifugation of ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA)–anticoagulated blood samples (<72 hours after collection) and plasma removal, the RBC were washed 3 times using phosphate‐buffered saline. Then, 5 μL of washed packed RBC were added to 500 μL of an antibody enhancement solution (low ionic strength saline; Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostics, Raritan, New Jersey, USA) to obtain a 0.8% RBC suspension. For AB blood typing, 50 μL of the 0.8% RBC suspension was added to 25 μL of triticum vulgaris lectin serving as an anti‐B reagent (8 μg/mL, stored at −20°C) or 25 μL of known type B serum (anti‐A reagent) on top of 2 gel columns on a buffered saline ID card (ID‐MTS Micro Typing system; Ortho‐Clinical diagnostics, Pompano Beach, Florida, USA). A 3rd gel column served as an auto‐control (ie, the RBC suspension was mixed with plasma from the same animal using the same ratio). All 4 blood donor cats also had been previously blood typed using a commercial blood typing kit (Feline QuickTest Blood Typing, Alvedia, Limonest, France).

A similar procedure was used for FEAs 1 to 5 blood typing, using instead as reagents 25 μL of known feline anti–FEAs 1 to 5 polyclonal antibodies (stored at −20°C) in each gel column. 1 The gel cards then were incubated at 37°C for 15 minutes (Ortho‐Clinical Diagnostic Workstation, Raritan, New Jersey, USA) and centrifugated at 80g for 10 minutes.

All results were interpreted by a single observer according to a standardized classification and graded from 0 (negative) to 4+ (strongly positive). Briefly, the result was considered negative if all RBC migrated at the bottom of the gel column. Positive results were graded 1+ if most of the RBC were at the bottom but few RBC agglutinates were dispersed in the lower 3rd of the column, 2+ if RBC were dispersed throughout the gel, 3+ if most RBC were agglutinated on the top 3rd with some staying on the surface of the gel, and 4+ if all RBC remained at the surface of the gel. Extensive blood typing was performed at least twice on all recipients and donors on the day of sensitization, notably to confirm results.

NOAb screening

To assess for the presence of NOAb, plasma of each enrolled cat was crossmatched against a panel of 5 different 0.8% washed feline RBC suspensions, which were generated from cats expressing a diversity of FEA phenotypes (ie, enough positive and negative phenotypes for each FEA to be able to infer against which FEA the detected NOAb were directed). Again, 25 μL of the tested feline plasma was mixed with 50 μL of each 0.8% RBC suspension in 5 separate gel columns. The last column contained 25 μL of the tested feline plasma with 50 μL of its RBC suspension (auto‐control). The card was incubated and then centrifuged as described above. A positive result was interpreted as the presence of NOAb, and the FEA against which it was directed was deduced if possible.

Selection of donor‐recipient pairs

Based on the extensive typing results, recipient‐donor pairs were established among type A cats lacking NOAb: potential donors had to be FEA 4‐positive and recipients FEA 4‐negative, but otherwise compatible with other FEAs. To assess compatibly between donor and recipient cats, minor and major crossmatches were performed within the pairs, further documenting the lack of NOAb.

Sensitization of FEA 4–negative cats

The day before sensitization, blood was collected from the selected donor using the CHUV's standardized feline blood donor program procedure. After sedation (0.25 mg/kg diazepam and 4 mg/kg ketamine), 50 mL of blood (approximately 10 mL/kg) was aseptically collected from the jugular vein of the donor cat and then stored at 4°C. While refrigerated, the blood was allowed to decant over several hours and was converted into approximately half volume of RBC concentrate after plasma removal.

Similarly, to avoid vascular overload of the recipient, 10 mL/kg was collected from the recipient, sedated using the same protocol. Once fully awake from sedation, the recipient was transfused with approximately 5 mL/kg of packed RBC from the selected donor, over a maximum of 4 hours to reflect a standard blood transfusion. Close monitoring was performed in accordance with the Association of Veterinary Hematology and Transfusion Medicine (AVHTM) guidelines 3 : every 15 minutes for the 1st hour of the transfusion, and then every 30 minutes, including heart and respiratory rate, rectal temperature, pulse and blood pressure, mucous membrane color, and urine color if applicable (for hematuria or hemoglobinuria). A spun PCV was measured within minutes of the blood collection (ie, pretransfusion) as well as 1 to 2 hours posttransfusion. In addition, recipients remained in the intensive care unit for monitoring over the next 24 hours, and clinical examinations were performed daily at for the next week (ie, at the time of blood sampling).

Additional transfusions, with half of the previous volume of packed RBC, were administered over a maximum of 1 hour for at least 30 days if alloantibodies were not detectable. Additional blood transfusions were not necessarily from the same donor, as long as the donor‐recipient pair was compatible (except for FEA 4).

Rate of appearance of alloantibodies and agglutination titers

After each sensitization, the recipient's plasma was investigated for the production of anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies using the gel column technique daily for the 1st week, weekly for the 1st month, and monthly thereafter. To do so, the recipient's plasma was tested against its donor's RBC (FEA 4‐positive), in addition to RBC from an additional FEA 4–positive cat and 1 FEA 4–negative cat serving as positive and negative controls, respectively. Auto‐controls (recipient's own RBC incubated with its own plasma) were performed in parallel each time. The results were interpreted and graded as described above, with any agglutination grade ≥1+ considered positive. The agglutination titer was defined as the highest plasma dilution at which a positive agglutination reaction was observed and was expressed as the whole number corresponding to the serum dilution.

Immunoglobulin class

The immunoglobulin class of posttransfusion alloantibodies was determined based on sulfhydryl treatment as described previously. 4 Briefly, agglutination titers were determined before and after incubating the plasma of interest with dithiothreitol (DTT) in a 1 : 1 ratio for 60 minutes at 37°C. Dithiothreitol breaks the disulfide bonds of immunoglobulin M (IgM), which decreases their agglutination and complement binding activity. Therefore, if the agglutination capacity of the alloantibody becomes null or is markedly decreased, the alloantibodies are considered IgM or mostly IgM, respectively. If agglutination capacity is unchanged (identical agglutination titer pre‐ and post‐DTT treatment), the alloantibodies are deemed to be IgG. 2

On the day of sensitization, extensive blood typing, as well as minor and major crossmatches between the selected pairs, was repeated and identical results were obtained, including lack of NOAb and compatibility between donors and recipients.

All blood transfusions were uneventful, with no clinical signs of acute or delayed transfusion reactions. Clinically, the cats' vital signs remained stable, and no signs of intravascular hemolysis were observed when serum and urine were assessed. Pretransfusion to posttransfusion absolute changes in PCV were +5, +7, and −2 in recipients 1 to 3, respectively, whereas recipient 4 experienced no change in PCV (median, 2.5%; total solids change, 0‐2 g/L; median, 1 g/L without any hemolysis noted).

Given the absence of alloantibody detection in recipients 1, 2, and 3, new sensitizations were performed using half of the volume (ie, approximately 2.5 mL/kg of packed RBC), according to the same procedures as previously described. Recipient 1 received a 2nd transfusion from donor 1 on day 42, recipient 2 from donor 3 on day 35, and recipient 3 received 2 additional transfusions from donor 2 (days 42 and 92). All transfusions were monitored closely and were uneventful. After subsequent transfusions, no alloantibodies were detected in recipients 1, 2, and 3.

Although no alloantibodies were detected in the plasma of recipient 4 on days 0 to 7 and day 14 using the donor's RBC as well as FEA 4–positive and FEA 4–negative controls, alloantibodies were detected 21 days posttransfusion against the donor's RBC (ie, now incompatible to donor 1; Figure 1). However, the plasma of recipient 4 was compatible with the FEA 4–positive control, but incompatible with 2 FEA 4–negative controls. Given this unexpected result (inverted compatibility pattern) and the established compatibility between the donor 1 and recipient 4 for other FEAs, it was deduced that the alloantibody was directed against an unidentified FEA, thus named FEA 6.

The plasma from recipient 4 containing the newly identified anti–FEA 6 alloantibody was used to blood type the remaining 18 cats as described above: 7 cats were found to be FEA 6‐positive and 12 were FEA 6‐negative. Recipients 1 and 2, which also had been transfused with blood from donor 1, were FEA 6‐positive, explaining their lack of sensitization for this specific antigen. Recipient 3 was FEA 6‐negative, but had received its transfusions from donor 2, which was also FEA 6‐negative, explaining the overall absence of sensitization for FEA 6 in recipients 1 to 3.

Because no alloantibodies against FEA 4 were detected, alloantibody titers against FEA 6 in recipient 4 then were followed over time (Figure 2). The highest titer of 1 : 64 was detected at 30 days. The titer decreased thereafter, although with a brief increase noted at day 54. The immunoglobulin class was assessed using the plasma sample with the highest titer (1 : 64). After incubating the plasma with DTT, the agglutination titer was 1 : 1, which supports that anti–FEA 6 alloantibodies are mostly IgM.

DISCUSSION

Based on our results, FEA 4 does not appear to be alloimmunogenic in cats because we were unable to detect posttransfusion anti–FEA 4 alloantibodies in the 4 recipients, despite repeated sensitization in 3 recipients. Although our study needs to be repeated on a larger population, FEA 4 does not appear to have clinical relevance for cats receiving transfusions and lacking NOAb.

The absence of alloimmunization, meaning the lack of production of postsensitization alloantibodies against FEA 4, does not mean that FEA 4 is not immunogenic. Indeed, anti–FEA 4 NOAb originally were documented in 3 different index cats leading to the discovery of FEA 4 (prevalence of 8.7% in a population of FEA 4–negative cats). 1 For ethical reasons, our study was not designed to evaluate the clinical relevance of such anti–FEA 4 NOAb, which may lead to an ineffective transfusion or hemolytic transfusion reaction in a cat's initial transfusion. In humans, the origin of NOAbs remains mainly unknown, but NOAbs are suspected to be associated with the human microbiome and secreted by B1‐lymphocytes, a new subpopulation of B‐lymphocytes which is generated mainly in the fetal and postfetal period. 5 , 6 The importance of performing a crossmatch in transfusion‐naive cats is debated in veterinary medicine, in part because the reported prevalence of NOAb is inconsistent, ranging from 0% to 29%, depending on the study population, methodology and crossmatching techniques used. 1 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 Posttransfusion incompatibilities outside of the AB system are more consistently reported, with prevalence of sensitization ranging from 25% to 32%. 7 , 9 , 13 Nonetheless, the clinical relevance of NOAb and of posttransfusion alloantibodies outside of the AB system remain a matter of controversy. Two cases of acute hemolytic transfusion reactions have been described in transfusion‐naive cats with anti‐Mik and anti–FEA 1 NOAbs (titers of 1 : 64 and 1 : 256, respectively), demonstrating that NOAb can have serious clinical consequences. 2 , 14 However, studies evaluating the clinical usefulness of crossmatching in transfusion‐naive or already transfused cats do not all agree. Some investigators have found a decrease in transfusion‐related febrile reactions, shorter duration of hospitalization or higher posttransfusion hematocrits with crossmatch‐compatible transfusion, whereas the studies of others do not support these findings. 9 , 10 , 13 A study such as ours, investigating the alloimmunogenicity of a specific erythrocyte antigen, is therefore important in order to gain a better understanding of the relative importance of FEAs outside of the AB system.

Although it was not the goal, our study allowed discovery a new FEA designated as FEA 6. The uniqueness of this antigen compared to FEAs 1 to 5 is that it was discovered posttransfusion after the production of alloantibodies directed against FEA 6, rather than based on the presence of NOAb. No cats with NOAb against FEA 6 were found in our study population. Clinical relevance of FEA 6 is plausible for several reasons. First, we documented its immunogenicity after accidental alloimmunization of recipient 4. Second, the produced anti–FEA 6 alloantibodies remained detectable for the duration of the study (ie, >4 months after sensitization), with a relatively high agglutination titer (1 : 64 at 30 days). 2 , 14 Third, anti–FEA 6 alloantibodies are IgM, similar to anti–FEA 1 and the anti–A alloantibodies found in type B cats. 2 , 15 Compared to IgG molecules that contain 1 Fc domain, pentameric IgM have different Fc domains, facilitating rapid complement activation and thus are generally more potent contributors to hemolytic transfusion reactions compared with IgG. 15 Finally, using the anti–FEA 6 alloantibodies produced post‐sensitization to blood type the remaining 18 cats from our study population, 7/19 cats were found to be FEA 6 positive, resulting in an estimated prevalence of 37%. If this prevalence is confirmed in a larger feline population, FEA 6 could be responsible for a substantial number of posttransfusion sensitizations in cats. In a given population, the immunogenicity of a blood group is estimated based on the probability of an antigen‐negative recipient receiving a transfusion from antigen‐positive donor (ie, 63% of FEA 6–negative recipient cats multiplied by 37% of FEA 6–positive donor cats, resulting in 23% of cats at risk of sensitization for FEA 6). Beyond this simple definition, immunogenicity also is influenced by variable factors such as multiple antigen exposures, dosage effects by antigen density, and the uncertainty of finding a low frequency antigen in a given population, all leading to more complex prediction calculations proposed in human medicine. 16 Interestingly, the reported prevalence of blood incompatibilities in previously transfused cats varies from 25% to 32%. 7 , 9 , 13 Although this finding is an arithmetic coincidence, further investigations into the role of FEA 6 in posttransfusion incompatibilities are warranted.

The importance of comprehensive extensive blood typing in cats outside of the AB system should be carefully considered in clinical settings, given its limited accessibility and the largely unknown clinical relevance of the various FEAs. Given that acute immunologic transfusion reactions have been documented in transfusion‐naive cats despite AB compatibility, we recommend crossmatching from the 1st transfusion, consistent with AVHTM guidelines, 3 in order to make transfusions as safe and effective as possible. Although AB blood typing is essential and can be performed in a matter of minutes, 17 , 18 , 19 crossmatching is more time‐consuming and requires some level of expertise. Therefore, in some emergency situations, only AB blood typing may be realistic. The recent commercialization of crossmatching kits, which are less laborious and do not require special skills, may improve the clinical accessibility of crossmatching, but clinicians must be aware of the poor agreement reported between some of the techniques or the lack of reported data in cats. 7 , 8 , 11 , 20 Clinically, extensive blood typing for the various FEAs may become relevant in cases of blood incompatibilities (eg, anemic cats incompatible with several blood donors or after a suspected immunologic hemolytic transfusion reaction) to facilitate identification of a compatible donor. Ultimately, given that multitransfusion recipients are increasingly common in feline medicine, extensive blood typing for FEAs ideally using a commercial test may be used to minimize posttransfusion incompatibilities, which are currently documented in almost a 33% of previously transfused cats. To do so, accessibility to FEA blood typing will need to be facilitated, although we failed to produce and bank anti–FEA 4 antibodies, and banked only limited a quantity of anti–FEA 6 antibodies because recipient 4 was placed for adoption.

Lastly, pretransfusion and posttransfusion changes in PCV were quite variable from 1 recipient to another (from −2% to 7%) and could have been influenced by several factors given that the study protocol was carried out in nonanemic cats, including a 10 mL/kg blood collection to avoid volume overload, and the variable delay in starting transfusion. 21 , 22 In this context, the use of biotin‐labeled or radiolabeled transfused RBC would have been ideal to document accurately their survival and clearance rate.

Our protocol aimed to mimic a clinical scenario in regard to the blood volume transfused but had limitations because of the small number of healthy individuals, which affects representativeness and statistical power. Additionally, we lost recipient 4, because the cat was adopted and thus not monitored for agglutination titer over time. Further research is required to explore the clinical relevance of anti–FEA 4 NOAbs and to assess posttransfusion cell viability.

CONCLUSION

Feline erythrocyte antigen 4 does not appear to be alloimmunogenic in FEA 4–negative transfusion‐naive cats lacking NOAb. Postsensitization, FEA 6 was discovered, demonstrating several features of immunogenicity: anti–FEA 6 alloantibodies of the IgM class were still detectable >4 months after sensitization with high agglutination. The prevalence of FEA 6 and possible association with NOAb should be further investigated in a larger population of cats.
