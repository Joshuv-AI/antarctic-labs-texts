CmSGT1, Transcriptionally Regulated by CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31, Improves Powdery Mildew Resistance in Cucurbita moschata
Authors: Wei‐Li Guo, Jin‐Peng Zhao, Xue‐Jin Chen, Bi‐Hua Chen, Qing‐Fei Li, Xin‐Zheng Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPowdery mildew (PM), mainly caused by Podosphaera xanthii, is a severe destructive disease that threatens the production of cucurbit crops globally. Heterologous transformation has shown that the CmSGT1 gene (suppressor of the G2 allele of skp1) improved PM resistance in tobacco. However, the function of the gene in pumpkins ( Cucurbita moschata ) is largely unknown. Herein, transient CmSGT1 overexpression in pumpkin cotyledons inhibited the spore germination and mycelia growth of P. xanthii by inducing an increase in salicylic acid (SA) content, and exogenous SA intensified the inhibitory effect of the gene on the growth of P. xanthii. The β‐glucuronidase activity of cotyledons transformed with the CmSGT1 promoter was induced by PM and signaling molecules (gibberellic acid, ethephon, SA, abscisic acid, and methyl jasmonate). The yeast one‐hybrid assay verified that transcription factor CmWRKY21, CmWRKY31, and CmWRKY75 proteins interact with the CmSGT1 promoter. Transactivation analysis revealed that CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 significantly triggered the expression of GUS driven by the CmSGT1 promoter under PM. Furthermore, transient co‐overexpression of CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 and CmSGT1 enhanced the inhibitory effect on the growth of P. xanthii. In conclusion, the CmSGT1 gene is a PM resistance gene in pumpkin and is transcriptionally regulated by CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31. Our study provides a reference for resistance breeding of pumpkins.

Introduction

Powdery mildew (PM) is a destructive disease that seriously threatens cucurbit crop production globally. The causal agent of PM in cucurbit is Podosphaera xanthii (formerly known as Sphaerotheca fuliginea), a biotrophic pathogen widely distributed worldwide (Fukino et al. 2013). The disease is very easy to break out at high temperatures under dry and wet alternating conditions (Mccreight 2003), and the incidence rate is as high as 90% in severe cases (Casulli et al. 2008), resulting in a 30%–70% reduction in yield in pumpkin ( Cucurbita moschata ) (Fu et al. 2024). Fungicide applications poorly control PM, and the long‐term use of pesticides leads to environmental pollution. Therefore, exploiting the resistance genes to breed resistant varieties can effectively control the disease.

Many research studies on the mining of PM‐resistant genes in cucurbit crops mainly focus on cucumber and melon. For example, the single mutant of CsMLO8 and CsMLO11 in cucumber can improve PM resistance (Dong et al. 2023), and the triple mutant of CsMLO1/CsMLO8/CsMLO11 obtains complete resistance to PM (Ma et al. 2024). The CsLRR1 gene, located at the major PM‐resistance quantitative trait locus Pm5.2, improves PM resistance of transgenic cucumber (Xu et al. 2024). In melon, the CmPIF8‐CmERF27‐CmACS10 module regulates red light‐induced ethylene biosynthesis to resist P. xanthii infection (Wu et al. 2024), and melon Pm‐w WMR29 significantly increases the resistance to P. xanthii race 1 (Boissot et al. 2023). PM resistance in zucchini is controlled by one major dominant locus CpPM10.1, and the expression of candidate gene Cp4.1LG10g02780 in resistant individuals is significantly upregulated after inoculation with the PM pathogen (Wang et al. 2021). The release of the pumpkin genome has contributed to exploring resistant genes (Sun et al. 2017). The pumpkin CmoAP2/ERF gene is identified to be involved in resistance to PM using a genome‐wide association study (Alavilli et al. 2022). In our previous work, the CmSGT1‐overexpressed tobacco showed noticeable alleviation in powdery symptoms caused by Erysiphe cichoracearum (Guo et al. 2019). Considering the specificity of parasitism by the PM pathogen, the function of the CmSGT1 gene in regulating the resistance of pumpkin is largely unknown.

SGT1 (suppressor of the skp1 G2 allele) is a vital resistance protein that extensively regulates hormone‐mediated signal transduction during plant disease resistance (Jiang et al. 2022). During protein ubiquitination, AtSGT1b interacts with the COI1 (JA receptor) and plays a role in hormone signaling by stabilizing the COI1 protein (Zhang et al. 2015). Simultaneously, COI1 encodes F‐box protein, a component of SCFCOI1 (Skp1/Cullin/F‐boxCOI1) E3 ubiquitin ligase, and ubiquitin protein degradation participates in regulating both JA signal transduction and disease resistance (Sheard et al. 2010; Zhang et al. 2015). SGT1 usually interacts with the SCF complex to regulate the ubiquitination and degradation of Aux/IAA, resulting in downstream gene expression in the auxin transduction signal pathway (Jiang et al. 2022). In addition, SGT1, HSP90, and RAR1 (Required for Mla12 Resistance) form a molecular chaperone complex, activating the NB‐LRR (leucine‐rich repeat) class R proteins that are involved in the defense response against pathogens (Zhu et al. 2017), participating in hypersensitive reaction cell necrosis induced by Pst elicitors (Wang et al. 2022). Furthermore, SGT1 phosphorylation plays a vital role in promoting plant immune activation. TaMAPK6 interacts with and phosphorylates TaSGT1 to promote its nuclear translocation, and the TaMKK2‐TaMAPK6‐TaSGT1 phosphorylation cascade plays an important role in wheat immunity (Shu et al. 2025). Silencing SGT1 reduces the accumulation of MEK2DD protein, while SGT1 contributes to cell necrosis formation by maintaining an active MEK2 protein level (Ichimura et al. 2016). The above reports indicate that SGT1 performs many biological functions by interacting with proteins to form functional complexes. However, the upstream transcriptional regulation of the SGT1 genes has rarely been studied.

Transcription factors (TFs) have a key role in activating or suppressing the expression of target genes by directly or indirectly interacting with gene promoters (Ptashne 2005; Wehner et al. 2011). Promoters contain various cis‐acting elements and regulate gene expression at the transcriptional level in response to many biological processes and stress attacks (Wang et al. 2019; Yang et al. 2020; Yu et al. 2023). Moreover, stress‐responsive promoters hold enormous promise for transgenic strategies in plant biotechnology (Wang et al. 2018). The Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation technology is an efficiently established approach for analyzing gene function and promoter activity (Xu et al. 2010; Yang et al. 2000). In the present study, the function of the CmSGT1 gene and the activity of the CmSGT1 promoter were studied in pumpkins using a transient transformation assay. Moreover, TFs that interact with the CmSGT1 promoter were identified by a yeast one‐hybrid (Y1H) method, and how to regulate CmSGT1 gene expression in pumpkins was analyzed.

Materials and Methods

Isolation of CmSGT1 Promoter Sequence

Genomic DNA extraction was performed on the true leaves of the highly resistant pumpkin material “03112‐2” and the susceptible pumpkin material “Jiujiang Qiaoding,” abbreviated “JJJD,” using the TaKaRa MiniBEST Plant genomic DNA Extraction Kit and used for cloning of the CmSGT1 promoter. DNA concentration and purity were detected by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis and micro‐spectrophotometer. The CmSGT1 promoter sequence was obtained from the cucurbit genome website (http://cucurbitgenomics.org/) based on C. moschata genome sequencing (accession number: CmoCh18G013110.1) and amplified using pumpkin genomic DNA as a template (Table S1), and then confirmed by sequencing (Invitrogen Co. Shanghai, China). Cis‐acting elements were analyzed using both PlantCARE (http://bioinformatics.psb.ugent.be//webtools/plantcare/html) and PLACE (http://www.dna.affrc.go.jp/PLACE/signalup.html) (Higo et al. 1999; Lescot et al. 2002).

Construction of CmSGT1 Promoter::GUS Plasmid, 35S::CmSGT1 Plasmid, and 35S::CmWRKYs Plasmid

Full length of the CmSGT1 promoter (pCmSGT1) was amplified using corresponding primers with the addition of Sca I/BamH I (Table S1), and the PCR products were cloned into the pBI121‐GUS vector (pCmSGT1::GUS construct). The construct 35S::CmSGT1 was taken from our previous report (Guo et al. 2019), and the full‐length CmSGT1 gene was inserted into the pBI221 vector under the control of the 35S cauliflower mosaic virus (CaMV) promoter. Referring to the C. moschata genome database, the open reading frame of CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31/CmWRKY75 TF from pumpkin “03112‐2” was obtained using a homologous cloning method and inserted into the pBI121‐EGFP vector under the control of the 35S CaMV promoter (35S::CmWRKYs construct) (Table S1 and Table S2), respectively. All the above‐mentioned constructs were verified by restriction enzyme digestion and separately introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV3101 cells (Guo et al. 2019), including pCmSGT1::GUS, 35S::CmSGT1, and 35S::CmWRKYs construct.

Agrobacterium‐Mediated Transient Transformation Assay

Pumpkin is recognized as one of the most difficult crops for stable genetic transformation. Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation in pumpkin plants is a valuable tool for studying gene function and promoter activity by a cotyledon injection method (Chen et al. 2021). When the cotyledon of susceptible pumpkin “JJJD” was flattened (at about 15 days), the lower part of the cotyledon was injected with the transformation solution (OD600 for 0.4) by needleless syringe. Transformation solution from both the 35S::CmSGT1 construct and an equal mixture of 35S::CmSGT1 and 35S::CmWRKYs was injected separately to analyze the function of the CmSGT1 gene. Meanwhile, transformation solution from both the pCmSGT1::GUS construct and an equal mixture of pCmSGT1::GUS and 35S::CmWRKYs by volume was injected separately to analyze the activity of the CmSGT1 promoter. After injection, the seedlings were placed in dark conditions for 48 h and then moved to a growth chamber at 25°C, with a 16‐h light/8‐h dark cycle and light intensity of 4400 lx for normal cultivation. The pBI121‐GUS vector containing the CaMV35S promoter was used as an empty vector control (35S::GUS, EV). Cotyledons were injected with water alone as an untransformed control. After dark cultivation, the gene‐overexpressed plants were identified by quantitative real‐time PCR (qRT‐PCR). In each experiment, three young cotyledons were collected from the three transient transformations (i.e., one sample). The treatments were arranged in a randomized complete block design with three independent biological replicates.

Podosphaera xanthii Inoculation (PXI) and Exogenous Hormone Treatment

For the function analysis of the CmSGT1 gene under PXI, pumpkin cotyledons injected with both 35S::CmSGT1 and a mixture of 35S::CmSGT1 and 35S::CmWRKYs for 48 h were sprayed with P. xanthii spore suspension (106 spores/mL), separately. Also, after pretreatment with SA for 24 h, transient CmSGT1‐overexpressed cotyledons were inoculated with the P. xanthii pathogen to analyze the effect of SA on the function of the CmSGT1 gene. The above‐mentioned CmSGT1‐transformed cotyledons were sampled to observe spore germination and mycelia growth of the P. xanthii pathogen at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h post‐inoculation (hpi) and to measure the hormone SA and ABA content at 48 hpi. Meanwhile, cotyledons co‐transformed with CmSGT1 and CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 were sampled to observe spore germination and mycelia growth of the P. xanthii pathogen at 24, 48, and 72 hpi.

For activity analysis of the CmSGT1 promoter in response to PXI and hormone, the cotyledons infiltrated with pCmSGT1::GUS construct for 48 h were sprayed with P. xanthii spore suspension (106 spores/mL) or exogenous hormone, including 100 μmol/L methyl jasmonate (MeJA), 100 μmol/L salicylic acid (SA), 100 μmol/L gibberellic acid (GA3), 100 μmol/L abscisic acid (ABA), and 0.5 g/L ethephon (Eth) (Guo et al. 2019). In particular, SA was dissolved in 10% ethanol, and MeJA and Eth in sterile water. Moreover, the control plant (CK) was sprayed with 10% ethanol and sterile water separately (Tang et al. 2017). Furthermore, pumpkin cotyledons infiltrated with both pCmSGT1::GUS and 35S::CmWRKYs for 48 h were also sprayed with spore suspension (106 spores/mL) and sampled at 24, 48, and 72 h to analyze the regulatory effect of CmWRKYs on the CmSGT1 promoter. The promoter‐transformed cotyledons were used for β‐glucuronidase (GUS) histochemical staining at different time points (12, 24, 48, 72, and 120 h), and GUS activity was examined at 48 h.

Histochemical GUS Assay, Mycelia Growth Observation, and Hormone Content Measurement

GUS assay was carried out using the procedures described by Jefferson (1987). Post‐infiltrated cotyledons were exposed to a −0.095‐MPa vacuum for 10 min and then submerged in GUS staining buffer at 37°C overnight. The cotyledons were decolonized with 70% ethanol, and their photographs were captured. Fluorometric determination of GUS activity was done using 4‐methylumbelliferyl glucuronides (Wang et al. 2019). GUS activity is shown as nM of 4‐MU generated per min per mg soluble protein. GUS measurements were technically repeated three times.

Histological observations of fungal growth were performed by methyl blue staining as described by Guo et al. (2018). The spore germination rate at 24 hpi was calculated using the formula: Germination rate = No. of germinated spores in the field/total No. of spores in the field × 100%. The hyphae length was measured at 48 hpi using the ImageJ software. Each cotyledon was observed for a minimum of 10 views.

Cotyledons transformed with 35S::CmSGT1 were collected at 48 hpi and placed in liquid nitrogen. SA was extracted and measured using an HPLC‐MS/MS system as previously described (Fung et al. 2008). The extraction, purification, and determination of endogenous ABA level by an indirect ELISA technique were performed as described by Yang et al. (2001). Hormone content measurements were technically repeated three times.

qRT‐PCR Analysis

The RNA extraction, first‐strand cDNA synthesis, and qRT‐PCR were performed as described by Guo et al. (2018). Relative gene expression level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method. The seedlings of two pumpkin genotypes (the highly resistant “03112‐2” and the susceptible “JJJD”) were sprayed with freshly prepared P. xanthii spore suspension (106 spores/mL) and then maintained in a growth chamber as previously described (Guo et al. 2019). Two upper leaves of four separate seedlings were sampled to detect the expression profile of three CmWRKYs (CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31/CmWRKY75) at 0, 6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 hpi. On the other hand, cotyledons transformed with CmSGT1 alone and CmSGT1 and CmWRKYs simultaneously were sampled to examine the expression of CmSGT1 and CmWRKYs at 120 and 72 h of normal cultivation, respectively. Pumpkin β‐actin gene was used as an internal control for normalization of gene expression (Wu and Cao 2010). Three biological and technical replicates were used for each group. All primers used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Y1H Screening

Y1H screening was carried out using the Matchmaker Gold One Hybrid Library screening system (Clontech, Cat. No. 630304). Based on the predicted distribution of the cis‐acting W‐box element, the truncated sequence of the CmSGT1 promoter, located −1634 to −1113 bp upstream of the start codon, was amplified to 521 bp (namely, pCmSGT1 A ) (Figure S2) and connected to the pAbAi vector, generating the Y1H bait. Y1H gold yeast receptive cells were prepared using the Yeastmaker Yeast Transformation System II (Cat. Nos. 630439, Clontech, USA). The BstB I enzyme digestion was carried out on the recombinant plasmid, transferred into the competent cells, and integrated into the yeast through homologous recombination. A 100‐μL yeast solution was coated on the SD/‐Ura media and cultured at 30°C to produce monoclones (2–3 days). A single colony was selected and incubated overnight in a YPDA medium, and the positive colony was identified by PCR. A positive bait yeast solution was uniformly added to the SD/‐Ura solid media with different concentrations of AbAr screening and cultured at 30°C for 2–3 days. Colony growth was observed under different concentrations of AbAr to determine the background AbAr concentration of the bait vector.

Open reading frame of CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31/CmWRKY75 TF was constructed into the prey vector pGadT7 to create prey (Table S1) and transferred into yeast strain containing pAbAi‐pCmSGT1 A , respectively. Then, uniformly coated in SD/‐Leu/AbAr plates, inversely placed for 3 days at 30°C, their growth status was observed, and yeast PCR detection was performed. The positive yeast solution was added to the SD/‐Leu liquid media for propagation, centrifuged the yeast solution at 6000 rpm, resuspended with 0.9% NaCl solution, diluted at OD600 = 0.2, and then continued to dilute the yeast solution to 100, 10−1, 10−2, and 10−3. Diluted yeast solution was added onto the SD/‐Leu solid media containing the corresponding AbAr concentration and incubated for 2–3 days to observe the colony growth. Both positive (p53‐AbAi/ad‐Rec‐p53) and negative (pGADT7‐AD + bait‐BD) controls were treated similarly.

Statistical Analysis

Values were expressed as the means ± standard errors (SEs) of three independent determinations. Data were compared by one‐way analysis of variance, and the significance of differences between CK and treatment was tested by a post hoc comparison test (Student–Newman–Keuls) at p < 0.05 with SPSS 19.0 for Windows.

Results

Transient CmSGT1‐Overexpressed Pumpkin Plant Improved PM Resistance

The function of CmSGT1 was performed by Agrobacterium‐mediated transient transformation analysis. The CmSGT1 in transiently transformed pumpkin cotyledons exhibited higher expression levels than that of the EV control under normal conditions, with 2.10‐fold and 1.89‐fold of EV at 0 and 120 h post‐dark cultivation (Figure S1), indicating that the CmSGT1 gene was successfully overexpressed in the transiently transformed plants.

The spore germination rate at 24 hpi, primary hyphae length, and secondary hyphae number at 48 hpi in the cotyledons of CmSGT1‐overexpressed pumpkin were counted to be significantly lower than that of the EV control (Table 1). In the inoculated EV plant, conidia germinated and grew into germ tubes at 24 hpi, mycelia bifurcated to form secondary hyphae at 48 hpi, a dense hyphal network developed and conidiophores appeared at 72 hpi, and chains of conidiophores formed at 96 hpi (Figure 1). However, in the transient CmSGT1 transformants, mycelia began to differentiate into secondary hyphae at 48 hpi, continuing to form tertiary hyphae at 72 hpi, and conidiophores appeared at 96 hpi. Furthermore, after exogenous SA pretreatment, mycelia bifurcated to form secondary hyphae at 72 hpi and continued to form tertiary hyphae at 96 hpi, with a lower spore germination rate at 24 hpi and primary hyphae length at 48 hpi (Table 1). These observations indicated that CmSGT1 overexpression in pumpkin plants exerts an inhibitory effect on the germination and growth of P. xanthii, and SA intensifies the inhibitory effect of the gene on the growth of this pathogen.

Cloning the CmSGT1 Promoter and Sequence Analysis

To reveal the transcriptional regulation of the gene under PXI, a 1634‐bp fragment, upstream of the start codon, was obtained and considered the full length of the CmSGT1 promoter. An alignment of the CmSGT1 promoter sequence between the resistant and susceptible pumpkin materials exhibited 100% identity (Figure S3). A search for cis‐acting elements in the promoter region identified three groups of putative regulatory motifs, including core motifs (TATA‐box, CAAT box), hormone‐associated motifs (TGA element, ABRE, ERE, MYC, etc.), and pathogen defense–associated motifs (W‐box motif, box S motif) (Table S3).

CmSGT1 Promoter Response to PXI and Exogenous Hormones

To explore whether the CmSGT1 promoter is responsive to these treatments, transiently transformed pCmSGT1::GUS pumpkin cotyledons were generated. During 24–120 h after dark cultivation, pumpkin cotyledons transformed with 35S::GUS (EV) under both CK and PXI exhibited an apparent dark blue color (Figure 3a). In contrast, cotyledons infiltrated with water alone (untransformed) were not stained blue, indicating that the GUS gene was successfully and efficiently expressed in pumpkin cotyledons. Consistently, the GUS activity of EV was high at 48 h (Figure 3b). Compared with CK, the overall GUS staining in pCmSGT1 transient transformants was observed to be a more noticeable blue color under PXI and exogenous hormone treatments (GA3, Eth, SA, ABA, and MeJA) (Figure 3a). Among them, GA3, Eth, and SA treatments appeared to be the most apparent staining during 12–48 h. GUS activity was also consistently and significantly induced by PXI and exogenous hormone treatments at 48 h, with the GA3 and SA‐induced activity levels upregulated by 3.60‐fold and 3.36‐fold, respectively (Figure 3b). These data supported that CmSGT1 promoter plays a positive role in pumpkin responses to pathogen infection and exogenous hormones. They showed that the tested 1634 bp promoter fragment is at least partially responsible for the transcriptional response to stress/hormone‐related treatments.

Identification of TFs Interacting With the CmSGT1 Promoter

A W‐box motif in the CmSGT1 promoter suggested that CmSGT1 may be regulated by WRKY TFs (Table S3). To test this, the expression patterns of three CmWRKYs from the 11 WRKY unigenes identified by transcriptome sequencing of pumpkin inoculated with P. xanthii pathogen (Guo et al. 2018) were studied by qRT‐PCR (Figure 4). During the 72‐h period, the expression of CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 in both susceptible and resistant plants was significantly induced by PXI treatment, with the resistant “03112‐2” plants increased by 4.7‐fold at 48 h and 15.2‐fold at 24 h, respectively. Meanwhile, the resistant “03112‐2” CmWRKY75 expression under PXI treatment was higher than that of CK during 12–48 h. The results indicated that PXI treatment induced the expression of CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 in both susceptible and resistant plants, as well as the expression of CmWRKY75 in resistant plants. Considering that the CmSGT1 expression in “03112‐2” plants was significantly upregulated by PXI treatment during 12 h (Guo et al. 2019), we selected these CmWRKYs (CmWRKY21, CmWRKY31, and CmWRKY75) consistent with its expression pattern in PXI‐treated resistant “03112‐2” plants for further research.

The Y1H method was next used to verify the interaction between these three CmWRKY TFs and the CmSGT1 promoter. The pCmSGT1 truncated fragment pCmSGT1 A was used to construct the bait vector. The growth of pCmSGT1 A bait yeast was inhibited at 400 ng/mL (Figure S4), and the concentration of AbAr that could inhibit AbAr background expression was determined to be 450 ng/mL. pGADT7‐CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31/CmWRKY75 and pABAi‐pCmSGT1 A co‐transformed yeast could grow normally on SD/‐Leu plates with relevant AbAr (Figure 5), and corresponding CmWRKYs were validated by PCR detection of co‐transformed yeast solution (Figure S5). These results demonstrated that the CmWRKYs (CmWRKY21, CmWRKY31, CmWRKY75) can specifically bind to the CmSGT1 promoter.

CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 Upregulated the Expression of CmSGT1

To further demonstrate that three CmWRKYs regulate the CmSGT1 promoter region, both the effector vector for 35S::CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31/CmWRKY75 and the reporter vector pCmSGT1::GUS were co‐transformed into pumpkin cotyledons by a transient transformation assay (Figure 6). Compared with the pCmSGT1 single transformation, co‐transformation of pumpkin cotyledons with the pCmSGT1 and the CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 was more intensely stained under PXI, and the GUS activity at 48 hpi was significantly upregulated, suggesting that CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 can trigger GUS gene expression. Meanwhile, there was no noticeable change in GUS staining and its activity on the co‐transformation of the pCmSGT1 and CmWRKY75 under PXI.

To explain the function of binding of CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 and CmSGT1 promoter under PXI, cotyledons co‐transformed with CmSGT1 and CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 were generated by a transient transformation analysis. The expression levels of CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 in co‐transformed cotyledons were higher, 2.01 fold and 2.26 fold of CmSGT1 single transformant at 72 h (Figure S6), indicating that the CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 were successfully overexpressed in the co‐transformed plants. Compared with the CmSGT1 single transformation, the spore germination and primary hyphae length of CmSGT1 and CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 co‐transformation were significantly decreased (Table 2). Microscopic observation of fungal growth showed that in the co‐transformed cotyledons, conidia germinated at 24 hpi, began germ tube branching and hyphae elongation at 48 hpi, and continued to form secondary hyphae at 72 hpi (Figure 7). In contrast, at 72 hpi, conidia in the single‐transformed cotyledons formed tertiary hyphae, while in untransformed plants, conidia initiated chains of conidiophores. These results indicated that CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 positively regulate the inhibitory effect of the CmSGT1 gene on the growth of P. xanthii by inducing its promoter activity.

Discussion

Pumpkin, a Cucurbitaceae vegetable that holds significant importance worldwide, is frequently affected by PM infection, which brings severe losses to its production and quality, especially under high temperatures. At present, the identification of disease‐resistant genes in pumpkins is largely unknown. Increasing evidence shows that SGT1, as a core immune regulator, confers a certain level of resistance to plants against diseases caused by bacteria, fungi, and other pathogens (Wang et al. 2022; Wang, Xiao, et al. 2023). TaSGT1 is a positive regulator for wheat resistance to stripe rust (Shu et al. 2025). Overexpression of CmSGT1 in tobacco significantly improved the resistance to E. cichoracearum (Guo et al. 2019), and transient overexpression of CmSGT1 in pumpkin plants also significantly inhibited the spore germination and mycelia growth of P. xanthii, indicating that the CmSGT1 gene has broad‐spectrum resistance to PM.

SA plays a central role in plant disease resistance against biotrophic pathogens. SGT1 signaling activation demands its phosphorylation by SA‐induced protein kinase, a MAPK activated upon pathogen attack, conferring effective resistance to tobacco mosaic virus (Hoser et al. 2013). In this study, transient CmSGT1‐overexpressed pumpkin significantly increased SA content. Moreover, exogenous SA pretreatment significantly inhibited the growth of P. xanthii in the transient CmSGT1 transformants. Therefore, we inferred that CmSGT1 overexpression in pumpkin enhances the resistance to PM by inducing an increase in SA content and subsequently activating the expression of the SA signaling related pathogen‐related genes (PR1a and PR5) (Guo et al. 2019). Although many studies have shown that the SGT1 gene performs many biological functions by interacting with proteins during plant disease resistance, there are no reports concerning the function of this promoter in response to stress.

Researchers have identified plant promoters associated with stress and hormones under disease stress. Yu et al. (2023) found that the CEBiP promoter was involved in regulating barley resistance to PM. The CmLOX08 promoter in oriental melon was induced in response to ABA, SA, H2O2, and salt treatment (Wang et al. 2019). In this study, the activity of the CmSGT1 promoter was induced after PXI and various plant hormones (GA3, Eth, SA, ABA, and MeJA), which may be related to identified pathogen defense‐associated and hormone‐associated motifs present in the CmSGT1 promoter sequence. In addition, the expression of the CmSGT1 gene in the resistant “03112‐2” was significantly induced by PXI and MeJA treatment, while inhibited by ABA treatment (Guo et al. 2019). This response may be attributable to positive and negative regulatory factors that affect the expression of CmSGT1 under MeJA and ABA treatments. Our results also coincide with the previous reports regarding the promoter of the GmTIP1;6 (Feng et al. 2022) and LmSAP gene (Ben et al. 2020): GUS activity of which is enhanced by ABA, MeJA, and GA treatments. Generally, the identification of function and interacting proteins in this promoter region highlights a potentially important role for CmSGT1 in responses to PM stress.

WRKY TFs can bind to the W‐box element of downstream gene promoters and participate in plant disease resistance processes (Choi et al. 2015; Liu et al. 2021; Yu et al. 2023). The conserved response of plants to pathogenic infection is probably mediated by the binding of the double W‐box elements by CaWRKY40 (Liu et al. 2021). Yu et al. (2023) found that HvWRKY2 can regulate barley resistance against PM by directly binding to the W‐box element of the CEBiP promoter region. Herein, the Y1H assay demonstrated that CmWRKY21, CmWRKY31, and CmWRKY75 bind to the CmSGT1 promoter, respectively, with CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 positively regulating gene expression. The binding site of these three CmWRKY TFs may be related to the W‐box element in the CmSGT1 promoter sequence, which reportedly is the binding site of WRKY TFs (Eulgem et al. 2000). In addition, there may be interactions between multiple WRKYs, such as NaWRKY3 and NaWRKY6 in Nicotiana attenuata , which can directly bind to the promoter region of the downstream gene (Yin et al. 2021). However, whether CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 directly bind to the W‐box element of the CmSGT1 promoter needs to be verified by EMSA assay.

WRKY TFs, as a key regulatory factor, participate in the resistance response induced by plant pathogens (Javed and Gao 2023; Wang, Chen, et al. 2023). Overexpression of CsWRKY50 in transgenic cucumber improved resistance to downy mildew (Luan et al. 2019). VqWRKY33 regulated VqNSTS3 expression by binding to the W‐box element of the promoter and enhanced resistance to PM in grapevine (Liu et al. 2023). Herein, the expression of CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 was significantly induced by PXI, and transient CmSGT1 and CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31 cooverexpression intensified the inhibitory effect of the CmSGT1 gene on the growth of P. xanthii hyphae. We concluded that CmWRKY21 and CmWRKY31 positively regulate the CmSGT1 gene expression by binding its promoter and thereby enhancing the resistance to P. xanthii in pumpkin. In addition, there are some reports that WRKY plays a positive regulatory role in SA accumulation, and CmWRKY42 directly activated the transcription of CmICS to enhance SA accumulation and improve the resistance of PM in oriental melon leaves (Wang, Wu, et al. 2023). In apples, upregulation of MdWRKY15 enhanced SA accumulation, thereby improving the resistance to Botryosphaeria dothidea (Zhao et al. 2020). However, the regulatory effect of CmWRKY21/31 and CmSGT1 binding in SA accumulation needs further investigation.

Conclusions

In summary, transient CmSGT1 overexpression in pumpkin cotyledons improved the inhibitory effect on the growth of P. xanthii, possibly by inducing SA content increase. Additionally, the CmSGT1 promoter was induced by PM and hormone signaling molecules, and identification of its interacting proteins CmWRKY21/31 confers high‐level gene expression in pumpkin. Based on our findings, we propose a model for the regulatory function of CmSGT1 in response to PM stress (Figure 8). Under PM stress, CmSGT1 activates SA accumulation, resulting in upregulated expression of SA signal‐related genes, such as PR1a and PR5, thereby inhibiting the growth of P. xanthii. The CmWRKY21/31 triggers CmSGT1 expression by binding its promoter. Upregulation of the CmWRKY21/CmWRKY31‐CmSGT1 module promotes resistance to P. xanthii in pumpkin.
