Correlation analysis of the urban community environment and health promotion among adults aged ≥ 55 years: the mediating role of physical activity
Authors: Yuting Xu, Chuntian Pan, Hongjun Yu, Bing Zhan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPhysical health (PH), including muscle strength, endurance, and the ability to perform daily physical tasks, gradually declines with increasing age, leading to disability and an increased disease burden. Both the urban community environment (UCE) and physical activity (PA) were linked to PH.MethodsA total of 625 participants aged 55 years and older from Haidian and Chaoyang Districts in Beijing, China, were included in the study from September to December 2023.PH was assessed by a combined score of four tests, including BMI, chronic disease, depression and self-rated health.The Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale-Abbreviated (NEWS-A), Center for Epidemiological Survey-Depression Scale (CES-D), Physical Activity Scale for Elderly (PASE) and Social Support Rate Score (SSRS) were collected. The analysis was carried out with SPSS v.20 and Amos 24, and the results were validated via structural equation modeling (SEM).ResultsThe urban community environment was positively associated with the health level of older adults. Specifically, the standardized path coefficients indicate that the influence of the built environment (0.72–0.88) was greater than that of the social environment (0.43–0.55) and personal attributes (0.22–0.37) on older adults’ health. Physical activity demonstrated both a mediating effect and an indirect effect, highlighting its significant value as a mediating variable.ConclusionsThe urban community environment has a positive impact on promoting the physical health of older adults, with the influence of the built environment being greater than that of the social environment and personal attributes on their physical health. Physical activity acts as a mediator between the urban community environment and the physical health of older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20303-4.

Introduction

In the 21st century, the process of global population aging has significantly accelerated [1]. It is projected that the population of older adults aged 60 and above will increase from 723 million in 2022 to more than 2 billion by 2050. Concurrently, insufficient physical activity will become a more prominent disease risk factor among the global older adult population. With the shift in population development patterns, aging has become a fundamental national condition in China, making older adults’ health a primary concern. In 1986, the WHO proposed that health promotion is the process of enabling people to increase control over and improve their health. To promote independent living for older adults in communities and mitigate the adverse effects of declining capacity on their health, the WHO launched the Global Age-friendly Cities (AFC) project in 2006. The core of this project is to develop and maintain functional capabilities through older adults’ friendly environments. The “person-environment fit” is a crucial driver for realizing the potential of older adults [2]. In 2016, the Central Committee of the Communist Party of China and the State Council issued the “Healthy China 2030” planning outline, which proposed the construction of a healthy environment. Subsequently, the “Sports Health Action Plan” introduced the concept of “Active Health,” emphasizing proactive health interventions driven by sports and creating a socioecological system [3]. The social ecological model of health promotion emphasizes the complex network of interactions between the physical environment, the social environment, and personal factors [4]. According to the Social Ecological Model of Health Promotion [5], an individual’s health and behavior are influenced by multiple levels of factors, including personal characteristics, the physical environment, the social environment, and broader community and policy environments. The ‘Person-Environment Fit’ theory further emphasizes that health outcomes depend not only on personal attributes but also on the extent to which these attributes align with the living environment, environmental factors [6] are upstream influences that significantly contribute to the physical health of older adults in urban communities.In this study, we apply this theoretical framework to analyze the influence of urban community environments, physical environments, social environments, and personal attributes on the physical health of older adults.

The “environment-behavior-health” relationship is linked through a series of feedback loops [7], explaining the interaction mechanisms between health elements and the environment mediated by human behavior [8]. In terms of the health of older adults, it is essential to understand the determinants that influence their high satisfaction with their health status [9]. participation in physical activities can prevent the decline in functional abilities [10] and reduce the risk of dementia [11], social isolation [12], and loneliness [13]. Physical activity has been repeatedly shown to effectively promote health [14–16], positively impacting various dimensions of well-being [17], including self-efficacy, self-esteem, positive emotions, and mental health. Maintaining a positive and optimistic attitude is fundamental to successful aging [18]. Chinese people are proven to be collectivists [19], Sports activities are an essential means of socialization [20], providing space for older adults to experience physical and social benefits [21]. Social support increases access to physical activity in older adults [22, 23]. The value of walkability, public transportation, and service accessibility in health promotion has become increasingly evident [24–28], and facilities within walking distance have significantly greater levels of physical activity than those who need to drive everywhere [29, 30]. The promulgation of the “Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Older Adults” in 2015 established the legal status of building an age-friendly environment. The “Medium- and Long-term Plan for the Prevention and Treatment of Chronic Diseases in China (2017–2025)” emphasized controlling chronic disease risk factors and building health-supportive environments. In December 2020, the State Council issued the “Opinions on Promoting the Healthy Development of Older Adult Care Services,” emphasizing optimizing home and community services and strengthening the construction of liveable environments, fully utilizing the role of communities from a top-level design perspective. However, most policies are independently formulated and implemented by single departments, and coordinated policies and safeguards for jointly formulating and implementing community health promotion strategies across multiple departments are lacking [31, 32]. With the interaction of multifactor complex networks, a single-factor model centered on individuals is insufficient to explain the high prevalence of physical inactivity among older adults.

As a cross-sectional study, the primary goal of this research is to analyze the relationship between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health, demonstrating the mediating role of physical activity between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health. Therefore, this study introduces physical activity into the analytical framework, aiming to comprehensively understand the pathways between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health, focusing on the following questions: (1) Does the urban community environment positively affect older adults’ physical health? (2) Does physical activity mediate this relationship? (3) If there is a mediating effect, how does physical activity function in this relationship? To answer these research questions, this study employs structural equation modeling (SEM) and bootstrap mediation analysis, selecting Chaoyang and Haidian Districts in Beijing as survey areas and analyzing the questionnaire results of community residents aged 55 and above. Promoting the construction of age-friendly communities has received strong governmental support, and enhancing the age-friendliness of urban community environments is one of the most critical planning measures [33]. This study aims to provide evidence-based suggestions for improving the age-friendliness of urban community environments and promoting older adults’ participation in physical activities within communities.

Materials and methods

Research participants

Our study recruited a sample of community-dwelling older adult residents aged ≥ 55 years from the Haidian and Chaoyang Districts in Beijing, China. A standardized questionnaire was used to collect information on respondents’ demographic background, socioeconomic status, health conditions, and family characteristics. In brief, the survey used a random sampling method, with the following criteria for selecting survey subjects: (1) aged 55 years and above; (2) able to live independently, with the ability to socialize and travel independently, excluding those with cognitive impairments; and (3) living in the selected community for at least six months. The survey period was from September to December 2023, with a total of 710 questionnaires recovered. The study was approved by the Academic Ethics Committee of Capital University of Physical Education and Sports. All participants provided informed consent before participating.

Assessment of urban community environment

This section mainly includes evaluations of personal attributes, social support, and the built environment. When selecting the dimensions for evaluating urban community environments, we considered key factors closely related to the physical health of older adults. According to existing literature, the physical environment (such as walkability, green spaces, and public facilities within a community) has been shown to play a significant role in promoting physical activity and reducing the risk of chronic diseases [34]. The social environment (including social support and neighborhood relationships) affects the mental health and social participation of older adults. Personal attributes (such as education level and type of residence) were included in the model to control for potential confounding effects on health outcomes and to explore their interactions with environmental factors [35]. The dimensions and measurement tools selected for this study (such as NEWS-A, CES-D, PASE, SSRS) are widely used and validated in related fields, offering high reliability and validity.

Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale (NEWS)

The Chinese Urban Community Resident Walkability Scale, revised based on the abbreviated version of the Neighborhood Environment Walkability Scale, includes five dimensions with a total of 18 items: convenience of supporting living facilities, road conditions, beautification, traffic conditions, and security. Each item is scored on a 1–5 scale, with 1 representing strongly agree, 2 representing agree, 3 representing neutral, 4 representing disagree, and 5 representing strongly disagree. The total score of the scale represents the perceived level of satisfaction with the neighborhood environment.

Social Support Rate Score (SSRS)

The Social Support Rate Score [36, 37] consists of ten items, with a total score of 66 points, indicating the sum of the scores for the ten items. The higher the score is, the better the social support. The scores are categorized into levels: a total score of 45–66 indicates a high level of social support, 23–44 indicates a medium level, and ≤ 22 indicates a low level.

Assessment of physical activity

A standardized self-report questionnaire was used to assess PA for all participants. The Physical Activity Scale for the Elderly (PASE), developed in 1993, includes 10 items and 26 questions covering leisure physical activity, household physical activity, and work-related physical activity. The questionnaire assesses physical activity over the past 7 days, calculating final physical activity scores through different test item weights and scores. It can be used to assess the physical activity levels of younger older adults, with results indicating that the highest PASE scores are observed in individuals aged 50–64 and older adults aged 65 and above [38, 39].The total PASE score ranges from 0 to 400, and the reliability and validity of the modified PASE questionnaire have been verified [40].

Assessment of physical health

This section focuses on the health status of older adults, including BMI, type of chronic disease, depression level, and self-rated health. BMI was calculated by dividing the participant’s weight (KG) by the square of their height (M). Chronic disease status, including hypertension, diabetes, stroke, arthritis, chronic lung diseases (e.g., chronic bronchitis or emphysema), cardiovascular diseases, memory-related diseases (e.g., Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease), and heart diseases (e.g., myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure), is self-reported. The revised 9-item Center for Epidemiological Survey-Depression Scale (CES-D) [41] measures older adults’ depression. The scale includes 9 items, with items 1, 4, and 9 being reverse scored. Scores range from 0 to 18, with higher scores indicating more severe depression symptoms. Depression levels were categorized as 0–9 for no depressive tendency, 10–16 for depressive tendency, and 17 or more for high-risk depression. The five-point scale assesses the following question: “How do you rate your health status?” for statistical evaluation.

Assessment of covariates

We collected demographic information, sports participation behavior, and health status information using a standard self-management questionnaire. We considered the following variables as potential confounding covariates: age (years), sex (male or female), height, and weight. All potential confounding covariates were collected during the survey.

Statistical analysis

First, descriptive analysis methods were used to understand the current state of older adults’ physical health, physical activity, and urban community environments, such as satisfaction with the built environment and utilization of social support. Second, two structural equation models (SEM) were used to estimate the relationships among older adults’ physical health, physical activity, and urban community environments and to identify the specific pathways through which urban community environments affect older adults’ physical health. Each observed variable is supported by 5 to 20 samples [42, 43], which are recognized and accepted. This study’s model includes four scales with 63 items, meaning that a sample size of over 300 is needed. A final sample of 625 was obtained through random stratified sampling in Beijing, making this method suitable for the study.

Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) (Table 1) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) were used to test the reliability and validity of the questionnaire. Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was calculated to assess the reliability of the 63 items, yielding a value of 0.857, which exceeds the commonly accepted threshold of 0.7, thereby indicating strong reliability. For validity measurement, the KMO and Bartlett tests were employed, resulting in a KMO value of 0.938, surpassing the threshold of 0.9, which demonstrates good validity and significance.The CFA fit indicators (Table 2) were used to evaluate the measurement quality of the latent structure. The modified fit indicators all met the excellent standards, and the model was acceptable.

Results

Descriptive statistics

This study collected 710 questionnaires. After excluding 85 invalid questionnaires—16 from respondents under 55 years old, 68 from those not residing in Chaoyang and Haidian Districts in Beijing, and 1 with significant missing data—the final effective sample size was 625, resulting in an efficiency rate of 88.03% Table 4. Participants’ characteristics according to BEIJING (N = 625)] shows the descriptive characteristics of the participants included in this study. A total of 625 participants were included. The mean (SD) age was 65.8 years (6.2 years), with 501 (80.16%) being female and 124 (19.84%) male. The greater proportion of females is possibly due to the earlier retirement age of women (55 years) compared to men (60 years) for this cohort born in the 1950s and 1960s. Participants’ satisfaction with the built environment had a mean (SD) of 4.09 (0.74), covering five dimensions: destination accessibility (mean ± SD: 4.13 ± 0.79), activity environment and facility safety (4.19 ± 0.79), aesthetic quality of the activity environment (4.09 ± 0.85), residential suitability (3.98 ± 0.86), and traffic accessibility (4.03 ± 0.79). Among the 625 participants, weekly physical activity intensity was categorized as sedentary for 25 participants (4.00%), low intensity for 230 participants (36.80%), and moderate to high intensity for 370 participants (59.20%). Overall, physical activity intensity was mainly low and moderate to high.

Interaction effect of UCE and PA on PH

All direct effects in Model 1 were significant. From the direct influence perspective, the urban community environment positively promoted physical activity (β = 0.472, C.R.=12.663, P < 0.001); physical activity (β = 0.802, C.R.=30.903, P < 0.001) and the urban community environment (β = 0.219, C.R.=8.385, P < 0.001) had positive direct effects on older adults’ physical health.

Mediating effects of PA and pH on the UCE

The bootstrap mediation effect analysis method was used to test whether there was a mediating effect in Model 2. The direct effect (urban community environment - older adults’ physical health) (β = 0.197, P = 0.001, 95% CI [0.137, 0.256], 36.75%), indirect effect (urban community environment - physical activity - older adults’ physical health) (β = 0.339, P = 0.001, 95% CI [0.284, 0.385], 63.25%), and total effect (β = 0.536, P = 0.001, 95% CI [0.435, 0.625]) indicated that physical activity has a significant partial mediating effect between the urban community environment and older adults’ physical health.

Joint mediating effects of UCE and PA on PH

Further mediation effect analysis using the bootstrap method was conducted to evaluate specific indicators and proportions. In the mediation model, direct effects, indirect effects, and total effects are present. If the 95% confidence interval for the indirect effect does not include 0, an indirect effect is present. If the 95% confidence interval for the direct effect does not include 0, a direct effect is present. (1) If both direct and indirect effects are present, the model is a partial mediation model. (2) If the direct effect is present and the indirect effect is absent, it is a nonmediation model. (3) If an indirect effect is present and a direct effect is absent, the model is a full mediation model.

After validation, there were both partial and full mediations between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health levels (Table 5). Objective support plays a full mediating role, while residential type, educational attainment, subjective support, and the number of transportation stations play partial mediating roles. These factors can promote health without necessarily affecting physical activity participation. Based on the direct promotion of physical activity and indirect promotion of health, the ranking of these factors was objective support > educational attainment > number of transportation stations > residential type > subjective support.

Discussion

This study aimed to exam the relationship between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health in Beijing. It is hypothesized that urban community environments directly or indirectly affect older adults’ physical health levels.Structural equation modeling (SEM), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and mediation analysis are used. The results indicate that physical activity has a positive effect on the physical health level of older adults and serves as a buffer mediator for the urban community environment and elderly physical health.

Direct positive correlations between urban community environment and older adults’ physical activity (β = 0.802, C.R.=30.903, P < 0.001) and health status (β = 0.219, C.R.=8.385, P < 0.001) can be seen from multiple perspectives [46, 47]. For older adults’ physical health, the built environment is the most critical environmental dimension (contribution rates of 0.72–0.88) [48], with walkability and facility convenience being important predictors of older adults’ physical activity [49, 50]. This may be because accessibility to essential resources and destinations meets the basic survival needs for health among older adults [51]. Destination accessibility is a core component of the built environment [52] and is directly positively correlated with older adults’ physical health. Destination accessibility measures the ease of reaching destinations and obtaining services for community residents [53]. Higher accessibility increases the likelihood of accessing health resources and the convenience of reaching nearby destinations, meeting daily needs for older adults [54].

Traffic accessibility ranks second in importance, with efficient public transport and nearby services crucial for maintaining older adults’ activity levels [55]. Retired individuals spend more time at home and in their communities, where a walkable environment significantly influences physical activity. Additionally, those in well-connected areas manage daily life more independently. Enhancing community connectivity and public transport access encourages outdoor activities, promoting better health. This is supported by the partial mediation pathway identified in this study: “transportation stations - physical activity - health.”

Residential suitability received the lowest satisfaction rating in the community built environment, likely due to China’s rapid urbanization, which has transformed housing systems since the 1998 reforms, leading to high residential density in Beijing. Despite efforts like the 2014 and 2015 barrier-free renovation initiatives, older communities still lack adequate public spaces and facilities. Enhancing accessibility with outdoor elevators, public spaces, and well-maintained facilities is vital for promoting older adults’ independence, mobility, and social participation.

In summary, perceived satisfaction with the built environment reflects to some extent the needs of residents [56]. Older people tend to be more willing to be physically active when they live in neighborhoods with more supportive built environments [57, 58]. The Finnish Ministry of Social Affairs and Health, in its national strategy “In Action - Physical Activity for Health and Well-being 2020” (2013), states that a more conducive environment should be created for physical activity [59].In the national strategy “In Action - For Physical Activity for Health and Well-being 2020” (2013), the Ministry of Social Affairs and Health of Finland suggested that a positive environment should be created that is more conducive to being physically active and that walking and cycling should become more popular modes of transportation. From the Chinese perspective, the finding that lounge seating in the community prolongs sedentary behavior among older adults and is instead positively associated with health contradicts the findings of some studies in European countries [60]. Overseas studies have concluded that prolonged sedentary behavior leads to social isolation [61] and a decrease in the amount of time spent participating in sports, which is detrimental to health. In the Chinese context, chess and card activities are the preferred leisure and recreation programs for elderly people, and senior activity centers and fixed board facilities are more popular, which is conducive to helping elderly people engage in more social activities [62].

The social environment significantly influences older adults’ health, with social support mediating physical activity’s impact: objective support (full mediation) > subjective support (partial mediation, 36.07%). Enhanced social support fosters stronger community ties [63], reducing loneliness and isolation [64].Additionally, higher social support levels among older adults provide a sense of security during neighborhood walks [65]. Community fitness teams and organizations enhance community cohesion and strengthen positive social norms for health behaviors [66]. Australia offers community-based group exercise programs to boost older adults’ physical activity and social engagement [67].Expanding and restructuring social support networks, mobilizing community resources, and enhancing peer networks significantly improve older adults’ physical health [68].

Compared to the built and social environments, personal attributes have a smaller impact on older adults’ health. Higher education correlates with better health, likely due to enhanced psychological resources [69] and greater activity levels [70].Older adults living alone have poorer health and higher depression levels than those living with family, likely due to reduced social support and care. Higher family income positively correlates with better health, providing financial security for healthcare and reducing anxiety linked to aging-related burdens [71].

This study confirms that physical activity serves as a key mediator between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health (β = 0.339, P = 0.001, 95% CI [0.284, 0.385]), with mediation accounting for 63.25% of the total effect. This indicates that tailoring environments to meet older adults’ needs can significantly boost physical activity levels, independent of their physical capabilities [72].

“Healthy Japan 21 (Phase II) (2013–2022),” “Healthy People 2010” and “Healthy People 2020” in the United States, and Denmark’s “KRAM Guidelines” and “Good, Better, Best” all focus on health promotion by improving access to physical activity. These policies emphasize creating healthy exercise environments that enhance destination accessibility and traffic convenience, thereby increasing walking time and extending the duration of physical activity [73].High accessibility encourages older adults to walk more for both transportation and recreation, which is vital for disease prevention, maintaining independence, and enhancing their quality of life [74]. Community facilities, space, peer support, and motivation enhance older adults’ activity beliefs [75], encouraging outdoor engagement and improving health and functional status [76].Promoting active environments and daily physical activity enhances older adults’ health by encouraging participation and yielding significant health benefits.

This study advises policymakers and urban planners to prioritize enhancing built environments, developing social support networks, and implementing integrated health strategies. These efforts promote active lifestyles, support community renovations, and fulfill accessibility and activity needs for the elderly, serving as a model for urban development.

This study has several key strengths. First, it offers a comprehensive perspective by integrating psychological, physiological, and social aspects of older adults’ health. Second, it examines urban community environments across personal, social, and physical dimensions, deepening the understanding of their impact on health. Third, it confirms the mediating role of physical activity between urban environments and health, providing valuable evidence from China and enriching the literature on physical activity’s mediating effects.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, health is also influenced by genetic factors and dietary habits, which were not considered. Second, the data were collected through a cross-sectional survey, relying on self-reported physical activity over the past week, leading to potential subjective bias. Third, the broad scope of the questionnaire resulted in a smaller sample size, limiting generalizability. Future research will expand to include Shenzhen and Shanghai, increasing the sample size and forming a multiregional study linking Beijing, Shanghai, and Shenzhen to improve reliability.

Conclusions

This study revealed the PA levels and health status of older adults (55 years and older) in Beijing. Empirical analysis based on multilevel goals of personal, social, and physical environments shows that (1) urban community environments positively promote older adults’ physical health. (2) Built environment > social environment > personal attributes affect older adults’ physical health. (3) Physical activity mediates the relationship between urban community environments and older adults’ physical health levels. The findings of this study can help policymakers and urban planners clarify the relationships among environment, behavior, and health; support community executives in building social support networks among individuals, families, and neighbors; and optimize built environments to improve the age-friendliness of community environments.

The future research value of this study lies in exploring multidimensional health promotion strategies, personalized age-friendly environments, precision health interventions, and cross-cultural comparative studies. These directions will not only deepen our understanding of the relationship between environment and health but also provide more precise theoretical and empirical support for urban planning and public health policy-making.
