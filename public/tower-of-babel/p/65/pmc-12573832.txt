Anterior Stafne bone cavity in mandibular canine-premolar region: a case report
Authors: Ritika Jindal, Vimal Arora
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStafne bone cavity is a rare, asymptomatic, nonprogressive, developmental pseudocyst typically found incidentally in the posterior mandible. Its occurrence in the anterior mandibular region, especially in the canine-premolar area, is extremely rare and often misdiagnosed as periapical pathology due to its radiographic appearance. This case is unique as it highlights an anterior lingual variant of Stafne bone cavity presenting as a periapical radiolucency, underlining the importance of advanced imaging and clinical correlation in avoiding unnecessary endodontic or surgical interventions.Case presentationA 79-year-old female patient of Indian ethnicity presented for routine dental evaluation. A panoramic radiograph revealed a well-defined, oval radiolucency in the periapical region of mandibular right canine and first premolar (#43, 44). Clinical examination showed vital teeth, absence of symptoms, and no mucosal or bony abnormalities. Cone bean computed tomography revealed a corticated, scooped-out bony defect on the lingual surface of the mandible in the region of the lesion, consistent with anterior variant of Stafne bone cavity. No invasive procedure was warranted, and the patient was placed under periodic follow-up.ConclusionAnterior Stafne bone cavity is a rare entity that can mimic pathological periapical lesions, posing a diagnostic challenge. Radiographic features supported by clinical assessment, including vitality testing and advanced imaging, are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and overtreatment. This case underscores the significance of considering Stafne bone cavity in differential diagnoses of asymptomatic periapical radiolucencies in elderly patients.Keywords: Anerior Stafne bone cavity, Anterior mandibular radiolucency, Mandibular bone depression, Anterior mandibular bone defect

Introduction

Stafne bone cavity is an idiopathic developmental pseudocyst often discovered as incidental findings on dental radiographs. The lesion typically presents as a well-defined radiolucency in mandibular posterior region with rare incidences recorded in anterior canine/premolar region. Most lesions are asymptomatic and nonprogressive. This idiopathic bony lesion was first described by Stafne, who reported 35 such cases [1].

These defects often present as well-defined round-to-ovoid radiolucent depressions located on lingual side of mandible below the inferior alveolar canal. Histologically, it is considered to be an entity that arises because of inclusion of salivary gland tissue. These are idiopathic bone cavities that do not require treatment.

Stafne bone cavity is known by many terms, such as static bone defect, Stafne cyst, static bone cyst, latent bone cyst, Stafne cavity, lingual mandibular bone depression, idiopathic bone cavity, mandibular embryonic defect, aberrant salivary gland defect, developmental bone defect of mandible, lingual cortical mandibular bone defect, and submandibular salivary gland inclusion. It is thought to be developmental in origin but it does not present since birth. The reported cases showed a male dominance and mostly occurring after 40 years of age [2, 3]. Histologically, it is considered to be a pseudocyst, formed by invagination in medial surface of mandible.

The scientific literature of reported cases of Stafne bone cavity states four variants—posterior lingual, anterior lingual, buccal aspect of ascending ramus, and lingual aspect of ascending ramus. The most common variant is posterior lingual variant. The anterior lingual variant is very rare, often situated between mandibular incisor and premolar area above the insertion of mylohyoid muscle. The other two variants are very unusual. The anterior variant of the lesion was first reported by Richard and Ziskind in 1957 and there are very few reported cases in literature [1, 4, 5].

This case report is the presentation of a case of anterior lingual variant of Stafne bone cavity with periapical presence with respect to apices of canine-premolar tooth, which can be provisionally misinterpreted as periapical cyst.

Case report

A 79-year-old female patient of Indian ethnicity came for dental treatment. Since there was generalized attrition and decayed teeth, a panoramic radiograph was advised. Panoramic radiograph of the patient revealed a well-defined radiolucency in periapical region of #43, 44. The lesion was roughly oval in shape with approximately dimension of 10 × 8 mm (Fig. 1). The radiographic findings suggested radicular cyst in relation to #43, 44. Patient was asymptomatic with the lesion being an incidental finding. On clinical examination, teeth associated were vital and asymptomatic. The overlying mucosa of the lesion was normal without any signs of inflammation or bony expansion. To explore the extent of the lesion cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) of the mandible was advised.

Panoramic radiograph with well-defined radiolucent lesion irt #43–44 region

CBCT finding revealed a well-defined bony defect on lingual side of #43, 44 with scooped out appearance of lingual cortical plate. Defect was well defined and corticated. Bone surrounding the defect had normal trabecular pattern. There was very thin residual buccal bone present in relation to the defect and the apex of canine (#43) was just at the periphery of the defect with intact lamina dura (Figs. 2, 3). CBCT mandible of the patient revealed anterior division of inferior alveolar canal bilaterally. The accessory canal on right side was very close to the periphery of the defect in the canine region. A typical scooped out corticated lingual mandibular bone defect led to a diagnosis of anterior variant of Stafne bone defect. Since patient was asymptomatic and defect was accidental discovery, no further investigations/histopathology were considered for a definitive diagnosis. Mostly the lesion does not require any intervention, thus patient was kept on follow-up (Figs. 2, 3, 4, 5).

Discussion

Stafne bone cavity is a rare incidental finding with very few reported cases. The posterior lingual variant of the defect is most common, with reported incidence of 0.009–0.300% for anterior lingual variant in literature. The bone cavity is three times more common in male individuals than female individuals. The highest incidence occurs in fifth and seventh decades of life. In anterior variant, most cases occur in canine-premolar region (65%), fewer in incisor (24%), and around 11% might affect jaw bilaterally [5, 6].

The developmental hypothesis involving pressure from developing salivary gland tissue is most accepted. As these defects are not present at birth and usually arise later in life, etiology is considered to be constant pressure atrophy from developing salivary gland tissue. Pressure from submandibular gland directly related with posterior variant and from sublingual gland is related to anterior variant, from parotid gland related with two variants of the ascending ramus of mandible. The histopathological aspiration of the content of bone defect mostly contains salivary gland tissue but may be empty or contain adipose tissue, connective tissue, muscles, blood vessels, and rarely, lymph nodes. The defect usually does not require any treatment except in cases where pathology is unusually severe [2, 5, 7].

The typical presentation of well-defined corticated radiolucency in relation to roots of teeth on routine dental radiographs may lead to an incorrect diagnosis. The conditions that should be considered under differential diagnosis of this type of presentation include early stage odontogenic and non-odontogenic cysts and tumors such as periapical cyst, lateral periodontal cyst, and keratocystic odontogenic tumor. Even the presentation may mimic a radiographic artifact, which can be eliminated by tube shift technique. To exclude periapical cyst from differentials and to eliminate unnecessary endodontic procedure, vitality of teeth should be performed, and status of lamina dura should be checked. A residual cyst may mimic Stafne bone cavity (SBC) in edentulous region. Unlike SBC, residual cyst tends to expand, causing bone resorption, root resorption of adjacent teeth, and displacement of teeth. Keratocystic odontogenic tumor (KCOT) tends to occur commonly in molar regions. They tend to grow aggressively and cause root resorption and bone resorption. KCOT mostly reveal cystic fluid on aspiration. Solid tumors are hard to differentiate from SBC as these lesions do not contain fluid on aspiration. Early unicystic ameloblastomas may mimic SBC, but ameloblastoma has tendency to cause tooth displacement and expansion of jaws [8].

The anterior variant of the lesion is the diagnostic challenge. The absence of guiding diagnostic hints as to the relation with inferior alveolar canal or with teeth leads to misdiagnosis as other pathologies. If bone cavity is related to root apices, as in the present case, it can mimic inflammatory cyst, or more often, radicular cyst. Presence of tooth decay, vitality of teeth, and changes in lamina dura can give clinical cues to reach diagnosis [9].

Usually the diagnosis is established on the basis of clinical presentation and radiographic findings. Three-dimensional CT scan or CBCT gives a clear picture of the lingual bone defect with a well-defined corticated bony depression on lingual side of mandible. To investigate the content of the defect magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) can be done. CT with sialography is useful to investigate connections with major salivary glands, but it is very difficult to selectively contrast sublingual glands to examine associated anterior defects; in these cases CT sialography is not useful.

No intervention or treatment is indicated for the Stafne bone cavity except in atypical cases [1]. In a case reported by Taysi et al., the SBC presented as well-defined radiolucency in canine-premolar region. CBCT finding revealed erosion of lingual cortex and expansion of buccal cortex with very thin buccal bone left intact. Because of unusual presentation of lesion, biopsy of lesion was done, which revealed salivary glandular tissue [1]. SBC are usually asymptomatic. If the lesion is associated with pain or paresthesia, biopsy or surgical exploration may be required to rule out neoplastic pathology or chronic sialdenitis. Periodic clinical and radiological monitoring is usually preferred in asymptomatic cases [5, 6].

Conclusion

Not all periapical radiolucencies are pathological in nature. Status of lamina dura, vitality testing of teeth, and clinical signs and symptoms are the adjuvant diagnostic parameters required to arrive at a conclusion. Since Stafne bone cavity is an incidental finding on the radiograph and is a mostly self-limiting or regressive entity, it does not require any treatment. Radiograph along with clinical correlation is sufficient alone for diagnosis. CT scan or MRI of the region can be considered to explore content of the cavity. Regular follow-up with radiographic monitoring is recommended.
