Whole-Exome Sequencing Improves Risk Assessments of Adult Moyamoya Disease
Authors: Eun Pyo Hong, Eun Jin Ha, Dong Hyuk Youn, Yuwhan Chung, Kang Min Kim, Sung Ho Lee, Won-Sang Cho, Hyun-Seung Kang, Jin Pyeong Jeon, Jeong Eun Kim, on behalf of The First Korean Stroke Genetics Association Research (The FirstKSGAR) Study
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and PurposeWhole-exome sequencing (WES) is a valuable tool for identifying causative mutations in adult moyamoya disease (MMD), thereby advancing our understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying this condition. Here, we conducted the first WES-based association study aimed at identifying genetic modifiers implicated in MMD.MethodsThis WES study involved 160 patients with MMD and 189 controls from a multicenter hospital-based biobank, and evaluated combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) scores. Mutant-allele frequencies were compared in 369,121 individuals derived from the UK Biobank (UKB) WES. Mutant-allele risk scores (MARSs) were created based on WES-identified mutations. Gene-based association analyses and pooled analyses in East-Asian populations were further performed.ResultsFourteen mutations reached the genome-wide significance criterion (p<5×10-8), among which the p.R4810K mutation in the ring finger protein 213 gene (RNF213) showed the strongest significance (odds ratio=117.4, p=8.54×10-24). Notably, two mutations—p.G576S (alpha-glucosidase [GAA]) and p.D54N (charged multivesicular body protein 6 [CHMP6])—exhibited high CADD scores of 32 and 25, respectively, whereas the RNF213 p.R4810K mutation demonstrated a moderate deleteriousness score of 10.63. Fourteen mutations exhibited significant differences in allele frequencies between patients and UKB controlled data (p<1×10-8). The MARS9 model (incorporating nine missense mutations) showed better predictability for MMD (90.89%). The analysis of gene-based associations revealed four candidate genes: GAA, RNF213, CHMP6, and CARD14 (p=5×10-19 to 4×10-7). The subsequent pooled analyses validated four mutations in East Asian populations: p.V1195M, p.D1331G, p.S2334N, and p.R4810K (p<3×10-8).ConclusionsThis pioneering study has corroborated the significance of p.R4810K and identified several causative mutations predisposing patients to MMD, which helps to improve the understanding of its polygenetic nature.Keywords: 17q25.3, moyamoya disease, mutant-allele risk score, whole-exome sequencing

INTRODUCTION

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic rare pathology of the cerebral arteries characterized by progressive steno-occlusive changes in the distal internal carotid or proximal middle cerebral arteries, and its cause remains unknown.1 The incidence rate of MMD is markedly higher in Japanese, Korean, and Chinese populations than in European and North American populations.2 Mutations in the ring finger protein 213 gene (RNF213) are broadly recognized as significant contributors to the development of MMD, particularly in East Asian populations.3 Comprehensive meta-analyses have demonstrated that the RNF213 rs112735431 and rs148731719 alleles play crucial roles in MMD development, whereas the TIMP-2 rs8179090, MMP-2 rs243865, and MMP-3 rs3025058 alleles exhibit inverse relationships with MMD.4 A recent genome-wide association study (GWAS) augmented by a fine-mapping process confirmed the presence of RNF213 mutations in adult MMD patients in the Korean population and found that additional candidate loci within the 17q25.3 region, such as TBC1D16, CCDC40, GAA, and ENDOV, indirectly contributed to MMD susceptibility.5 Most previous microarray-based studies of MMD have explored the associations of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) with disease phenotypes across the human genome.

Following GWAS, whole-exome sequencing (WES) offers another perspective on MMD pathogenesis by identifying functional mutations and allied mechanisms.6,7 Wiedmann et al.7 identified a novel potential AGXT2 locus associated with MMD in Northern European populations that is implicated in the regulation of nitric oxide metabolism. Nevertheless, patient cohorts have generally been smaller in WES studies than in GWASs, with the former also not including separate phases for discovery and validation, nor considering the age of MMD patients. To address these gaps, we undertook the first WES-based association study to identify and confirm coding modifiers in adult MMD patients through a multicenter hospital-based patient–control investigation. We further contrasted mutant-allele frequencies between Asian- and European-ancestry populations using 454,787 WES entries from the UK Biobank (UKB).8

METHODS

Study populations and UKB resources

This research included a cohort of adult patients diagnosed with MMD or undergoing revascularization procedures, as well as a control group devoid of MMD evidence from radiological assessments across multiple institutions.5 The study initially enrolled 105 MMD patients and 115 controls from December 2021 to April 2022, and included a further 55 MMD patients and 74 controls from November 2022 to May 2023. The definition of adult MMD patients was based on the chronological age at diagnosis (18 years or older) and the presence of singular or bilateral angiographic manifestations of MMD vasculopathy, while excluding cases of intracranial irradiation, fibromuscular dysplasia, Marfan syndrome, tuberous sclerosis, or polycystic kidney disease.9,10 Control subjects were selected from individuals undergoing routine medical or headache evaluations who had received magnetic resonance angiography or computed tomography angiography, to ensure the exclusion of silent steno-occlusive disease. To minimize bias from subclinical cerebrovascular abnormalities, those with a history of ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, intracranial aneurysm, vascular malformations including MMD, intracranial arterial narrowing, or systemic vasculopathies (e.g., peripheral, renal, or pulmonary artery stenosis) were excluded, as implemented by The First Korean Stroke Genetics Association Research (FirstKSGAR) consortium.11 The analyzed UKB WES dataset comprised 3 MMD patients and 369,118 individuals without cerebrovascular disease.8

Clinical and radiological data for the patients were meticulously reviewed by three medical doctors, focusing on sex, age at diagnosis, hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, smoking history, initial symptoms, surgical revascularization interventions, clinical outcomes, and angiographic progression. All of the methodologies conformed with the applicable guidelines and regulations, and all processes were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study including all protocols and the obtaining of informed consent from patients was approved by the institutional review boards and ethics committees of Hallym University (HIRB-2022-042-CR), Hallym University Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital (2019-06-006), and Seoul National University Hospital (1806-105-951). Access to all UKB data was acquired following project approval (application number 80385).

WES

Genomic DNA (gDNA) at a concentration of 100 ng/µL was extracted from the peripheral blood of the study participants. The PicoGreen assay and agarose gel electrophoresis were employed to assess the quantity and quality of gDNA, respectively, which subsequently underwent a washing and amplification process.5 The final purified product was quantified using quantitative polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in accordance with the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA Library Quantification Kits for Illumina Sequencing platforms), and its quality was ascertained using the TapeStation DNA Screentape D1000 assay (Agilent Technologies). Sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina), employing paired-end reads and achieving a coverage depth of 300×. The base calling files, which are expressed in binary, were converted to FASTQ format using the Illumina bcl2fastq package (version 2.20.0). The demultiplexing option (–barcode-mismatches) was set to a value of 0.

Read alignment, base recalibration, and variant calling

A comprehensive overview of the methodologies is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 (in the online-only Data Supplement). With the exception of the UKB WES dataset, which was provided in the PLINK binary format, paired-end sequences in FASTQ format were subjected to a quality assessment, filtering out those with a quality score and read length of <20 and <70, respectively, using the NGSQCToolkit (version 2.3, https://github.com/mjain-lab/NGSQCToolkit).12 Following this initial quality control step, the sequences were mapped and aligned utilizing the BWA-MEM (build 37) program against the hg19 reference genome.13 Sequence Alignment Map (SAM) files generated from this process were subsequently transformed into Binary Alignment Map (BAM) files and organized by chromosomes using the SAMtools program (version 1.9),14 which facilitated the evaluation of the alignment’s read conversion rate. PCR duplicates were excised when preparing the aligned BAM files for variant calling, and the files underwent local realignment and a base recalibration processes using the Picard MarkDuplicates program (version 2.22.4; Broad Institute, http://picard.sourceforge.net/) and the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK; version 4.1, Broad Institute, http://www.broadinstitute.org/gatk), respectively.

A single-sample genomic variant call file (gVCF) was produced for each sample using the HaplotypeCaller program in the “-ERC GVCF” mode.15 A joint genotyping analysis was then conducted to merge all gVCF files using the GATK GenotypeGVCFs tool. To ensure variant quality, SNVs and short insertions/deletions were filtered using thresholds for the variant quality scores of 99.5 and 95.0, respectively.

All of the aforementioned procedures adhered to the guidelines recommended in the GATK Best Practice (https://gatk.broadinstitute.org/hc/en-us/articles/360035535932-Germline-short-variant-discovery-SNPs-Indels-).

Variant annotation and quality controls

The ANNOVAR program (https://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/en/latest/)16 was used to meticulously annotate 244,623 sites within exome regions. The functional impact of the identified mutations was predicted by using a combined annotation-dependent depletion (CADD) tool (https://cadd.gs.washington.edu/), with a Phred-scaled CADD score cutoff of 20 used to identify potentially pathogenic variants.17 The refinement process that yielded 70,333 sites for use in the current WES included stringent quality control measures, including the enforcement of a genotyping call rate of ≥95%, the exclusion of monomorphic sites, and the maintenance of Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium with p values ≥1×10-6 in the control group. All participants underwent rigorous checks to ascertain their relatedness to one another using the PLINK program (version 1.9) with the “--genome” flag (https://www.cog-genomics.org/plink/1.9/)18 to calculate the statistics of identical-by-descent pairs with a genetic relatedness metric (PI_HAT). The derived genetic relatedness values ranged from 0 to 0.0489 among the adult MMD patients and from 0 to 0.177 among the control participants, confirming that none of the individuals involved were related.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as numbers and percentages for discrete variables and as mean±standard deviation values for continuous variables. All summary statistics were computed using STATA software (version 17.0, Stata Corporation). A whole-exome-wide association study (WEWAS) was performed, assuming an additive inheritance model after adjusting for age, sex, and four genetic ancestry values identified in a principal-components analysis (PCA) using PLINK (version 1.9).18 Furthermore, the pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) and associated r2 values were calculated using PLINK (version 1.9) with the “--r square” option18 for MMD-associated mutations, and displayed as an LD-based heat map. A conditional analysis was performed to assess whether the mutations overlapped statistically. PCA values were transformed into four multidimensional scales and calculated in the WEWAS with shared variants in the 1000 Genome Reference Panel Phase 3 (ftp://ftp.1000genomes.ebi.ac.uk/vol1/ftp/release). The genomic inflation factor (GIF) was derived using PLINK (version 1.9) with the “--assoc” and “--adjust” options.18 Manhattan, quantile–quantile, and regional association plots were generated using the “qqman” package in R software (version 3.6.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and LocusZoom (version 1.3, http://locuszoom.org/),19 with modified scripts written in the Python (version 2.7.5, Phython Software Foundation) and R (version 3.6.2) languages. We subsequently calculated mutant-allele frequencies in the UKB WES dataset after dividing the data into Asian- and European-ancestry populations according to the “genetic ethnic grouping” variable in the UKB, and compared them with the Korean population.

In addition, mutant-allele risk scores (MARSs) were calculated by weighting each mutation with its beta coefficient and multiplying by the number of risk alleles (i.e., 0, 1, or 2) to estimate the diagnostic probability of MMD. The predictive accuracy for an MMD diagnosis was assessed by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). Given the absence of independent validation datasets, we conducted a limited fivefold cross-validation of both the MARS14 and MARS9 models (encompassing all 14 and 9 mutations, respectively) involving the present study cohort using the “CVAUROC” (cross-validated AUROC) package in STATA (version 17.0, Stata Corporation). A MARS-based examination correlated with MMD susceptibility was performed using multivariate logistic regression models.

A gene-based association assessment was performed using the Sequence Kernel Association Test (version 2.2.5) in R software with the “SKAT_CommonRare” function, which was designed to appraise the collective impact of rare and standard variants (http://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/SKAT).20 Variants with a minor-allele frequency of <0.01 were classified as rare, with the remainder considered common. The p values for each gene were derived using the bootstrap method to perform resampling 10,000 times, thus computing Q statistics that reflect cumulative chi-square distributions.

A pooled analysis of RNF213 mutations identified in the exome sequencing was performed using the inverse variance method (https://genomics.ut.ee/en/tools),21 integrating our results and those of Moteki et al.22 Cumulative estimations were generated for fixed- and random-effect models. The Cochran Q test was used to determine I2 statistics, and the q value was used to assess the degree of genetic heterogeneity.

RESULTS

Clinical characteristics of the study populations

This study applied a WEWAS to participants from the multicenter hospital-based biobank and expanded the comparisons to include other WES data from the UKB. Fig. 1 shows the study methodologies and baseline characteristics for each group of participants in detail, which are documented in Table 1. The study applied a multicenter hospital-based patient–control methodology to 160 patients with MMD and 189 controls. The MMD patients comprised more females than males, and the mean age at diagnosis differed between the MMD patients and controls (p=0.0032). All variables except the incidence of hypertension differed significantly between the patient and control groups (p=2.8×10-4 to 0.05) (Supplementary Table 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). Routine cerebral angiography was conducted for 138 patients (86.3%) with a follow-up period of 61.4±40.3 months, during which angiographic progression was exhibited by 10 patients. The 160 patients with MMD comprised 13 (8.1%) who were asymptomatic, 92 (57.5%) who experienced transient ischemic attacks, 25 (15.6%) who had cerebral infarctions, and 30 (18.8%) who had hemorrhagic strokes (Table 1). The UKB WES dataset of 9,643 Asian and 359,478 European individuals were analyzed (Table 1), including 9,641 Asians and 359,477 Europeans without cerebrovascular disease, along with 2 Asian-ancestry and 1 European-ancestry patient.

WES and comparison of mutant-allele frequencies in moyamoya disease

The clustering of genetic ethnicity between the first and second principal components was nearly identical among 349 Korean and 504 East Asian subjects, additionally confirming the absence of heterogeneity between the inner populations in our study (Supplementary Fig. 2 in the online-only Data Supplement). The 70,333 coding variants were categorized into 32,455 nonsynonymous SNVs, 30,683 synonymous SNVs, 133 frameshift insertions, 260 nonframeshift insertions, 242 frameshift deletions, 454 nonframeshift deletions, 360 stop-gain SNVs, 30 stop-loss SNVs, 250 splicing sites, 4,382 ncRNA SNVs, and 1,084 sites of unknown function in the current WEWAS. There were 14 mutations within the 17q25.3 region—including in GAA, RNF213, and charged multivesicular body protein 6 (CHMP6)—that surpassed both the genome-wide significance criterion (p<5×10-8) and the false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted significance criterion (<0.001) in the 160 patients with MMD and the 189 controls (Fig. 2A), revealing a GIF (λ) of 0.9528 that indicated a minimal substantial population stratification or systematic bias (Fig. 2B). The minor allele “A” in p.R4810K (the RNF213 rs112735431 allele) exhibited the strongest effect size and the most-significant association with MMD development (p=8.54×10-24, odds ratio [OR]=117.40, 95% confidence interval [CI]=46.39–297.30) (Fig. 2C). Table 2 presents detailed information on the significant genome-wide associations. The p.G576S, p.E689K, and p.T711T mutations in GAA as well as that of p.D54N in CHMP6 significantly increased the risk of MMD (OR=3.79, 2.78, 2.72, and 31.53, respectively). In contrast with the p.R4810K mutation in RNF213, the minor alleles of other mutations in RNF213 were protective for the development of MMD. The three mutations of p.T1070T, p.Q1133K, and p.A1550A demonstrated significantly suggestive associations with MMD development (p=9.83×10-6, 6.40×10-6, and 5.58×10-8, respectively), all of which achieved an FDR-adjusted significance of <0.05 (Supplementary Table 2 in the online-only Data Supplement).

The RNF213 p.R4810K mutation exhibited residual LD with other mutations in GAA, RNF213, and CHMP6 (r2=0.14–0.31), but showed minimal LD (r2<0.1) with six additional RNF213 mutations (p.V1195M, p.E1272Q, p.D1331G, p.V1340V, p.P2415P, and p.S2334N), suggesting that these mutations are independent of p.R4810K (Supplementary Fig. 3 in the online-only Data Supplement). Despite the presence of residual or weak LD relationships between p.R4810K and other mutations, conditional analyses did not reveal any additional genome-wide or suggestive associations after adjusting for p.R4810K, indicating overlapping genetic effects (Supplementary Fig. 4 in the online-only Data Supplement). Nevertheless, nine RNF213 mutations retained significant associations following the conditional adjustment (p=4×10-4 to 0.02) (Table 2). An additional subgroup analysis of whole-exome variants comparing patients with hemorrhagic (n=30) versus ischemic (n=117) manifestations (after excluding 13 asymptomatic individuals) did not identify any significant or suggestive genome-wide associations (Supplementary Fig. 5 in the online-only Data Supplement).

In silico functional annotation further revealed that the GAA p.G576S mutation (rs1800307) exhibited the highest predicted deleteriousness (CADD Phred-scaled score=32), followed by the CHMP6 p.D54N (rs572398921, score=25) and GAA p.E689K (rs1800309, score=17.52) mutations (Table 2), indicating that these have potential pathogenic relevance to MMD development. In comparison, the RNF213 p.R4810K mutation demonstrated a moderate deleteriousness score (10.63), whereas nine additional RNF213 mutations showed lower predicted pathogenicity (Phred-scaled score=0.014–6.311). However, given that CADD scores can yield false-positive results and provide only weak biological evidence, further functional validation is required to elucidate their mechanistic contribution to MMD pathogenesis.

Supplementary Table 3 (in the online-only Data Supplement) compares the mutant-allele frequencies of the 14 WEWAS-identified mutations between the 160 patients with MMD and the 189 controls, as well as with those in the UKB WES dataset. Because only three MMD patients were identified in the UKB WES, allele frequencies were instead compared against the control populations stratified by ancestry. In the control group, the effect-allele frequency (EAF) of p.G576S (GAA) was higher in Koreans (16.4%) than in East Asians (4.49%) and Europeans (0.03%). Notably, two mutations—p.R4810K (RNF213) and p.D54N (CHMP6)—were absent in European-ancestry populations but present among Korean- and East-Asian-ancestry individuals in the UKB (EAF=0.03%–1.59%). These two mutations were particularly common among Korean MMD patients, being detected in 126 carriers for p.R4810K and 52 carriers for p.D54N.

Assessment of the integrated MARSs in moyamoya disease

The use of the distributions of summed MARSs per individual to predict the development of MMD was based on WEWAS-identified mutations within the 17q25.3 region (Fig. 3A). Individual weighted values and corresponding AUROCs for the identified mutations are provided in Supplementary Table 4 (in the online-only Data Supplement). Among the 14 mutations, which comprised 5 silent and 9 missense mutations, RNF213 p.R4810K exhibited the highest AUROC of 0.8781 (95% CI=0.8439–0.9122). Two additional mutations (GAA p.G576S and RNF213 p.E3490E) had AUROCs of approximately 0.7. The combined predictive power using the MARS14 model (encompassing all 14 mutations) yielded an AUROC of 0.8747 (95% CI=0.8387–0.9108) in all 160 MMD patients and 189 controls (Fig. 3B).

With its exclusion of the five silent mutations, the MARS9 model demonstrated a better predictive performance for MMD (AUROC=0.9089, 95% CI=0.8772–0.9407) than did the MARS14 model. Moreover, the MARS9 model showed a significantly stronger association with MMD development (OR=1.55, 95% CI=1.41–1.69, p=3.6×10-20) than did the MARS14 model (OR=1.28, 95% CI=1.22–1.35, p=3.7×10-20). The fivefold cross-validation showed a consistently relevant predictive accuracy for both the MARS14 and MARS9 models, with mean AUROCs of 0.8772 and 0.9115, respectively, and bootstrap-bias-corrected 95% CIs of 0.8241–0.9058 and 0.8588–0.9362 (Table 3). The RNF213 p.R4810K mutation exerted a substantially greater effect than the other mutations in both polygenic MARS models, which suggests that while polygenic contributions and residual shared effects may underlie MMD pathogenesis, the p.R4810K mutation remains the principal genetic determinant of MMD susceptibility.

Gene-based association study in moyamoya disease

A comprehensive analysis of 14,225 genes was conducted to explore gene-based associations with MMD using a WEWAS approach (Fig. 4). Four genes—GAA, RNF213, CHMP6, and CARD14—were intimately linked to MMD susceptibility (p=5×10-19 to 5×10-7) (Fig. 4). RNF213, with 28 common and 12 rare mutations, showed the most-significant association with the development of MMD. CARD14 was not significant at the level of single mutations (10 common mutations, p=1×10-5 to 0.6), but showed a suggestive association after pooling the mutation effects (p=3.23×10-7). CHMP6 showed dramatically improved significance after integrating the effects in three mutations (p=6.9×10-18), whereas RNF213 and GAA were underpowered relative to the single-mutation level.

Pooled analysis of RNF213 in East Asian populations

An inverse variance meta-analysis was applied to WES datasets for 263 MMD patients and 473 controls, including the current WEWAS data (160 MMD patients and 189 controls) and a Japanese cohort (103 MMD patients and 184 controls)22 (Supplementary Table 5 in the online-only Data Supplement). This showed that 4 specific mutations in RNF213 (p.V1195M, p.D1331G, p.S2334N, and p.R4810K) out of 11 mutations exhibited strong associations with MMD, without significant heterogeneity across both fixed- and random-effect models (p=1×10-38 to 3×10-8) (Supplementary Table 6 in the online-only Data Supplement). Notably, the p.R4810K mutation was identified as having the strongest association with MMD risk (OR=119.35, 95% CI=57.93–245.87, p=2.05×10-38). Furthermore, another mutation, p.Q1133K, was statistically significant in pooled analyses in both fixed- and random-effect models (p=8.91×10-8 and 8.60×10-7, respectively), signifying greater significance than in the individual studies involving Japanese and Korean populations (p=0.0023 and 6.40×10-6, respectively).

DISCUSSION

This was the first WES-based association study involving Korean adults with MMD, and comparing its findings with UKB WES data reaffirmed the importance of RNF213 mutations. Our across-ethnicity analysis using the UKB dataset provided additional context for the allele frequency spectrum of the 14 mutations identified in our Korean cohort. However, the identification of only three MMD patients in the UKB dataset made it difficult to interpret the ethnicity comparisons, and so we confined our analyses of allele frequencies to control populations stratified by ancestry. The observed differences among the Korean, East-Asian, and European groups should therefore be considered as preliminary and hypothesis-generating findings rather than conclusive. Future large-scale sequencing efforts that include ethnically diverse MMD cohorts will be essential to identify potential population-specific effects and to validate such findings. Additionally, potential mutations in GAA and CHMP6 were identified that had relatively weak LD relationships with RNF213. Intriguingly, WEWAS-identified mutations within the 17q25.3 region (i.e., those included in the MARS9 model) demonstrated AUROCs ranging from 0.8296 to 0.8380 for predicting the development of MMD.

No mutations were found to be significantly associated with angiographic progression despite MMD patients with progression carrying at least five missense mutations in RNF213. Angiographic progression is a clinically relevant aspect of MMD and was defined based on changes in the Suzuki stage on follow-up digital subtraction angiography. The analyses accounted for all pertinent clinical subgroups, including ischemic, hemorrhagic, asymptomatic/nonvascular symptomatic onset, and surgical versus nonsurgical patients. The absence of significant associations is likely attributable to the small number of patients (n=10) with serial angiographic data reducing the statistical power. Additional subgroup analyses comparing hemorrhagic and ischemic manifestations also did not reveal any significant genetic differences. These findings highlight the need for larger longitudinal cohorts to clarify the genetic determinants of angiographic progression, assess their clinical relevance across MMD subtypes, and elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying disease evolution.

The RNF213 mutations within the 17q25.3 region, notably the p.R4810K (rs112735431) mutation that has been associated with MMD risk, have been acknowledged as a potential risk factor for MMD.23,24,25 Wang et al.25 reported that carrying the “A” homozygous mutation in p.R4810K was closely associated with the early onset of MMD and posterior cerebral artery involvement. The p.R4810K mutation in RNF213—which encodes a RING finger and two AAA+ domains—is less common in Caucasian than East Asian MMD patients, suggesting a diverse genetic predisposition based on ethnicity.26 A WES analysis of Northern European adults with MMD revealed de novo mutations affecting nitric oxide metabolism, but not the p.R4810K mutation.7 Our study found that 14 mutations within the 17q25.3 region—including 9 missense functions in p.G576S, p.E689K, p.K1034M, p.V1195M, p.E1272Q, p.D1331G, p.S2334N, p.R4810K, and p.D54N—exhibited significant genome-wide associations with adult MMD in Koreans. Three missense mutations—p.G576S, p.E689K, and p.D54N—were predicted to have a potentially deleterious impact on MMD pathogenesis, showing CADD scores higher than 17. These findings suggest that MMD is markedly influenced by a polygenic effect, and not by a single mutational effect such as p.R4810K. Although our analysis validated certain mutations (e.g., p.R4810K), further studies are necessary to understand the structural role and overall impact of other functional mutations in RNF213.

In addition to RNF213, our research also identified significant mutations in two other loci—GAA and CHMP6—as additional major contributors to MMD. The missense mutations p.G576S and p.E689K in GAA, which were identified for the first time in the current WEWAS, play crucial roles in producing the acid α-glucosidase enzyme, which is pivotal for converting glycogen polymers to glucose in lysosomes. The absence or a deficiency of GAA products causes glycogen accumulation and subsequent cellular damage.27 Jia et al.28 reported that heterozygous mutations in GAA were related to cerebral inflammation in patients with Pompe disease and glycogen storage disease type II. However, few studies have investigated how GAA affects MMD. Glycogen accumulation caused by GAA deficiency leads to abnormal lysosomes, which impair autophagy.29 Although the relationship between RNF213 mutations and autophagic dysfunction is known, autophagic inhibition has been shown to impair endothelial function in human endothelial cells under oxygen–glucose deprivation conditions.30 Youn et al.31 also reported that autophagic and mitophagic dysfunction in cerebrospinal fluid cells may affect the neurological outcomes in adult MMD patients. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that dysfunction due to GAA mutations damages the cerebral endothelium and causes arterial abnormalities via autophagic impairment, resulting in an abnormal vascular network.

CHMP6 is responsible for encoding a chromatin-modifying protein and charged multivesicular body protein. Overexpression of the CHMP6–GFP (green fluorescent protein) fusion protein leads to the accumulation of transferrin receptors in the cytoplasm that play a crucial role in regulating the cellular iron supply by binding to transferrin.32 Furthermore, elevated levels of ubiquitinated membrane proteins, including the epidermal growth factor receptor, can be observed in cells expressing CHMP6–GFP. An increase in ubiquitinated proteins during endocytosis can damage vascular smooth-muscle cells (VSMCs). Dziewulska and Rafalowska33 suggested that aberrant ubiquitin-dependent endocytosis of the Notch 3 ligand is involved in the VSMC damage seen in patients with cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy. Additionally, RNF213 contains a unique class of E3 ubiquitin ligases, and pathogenic mutations in this gene that cluster within the composite E3 domain could lead to abnormal ubiquitination interactions with substrates.34 However, the role of CHMP6 mutations in the development of MMD has not been determined, necessitating further in vivo and in vitro research to elucidate the pathogenesis of CHMP6.

Our polygenic MARS models—MARS14 and MARS9—have provided valuable insights into the cumulative genetic risk of MMD, achieving a diagnostic accuracy exceeding 80%. The incorporation of nine missense mutations in the MARS9 model emphasizes the polygenic and complex nature of MMD, contrasting with a simple Mendelian disorder. Notably, the RNF213 p.R4810K mutation exerted a substantially larger effect than the other mutations, confirming its role as the principal genetic determinant of MMD susceptibility. Although the inclusion of additional mutations in the MARS9 model modestly improved the predictive performance, the presence of residual overlapping effects and potential redundancy among mutations warrants cautious interpretation. Given that AUROCs were derived from internal fivefold cross-validation, these findings remain exploratory. Polygenic risk assessments may nevertheless represent a powerful tool for facilitating early disease detection and timely intervention, despite inherent challenges such as reduced effectiveness in the general population and reliance on statistical algorithms or prior knowledge.35,36,37 Once the functional effects of key mutations are validated in independent cohorts, polygenic risk models could provide cost- and time-efficient alternatives to unnecessary screening for MMD. Furthermore, the identification of functional mutations can avoid the use of unnecessary genotyping tests.38 A reliable MARS-based prescreening panel could also mitigate potential adverse effects associated with unnecessary screening, radiation exposure, overdiagnosis, and neurodegenerative stress following symptom onset, thereby supporting more-precise and safer clinical decision-making.39

Our study had several limitations. First, the relatively smallness of the datasets may have hindered the ability to draw definitive conclusions, although it should be remembered that this study performed the largest WES-based investigation of MMD to date, and encompassed discovery analysis as well as comparisons with publicly available genetic datasets. Given that MMD is a rare cerebrovascular disorder, assembling a sufficiently powered cohort with comprehensive genomic and clinical data presents substantial logistical and practical challenges. These limitations underscore the need for future multicenter collaborations involving larger longitudinal cohorts to validate the present findings and further elucidate the genetic architecture of MMD. Second, while we identified several mutations in RNF213, GAA, and CHMP6 localized at 17q25.3, unlike the point mutations p.R4810K (RNF213) and p.D54K (CHMP6), mutations with strong LD relationships are thought to merely influence gene expression rather than also definitively demonstrating the induction of structural dysfunction. While several variants—including those in GAA and CHMP6—emerged as potential contributors to MMD susceptibility, their functional impact remains hypothetical, since our current interpretations relied primarily on in silico CADD scores. In vitro and in vivo validation processes will be necessary to confirm their biological relevance. Nevertheless, these findings provide a valuable framework for exploring plausible mechanisms underlying MMD pathogenesis and can be used to prioritize the candidate genes to be investigated in future experimental studies. Third, we have proposed Korean-specific MARS models (MARS14 and MARS9) for predicting the MMD risk, and so their broader application to clinical practice and general cerebrovascular populations requires validation across larger and multiethnicity cohorts. Differences in allele frequencies and genetic architectures between East Asian and European ancestries must also be considered. Notably, performing functional investigations at the molecular level will increase the reliability of the nine mutations in the missense mutant-based MARS9 model, which could then be manufactured using the MMD-diagnosed panel for performing genetic testing. Fourth, heterogeneity in our results could have arisen because gDNA was extracted from peripheral blood containing various circulating cell types. Immune-related cells such as T cells, endothelial cells, smooth muscle cells, and dendritic cells are known to be involved in the inflammation, atherosclerosis, and vascular remodeling observed in MMD patients, ultimately inducing endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress that contribute to the formation of abnormal vessels.40 Fifth, our study integrated diverse bioinformatic and genomic approaches to identify genetic modifiers that influence MMD in multifactorial contexts. Although such a multilayered strategy may introduce analytical complexity, it provides a comprehensive framework for understanding the genetic architecture and molecular pathogenesis of MMD. Sixth, the lack of mutations associated with angiographic progression may reflect the small number of patients with longitudinal imaging data, and so future large-scale studies incorporating angiographic follow-up are necessary.

Our ongoing and next-phase efforts include 1) assessments of allele-specific and total transcript levels of genes harboring candidate variants using RNA-seq in peripheral blood and, where available, vascular tissues, 2) integration of variant calls with expression quantitative trait loci data from blood and publicly available vascular datasets to determine whether the identified variants modulate gene expression, and 3) implementation of an independent WES-based replication study involving an expanded MMD cohort to validate the identified associations and increase the model generalizability. These complementary studies will provide molecular-level insights into the regulatory mechanisms underlying non-RNF213 variants and strengthen the biological plausibility of the present findings. Furthermore, cell-type-specific investigations that include in T-cells and endothelial cells will be pursued to elucidate immunovascular interactions that contribute to MMD susceptibility.

In conclusion, this WES-based study has identified coding modifiers and mutations associated with adult MMD, and offered valuable insights into its genetic underpinnings. The best-fitting MARS approach proposed herein holds promise in improving the ability to diagnose MMD and understand the potential for disease development. Further in-depth investigations are needed to confirm our findings, to evaluate gene–gene and gene–environment interactions, and to determine the clinical relevance of genetic mechanisms to understanding MMD etiology.
