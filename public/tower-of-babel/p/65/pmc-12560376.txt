Telerehabilitation in cystic fibrosis: a randomized clinical trial protocol for increasing exercise loads and difficulty levels (TeleCist)
Authors: Ítalo Ribeiro Paula, Carlos Fernando Ronchi, Vivian Mara Gonçalves Oliveira Azevedo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with cystic fibrosis (CF) often face respiratory and gastrointestinal impairments, leading to muscle loss and altered exercise responses. Despite exercise recommendations, this population still lacks structured protocols with progressive load increments, making training adaptation challenging. In this context, telerehabilitation emerges as a promising alternative for remote supervision, improving adherence, and enabling personalized adjustments. Thus, this study aims to develop and test an exercise protocol for progressive load increment and increased exercise difficulty in individuals with CF through telerehabilitation. MethodsThis prospective clinical study will compare two randomized groups: a control group receiving general CF outpatient guidance and an experimental group following a structured exercise protocol with progressive load increments. Exercises will be performed for 40 min/day, three times/week for 12 weeks. Primary outcomes include distance covered (6-Minute Walk Test), muscle strength (Handgrip Strength Test), pulmonary function, fatigue levels (FACIT-Fatigue Questionnaire), and quality of life. Inflammatory biomarkers will be quantified from saliva and peripheral blood samples. All outcomes will be assessed before and after the protocol. Participants will receive initial instructions and instructional videos via a messaging app, which will also transmit vital signs and physical effort data. Biweekly online meetings will monitor exercise execution and guide load progression. DiscussionPhysical exercise promotes airway clearance, improves pulmonary and functional capacity, and enhances the quality of life. With respect to patients with CF, little is known about exercise recommendations and how exercise and load progression should proceed, especially through telerehabilitation. This study provided data about exercise guidance provided remotely (telerehabilitation) to the study participants.Trial registrationBrazilian Registry of Clinical Trials (REBEC; U1111-1277–5378). Registered on November 9, 2022.

Introduction

Background and rationale

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is an autosomal recessive disease caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane regulator (CFTR) gene located on the long arm of chromosome 7. This gene encodes the CFTR transmembrane conductance regulator protein, which acts as a chloride channel on the apical membranes of secretory epithelial cells. It plays a crucial role in maintaining electrolyte balance in the respiratory and gastrointestinal tracts, liver, reproductive system, and salivary, sweat, and pancreatic glands [1, 2].

Currently, more than 90,000 diagnoses for CF are made worldwide each year, and the life expectancy of those born with CF now reaches the fifth decade of life [3]. In the European Union, 1 in every 2000 to 3000 live births is diagnosed with CF. In the USA, this number is 1 in every 3500 births [4]. According to the Brazilian CF Registry, the southeastern region of Brazil has the highest number of individuals with CF (2692), followed by the southern, northeastern, midwestern, and northern regions, with a total of 5773 Brazilian patients with the disease in 2019, the year of the last survey [5].

In CF, due to CFTR mutations, sodium absorption through the plasma membrane is increased, resulting in thicker and more viscous mucus. Consequently, impaired ciliary clearance occurs, facilitating bacterial colonization [6]. The accumulation and increased viscosity of mucus in the airways can lead to bronchial obstruction, infection, and respiratory failure. Furthermore, in the gastrointestinal system, excessive mucus leads to the insufficient production of digestive enzymes and consequently reduces nutrient absorption. These effects contribute to complications, such as protein malnutrition and muscle mass loss, that can affect the exercise response in these individuals [3, 7].

Physical exercise offers various health benefits to individuals with CF, including improved lung function, functional capacity, quality of life, and reduced hospitalizations [8]. Despite these benefits, there is significant variability in the assessment instruments used to prescribe appropriate types of exercise for individuals with CF. Curran et al. analyzed several studies related to physical exercise and CF and found that aerobic exercise and guidance are highly valuable, with most studies showing short- to long-term improvements in the health of individuals with CF [9].

Innovative and safe home-based monitoring programs have emerged to supervise individuals with CF and improve their health. For example, a supervised physical activity program was conducted in a bicentric study involving eight individuals from Ireland and France. In this study, adults with CF engaged in 8 weeks of exercise, including two 60-min sessions per week of resistance and aerobic exercises. They experienced positive effects on muscle fitness and increased physical activity levels without any adverse effects from the teleguided exercise program [10].

Monitoring tools have also emerged. An Australian study evaluated teleguided exercise using a digital platform with online access. The goal was to guide activities in terms of exercise quantity and intensity based on the objectives and preferences of children and young adults with CF during their initial platform access. Additionally, it aimed to provide feedback on the effects of exercise by monitoring via an exercise tracker that fed into the platform. Although this study aimed to evaluate the effects of exercise, it did not identify specific or standardized exercise protocols for all participants [11].

Consensus documents extensively explore exercise types and frequencies, often noting the absence of adverse effects from the prescribed exercises [12, 13]. However, the literature lacks a specific exercise protocol based on progressive exercise difficulty levels and load increments for this population. This study aims to fill this gap and provide valuable insights.

Groups of doctors, patients, and caregivers involved with CF have joined forces to create a document listing the top 10 research priorities for clinical trials, giving rise to the first James Lind Alliance (JLA) Priority Setting Partnership (PSP). This document highlights a recognition by the scientific community of the real-life conditions and health perceptions of patients with CF and their families. Central, prioritized themes should be researched to yield benefits for individuals with CF and society as a whole. The present study will address one of the highlighted themes in the form of a question that relates to motivating tele-guidance resources: “Can we support treatment motivation through a mobile phone application or by phone calls from the CF team when patient engagement wanes?” [13].

Telerehabilitation is a valuable alternative in contexts where in-person care is limited. While exercise guidelines exist for this population, this is the first study, to our knowledge, to assess load progression and increased exercise difficulty exclusively via telerehabilitation. A structured protocol may enhance the accessibility, standardization, and adherence to remote training in individuals with CF. Evaluating its effects will offer insights into functional, clinical, and quality-of-life outcomes, supporting its application in clinical practice.

Objectives

Therefore, recognizing telerehabilitation as a powerful tool for exercise supervision, this study aims to develop and test an exercise protocol for progressive load increment and increased exercise difficulty in individuals with CF through telerehabilitation.

The participant will be invited to participate in the study on the day of their already scheduled multidisciplinary consultation. If they agree, they will sign the Informed Consent Form (ICF). In the case of participants under 18 years old, the individual with CF will sign an Assent Form, and their legal guardian will sign the ICF. Trained professionals will assist participants throughout the process, and they may stop the test at any time.

The blood and salivary samples will be stored for a determined period only to ensure the quality of the analysis. If the analysis is successful, the material will be discarded. The greatest risks involving blood collection are the possibility of local pain, bruising, fainting, or infection. However, these risks are rare and temporary, and will still be performed by trained professionals to minimize any of them.

In the event of significant health-related issues such as worsening exercise tolerance, deterioration of health conditions, or reports of excessive joint fatigue or pain, the study will be suspended by the investigators. These data will be collected daily through interactions via the messaging application.

Confidentiality and data protection

All participant data, including identification and assessment results, will be securely stored in a password-protected database, accessible only to authorized researchers. Each participant will receive a unique identification code, replacing personal information in all analyses. The key linking identities to codes will be stored separately with restricted access.

Communication will occur via a secure messaging application, and all data handling will comply with confidentiality regulations. Upon study completion, identifiable data will be anonymized, and electronic records stored on restricted institutional servers for 5 years. If data sharing is required, only anonymized information will be provided.

Participants

This is a parallel-group, randomized clinical trial with a 1:1 allocation ratio, designed to assess the superiority of telerehabilitation over standard care in patients with CF. Two groups will participate in the study: 1—the control group will receive routine outpatient clinic guidance and a series of educational videos; 2—the experimental group will follow the proposed exercise protocol in addition to routine guidance.

Currently, 33 individuals aged 12 years or older are in the CF outpatient clinic of this hospital. Due to the limited number of eligible individuals, all individuals within this age range will be invited to participate in the study to ensure the representativeness of the target population. Participant allocation into groups will be conducted randomly to minimize selection bias.

Individuals with CF will be invited to participate, and eligible participants will be evaluated by trained researchers using the instruments highlighted below before and after the proposed protocol. This assessment will coincide with their regular follow-up appointments with the multidisciplinary team in the existing service. After the assessment, the participants will receive information from the researchers about the proposed execution of the Physical Training Protocol (Tables 1 and 2), the Load Increment Flowchart (Fig. 2), exercise execution using dumbbells and elastic bands provided or body weight, the monthly activity schedule, and clarifications regarding possible doubts. Given the standard patient follow-up practices in many centers, a control group receiving standard care was selected to reflect real-world clinical practice and allow for a more accurate assessment of the telerehabilitation program’s impact. In addition to standard care, this group will receive educational videos, specifically designed for individuals with CF, covering topics such as infection prevention through proper hygiene, nutritional guidance, mental health care, nebulizer and physiotherapy equipment maintenance, inhalation therapy, and respiratory physiotherapy.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

All individuals attending the CF Center at the Brazilian University Hospital, aged between 12 and 34 years, who agree to participate in the research, have availability for vital data monitoring and internet access via a cellphone or computer, and who do not have any cognitive and/or orthopedic impairments, or other comorbidities limiting physical activity or are pregnant individuals.

Participants in the intervention group who discontinue the prescribed exercises for two consecutive weeks will be withdrawn from the intervention phase but will remain in the final analysis following the intention-to-treat principle.

Randomization

Researchers have determined that the participants should be at least 12 years old due to telerehabilitation involving individuals with CF for better exercise execution. Additionally, participants’ family members will be asked to accompany them. The maximum age will correspond to the age of the oldest individual with CF in the outpatient clinic, approximately 30 years.

The allocation sequence will be generated in advance by an independent researcher using Microsoft Excel, with random numbers and permuted blocks of varying sizes to ensure both balance (1:1 ratio) and unpredictability of assignment. Participants will be enrolled sequentially, and group allocation will follow the concealed, pre-generated randomization list. A separate researcher, not involved in recruitment or intervention delivery, will perform the group assignments strictly according to this sequence, ensuring allocation concealment and methodological rigor.

Plan for data collection and retention

The initial and final assessments of participants will be conducted in person at the university’s CF outpatient clinic, while follow-up will be carried out exclusively through telerehabilitation. The total follow-up period will be 3 months.

The data collected will include baseline variables as well as the study’s primary and secondary outcomes, using validated instruments to assess muscle strength, lung function, physical activity level, fatigue, quality of life, and inflammatory biomarkers. Data collection will be conducted by trained evaluators to ensure standardization and reliability. When applicable, duplicate measurements will be performed to minimize errors and enhance the accuracy of the results.

As a nationally recognized CF outpatient clinic, participants receive their regular follow-up care at this facility and undergo periodic multidisciplinary monitoring. Therefore, our follow-up assessments will be conducted at this outpatient clinic. To promote adherence to the program, participants will be regularly contacted via a messaging application to reinforce the importance of follow-up and address any questions regarding the intervention. If a participant fails to perform the prescribed exercises for an extended period or deviates from the intervention protocol, they will be considered as having withdrawn from the intervention phase but will remain in the final analysis following the intention-to-treat principle.

This study will not be blinded to the assessor or researcher, as the assessment and exercise guidance will be provided on the same day as the individuals’ routine outpatient clinic appointments. Thus, the exercise group participants will be aware of their allocation as they will receive protocol execution instructions from the researcher on that day. However, both groups will receive the same routine guidance regarding health and quality of life from outpatient clinic professionals and motivational videos from the researcher through a mobile phone application.

The data analyst will be independent and blinded to the statistical analysis. It will receive data from the groups for analysis, but it will not know which group it belongs to. In this way, the analysis will be carried out by identifying Group 1 and Group 2 to block the interference from the analysis of results.

Outcomes

This study aims to develop and test an exercise protocol for progressive load increment and increased exercise difficulty in individuals with CF through telerehabilitation. The primary outcomes will be the distance covered (assessed by the 6-Minute Walk Test), muscle strength (evaluated using the Handgrip Strength Test), pulmonary function (assessed by spirometry), fatigue levels (assessed using the FACIT-Fatigue Questionnaire), and quality of life. Additionally, the inflammatory biomarker profile will be analyzed through the quantification of inflammatory mediators in peripheral blood samples and salivary. All test results will be compared before and after the exercise protocol.

The secondary outcomes will explore aspects related to the progression of load increments, identifying potential facilitators and barriers for these individuals, adherence frequency to the proposed exercises, and possible occurrences of exacerbations or hospitalizations throughout the intervention period. These additional analyses will provide further insights into the feasibility, adherence, and clinical impact of telerehabilitation in individuals with CF.

Assessment instruments

For both groups, functional assessment and collection of biological materials will take place at the beginning and end of the 12-week exercise protocol. The following tests will be conducted: 6-Minute Step Test [15], Handgrip Strength Test [16], pulmonary function assessment by spirometry [17], FACIT-Fatigue Questionnaire [18], and administration of a Quality-of-Life questionnaire [19].

Additionally, biomarkers present in saliva and blood will be evaluated. A total of 4 mL of blood will be collected from the antecubital vein of each individual and placed in ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid-coated tubes by a qualified phlebotomist using standardized venipuncture techniques. Based on a systematic review of blood biomarkers in CF pulmonary exacerbations, blood monitoring is relatively simple for patients of all ages and shows the potential to reflect inflammation status throughout the lungs in a systemic manner. Promising biomarkers include the C-reactive protein (the most widely studied biomarker with potential clinical utility), neutrophil elastase, IL-6, and myeloperoxidase [20]. Saliva will be collected in plastic tubes using the spitting method. The participants will rinse their mouths with water and swallow the remaining saliva in the oral cavity, waiting for 1 min before collection. Saliva can serve as an alternative source for biomarker analysis and has the potential to be used as a biological fluid for CF monitoring. It can be collected noninvasively and has a direct anatomical connection to the lower airways [21, 22].

Sociodemographic characteristics will be assessed using electronic medical records, including age, sex, socioeconomic status, and education level. Anthropometric characteristics will be evaluated through measurements of weight, height, and body mass index (BMI) [23]. The “International Physical Activity Questionnaire—IPAQ” will be used to assess the physical activity levels of the study participants [24]. Socioeconomic status will be analyzed using the Brazilian Economic Classification Criteria (CCEB) developed by the Brazilian Association of Research Companies (ABEP) [25] that provides an operational rule for household evaluation. Additionally, the modified Borg scale [26] will be used to guide exercise progression throughout the intervention.

Procedures

Physical training protocol

The experimental group will adhere to a physical training protocol that will be explained during the assessment and will be delivered through mobile devices over a 12-week period under the supervision of physiotherapists experienced in CF treatment. The physical training program will be conducted thrice a week, with supervision by the physiotherapist through recorded videos and accompanied by messaging through a cell phone application after each session that will last for 40 min. To promote program adherence, online follow-ups will be conducted every 2 weeks, and researchers will provide motivational health education videos through the phone application. The aim will be to facilitate dialogue, create room for reflection, and provide opportunities for participants to enhance their awareness and knowledge of their condition.

As outlined in Table 1 below, the physical training session will have an approximate duration of 40 min and will be divided into three phases: warm-up, resistance training, and cool-down. During the warm-up phase, breathing exercises will be performed for bronchial clearance, lung expansion, and ventilation optimization [27]. Upper and lower extremity exercises will also be included to mobilize joints, stimulate muscles, and promote cardiorespiratory warm-up [28].

The goal of resistance training is to offer a moderate level of effort during execution. Therefore, exercise intensity will be self-evaluated by the participants using the modified Borg scale [26] (scores from 0 to 10) to achieve a training intensity corresponding to a score of 4–7 (moderate to very strong sense of breathlessness during exercise). This scale will be scored for both respiratory and lower limb fatigue at the beginning, during, and after cool-down in each training session. The volume, intensity, and specific content of the protocol have been adapted from Hansen et al. [29] [29] and are in line with the Brazilian Recommendation for Physiotherapy in CF [30].

Each exercise set will be performed within a predetermined period of 20–40 s, depending on the functional capacity and motivation of the participant, while respecting the proposed protocol. The remainder of the interval will be 20–40 s. The exercise speed will be moderate to fast, with 1–2 s for both concentric and eccentric movements.

The initial 2 weeks will serve for familiarization, aiming for exercise adaptation, load adjustment, and optimization to prevent muscular overload injuries. As such, exercises for the upper and lower extremities will be performed without additional load beyond the body weight in the first session.

The flowchart for load progression overload incorporates one of the training principles for improving physical performance, aiming to disrupt cellular homeostasis and elicit the desired response to stress [31]. The expected inflammatory process is one that, when given sufficient rest, allows for regeneration of skeletal muscle tissue and subsequent activation of contractile and metabolic properties [32].

The variables typically used for assessing overload are load, duration, rest between stimuli, muscle action, movement execution speed, exercise frequency per week, number of exercises per session, range of motion, and exercise combination within a session [31]. This protocol employs mixed characteristics as overload is achieved through a combination of exercise types within the session and load.

The load for the proposed exercises will consist of body weight supplemented by dumbbells with corresponding weights and graduated elastic bands, including a single band, double band, and triple band for the clamshell exercises and hip abduction and adduction exercises.

The progression of exercises outlined in the protocol will consider transitioning from less reliance on dumbbells and bands to functional exercises that use body weight. This often exceeds the weight in kilograms of any dumbbell available for home use. We will provide weights ranging from 500 g to 4 kg. This approach will make the exercises more engaging for some participants and increase adherence, as no additional equipment is needed—only their own body weight—with a focus on proper execution, guided in-person and through videos, thus ensuring proper adjustments to avoid compensations.

Participants beginning the protocol may have already performed strength or resistance exercises. For these individuals, or those who reach the maximum weight available at home, progression will not occur through load increments but rather through an increase in the number of sets and subsequent repetitions. Thus, when the exercise set reaches four, progression will occur in increments of two repetitions for each exercise set, as this protocol targets muscular endurance. For example, if an individual reaches a maximum weight of 4 kg for the biceps curl exercise, the progression will be as follows:

2 × 10–3 × 10–4 × 10 ➞ 4 × 12 ➞ 4 × 14 ➞ 4 × 16, and so forth.

Exercises for the first, second, and third months have been chosen to provide variety and avoid monotony, while aligning with the progression toward functional exercises. The goal is not hypertrophy, but rather resistance exercises that contribute to improved functional performance in the daily activities of the participants, as shown in Table 2.

The exercises will target the major muscle groups for the upper limb (UL), lower limb (LL), abdominal muscle, and dorsal muscle groups. The incremental progression of load will be most prominent for UL exercises involving the biceps, triceps, pectoral muscles, frontal shoulder, and lateral shoulder. Elastic bands will be used for hip abduction and adduction. The elastic band will be tied into an arc with individualized measurements to provide minimal resistance during exercise execution. For load progression and increased difficulty, one, two, or three arcs may be added, adhering to the criterion of maintaining a moderate or higher Borg scale score. For other exercises, the progression over time will be toward functional exercises that increase exercise difficulty or demand greater muscular force and muscle group activation. Examples include push-ups, abdominals, planks, bird dogs, bridges, unilateral bridges, clamshell, heel raises, lower vertical jumps, and alternate lunges.

The possibility of load progression will be assessed individually on a biweekly basis, based on how long it takes the participant to complete 20 repetitions per set of exercises outlined in the protocol, specifically those exercises that need to be performed with dumbbells. The load increment may or may not occur according to the load progression flowchart (Fig. 2).

The proposal is to perform the concentric and eccentric phases of the exercise for the same duration (1 or 2 s). If the exercise is performed within the shortest possible time of 1 s for each movement phase, the total time will be 2 s for both phases. We will measure the time it takes for the participant to complete 20 repetitions. If this time is less than 40 s, it will indicate that the exercise is being performed in less than 1 s for each phase, suggesting that the exercise is too easy to perform. In this case, an increase in load will have to be applied. Another situation in which an increase in load will always occur pertains to a low Borg scale score. As explained earlier, the objective of the exercise protocol is to maintain a Borg scale score at a moderately high level with minimal effort.

Data analysis

The data will be extracted to an Excel table and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 22.0 software. The results will be analyzed and described quantitatively and descriptively, expressed in terms of means, medians, minimum and maximum values, standard deviations (for quantitative variables), and frequencies and percentages (for qualitative variables). The findings will be presented in tables and graphs.

The data monitoring process in our study will be conducted by the research team, ensuring adherence to the study protocol and data integrity. Any inconsistencies or protocol deviations will be documented and addressed accordingly.

The Bayesian model was selected due to the small sample size, as it allows for more precise estimation by incorporating prior knowledge and updating it with observed data. In this study, we will use Jeffreys’ prior, a non-informative prior, particularly appropriate in the absence of reliable prior information. Derived from the Fisher information, Jeffreys’ prior is invariant under reparameterization and minimizes subjective bias, making it a suitable choice for studies involving rare conditions. This approach improves statistical power and yields credible intervals, offering a more robust analysis than traditional frequentist methods in settings with limited data. Additionally, Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) techniques will be employed to ensure reliable parameter estimation through efficient sampling of complex posterior distributions [33].

An adjusted statistical analysis will be performed if deemed necessary to account for potential confounding variables and ensure the robustness of the results.

A specific statistical analysis will be employed to handle missing data, ensuring the accuracy and completeness of the results. Techniques such as multiple imputation or maximum likelihood estimation will be utilized as appropriate.

The outcomes will be disseminated through a peer-reviewed scientific journal. Requests for access to the data can be made by interested parties, and their feasibility will be assessed while ensuring participant anonymity. The authors overseeing the study bear the responsibility for the manuscript.

Data collection, entry, and storage

Study data will be collected in person at the university’s CF outpatient clinic and recorded using standardized paper data collection forms. These data will then be entered and stored in an electronic spreadsheet using Microsoft Excel®. To ensure data accuracy and integrity, double data entry will be performed by two independent researchers, followed by a verification process to identify and correct any discrepancies. Additionally, range checks will be implemented to prevent the entry of values outside expected limits.

The data will be securely stored on a restricted-access server, accessible only to the responsible researchers. Security measures, including regular backups and password protection, will be implemented to prevent data loss or breaches.

Discussion

There is a recommendation for resistance exercises for adult individuals with CF that provides detailed characteristics that our study aligns with precisely. The document also emphasizes that these resistance exercises should not replace cardiorespiratory exercises but should be performed with them [34].

The recommendation advises that resistance exercises should be conducted for two or more days a week, and exercising on consecutive days should be avoided. Our study incorporates three sessions per week and recommends alternating exercise days. Weight training is recommended as the preferred form of resistance exercise due to its health benefits and the possibility of monitoring progression. The use of various loads is highlighted in the recommendation, including body weight, resistance bands, free weights, medicine balls, or weight machines. Our study aligns with the above recommendations and includes exercises targeting all the major muscle groups of the upper and lower limbs. The choice of load is suggested to be 70 to 85% of the one-repetition maximum (1RM) for improving muscle strength and less than 70% of 1RM for resistance exercises. If the 1RM test is not feasible, a load that induces local muscle fatigue after the desired number of repetitions should be selected. Given the tele-oriented nature of our exercise protocol, the Borg scale will be used to guide the appropriate level of muscle fatigue [34].

Individuals with CF who combine standard therapy with regular exercise benefit from improved bronchial tree clearance, reduced insulin resistance, enhanced body composition and self-esteem, bone development assistance, decreased protein degradation, elevated levels of insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) for anabolic effects, enhanced immune function, and decreased resting heart rate [35–38].

Pulmonary rehabilitation (PR) is a multidisciplinary intervention and a standard therapy component for individuals with CF. It aims to enhance the physical and psychological condition of patients with chronic respiratory diseases like CF [39]. According to the National Institute of Health, pulmonary rehabilitation aims to maintain patient independence, functionality in society, and quality of life [40].

In general, engaging in physical activity leads to changes in posture, improved pulmonary function, cardiovascular capacity, and clinical stability in patients, not to mention increased self-esteem [41–43].

The latest international consensus, based on evidence and guided by CF experts, published in August 2022, highlights the integration of physical activity and exercise into standard therapy for CF individuals [13].

The consensus involved 39 stakeholders from 11 countries, including researchers, healthcare professionals, and patients. The consensus was reached through multiple stages, resulting in statements and a single final consensus. Experts voted on specific CF-related questions. The possible responses to these questions were “agree/disagree/abstain.” All of the participants agreed with the following statements: “Exercise with huffing or coughing improves mucus clearance; however, there is no evidence in the medium or long term that exercise can adequately replace airway clearance techniques in individuals with CF;” and “Future trials of physical activity and exercise should seek to include outcomes that are currently underestimated, such as cardiovascular health, mental health, endocrine function, inflammation, and patient-reported outcomes, to better evaluate the effects of physical activity and exercise on overall health.” Another statement with 97% agreement relates to the central theme of our study on telerehabilitation, thus providing strong support from the consensus standpoint: “Remote physical training via video call is feasible and acceptable for individuals with CF in a small number of pilot studies, and clinical teams may consider its use as a supplement to face-to-face contact” [13].

Considering the most recent studies published on the topic of CF and physical exercise, it is evident that there are few validations that underpin the guidance and prescriptions of exercises for this population. Our study intends to address some of the questions highlighted by the abovementioned consensus regarding the effects of these exercises on the health of individuals with CF, as well as to propose a specific exercise regimen and a load progression flowchart to improve muscular endurance. To date, no published study has been found to address this need.

Despite all the mentioned benefits, it is still necessary to differentiate between physical activity and exercise. Physical activity refers to any bodily movement that results in energy expenditure, including free play, exercise, or organized sports. On the other hand, physical exercise is planned, structured, and repetitive bodily movement aimed at improving or maintaining physical fitness [44, 45].

Before this consensus, the only material and recommendation for prescribing physical activity in individuals with CF had been published in 2015 that outlines the frequency of aerobic and muscular resistance exercises per week; however, there has been no study that recommends load progression and other long-term follow-up details, especially in the context of remote rehabilitation (telerehabilitation). For adolescents and adults, research has recommended engaging in 30 to 60 min of aerobic exercise three times a week or up to 60 min a day. These exercises aid in mucus clearance, but studies have also indicated similar effects when considering muscular resistance exercise. The recommendation for resistance exercises for children and adolescents is two to three times a week, with an intensity of 50 to 85% of 1RM and a volume of one to three sets of six to 15 repetitions. For adults, it is recommended to exercise two to three times a week, with an intensity of at least 70% of one-repetition maximum and a volume of one to three sets of eight to 15 repetitions. Muscular resistance exercises impact bone mineral density and improve day-to-day functionality [12].

A survey-type study conducted among physiotherapists and healthcare professionals indicated that most respondents recognized that it is their role, as healthcare professionals, to discuss physical activity with their patients. The majority of physiotherapists (95%) reported knowing to prescribe these exercises. However, barriers to implementation were observed, including a lack of motivation in patients to adhere to exercise recommendations, a lack of available follow-up programs, reduced time with patients during clinical visits, and a lack of knowledge to prescribe precise exercise regimens [46].

Thus, telerehabilitation has grown in importance as a powerful tool, because it employs communication technologies for remote rehabilitation, eliminating the need for physical presence of the professional, and increasing and broadening contact with the individual. Compared to conventional clinical rehabilitation practices, this method has proven to be efficient [47].

The latest consensus also points out the lack of guidelines for physical activity for chronic patients with CF that still need to be studied, particularly exploring information such as exercise intensity, duration, and frequency. Although exercise guidelines for disabilities have been released by the World Health Organization, the list of covered diseases does not include CF. However, these guidelines have been directed toward multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, intellectual disability, Parkinson’s disease, stroke, schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. The latest international consensus, published in August 2022, focuses on the impact of physical activity on individuals with CF, covering important themes for our discussion: increased lifespan and well-being; cardiovascular health improvements; enhancement of muscular strength through resistance training; suitable exercise frequencies and intensities to promote good physical fitness; and self-reported improvements in quality of life [13].

In this way, the implementation of this physical training protocol will offer insights into exercise practice, aiming to improve telerehabilitation conditions and address some gaps found in the literature and clinical practice for individuals with CF. Studying the effects of this protocol will contribute to improvements in exercise practices that may lead to better health conditions for these individuals. However, an important limitation of this study is the small sample size, which reflects the rarity of CF and the limited number of eligible participants. While all individuals meeting the inclusion criteria will be invited to participate, the reduced sample may limit the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Nevertheless, this study is intended as a preliminary investigation, and the results may serve as a foundation for future, larger-scale trials evaluating telerehabilitation strategies in this population.
