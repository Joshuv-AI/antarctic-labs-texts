Comprehensive analysis of risk factors for flap necrosis in free flap reconstruction of postoperative tissue defects in oral and maxillofacial tumors
Authors: Shao-bo Ouyang, Zhi-hong Wu, Yan-ping Zhang, Xiao-li Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractFree flap reconstruction for postoperative tissue defects in oral and maxillofacial tumors is a critical component of reconstructive surgery. Identifying risk factors for flap necrosis is essential for improving surgical outcomes and patient quality of life. A retrospective study was conducted on patients who underwent free flap reconstruction between January 2020 and December 2023. Patients were included if they had comprehensive medical records and at least a six-month follow-up. We excluded those with a history of flap necrosis, uncontrolled systemic diseases, non-adherence to postoperative care, or concurrent malignancy treatments. Data on demographics, comorbidities, flap characteristics, and operative details were collected and analyzed using univariate analysis and logistic regression tests. Univariate analysis did not find a significant correlation between flap necrosis and factors such as hyperlipidemia, lymph node metastasis, or flap type. However, diabetes mellitus, oral infections, and albumin levels below 35 g/L were significantly associated with flap necrosis. Multivariate logistic regression showed diabetes mellitus increased the odds of flap necrosis by approximately ninefold, and oral infection increased it by over tenfold. Diabetes mellitus, oral infection, and low albumin levels are significant risk factors for flap necrosis in free flap reconstruction after oral and maxillofacial surgery. Prompt identification and management of these factors are crucial to mitigate the risk of flap necrosis.Keywords: Flap necrosis, Tissue defects, Free flap reconstruction, Oral and maxillofacial surgerySubject terms: Cancer, Oral cancer

Introduction

Oral and maxillofacial tumors encompass a heterogeneous group of neoplasms originating from structures within the oral cavity and maxillofacial region. These tumors, whether benign or malignant, often necessitate surgical intervention, which typically results in significant tissue defects following excision1,2. The management of these postoperative defects is crucial, not only for aesthetic reconstruction but also for the functional restoration of the oral and maxillofacial areas, which are integral to various physiological functions including mastication, speech, and facial expressions3,4. Free flap reconstruction has emerged as a cornerstone in the reconstructive arsenal, offering the possibility of replacing the resected or damaged tissues with similar tissues harvested from a distant site of the patient’s body. This technique involves the transfer of skin, muscle, or bone tissue along with its accompanying blood supply to the surgical site5,6. The survival of the free flap is paramount to the success of the reconstruction; however, it is occasionally compromised by flap necrosis, a devastating complication that can lead to prolonged hospital stays, additional surgeries, and significant morbidity.

Flap necrosis is influenced by various risk factors that can be broadly categorized into patient-specific and procedural variables. Patient-specific factors include underlying comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, smoking, and peripheral vascular disease, which can impair wound healing and microcirculation7,8. Procedural variables involve the choice of flap, surgical technique, and the inherent skills of the operating surgeon. Furthermore, the duration of surgery and the perioperative management of the patient also play critical roles in the survival of the free flap.

This paper aims to provide a comprehensive analysis of the risk factors associated with flap necrosis in free flap reconstruction following the surgical treatment of oral and maxillofacial tumors. By identifying and understanding these risk factors, it is possible to enhance surgical outcomes and reduce the incidence of flap necrosis. This will not only improve the quality of life for patients but also optimize resource utilization within healthcare settings.

Methods

Study design and participant selection criteria

This retrospective study focuses on patients diagnosed with oral or maxillofacial tumors who required surgical intervention, specifically free flap reconstruction, conducted between January 2020 and December 2023.

Participants included must have comprehensive medical records and a follow-up period of at least six months post-surgery to monitor flap necrosis effectively. All patients included in the study were managed by the same multidisciplinary team, ensuring a consistent approach in both surgical and postoperative care. A standardized preoperative assessment protocol was followed for each patient. Detailed postoperative monitoring protocols were implemented, including regular medical follow-ups and necessary medical examinations to effectively track the occurrence of flap necrosis.

By applying these exclusion criteria, we aimed to create a cohort that was as homogeneous as possible, thereby enhancing the reliability of our findings regarding the factors influencing flap necrosis in free flap reconstructions for oral and maxillofacial tumors.

A total of 20 patients who experienced flap necrosis during this timeframe were selected for detailed analysis in the Flap Necrosis Group. To establish a baseline for comparison, a Flap Success Group was also formed, comprising 60 patients from the same period who did not experience flap necrosis, thereby ensuring comparability between the two cohorts. All participants involved in the study provided informed consent. The research methodology, purpose, and protocols were rigorously reviewed and subsequently approved by the ethics committee of our hospital. The methods employed in this study conformed to applicable guidelines and regulations. The study’s design, execution, and documentation rigorously followed the ethical norms set out in the Declaration of Helsinki for medical research involving human subjects. Confidentiality was maintained throughout the data processing phase, and all personal identifiers were eliminated to ensure the privacy of the participants.

Criteria for assessing flap viability in free flap reconstruction

In our surgical protocols, specific criteria are established for assessing the viability of full-thickness flaps in free flap reconstruction procedures. The criteria for determining flap survival are as follows: Complete Necrosis: This is confirmed when the full-thickness flap turns black in color, and no fresh blood is observed upon incision. This indicates a total loss of vascular supply and cellular death throughout the entire flap. Partial Necrosis: This condition is identified by the presence of blisters on the flap, and discoloration to dark or purplish-red. Additionally, the skin at the edges of the flap and the surrounding areas may appear grayish-white. These signs suggest that while there is some level of blood supply compromise, it is not extensive enough to affect the entire flap.

Data collection

In our study aimed at identifying risk factors contributing to flap necrosis in free flap reconstructions of postoperative tissue defects in oral and maxillofacial tumors, we meticulously collected a comprehensive set of patient data. The variables included were the location of the tissue defect, patient age, presence of comorbidities such as diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia, gender, source of the flap, status of lymph node metastasis, maximum tumor diameter, occurrence of oral infections, and key laboratory values including albumin levels (ALB), and body mass index (BMI). Postoperative assessments of the flap included monitoring its temperature, color, and elasticity to evaluate its survival. The selection of these variables was based on their potential influence on surgical outcomes, specifically flap necrosis, and was intended to provide a detailed analysis of factors affecting the success of surgical interventions. Data collection was conducted in strict adherence to ethical standards, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was meticulously conducted using SPSS software (Version 27.0), adhering to established medical research standards. Initially, categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. The independence and relationships among these variables were scrutinized using Chi-square (χ2) tests. Upon the identification of significant predictors of flap necrosis in free flap reconstructions of postoperative tissue defects in oral and maxillofacial tumors during univariate analysis, a more detailed multivariate analysis was initiated. Logistic regression analysis was utilized in this stage to compute odds ratios and confidence intervals, offering an in-depth evaluation of the factors influencing the outcome. The statistical approach was comprehensive, employing two-tailed hypothesis tests, with a threshold for significance set at a p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Univariate analysis for risk factors associated with flap necrosis

A univariate analysis was conducted to ascertain potential risk factors for flap necrosis in free flap reconstructions following oral and maxillofacial tumor resections. The analysis included a variety of clinical and demographic factors. Among the studied parameters, the prevalence of hyperlipidemia, lymph node metastasis, operation time, and the use of flaps such as the pectoralis major flap, anterolateral thigh flap, and forearm flap showed no statistically significant differences between the Flap Necrosis Group and the Flap Success Group, with p-values of 1, indicating no association with an increased risk of flap necrosis. Patient age, specifically those over 60 years, and BMI of 25 kg/m2 or greater were also evaluated. These factors, along with intraoperative blood loss exceeding 500 mL and tumor diameter larger than 2 cm, were not found to significantly affect flap necrosis rates, supported by p-values greater than 0.05. Conversely, albumin levels below 35 g/L, presence of diabetes mellitus, and oral infections emerged as significant predictors of flap necrosis. Albumin levels, in particular, had a chi-square value of 5.104 and a p-value of 0.024, suggesting a strong association with flap necrosis. Diabetes Mellitus and oral infections showed even stronger associations with chi-square values of 13.413 and 13.763, respectively, and p-values indicating high statistical significance (0.00025 for Diabetes mellitus and 0.00021 for oral infection) (Table 1). These findings underscore the importance of preoperative screening and postoperative monitoring for these conditions, as they may be pivotal in predicting and preventing flap necrosis in reconstructive surgery patients.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis for risk factors associated with flap necrosis

The multivariate logistic regression analysis identified several significant risk factors that influence the incidence of flap necrosis in postoperative patients of oral and maxillofacial surgery. Notably, diabetes mellitus emerged as a substantial risk factor, with a marked increase in the odds of flap necrosis as indicated by an odds ratio of approximately 9, suggesting that patients with DM are significantly more likely to experience flap necrosis compared to non-diabetic patients. Additionally, the presence of an oral infection at the time of surgery was also strongly associated with an increased risk of flap necrosis, with an OR exceeding 10. This underscores the critical importance of diagnosing and managing oral infections preoperatively to mitigate postoperative complications. Another pivotal factor was the ALB level, with levels below 35 g/L being linked to a higher likelihood of flap necrosis. Patients with lower albumin levels had over four times the odds of developing flap necrosis, which might reflect the impact of nutritional status on surgical recovery and wound healing (Table 2).

Discussions

In this retrospective study, we have systematically identified and analyzed multiple risk factors contributing to flap necrosis in patients undergoing free flap reconstruction for oral and maxillofacial tumors. The novel findings of our research underline the significant influence of specific comorbidities, particularly diabetes mellitus and oral infections, as well as nutritional status represented by albumin levels, on the success of flap survival. This study is among the first to quantify the impact of these variables in a structured and comparative manner, offering new insights into how preoperative and postoperative factors interplay to affect surgical outcomes. Clinically, our results emphasize the necessity for rigorous preoperative screening and tailored management strategies to mitigate risks associated with these identified factors. By integrating these findings into clinical practice, healthcare providers can enhance surgical planning, patient counseling, and personalized care protocols, thereby improving overall patient outcomes and reducing the incidence of post-surgical complications. The identification of diabetes and oral infections as predominant risk factors also suggests potential areas for intervention, such as stringent glucose control and preoperative dental treatments, which could substantially influence the prognosis of surgical reconstructions in this patient population.

In the field of reconstructive surgery, particularly following resection of oral and maxillofacial tumors, the success of free flap procedures is critical for functional restoration and aesthetic recovery9. Flap necrosis remains a daunting complication, threatening patient outcomes and burdening healthcare resources. Understanding and mitigating the risk factors associated with flap necrosis is paramount for enhancing postoperative success rates10,11. Our comprehensive analysis has delved into an array of risk factors, some modifiable and others inherent to patient demographics or tumor pathology. The vascular nature of free flap survival necessitates a multifaceted approach to patient assessment, encompassing systemic health, local tissue conditions, and surgical proficiency. The intrinsic complexity of oral and maxillofacial anatomy, coupled with the diverse etiology of tumors in this region, underscores the need for a meticulous surgical technique and vigilant postoperative care. The results of our study illuminate the complex interplay of systemic health, local tissue conditions, and surgical variables in the development of flap necrosis following oral and maxillofacial reconstruction. Univariate and multivariate analyses have converged to pinpoint diabetes mellitus (DM), oral infection (OI), and decreased albumin levels as significant contributors to flap necrosis.

Diabetes mellitus, with its well-documented impact on microvascular circulation, presents a mechanistic foundation for its association with poor flap survival. The hyperglycemic environment characteristic of DM is known to induce glycation of proteins and the subsequent formation of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs). These AGEs contribute to endothelial dysfunction, reduced nitric oxide availability, and increased vascular permeability—all factors that can compromise the microcirculation essential for flap viability12,13. Moreover, the impaired immune response in diabetic patients exacerbates their susceptibility to infection, further increasing the risk of flap necrosis. The pronounced risk attributed to oral infections may be understood through the lens of local and systemic inflammation. Bacterial colonization and the resultant infection create an inflammatory milieu, characterized by the upregulation of cytokines and proteases, which can disrupt the delicate balance required for angiogenesis and tissue remodeling—key processes in flap survival14,15. Additionally, the presence of oral pathogens can trigger a systemic inflammatory response, compounding local effects and potentially leading to thrombotic events within the delicate anastomoses of the flap's microcirculation. Albumin, a marker of nutritional status, has emerged as a critical factor with implications extending beyond its role as a simple serum protein. Its association with flap necrosis likely reflects underlying malnutrition, a condition that impairs wound healing and immune competence16,17. Albumin's role in maintaining colloid osmotic pressure also influences tissue edema and interstitial fluid dynamics, which are crucial for tissue perfusion and oxygenation postoperatively.

Despite the non-significance of other factors such as hyperlipidemia and lymph node metastasis in our analysis, their roles in overall patient morbidity cannot be dismissed. Their influence on systemic health may exert subtle effects on wound healing and tissue viability, meriting consideration in a comprehensive patient assessment. Similarly, the finding that longer operation times, greater blood loss, and larger tumor diameters did not reach statistical significance for flap necrosis does not negate the clinical observation that such factors can strain the patient's physiological reserves and may still impact individual outcomes18,19. Furthermore, while age and BMI were not statistically significant in this study, they remain important considerations in the perioperative planning and management of patients. Older age may be associated with comorbid conditions and a decreased physiological capacity for healing, and higher BMI may pose technical challenges in surgery and potentially affect microvascular anastomosis.

This study’s findings have important implications for preoperative preparation and intraoperative strategy. It is suggested that rigorous control of blood glucose levels, aggressive management of oral infections, and nutritional optimization could be pivotal in improving free flap outcomes. Additionally, these risk factors may serve as benchmarks for developing predictive models and tailored perioperative protocols aimed at minimizing the risk of flap necrosis.

Our study provides valuable insights but has several limitations. The retrospective design restricts our capacity to establish causality between identified risk factors and flap necrosis. Conducted at a single institution, our findings may not fully represent broader patient populations or diverse surgical practices. Furthermore, we did not examine genetic predispositions or the preoperative functional status of patients. The classification of diabetes and oral infections, as well as smoking history, relied heavily on patient-reported data and available medical records, which may introduce recall bias and data variability. This could lead to potential underrepresentation or misclassification of these factors, affecting the precision of our findings and possibly obscuring the specific impacts of different diabetes types and oral health conditions on flap necrosis. Additionally, the granularity of data on diabetes types and oral infections was limited, reducing the specificity of our results. Due to the low incidence of complete necrosis, this study combined necrosis categories for analysis, potentially masking unique risk factors and limiting the differentiation between complete and partial necrosis. This approach may affect the generalizability and clinical applicability of our findings. Lastly, while our sample size was statistically sufficient, it may lack the power to detect more subtle associations, particularly with less common risk factors.

To enhance the generalizability and robustness of our findings, future studies should adopt multicenter, prospective designs with larger sample sizes. This approach will allow for a more detailed exploration of the interactions among risk factors for flap necrosis. Specific improvements should include the implementation of standardized diabetes classification systems and structured data collection methods for patient intake, ensuring accurate documentation of diabetes status, oral health conditions, and smoking habits. Prospective studies could also systematically record the onset and management of diabetes and integrate electronic health records with clinical databases to improve the reliability of data on metabolic control and its impact on surgical outcomes. Future research will differentiate between complete and partial necrosis by analyzing a larger cohort, enabling separate evaluations. Additionally, incorporating oral microbiome analyses could elucidate the role of specific pathogens in surgical site infections and flap necrosis. Longitudinal tracking of these factors would facilitate a deeper understanding of their temporal relationships with post-surgical recovery, enabling more precise risk stratification and personalized management strategies in oral and maxillofacial surgery.

Conclusions

In conclusion, diabetes mellitus, oral infection, and albumin level below 35 g/L have been identified as significant risk factors for flap necrosis following free flap reconstruction for oral and maxillofacial tumors. It is imperative that these factors be promptly addressed and managed in a clinical setting to actively prevent flap necrosis and improve patient outcomes.
