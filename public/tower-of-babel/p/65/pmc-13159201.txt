Development of Rwanda country-specific greenhouse gas emission factors for livestock
Authors: Svetlana Gaidashova, Mupenzi Mutimura, Richard German, Richard Claxton, Pearl Nkusi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
National greenhouse gas (GHG) emission inventories have consistently highlighted the significant contribution of livestock. In Rwanda, livestock contributed 43.5% to national emissions in 2018. Most of non-annex 1 countries, including Rwanda, have relied on default Tier 1 emission factors from IPCC for livestock due to the lack of country-specific data. This study aimed to improve Rwanda-specific methods for livestock feed intake, diet composition, volatile solids, nitrogen excretion and manure management systems (MMS) to facilitate Tier 2 GHG emission calculations in future inventories. Literature review and farm survey have been conducted. The country-wide survey generated data missing from literature review: population structure; production system types and their respective proportions; live weight and daily feed intake; volatile solids; nitrogen excretion; types and proportions of MMS for cattle, sheep, goats, swine, rabbits and poultry. The population-weighted methane (CH4) emission factors for enteric fermentation were 62.5 kg/head per year for cattle and 8.6 kg/head per year for sheep. These values were higher than the default values stipulated in the Tier 1 IPCC guidelines but fell below the values previously used in Rwanda national inventories. The data collected did not allow full Tier-2 emission factor calculations for goats, swine, poultry and rabbits. With respect to MMS, the reported emission factors for CH4 were 1.2–1.9 times lower for dairy cattle, sheep, goats and swine and 2.8–18 times higher for poultry and rabbits as compared to the IPCC 2019 default values, and nitrous oxide were notably greater, ranging from 3 to 26 times greater than the default values set forth in the IPCC 2006 guidelines for Africa and used in Rwanda BUR-1. This disparity may be attributed to Rwanda’s zero-grazing policy implementation and predominantly inadequate MMS, with 46% of these facilities being open pits. This study was the first to systematically survey MMS in Rwanda against the IPCC categorization related to GHG emission characteristics. The datasets and methods demonstrated here to calculate the parameters could be adopted for the compilation of the next Rwanda agricultural GHG inventory.

Background

While the Africa’s current role in the global carbon cycle remains limited, the economic development trend suggests that Africa will become a key player in the carbon cycle of the 21 st century [1]. Increasing GHG concentrations in the atmosphere, associated with global temperature increase, have prompted countries to embrace the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC). The inaugural world summit took place in Rio in 1992 [2]. According to the UNFCCC [3], all countries are mandated to submit national GHG inventories regularly and report on key categories. Worldwide, the most significant contributors in the developing world are emissions originating from agriculture and land use change [4]. Agriculture has been identified as a primary GHG source category under the UNFCCC, with CH4 emissions from enteric fermentation in ruminants representing one of the most substantial subcategories [5]. The CH4 is a product of rumen fermentation of carbohydrates through the anaerobic digestive process of cellulose in feed [6]. The quantity of CH4 emitted per cow can reach 500 L per day [7]. The amount of CH4 emitted depends on the level of feed intake, type and quality of feed and environmental temperature [8]. Body size, growth rate and the production level of the animals were also highlighted [9]. In addition, low-quality forage, such as grasses, has reduced digestibility, leading to increased amounts of CH4 production in the rumens [10]. Notably, the majority of local cattle in Rwanda primarily feed on grass and/or crop residues [11, 12]. The CH4 from enteric fermentation contributes approximately 17% of the global GHG emissions [13].

Rwanda submitted three National Communications (NCs) on GHG inventories covering the period from 2003 to 2015 [14], as did its Biennial Update report (BUR-1) for the years 2016–2018 [15]. Currently, the country is in the process of developing its 4th NC on GHG inventories. In the last reported BUR-1, GHG emissions from livestock contributed 49.3% of the total national emissions [15]. In NC-3, Rwanda followed Tier 1 of the IPCC 2006 Guidelines, while in BUR-1 only dairy cattle emissions from enteric fermentation were reported using the simplified Tier-2, and Tier 1 was used for other species, for enteric CH4 and MMS to report livestock GHG emissions [14, 15]. The IPCC 2006 Guidelines encourage the use of country-specific emission factors whenever they have been developed.

Agriculture and livestock in Rwanda accounted for 18.6% and 4.0%, respectively, of the national GDP in 2018 [16]. In the same year, they contributed 14.3% and 49.3% of Rwanda national GHG emissions, respectively [15]. The main categories of livestock in the country include dairy cows, sheep, goats, swine, rabbits, and poultry. Over the years, the number of cows increased from 1,059,000 in 2006 to 1,293,768 in 2018 [15]. They were the main contributors to livestock emissions [16], particularly with One cow per poor family program and implementation of zero-grazing policy resulting in increased manure storage [17]. Swine and poultry were other species experiencing rapid growth [18]. This has also led to an increase in GHG emissions from both, enteric fermentation and MMS from 2,530 Gg CO2e in 2006 to 3,332 Gg CO2e in 2018 [19].

Given the government’s efforts to improve local breeds and promote artificial insemination for cows and swine, a significant proportion of the national herd consists of crossbreeds (19; Opoola, pers.comm.). This underscores the need to develop country-specific emission factors (EFs) for CH4, particularly if these crossbreeds have greater weight (350 kg per mature cow) [41], and thus they are not typical as the IPCC default assumptions for the African region (275 kg for mature cow) [20]. In 2018, emissions from livestock became the primary key category of agricultural emissions [15]. Therefore, there is a pressing need to provide a more comprehensive characterization of all main livestock categories, which will facilitate the derivation of country-specific EFs for enteric fermentation MMS.

This study aimed to assess Rwanda-specific data on livestock feed intake, diet composition, the excretion of volatile solids (VS) and nitrogen (N) to facilitate Tier 2 GHG emission calculations [21, 22] in future inventories. These activity data contributed to [1] the characterization of livestock production systems for cattle, sheep, goats, swine, poultry and rabbits; estimationof country-specific CH4 EFs for enteric fermentation, following the Tier 2 IPCC 2006 methodology from the 2006 IPCC guidelines [21] and the 2019 Refinement [22]; and [3] the comparison with the default EF values provided in the 2006 IPCC guidelines for Africa.

Materials and methods

The study used two distinct approaches for data collection. The first approach involved analysing pre-existing data sources, research and literature. The second approach consisted of conducting survey aimed to gather missing activity data.

Animal characteristics included live weight, milk yield and milk fat content, feed ration composition (i.e., % breakdown of diet by feed type), types and proportions of MMS. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the study, data on livestock population by species and subcategory, milk production and milk composition were collected from national statistics reports [27]. Each data source was assessed for its suitability and relevance, representativeness of Rwanda as a whole, consistency across different times of the year, alignment with definitions in accordance with the IPCC Guidelines [21], transparency of data collection methods, and potential for generating a time series or obtaining future updates. In this evaluation, the most recent available literature was prioritized. However, in cases where recent studies (within the past 5–10 years) were unavailable, older literature (published more than 10 years ago) was also reviewed and considered. For example, in the case of the nutritive values of individual feed plants, the oldest citation considered is from within the last 10 years, whereas for the growth rates of sheep, the oldest citation used dates back to 1989 for Rwanda [30]. Plant nutritive values vary as related to growth stage and seasonal conditions, thus, use of multi-seasonal studies would provide more precise data. Growth rates of sheep can be influenced by breed and management practices [30], however, none of these factors are believed to have undergone significant changes over the past 30 years.

The abovementioned parameters were obtained from published papers and reports from national statistics, research and academic institutions. Additionally, a keyword search of bibliographic databases such as Scopus, Web of Science and Google Scholar was conducted to identify relevant publications related to feed resources, ration formulation, livestock characteristics and performance in Rwanda. Specifically, for feed characteristics, we reviewed a total of 67 reports on feed resources, of which 67 contained information on crude protein and dry matter, 59 on NDF, 34 on ADF, 42 on Ash, 13 on DE, 21 on GE and 42 on metabolisable energy (ME). As the number of reports containing information on digestible energy is limited in the available literature and DE is a critical parameter for estimating DMI and VS excretion, it was instead predicted on the basis of the following empirical equation (Eq. 1; 28):

Survey

A field survey was conducted in June 2022 in four provinces of Rwanda. A total of 95 farms were visited: 29 for dairy cattle; 18 for sheep; 16 for goats; 16 for swine; 15 for poultry and 9 for rabbits. A total of 582 individual animals were observed, 21 samples of manure and 10 feed samples were collected. The distribution of farms was based on the country’s livestock statistics, with the Eastern province having a greater number of cattle. Kigali Province was not included, except for rabbits, which were less represented in four provinces. Thus two rabbit farms from Gasabo district of Kigali City were included and reported under Eastern province dataset considering similar conditions.

The sample size was determined on the basis of the proportion of the livestock population in each province, as reported by NISR [27]. In each province, districts were purposefully selected to represent all major agro-ecologies and types of livestock farms. The farms were chosen randomly, except for the rabbits and poultry farms, where selection was purposeful to ensure sufficient data. The interviews were conducted with farmers from all visited farms through a structured questionnaire pretested before the survey and administered by trained enumerators. The data was collected about all types of livestock present, livestock subcategories (e.g., male, female and age category), the number of livestock, daily milk production, type of feed, feed proportions for different seasons (dry and wet), and live body weight in kilograms. The number of livestock sampled was dependent on the population present on the surveyed farms. Additionally, data on MMS and feeding systems for each livestock category were recorded. Furthermore, measurements of animal live weight were conducted via specially designed measuring tapes or direct weighing, and samples of poultry and swine manure, as well as rabbit feed, were collected for subsequent laboratory analysis. In total, data were collected from 582 individual animals, including cattle, sheep, goats, swine, poultry and rabbits, along with 21 samples of poultry and swine manure and 10 samples of rabbit feed. Cattle was not included in sampling because of sufficient data on their diets were available from published research.

Laboratory analysis

Rabbit feed samples were collected from farms and analysed for ash content, DM content, total nitrogen content, NDF and ADF content using the methods outlined in AOAC (30; 31), as well as Van Soest et al. [33]. The digestibility was computed on the basis of the NDF, ADF, ash and CP of the feed samples. For other livestock types, sufficient data regarding feed nutritive values were obtained from the literature sources. Besides, faecal samples were collected from poultry and pigs, and analysed to determine nitrogen content.

Information on the proportions of each specific type of feed consumed was collected from the farmers. These parameters were subsequently recalculated on DMI basis for each type of livestock. Diet-level digestibility, ash and CP content were calculated as weighted averages of the individual feed types. The CP was derived from total nitrogen, whereas GE was estimated via other parameters derived from the feed samples.

Daily feed intake

For cattle and sheep, we estimated GE intake per animal via the Tier 2 methodology of the IPCC 2006 Guidelines (Vol. 4, Chap. 10). This methodology combined the parameters listed above, including live weight, milk yield and composition, pregnancy rate and growth rate, to estimate the net energy requirements of each animal via Eq. 10.3–10.13 from the IPCC 2006 guidelines. In conjunction with estimates of feed digestibility, this allowed us to calculate GE intake requirements as outlined in Eq. 10.14–10.16 of the IPCC 2006 guidelines. Tier 2 calculations were conducted via IPCC inventory software v2.691 [33].

For goats and swine, where the IPCC 2006 Guidelines do not provide Tier 2 livestock characterization methods, we calculated DMI at 3 kg per 100 kg live weight (i.e., 3% of body weight), which is a common rule of thumb used to estimate typical feed intake [35]. In the case of poultry, a value of 0.12 kg DMI/day to feed a 1 kg chicken was recommended by the Rwanda Agriculture Board (RAB) Annual report 2022–2023 [36], which was then scaled up for the average live weight observed in this study. For rabbits, the DMI was estimated to be 7% of the average live weight, following the method proposed by Lebas [36].

An alternative method frequently used in livestock nutrition science is to estimate typical DMI at a standard rate of 2.5–3 kg DMI per 100 kg of animal live weight (354). Using this method, even in cases where full Tier 2 livestock characterization is not possible, robust data on animal live weights for specific subcategories (males, females and young animals) of each species can be used to directly estimate the DMI for typical animals within each subcategory. This method still aligns well with the broader IPCC methodology and represents a significant improvement over the current Tier 1 method. This method was applied to goats and swine.

For poultry and rabbits, the animal weights were collected during the survey from records provided by farmers. Live weight records were maintained at all the rabbits and poultry farms visited, and the animals were weighed before processing.

Volatile solids excretion

VS excretion was determined via the Tier 2 methodology specified in the IPCC Guidelines, Eq. 10.24, which incorporates country-specific data on DMI and GE, DE% and the ash content of the feed. The DE% and ash content of the diet are both related to the chemical properties of the individual forage species and their relative proportions in the diet. To achieve this goal, we synthesized information on the chemical properties of commonly used forage species in Rwanda and their typical contributions to the diet.

However, the availability of data on DE% and GE in the literature for the used feed resources was relatively limited, leading to a certain level of uncertainty in the empirical DE% calculations described above. As a result, we opted for a conservative approach by using DE% values as per the most appropriate defaults from the IPCC 2006 Guidelines (Vol 4, Chap. 10). Specifically, for cattle, sheep and goats, the DE% values ranged from 45 to 55% (indicative of low-quality forage), whereas those for swine fell within the range of 70–80% (reflecting mature swine in confinement). Poultry had DE% values ranging from 85 to 93% (typical for broiler chickens in confinement), and the DE% of rabbits was estimated to be 65% on the basis of expert judgment.

Nitrogen excretion

A Rwanda-specific Tier 2 approach was implemented in accordance with the IPCC Guidelines to estimate N excretion. This approach requires data related to N intake from feed and N retention within the animal’s body, which accounts for tissue growth and other products such as offspring, eggs, and milk. The data requirements overlap with the methods used to estimate VS excretion, including the need for information on total DMI and the composition of different forage species in the diet. As such, we employed a consistent approach whenever possible - that is, synthesizing information about the chemical properties of common forage species used in Rwanda and their typical contributions to the diet—to estimate N intake. Our primary focus was on the crude protein content of each respective forage species both at the individual forage level and in the overall diet.

In cases where information on the diet of specific livestock categories was limited, we collected manure samples from which the N content was directly measured. This was particularly relevant for non-commercial swine and poultry, where data on N and GE intake were limited owing to the scavenging nature of their diet.

To estimate N excretion for cattle, sheep, and goats, the Tier 2 methodology, which involves subtracting N retention from N intake, was used. For all livestock types except poultry, N intake was calculated by multiplying estimated GE intake per day by the average CP content in the diet, as per the IPCC 2006 Guidelines (Eq. 10.32). The average CP content was calculated as a weighted average, taking into account the CP content of all the different feed resources used by a given livestock category, proportionate to their share of the diet.

In the case of poultry, where data on dietary N intake were lacking, the N content in the diet was estimated from the measured N excretion in the manure collected during this study. We applied the default IPCC assumption that 30% of N is retained (with 70% excreted). For cattle, N retention was estimated via the Tier 2 Eq. 10.33, as provided in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (Vol 4, Chap. 10). This equation takes into account data related to milk production, protein content, and body weight gain, as presented in this report. For other livestock types, although data on weight gain were collected, a country-specific method for calculating N retention is not currently available. Hence, we relied on the default fraction of N retained, as per the IPCC 2006 Guidelines (Vol 4, Chap. 10, Table 10.20), to estimate N retention.

CH4 and N2O emission factors

To allow a direct comparison with the default EFs provided in the IPCC guidelines and those used in the BUR1, we computed per individual animal EFs via the IPCC software. These EFs were determined on the basis of the newly established values of DMI (GE or DM), depending on the livestock species), VS excretion and N excretion, as described earlier. The determination of CH4 emissions from enteric fermentation involved the application of a CH4 conversion factor (Ym) per Eq. 10.21 in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines. In this case, we used the default values from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines for cattle and sheep, as there were no corresponding defaults available for other livestock categories. When calculating the CH4 EF from MMS, we used Eq. 10.23 from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines. This equation takes into account the quantity of VS excreted and combines it with IPCC default values for the maximum CH4-producing capacity (B0) and MCF for each specific MMS. These values were derived on the basis of the proportion of manure handled in each system, as estimated from the data collected in this study. For the nitrous oxide (N2O) EF, we used Eq. 10.25 from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines. This equation allocates the calculated N excretion to various MMS on the basis of the proportion of manure handled in each system, as estimated from the study. The default 2006 IPCC EF for each specific MMS was subsequently applied.

Methods for converting research outputs into inventory data

Inventory compilation for livestock requires two types of activity data (e.g., the number of livestock in category i) and representative average parameters for distinct, homogeneous categories of livestock. To be included in the inventory, the results of the literature review and field sampling work needed to be aggregated and, if necessary, weighted to ensure that they provided a representative overview of Rwanda as a whole. The parameters used in inventory compilation for the livestock sector should accurately represent a typical individual animal within a specific homogeneous livestock species and category. Owing to the survey design, a weighting scheme was applied to the results to generate average parameters that accurately represented a typical animal in Rwanda. The weighting scheme considers the following factors:

i.The varying number of animals at each of the sampled farms. Assuming that the sample of farms was representative (i.e., without bias toward larger or smaller farms), farms with more animals were given proportionally more weight when aggregating the results;ii.The potential presence of geographical differences in animal or management characteristics within a specific livestock category. If certain provinces were over- or underrepresented in the sample data concerning their share of the total livestock population, post-stratification was applied to ensure that the data from each province received the appropriate weighting in the overall average for Rwanda.

The weighting scheme involved first calculating provincial averages and then combining these with weights to derive an overall figure for Rwanda for each parameter of interest. Within each province, a weighted average of the relevant parameter was calculated across farms from that province, with each farm assigned a “weight” on the basis of the proportion of surveyed animals of a given type represented by each farm (Eq. 3).

In the mentioned scheme, the term Vf, p,i is a single number from each farm, representing the “typical” value for individual animals at that farm. This format aligns with the response requested for many of the survey items (e.g., feed type proportions, milk production). However, for some measurements of animal mass and fodder chemical analysis, multiple readings may have been taken from a single farm. In such cases, a farm-level average was initially calculated, and this average was then used as Vf, p,i.

Data analysis

The chemical composition data obtained from the laboratory were analysed via general linear model (GLM) procedures and PDIFF test for separation of homogenous groups of the Statistical Analysis System (SAS) [38]. Data regarding the percentage of feed resources used by farmers during both the wet and dry seasons were subjected to analysis via the descriptive statistics procedures of SAS [38].

Results and discussion

Characterization of livestock populations

Population and its distribution

A survey and literature review revealed that cattle were represented exclusively by dairy cows in this study. These cows are composed of 7% exotic dairy breeds, 60% crossbreeds and 33% local cows (of the Ankole/Inyambo type), with other categories of cattle, such as meat or traction cattle, notably absent [27].

The literature review revealed varying estimates of cattle populations. According to livestock reproduction experts, 70% of mature cows are lactating. However, data from NISR [27] and our study survey indicated proportions of 25%−40% and 53%, respectively (Table 2). This difference would be the result of a momentary survey and not covering the whole yearly period for both, our survey and the earlier study [27].

Commercial small-scale production

Distinct MMS were also established on the basis of the type of livestock production, i.e., commercial-scale versus subsistence/smallholder farming. Commercial livestock keeping was predominantly associated with swine and poultry production.

Milk yield

The identified milk composition revealed that the milk fat content was above 4%. While few literature sources support this percentage, it represents the average fat content across both wet and dry seasons [40]. Average milk yield from the survey was about 7.7 L per cow per day (data not shown), but the national milk production was reported as low as 3.7 L per cow per day as derived from Rwanda national statistics [27]. Reports from research stations reported similar average milk production for mixed breed types and including annual milk yield data estimated at 3.6 L per cow per day [48].

Data on body weight gain for selected livestock species were obtained from published papers. Cattle are the most extensively studied in terms of growth parameters, including daily weight gain (Table 6). Information on other species of livestock was limited, covered by only a few publications, and often lacked detail regarding growth stage. Notably, there is a lack of information on body weight gain in poultry and rabbits.

The EF for CH4 from enteric fermentation, which is based on daily intake estimates under Tier 2, was 62.5 kg per animal place per year for dairy cows and 8.6 kg per animal place per year for sheep.

Manure management systems and nitrogen excretion rates

The survey recorded MMS on all surveyed farms. Within the same species of livestock, there were no significant differences between the subcategories of the animals (e.g., lactating females, young animals). On most farms, the location for manure storage was consistent for all livestock species kept at that farm. However, the frequency of manure removal was linked to the quantity of manure generated. Manure was removed more frequently for small animals and sometimes stored separately in bags with a short storage time (often applied to crops or sold after bag filling). Various types of MMS were observed on farms, including pit storage, dry lots, solid storage, pasture, daily spread, and chicken manure with or without litter (Table 8). Provincial variations in MMS usage are not detailed here, but it is worth noting that Eastern Rwandan farmers favoured solid storage systems, while pits were most common in the rest of the country.

Live body weight and body weight gain

The population size and weight of livestock categories are fundamental in GHG emission calculations. Live body weights of cattle observed in this study were lower (251 kg/head) as compared to the default values for IPCC Tier 1 (275 kg/head) and cattle body weights from Rwanda BUR-1 report (384 kg/head) [15, 21]. However, this could be due to substantial presence of young animals with lower body weight in this study contrary to other authors reported live weight of adult cows (350 kg/head) [41]. The data on live weights for other livestock species were of similar range in this study and published sources [38, 42–45]. For swine, the mean live weight (65 kg) was significantly greater than the 28 kg default in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines but lower than the literature value (78 kg/head) [42]. This illustrates the importance of using Rwandan-specific parameters.

Growth performance data for other livestock species is scarce, highlighting a gap in related research. Most of available data is focused on cattle [46]. No data were recorded in the literature on weight gain in poultry or rabbits. This would be potential research area to address this gap.

Milk production

The average milk production in Rwanda was reported by Rwanda National Institute of Statistics, and it was 3.7 L per cow per day only. Similar reports from research stations have shown that the average milk production for different breed types is estimated to be 3.6 L per cow per day [48]. Given the substantial variations in milk productivity due to the seasonal availability of food and water, our survey data (7.7 L/cow per day), obtained during a snapshot study at a relatively nutritionally favourable time of year, may have led to an overestimation of the annual average milk production. Therefore, in general, published data on milk yield, especially those based on average annual milk production, should be preferred over a momentary survey data.

Fat content of milk can fluctuate between the dry and wet seasons and diets variation. The identified milk composition revealed that the milk fat content was greater than 4% [40]. Although it was reported in a limited number of studies, it represents the average fat content across both the wet and dry seasons [40]. production and a greater proportion of lactating cows indicate that the cows are lactating at any point during the year, including during dry periods, and are an average throughout all seasons. Higher milk yields and a lower proportion of lactating cows are related to cows lactating on a single survey day, and only milk production during lactating period is considered. For the inventory, using lower (year-round) production figures in combination with a higher proportion of lactating cows is the preferred option.

Dairy cow pregnancy rates

The survey data from our study (53% pregnancy rate) and National survey revealed (up to 40%) seem both too low, as the opinion from expert judgement reported 70% pregnancy rate. This discrepancy underlines the limitation of short-term surveys and lack of records on farm to allow annual data collection, which could be resolved by conducting studies of annual duration to close this gap.

Digestible energy percentage

The estimation of digestible energy percentage is challenging. There are two components that introduce uncertainty into the estimates of DE%. First, estimates of the proportion of different feed types in a diet may be uncertain due to the difficulty in farmers estimating the dry matter content of wet feeds and when animals are grazing. Second, the method used to predict the DE% content (% of GE) from other chemical analysis parameters (when not available directly) increases the uncertainty of this parameter. As digestibility is expensive and time-consuming to measure directly in vivo and in vitro, so it would have been beyond the resources of this study, and it was not commonly estimated in the scientific literature. Therefore, the use of conservative approach with the IPCC 2006 Guidelines default DE% value for low-quality forage was justified. Nevertheless, for future inventories, using the results of research would be a considerable improvement in the default assumption, as it allows tracking of changes in feeding strategies over time, which can constitute an important part of GHG mitigation efforts.

Feed composition

Feed resources for ruminants were well reported. Most common fodder species reported were Napier grass (7.5% of the publications) and Calliandra (4.5% publicatons). Other feed resources were reported in only 1.5% − 3% of published papers. Although the fodder species were diverse, a substantial percentage (63%) of fodder species were only studied in a single report, with 32% being covered by only two reports. This would reflect poor availability of high-quality fodder species from one side, and farmers attempt to diversify feed with locally available but not necessarily the best fodder species.

Daily feed intake

This study generated DMI estimates for all livestock types, which are specific to the livestock characteristics and diet composition in Rwanda. This value was calculated via the Tier 2 2006 IPCC Guidelines methodology for cattle and sheep (taking into account Rwanda-specific energy requirements), and for other livestock, it was based on the empirical relationship between the DMI and the animal weight or literature values. In BUR-1, the DMI was only calculated directly for cattle. In this study, calculating the DMI enabled more Rwanda-specific estimates of VS and N excretion for all livestock types, so the impact of this can be seen indirectly in the effects on manure CH4 and manure N2O emission factors per head.

In addition, the new DMI estimates enable updated enteric CH4 EFs to be derived via a Tier 2 method for cattle (for which a Tier 2 method was used in the BUR-1) and for sheep (where a Tier 1 EF was used in the BUR-1). A comparison of the EFs revealed that the cattle EF of 62.5 kg CH4/animal/year was lower than the value used in BUR1 (95.6) but higher than the IPCC default value (47). This reflects a lower average live weight of 251 kg for cattle based on the data collected in this study, which includes young cattle, than the BUR1 value of 385 kg, which included only mature cattle, and as such, the value in this study is more representative of the entire cattle population. The IPCC default value assumes a lower average weight than this study does, as well as a lower milk production level (1.3 kg/day compared with 3.7 L/day in this study). For sheep, the value from this study (8.61 kg CH4/animal/year) is more similar to the value in the IPCC Guidelines for developed countries [8] than for developing countries [5], which may reflect differences between the data on growth rates of young sheep, as well as pregnancy rates and milk production of mature ewes derived from this study, compared with those assumptions made for developing countries as a whole underlying the IPCC default.

Volatile solids

In BUR1, Tier 1 default emission factors from the 2006 IPCC Guidelines were used for the “Africa” region (cattle and swine) or “developing countries” (other livestock), relying on assumptions regarding (i) VS excretion on the basis of typical livestock characteristics and (ii) the typical share of MMS. In contrast, this study estimated volatile solid excretion for all livestock categories, enabling the use of a Tier 2 approach to calculate methane emission factors from MMS. This approach considered the actual share of MMS used for each livestock type in Rwanda.

For manure management systems, per-animal CH4 emission factors obtained in this study were 0.2–2.2 times lower as compared to the default values for IPCC 2019 [22] for dairy cattle, sheep, goats and swine (Table 15) and 2.8 and 18 times higher for poultry and rabbits than the respective emission factors from IPCC2019 [22]. The main driver of this disparity was the utilization of actual data on the share of manure managed in different MMS in Rwanda, facilitated by the Tier 2 approach. This contrasts with the assumption for typical “African” or “developing country” MMS that underlie Tier 1 default EFs. The weighted average MCF determined in this study is approximately 5 times greater (for swine) to 22 times greater (for rabbits) than the default values in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines (Vol 4, Chap. 10, Table 10A–5 to 10A–9). For cattle, Tier 1 EFs assume that most manure is deposited on pasture, range and paddock with an MCF of 1.5%. In contrast, Rwanda-specific data show that pit storage (for > 1 month) is the predominant MMS for all livestock types except poultry. This deviation from “typical” African conditions results in significantly higher CH4 emissions due to the higher MCF of pit storage (46% in Rwandan average temperatures; Table 10.17 in the 2006 IPCC Guidelines).

For all livestock types except rabbits, daily estimates of VS excretion were slightly greater than assumed in the IPCC Tier 1 default EFs, which is likely linked to higher feed intake estimates. While this contributed to higher per animal CH4 EFs in this study, the primary factor was the changes in the data on manure allocation to different MMSs.

Despite uncertainties in the collected data on MMS and estimates of VS excretion, the Tier 1 EFs used in BUR1 were likely underestimated for Rwanda across all livestock species. Therefore, using a Tier 2 method, as demonstrated in this study, for the next inventory is expected to improve accuracy.

Nitrogen excretion rate and N2O emission factor from MMS

To track the impact of changes in livestock productivity and growth rates on N excretion, the estimation of N retention based on observed data via the Tier 2 method is important. In this study, this was possible for cattle and is an improvement on previous methods used in the BUR1, where most parameters were default or expert judgments. However, further studies are needed to use similar method for other livestock types, making use of the growth rate and additional productivity data, for example, egg production. The IPCC 2019 Refinement [22] provides additional Tier 2 methods for swine, laying hens and broilers, but Rwanda-specific models would need to be developed for other livestock categories if the size of emissions justifies this. An analysis of swine and poultry manure VS and N contents, as undertaken in this study, has the potential to be used in the future to provide a direct estimate of VS and N excretion rates where feed resources are not well characterized but it must be further combined with estimates of manure production rates to provide robust figures.

The new data on MMS also have a significant effect on the overall direct per animal N2O EF from manure management. The weighted average EF per kg of N in storage across all MMS used was between 2.5 times (for rabbits) and 4.8 times greater (for swine) than the equivalent values in BUR-1. This is attributed to the updated manure management data, which indicate a substantial fraction of manure in dry lot and solid management (ranging from 13% to 56% combined, depending on the species) in place of some of the pit storage. According to the expert judgment used in BUR-1, no manure was allocated to dry lots or solid storage. Notably, dry lot and solid storage resulted in much greater N2O emission factors (0.02 and 0.005 kg N2O-N/kg N, respectively) than did pit storage (0.002 kg N2O-N/kg N).

Despite the uncertainty in N excretion rates, manure allocation and N2O emission factors for different MMS, it is likely that the implied per-animal N2O EFs used in BUR-1 were underestimated. Using the methods presented in this study in future inventories is expected to yield more accurate emission estimates.

Manure management systems

Our study may be the first to report on MMS n livestock production systems in Rwanda. While some published reports provide few details about manure quality [52, 53], we could not find any data related to the IPCC classification of the MMS reported for Rwanda. Thus, our study is the first to use survey data from the ground instead of the earlier expert judgment used in the 3rd National Communication and BUR-1. This study provided a significant improvement as it generated country-specific data on the Rwandan MMS. Compared to the default types and shares of the MMS for Africa provided in the IPCC 2006 Guidelines, Rwandan conditions are not very representative, especially after 2007 when zero-grazing policy entered in force with domination of pit storage generating higher GHG emissions.

Suitability for the future GHG inventories

In general, the results of the literature review and survey represent a significant improvement over the use of IPCC default parameters and previous country-specific estimates for Rwanda on the basis of other data. However, some limitations affect the applicability of the results to inventories.

Seasonality

The survey was conducted at one point of time in a year, so seasonal variation is not reflected in the results. When this difference is considered to be significant, the results are compared with literature values that consider the entire year. The key assumptions related to this increase in uncertainty are as follows.

Livestock births are not seasonal, so live weights recorded during the survey are a good estimate of the average across the year. The survey undertaken as part of this work is not part of a regular data collection programme with a committed future budget. In Rwandan strategic documents, Vision 2050 [54] and the National Land-Use Development Master Plan 2020–2050 (55), significant shifts in agricultural production practices are expected in Rwanda over the next few decades. For example, a greater share of commercial agriculture and greater use of imported animal feed will have an impact on the parameters used in the GHG inventory. Therefore, to monitor GHG emissions effectively from agriculture, future surveys should be conducted regularly, and match national communications to reflect changes in the agriculture sector.

Conclusion

This study aimed to improve Rwanda-specific methods concerning livestock feed intake, diet composition, VS and N excretion to facilitate Tier 2 GHG emission calculations in future inventories. Tier 2 methods enable the capture of the impact of changes in livestock traits or feed characteristics, including those resulting from GHG mitigation measures.

The compilation of data from the literature, in conjunction with the results of the survey and laboratory work, provided extensive information sufficient to derive feed intake, VS, and N excretion values for all major animal types. For dairy cattle and sheep, data on milk production and composition (cattle only), live weight, and growth rates were used to calculate GE and DMI via the IPCC Tier 2 livestock characterization methodology. For other livestock categories where this methodology is not available from the IPCC 2006 Guidelines, the collected live weight data enabled the prediction of dry matter intake via an empirical relationship derived from the literature.

This study was the first to systematically survey MMS practices in Rwanda against the IPCC categorization related to emission characteristics. It is recommended that the next Rwandan GHG inventory use these actual survey data instead of the expert judgment used in the BUR-1 and previous inventories.

Recommendations for future work

The exact values presented for DMI, VS and N excretion are a combination of many inputs, some of which are likely to change over time. Therefore, rather than focusing on the precise values of parameters per se, the datasets and methods demonstrated here to calculate the parameters should be adopted for the compilation of the next Rwanda agricultural GHG inventories.

Given the anticipated changes in agriculture over the next decades, surveys should be conducted regularly, preferably every 5 years, by an organization with the mandate and capacity to ensure consistency and comparability of data over time. Many parameters in this research report were also drawn from the scientific literature, and there is no guarantee that appropriate academic research projects will be conducted on a regular basis. Therefore, future surveys may also need to be larger in scope than the survey conducted under this study, encompassing parameters taken from the literature.

Further research would be conducted to improve the data available on the digestibility of feed. Robust digestibility data would be generated for all commonly used feed types in Rwanda.
