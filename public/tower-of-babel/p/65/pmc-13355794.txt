Impact of SGLT2 inhibitors in cardiac amyloidosis: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shreyas Nandyal, Aviral Vij, Revati Varma, Rohan Gajjar, Angelo Caputi, Samuel Odoi, Sukriti Banthiya, Saketh Vinjamuri, Saurabh Malhotra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionCardiac amyloidosis (CA), including transthyretin (ATTR) and light-chain (AL) subtypes, is an underrecognized mimic of heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. While sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) benefit general heart failure (HF) populations, their role in CA is unclear. This study evaluated the impact of SGLT2i on outcomes in patients with CA.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis of seven observational studies (N = 13,303) compared patients with CA (AL and ATTR) receiving SGLT2i to matched controls. Outcomes included mortality, HF exacerbations, estimated GFR (eGFR), and NT-proBNP. Random-effects models were used to pool hazard ratios (HRs) and mean differences.ResultsIn the primary analysis restricted to ATTR-CA, SGLT2i use was associated with lower observed all-cause mortality (the primary outcome; HR 0.71, 95% CI 0.58–0.87, p = 0.001). Among secondary outcomes in the ATTR-CA subgroup, SGLT2i use was associated with reduced NT-proBNP (−414 pg/mL, 95% CI –694 to −134, p = 0.004) and improved eGFR (+4.3 mL/min/1.73 m2, 95% CI 1.4 to 7.2, p = 0.004). In a secondary, exploratory any-CA (AL + ATTR combined, 4 studies) analysis, the pooled mortality HR was 0.67 (95% CI 0.50–0.91, p = 0.010); between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 75%), and this combined estimate should not be interpreted as a single class effect across CA subtypes.ConclusionsIn this meta-analysis of non-randomized observational studies, SGLT2 inhibitor use was associated with lower observed mortality and improved cardiac biomarkers in CA, with renal benefits most evident in ATTR. Because all included studies were observational and susceptible to confounding by indication, immortal time bias, and survivor bias, these findings are hypothesis-generating and should not be interpreted as evidence of efficacy; randomized trials are needed before treatment recommendations can be made.

Introduction

Cardiac amyloidosis (CA) is a progressive, infiltrative cardiomyopathy caused by the deposition of misfolded proteins (light-chain or transthyretin) in the myocardium, leading to restrictive heart failure [1]. Once considered a rare and underdiagnosed disease, CA is increasingly being recognized among patients with heart failure [2], [3]. Novel therapies have been shown to improve survival and cardiovascular outcomes among patients with CA [4], [5], [6].

Sodium–Glucose Cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have emerged as effective treatment strategies among patients with heart failure (HF) and have been firmly incorporated as an integral part of guideline-directed medical management of HF [7], [8]. While not specifically studied among patients with CA, early evidence from observational studies suggests favorable outcomes among these patients with the use of SGLT2 inhibitors [9], [10]. In this meta-analysis, we synthesize the current evidence on the association between the use of SGLT2 inhibitors and patients with CA, focusing on key clinical outcomes among patients with CA.

Methods

Search strategy and study selection

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines; a review protocol was not pre-registered. A comprehensive search of PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar was performed (as of March 30, 2025). We used search terms combining “SGLT2 inhibitor” OR “sodium-glucose cotransporter 2” with “amyloid” OR “amyloidosis” and “heart failure”. The search was restricted to human studies and to English-language publications (consistent with the PubMed filter detailed in Supplementary Table S1). The PubMed search yielded 40 records, of which seven studies met the inclusion criteria after screening titles/abstracts and reviewing full texts. The Cochrane Library search found three results (including one ongoing trial in Italy), but none met the inclusion criteria for this analysis (ongoing trials without results were excluded). Google Scholar identified 153 results, which were screened against the same pre-specified inclusion criteria by two independent reviewers and cross-checked against the PubMed and Cochrane Library yields for non-uniqueness (no duplicate records were identified for removal at this stage). Of the 40 PubMed records, all proceeded to full-text review owing to the highly targeted query design; 33 were excluded at full-text screening, with reasons documented in Supplementary Table S2 (12 narrative reviews or commentaries, 9 studies of SGLT2i in non-amyloid cardiomyopathy, 6 mechanistic or bench studies, 3 studies in non-amyloid chronic kidney disease, and 3 case reports without comparator). A backward citation (snowball) search of all included studies and of two recent narrative reviews identified three conference abstracts and two research letters that did not meet inclusion criteria. Seven studies met all inclusion criteria and were retained for meta-analysis. Full Boolean search strings, search dates, and per-database yields are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Data extraction and quality assessment

Two reviewers independently extracted data from each included study using a standardized form. Extracted data included study design, sample size, amyloidosis subtype (AL vs ATTR), presence of heart failure at baseline, details of SGLT2i use (agent, dose, and concomitant therapies for CA), length of follow-up, and outcomes of interest (mortality, heart failure hospitalizations, changes in NT-proBNP, eGFR, and any reported adverse events). For each outcome, we recorded effect measures (hazard ratios [HR], risk ratios, or mean differences) along with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). Risk of bias was ascertained using the Newcastle Ottawa Score. The Risk of Bias dot plot is depicted in Fig. 2. Overall, the included studies were observational in nature. No randomized trial results were available for appraisal. Effect estimates were extracted preferentially as adjusted (propensity-score-matched, multivariable, or time-dependent) hazard ratios; per-study extraction handling, matching and adjustment variables, and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) scores are detailed in Supplementary Table S3.Fig. 2Risk of Bias summary of included studies assessed with the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We conducted a meta-analysis for outcomes reported by the selected studies. For time-to-event outcomes (mortality and heart failure hospitalization), we pooled the HR using a DerSimonian-Laird random-effects model (anticipating inter-study heterogeneity due to different study designs and populations). For continuous outcomes (changes in NT-proBNP and eGFR), we pooled the mean differences between SGLT2i and control groups at follow-up, using random-effects inverse-variance modeling. Heterogeneity was evaluated with the I2 statistic. To assess the robustness of pooled estimates to individual studies, we conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each primary outcome; full results are reported in Supplementary Table S4. For continuous outcomes (NT-proBNP and eGFR), data extraction was complicated by variability across studies in how outcomes were reported. When studies reported medians and interquartile ranges, these were converted to means and standard deviations using the method of Wan et al. (BMC Med Res Methodol 2014). For studies reporting baseline and follow-up values without an explicit change-SD, the change-from-baseline SD was imputed using the Cochrane formula assuming within-subject correlations of r = 0.3 and r = 0.7 as a sensitivity analysis; pooled estimates were materially identical at the two correlation values. Per-study extraction logic, follow-up windows, and the value type pooled (change-from-baseline versus end-of-follow-up) are detailed in Supplementary Table S5. Because NT-proBNP is highly skewed and follow-up durations varied (3–12 months across contributing studies), we also computed a robustness analysis using the standardized mean difference, which yielded effect estimates concordant in direction and significance with the primary mean-difference analysis.

The studies enrolled predominantly elderly patients (study-level median ages ranged from 66 to 80 years; per Table 2), with most patients being male [14], while in two others, tafamidis use was reported in 93–94% of cases [11], [12]. In the AL amyloidosis cohort, most patients were receiving chemotherapy for their underlying plasma cell dyscrasia [13]. Across the included cohorts, the use of renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors was heterogeneous. The use of ACE inhibitor use ranged from 12 to 44% in SGLT2i groups and 12 to 44% in controls, ARB use ranged from 41 to 53% in SGLT2i groups and 42 to 50% in controls, while ARNI therapy was rare, reported in only 1–8% of patients (Lang F. et al.), with the highest use seen in Dobner et al. (45–77%). The included studies had follow-up durations ranging from 3 months to 5 years, with the average follow-up duration being 2.4 years. In the pooled analyses, all SGLT2 inhibitors were considered as a class, and the effect of SGLT2 inhibitors on outcomes was treated as a class effect. We acknowledge that pooling distinct agents (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) as a class is an assumption that may not hold uniformly across all clinical effects; agent-specific subgroup analyses were not feasible given the small number of included studies and inconsistent reporting of agent use within cohorts.

While all studies were non-randomized, four employed propensity score matching or multivariable adjustment to balance baseline differences [9], [10], [12], [15]. The proportion of comorbid conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, and coronary artery disease, varied across cohorts (highlighted in Table 2), with most patients having heart failure at baseline. Mean NT-proBNP levels among patients were generally consistent with advanced cardiac involvement.

All-cause mortality

Four of the seven studies reported all-cause mortality outcomes for SGLT2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) compared to the control [9], [10], [11], [15]. Heart failure exacerbation

Four studies specifically evaluated heart failure hospitalizations and/or acute HF exacerbation rates as outcomes [9], [10], [11], [15]. A pooled analysis of these studies revealed a trend toward a reduction in HF exacerbations with SGLT2i therapy, although statistical significance was not achieved (HR 0.84; 95% CI 0.58–1.20; p = 0.338). Of note, the four contributing studies used heterogeneous endpoint definitions: Augusto and Schwegel used HF hospitalization, Byer used acute-care HF exacerbations, and Porcari reported a composite of CV death plus HF hospitalization (Supplementary Table S6); the pooled estimate should therefore be interpreted as a mixed HF-event endpoint rather than a single homogeneous outcome [11]. In the AL-CA cohort (Lang et al), a formal comparison of HF hospitalization between the efficacy cohort and control cohort was not reported. However, 13 hospitalizations in the SGLT2i group were reported, all of which were deemed unrelated to SGLT2i therapy [13].

Change in biomarker levels

SGLT2i therapy was associated with improved NT-proBNP levels over time compared to the control group. When pooling the NT-proBNP change data (from Lang AL, Lang ATTR, Porcari, and Dobner), a significant overall reduction in NT-proBNP with SGLT2i was noted (pooled mean difference: −464.68 pg/mL; 95% CI: −712.22 to −217.15; p = 0.0002). This finding was also noted among the ATTR-CA subgroup exclusively (pooled mean difference: −414.32 pg/mL; 95% CI: −694.47 to −134.16; p = 0.004). Similarly, in patients with AL-CA, Lang et al. reported a significant reduction in NT-proBNP over the ∼11-month follow-up in the SGLT2i group (median decrease: −528 pg/mL), whereas it increased in untreated patients (median increase: +116 pg/mL), resulting in a between-group difference (p = 0.033) favoring SGLT2i [13].

On pooled analysis of any CA (AL and ATTR), SGLT2 inhibitor therapy use was not associated with a statistically significant improvement in eGFR (Mean difference: +2.42 mL/min/1.73m2; 95% CI: −1.95 to 6.79; p = 0.28). Sensitivity analyses for outcomes

We conducted leave-one-out sensitivity analyses for each outcome (Supplementary Table S4). For all-cause mortality, the combined any-CA estimate was substantially influenced by the largest cohort: removal of Augusto et al. attenuated the pooled HR from 0.67 to 0.77 (95% CI 0.57–1.03; p = 0.075), no longer reaching statistical significance, whereas removal of Schwegel et al. — the study whose unadjusted survival benefit disappeared after correction for immortal-time bias — preserved significance (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.47–0.85; p = 0.003), indicating that the mortality association is not driven by the most bias-prone study. In a quality-stratified analysis restricting the primary ATTR-only mortality pool to studies with a Newcastle–Ottawa score of 7 or higher (excluding Schwegel et al., NOS 6; Supplementary Table S3), the association was preserved (HR 0.69, 95% CI 0.56–0.84; p = 0.0002). For heart-failure exacerbation, leave-one-out estimates remained non-significant across all single-study removals. The NT-proBNP pooled estimate was robust to leave-one-out, with sensitivity estimates ranging from approximately −405 to −619 pg/mL (all retaining p ≤ 0.009). In contrast, the pooled eGFR estimate was sensitive to study choice: removal of Porcari, Dobner, or Lang F (ATTR) shifted the pooled effect across zero, consistent with the substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 80%) observed in the any-CA eGFR analysis.

Discussion

SGLT2 inhibitors offer multifaceted cardioprotective effects, including diuresis, antifibrotic signaling, inflammasome suppression [16], [17], decreased reactive oxygen species through mitochondrial housekeeping functions, and reversal of cardiac remodeling. SGLT2 inhibitors have also been shown to reduce epicardial adipose tissue volume and adipocyte signaling, leading to decreased inflammation [18], [19], [20]. They also reduce oxidative stress [21], [22]. In addition, they contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling and reducing cardiac fibrosis. These mechanisms align with the diastolic dysfunction that is a hallmark of amyloid cardiomyopathy [23]. The landmark HFpEF trials (EMPEROR-Preserved, DELIVER) demonstrated that SGLT2 inhibitor therapy substantially reduces HF hospitalizations and improves survival in non-amyloid HFpEF [24], [25]. Notably, CA often mimics an HFpEF phenotype (especially ATTR-CM, which is characterized by preserved EF in early disease and diastolic dysfunction).

Mortality

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the primary analysis restricted to ATTR-CA (4 studies) showed an association between SGLT2 inhibitor use and lower observed all-cause mortality (pooled HR: 0.71 [95% CI 0.58–0.87; p = 0.001], corresponding to a 29% relative reduction). In the exploratory secondary any-CA analysis (4 studies), the pooled HR was 0.67 (95% CI 0.50–0.91; p = 0.010); however, between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 75%) and this combined estimate should not be interpreted as a single class effect.AL-CA mortality could not be pooled separately (Lang AL reported 0 deaths in either arm over ∼11 months); mortality was therefore pooled for the ATTR-CA subgroup as the primary analysis and for the combined any-CA cohort as a secondary, exploratory analysis. The most extensive cohort study by Augusto et al., reported that SGLT2 inhibitor use in CA patients with heart failure resulted in a 46% risk reduction in 5-year all-cause mortality (HR 0.54, 95% CI 0.50–0.59). Similarly, in the multicenter study of ATTR-CA by Porcari et al., a significantly lower mortality rate was observed over a ∼2-year follow-up (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.37–0.89, p = 0.010). In this study, 31.9% had died from cardiovascular causes or had HF hospitalization compared to only 16.8% of SGLT2i patients (p < 0.01). In the study by Byer et al., a non-significant trend toward reduced 5-year mortality (HR 0.83, CI 0.63–1.08) was observed in SGLT2i-treated ATTR patients, with an absolute mortality rate of 13.2% on SGLT2i compared to 27.3% in the control group. The only study that did not demonstrate a definitive mortality benefit was the single-center registry by Schwegel et al.: in unadjusted analysis, SGLT2i use was associated with improved survival (HR 0.457, p = 0.029), but after adjusting for immortal time bias, this association lost statistical significance (HR 1.075, 95% CI 0.524–2.206, p = 0.843). The Schwegel observation is methodologically pivotal: it demonstrates that the apparent survival benefit observed in unadjusted analyses can be substantially explained by immortal-time and survival-cohort effects, and is the single most important caveat to our pooled mortality finding. As an additional robustness check, the E-value for the ATTR-only primary mortality estimate (HR 0.71) is 2.18 with a CI bound of 1.57 (Supplementary Table S7), meaning an unmeasured confounder would have to be associated with both SGLT2i prescription and mortality with risk ratios ≥2.18 to fully explain the observed association — a high bar that does not preclude residual confounding but suggests the observed association is unlikely to be entirely explained by a single weak confounder. Whether any survival association extends to AL-CA remains uncertain, as AL-CA mortality could not be pooled. In the report by Augusto et al., the mortality reduction with SGLT2i was evident in both the ATTR-CA and AL-CA subgroups. The mortality data from Lang et al. were reported in numbers and were thus not suitable for inclusion in the pooled HR. A noteworthy finding in the AL amyloidosis cohort in Lang et al. was that no deaths occurred in the SGLT2i group during ∼11 months of follow-up, and in the matched control group. Thus, mortality data in AL-CA remain too limited to support any conclusion, and the ATTR-CA findings should not be extrapolated to AL-CA.

Heart failure hospitalizations

In patients with ATTR CA, both Byer et al. and Porcari et al. found that SGLT2i significantly reduced HF hospitalization events. Byer et al. noted a 36% relative risk reduction in HF exacerbations requiring acute care (HR 0.64, 95% CI 0.48–0.86, p < 0.01) over 5 years [15]. Porcari et al. similarly found fewer HF hospitalizations in the SGLT2i group: 11.8% of SGLT2i-treated patients were hospitalized for HF, compared to 22.3% of controls, translating to an HR of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.36–0.91, p = 0.014) [10]. These reductions were consistent regardless of diabetes status or ATTR mutation type (wild versus variant) [10]. In contrast, Schwegel et al. did not show a significant difference in HF hospitalizations between SGLT2i users and non-users [11]. However, that study may have been underpowered for this endpoint with only 32 total HF hospitalization events. It is notable that in studies reporting composite outcomes (e.g., “CV mortality and HF hospitalization”), SGLT2i showed significant benefit. Porcari et al. found that the composite of CV death and HF hospitalization was significantly lower with SGLT2i (HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.38–0.84, p = 0.003) [10]. This suggests that when considering overall heart-failure-related events, SGLT2i therapy may be associated with improvements in composite heart-failure outcomes in patients with CA, though the pooled mixed HF-event estimate did not reach statistical significance. Importantly, the four contributing studies defined the HF endpoint differently — Augusto used inpatient HF events from claims data; Byer used HF exacerbations requiring acute care including ED visits; Schwegel used registry-adjudicated hospitalizations; and Porcari used a composite of CV death plus HF hospitalization (Supplementary Table S6). This definitional heterogeneity, together with the high statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 78%), is a substantial limitation of the pooled HF estimate and supports interpreting it descriptively rather than as a class effect.

Biomarkers

SGLT2i use was also associated with improvements in NT-proBNP, further supporting the hypothesis that their use results in reduction in myocardial stress among patients with CA [10], [13], [26]. In the study by Porcari et al., SGLT2i-treated patients had a much smaller rise in NT-proBNP at 1 year compared to matched controls; after adjusting for baseline levels (p < 0.001). Specifically, at baseline, the SGLT2i group had slightly lower NT-proBNP levels (4148 ng/L) compared to the control group (5032 ng/L). However, over 12 months, the control group's NT-proBNP levels increased substantially (+914 ng/L), while those in the SGLT2i group rose only marginally (+323 ng/L) [10]. In a pilot study by Dobner et al. with ATTR-CA patients on tafamidis, there was no statistically significant change in NT-proBNP at 3 months; however, 76.5% of SGLT2i patients experienced a numerical decrease in NT-proBNP, compared to 67.5% of controls [14]. Consistent with the biomarker findings, clinical HF status was also better maintained with SGLT2i. Porcari et al. noted that SGLT2i-treated patients were less likely to worsen in NYHA functional class at 1 year (odds of functional class deterioration were significantly lower with SGLT2i) [10]. Lang et al. (AL-CA) similarly found that loop diuretic requirements decreased over time in the SGLT2i group. In contrast, they increased in controls, implying improved or stable volume status with SGLT2 inhibition [13]. Lang et al. (ATTR-CA) observed a significant weight reduction in the SGLT2i group over 6 months (despite a decrease in diuretic dose), again consistent with net decongestion [12]. Although no study reported improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction attributable to SGLT2i, most patients had preserved ejection fractions to begin with. The mechanisms underlying improvements in NT-pro BNP likely stem from enhanced diuresis and myocardial unloading [26], [27]. The effects of SGLT2i were sustained regardless of the presence of type 2 DM [10]. Interestingly, it was found that SGLT2i therapy led to reduced loop diuretic requirements and weight loss in patients with CA [13], possibly due to its osmotic diuretic effect. For instance, in Porcari et al.'s study, baseline diuretic use was similar between groups, but patients treated with SGLT2 inhibitors had significantly fewer new initiations of loop diuretics over 12 months compared with controls (OR:0.14; 95%CI:0.04–0.47; P = 0.001) [10].

Pooled analysis of all studies did not show a statistically significant improvement in eGFR. This could be attributed to a disproportionately greater renal amyloid deposition among patients with AL, a recognized clinical co-morbidity in the AL population. Consistent with this understanding, our analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in eGFR in the ATTR-CA subgroup but not in the combined any-CA analysis; the single available AL-CA cohort (Lang et al.) showed an eGFR decline rather than improvement and was too limited to pool separately. Porcari et al. found that SGLT2i-treated patients had a smaller decline in eGFR at 12 months compared to controls [10]. Byer et al. observed a significantly lower incidence of acute kidney injury (AKI) episodes in the SGLT2i group (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.35–0.79), implying a protective effect against acute renal events, perhaps due to better HF management and perfusion [15]. Lang et al. ATTR reported a significant eGFR drop at 1 month in the SGLT2i group (mean change −5 mL/min, p = 0.002) relative to controls. However, importantly, there was no difference in eGFR between groups by 6 months [12]. This pattern mirrors what is seen in other populations on SGLT2i (an acute hemodynamic GFR reduction, then stabilization). No study has found a long-term decline in renal function attributable to SGLT2 Inhibitors.

Safety and tolerability

All included studies reported that SGLT2-inhibitor therapy was generally well tolerated in this patient population. Common SGLT2i-related side effects (e.g., genital yeast infections, volume depletion symptoms) were observed at rates comparable to other HF trials. Discontinuation rates with SGLT2i were generally low. In one study, 11.5% stopped therapy (primarily due to genitourinary symptoms) [12]. In contrast, among AL-CA patients, 7% discontinued therapy due to hypovolemia or genital irritation; an additional ∼22% temporarily held therapy for volume-related side effects but later resumed it. Importantly, no cases of diabetic ketoacidosis, severe hypoglycemia, or urosepsis were reported [13]. Other studies documented 4–5% discontinuation, and registry data confirmed SGLT2i to be ‘safe and well-tolerated’ [10], [11], [15]. None of the studies reported an exacerbation of arrhythmias with SGLT2 Inhibitors. Collectively, these findings suggest that SGLT2 inhibitors appear generally well-tolerated in both ATTR and AL CA at rates comparable to other heart failure trials. However, because adverse events were not pooled quantitatively and were reported inconsistently across studies, these safety observations are descriptive only and should be interpreted with caution.

Limitations

This study has some limitations, and conclusions about the use of SGLT2 inhibitors in this patient population require careful, individualized consideration. First, studies involving CA have an inherent survivor bias. SGLT2i would likely be prescribed only to those patients with CA who had survived long enough. This could falsely inflate the apparent mortality benefit, especially among the AL-CA population, where malignancy is the key driver of mortality. Schwegel et al. considered this possibility and adjusted their analysis for immortal time. In doing so, the study found no significant mortality benefit from SGLT2i therapy [11]. However, in contrast, a similar time-dependent analysis was performed by Porcari et al., who saw substantial benefits [10]. Second, all studies were done with SGLT2i added to novel targeted therapies for ATTR-CA and also to chemotherapy for AL-CA. In most studies, tafamidis was used for ATTR-CA, whereas a subset of patients in Porcari et al. and Lang et al. were treated with patisiran [10], [12]. Hence, the effects of SGLT2i are additive to those of novel targeted therapies for ATTR-CA, which have been shown to improve outcomes on their own. For instance, Byer et al. reported outcomes with SGLT2i used alongside tafamidis [15], underscoring that the observed associations are inseparable from concurrent disease-modifying therapy. All included studies were observational and hence susceptible to bias that are inherent in such studies. The number of studies included was small (n = 7), despite an exhaustive search strategy. It is unclear if these findings could be generalized to the population with AL-CA. Most studies included patients with predominantly ATTR-CA, with a small population affected by AL amyloidosis. Some outcomes, like quality of life, were not reported in the meta-analysis. Embase was not searched because the corresponding authors did not have institutional access; the search was therefore restricted to PubMed, the Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar, which may have missed studies indexed only in Embase. A review protocol was not pre-registered with PROSPERO or a comparable registry. Formal meta-regression was not performed because each outcome was informed by at most four studies, precluding stable estimation of between-study covariate effects; amyloid subtype was instead addressed through pre-specified stratified analyses (ATTR-only as the primary analysis and the combined any-CA cohort as secondary). Publication bias could not be reliably assessed: with only seven included studies, formal funnel-plot or Egger-test methods are underpowered, and an exploratory Egger's test for the mortality outcome (k = 4 studies, intercept = 2.03, p = 0.23) is reported in Supplementary Table S7 with the explicit caveat that it should not be interpreted definitively. Finally, although two of the included observational cohorts (Augusto and Byer) extended to 5-year follow-up, long-term outcomes from randomized controlled trials remain unavailable in this population.

Conclusions

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of non-randomized observational studies, SGLT2i use was associated with lower observed all-cause mortality and improvements in cardiac biomarkers in patients with CA, with renal benefits most evident in the ATTR subgroup. Available descriptive safety data suggest the class is generally well-tolerated in both ATTR and AL CA. Because every included study was observational and susceptible to confounding by indication, immortal time bias, and survivor bias, these findings should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than evidence of efficacy and do not constitute a treatment recommendation. Randomized controlled trials are required before SGLT2 inhibitors can be routinely recommended in CA, particularly in AL-CA, where the evidence base remains sparse. Further data on the role of SGLT2 inhibitors in CA — particularly in AL-CA, where the current evidence base remains sparse — are needed from adequately powered randomized trials before clinical use can be guideline-supported.
