New twists of a TAIL: novel insights into the histone binding properties of a highly conserved PHD finger cluster within the MLR family of H3K4 mono-methyltransferases
Authors: Claudia B Zraly, Richard Schultz, Manuel O Diaz, Andrew K Dingwall
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractEnhancer activation by the MLR family of H3K4 mono-methyltransferases requires proper recognition of histones for the deposition of the mono-methyl mark. MLR proteins contain two clusters of PHD zinc finger domains implicated in chromatin regulation. The second cluster is the most highly conserved, preserved as an ancient three finger functional unit throughout evolution. Studies of the isolated 3rd PHD finger within this cluster suggested specificity for the H4 [aa16–20] tail region. We determined the histone binding properties of the full three PHD finger cluster b module (PHDb) from the Drosophila Cmi protein which revealed unexpected recognition of an extended region of H3. Importantly, the zinc finger spacer separating the first two PHDb fingers from the third is critical for proper alignment and coordination among fingers for maximal histone engagement. Human homologs, MLL3 and MLL4, also show conservation of H3 binding, expanding current views of histone recognition for this class of proteins. We further implicate chromatin remodeling by the SWI/SNF complex as a possible mechanism for the accessibility of PHDb to globular regions of histone H3 beyond the tail region. Our results suggest a two-tail histone recognition mechanism by the conserved PHDb domain involving a flexible hinge to promote interdomain coordination.

INTRODUCTION

In vertebrates, one of the most frequently mutated class of proteins in a wide variety of cancers are the histone lysine methyltransferases (1,2). The methyltransferases are catalytic components of large complexes known as COMPASS (Complex of Proteins Associated with Set1) that methylate histone H3K4 and include the MLX complexes (KMT2A/MLL1, KMT2B/MLL2, Trx) which deposit a di- and tri-methyl mark at promoter regions, and the MLR complexes (KMT2C/MLL3, KMT2D/MLL4, Trr) which mono-methylate H3K4 at enhancers (3,4). The catalysis of mono-methyl K4 by MLR complexes, in combination with acetylation of H3K27 by the CBP/p300 histone acetyltransferase, serves to activate enhancers and guide gene expression programs for proper development (5,6). The MLR proteins contain several highly conserved domains including the SET methyltransferase, high mobility group, nuclear receptor interaction motifs, and 2 clusters of plant homeodomain (PHD) zinc fingers which are responsible for a variety of heterotypic protein-protein interactions. Compared to current knowledge of the functions of the SET methyltransferase activity, our insights are far more limited with respect to the role of the highly conserved PHD finger domains. It has been presumed that one of the key protein interaction modules targeted by the MLR PHD fingers is the mediation of contacts between the methyltransferase activity and chromatin. Thus, a characterization of the MLR complex PHD finger histone binding specificity is critical to our understanding of enhancer activation in normal development and the potential role of enhancer dysregulation in cancers.

PHD zinc fingers are found in a variety of chromatin regulatory proteins and are critical to the regulation of gene transcription (7). The importance of the PHD domains is underscored by the fact that mutations in the fingers can result in misinterpretation of epigenetic marks which has consequences in both developmental processes and disease outcomes (3,8). The individual PHD fingers are small protein domains typically consisting of 50–80 amino acids and contain a series of Cys4-His-Cys3 motifs that coordinate zinc atoms in a cross-brace topology (9). PHD fingers are found in both transcription activating (e.g. MLL family) (10) and repressing (e.g. KDM5, Lid demethylase) proteins (11,12) which bind to chromatin to either stabilize proteins on nucleosome substrates or to allow for subsequent modification of the histone tails (methylation/demethylation). These histone ‘reader’ domains preferentially bind modified or unmodified histone tails, typically H3 and H4 (13), as well as non-histone proteins, and certain DNA structures (14). PHD fingers can exist singularly or in multiple copies as well as in combination with other effector domains (15). Tandem PHD domains are typically present in proteins that are part of large protein complexes that associate with chromatin. Composite tandem PHD finger domains are associated with the lysine methyltransferases (10). In the MLX proteins, including human MLL1 and Drosophila trithorax proteins, a single 3-PHD finger domain that recognizes and binds H3K4me3, is followed by a bromodomain that recognizes acetylated lysines (16). In MLR proteins, including MLL3 and MLL4, and the Drosophila protein Cmi (also known as Lpt), two separate PHD domain clusters are present; however, the first PHD finger cluster (a) is more evolutionarily diverged than the second cluster (b) which has been conserved throughout evolution for millions of years. In MLL3 and Cmi, both clusters consist of 2 tandem PHD fingers separated from a 3rd PHD finger by a 30 amino acid spacer. In MLL4, the arrangement of the 3 PHD fingers and spacer in cluster b is conserved however, the third finger in cluster (a) is missing. The PHD fingers in cluster (a) of MLL3 do not bind histone tails (17), but rather associate with BAP-1 deubiquitinating complex proteins (2).

Characteristic residues at defined positions have allowed for a classification of PHD finger subtypes and predictions of histone binding preferences (18,19). For instance, the presence of an aromatic residue such as tryptophan (W) in the second β strand is often found in PHD fingers that recognize and bind H3K4me3. In proteins without tryptophan (nW), which typically bind unmodified H3, there is often a pair of aspartate (D) residues that form a structure known as the treble clef (DD). In addition, several conserved position-specific non-polar residues further define this subclass. Based on this designation, in MLR proteins triple PHD cluster b (PHDb.f1-3, referred to here as PHDb) consists of two subtypes: a PHD-nW (PHDb.f1) and a PHD-nW-DD (PHDb.f2) closely apposed, separated by only two amino acids, and a third C-terminal PHD (PHDb.f3) of the PHD-W subtype separated from the other two by a zinc finger spacer.

Although PHDb consists of a triple PHD finger module, studies of binding specificity have primarily focused on characterizations of the 3rd PHD finger (PHDb.f3). This is mainly due to its homology to well-described PHD fingers that bind methylated lysines of histone H3 (20), including an invariant tryptophan (W) residue that potentially can form a binding pocket to recognize H3K4me3 (13). Interestingly, despite conserved features, PHDb.f3 of the MLR proteins does not exhibit preferential binding to H3K4me3 (10,21,22). In fact, the human MLL3 and MLL4 PHDb fingers are reported to preferentially bind histone H4 tails (17). Two recent studies have focused primarily on the sole contribution of PHDb.f3 of human MLR proteins to H4 tail binding specificity. In a NMR spectral analysis of the isolated PHDb.f3 of MLL4, Zhang et al. reported selective binding of the single PHD finger to acetylated H4K16 and suggested conservation of selectivity for H4K16ac in the analogous finger of MLL3 (23). Similarly, the use of both ITC and structural studies with only an extended PHDb.f3 from MLL3 identified specific recognition of an H4H18 containing fragment of H4 with binding sensitivity to specific modifications surrounding this residue (22). However, the strong evolutionary conservation of the 3 finger unit of PHDb including the zinc finger spacer region, from primitive eukaryotes through vertebrates, strongly suggests an interdependence of functions within the cluster and emphasizes that studies of just a single PHD finger of the larger cluster could be misleading.

It is important to note that in humans, both PHD finger clusters (a and b) and SET methyltransferase functions are present in a single large protein. However, in Drosophila and other dipterans an ancient split resulted in the separation of the two, such that the PHD clusters are present in one protein-Cmi, and in Trr resides the SET domain and its associated methyltransferase activity (21). This gives the study of the Drosophila proteins a unique perspective in allowing for the examination of the PHD finger domains involved in chromatin binding in isolation from the enzymatic function. For instance, null mutations of cmi are lethal in the early larval stages prior to a major metamorphic hormone pulse suggesting critical functions during transitional developmental programs (6). We further showed through examination of polytene chromosomes in cmi knockdown animals that Cmi and its associated PHD fingers are not required for Trr localization on chromatin. Interestingly, we found that the mono-methylation of H3K4 was much reduced in the absence of Cmi, implying that the PHD fingers are essential for Trr methyltransferase function (6).

In this study, we focus on characterizing the histone binding specificity of the highly conserved PHDb cluster of Cmi from Drosophila and show conservation of binding specificity with the human MLR proteins. By considering the contribution of all 3 PHD fingers within the cluster b module, we describe for the first time the preferential binding not only to H4 tails, but additionally, present evidence suggesting a previously unrecognized ‘long’ engagement of the PHDb fingers with N-terminal tails and internal regions of H3. Using genetic and biochemical approaches, we implicate the Brm (SWI/SNF) remodeling complex in facilitating the recognition and binding of Cmi to histones. Further, we suggest that the zinc finger spacer separating PHDb.f1and f2 from PHDb.f3 serves as a flexible hinge that brings the 3 PHD fingers into proper alignment/juxtaposition for augmentation of histone binding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protein preparation and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) measurements

Recombinant Cmi PHD finger-MBP fusion proteins were overexpressed and purified by affinity chromatography on amylose resin (NEB) followed by TEV protease treatment (Millipore Sigma) to remove the His-MBP tag. The protein was further purified from the His-tagged TEV protease and the cleaved His-MBP tag by Ni-NTA resin (Qiagen). Size exclusion chromatography was performed for additional purification, as necessary. Purified proteins were dialyzed in a buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 50 uM ZnCl2, 0.5 mM TCEP followed by concentration using Amicon centrifugal filter units. ITC measurements were performed using a Nano-ITC microcalorimeter (TA, Inc.). Histone peptides (1–2 mM) (Peptide 2.0; Genscript) were injected into a sample of either wild type or mutant Cmi PHD finger proteins (50–100 μM) in a reaction buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM NaCl, 50 uM ZnCl2 and 0.5 mM TCEP. A typical titration consisted of 20 injections of 2.49 μl of peptide into 170 μl of protein solution at time intervals of 300 s. A reference titration for each peptide into buffer was subtracted from the data to correct for heat of dilution. ITC experiments were repeated 3 times and data was analyzed using the Independent model within the Nano-analyze (TA, Inc.) software. An extra tyrosine residue was added to the C-terminus of histone peptides to accurately determine peptide concentration by A260nm measurement. The sequences of the histone peptides used in the ITC binding assays are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Thermodynamic parameters for ITC experiments are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Biotinylated peptide and recombinant histone pulldown assay

Streptavidin beads (Pierce) were blocked with peptide binding buffer (BB) (50 mM Tris pH 7.5, 150 mM or 300 mM NaCl, 0.1% Nonidet P-40) containing BSA before incubation with 1μg biotinylated histone peptides (Anaspec; Epicypher; Innopep), or 1ug biotinylated full length recombinant histone H3.2 (Active Motif) or 5 pmoles biotinylated recombinant mononucleosomes (Epicypher) in 300 μl of BB for 1 h at 4°C. The peptide or recombinant histone bound beads were washed and then incubated with 2 μg of purified MBP-tagged Cmi, MLL3 or MLL4 PHD fusion proteins in BB overnight at 4°C. Immobilized complexes were collected by centrifugation, washed extensively with BB, resuspended in 2× Laemmli sample buffer (BioRad), boiled and loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE gels. Western analysis was performed using antibody to MBP [DSHB-MBP-2A1] (Developmental Hybridoma). MBP-PHD fusions without histone peptides served as negative controls. For assays with recombinant H3, H4 and H3/H4 tetramers (no biotin tag) MBP Cmi fusion proteins and control were bound to amylose resin in equivalent amounts and incubated with recombinant histones overnight at 4°C. After extensive washing, SDS-PAGE analysis was performed and bound recombinant histones were detected by Coomassie Blue staining. All pulldown experiments were performed at least three times.

Native histone binding assays

Bulk native histones were prepared from Drosophila embryo insoluble nuclear extracts by acid extraction with 0.4 N HCl overnight at 4°C. After centrifugation, the acid soluble supernatant containing the cellular histones was precipitated with acetone using 5x volume overnight at −20°C, spun and resuspended in binding buffer (50 mM Tris pH7.5, 300 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40). For native histone pull down assays, MBP-Cmi fusion proteins and control MBP were bound to amylose resin in equivalent amounts as assessed by Coomassie Blue gel staining and incubated with native histones overnight at 4°C. After extensive washing, bound histones were detected by Western blots using antibodies specific for histone H3 and H4 (Abcam).

Site-directed mutagenesis

Mutations in the PHD fingers of Cmi, MLL3 and MLL4 were generated using a QuikChange Lightning site directed mutagenesis kit (Agilent Technologies) according to manufacturer's protocols. Mutations were verified by DNA sequencing and mutant proteins were purified as described above. All primers are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

The bacterial expression plasmids encompassing the PHDb domains of Cmi, MLL3, MLL4 were constructed using the pMCSG23 vector (DNASU) by the T4 ligase independent method (24) to generate N-terminal MBP, His tagged fusion proteins. Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Larvae of the appropriate genotype were collected and freshly prepared according to methods described in (25) with minor modifications. In brief, approximately 100 blue gut larvae, sufficient for at least four independent immunoprecipitations, from UAS-brmK804R/GAWB69B and GAWB69B control animals were homogenized in buffer A [60 mM KCl, 15 mM NaCl, 4 mM MgCl2, 15 mM HEPES (pH 7.6), 0.5% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, 10 mM sodium butyrate, cOmplete Mini, EDTA-free protease inhibitors (Sigma)] and 1.8% formaldehyde at room temperature with a dounce homogenizer, transferred to tubes and incubated with rotating for a total time of 15 min. Crosslinking was stopped by the addition of glycine to 225 mM for 5 min. Tubes were put on ice and clarified at 1 K for 5 min to remove cuticles. The supernatant was removed, and the pellet was washed with buffer A followed by centrifugation. Washes with buffer A were repeated an additional 3 times and pellet was next washed with lysis buffer [140 mM NaCl, 15 mM HEPES pH 7.6, 1 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM EGTA, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5 mM DTT, 0.1% sodium deoxycholate, 0.05% SDS, 10 mM sodium butyrate, protease inhibitors] without SDS. The washed pellet was resuspended in lysis buffer with SDS to 0.1% concentration, with protease inhibitors and incubation was continued for 10 min at 4°C on a rotating platform. Chromatin was sheared using a Branson 250 sonifier until an average fragment size range of 200–500 bp was achieved. The solubilized chromatin fraction was cleared by centrifugation, aliquoted in tubes and used for immunoprecipitation either immediately or frozen at −80°C until sufficient material was collected. Prior to immunoprecipitation, chromatin fractions were diluted 10× in ChIP dilution buffer [16.7 MM Tris pH 8.1, 167 mM NaCl, 1.1% Trition X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 0.01% SDS] and pre-cleared with salmon sperm DNA/protein A beads (Millipore 16-157C) with rotation for 1 h at 4°C. A portion of each diluted sample (1%) was saved to quantitate the amount of DNA present and subsequently used as the input fraction. Immunoprecipitation was performed with antibodies to H3 [Abcam ab1791], H3K4me1 [Active Motif 39297], H3K27ac [Active Motif 39133], brm (26), Cmi (6) and trr Ab (27) and IgG [Abcam ab171870] as negative control. IP reactions were incubated overnight at 4°C on a rotating platform. Salmon sperm DNA/protein A beads were added to capture the antibody-chromatin complexes with rotation for at least 1 h at 4°C. The chromatin/antibody bead complexes were collected by centrifugation and beads were washed for 5 min at 4°C with rotation using the following buffers: low salt buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.1, 150 mM NaCl, 1% TritonX-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 0.01% SDS), high salt buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.1, 500 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 1.2 mM EDTA, 0.01% SDS), LiCl buffer (10 mM Tris pH 8.1, 0.25 M LiCl, 1% IGEPAL, 1% deoxycholic acid, 1 mM EDTA) followed by two washes with TE wash buffer. Histone complexes were eluted using a buffer containing 1% SDS, and 1 M NaHCO3 and histone–DNA crosslinks from samples and input fraction were reversed at 65°C for at least 4 h, followed by proteinase K treatment (Promega V302B) for 1 h at 45°C. DNA was recovered by phenol/chloroform extraction and ethanol precipitation with 20 ug of glycogen as carrier, and pellets were finally resuspended in TE buffer for qPCR analysis. ChIP-qPCR experiments were performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master mix (Applied Biosystems A25742) and analyzed on a QuantStudio6™ Flex Real-Time PCR System. Triplicates from three independent biological replicates were analyzed using the 2–ΔΔCt method. IgG was used as a negative control. Data is expressed as fold enrichment compared to IgG control and relative to input chromatin. Data are presented as the mean standard deviation (error bars). Statistical values were calculated by an unpaired Student's t test. *P-value ≤0.05; **P-value ≤0.01 indicate statistically significant changes. Primer sequences were manufactured by IDT technologies and are listed in Supplementary Table S3.

Drosophila strains and genetic manipulations

Drosophila crosses were performed at either 25°C or 29°C with flies grown on standard yeast/dextrose/cornmeal food. shRNAi knockdowns were performed by crossing a UAS-Gal4 driver line to the appropriate inverted repeat (IR)-containing line and scoring the progeny (21). Drosophila stocks containing UAS-Gal4 drivers, gene mutations or inverted repeats were as described or obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center: UAS-Dcr2;UAS-cmi-IR (21), UAS-Cmi (21), (UAS-trr-IR (#25963), UAS-brm[K804R] (28), snr1[R3] (29), P{GawB}C765-Gal4 (#36523).

RESULTS

The PHDb domain of Cmi is composed of 3 PHD fingers and is evolutionarily conserved

The MLR proteins including human proteins MLL3, MLL4 and Drosophila Cmi are unique among PHD finger proteins in having two sets of tandem PHD modules residing in cluster a and cluster b (Figure 1A) (21). A dendrogram depiction of the evolutionary relatedness among tandem PHD finger proteins suggests that cluster b is the most highly conserved of the two PHD finger modules (Figure 1B, Supplementary Figure S1A). The MLR PHDb cluster also contains a closely spaced third finger PHD domain (PHDb.f3) that is also conserved, but to a lesser extent. This PHDb.f3 harbors many of the conserved features present in PHD fingers that preferentially bind methylated H3K4, including an invariant tryptophan (W) residue which is important for H3K4me3 recognition. However, recent experimental evidence suggests that the isolated PHDb.f3 does not bind H3K4me3 (10,21,22), but instead preferentially binds H4 tails (17,22,23). It is important to note, however, that a striking feature from MLR protein amino acid sequence comparisons is that PHD finger cluster b is more highly conserved as a 3 PHD finger unit than is the isolated third finger. Moreover, as shown in Figure 1C and Supplementary Figure S2, amino acid comparisons suggest that PHDb fingers 1 and 2 are the most highly conserved of the three finger cluster. Importantly, the PHDb cluster, including the spacer region, is an ancient module, having been kept together and organized as a unit throughout eukaryotic evolution (Supplementary Figure S1B), implying that the 3 fingers are inter-dependent and must coordinate their functions. PHD cluster (a) is not only less conserved, but the third PHD finger is missing from MLL4, suggesting that PHD cluster (a) serves a distinctly different role than PHD cluster (b). Therefore, due to its evolutionary significance as well as extensive amino acid sequence conservation, we directed our attention to characterizing the full 3 PHD finger module in PHDb using Drosophila as a model system to gain insight into its potential role in targeting MLR proteins to chromatin.

PHDb binding to the H4 tail is evolutionarily conserved and is sensitive to acetylation of specific H4 lysine residues

We cloned, expressed and purified the PHDb cassette from MLL3, MLL4 and Cmi as MBP fusion proteins. Like its mammalian counterparts MLL3 and MLL4, the Cmi PHDb MBP fusion protein binds to unmodified H4 tail peptides and is inhibited by symmetrically di-methylated R3, with a preference for the asymmetric orientation (Figure 2A, and Supplementary Figure S3C). Further characterizations of the effects of common H4 tail modifications by histone peptide pull down assay suggest that Cmi PHDb binding was not disrupted when H4R3 was monomethylated or when H4K20 was methylated (Supplementary Figure S3A); however, acetylation of key lysine residues diminished binding to the H4 tail peptides (Figure 2B). Interestingly, whereas acetylation of lysine 5, 8 and 12 residues on the H4 tail are inhibitory, an acetyl mark on lysine 16 is tolerated except when present on a tetra-acetylated H4 tail. Even a single acetylated residue (e.g. H4K12ac) in combination with H4K16ac resulted in diminished binding, yet a single methylated lysine (K20) in combination with K16ac had no effect (Supplementary Figure S3B). Both MLL3 and MLL4 PHDb MBP fusions share a similar inhibition to binding to the H4 tail in the presence of K5ac, K8ac and K12ac and like Cmi, K16ac had no effect on binding to the H4 tail peptide (Figure 2B; Supplementary Figure S3D). This is in contrast to other reports showing selective binding of the single third finger of cluster b (designated as PHDf6) of MLL4 to H4K16ac (23). Quantitation of the interaction by Isothermal Calorimetry demonstrated that Cmi and MLL3 PHDb proteins bound the unmodified H4 [aa1–25] tail with similar affinities, with Kd values measured at 5.8 and 6.6 μM, respectively (Figure 2C, D). The sensitivity of Cmi PHDb to acetylated lysines was further corroborated by ITC measurements in which no binding was detected for H4K5, K8 and K12 acetylated H4 [aa1–25] peptides, nor for both Cmi and MLL3 for the double acetylated K12,K16 H4 [aa1–25] peptide (Figure 2E, Supplementary Figure S4). Consistent with the results from the histone peptide pull down assays, ITC measurements of the interaction of Cmi and MLL3 PHDb with the K16ac H4 [aa1–25] peptide detected Kd values of 14 and 16 μM, respectively.

Results from these experiments suggest that MLL3, MLL4 and Cmi PHDb fingers sense the acetylation status of chromatin and are particularly antagonized by acetylation of H4 residues K5, K8 and K12. Since acetylation is a common modification of histone H4, we addressed whether this antagonism is observed on chromatin in an in vivo setting. Analysis of genome wide enrichment patterns of Cmi and H4K16ac in cultured embryonic Drosophila S2 cells revealed that H4K16ac (modENCODE_316, modENCODE_320) was at least 2 times more prevalent than Cmi. Cmi was only localized (±1 kb) at 12% of H4K16ac sites, while ∼30% of Cmi sites were associated with H4K16ac (6). We next examined a well characterized target of COMPASS complex regulation in Drosophila, the E74A gene, for which developmental ChIP seq analysis of Cmi binding to enhancers in this region has been well described (6). As can be seen in Figure 2F, while ChIP-Seq signals for Cmi protein are surrounded by regions rich in acetylated H4 lysines, Cmi binding peaks are largely centered in locations devoid of acetyl H4 marks, thus lending further support to our in vitro experiments that detect a sensitivity to select acetylated H4 marks.

MLR PHDb fingers bind both H3 and H4 and function together as a unit for proper recognition

Our early binding studies with the isolated PHDb.f3 of Cmi, using histone peptides, found that it was able to bind unmodified H3 and mono- and di-methylated H3K4 tails but not H3K4me3 peptides (21). Likewise, the inability of PHDb.f3 of MLL3 and MLL4 to bind H3K4me3 was supported by chemical shift experiments (10) and by recent modeling studies using a H3K4 peptide and the extended PHDb.f3 domains of MLL3/MLL4 (22). However, since studies conducted so far have relied heavily on measuring interactions of the isolated PHDb.f3 finger with peptides containing defined modifications on histone tails and did not include additional (internal) regions nor a systematic study of all 4 histones, we decided to more closely examine the binding affinity of intact PHDb finger cluster of Drosophila Cmi using native histones. In agreement with the peptide binding studies, histone H4 was effectively pulled down in this assay (Figure 3A). H3 was also pulled down either as a consequence of an indirect interaction, with H3 being brought down via H4 by formation of H3/H4 dimers, or alternatively by direct binding of Cmi to H3 itself. To distinguish between these possibilities we performed pull down assays using full length recombinant H3 and H4 individually, as well as H3/H4 dimers. Significantly, as shown in Figure 3B, in addition to selective binding to H4, our results confirm that Cmi PHDb exhibits a direct interaction with H3. We then tested MLL3 and MLL4 in a pull down assay using a biotinylated full length H3 recombinant protein and observed conservation of histone H3 binding. (Figure 3C).

Since our studies suggest that PHDb fingers are capable of binding H3 and H4, we next sought to determine whether all three PHD fingers cooperate in H3/H4 binding. We cloned and expressed combined MBP fusions for Cmi PHDb.f1f2, as well as the individual PHDb.f3 for comparison with the intact PHDb. We used a limiting concentration of native histones so we could sensitize our assay to detect small differences in histone binding affinity. Results from these experiments suggest that while the separated PHDb fingers bound histones, the most robust pull down of histone H3 and H4 was exhibited when all 3 PHD fingers were combined (Figure 3D). Further verification, using just a single biotinylated recombinant histone H3, showed a similar trend and revealed that the limited interaction of the PHDb.f1f2 and PHDb.f3 fingers detected was sensitive to increasing salt concentration in the assay (150mM vs. 300mM NaCl; Figure 3E). The results of these experiments suggest that that all 3 PHDb fingers of Cmi cooperate as a functional unit for proper recognition of both H3 and H4.

To determine whether there was a portion of the H3 N-terminal tail specific for interaction, we used a series of H3 biotinylated peptides spanning distinct regions and tested for binding by PHDb of Cmi in a peptide pull down assay. As can be seen in Figure 3F, we detected binding to peptides containing unmodified H3 [aa1–21] and H3 [aa22–44] with minimal binding to H3 [aa44–63]. Next, we tested peptides that span common modifications found within the H3 [aa1–21] and [aa22–44) tail regions. As summarized in the schematic in Figure 3G (see data in Supplementary Figure S3), there was no specific affinity for histone marks tested within these regions compared to unmodified H3 tails; however, binding was particularly sensitive to certain modifications. H3 tail peptides containing increasing methylation states for K9, K27, K36 did not markedly alter binding; however, Cmi binding was unaffected by H3R2 monomethylation, yet sensitive to asymmetric dimethylation (Supplementary Figure S3E). Sensitivity to asymmetric dimethylation of H3R2 was also observed in MLL3 and MLL4 to varying degrees. Consistent with our earlier studies, H3K4me3 reduced binding of Cmi PHDb, (Supplementary Figure S3E), although not to same degree as was observed with the isolated third finger (21). Binding was unaffected by acetylation of N-terminal H3 lysine residues K5, K9, K14 and K23; however, Cmi PHDb binding was sensitive to phosphorylation of H3S28, as well as acetylation of H3K27, a common mark of active enhancers (Supplementary Figure S3F). Sensitivity to H3K27ac was also conserved in MLL3 and MLL4. This is surprising since MLR proteins recruit the H3K27 acetyltransferase CBP/p300 and reside in chromatin rich in acetylated H3K27 (6,30). Thus, similar to Cmi's sensitivity to select acetylated H4 marks, these observations with acetylated H3 further suggest that in early development, Cmi PHDb may preferentially bind to certain unmodified regions of histones H3/H4 prior to their acetylation.

MLR PHDb binds to an extended region of the H3 tail

The observation that Cmi was capable of binding separate H3 peptides to varying degrees which together span a wide region of H3 prompted us to inquire whether a longer continuous region of H3 present on the same peptide would enhance binding. We envisioned that it was possible that the individual PHD fingers of Cmi may engage distinct regions of the H3 tail with different affinities, such that when the separated regions were combined on a single peptide, a synergic effect for the longer peptide may ensue. Alternatively, an extended histone peptide may simply provide more binding surfaces for the PHD fingers to engage with and bind, and the effect would be additive for a longer peptide. To explore these possibilities, we tested increasingly longer H3 peptides for PHD finger binding, all unmodified (Figure 4A). Interestingly, a comparison of the H3 [aa1–21] peptide to a longer H3 [aa1–43] peptide showed enhanced pull down by Cmi PHDb and correlated closely with the regions encompassed by our previous assays with the single peptides (H3 [aa1–21] and [aa21–44]). A longer peptide H3 [aa1–50] showed even greater pull down than the H3 [aa1–43] peptide indicating that binding still occurred beyond the region covered by the 2 separate peptides H3 [aa1–21] and H3 [aa22–44]. In addition, pull downs with MLL3 and MLL4 PHDb proteins showed identical results with enhanced binding to the [aa1–50] H3 region (Figure 4B). Importantly, however, a comparison of the separated Cmi PHDb fingers showed similar specificity for H3 [aa1–21] and H3 [aa22–44] regions, yet also exhibited enhanced pull down of the extended H3 [1–50] peptide, as did the full PHD finger fusion protein (Figure 4C). Thus, these results suggest that it is unlikely that there is bipartite recognition of the H3 [aa1–50] tail by the individual fingers, PHDb.f1f2 and PHDb.3. Consistent with the data from the histone pull down experiments, ITC quantification of the unmodified H3 [aa1–21] peptide with Cmi PHDb protein displayed weak binding; whereas, the unmodified H3 [aa1–50] regions measured a dissociation constant (Kd) of 2.1 μM (Figure 4D) which is in line with Kd measurements for the H4 [aa1–25] peptide (Kd of 5.8 μM). Altogether these results suggest that MLR PHDb recognizes and binds to an extended region of the H3 tail.

The zinc coordinating cysteine residues in the spacer region are necessary for appropriate recognition of H3 and H4

Only two amino acids separate PHDb.f1 and PHDb.f2 followed by a 30 amino acid zinc finger spacer which precedes PHDb.f3. An examination of the amino acid sequence of the zinc finger spacer among the 3 MLR family members suggests that there is limited evolutionary conservation compared to the PHD fingers themselves; however, both the spacer length as well as the 4 zinc coordinating cysteines are well conserved (Figure 5A). Our previous experiments with native as well as recombinant full-length histones suggesting that all 3 PHD fingers contribute to binding (Figure 3D, E) prompted us to decipher the contribution of the zinc finger spacer itself. We tested MBP fusion constructs of Cmi PHDb.f1f2 and PHDb.f3 both with and without spacer for H3 and H4 tail binding. As shown in Supplementary Figure S5A and B, the zinc finger spacer in Cmi is essential for PHDb.f3 binding to both biotinylated H3 and H4 peptides. The zinc finger spacer was also necessary for MLL3 PHDb.f3 binding to H4 tail peptides (Supplementary Figure S5C), confirming the findings of Liu et al. (22) which affirm the importance of an extended PHDb.f3 domain for binding of MLL3 to H4, including the zinc finger spacer region. Next, we examined the importance of the zinc coordinating cysteines by systematically mutating them to alanine within the full length PHDb of Cmi. Since we observed Cmi binding to an extended region of H3, we reasoned that binding reactions utilizing either full-length recombinant H3 and/or recombinant nucleosomes may be more representative substrates for histone binding, rather than tail peptides. Unexpectedly, however, comparisons of Cmi PHDb binding suggest that its interaction with mononucleosomes is much weaker as compared to binding to recombinant H3 (Supplementary Figure S5D, E) or even H3 [aa1–50] tail peptides (Figure 4A). Similar observations noting differences in binding to nucleosomes versus histone tail peptides have also been made by others (31,32) and has been largely attributed to the accessibility of the histone tail for binding in free peptides as opposed to the intact nucleosome. For instance, NMR studies suggest that the conformation adopted by H3 tails in nucleosome binding assays was inhibitory to BPTF PHD finger binding because the histone tail was strongly engaged with the nucleosome core through interaction with DNA unless specifically modified (e.g. lysine acetylation; arginine citrullination) to weaken tail associations with DNA (31). Since recognition of the histone tail by Cmi PHDb appears to be similarly impeded in the context of the nucleosome, for these studies we utilized a recombinant full-length H3 to ensure adequate binding comparisons. As a control for the effects of cysteine mutagenesis (Figure 5B; Supplementary Figure S5D), we tested our PHD finger constructs without spacer for binding, analogous to the experiments performed in Figure 3E, but in that case using PHDb constructs with zinc finger spacer intact. As expected, the separated Cmi PHDb.f3 MBP fusion protein without the zinc finger spacer did not bind the biotinylated recombinant histone H3 while the Cmi PHDb.f1f2 bound the recombinant H3 weakly compared to full length PHDb. As shown in Figure 5C and Supplementary Figure S5E, binding to the H3 recombinant was further diminished with increasing number of cysteines mutated. We confirmed our findings on native histones (Figure 5D) and found that both H3 and H4 binding was greatly reduced when just 3 or all 4 of the zinc coordinating histones were mutated to alanine.

Results of these experiments suggest that zinc coordination of the 4 cysteine residues in the zinc finger spacer is essential for Cmi PHDb binding to H3 and H4. Further, the effects of loss of cysteine coordination within the intact spacer region mimics the lack of histone binding observed when the individual PHD fingers are separated from the full cluster (Figure 3E). Thus, in the presence of chromatin, the zinc finger spacer that links PHDb.f1f2 and PHDb.f3 may serve a structural role in the collaboration of the fingers, promoting the interaction of PHDb fingers 1 and 2 with finger 3, thereby increasing the overall affinity of PHDb for a histone substrate.

Mutagenesis within individual fingers of PHDb reveal residues important for histone binding

Our findings that MLR PHDb proteins bind H3, in addition to H4, is perhaps not surprising as amino acid sequence comparisons of the most conserved PHDb fingers, f1 and f2, suggest that the human and Drosophila proteins share many of the same features with tandem PHD finger proteins that bind H3 (Figure 6A). These include a conserved hydrophobic pocket in PHDb.f1, acidic pockets in PHDb.f2, as well as a conserved H3A1 binding pocket in PHDb.f2 (33–35). Close inspection of PHDb.f1f2 reveals that in contrast to PHDb.f3, the first 2 fingers lack the characteristic tryptophan (W) typically found in H3K4me3 readers (20). Particularly noteworthy are a pair of aspartate (DD) residues in acidic pocket 1 of PHDb.f2 that have the capacity to form a treble clef structure (19). For this reason, Cmi PHDb.f2 would be classified as subtype PHD-nW-DD, PHDb.f1, as subtype PHD-nW and PHDb.f3 as subtype W-PHD, despite lack of H3K4me3 binding specificity. In addition, many of the non-polar residues shown to be critical for the PHD-nW-DD class of tandem PHD proteins to bind chromatin appear to be conserved in Cmi PHDb.f2 (19). In particular, conservation of the pair of aspartate residues that form the treble clef in PHDb.f2 prompted us to investigate whether mutation of these residues would lead to alteration in Cmi PHDb histone tail binding. In DPF3b the Asp residues of the treble clef (xCDxCDx) anchor the R2 residue of histone H3 as well as the R17-K20 residues of the H4 tail (34). We mutagenized D597 and D600 to alanine (Figure 6B) and performed histone tail and recombinant H3 pull down assays, as well as ITC measurements. Results of the histone tail pull down experiments suggest that the PHD-nW-DD subtype-specific aspartate residues (D597 and D600) participate in differential H3 and H4 tail recognition, and surprisingly, the D597A mutation showed specific reduction in H3 binding, while the D600A mutant showed reduced H4 tail binding (Supplementary Figure S6). These results were confirmed using biotinylated recombinant H3 in pull down assays (Figure 6C) and ITC measurements (Figure 6D, Supplementary Figure S7). The D600A mutation measured no binding to a H4 [aa1–25] peptide, while the D597A mutation measured a Kd of 27μM compared to 5.8 μM for WT. To further delineate the residues within the PHD fingers important for interacting with H3 and H4, we also introduced point mutations in various residues that are conserved among the 3 MLR proteins, particularly charged residues, along with critical non-polar residues well conserved in PHD-nW-DD subtype proteins (19) including the PHDb.f2 of Cmi, MLL3 and MLL4. In addition, we took advantage of several missense mutations within PHDb of MLL3 and MLL4, reported in Cancer databases (cBioPortal), and made the corresponding changes to conserved residues in Cmi at identical positions (see Figure 6B; Supplementary Table S4). It is likely that a number of the individual cancer mutations may cause misfolding of one of the PHD fingers and thus affect histone interactions. Initially, candidate mutations were screened using a biotinylated H4 or H3 peptide assay (Supplementary Table S4). Mutations that affected binding include D542K, C559Y, C559F, C554A in PHDb.f1; D597A, D600A, E598A, E582A, P616L in PHDb.f2; W680A, E669A, E640A, K630Q in PHDb.f3 and C631A, C652A, C628A, C649A in the zinc finger spacer. Thus, while only a limited number of PHD finger mutations had a noticeable effect on binding in our initial pull down assays, these experiments were still informative for identifying residues which potentially make crucial contacts with histones and those which may affect the tertiary structure of the folded PHDb. Representative mutations that showed the most significant effects in peptide binding assays were further screened using a H3 recombinant assay and ITC measurements. In addition to the PHDb.f2 aspartate mutations (D597A, D600A), these included the conserved tryptophan (W680A) in PHDb.f3, a corresponding PHDb.f1 missense cancer mutation (D542K) found in MLL3(E971K), as well as a conserved charged residue (E669A) found in all 3 MLR PHDb.f3. As shown in Figure 6C, D and Supplementary Figure S7, all of these mutations had a pronounced effect on histone binding as measured by H3 recombinant assay and ITC measurements. It is important to note that each of these mutations were single amino acid changes in the context of the full-length Cmi PHDb protein. Thus, most importantly, our mutagenesis studies reveal that even a single mutation in just one of the three fingers can affect interaction with H3 or H4 and suggest the importance of coordination among the three PHD fingers for effective histone binding.

Cmi interacts genetically with a chromatin remodeler

Surprisingly, the H3 sequence [aa1–50] preferentially bound by MLR PHDb proteins in our assays encompasses a region of H3 that is typically engaged with nucleosomal DNA. While residues [aa1–38] are located in the tail region, residues [aa39–43] pass through the DNA gyres and residues [aa45–56] are part of the alpha N helix which is located within the nucleosome core, near the DNA exit and entry point (36). This suggests that for MLR PHDb proteins to bind to residues beyond the H3 tail region, chromatin would have to be made accessible for interaction to occur. Accessibility of chromatin can be altered in a variety of ways including when histone octamers are disrupted during S phase of DNA replication, during transcription, recombination and histone variant exchange as well as during chromatin remodeling events. Chromatin remodeling complexes such as the SWI/SNF complex use the energy of ATP hydrolysis to destabilize nucleosome structure (37). It is interesting to note that the human SWI/SNF subunits, Brg1 and Brm, can also associate with globular regions of H3 (38), particularly the alpha N helix. In yeast, H3 residues K56 (39) and L61 (40) appear to be critical for SWI/SNF recruitment. Furthermore, the interaction of Brg1 with the alpha N helix has been shown to be essential for chromatin remodeling by hSWI/SNF and, most relevantly, facilitates the loading of the heterochromatin protein, HP1, onto the exit/entry point of the nucleosome barrel, normally inaccessible to binding due to occlusion by nucleosome structure (38). To investigate whether a remodeling complex may facilitate interaction of Cmi PHD fingers with this inaccessible region of the chromatin, we performed genetic interaction tests with the ATPase chromatin remodeler, Brm (SWI/SNF) using our sensitive wing phenotype assay. Depletion of either cmi or trr in the developing Drosophila wing using tissue-directed RNAi results in specific wing phenotypes, including retraction of the L2 and L5 longitudinal veins, and anterior wing bristle disruption with diminished trr function compared to wild type wings (Figure 7A, C, F) (21,41). These phenotypes are genetically enhanced by further reductions in MLR complex functions. In contrast, wing-specific overexpression of Cmi (HA-Cmi) produces ectopic veins that are suppressed by reducing MLR complex functions (21). We used this sensitive assay to determine whether cmi and trr genetically cooperate with members of the Brahma (SWI/SNF) ATP-dependent chromatin remodeling complex. We employed both overexpression of a dominant negative Brm ATPase loss of function (UAS-brmK804R) and a recessive null allele of another Brm complex subunit Snr1 (snr1R3) (29,42,43). The brmK804 dominant negative mutant contains a single amino acid change that eliminates Brm ATPase activity, yet is still properly assembled into complexes (43). Expression of the UAS-brmK804R in the wing shows loss of veins and wing notching (Figure 7B). Combinations of the UAS-brmK804R and UAS-cmi-IR or UAS-trr-IR exhibited substantial loss of wing veins and increased wing notching (Figure 7D,G), strongly suggestive of genetic interaction. Although milder, loss of Snr1 function also enhanced loss of cmi and trr functions (Figure 7E,H). Importantly, expression of the UAS-brmK804R suppressed the effects of overexpressing Cmi (HA-Cmi) (Figure 7J). Together, these assays suggest that the MLR complex cooperates with the Brm (SWI/SNF) complex to regulate gene transcription and that impaired chromatin remodeling enhances the effects of reduced MLR function.

Our results, as shown in Figure 7 and Table 1, suggest that in Drosophila, Brm and Cmi interact genetically. As another test of the relationship between Brm and Cmi/Trr, we wanted to determine whether COMPASS complex and BRM co-localize on chromatin and if so, whether binding by Cmi would be impacted if Brm ATPase function was impaired. We again took advantage of the brmK804dominant negative mutant and performed ChIP experiments on both wild type and mutant animals on a target of MLR regulation, the Drosophila E74 gene, to determine whether there was co-localization of Brm and Cmi at an identified enhancer within the region. Brm plays a critical role in remodeling nucleosomes at enhancer regions (44) and BAF complexes have been shown to colocalize with MLL4 on active enhancers during adipogenesis (45). As shown in Figure 7K, we observed binding by Brm at the E74 enhancer in both WT and mutant animals; however, the Brm ChIP signal in the brmK804Rmutant animals was reduced by about half compared to WT. As expected, both Cmi and Trr co-localize to the E74 enhancer region (6) and most significantly, we also detected a similar reduction in binding by both Cmi and Trr proteins in the brmK804R mutant animals compared to WT. Thus the concurrent residence of Brm and Cmi/Trr on a developmentally induced enhancer is in accord with our genetic data and further suggests a positive relationship between MLR complex binding to chromatin and Brm (SWI/SNF) complex functions.

DISCUSSION

Strong conservation of the three PHD fingers and the Zn-finger spacer region as a unit in the MLR complex PHDb cluster may be interpreted as evolutionary retention of interdependent functions in chromatin control. This view, together with the large number of missense mutations found affecting PHDb in a variety of cancers suggest that these functions are critical for enhancer regulation and cellular homeostasis. We have characterized the chromatin recognition/interaction capacity of the conserved PHDb cluster of the MLR class of proteins, comparing both the Drosophila and human clusters and reveal that in fact, these patterns are indeed retained and we provide three novel insights important for chromatin regulation in higher eukaryotes. First, we identified a previously unrecognized engagement of MLR PHDb with histone H3. A survey of possible contact points using defined portions of the H3 tail suggest that the PHDb fingers from Cmi engage with a broad, rather than single discrete H3 sequence. Our peptide binding studies and ITC data with a long H3 peptide [aa1–50], which enhanced histone binding/recognition, combined with the PHDb finger's sensitivity to particular histone modifications throughout the [aa1–44] region of H3 suggests that the PHD fingers make extensive contacts along the H3 tail. The extended [aa1–50] peptide includes residues [aa39–43] of H3 which pass through the DNA gyres and residues [aa45–50] are part of the α-N helix which is located within the nucleosome core, near the DNA exit and entry point. Interestingly, this section of H3 is normally engaged with DNA and is relatively inaccessible, and our genetic interaction data with the dominant negative UAS-brmK804R and cmi/trr knockdowns suggests that the Brm ATP-dependent remodeling complex likely cooperates with the MLR complex to increase the accessibility of portions of H3 for MLR interaction. Further support comes from our ChIP data, which indicates enhanced binding of Cmi/Trr to nucleosomes that have been remodeled by Brm, thereby substantiating our in vitro binding studies. It is also interesting to note that in nucleosome binding assays with Cmi PHDb, we observed that the binding signals obtained from nucleosome pull downs were much reduced relative to similar assays using histone tail peptides or free histones. Previous studies (31,32) have revealed that regions of H3 and H4 beyond the tail region are largely associated with nucleosomal DNA and therefore are not as accessible as they are in a free histone or tail peptide. These findings reinforce our hypothesis that in the context of a nucleosome, chromatin remodeling would facilitate the accessibility of Cmi PHDb to the H3 [aa1–50] region, which is beyond the free N-terminal H3 tail in these assays. Thus, in addition to histone H4 recognition, we propose that the MLR proteins are long H3 tail readers, recognizing several residues throughout the H3 tail and beyond. In accord with the findings presented here, there have been several reports of proteins that recognize an extended region of the H3 tail. For instance, PHF14 protein is a ‘bipartite’ long H3 N-terminal H3 tail reader that engages the H3 [aa1–34] region using 2 reader surfaces that recognize separate segments [aa1–15] and [aa14–34] of the H3 tail with different affinities (46). We did not detect a similar engagement of the Cmi PHDb with the extended H3 tail, as the separated PHD fingers did not show unique preferences for the different peptides tested.

Second, our data suggests that both H3 and H4 have the potential to interact simultaneously with the MLR PHDb domain. Particularly noteworthy about these findings is that it was previously assumed that in order to gain access to the H3 tail for monomethyltransferase activity, the PHD finger domain would have to engage the H4 tail first, and then by a trans-tail mechanism reach over to encounter the H3 tail for deposition of the H3K4 monomethyl mark (17). In this model, MLR PHD fingers ‘read’ the H4 tail which is coupled to a ‘writer’ role for the H3 tail by the methyltransferase activity. Our data expands the current thinking of the trans-tail model by suggesting that contacts internally within histone H3 as well as the H4 tail may potentially both play a role in ‘reader’ functions of the methyltransferase. It is interesting to note that cryo-EM structural studies using regions of MLL1 and MLL3 which include the catalytic SET domain along with other MLL complex proteins bound to nucleosomes, suggest that these complexes make extensive contacts with the histone fold and DNA regions of the nucleosome (47). In fact, it was the ‘proper alignment’ of the complex proteins/ subunits facilitated by recognition of H2BK120ub by complex protein RBBP5 that was found to be essential for access to the H3 tail and efficient methylation of histone H3K4. It is important to note, however, that the PHD domains of MLL1 or MLL3 were not included in these studies. In our study, the globular region of histone H3 was part of the longer peptides bound by MLR cluster b PHD fingers. The fact that MLR PHDb fingers show enhanced binding when normally inaccessible H3 surfaces disengage from the nucleosome surface, perhaps through Brm ATPase nucleosome remodeling, suggest that when bound to chromatin, the PHD fingers may also play an important role in prolonging proper engagement of the complex on histone H3, thereby facilitating H3K4 methylation activity of the complex. This is consistent with our previous findings (6) that despite retention of Trr on polytene chromosomes upon Cmi removal, the product of Trr's methyltransferase function, the H3K4me1 mark, is much reduced suggesting that the Cmi PHD fingers may have an important role in maintaining Trr's interaction with the H3 tail. Thus, these findings transform our understanding of how PHD fingers in MLR proteins recognize chromatin and point to the importance of the PHDb recognition/binding to not only the H4 tail, but particularly to recognition of the H3 globular region as a unique feature of enhancer activation.

Third, we provide an alternative view of the role of histone H4 acetylation and its recruitment of PHDb to chromatin. Previous investigations using just the isolated MLL3 PHDb.f3, including the zinc finger spacer region, suggest specific recognition of residues within the basic [aa16–20] tail region of H4, particularly residue H4H18 (22). Similarly, studies with MLL4 PHDb.f3 alone lacking the spacer, suggest that H4 containing the H4K16Acetyl mark is selectively bound (23). We reasoned that a more appropriate characterization of histone binding was to examine all 3 fingers together, rather than the 3rd finger by itself, since a single finger may be more likely to have different binding properties when removed from the cluster. This may explain the discrepancy in binding preferences we found in our studies, particularly with regard to the H4K16ac mark which we find to be tolerated but not selective in our ITC measurements and binding assays with the 3 finger PHDb MBP fusion proteins. As opposed to H4K16ac, the acetylation of residues H4K5, K8 and K12 were inhibitory to binding in both our ITC measurements and pull down experiments. Our in vivo data using ChIP-seq analysis also support our conclusion that H4 acetyl marks particularly K5, K8 and K12 do not exhibit co-occupancy in Cmi binding peaks, nor does Cmi co-localize frequently with H4K16ac. It is interesting to note that histone binding by the tandem PHD fingers of both MOZ protein and DPf3b, which share many of the same conserved features as found in PHDb.f1 and f2 of the MLR family, was also negatively impacted by acetylation of H4K5, H4K8, H4K12 either alone or in combination (34,48). This, together with the data obtained from our mutagenesis studies that reveal single amino acid changes in any one of the 3 fingers has the potential to disrupt histone binding, further highlights the importance of the first two PHD fingers for contributing to the selectivity of the entire MLR PHDb module for specific histone marks. Thus, we suggest that the H4K16 acetyl mark is not ‘instructional’ for binding by the MLR PHDb proteins, but rather it is ‘permissive’. An alternative view supported by our data is that selectivity is provided by the combined acetylation of K5, K8 and K12 of H4 through its inhibition of PHDb binding to certain regions of chromatin.

In combination with the strong amino acid sequence conservation of PHDb.f1 and f2, our data supports the notion that there is an essential requirement for participation by all three fingers in the cluster for maximal histone recognition and binding. An intriguing possibility suggested from our findings is that, in the presence of histones, the zinc finger spacer is necessary for the proper positioning of PHDb.f3 into alignment with PHDb.f1 and PHDb.f2. The important role of a spacer region in modulating histone binding affinity is not without precedent. For instance, in the case of the double PHD fingers of CHD4 which are connected by a short unstructured linker/spacer, the binding affinity in the presence of the linker/spacer was increased over binding by the PHD fingers alone (49). On the other hand, in the BAZ2B protein, the lysine-rich 72 amino acid unstructured linker/spacer was been shown to competitively reduce binding affinity of the PHD domain for histones by resembling the positively charged surfaces of the N-terminus of histone H3 (50).

Recently, a potential structural model for the Drosophila Cmi protein was generated using AlphaFold (51). In this model, the 3 PHD fingers in cluster b are predicted to reside within the complete Cmi protein in a linear organization (Figure 8A-C). However, it is important to consider that the AlphaFold models did not take into account how the PHD fingers may be orientated when they are bound to chromatin or that alternative conformational states were even likely in certain contexts (52). Our data suggests that all three PHD fingers have the potential to simultaneously contact both histones H3 and H4 and supports a model whereby zinc coordination of the 4 cysteines in the zinc finger spacer that precedes PHDb.f3 serves to act like a hinge, thereby juxtaposing PHD fingers 1 and 2 with finger 3. We used the I-TASSER program to generate a best-fit model taking into account our binding and mutagenesis data and the potential relationships of the PHD fingers with the zinc finger spacer (Figure 8D-G; Supplementary Figure S8; Supplementary methods and discussion) (53,54). We also used known histone-PHD structures from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) to generate hypothetical binding sites for H3 and H4 in the PHDb domain of Cmi (Supplementary Figures S9 and S10; Supplementary discussion). The I-TASSER modeling predicts a more compact conformation with multiple inter-domain contacts possible. This more compact PHDb model reveals the potential formation of a crevice that may accommodate histone tail interactions involving all three fingers simultaneously. In these models, the zinc-coordinated spacer region could potentially contribute to the formation or stability of the structure, even though it remains outside the crevice and likely not involved in direct histone contacts, consistent with the spacer region being conserved in length but not amino acid identity (Supplementary Figures S8A, S9, S10). It remains to be explored whether the PHD fingers and zinc finger spacer form into such a folded tertiary structure. However, our binding data in which all 4 cysteines in the spacer are mutated support the idea that the spacer region is critical for the augmentation of binding that results when all 3 PHD fingers contact a chromatin substrate.

In conclusion, one of the key features of transcription regulation is the appropriate recognition of histones to facilitate both epigenetic programming of enhancers as well as their proper timing utilization during development. The numerous cancer associated missense mutations and truncations identified within the PHD clusters of the mammalian MLR proteins, ML3 and MLL4 point to the importance of these functional domains in tumor suppression. Our findings in this present study expand the view of histone recognition of the highly conserved PHD cluster b to include regions of H3, in addition to H4, and implicates regions of H3 that may necessitate accessibility through transcription and remodeling events. The strong conservation of this ancient 3 finger module of PHDb as a functional unit throughout evolution lends support to the broader significance of our findings and the pursuit of further study.
