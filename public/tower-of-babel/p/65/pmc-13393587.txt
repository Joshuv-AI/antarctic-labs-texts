Gestational choriocarcinoma following an ectopic pregnancy: A diagnostic dilemma in low-resource settings
Authors: Emmanuel Dusuri, Joseph Arkorful, Bright Adu Gyamfi, Cathy Gyamfua Asante Larbi, Ofosu Atta Amaning, Benedict Apaw Agyei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGestational choriocarcinoma is a highly aggressive form of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia that follows an ectopic pregnancy in fewer than 1.5 cases per million births. In low-resource settings, diagnosis is frequently delayed due to a lack of standardized postoperative surveillance and clinical presentations that mimic other conditions. A 34-year-old woman presented with hemodynamic instability and a beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level of 304,000 IU/L, one month after undergoing surgery for a cornual ectopic pregnancy. Ultrasound revealed a 14.8-cm hypervascular uterine fundal mass and massive hemoperitoneum. An emergency exploratory laparotomy was performed, revealing 4 L of hemoperitoneum and an extensive infiltration of placental tissue on the small bowel, omentum, and fundus of the uterus, which histopathological analysis confirmed as gestational choriocarcinoma infiltrating the regional omental fat. Delayed recognition of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia following ectopic pregnancy surgery remains a significant risk in resource-constrained settings due to inadequate postoperative β-hCG surveillance. Routine β-hCG monitoring and prompt ultrasound evaluation are essential for early detection and emergency decision-making. At the same time, persistent postpregnancy symptoms with markedly elevated β-hCG warrant urgent suspicion for GTN and histopathological confirmation.Keywords: Gestational choriocarcinoma, Ectopic pregnancy, Β-hCG monitoring, Ultrasound, LMIC, Hemoperitoneum

Introduction

Gestational choriocarcinoma (GC) represents the most severe manifestation of gestational trophoblastic neoplasia (GTN), a category encompassing various neoplastic disorders arising from the placental trophoblast. Although benign forms such as hydatidiform moles are prevalent, GTN also includes placental site trophoblastic tumors, epithelioid trophoblastic tumors, and GC [1,2]. Notably, choriocarcinoma is characterized by its rapid dissemination through bloodstream and early metastasis. This malignancy may originate from both gestational-associated and nongestational-associated sources, making it challenging to draw a definitive distinction between them; however, treatment approaches generally remain similar [2,3]. Histologically, choriocarcinoma is characterized by hyperplasia of cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, along with necrosis and hemorrhage, and is devoid of chorionic villi. The majority of choriocarcinoma cases arise following atypical pregnancy scenarios, such as hydatidiform moles, ectopic pregnancies, and spontaneous abortions; notably, approximately 25% of cases occur following a full-term pregnancy [1]. Ultrasonography (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) are often used to diagnose uterine masses. On grayscale US, the uterus may appear asymmetrically enlarged, with choriocarcinomas typically presenting as heterogeneous masses that infiltrate the myometrium. These lesions are further characterized by intralesional anechoic cystic areas, indicative of their vascular, hemorrhagic, and necrotic nature. Color Doppler US enhances the visualization of choriocarcinomas, revealing their highly vascularized structure, which often demonstrates chaotic blood flow patterns and arteriovenous shunting, accompanied by color aliasing. However, the appearance of GTN on MRI is nonspecific and is limited to differentiating between the various types of GTN [4,5]. It is particularly noteworthy that gestational choriocarcinoma associated with ectopic pregnancy is exceedingly rare, occurring in fewer than 1.5 cases per million births, often stemming from malignant transformation in a molar pregnancy [1,3]. This report presents a rare case of a ruptured gestational choriocarcinoma and its aggressive progression following a prior diagnosis of a cornual ectopic pregnancy.

Case report

A 34-year-old woman, gravida 2, para 0, with a history of 1 previous abortion, presented with a 2-day history of vaginal bleeding, abdominal distension, and pain in late November 2025. The patient had a history of cornual ectopic pregnancy, for which she underwent an exploratory laparotomy 1 month prior, in early October 2025, at a nearby district hospital; however, she was discharged after recovery without postoperative serum beta-human chorionic gonadotropin (β-hCG) level monitoring. Her laboratory evaluation at our facility, a regional hospital, at the time of her admission revealed a critically low hemoglobin level of 5.7 g/dL, indicating severe anemia likely secondary to significant blood loss. Additionally, her quantitative serum β-hCG level was markedly elevated at 304,000 IU/L.

Her transabdominal US examination done the next day at our facility revealed a large, heterogeneous solid mass originating from the fundus of the uterus, measuring 14.8 × 9.7 × 12.9 cm (Fig. 1), with hypervascular flow observed on color Doppler analysis (Fig. 2). The mass extended to both adnexal regions, and neither of the ovaries was visualized. Additionally, there was a significant intraperitoneal free fluid collection with low-level internal debris consistent with hemoperitoneum (Fig. 3). The liver appeared enlarged, measuring 19.4 cm in the cranio-caudal dimension, but did not show any metastatic lesions. The spleen and both kidneys appeared sonographically normal (Fig. 4). Given the patient's previous and present clinical history, as well as the ultrasound findings, a differential diagnosis of uterine malignancy and GTN was made.

Following the US examination, the patient and her family were counselled regarding the findings and implications of the pelvic mass, substantial hemoperitoneum, and elevated β-hCG levels. Due to the emergent nature of the case, an exploratory laparotomy was performed by a team of gynecologists and surgeons on the same day after obtaining informed consent. Intraoperatively, the surgical team identified extensive infiltration of placental tissue on the small bowel, omentum, and fundus of the uterus. The uterus was enlarged to a size equivalent to 12 weeks of gestation, with a notable defect observed. Bilateral ovarian cysts and inflamed fallopian tubes were also noted. Approximately 4 L of hemoperitoneum were present, corroborating the preoperative ultrasound findings. The patient received a total of 5 units of blood. Uterine repair, removal of placental tissue, and partial omentectomy were performed, and tissue samples were collected for histopathological characterization.

Histopathological examination was performed at an external facility, with results returned 3 days after the tissue sample was submitted. Microscopic analysis of the resected specimen revealed a malignant trophoblastic proliferation composed of both cytotrophoblasts and syncytiotrophoblasts arranged in characteristic biphasic plexiform patterns. Notably, there was a complete absence of chorionic villi, which, combined with extensive areas of intratumoral hemorrhage and necrosis, confirmed the diagnosis of GC. The tumor showed direct infiltrative growth into the omental adipose tissue. Following this diagnosis, staging investigations were initiated to screen for distant metastasis. A chest radiograph was unremarkable, showing no evidence of pulmonary metastasis or pleural effusion (Fig. 5). Although a cranial computed tomography (CT) scan was indicated to exclude cerebral involvement, it could not be performed due to the patient's financial constraints. Similarly, other advanced imaging modalities, such as MRI or PET-CT, which offer greater sensitivity in detecting distant metastases and excluding other malignant pathologies, could not be performed for the same reason. After histopathological confirmation of choriocarcinoma, the patient was evaluated using the FIGO/WHO prognostic scoring system. Her total score of 9 categorized her as high risk. This score included factors such as her age (34 years, 0 points), an antecedent ectopic pregnancy (1 point), a 1-month interval since surgery (0 points), a pretreatment serum β-hCG level of 304,000 IU/L (4 points), a maximum tumor diameter of 14.8 cm (2 points), and omental involvement (2 points). The patient was transferred to a tertiary oncology facility for further management, including chemotherapy. As her subsequent care was managed at the receiving institution, postoperative serial β-hCG levels and treatment outcomes were not available to the reporting team.

Posteroanterior (PA) chest radiograph obtained during postoperative metastatic staging. The lung fields are clear bilaterally, demonstrating no evidence of focal consolidation, pleural effusion, or nodular lesions. The heart size, vascular margins, hila, and diaphragmatic contours are within normal limits.

Discussion

GC is a highly malignant trophoblastic tumor characterized by the absence of villous structures. It is known for its rapid spread through the bloodstream and a significant risk of hemorrhage [1]. The patient presented with clinical features indicative of a high-risk form of GTN, notably an extremely elevated β-hCG level of 304,000 IU/L and severe anemia, with hemoglobin measured at 5.7 g/dL. This clinical history is particularly significant, as the occurrence of choriocarcinoma following an ectopic pregnancy is rare, estimated to occur in only 1 in every 15,000-40,000 pregnancies [[6], [7], [8]]. The rapid progression from the initial surgical intervention for ectopic pregnancy to a critical oncological emergency within a mere 30 days highlights the aggressive nature of this tumor [1,2]. As noted, the patient was referred to a tertiary facility for oncological treatment shortly after surgery, which resulted in the unavailability of serial β-hCG data to confirm treatment response. The β-hCG trend serves as the primary biomarker for assessing the resolution of GTN. Consequently, the absence of these data prevents confirmation of disease resolution postsurgery.

US, intraoperative observations, and histopathological findings in this case closely align with the established literature and demonstrate the disease’s severity. In resource-limited settings, where access to advanced imaging modalities such as CT or MRI may be either unavailable or prohibitively expensive, diagnostic ultrasound plays a crucial role in guiding clinical and surgical decision-making. US serves as the initial imaging modality for evaluating pelvic conditions, particularly to rule out normal or ectopic pregnancies in patients presenting with elevated levels of β-hCG [5]. While it lacks specificity in diagnosing GTN, it is effective in identifying mass lesions. Accurate differentiation of conditions such as choriocarcinoma relies heavily on the integration of clinical observations and β-hCG measurements [4]. GTN, including choriocarcinoma, is characterized by the production of β-hCG at concentrations 3-100 times greater than those found in normal pregnancies, making serial assessments of this hormone critical for diagnosis and post-treatment monitoring [9]. On grayscale US, the uterus may appear asymmetrically enlarged, with choriocarcinomas typically presenting as heterogeneous masses that infiltrate the myometrium. These lesions are further characterized by intralesional anechoic cystic areas, indicative of their vascular, hemorrhagic, and necrotic nature. Color Doppler US enhances the visualization of choriocarcinomas, revealing their highly vascularized structure, which often demonstrates chaotic blood flow patterns and arteriovenous shunting, accompanied by color aliasing. This technique is particularly advantageous for distinguishing choriocarcinoma from other conditions that are hypovascular or avascular, such as retained products of conception, focal adenomyomas, fibroids, and other hematomas that may mimic solid masses [5]. In our case, US served as an essential tool for rapid clinical assessment and surgical planning, translating the patient's biochemical abnormalities and hemodynamic instability into a clear anatomical emergency. It identified a large 14.8 cm heterogeneous mass in the uterine fundus, exhibiting significant blood flow on color Doppler interrogation and a considerable amount of hemoperitoneum, both of which were confirmed intraoperatively. This finding transformed an ambiguous clinical scenario into a clear anatomical emergency requiring immediate exploratory laparotomy. Although the US findings remained nonspecific and did not yield a conclusive diagnosis of choriocarcinoma, they were essential in identifying the source of the hemorrhage and raising high clinical suspicion for an invasive pathology rather than a benign condition or a typical recurrent ectopic pregnancy [4]. Additionally, the detection of hepatomegaly offered preliminary surgical insights and early signals of possible systemic involvement. Ultimately, while US was invaluable for identifying surgical complications and informing intervention strategies, it could not provide a definitive diagnosis; the diagnosis of choriocarcinoma was confirmed only through subsequent histopathological analysis [4,10,11].

Intra-operatively, the identification of omental fat involvement and significant infiltration of placental tissue on the small bowel suggests that the initial gestational event may have been either a primary cornual choriocarcinoma misdiagnosed as a cornual ectopic pregnancy or a rapidly evolving trophoblastic cancer that breached the uterine fundus. The presence of approximately 4 L of hemoperitoneum during the procedure further highlights the tumor's hemorrhagic characteristics, consistent with the hypervascular nature of gestational choriocarcinoma observed on preoperative Doppler ultrasound. This vascularity, combined with her critically low admission hemoglobin of 5.7 g/dL, necessitated transfusion of 5 units of packed red blood cells to restore oxygen-carrying capacity, correct hypovolemic shock, and provide hemodynamic support throughout the emergency laparotomy.

Histopathologically, the presence of cytotrophoblast and syncytiotrophoblast, substantial necrosis, bleeding, and omental fat infiltration, coupled with the absence of chorionic villi, constitutes the definitive diagnostic finding for gestational choriocarcinoma. This finding supports the notion of an aggressive dual-phase growth pattern of syncytiotrophoblasts and cytotrophoblasts, which infiltrate adjacent tissues and blood vessels, thereby elucidating the patient’s critical condition [1,12].

The management of this case underscores the progression from emergency surgical stabilization to definitive oncological care. Existing literature indicates that GC is highly responsive to chemotherapeutic agents, suggesting that surgery serves primarily as a life-saving measure to control hemorrhage rather than as a radical curative intervention. The decision to perform uterine repair and partial omentectomy effectively addressed the 4-L hemoperitoneum while preserving fertility, aligning with modern standards for young patients with a low parity history [13,14]. Importantly, the incomplete excision noted on pathology is considered oncologically acceptable in gestational choriocarcinoma, as systemic multi-agent chemotherapy remains the gold standard for eradicating residual disease [[15], [16], [17]]. The effective management of this intricate clinical scenario, characterized by a ruptured and bleeding pelvic mass leading to systemic oncological intervention, underscores the critical importance of a multidisciplinary approach [18,19]. The coordinated efforts of the radiologist and sonographers, who assessed the immediate surgical complications; the gynecological surgeons responsible for achieving hemostasis and performing omental debulking; the pathologists who provided the definitive tissue diagnosis; and the oncologists were pivotal in optimizing the patient’s survival trajectory. Given her classification as high-risk (FIGO score 9), the multidisciplinary team established a referral protocol to facilitate her transfer from the regional surgical facility to a specialized tertiary oncology center. The proposed therapeutic strategy involves the initiation of the multi-agent EMA-CO regimen, which consists of etoposide, methotrexate, actinomycin D, alternating with cyclophosphamide and vincristine. This rigorous regimen represents the global standard of care for high-risk choriocarcinoma, demonstrating long-term remission rates exceeding 85% [20]. As of the time of this report, the patient had been successfully stabilized postoperatively and transferred to the tertiary oncology facility, where she was scheduled to commence her first cycle of the EMA-CO regimen. While staging was partially constrained due to financial limitations, specifically the inability to obtain a cranial CT scan to exclude brain metastasis, her baseline chest X-ray showed no pulmonary nodules, and she remained neurologically intact. A further limitation of this case is the inability to perform other advanced imaging modalities, including MRI or PET-CT, which are valuable in excluding cerebral and other distant metastases and in ruling out alternative malignant pathologies; their absence limits the completeness of staging in this case. Clinicians managing similar cases in resource-limited settings should be aware of this diagnostic gap and consider alternative, more accessible means of monitoring disease progression, such as serial serum β-hCG measurement, where advanced imaging is not feasible. Accordingly, the long-term monitoring protocol will encompass weekly quantitative serum β-hCG measurements to evaluate her response to chemotherapy, followed by monthly surveillance for at least one-year postremission achievement.

This case underscores 3 key teaching points relevant to the management of GTN in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Firstly, it reveals a significant gap in follow-up care after surgery, particularly the absence of regular β-hCG testing following the surgical treatment of ectopic pregnancies or miscarriages. In well-resourced healthcare environments, standard practice involves monitoring β-hCG levels until they return to baseline following any pregnancy loss or ectopic surgery [[21], [22], [23]]. It emphasizes the critical need for histopathological testing of all products of conception, including those from cases initially considered standard ectopic surgeries, a practice often neglected in resource-limited settings due to associated costs. It also highlights a problem within certain LMIC facilities, where patients are discharged after surgery without plans for biochemical follow-up. In this case, the absence of postoperative β-hCG monitoring at the district hospital allowed this manageable condition to escalate into a serious emergency. It is therefore imperative to incorporate low-cost β-hCG monitoring into basic maternal healthcare protocols to prevent serious complications such as those encountered in this case. Lastly, it emphasizes the pivotal role of US as an accessible diagnostic tool for emergency decision-making in resource-constraint environment. In this patient, it transformed her ambiguous clinical scenario into urgent surgical intervention without reliance on advanced imaging modalities that were financially inaccessible. This underscores the utility of ultrasound as a first-line diagnostic tool in acute gynecologic presentations where access to CT, MRI, or PET-CT is limited.

Conclusion and lesson learned

This case underscores the need for greater diagnostic vigilance in resource-constrained settings, particularly the risk of delayed recognition of GTN following ectopic pregnancy surgery. Routine postoperative β-hCG monitoring and prompt ultrasound evaluation are essential tools for early detection and emergency decision-making in low-resource environments. Persistent post-pregnancy symptoms accompanied by markedly elevated β-hCG should prompt urgent suspicion for GTN and histopathological confirmation.
