Introduction

Anomalous venous drainage of the right upper pulmonary lobe into the superior vena cava is a rare congenital vascular anomaly [1]. This specific variant entails the right upper lobe pulmonary vein directly entering the superior vena cava. The prevalence of pulmonary anomalous venous drainage has been reported to be between 0.4 and 0.7 %. It is more common on the right and can results in the formation of a left-to-right shunt [2].

This venous drainage anomaly is often asymptomatic and discovered incidentally during imaging or surgical procedures. However, unrecognized anomalous venous drainage can pose significant challenges during thoracic surgeries, increasing the risk of inadvertent vascular injury [3].

We report a case of anomalous venous drainage of the right upper lobe into the superior vena cava, as an intraoperative finding during video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. We present this case according to the Surgical CAse REport (SCARE) guidelines 2025 [4].

Presentation of case

We present the case of an asymptomatic 76-year-old male patient with a history of high-grade urothelial carcinoma, who during routine oncologic surveillance, was found to have two suspicious lesions: an ill-defined pulmonary lesion in the right upper lobe (a 37 × 24 mm consolidative area associated with bronchiectasis) and a lesion at the gastroesophageal junction (GEJ). Physical examination and laboratory tests performed were unremarkable.

Subsequently, whole-body staging with positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) was performed, which showed a consolidative area in the right upper lobe measuring 37 × 19 mm with low uptake (SUVmax 2.2) and diffuse wall thickening in the lower third of the esophagus with high metabolic activity (SUVmax 21.8).

Given the differential diagnosis of two synchronous primary tumors versus metastatic disease from esophageal carcinoma, an initial biopsy of the GEJ lesion was performed via esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD). Histopathological analysis revealed a moderately differentiated adenocarcinoma with signet ring cells, located 38–40 cm from the dental arcade. A percutaneous biopsy of the pulmonary lesion was also performed, confirming a primary lung adenocarcinoma (TTF-1 positive), thus ruling out metastatic spread from the esophageal tumor.

After diagnosis, the patient received induction (neoadjuvant) chemotherapy with 4 cycles of the FLOT regimen (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, oxaliplatin, and docetaxel). Restaging PET/CT after chemotherapy showed no residual uptake at either the gastroesophageal junction or in the pulmonary lesion.

The case was discussed in a multidisciplinary tumor board meeting, and it was decided to proceed with a combined surgical approach: a minimally invasive subtotal esophagectomy (Ivor Lewis technique) and a video-assisted thoracoscopic right upper lobectomy (VATS).

Intraoperatively, an anomalous venous drainage of the right upper lobe into the superior vena cava was identified, which required careful vascular management during the pulmonary resection but did not warrant changes in the surgical approach or hilar dissection. Despite the anomalous drainage of the vessel into the superior vena cava, division was carried out using a standard endoscopic vascular stapler, consistent with our usual technique for venous structures in this region. The anomaly was not suspected preoperatively, as the patient exhibited no clinical signs or symptoms suggestive of a left-to-right shunt. The patient did not present with any complications or symptoms after pulmonary surgery.

The surgical specimens were submitted for anatomopathological analysis, which confirmed the presence of two synchronous primary malignancies. The esophageal tumor was identified as an invasive signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma (G3), located at the gastroesophageal junction, measuring 2 cm in its greatest dimension. There was no evidence of lymphovascular or perineural invasion. All retrieved lymph nodes—including pericardial and perigastric nodes—were negative for metastatic involvement. Both proximal and distal resection margins were free of tumor. Concurrently, the pulmonary lesion was diagnosed as an invasive acinar adenocarcinoma of the lung, with a predominant acinar pattern (70 %), accompanied by papillary (20 %) and micropapillary (10 %) components. This tumor measured 3 cm, with no evidence of spread through air spaces (STAS), and no lymphovascular or perineural invasion. Surgical margins were negative for malignancy. Furthermore, all lymph nodes retrieved during the abdominal (D2) and thoracic dissections—corresponding to stations 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, and 12—were free of neoplastic involvement.

Discussion

The earliest documented case of pulmonary vein drainage anomaly was described by Winslow in 1739 [5].

Embryologically, this condition is thought to arise from incomplete regression of the right anterior cardinal vein, leading to an anomalous persistence of venous drainage into the systemic circulation via the superior vena cava [6]. Although this type of variant is rare and typically asymptomatic, failure to identify it may lead to inadvertent injury, particularly if it drains a segment or lobe not planned for resection.

This venous drainage anomaly is observed in approximately 0.4 % to 0.7 % of the general population and is frequently associated with congenital intracardiac anomalies, most notably atrial septal defects [2].

Anomalous venous return is typically classified into four types: supracardiac, cardiac, infradiaphragmatic, and mixed, with the supracardiac variant being the most commonly encountered [7].

Patients with this type of vascular anomaly are typically asymptomatic. On the one hand, if patients present symptoms suggestive of a left-to-right shunt, with the resection of the lobe with anomalous drainage these symptoms may cease. On the other hand, when the anomalous drainage is located on the lobe that is not intended for resection, it may lead to hemodynamic disturbances, potentially resulting in clinical symptoms and, in severe cases, heart failure [8].

In our case, a supracardiac variant was observed, characterized by direct drainage of the right superior pulmonary vein into the superior vena cava. Notably, no concomitant structural cardiac malformations were identified on preoperative imaging.

While this finding may resemble partial anomalous pulmonary venous connection (PAPVC) which has been reported by several authors [[9], [10], [11]], it is distinct in that the anomalous vein exclusively drained the right upper lobe, which was completely resected. Therefore, it did not pose a risk of postoperative left-to-right shunting. However, intraoperative recognition of such vascular anomalies is crucial, particularly during minimally invasive procedures such as VATS, where unexpected vascular anomalies can increase the risk of intraoperative bleeding or necessitate conversion to open surgery [12].

Conclusion

Anomalous venous drainage of the right upper pulmonary lobe into the superior vena cava can pose significant challenges during thoracic surgeries, increasing the risk of inadvertent vascular injury if not recognized intraoperatively. This case highlights the importance of careful intraoperative assessment of vascular anatomy, which is mandatory during thoracic oncologic surgery. Multidisciplinary evaluation, combined with a thorough review of preoperative imaging, can facilitate the identification of this vascular anomaly before surgery.
