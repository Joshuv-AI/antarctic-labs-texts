Improving Pneumococcal Vaccination Rates During Hospitalization via Resident Education Intervention: A Hospital-Based Quality Improvement Project in a Teaching Community Hospital
Authors: Shun Nakahara, Takahiko Tsutsumi, Masaru Kurihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumococcal vaccination coverage among eligible adults in Japan remains suboptimal. This gap may be partly attributable to insufficient physician and patient education, as well as limited coordination in delivery of preventive care. Therefore, we conducted a hospital-based quality improvement project combining resident education with system-level workflow modifications to enhance pneumococcal vaccination rates.MethodsA single-center quality improvement study was conducted at a teaching community hospital in Japan, comparing outcomes between pre-intervention and intervention periods. The intervention included resident education, patient educational materials, integration of vaccination status into admission documentation, discharge checklists, and weekly reminders. The primary outcome was the proportion of eligible patients vaccinated during hospitalization, while secondary outcomes included overall vaccination rates at discharge. Process measures assessed residents’ knowledge and confidence before and after the educational session.ResultsDuring the pre-intervention period, 40 eligible patients were identified, and none were vaccinated (0%). During the intervention period, 36 eligible patients were identified, of whom 29 (80.6%) were vaccinated. Overall vaccination rates at discharge increased from 22.4% (22/98) to 59.5% (50/84). Among 15 residents, mean knowledge scores improved from 57% to 92%, and confidence in counseling increased from 2.0 to 4.4 on a 5-point Likert scale.ConclusionsA resident-led, hospital-based quality improvement intervention substantially increased pneumococcal vaccination rates among eligible hospitalized patients. Educational initiatives combined with system-level workflow modifications represent a practical strategy to improve adult vaccination coverage in inpatient settings.Keywords: Pneumococcal vaccine, Medical education, Quality improvement

Introduction

Invasive pneumococcal disease (IPD) remains a major public health concern worldwide with substantial morbidity and mortality. In Japan, the mortality rate of IPD has been reported to be approximately 20 %, highlighting the importance of effective prevention strategies.1 Despite its proven efficacy, vaccination coverage among eligible adults in Japan remains suboptimal.

A recent nationwide survey reported that pneumococcal vaccination coverage was 55.1 % among adults aged ≥65 years but was markedly lower among younger adults with chronic medical conditions: 12.4 % among those aged 19–49 years, 3.2 % among those aged 50–59 years, and 4.0 % among those aged 60–64 years.2 Several system-level factors may contribute to these suboptimal vaccination rates. Japan operates a free-access healthcare system in which preventive care is not systematically coordinated through a designated primary care provider. Preventive services such as vaccination are delivered through both physician-based care and municipal public health programs.3 Gaps in vaccination coverage have been attributed to multiple factors, including lack of physician recommendation, insufficient patient education, out-of-pocket costs, and the absence of direct notification to target populations.2

In this context, hospitalization may provide an important opportunity to identify eligible patients and administer pneumococcal vaccination. Several studies conducted in other countries have demonstrated that hospital-based quality improvement initiatives can successfully increase vaccination rates through educational interventions and system-level workflow modifications.3 Therefore, we conducted a quality improvement project at a community hospital in Japan to evaluate whether targeted hospital-based interventions could improve pneumococcal vaccination rates.

Methods

2.1. Study setting

We conducted a single-center quality improvement study to evaluate the impact of a hospital-based intervention on pneumococcal vaccination rates. The study was conducted in the Department of General Internal Medicine at Urasoe General Hospital, a 334-bed teaching community hospital in Japan with an internal medicine residency program, operating under a hospitalist model that manages a broad spectrum of medical conditions. The institutional review board determined that this project qualified as a quality improvement initiative and waived the requirement for formal ethical review. Before the intervention, there was no standardized workflow to assess pneumococcal vaccination eligibility or administer vaccination during hospitalization. The study compared pneumococcal vaccination rates before and during implementation of a hospital-based intervention program. We conducted a single-center pre–post quality improvement study in accordance with the SQUIRE 2.0 guidelines.

2.2. Study population and period

The study included all adult patients admitted to the department during the study period at a single teaching community hospital in Japan. The study compared outcomes between a control period (usual care; September 2023 to December 2023) and an intervention period (January 2024 to April 2024). During the study period, the 20-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine had not yet been approved in Japan.

2.3. Eligibility criteria

Eligibility criteria were defined according to recommendations from the Japanese Respiratory Society and the Japanese Association for Infectious Diseases.4–6 Eligible patients included adults aged ≥65 years and adults aged 19–64 years with risk factors for pneumococcal disease who had no prior documented pneumococcal vaccination. Patients were excluded if pneumococcal vaccination was deferred due to acute illness or if they had received COVID-19 vaccine within 14 days.

2.4. Intervention

The intervention was developed based on a fishbone diagram for root cause analysis (Fig. 1) and a driver diagram (Fig. 2). The intervention consisted of multiple strategies.

2.5. Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the proportion of eligible patients who received pneumococcal vaccination during hospitalization. The secondary outcome was the proportion of all hospitalized patients who were vaccinated by the time of discharge, including both previously vaccinated patients and those newly vaccinated during hospitalization.

Patients whose prior pneumococcal vaccination history could not be confirmed were classified as having uncertain vaccination status. These patients were excluded from the primary analysis but included in a prespecified sensitivity analysis assuming they were eligible and unvaccinated.

Process measures included the proportion of admission notes documenting vaccination status, as well as residents’ knowledge scores and confidence levels before and after the educational session. Residents completed a 7-item multiple-choice test assessing knowledge of pneumococcal vaccination and rated their confidence in counseling patients using a 5-point Likert scale before and after the educational session.

2.6. Statistical analysis

Vaccination rates were compared between the pre-intervention and intervention periods using absolute risk differences with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager software. Eligibility and vaccination status were determined through review of EMR of all hospitalized patients during the predefined study periods. EMR review was conducted independently by a medical clerk who was not involved in implementing the intervention.

Results

During the pre-intervention period, 98 hospitalized patients were screened. Of these, 22 had documented prior pneumococcal vaccination. Among the remaining patients, 40 were eligible for vaccination, and 32 had uncertain vaccination status. None of the eligible patients received pneumococcal vaccination during hospitalization (0%) (Fig. 3).

Patient flow diagram for the pre-intervention period.

During the intervention period, 84 hospitalized patients were screened. Of these, 21 had documented prior pneumococcal vaccination. Among the remaining patients, 36 were eligible for vaccination, and 24 had uncertain vaccination status. Of the eligible patients, 29 (80.6%) received pneumococcal vaccination during hospitalization, while 7 did not (Fig. 4).

Patient flow diagram for the intervention period.

The pneumococcal vaccination rate among eligible patients increased from 0% in the pre-intervention period to 80.6% in the intervention period, representing an absolute increase of 80.6 percentage points (95% CI, 56.2–90.2). As a secondary outcome, the proportion of all hospitalized patients who were vaccinated against pneumococcal disease by discharge, including both previously vaccinated patients and those newly vaccinated during hospitalization, increased from 22.4% (22/98) in the pre-intervention period to 59.5% (50/84) in the intervention period, representing an absolute increase of 37.1 percentage points (95% CI, 17.2–54.1). No patients or their family members declined vaccination after counseling.

The proportion of admission notes documenting vaccination status was 100%. Among 15 residents, the mean pre-test score on a multiple-choice questionnaire assessing knowledge of pneumococcal vaccination was 57%, which improved to 92% after the educational session. Residents’ confidence in counseling patients about pneumococcal vaccination improved from a mean score of 2.0–4.4 on a 5-point Likert scale.

Discussion

In this quality improvement study, root cause analysis identified several barriers to vaccination, including limited awareness among both patients and physicians, limited opportunities for counseling, and time constraints in outpatient settings. Importantly, many of these barriers were considered modifiable through targeted hospital-based strategies. A hospital-based intervention based on the root cause analysis and previous studies significantly increased pneumococcal vaccination rates. Process measures also demonstrated a significant improvement in residents’ knowledge and confidence in counseling patients about pneumococcal vaccination.

Several factors likely contributed to the success of the intervention. First, physician education improved awareness of pneumococcal vaccination indications and the importance of adult immunization, which increased residents’ confidence in counseling. Previous studies have shown that physician recommendation is one of the most influential factors in patient acceptance of vaccination.7 Second, incorporating vaccination status into the EMR workflow ensured that vaccination eligibility was routinely assessed during hospitalization.8 System-based interventions such as reminders and standardized documentation templates have been shown to improve adherence to preventive care measures.9 Third, providing education and counseling to patients and their families likely improved patient understanding of vaccination benefits and increased acceptance.10 Hospitalization offers an important opportunity to improve vaccination coverage. Unlike outpatient care, hospitalized patients in Japan may have more time to receive education and preventive services before discharge.11

Notably, approximately 30% of hospitalized patients had an unknown pneumococcal vaccination status. This finding highlights a major barrier to vaccination in Japan. The limited interoperability of different EMR may hinder information sharing. In addition, vaccination records in older adults are often maintained in paper-based formats, such as personal health record booklets, rather than centralized digital systems. These factors may contribute to incomplete documentation and represent an important target for future system-level improvements.

This study has several strengths. First, it demonstrates that a combination of resident-led educational interventions and system-based workflow modifications can effectively improve vaccination rates. Second, the intervention required minimal additional cost, suggesting that it is a practical and scalable approach that could be implemented in other hospitals in Japan and in healthcare systems with similar structures.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, and the duration of the intervention was short, limiting assessment of long-term sustainability. Second, hospital length of stay in Japan is generally longer than in other countries,11 and most patients were clinically stable at the time of discharge, allowing for vaccination. Therefore, the effectiveness of this intervention in settings with shorter hospital stays remains uncertain. Third, the Hawthorne effect may have contributed to improved documentation and resident compliance, as residents were aware of the quality improvement initiative.12 Thus, the improvement in documentation may not be fully attributable to increased knowledge or confidence alone. However, the intervention included system-based workflow changes, which likely supported behavioral change beyond observation alone. Nevertheless, this project may have helped establish a new routine among residents to assess pneumococcal vaccination status and consider vaccination as part of inpatient care. This change in practice culture is a meaningful outcome of the project. Finally, the intervention relied heavily on resident workload, and future studies should explore more efficient workflows incorporating multidisciplinary teams.

Conclusion

A resident-led hospital-based quality improvement intervention significantly increased pneumococcal vaccination rates in a teaching community hospital in Japan. Educational initiatives combined with system-based workflow modifications represent a practical strategy for improving adult vaccination coverage in inpatient settings. Future studies involving larger populations and longer follow-up periods are needed to confirm the sustainability of these interventions.
