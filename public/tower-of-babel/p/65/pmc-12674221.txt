Effect of Aeromonas hydrophila infection on leptin receptor overlapping transcript expression in Rana amurensis
Authors: Yugang Sun, Tong Wu, Zhaodong Chen, Huimin Ren, Yufen Liu, Peng Liu, Wenge Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe leptin receptor overlapping transcript (LepROT) has been suggested to play several roles in immunomodulatory mechanisms; however, the understanding of its role in Rana amurensis immunity is still very limited. Here, we performed hematoxylin-eosin staining, quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), immunofluorescence and western blotting to investigate the roles of LepROT in the immunomodulatory mechanism and the influence of its expression on the nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling pathway, such as the activation of IκB kinase and NF-кB, in amphibian resistance to infection with Aeromonas hydrophila (Ah). After Ah infection, the liver, lung, kidney, skin, muscle, and stomach of R. amurensis showed cell structure disturbance, bleeding, and texture abnormalities. In addition, the relative expression levels of LepROT, NF-кB, IKKα, and IKKβ were all upregulated after Ah infection; however, they showed time-dependent differential expression. The NF-кB signaling pathway exhibited robust expression levels, which might be explained by the positive feedback regulation function of LepROT. Overall, this study provides a basis for further assessment of the biological functions of LepROT and highlights its role in the regulation of immune mechanisms.Keywords: Leptin receptor overlapping transcript, nuclear factor-kappa B signaling pathway, hematoxylin-eosin staining, quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction, Rana amurensis, Aeromonas hydrophila

Introduction

Leptin not only regulates energy homeostasis, food intake balance, and metabolism but also regulates hormone secretion, body growth, reproduction, and the immune response to pathogen invasion.1–3 However, it is biologically active only when it binds to the leptin receptor (LEPR). LEPR expression can be detected on the surface of immune cells, revealing a close relationship of LEPR with the immune system.4

LEPR, a member of the type I cytokine receptor superfamily, is encoded by the LEPR gene and possesses many typical structural characteristics of cytokine receptors.5 It exists as a dimer without leptin and is activated when the ligand binds to it. In addition, selective splicing of LEPR helps to form at least one different splicing product, including one short and one widely present long transcript, as well as a unique protein known as the leptin receptor overlapping transcript (LepROT).

LepROT has a molecular weight of 14 kDa and shares a promoter and two exons with LEPR but has no similarities in amino acid structure.6 LepROT and LEPR were reported to be co-expressed in the hypothalamus of rats.7 The primary function of LepROT is to inhibit the cell surface activity of LEPR and affect its biological functions; however, related information regarding the underlying biochemical mechanism is still limited.

LepROT homologs have been cloned from various species, such as Homo sapiens,8 Mus musculus,5 Danio rerio,9 Xenopus tropicalis (National Center for Biotechnology Information [NCBI] Reference Sequence: NM_001017102.2), Bufo bufo (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_040407057.1), Nanorana parkeri (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_018563079.1), and Rana temporaria (NCBI Reference Sequence: XM_040360761.1), but not from R. amurensis. The latter is the most widely distributed amphibian species in the Palearctic and lives mainly in low-mountain coastal areas (< 600 m above the sea level) in China, Russia, and Mongolia. It often inhabits open and humid places, such as wet meadows, swamps, lakes overgrown with weeds, riverbanks, grasslands, farmlands, shrubs, and meadows.10 R. amurensis is widely used in traditional Chinese medicine and transcriptomic data from several tissues including liver and skin have been published.11 Living in humid environments that support the growth of complex microbial communities, without the protection of a thick and hard keratin layer that vertebrates have, the immune function that their skin and other immune organs provide to fight infection is particularly important.12

Aeromonas hydrophila (Ah) is a common conditional pathogenic microorganism in the aquatic environment with a broad host range. In 1891, Ah was first reported to cause red leg disease in frogs, leading to sepsis, edema, and damage to the main organs.13 The disease has a rapid course, strong infectivity, and high mortality rate and is one of the major diseases affecting amphibians in the wild.Some studies have found that the A. hydrophila could disrupt the homeostatic environment in fish, triggering an immune response in tissues such as the gut and skin, which in turn activates relevant signaling pathways.14–17 In amphibian studies, it has been reported that the immune responses of Rana dybowskii upon exposure to A. hydrophila infection activate various functional proteins at the initial stage of the immune response, which complete their corresponding functions in a short time.18,19

Nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) is an important nuclear transcription factor in the nucleus that functions in various cell stimulation-mediated signaling modulations.20 Moreover, the NF-κB/inhibitor factor (I) kappa B pathway is considered one of the most crucial signal transduction pathways in cytokine production regulation and inflammatory cell responses.21

The aim of this study was to explore the effects of Ah on the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, skin, muscle, and stomach of R. amurensis, and assess the defensive mechanisms by observing the expression of LepROT, NF-кB, IκB kinase (IKKɑ and IKKβ) in the different tissues.

Materials and methods

Animals, immune challenge, and sample collection

All animal experiments were conducted in the Animal Building of Harbin Normal University. The experimental animals were purchased from the R. amurensis breeding farm in Acheng District, Heilongjiang Province. R. amurensis were all adult male and healthy, and were cultivated in well-ventilated containers at 20 °C, 3 in each experimental group and 3 in the control group, total of 99, weighing 20 ± 5 g, were intraperitoneally injected with 500 µL of lysogeny broth medium containing Ah (1.5 × 107 colony forming units⋅mL−1; treatment group) or 500 µL of lysogeny broth medium without Ah (control group). Ah from a strain isolated from a diseased R. amurensi at Northeast Forestry University in 2016 (No. RY-1). Tissues were dissected from double myelin-damaged R. amurensis after immune stimulation and immediately stored in liquid nitrogen for RNA extraction. All the experimental animals were treated according to the recommendations of the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of the National Institutes of Health. The study protocol was approved by the Committee on the Ethics of Animal Experiments of our institution (No. HFRI2020). All surgery was performed under anesthesia by ether mixed with alcohol, the frogs were euthanized by pithing the spinal cord and brain, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

Quantitative reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from various tissues (heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, skin, muscle, stomach from the R. amurensis) using TRIzol (R401-01-AA, Vazyme, Nanjing, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and assessed using an Ultraviolet spectrophotometer, OD = 260 nm (Beckman, Brea, CA, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript II QRT Super Mix (R223-01, Vazyme). The mRNA expression was measured using an ABI7500 real-time fluorescence qPCR (Beckman). The thermal conditions were 5 s at 95 °C and 30 s at 60 °C. According to previous studies, R. amurensis and Rana dybowskii are highly conserved at the genetic level, so primers for LepROT, β-actin, NF-кB, IKKɑ and IKKβ were obtained from references.22

The primer sequences are shown in Table 1. The transcript levels of the target genes were normalized to those of β-actin after correcting any differences in the amplification efficiency. The expression level of each target mRNA relative to that of β-actin mRNA was determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining analysis

Tissue samples from R. amurensis’s heart, spleen, liver, lung, kidney, skin, muscle, and stomach were collected at multiple post-infection time points (0-168 hpi), fixed, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections (6 µm) were prepared and stained with HE for histological analysis or immunofluorescence. The sections underwent deparaffinization, rehydration, hematoxylin-eosin staining, dehydration, and mounting with gum.22

Western blot analysis

Liver samples were harvested and homogenized in RIPA lysis buffer. The BCA Protein Assay was performed to detect the protein content. Protein samples were separated by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and were transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, where they were blocked with blocking buffer for 15 min (The above reagents are purchased from Beyotime

Biotechnology, Jiangsu, China) and subsequently stained with specific primary antibodies of nuclear factor κB p65 subunit (NF-κB p65)(1/5000), (bs-0465R, rabbit, Bioss Antibodies, Beijing, China) and β-actin (1/5000), (bsm-33036M, mouse, Bioss Antibodies, Beijing, China) overnight at 4 °C. Next, the membranes were washed thrice with TBST. After staining with horseradish peroxidase- (HRP-) conjugated secondary antibodies(1/8000), (bs-0295P-HRP, Rabbit IgG/HRP and bs-0296P-HRP, Mouse IgG/HRP, Bioss, Beijing, China), the blots were washed again and visualized by chemiluminescence (ECL), (Tanon, Shanghai, China).

Immunofluorescence analysis

The paraffin sections of R. amurensis were subjected to immunofluorescence staining according to standard procedures. Briefly, the paraffin sections were blocked with 10% normal goat serum (C01-03001, Bioss, Beijing, China) for 30 min at 37 °C and then incubated 2 hours at 37 °C with primary antibodies against NF-кB p65 (1:400) (bs-0465R, rabbit, Bioss) followed by co-incubation with FITC (1:400) - conjugated (bs-0295G-FITC, goat, Bioss) secondary antibodies for 1 h and counterstaining with DAPI (C02-04002, Bioss) for 8 min at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, the paraffin sections were visualized using fluorescence microscopy (Zeiss Axio Imager A2, Germany).

Statistical analysis

The qRT-PCR experiment was conducted which included 3 replicates per experimental grouping (control and Ah treated) Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. One-way analysis of variance followed by Dunnet were used to identify differences between the means. Significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Pathological identification

Histopathological examination of the liver in the control group showed normal tissue structure (Fig. 1A 0h), whereas the experimental group showed histological changes. In the first 8 h, the splenic tissue was dense, the medulla was clearly differentiated between red and white, lymphocytes were abundant, while the number of erythrocytes in the splenic medulla was relatively low; after 16 h, the splenic tissue became loose, hemolysis of erythrocytes occurred, and there was a marked decrease in the content of many lymphocytes, infiltration of inflammatory cells, necrosis of vascular foci, the red and white medulla were poorly demarcated, erythrocytes were increased, and hemolysis occurred (Fig. 1, A-16 h, 24h, 36h, 48h, 72h); after 96 h, hepatocyte walls disappeared, vacuoles were degenerated, cell gaps were increased and hepatocyte nuclei were reduced in size. There was also dilatation of the hepatic sinusoids with massive hemorrhage and hemolysis. Hepatic tissue was anemic, with the appearance of large numbers of brown, iron-containing haemosiderin granules (Fig. 1, A-96 h,120h, 168h).

In the treatment group, lung tissue lesions mainly appeared when the alveolar lumens were filled with different amounts of red blood cells, and inflammatory cells were observed in some lumens. Pulmonary artery congestion and a little erythrochromic protein substance were observed in the smooth muscle layer along with partial muscle fiber vacuolar degeneration and nuclear lysis (Fig. 1B).

Kidney tissue lesions mainly occurred in renal arterial congestion, leading to renal interstitial hemorrhage with high red blood cell infiltration. Renal tubular epithelial cell nucleus dissolution and rupture destroyed the epithelial cells, red blood cells, and homogeneous, red-stained protein-like substances in the lumen. Moreover, the space of the glomerular balloon increased, and the peripheral connective tissue was loose with edema (Fig. 1C).

In the skin tissue, a large number of blue-purple-stained basic cells, light-pink collagen fibers and purple-colored fibroblasts can be seen. Numerous melanocytes were observed between the sub-epidermis and dermis. During the disease, the boundaries between cells became unclear, and the overall structure became loose. Additionally, the epidermis was peeled off, the skin structure became incomplete, the acinar cavity was filled with numerous red blood cells, and the capillaries were congested. Moreover, the texture of muscle fibers changed and became twisted, broken, and disintegrated, accompanied by bleeding (Fig. 1D).

In muscle tissue, muscle fiber myogenesis is altered, twisted, broken and disintegrated with hemorrhage (Fig. 1E).

In the stomach tissue, we observed gastric mucosa shedding, swelling, and breaking of the muscle cells of the lateral longitudinal muscle layer, an increased gap between the muscle layer and the serous layer, as well as some areas of extensive membrane shedding (Fig. 1F).

As previously described, there are significant differences in cellular infiltration and lesions between tissues that accompany the passage of time, which is of interest. What is clear is that throughout the entire disease process, we observed ulcers appeared at the head and back of the R. amurensis treatment group, along with bleeding spots at the back and legs. In addition, ascites, hepatomegaly, congestion, as well as bleeding spots in the muscles were observed (Fig. S1).

Effect of Ah on the expression of LepROT

LepROT was expressed in all studied tissues at different levels (Fig. 2A–H) based on sampling time. In the heart, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 72 and 96 h (p < 0.01). Specifically, they presented an upregulated trend and reached the maximum value at 96 h that was 2.18-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2A). In the liver, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 8-48 h and168 h (p < 0.01). The highest value was reached at 8 h and was 13.24-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2B). In the spleen, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 8-48 h (p < 0.01). The highest value was reached at 24 h and was 2.72-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2C). In the lung, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group showed a downward trend but were still significantly higher than those in the control group at 36-168 h (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively; Fig. 2D). In the kidney, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 36 h, 96 h, and 168 h (p < 0.01; Fig. 2E). In the skin, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 8-24 h. The highest value was reached at 24 h and was 6.85-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2F). In the muscle, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group at 8, 24 and 48-72 h (p < 0.01). The highest value was reached at 24 h and was 2.91-fold higher than that in the control group (Fig. 2G). In the stomach, the relative LepROT expression levels in the treatment group were significantly higher than those in the control group only at 48 h (p < 0.01; Fig. 2H). In summary, it can be seen that the expression time and amount of LepROT will be different after being stressed by Aeromonas hydrophila in different tissues of R. amurensis.

Effect of Ah on the expression of the NF-кB signaling pathway

Our results showed a significant positive relationship in the relative expression of NF-кB with that of IKKα and IKKβ in the treatment group, revealing that Ah infection greatly stimulated the NF-кB signaling pathway expression levels and caused the highest increase in transcription. In the treatment group, the NF-кB signaling pathway (NF-кB, IKKα and IKKβ) was activated earlier for a greater time, with higher expression levels than those in the control group, regardless of tissues. The relative NF-кB, IKKα, and IKKβ expression levels were higher in the treatment group than in the control group at 24 h or 36 h in all tissues (p < 0.01). In addition, the relative expression levels showed a trend of periodic upregulation and then down-regulation, whereas the expression trends of IKKα and IKKβ generally followed those of NF-кB (Fig. 3A–H). The highest values of NF-кB, IKKα, and IKKβ were observed in the skin of the treatment group and were 11.29-fold, 7.38-fold, and 12.31-fold higher than those in the skin of the control group, respectively (Fig. 3F).

Effect of Ah on the NF-кB p65

To elucidate the underlying mechanism of NF-кB after Ah, western blotting was performed to explore the NF-кB signaling pathway that was in charge of the inflammatory reaction. The primary antibody we used was derived from human NF-кB p65 (Accession No. NP_068810.3), which has 82.35% amino acid homology with European frog (Accession No. XP_040184801.1) (Fig. S3), and the R. amurensis and R. temporaria are closely related and highly similar at the genetic level.As shown in Fig. 4, Ah decreased the expression level of NF-κB p65 in liver tissues compared with that in the control group, it indicated that NF-κB p65 is phosphorylated to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway.

Discussion

R. amurensis has a keen awareness of changes in the external environment and inhabits areas near wet bushes and puddles. In the R. amurensis A. hydrophila inflammatory infection model, pathological sections of all tissues revealed that significant damage lesions occurred, including numerous cells undergoing pyknosis and karyorhexis and inflammatory cell infiltration. The study on grass carp revealed typical inflammatory symptoms, including bleeding and redness on the skin, post-A. hydrophila infection, mirroring the morphological alterations observed in the leg skin and muscle of R. amurensis similarly infected.23 Concurrently, histopathological examination of grass carp head kidney tissue during initial infection showed multiple vacuoles and blood extravasation. In contrast, R. amurensis exhibited severe blood extravasation but relatively few vacuoles. This indicates that the kidney of R. amurensis plays a critical role in eliminating pathogenic microorganisms from the bloodstream. Research by Kuiquan Pan and Jin-hua Gao demonstrated that A. hydrophila triggers an inflammatory response, resulting in the overexpression of immune-related genes.24,25 Following A. hydrophila infection, the expression levels in the liver exhibit an initial increase, subsequently followed by a decrease. In mandarin fish, the majority of antimicrobial peptide genes respond promptly in the early stages post-A. hydrophila infection. The qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the expression of LepROT, NF-κB, IKKα, and IKKβ was generally elevated in the liver of R. amurensis compared to other tissues in this study. During the initial phase of A. hydrophila infection, there was a marked increase in expression levels. The overall pattern demonstrated an initial upregulation, followed by a subsequent downregulation. In the early stages, A. hydrophila triggers inflammatory responses, resulting in the overexpression of immune-related genes across various immune organs. Notably, liver tissue stands out as a crucial immune organ.

Current studies suggest that leptin can regulate the body’s metabolism, energy homeostasis, and immune response, and is closely related to the occurrence and development of obesity, inflammation, a variety of tumors, as well as the immune response to infection.26 Leptin is involved in the activation of multiple signaling pathways that increase cell proliferation and inhibit apoptosis, such as NF-кB, PI3K, and STAT3.27,28 Leptin, which has been proven to perform numerous functions in mammals, regulates digestive functions in yak cattle through paracrine or autocrine mechanisms, thereby affecting absorption in their rumen.29 Leptin simultaneously inhibits apoptosis and promotes cell proliferation and migration in sheep endometrial cells, thereby regulating embryo implantation.30 Leptin also controls energy homeostasis and reproductive development in the mouse brain.31 However, LepROT can reduce the expression of leptin receptors on cell membranes, negatively regulating leptin signaling and participating in its signaling pathway.32 LepROT is widely expressed in various tissues of R. amurensis, as well as in those of various mammals and D. rerio.33,34 Following A. hydrophila infection, LepROT was significantly upregulated in most of the examined tissues and especially in the skin and liver. This finding is consistent with the primary organ affected by redleg disease, suggesting that A. hydrophila targets specific organs Previous studies in pufferfish and yellow catfish shown high LepROT expression in the skin, suggesting that the skin is a primary site for antigen presentation in aquatic animals and amphibians.35

LepROT signal regulation is intimately linked to the NF-κB signaling pathway. NF-κB regulates and induces numerous pro-inflammatory cytokines, including p52, c-Rel, and p65 subunits in humans,36 and its activation relies on the phosphorylation of its inhibitor IκBα. The kinases IKK-α, IKK-β, and IKK-γ, which are closely related, play a pivotal role in IκBα degradation through the phosphorylation of critical serine residues. In its natural state, NF-кB binds to IκB and remains in the cytoplasm. The Nuclear Localization Sequence (NLS) on NF-кB is masked by IκB, preventing NF-кB from entering the nucleus and enabling IκB to inhibit the NF-кB pathway.37 Leptin plays a crucia role in energy homeostasis and metabolic homeostasis, the molecular targets of leptin action are also responsible for modulating and terminating intracellular signaling pathways in leptin-responsive cells.38,39 Upon binding to LEPR, leptin initiates a cascade of reactions that activate the IKK complex, leading to the phosphorylation and ubiquitination of IκB. This results in the dissociation of the NF-кB-IκB complex, enabling NF-кB to translocate to the nucleus and regulate downstream gene expression.40 The mRNA expression levels of the NF-кB, IKKɑ, and IKKβ in R. amurensis were comparable, suggesting their correlation and confirming the activation of the NF-кB signaling pathway after A. hydrophila infection.

In this study, LepROT was expressed in heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, skin, muscle and stomach, but there was some variation in the in different tissues. Gene expression was significantly higher in liver, skin and spleen during the early stages of infection, these tissues are important immune organs in amphibians and LepROT may have an important involvement in the function of the frog immune system.41 LepROT expression in the lung remained at a high level during the late stages of infection. In contrast, skin LepROT expression was at low levels for 36h after infection, suggesting that the inflammatory response brought about by A. hydrophila caused irreversible damage to skin tissue and that lung tissue has been able to respond positively to this effect.Of particular interest is the expression of LepROT in gastric tissue at 48h of A. hydrophila infection, which would be an important time point, the period when the stomach is most sensitive to the corresponding feedback generated during the body’s immune regulation.At 24 h before A. hydrophila infection, we found that altered expression of LepROT could affect the expression of mRNA and protein of NF-кB signaling pathway, and their expressions changed in similar trends, at which time LepROT might exert its biological function by activating NF-кB signaling pathway. However, the mRNA expression of LepROT and NF-кB signaling pathway after 24h did not follow a regular pattern, and it was speculated that there might be crosstalk of other related signaling pathways, or some of the cells might be damaged leading to the disruption of the immune system. In Fig. 3, NF-кB IKKa and IKKb mRNAs were upregulated in the stomach at 24 h, while LepROT mRNA expression was only upregulated at 48 h in the stomach. This might be due to the involvement of leptin, which is highly expressed in the stomach to take in more nutrients when there is a persistent inflammatory state in the body, and the high expression of leptin genes allows for increased LepROP expression to perform its antagonistic function against leptin.

So far, the information about the immune response of R. amurensis to LepROT after A. hydrophila stress is still unclear. This is the first report on the effect of A. hydrophila infection on the expression of LepROT in various tissues of R. amurensis and the NF-кB signaling pathway. In this study, we observed a significant upregulation of NF-кB IKKa and IKKb mRNA expression during the early stages. Subsequently, after LepROT mRNA expression significantly increased and reached its peak, the expression levels of NF-кB IKKa and IKKb mRNA decreased significantly and remained stable thereafter. These findings suggest that during the initial stages of A. hydrophila attack on R. amurensis, leptin first functions to enhance digestion and improve energy intake efficiency. Subsequently, it activates the NF-κB pathway, allowing the body to respond to external stimuli, promote inflammatory responses, and resist bacterial invasion, thereby protecting the body from external pathogens. However, excessive activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway can lead to excessive inflammatory responses, potentially causing tissue damage and disease. LepROT can reduce the expression of leptin receptors on the cell membrane, negatively regulating leptin signaling and participating in its signaling pathway.32 Therefore, the increase in leptin transcription level activates the NF-κB pathway, while the increase in LepROT can inhibit the function of leptin protein by reducing the expression of leptin receptors, thus negatively regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway and reducing the damage caused by excessive activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway to the body, maintaining cellular homeostasis. In this process, LepROT does not directly affect the transcription level of leptin but rather influences its protein expression function. Therefore, in our immunoblotting experiments, we verified the reduction in NF-κB p65 protein expression, demonstrating that LepROT only affects the downstream function of leptin protein at the protein level, specifically its ability to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway. However, further studies are needed to better understand the underlying biochemical mechanisms, to explore the relationship between LepROT and immunity, to elucidate the function of LepROT, and to propose methods for nutritional immunity in amphibians.In addition, the association of the signal regulation pathway of leptin with natural immunity needs further investigation to provide information for disease prevention and resistance in R. amurensis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we show that that under A. hydrophila stress, LepROT responds during the overexpression of NF-κB signaling pathway-related genes in various tissues of R. amurensis. LepROT inhibits the expression and function of NF-κB signaling pathway-related proteins, regulates the homeostasis of the internal environment, and reduces the harm caused by excessive immune response to the body.
