Porto Sinusoidal Vascular Disorder Manifesting as Diffuse Arterio-Portal Fistulae and Portal Hypertension After Donation-After-Circulatory-Death Liver Transplantation
Authors: Drew T. Castleberry, Fares A. Mashal, James Meek, Felicia Allard, Mauricio Garcia Saenz de Sicilia, Emmanouil Giorgakis, Ragesh B. Thandassery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIschemic cholangiopathy (ICP) is a rare complication of orthotopic liver transplantation (LT), with an increased incidence in donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) recipients, particularly without machine perfusion. As DCD utilization increases, challenges arise. We report a rare case of DCD-LT resulting in ICP, complicated by diffuse intrahepatic arterio-portal fistulae (APF) leading to portal hypertension and refractory ascites without any other cause. The candidate was relisted for LT, and the explant revealed diffuse intrahepatic APF and porto-sinusoidal vascular disorder (PSVD) without cirrhosis. To the best of our knowledge, no similar reports exist of LT recipients developing PSVD or diffuse APF, and their concurrence with ICP after DCD-LT raises concern for an association. This case report highlights the importance of considering diffuse APF and PSVD in LT recipients with ICP who develop features of portal hypertension without a clear cirrhotic etiology.

INTRODUCTION

Ischemic cholangiopathy (ICP) is a well-documented complication of donation-after-circulatory-death (DCD) orthotopic liver transplantation (LT), with reported incidence of 12%-34% of recipients.1,2 Arterio-portal fistulae (APF) are rare communications between the portal venous system and hepatic arterial circulation and may lead to portal hypertension, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and varices. Porto-sinusoidal vascular disorder (PSVD) is defined as vascular abnormalities impacting sinusoids and portal venules in the absence of cirrhosis.3 This case report describes a DCD-LT complicated by ICP and found to have PSVD and APF on explant, a concurrence not previously reported in the literature.

CASE REPORT

A 64-year-old man with a history of alcohol-related decompensated cirrhosis underwent DCD-LT without machine perfusion support. The organ had a cold ischemia time of 5 hours and a warm ischemia time of 15 minutes; the surgery was uneventful. After anastomosis, the hepatic artery was noted to be patent intraoperatively. Postoperatively, he experienced resolution of ascites and marked improvement in thrombocytopenia in the initial weeks. Two months post-transplant, he developed progressive elevation of liver function tests (Table 1). Initial laboratory test were noncontributory; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was normal, and liver biopsy showed no rejection. Doppler ultrasound revealed patent vasculature with no concern for hepatic artery thrombosis or stenosis. Endoscopic retrograde pancreato-cholangiography revealed early changes of diffuse biliary narrowing, with repeat endoscopic retrograde pancreato-cholangiography 2 months later demonstrating progression to multifocal progressive biliary disease, suggestive of type C ICP. His cholestatic symptoms, including jaundice and pruritus, had worsened, requiring multiple hospitalizations for cholangitis, serial biliary stricture dilation, stent exchanges, and removal of biliary casts. Eight months post-LT, he developed small esophageal varices on endoscopy and gradually worsening ascites, requiring repeated large-volume paracenteses; ascitic fluid showed a high serum-ascites albumin gradient. MRI revealed diffuse APF in the liver, subsequently confirmed with hepatic angiography (Figure 1). Owing to diffuse APF in both lobes, angioembolization and closure were not feasible. Ascites recurrence was postulated to have occurred from portal hypertension secondary to diffuse APF. He met criteria for retransplantation for ICP with a baseline Model for End-Stage Liver Disease 3.0 approximately 20, with higher scores during cholangitic episodes. He underwent retransplantation with a donation-after-brain-death graft 10 months postinitial transplant. Liver explant revealed absent or indistinct small portal veins and muscular hypertrophy in medium-sized portal veins; there were no features of cirrhosis. The biopsy findings (Figure 2) were consistent with hepatoportal sclerosis, indicative of PSVD. Biopsy showed no changes of rejection. His postretransplant course was uneventful and showed excellent graft function at 2 years of follow-up.

DISCUSSION

PSVD describes a group of vascular disorders of the noncirrhotic liver encompassing lesions in portal venules and sinusoids. Liver biopsy is essential for diagnosing PSVD.3 Specific histology findings include nodular regenerative hyperplasia, hepatoportal sclerosis, and incomplete septal fibrosis. PSVD has been associated with drug exposure, immune disorders, autoimmune diseases, vascular disorders, and rare congenital conditions. PSVD can result in abnormal hepatic arterial inflow and often presents clinically with unexplained portal hypertension.3 No coexisting condition could be identified in the index case other than ICP. We report the first instance of ICP after DCD-LT with associated diffuse APF and PSVD, resulting in decompensated liver disease successfully managed with re-transplantation.

APF represents abnormal connections between the hepatic arterial and portal venous systems.4 Etiologies of APF include congenital and idiopathic causes, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and inflammatory or infective diseases of the liver. APF can lead to obstruction of the hepatic or portal vein and iatrogenic vascular injury resulting from procedures, including percutaneous liver biopsy, percutaneous hepatic cholangiograms, chemoembolization, and surgery. APF is noted in up to 5.4% of cases in hepatic arteriograms of LT recipients, although hemodynamically significant APF in the transplanted liver occurs in 0.2% of recipients.5 Incidence is similar between living-related (split grafts) and deceased donor (split or whole liver) transplant recipients. Mechanistically, current belief is that compromised arterial flow and the relatively poor compliance of transplanted livers result in either resolution or, less commonly, progression of small APF to larger or hemodynamically significant APF.5 In the index case, ICP was associated with and potentially led to diffuse intrahepatic APF and PSVD; a causal association could not be established. Symptomatic APF usually presents with portal hypertension-related complications, including ascites, gastrointestinal bleeding, or high-output heart failure. Less common complications include sepsis, haemobilia, biliary obstruction, and, rarely, pulmonary hypertension. While catheter-directed digital subtraction angiography remains the gold standard for diagnosing APF, noninvasive modalities, including MRI and Doppler ultrasound, are increasingly used due to their noninvasiveness, high sensitivity, and specificity. Traditionally, management of a symptomatic, hemodynamically significant fistula involves surgical intervention.5,6 However, most of these cases are now treated with endovascular methods. LT becomes imperative in challenging and complicated cases of APF where no treatable connection is identified and when significantly decompensated liver disease is present.

LT in PSVD is associated with impressive outcomes, especially when no other systemic diseases are present. In a retrospective multicenter study of 79 LT patients, post-LT survival was 82.2%, 80.7%, and 68.6% at 1, 2, and 5 years, respectively.6 In this study, patients with PSVD with other comorbidities had lower survival. High pre-LT bilirubin and creatinine levels were associated with poor survival, with 7.6% required to retransplant and 5% had a probable recurrence of PSVD.7

Post-transplant biliary complications present significant challenges and implications for morbidity, retransplantation, and mortality. Among the various factors contributing to its etiology, ICP is characterized by inadequate blood supply to the biliary system, resulting in diffuse strictures in the absence of hepatic artery thrombosis or stenosis.8 ICP is more commonly reported in recipients of DCD grafts without machine perfusion, the underlying pathophysiology largely attributed to ischemia-reperfusion injury from prolonged warm ischemia time during organ retrieval and extended cold ischemia time during preservation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first documented case of ICP-associated APF manifesting as diffuse APF post-DCD-LT. Remarkably, no other antecedent cause accounts for this finding except for clinically evident ICP. Previous case reports of APF in post-transplant patients were due to vascular trauma during percutaneous procedures, donor liver trauma, or portal vein thrombosis.5,6,9 The diffuse pattern of the observed fistulae in the index case makes it highly unlikely that they were linked to the liver biopsy procedure performed during the patient's initial evaluation. With the increased utilization of DCD organs in recent years, these rare presentations have clinical significance, and clinicians should consider diffuse APF and PSVD in a post-LT patient with ICP who develops features of portal hypertension without a clear cirrhotic etiology. Further investigation is needed to clarify the pathophysiology of APF formation and PSVD in the context of ICP and to explore effective treatment strategies.
