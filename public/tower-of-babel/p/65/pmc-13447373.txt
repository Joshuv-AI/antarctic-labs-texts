Transcutaneous Auricular Vagus Nerve Stimulation to Improve Daily-Life Gait in Parkinson’s Disease: A Case Report
Authors: Yohei Okada, Yuki Nishi, Naoya Hasegawa, Hiroshi Kataoka, Junji Nakamura, Taiyo Kai, Koki Ikuno, Sachio Nabeshima, Ryotaro Doko, Rina Nishimoto, Rio Fujita, Yuki Mori, Ayato Yoshinaga, Koji Shomoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTranscutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a promising non-invasive neuromodulation approach for addressing gait impairments in Parkinson’s disease (PD). However, evidence regarding its effects on daily-life gait remains limited. We report a case of a 67-year-old woman with PD and freezing of gait (FOG), based on the hypothesis that taVNS may enhance gait automaticity under daily-life conditions. A single-case A–B–C design was used, consisting of baseline, sham stimulation, and taVNS phases. Gait performance was assessed using wearable accelerometry in both experimental and daily-life settings. While no significant changes were observed in gait parameters during experimental assessments, daily-life walking measures—step velocity, step length, variability, and asymmetry—showed improvements during the taVNS phase. Importantly, no falls or FOG episodes were observed during the taVNS phase. These findings support the feasibility of taVNS and provide preliminary support for its potential therapeutic role in enhancing gait automaticity in daily life. Further controlled studies are warranted.Keywords: Parkinson Disease, Vagus Nerve Stimulation, Gait, Wearable Electronic Devices

INTRODUCTION

Transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation (taVNS) is a promising non-invasive neuromodulation technique for addressing gait impairments in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) [1,2,3]. Previous studies [2,3] have reported improvements in gait speed, step length, and reductions in gait variability and asymmetry following taVNS—parameters associated with impaired gait automaticity in patients with PD [4]. Mechanistically, taVNS may enhance gait automaticity by modulating the cholinergic system and decreasing sensorimotor cortex activity during walking [1,3]. Importantly, temporal gait abnormalities in PD are often insufficiently improved by pharmacological treatment [5].

Gait disturbances in PD often worsen in daily-life environments compared to experimental settings, underscoring the importance of evaluating gait under daily-life conditions [6,7]. Walking in daily-life contexts is thought to rely more heavily on automatic control than gait in controlled environments. Therefore, taVNS, which may promote gait automaticity, may exert greater effects in daily-life settings than in experimental assessments. However, previous taVNS studies in PD have primarily focused on gait performance under structured experimental conditions, and the effects of taVNS on free-living daily-life gait behavior remain unclear. Therefore, this exploratory single-case study aimed to investigate the feasibility and potential therapeutic benefits of taVNS in improving daily-life gait performance in a patient with PD and freezing of gait (FOG).

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 67-year-old cognitively normal woman (Mini-Mental State Examination score: 30) with a 17-year history of PD sought further improvement in walking after a 3-month outpatient physical therapy program. She reported worsened walking and occasional FOG during OFF periods and had no history of deep brain stimulation, cardiovascular disease, or pacemaker use. This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (Kio University, R5-33-2) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Both sham and taVNS were delivered in a single 30-minute seated session at the beginning of each respective phase using identical parameters (20 Hz, 500 μs [3], 30 seconds on/off), differing only in electrode placement (left earlobe vs. left cymba conchae) (Fig. 1B). Stimulation intensity was gradually increased from 0 mA to the highest tolerable level without pain, resulting in a final intensity of 1.6 mA. The same intensity was applied in both sham stimulation and taVNS conditions to maintain comparable stimulation parameters across conditions. No formal washout period was incorporated between phases. However, stimulation sessions were separated by one week of daily-life gait monitoring, and the sham phase preceded the taVNS phase, which may have reduced potential carryover effects.

Gait was assessed using a wearable accelerometer (AX6, L5 placement, 100 Hz; Axivity Ltd., Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) in both experimental and daily-life settings. Experimental assessments included two 10-meter walk tests and the Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS) Part III. During free-living daily life conditions, the accelerometer was worn continuously, except during bathing and sleeping. Daily-life gait was continuously monitored under daily-life conditions, and walking bouts (≥ 8 steps) [6,7] were analyzed by a blinded analyst. Walking activities were identified based on the upright orientation of the sensor and predefined thresholds for vertical acceleration, defined as a mean value > 0.05 and a standard deviation > 0.77 [8].

Gait outcomes—step velocity, step length, step time variability, and step time asymmetry—were derived from lower-back (L5) accelerometry sampled at 100 Hz using validated algorithms. Accelerometer data were downloaded to a computer and processed using MATLAB 2022a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA). Signals were converted into a horizontal–vertical coordinate system and filtered using a 20 Hz fourth-order Butterworth filter. Initial contacts (ICs) were identified using continuous wavelet transform (CWT) of vertical acceleration signals, processed in MATLAB with the cwt function. The acceleration signal was first integrated (cumtrapz) and then differentiated using Gaussian CWT. IC events were determined as the local minimum of the CWT signal (detected with the findpeaks function). After identifying the IC event for each foot, step time was calculated for each step. Step length was estimated using an inverted pendulum model, and step velocity was calculated as step length divided by step time. Step time variability was defined as the standard deviation of step times within each walking bout, and step time asymmetry as the absolute mean difference between consecutive steps. Phase comparisons were conducted using Tau-U statistics with a significance level of p < 0.05.

The participant completed the study without adverse effects. Under experimental conditions, no FOG episodes were observed, and no clear changes were found in the MDS-UPDRS gait and FOG subscores or gait parameters across the baseline, sham, and taVNS phases (Table 2). In contrast, one FOG episode and one fall occurred in phase A, one fall in phase B, and neither event occurred in phase C under daily-life conditions. In daily-life gait, step velocity and step length significantly increased, while step time variability and asymmetry decreased during taVNS (Table 3, Fig. 1C-F).

DISCUSSION

Gait parameters—including step velocity, step length, step time variability, and step time asymmetry—improved under daily-life conditions during the taVNS phase, while no significant differences were observed in experimental assessments. These findings suggest that taVNS may improve gait control in daily-life settings. One possible explanation for this dissociation is that gait automaticity is more strongly challenged under complex and unpredictable daily-life conditions than in structured experimental settings, where attentional resources may partially compensate for impaired automatic control. Daily-life environments require ongoing adaptation to dynamic contexts, which may place greater demands on automatic gait control than controlled laboratory assessments. taVNS is known to modulate the cholinergic system [1], which has been associated with gait variability. Degeneration of the cholinergic basal forebrain, particularly the nucleus basalis of Meynert, has been shown to predict future increases in step time variability in PD, highlighting the importance of cholinergic pathways in gait control [9]. Given that temporal gait abnormalities often show limited response to dopaminergic medication [5], taVNS may serve as a complementary, non-dopaminergic intervention. The observed reduction in temporal gait variability in this case may therefore reflect enhanced cholinergic network function.

Reduced gait variability and asymmetry under daily-life conditions suggest enhanced gait automaticity, potentially supported by previously reported reductions in sensorimotor cortex activity during walking after taVNS [3]. Daily-life assessments may therefore be more sensitive to detecting changes in automatic gait control than structured tests that require attentional resources. The absence of measurable changes under experimental conditions may also reflect a ceiling effect, as the participant exhibited relatively mild gait impairment during the ON medication state. Notably, modest improvements were also observed during the sham phase, suggesting a possible placebo or expectation-related effect. However, the magnitude of change was greater during the taVNS phase, which may indicate an additional stimulation-related contribution beyond nonspecific effects. Although causal inference cannot be established from a single case, the consistency of improvements across multiple gait parameters strengthens the plausibility of a stimulation-related effect.

Importantly, no FOG episodes or falls occurred during the taVNS phase. This case highlights the feasibility of taVNS and its potential therapeutic role in improving daily-life gait. These findings serve as proof-of-concept and provide preliminary support for taVNS as a strategy to enhance gait automaticity in daily life, underscoring the importance of evaluating neuromodulation effects in ecologically valid conditions. The present findings also suggest that daily-life wearable gait assessment may capture clinically meaningful changes that are not always detectable during structured experimental evaluations.
