Economic cost of movement behaviours: a systematic review
Authors: Sitong Chen, Tena Matolic, Aamir Raoof Memon, Jerome N Rachele, Jozo Grgic, Manuel Antonio Espinoza, Zeljko Pedisic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPublic health research and practice are shifting towards integrative consideration of physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep, as interdependent components of daily time use. However, no previous reviews have synthesised evidence on economic costs associated with these movement behaviours. The aim of this systematic review was to synthesise evidence on economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration. Literature searches were conducted in Open Dissertations, PsycINFO, PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science. Publications that provided estimates of total, direct, or indirect monetary cost of any of the three behaviours in the general adult population were included. Out of 21,418 screened references, 40 papers met the inclusion criteria (77.5% from high-income countries; 77.5% on economic costs of PA only; 90% reporting estimates based on self-reported movement-behaviour data). In Canada (the only country for which estimates of economic costs of all three movement behaviours were available) the combined total annual cost of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration was 9.2 billion USD or 0.41% of the gross domestic product. In studies using the population attributable fraction (PAF) based approach, the total annual national costs of insufficient PA and excessive SB ranged from 1.94 to 9.78 billion USD and 1.82 to 1.97 billion USD, respectively. In the only study that used the PAF-based approach to estimate the cost of inadequate sleep duration, the total national cost in Canada was 0.44 billion USD per year. Globally, billions USD per year are cumulatively spent on adverse consequences of unhealthy movement behaviours. However, the exact cost could not be estimated, because evidence was available only for a limited number of mostly high-income countries. In the included studies, the cost of insufficient PA was approximately 2–4 times higher than the cost of excessive SB and 16 times higher than the cost of inadequate sleep duration; hence it seems that public health initiatives should focus primarily on promoting PA. More research is needed, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, using device-based movement-behaviour data, and exploring costs of excessive SB, inadequate sleep duration, and not meeting the overall 24-hour movement guidelines.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s44167-026-00108-z.Keywords: 24-hour movement behaviours, Physical activity, Sedentary behaviour, Sleep, Economic burden, Cost analysis

Introduction

Movement behaviours, including physical activity (PA), sedentary behaviour (SB), and sleep, are associated with various health outcomes [1]. Given that the amounts of time spent in PA, SB, and sleep always add up to 24 h/day, the time in one of the behaviours can only be increased at the expense of the time in the remaining behaviours, and vice versa [2]. Due to this interdependency between PA, SB, and sleep, it is important to consider these behaviours collectively, for both research and public health promotion purposes [3]. To address this, integrative 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines have been issued in several countries [4–7]. According to the guidelines, to achieve and maintain good health, adults should engage in 150–300 min of moderate or 75–150 min/week of vigorous PA, limit their SB to no more than 8 h/day, and sleep 7–9 h/day (young and middle-aged adults) or 7–8 h/day (older adults) [4]. Given that not meeting these guidelines is highly prevalent in different populations [8–10], there is a need to develop and implement population-level policies and interventions aimed at increasing PA, reducing sedentary time, and achieving adequate sleep duration.

Unhealthy behaviours, such as insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration, can be associated with direct and indirect economic costs resulting from associated diseases and health conditions [11–13]. The former primarily include expenses on inpatient and outpatient healthcare services (e.g. doctor consultations, medical treatments, hospitalisation, prescription medication, and rehabilitation), while the latter encompass losses due to absenteeism, presenteeism, early retirement, disability pensions, and premature mortality. Direct costs can often be quantified relatively accurately at the individual (e.g. via health insurance records) and population (e.g. via national healthcare expenditure statistics) levels. In contrast, indirect costs are often approximated (e.g. via estimated productivity loss in the friction period—the time needed for an organization to replace an ill or deceased worker).

To provide an economic justification for the development public health policies and interventions aimed at increasing PA, reducing SB, and achieving optimal duration of sleep, it is important to determine how much money could be saved at the national level if more individuals would meet movement behaviour guidelines. Such calculations need to take into account both direct and indirect economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration. Estimates of economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration may increase political will and motivate policy actions to address unhealthy movement behaviours in the population [14]. They could also help prioritise resource allocation and inform decision making in the public health sector [15].

For this purpose, previous studies have estimated national economic cost of low PA, high SB, and inadequate sleep in various countries [11, 16–18]. Findings of individual studies on economic cost of low PA and high SB were synthesised in the systematic reviews by Ding and colleagues [16] and Nguyen and colleagues [17], respectively. The Ding et al. review [16] included 40 cost-of-illness studies, of which 23 used the population attributable fraction [PAF]-based approach and 17 used the individual-level data approach to calculate direct costs, while 13 of them also estimated indirect costs. Based on the studies using the PAF approach, the review concluded that 0.3% to 4.6% of the national healthcare expenditure is attributable to low PA, while noting that estimates varied significantly, even within a single country [16]. The Nguyen et al. review [17] included six economic evaluations and three cost-of-illness studies (of which one used PAF-based approach and two used the individual-level data approach to calculate direct costs). The review concluded that healthcare costs of high SB are substantial, and that sedentary behaviour interventions in the workplace are generally cost-effective [17].

The Ding et al. [16] and Nguyen et al. [17] reviews encompassed studies published by October 2016 and March 2021, respectively. Several studies on economic cost of insufficient PA and excessive SB have been published since these two systematic reviews [16, 17]. For example, recent studies reported on economic costs of insufficient PA in Brazil [19], USA [20], Thailand [21] and on economic cost of excessive SB in Australia [22], Canada [12], and Finland [23]. Their findings remain to be synthesised in a systematic review. Furthermore, the two reviews included studies that calculated costs for various categories of low PA and high SB. For example, in the review on PA [16], the economic costs were calculated for any vs. no vigorous PA, leisure-time energy expenditure ≥ 1.5 vs. <1.5 kcal/kg/day, and PA quartiles. In the review on SB [17], the economic cost were calculated for low (≤ 4 h/day) vs. moderate (5–7 h/day) vs. high (≥ 8 h/day) SB, reduction in sitting time, and increase in standing time. The differences in definitions of exposure and comparison groups make it challenging to synthesise findings of different studies included in the reviews. Focusing on studies that used more consistent categorisation of PA and SB (e.g. using thresholds according to the 24-hour movement guidelines) is needed to enable direct between-study comparisons. In contrast, while several individual studies have examined the economic cost of sleep [11, 20, 24, 25], their findings have not been systematically reviewed. Importantly, no previous systematic review has examined the economic costs all three movement behaviours combined.

Therefore, the aim of this review study was to systematically review and synthesise evidence on economic cost of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration.

Methods

Study protocol

The protocol for this systematic review was registered in the International Platform of Registered Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (ref: INPLASY202540110). Discrepancies from the registered protocol are listed in Supplementary file 1. This review was written according to the updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist [26].

Literature search and study selection

The literature search was conducted through titles, abstracts, and keywords of documents published in English and indexed in the following electronic databases: Open Dissertations; PsycINFO; PubMed/MEDLINE; Scopus; SPORTDiscus; and Web of Science (including Emerging Sources Citation Index, Science Citation Index–Expanded, and Social Sciences Citation Index) from database inception until 30th of June 2025, using a set of keywords for movement behaviours in combination with a set of keywords for economic costs. The full list search syntax is presented in Supplementary file 2. The reference lists of included documents were screened for any additional relevant studies. The study selection was performed independently by two researchers (SC and TM) according to the predefined criteria. Any potential disagreements in study selection were resolved by their discussions with a third author (ZP).

Inclusion criteria

Peer-reviewed journal articles, theses, and reports published in English that provided quantitative estimates of the economic cost (including total, direct, or indirect monetary cost) of movement behaviours (operationally defined as “not meeting public health recommendations for PA, SB, and/or sleep”) in the general adult population (aged 18 years and above) were included in this review.

Data extraction

From the included studies, we extracted the following information: author(s), published year; country/region; study perspective; data sources; sample characteristics; measures of movement behaviours and healthcare costs; operational definitions of not meeting PA, SB, and/or sleep recommendations; health outcomes (e.g., cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer) considered in the calculation of economic costs; estimation method (i.e., PAF-based approach or individual-level data approach); time frame (e.g., 1 year, or multiple years); cost type (i.e., total, direct or indirect, as specified in the given study); discount rate; and monetary cost of not meeting PA, SB, and/or sleep recommendations estimated using the PAF-based approach or individual-level data approach. Data extraction was performed by one author (SC) and checked for accuracy by another author (TM). Two multi-country studies included in this review [13, 27] used the data on healthcare costs of different diseases from the European Union member states to estimate the respective costs in the remaining countries. Given that such estimates may not accurately reflect the true economic costs, from these two studies, we only extracted results for the European Union member states.

Data synthesis

For studies that used the individual-level data approach in a population-representative sample and reported the average cost of not meeting PA guidelines per person, the total population cost was calculated as: . For this purpose, the national prevalence of insufficient PA in the general adult population was taken from Strain et al. [28], while the population size in the year for which the cost was estimated was taken from the United Nations [29]. These calculations were only done for economic costs of PA, because none of the included studies on SB and sleep that used the individual-level data approach were conducted in a national-representative sample.

For all studies, the reported costs were adjusted for inflation using the annual consumer prices index (CPI) inflation indicators from the World Bank for 2024 [30]. For example, for a study that reported a cost in 2015, the cost adjusted for inflation was calculated as: . These calculations were performed using CPIs both adjusted and unadjusted for Purchasing Power Parity (PPP). The costs adjusted for inflation were converted into USD using the 2024 annual exchange rate (official exchange rate) provided by the World Bank [31]. The nominal total, direct, and indirect costs were expressed as a percentage of the nominal national gross domestic product for 2024 (the most recent available data), provided by the World Bank [32]. The nominal direct costs were also expressed as a percentage of the nominal overall national healthcare cost (calculated based on the nominal gross domestic product) in 2022, using the most recent data provided by the World Bank [33].

To enable comparisons between countries, the most recent national estimates were presented in bar plots. Plots were created separately for studies that used PAF-based approach and studies that used the individual-level data approach and included similar health outcomes in their cost estimation. In the plots with costs estimated using the PAF-based approach, we included only the studies that used a similar set of health outcomes in their calculations, namely, 3–5 of the following major non-communicable diseases: coronary artery disease, stroke, hypertension, cancer, and type 2 diabetes. In the plots with costs estimated using the individual-level data approach, we included only the studies that were conducted in population-representative samples of adults (18 + years of age) and used medical care cost data in their calculations.

Methodological quality assessment

The methodological quality of included studies was assessed using the Consensus-Based Checklist for the Critical Appraisal of Cost-of-Illness (COI) Studies [34]. The checklist consists of 17 items related to: (1) question and objective of the study; (2) study population; (3) study perspective; (4) epidemiological approach; (5) costing approach; (6) data collection; (7) identification of resources; (8) measurement of resources; (9) valuation of resources; (10) time horizon; (11) discounting; (12) sensitivity analysis; (13) cost sectors; (14) generalisability; (15) limitations; (16) ethical and distributional issues; and (17) conflict of interest, with “Yes”, “No”, “Partially”, “Not applicable”, and “Unclear” as response options. Two authors (SC and ARM) independently conducted the quality assessment, and disagreements between the assessments were resolved through discussion. The overall score on a scale from 0 to 100% was calculated as the sum of points for affirmative (1 point) or partially affirmative (0.5 points) responses to the items divided by the number of applicable items and multiplied by 100. Methodological quality of studies with the overall score of 0–33.3%, 33.3%–66.6%, and 66.6%–100.0% was considered as “low”, “moderate”, and “high”, respectively.

Results

Search results

A total of 19,746 documents were identified through the search in bibliographic databases (Fig. 1). After removing 6,645 duplicates and 12,902 ineligible documents through title and abstract screening, 199 full-texts were reviewed. Of these, 40 papers met the inclusion criteria [11–13, 19–25, 27, 35–63]. An additional 1,672 references were screened as part of the secondary searches. However, no additional eligible papers were identified.

Study characteristics

Out of 40 included studies, 77.5% (n = 31) were conducted in high-income countries, including Australia, Canada, the Czech Republic, Finland, Germany, New Zealand, Saudi Arabia, Switzerland, the UK, and the US (Table 1 and Supplementary Table 1). The remaining studies (17.5%, n = 7) were conducted in upper-middle-income countries, including Brazil, China, and Thailand, or in multiple countries with different income levels (5.0%, n = 2). Most of the included studies (77.5%; n = 31) calculated economic cost of insufficient PA only. Two studies (5.0%) considered both insufficient PA and excessive SB. The remaining studies considered either inadequate sleep duration (12.5%; n = 5) or excessive SB (5.0%; n = 2) only. The vast majority (90%; n = 36) of the included studies used self-reported movement behaviour data, while only four (10%) studies used device-based data.

Out of 33 studies that assessed PA, 72.7% (n = 24) defined insufficient PA as less than 150 min/week of moderate to vigorous PA (MVPA), while the remaining studies (27.3%, n = 9) used other definitions (e.g. <600 MET-min/week, < 5 days/week of at least 30 min of moderate PA). Excessive SB was defined as 8 h or more in all four studies that assessed SB. In four (80%) out of five studies that assessed sleep, the operational definition of inadequate sleep duration was “less than 6 hours” or “less than 7 hours”, while one study considered both too short (< 6–7 h) and too long (> 8–9 h) sleep duration.

Costs associated with cardiovascular diseases (e.g., coronary heart disease and stroke), cancers (e.g., breast and colon cancer), and type 2 diabetes were included in 21 (52.5%), 12 (30%), and 12 (30%) out of 40 studies, respectively. Other health outcomes (e.g. dementia, depression, osteoporosis) were considered in 11 (27.5%) out of 40 studies. Fourteen out of forty (35%) studies did not specify which health outcomes were considered in their calculations.

Direct, indirect, and total costs were reported in 38 (95.0%), 14 (35.0%) and 12 (30.0%) out of 40 included studies, respectively. Twenty-five (62.5%) out of 40 studies estimated cost for one year, while the remaining 15 (37.5%) studies used longer time frames. Half of the included studies (n = 20) used the individual-level data approach, while the remaining half (n = 20) used the PAF-based approach.

Cost of inadequate sleep duration

The only study that used the PAF-based approach to estimate the cost of inadequate sleep duration was conducted in Canada [11]. The direct, indirect, and total national costs were 0.42 (self-reported sleep data; cost of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, cognitive disorders, work injuries/accidents, obesity, and hypertension), 0.02 (self-reported sleep data; cost of health-related productivity losses), and 0.44 (self-reported data; cost of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, cognitive disorders, work injuries/accidents, obesity, hypertension, health-related productivity losses) billion USD per year, which was equal to 0.02% (self-reported sleep data; cost of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, cognitive disorders, work injuries/accidents, obesity, and hypertension), 0.001% (self-reported sleep data; cost of health-related productivity losses), and 0.02% (self-reported sleep data; direct and indirect costs combined) of the gross domestic product, respectively. The direct cost of inadequate sleep duration comprised 0.17% (self-reported sleep data; cost of coronary heart disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, cognitive disorders, work injuries/accidents, obesity, and hypertension) of the overall national healthcare cost.

In two of the studies that used the individual-level data approach [25, 63], the direct costs of inadequate sleep duration were 53.95 (China; self-reported sleep data; overall healthcare cost) and 130.19 (the US; self-reported sleep data; outpatient, inpatient, emergency, home healthcare, office-based visit, prescription, and other costs) billion USD per year, which was equal to 0.29% (China; self-reported sleep data; overall healthcare cost) and 0.45% (the US; self-reported sleep data; outpatient, inpatient, emergency, home healthcare, office-based visit, prescription, and other costs) of the gross domestic product or to 2.83% (the US; self-reported sleep data; outpatient, inpatient, emergency, home healthcare, office-based visit, prescription, other costs) and 5.83% (China; self-reported sleep data; overall healthcare cost) of the overall national healthcare costs, respectively. In the only study that reported the indirect cost of inadequate sleep duration [25], the estimated value was 832.41 (China; self-reported sleep data; overall healthcare cost) billion USD, which was equal to 4.44% of the gross domestic product.

Overall cost of movement behaviours

In Canada, data on national costs calculated using the PAF-based approach were available for insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration; hence, the overall cost of movement behaviours could be estimated as the sum of their individual costs. The direct, indirect, and total national costs of movement behaviours in Canada were 4.71 billion, 4.49 billion, and 9.20 billion USD per year (Fig. 2), which was equal to 0.21%, 0.20%, and 0.41% of the gross domestic product (Supplementary Fig. 1), respectively. The direct cost of movement behaviours in Canada comprised 1.89% of the overall national healthcare cost (Supplementary Fig. 2). In other countries, data on national costs were not available for all three movement behaviours.

Comparisons between direct and indirect costs

When comparing cost types for a given country reported within a single study (Supplementary Table 4), the direct costs were higher than indirect costs in 84.2% of comparisons. The median direct and indirect costs of movement behaviours in 38 such comparisons amounted to 70.4% and 29.6% of the overall cost, respectively.

Cross-country comparisons

Direct comparisons of costs calculated using the PAF-based approach were possible for a total of 12 studies including estimates from 32 countries (Supplementary Table 2). The highest relative direct and total costs of insufficient PA were found in Finland, followed by Canada and Switzerland (Supplementary Table 2). The highest relative indirect costs of insufficient PA were found in Finland, followed by Canada, and Switzerland (Supplementary Table 2).

Direct comparisons of costs calculated using the individual-level data approach were possible for a total of 5 studies from 3 countries (Supplementary Fig. 3). The highest relative direct cost of insufficient PA was found in China, followed by the US.

Methodological quality of included studies

The overall methodological quality assessment scores for the included studies ranged from 61.3% to 96.8% (Supplementary Table 3). The methodological quality of most (87.50%; n = 35) studies was categorised as “high”. The remaining studies (12.50%; n = 5) were of moderate methodological quality. The most common methodological issue in the included studies was not clearly stating and justifying the study perspective, followed by not conducting sensitivity analyses, and not considering potential distributional issues.

Discussion

Key findings

The key findings of this review are: (1) cumulatively, billions USD per year could be saved globally, if the whole world’s population met the 24-hour movement guidelines, but the exact cost could not be estimated, because evidence was available only for a limited number of mostly high-income countries; (2) in Canada (the only country for which estimates of economic costs of PA, SB, and sleep were available) the combined total annual cost of all three movement behaviours was 9.2 billion USD or 0.41% of the gross domestic product, likely representing an upward-biased estimate of the total cost, as the calculation was done by simply adding up estimated costs for individual behaviours from three separate studies, without taking into account compositional properties of time-use data; (3) the economic cost of PA, SB, and sleep in other countries with available data were generally also very high, with significant variability across different studies; (4) in the included studies, the costs of insufficient PA was approximately 2–4 and 16 times higher than the costs of excessive SB and inadequate sleep duration, respectively; (5) most studies were conducted in high-income countries, while knowledge about the economic cost of movement behaviours in low-and-middle-income countries remains limited; and (6) very few studies provided estimates for SB and sleep, while no studies assessed the cost of all three movement behaviours combined.

Combined economic cost of movement behaviours

According to the Viable Integrative Research in Time-Use Epidemiology (VIRTUE) framework, movement behaviours should be considered together when assessing their economic and other impacts [2]. However, our review found that no studies have assessed the combined cost of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration. Moreover, we found that economic costs of all three movement behaviours (albeit from separate studies) are available only for Canada. The combined economic cost of movement behaviours in Canada, estimated as the sum of economic costs for PA, SB, and sleep, is almost two times higher than the economic cost of obesity (5.07 billion USD per year, adjusted for inflation) [64] and approximately the same as the economic cost of tobacco use (9.28 billion USD per year, adjusted for inflation) [65]. When expressed as the percentage of GDP, the cost of movement behaviours is Canada is almost as high, highlighting the relevance of movement behaviours in the context of other economic costs related to non-communicable disease. When interpreting this combined cost of movement behaviours, it should be taken into account that it was calculated by simply adding up estimated costs of individual behaviours from three separate studies. Summing costs across co-dependent exposures may inflate the combined estimate, unless dependencies were explicitly addressed in their calculations. Given that the three Canadian studies [11, 12, 46] from which we calculated the combined costs of movement behaviours did not take into account compositional properties of time-use data, our estimate likely represents an upward-biased estimate of the total cost.

Our findings suggest that a significant amount of money could be saved in Canada by reducing the prevalence of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration. Based on the Canadian data, high combined economic costs of movement behaviours could also be assumed for other countries with similar healthcare expenditure and prevalence of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration, suggesting that cumulatively billions USD per year could be saved globally, if the whole world’s population met the 24-hour movement guidelines. However, the exact cost could not be estimated in our review, because evidence was available only for a limited number of mostly high-income countries.

Economic costs of physical activity, sedentary behaviour, and sleep

The economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration found in this review were generally very high, which is in accordance with findings from previous reviews [16, 17]. However, it should be noted that the variability of economic costs across different countries was also very high. The high variability in absolute economic costs across countries could be explained by between-country differences in the national healthcare expenditure and prevalence of “unhealthy” movement behaviours. For example, the overall healthcare expenditure adjusted for inflation was 6.94 billion USD in Canada [46] and 1.94 billion USD in Switzerland [51], while the prevalence of insufficient PA was 40.4% in Cananda [28] and 21.9% in Switzerland [28].

Large differences between countries were also found for relative economic costs, expressed as the percentage of gross domestic product and healthcare expenditure, which further highlights the importance of country-specific estimations of economic costs of movement behaviours. Furthermore, we found large differences in economic cost between studies from the same country, even when the same type of cost (i.e. direct, indirect, or overall) was reported and the same cost estimation method (i.e. PAF-based approach or individual-level data approach) was used. For example, the estimated direct economic costs of insufficient PA in Brazil was 0.23 billion USD per year in Prodel et al. [54] study and 0.02 billion USD per year in Rezende et al. [55] study.

The large differences in estimated economic costs from different studies could be due to differences in other aspects of methodology, such as the assessment of movement behaviours (e.g., self-reported vs. device-measured) and the number and type of diseases for which costs were estimated. For example, Prodel et al. [54] study assessed healthcare expenditure on breast cancer, colon cancer, cerebrovascular diseases, ischemic heart disease, hypertension, diabetes, and osteoporosis, while Rezende et al. [55] study assessed only costs of colorectal and breast cancer. Therefore, when comparing and interpreting the findings of the studies included in our review, it is important to consider the methods they used for the cost estimation. Given that most studies relied on self-reported data on movement behaviours and did not consider all adverse health outcomes associated with “unhealthy” movement behaviours, their findings are likely conservative estimates of the true costs.

The estimates for Australia [22], Canada [11, 12], and Finland [23], suggest that the overall economic cost of insufficient PA is approximately two to four times higher than the overall economic cost of excessive SB. The estimates for Canada suggest that the overall economic cost of inadequate sleep duration is approximately 16 and 4 times lower than the overall economic costs of insufficient PA and excessive SB, respectively. The highest costs of insufficient PA could be explained by consistently strongest favourable associations with different health outcomes found for reallocations of time to MVPA, compared with SB and sleep [66, 67]. However, given the limited number of directly comparable estimates for the three movement behaviours, these findings should be taken with caution. It is possible that in countries for which we found no available data, the differences between economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration are smaller or larger than the ones presented herein.

We found that the direct costs of movement behaviours were on average 2.38 times higher than the respective indirect costs determined within a given study. This may reflect a true difference between the two cost types, or it could be that the direct costs were generally estimated more comprehensively than indirect costs. Challenges in comprehensive assessment of indirect costs have been noted in previous studies [13, 16]. Direct comparisons between PAF-based and individual-level costs could not be made due to a lack of studies from the same country and with matching measurement (i.e. self-report or device-based) and cost type (i.e. direct, indirect, or total). For an analogous reason, we could not directly compare costs of movement behaviours calculated from self-reported and device-based data. More studies are needed to enable such comparisons.

Evidence gap for low- and middle-income countries

There is a lack of evidence on economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration in in low-income and lower-middle income countries, because: (1) all included single-country studies were conducted in upper-middle-income and high-income countries; and (2) two included multi-country studies [13, 27] used the data on healthcare costs of different diseases from the European Union member states to estimate the respective costs in the remaining countries, which may not accurately reflect the true economic costs, especially in low-income and lower-middle-income countries. Given that economic costs of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration are likely dependent on the national economic standard, there is a need for future research focused specifically on low-income and lower-middle income countries.

Limited evidence on economic costs of sedentary behaviour and sleep

A possible reason for a relatively low number of studies on economic costs of excessive SB is that most countries still do not have national SB guidelines [68]. Moreover, given that quantitative recommendations on SB duration for adults have only recently been issued as part of the Canadian movement-behaviour guidelines [4], the vast majority of national guidelines do not include the recommended upper threshold for SB duration. This may have resulted in a lower number of studies on not meeting quantitative SB guidelines, including the studies with economic costs as outcome variables. A similar reason might also be behind a relatively low number of studies on economic costs of inadequate sleep duration, despite the fact that sleep duration guidelines have a longer history, compared with the quantitative guidelines on SB duration [69]. To enable estimation of the overall economic cost of unhealthy movement behaviours, more research is needed on economic costs of excessive SB and inadequate sleep duration across the globe.

Implications for public health policy and practice

Our findings suggest that globally billions of dollars could be saved directly or indirectly by increasing the prevalence of meeting 24-hour movement guidelines. This information could be used to advocate for increased investments in the promotion of more PA, less SB, and adequate sleep duration, especially when developing the national health strategy and making the national and municipal annual financial plans. However, it should be noted that our findings are based on data from a limited number of mostly high-income countries, and they may not be generalisable to other high-income countries and countries with lower economic standard. The promotion of healthy movement behaviours could be facilitated by integrating 24-hour movement guidelines into national health policy and practice (e.g. national campaigns to raise health awareness). Current evidence suggests that public health initiatives should focus primarily on PA, as the cost of insufficient PA seems to be substantially higher than the costs of excessive SB and inadequate sleep duration. Furthermore, periodic analyses of economic costs of movement behaviours are needed to observe trends and enable timely and well-informed decision making. To enable periodic assessments of the cost of all three movement behaviours combined, suitable measures of PA, SB, and sleep duration should be integrated in national health surveillance systems.

Methodological quality of included studies

The key strengths of the included studies were: (1) well-defined research questions and objectives; (2) clear reporting of the epidemiological and costing approaches, study limitations, and potential conflict of interest; (3) inclusion of relevant components in the monetary valuation of costs; (4) transparent reporting of costs by category/sector; and (5) adequate discussion about generalisability of findings.

Although the overall methodological quality of most included studies was moderate or high, it is important to consider several of their limitations. First, some of the included studies were not based on data from population-representative samples, which limits the generalisability of their results. In some of the studies that used individual-level data approach, the samples were limited to a certain population group, lacking national representativeness. In some of the studies that used the PAF-based approach, the calculations of economic costs included pooled risk ratios from meta-analyses of studies from diverse populations, which may have led to overestimation or underestimation of the economic costs. Second, most of the included studies were based on self-reported data on movement behaviours, which may have affected the accuracy of classifying participants into meeting/not-meeting the respective public health guideline(s), and consequently the estimates of economic costs. Third, some of the included studies considered a limited number of health outcomes (e.g., cardiovascular disease only) and only specific cost types (e.g., medical cost during hospitalisation only), likely leading to cost underestimation.

Strengths and limitations of this review

The key strengths of this review include: (1) a wide range of bibliographic databases that were used for literature search; (2) the use of strict study inclusion criteria, requiring operational definition of exposure variables as “not meeting public health recommendations for PA, SB, and/or sleep”, which likely improved comparability of findings; (3) making between-country comparisons only based on data from studies that used a similar set of health outcomes in their calculations of economic costs.

This review has several limitations that need to be considered when interpreting summary findings. First, only studies published in English were included, possibly resulting in exclusion of relevant studies published in other languages. Second, when estimating the total population cost based on the mean cost per participant (for studies using the individual-level data approach), we used national prevalence of movement behaviours calculated based on self-reported data [28], which may have resulted in underestimation or overestimation of economic costs. Third, our calculation of the combined cost of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration in Canada was based on studies that considered economic costs of each movement behaviour separately. The combined economic cost estimated in this way may not be the same as the economic cost of not meeting the overall 24-hour movement guidelines. Fourth, the “global cost” of unhealthy movement behaviours would ideally be calculated as , where is the cost in one country and N is the total number of countries. However, in our review, we only included data from a limited number of countries (n). Given that n is a subsample of N, and costs can only take non-negative values, it can be concluded that . Hence, even from the limited available evidence, it is justified to conclude that globally billions of US dollars are spent on adverse consequences of unhealthy movement behaviours.

Recommendations for future research

Given that most previous studies on economic costs of movement behaviours were conducted in high-income countries, more focus in future research should be placed on low- and middle-income countries, as they include more than 80% of the global population. Future studies should also consider focusing on economic costs of SB and sleep, as evidence-base on these movement behaviours is very limited, compared with PA. Importantly, in accordance with the integrated approach to movement behaviours, future studies should also consider examining the cost of not meeting the overall 24-hour movement behaviour guidelines.

Several recommendations for future research can also be made based on the findings of our methodological quality assessment of included studies. First, to improve the cost estimates, when possible, future studies should be conducted in national representative samples. Second, in the cost calculations, future studies that apply the PAF-based approach should consider using risk ratios determined in the population for which the economic analysis is being performed. For example, if the economic cost of insufficient PA is calculated for China, it should be based on data on the association between PA and health outcomes in the Chinese population. Third, more studies should use data collected via device-based measurement of movement behaviours, as this could improve the accuracy of classifying participants into meeting/not-meeting the respective public health guideline(s), and consequently the estimations of economic costs. Fourth, to enable more comprehensive estimations of economic costs of movement behaviours, future studies should consider covering a broad range of health outcomes and different types of costs in their analyses.

Conclusion

Cumulatively, billions USD per year are spent globally on adverse consequences of unhealthy movement behaviours. It should be noted that this is a cumulative global estimate, while national cost of unhealthy movement behaviours in each individual country may not necessarily amount to billions of USD. While the economic costs of PA, SB, and sleep vary substantially, in the included studies they generally seemed to be very high. This calls for increased investments in the promotion of healthy movement behaviours, particularly in countries with a high prevalence of insufficient PA, excessive SB, and inadequate sleep duration. From the available data, it seems that the cost of insufficient PA is approximately 2–4 higher than the cost of excessive SB and 16 times higher than the cost of inadequate sleep duration; hence, the focus of health promoting initiatives should be on increasing levels of PA in the population.

More studies on economic costs of movement behaviours are needed, particularly in low-and middle-income countries and with focus on excessive SB, inadequate sleep duration, and not meeting the overall 24-hour movement guidelines. The accuracy of estimated economic costs could be improved by analysing data collected among population-representative samples using device-based measures of movement behaviours, and by considering different cost types and a broad range of health outcomes.
