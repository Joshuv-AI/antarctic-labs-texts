Efficacy of Magnetic Resonance Elastography in Fontan‐Associated Liver Disease
Authors: Michihiro Iwaki, Takashi Kobayashi, Naohiro Wada, Yumi Saiki, Koki Nagai, Asako Nogami, Shun Kawai, Shingo Koyama, Daisuke Utsunomiya, Atsushi Nakajima, Masato Yoneda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsMany patients develop Fontan‐associated liver disease (FALD) after undergoing the Fontan procedure—a surgical treatment for congenital heart disease such as single ventricle—owing to changes in venous pressure and cardiac output. Liver biopsy is the gold standard for diagnosing FALD, but has limitations. Magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) is a popular non‐invasive method for evaluating liver stiffness and fibrosis in FALD; however, no unified view exists. This study aimed to assess the usefulness of MRE in evaluating the pathophysiology of FALD, including its correlation with cardiovascular parameters and histological findings.Methods and ResultsThis retrospective cohort study included 22 patients with FALD who underwent MRE at Yokohama City University Hospital. Patients with other liver diseases or a history of heavy alcohol consumption were excluded. Liver biopsies were assigned a congestive hepatic fibrosis score (CHFS). Liver stiffness was measured using MRE, and hemodynamic data were obtained using cardiac catheterization. The correlation between MRE‐based liver stiffness and clinical, laboratory, and pathological findings was analyzed.Patients' median age was 21.5 years, and the Fontan procedure was performed 16.3 years (mean) ago. Biochemical findings showed elevated gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) levels. Mean liver stiffness measured using MRE was high (5.3 kPa), which significantly correlated with CHFS stage of liver fibrosis, and also correlated with GGT levels, fibrosis 4 index, and central venous pressure.ConclusionMRE seems a promising non‐invasive tool for liver fibrosis evaluation in FALD. However, it may also reflect hepatic congestion. Further studies are needed to establish its clinical utility and standard cutoff values.

Introduction

Fontan procedure is a well‐established surgical treatment for patients with single‐ventricle congenital heart diseases, such as tricuspid atresia and hypoplastic left heart syndrome. This procedure achieves improved arterial oxygen saturation and reduced volume load on the single ventricle by redirecting systemic venous return to the pulmonary circulation, thereby minimizing intracardiac mixing [1, 2]. Surgical outcomes for the Fontan procedure are excellent, with 15‐ and 20‐year transplant‐free survival rates of 87% and 83%, respectively, for early survivors [3, 4]. Despite these positive outcomes, adverse effects of the Fontan circulation on non‐cardiac organ systems have been increasingly recognized [5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

The liver, in particular, is highly susceptible to alterations in cardiac output and venous pressure, as hepatic venous outflow is directed into the Fontan circuit. Perioperative injury, hypoxia, decreased cardiac output, and elevated central venous pressure (CVP) contribute to cirrhosis and portal hypertension, characterized by sinusoidal and portal fibrosis—a condition now recognized as Fontan‐associated liver disease (FALD) [10]. Liver biopsy remains the gold standard for diagnosing FALD and evaluating fibrosis. Histologically, FALD is marked by substantial structural distortion, pronounced sinusoidal expansion, and perisinusoidal fibrosis, typically without significant parenchymal inflammation [11]. However, liver biopsy poses risks, particularly due to the antithrombotic therapy commonly required by Fontan patients and frequent sampling errors caused by heterogeneous fibrosis patterns in FALD [12]. Several non‐invasive methods for diagnosing FALD have been proposed; however, none have demonstrated sufficient reliability across studies.

Recently, magnetic resonance elastography (MRE) has garnered attention as a non‐invasive technique for assessing liver stiffness, demonstrating potential as an early screening and fibrosis‐severity assessment tool in FALD [13, 14]. Initially reported by the Mayo Clinic in 1995, MRE measures the distribution of tissue stiffness by capturing the shear modulus, a mechanical property of biological tissues. The shear modulus is obtained by converting the vibration phase of a shear wave—induced by an external vibrating source—into the rotation phase of the proton, and subsequently detecting the phase difference on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) phase images [15]. MRE is highly accurate in diagnosing advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis across various liver pathologies [16, 17]. In adult Fontan patients, MRE‐assessed liver stiffness is elevated, typically between 5.1 and 5.5 kPa [18, 19]. Nevertheless, standardized cutoff values for FALD‐related liver fibrosis are not yet established. Increased liver stiffness has been correlated with elevated gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase (GGT) levels, model for end‐stage liver disease (MELD) and model for end‐stage liver disease excluding International normalized ratio (MELD‐XI) scores, central venous pressure (CVP), Fontan pressure, and pulmonary vascular resistance index in adult Fontan patients [14, 18, 20]. However, only one study, involving 10 patients undergoing liver biopsy, has demonstrated a correlation between MRE‐assessed liver stiffness and histological findings, highlighting the need for further investigation [18]. Additionally, distinguishing liver congestion from fibrosis using MRE remains challenging [13, 21], and thus, the clinical utility of MRE in FALD remains uncertain. Therefore, this study evaluated the utility of MRE in assessing FALD pathophysiology, including cardiovascular parameters and histological evaluation of liver biopsies.

Methods

Study Participants

This was a single‐center, retrospective cross‐sectional study. Patients diagnosed with FALD between January 1, 2010, and December 23, 2023, and who underwent MRE were registered. The exclusion criteria were other hepatic diseases, such as hepatitis C, hepatitis B, autoimmune hepatitis, primary biliary cholangitis, sclerosing cholangitis, Wilson's disease, hemochromatosis, α1‐antitrypsin deficiency, drug‐induced steatohepatitis, and current or past history of significant alcohol consumption (> 30 g/day and > 20 g/day of ethanol in men and women, respectively). This study adhered to the reporting guidelines provided by Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE).

Patients and Public Involvement

Patients were involved in the conduct of the study, particularly in shaping the research question based on their experiences. The results of this study will be disseminated in the form of an international report to the patients and medical staff.

Clinical and Laboratory Evaluation

We obtained a detailed history of all patients with FALD, and detailed medical records were assigned to all participants. Laboratory tests were performed using the standard techniques. Diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and dyslipidemia were diagnosed according to the standard criteria [22, 23, 24].

Pathological Evaluation

Liver biopsy samples were collected using an 18‐G needle and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Samples from liver biopsies were scored using the congestive hepatic fibrosis score (CHFS) [25]. The CHFS comprises the following scores: 0 (no significant fibrosis), 1 (central fibrosis only), 2a (central and portal fibrosis with accentuated central fibrosis), 2b (central and portal fibrosis with accentuated portal fibrosis), 3 (bridging fibrosis), and 4 (cirrhosis).

Magnetic Resonance Elastography

Each patient underwent MRE using a 3.0‐T MR system (GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) after fasting for at least 4 h. MRE measurements were conducted by experienced hepatologists or radiologists as previously reported [26]. MRE sequence was used to acquire wave images in transverse sections with the following parameters: repetition time ms/echo time ms, 50/23; continuous sinusoidal vibration, 60 Hz; FOV, 32–42 cm; matrix size, 256 × 64; flip angle, 30°; section thickness, 10 mm; four evenly spaced phase offsets; and four pairs of 60‐Hz trapezoidal motion‐encoding gradients with zero‐ and first‐moment nulling along the through‐plane direction. All processing steps were applied automatically, without manual intervention, to yield quantitative images of tissue shear stiffness in kilopascals. In each section of the MRE image, the region of interest (ROI) was delineated to include only the parenchyma of the right lobe, avoiding the hepatic margins and great vessels. If there were no liver parenchyma that could be measured by elastography using reliability maps, the study was considered invalid. Additionally, ROIs excluded regions where the phase signal‐to‐noise ratio (ratio of the wave amplitude to noise in the wave images) was < 5. For analysis, the average of the measurements of the four slices was calculated. Given the heterogeneous progression of liver fibrosis, this method was used to assess liver fibrosis over a wide area of the right lobe [26].

Cardiac Catheterization

Cardiac catheterization was used to investigate representative parameters that reflect the hemodynamics of FALD. It was performed according to the same protocol for all patients, which included blood sampling for measurement of the oxygen content (SaO2) and determination of pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP), pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), CVP, pulmonary artery resistance (Rp), systemic artery resistance (Rs), pulmonary blood flow (Qp), systemic blood flow (Qs), cardiac index (CI), and ejection fraction (EF).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. Data were analyzed using JMP Pro 17.0 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, North Carolina, USA). The level of significance was set at 5% (p = 0.05) for the test. To compare parameters of continuous variables, including liver stiffness measurement (LSM), across CHFS stages, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used due to the ordinal scale characteristics of CHFS and the small sample size. Correlations between LSM and other continuous variables (e.g., central venous pressure, gamma‐glutamyl transpeptidase, FIB‐4 index) were evaluated using Pearson's correlation coefficient, after confirming the linearity and approximate normal distribution of variables. No imputation was performed for missing values; only available data were used in the analysis.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 22 patients with FALD were enrolled in the study. The clinical, laboratory, and pathological characteristics of the study participants are tabulated (Table 1). Among them, nine (40.1%) were female with a median age of 21.5 years (SD = 6.1) at the time of MRE imaging. The anatomical heart structures included tricuspid atresia in three cases (13.6%), hypoplastic left heart syndrome in one (4.5%), pulmonary atresia with intact ventricular septum in two (9.1%), double outlet right ventricle in five (22.7%), double inlet left ventricle in one (4.5%), atrioventricular septal defect in three (13.6%), and hypoplastic left heart syndrome in two (9.1%). Regarding the Fontan type, the extracardiac conduit was more common (18 cases, 81.8%) compared with that of the atrio‐pulmonary connection (4 cases, 18.2%). The average time from the Fontan procedure to MRE imaging was 16.3 years. Blood test results show that the mean GGT level was elevated in patients with FALD (122.4 ± 156.1 IU/L), whereas the mean values of aspartate aminotransferase (AST) (25.5 ± 4.3 IU/L), alanine aminotransferase (24.9 ± 7.7 IU/L), platelet count (17.7 ± 7.2 × 104/μL), and albumin (4.7 ± 0.4 g/dL) were within their respective normal ranges. The FIB‐4 (fibrosis‐4) index was 0.7 ± 0.4, and the AST to platelet ratio index was 0.8 ± 1.2. Among liver fibrosis markers, mean values of type IV collagen 7 s (6.8 ± 1.2 ng/mL) and procollagen type III peptide (PIII‐P) (16.4 ± 7.0 ng/mL) exceeded their respective normal ranges. Catheterization, MRE, and pathological data are also tabulated (Table 1). In this cohort, neither the pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), an indicator of Fontan pressure (7.9 ± 3.3 mmHg), nor the PAP (11.6 ± 3.5 mmHg) was elevated. The CVP was mildly elevated (12.7 ± 3.3 mmHg), and the pulmonary artery index was low (217.4 ± 92.8 mm2/m2). The ratio of pulmonary (Qp) to systemic blood flow (Qs) was 0.9 ± 0.1, and the EF was 55.5% ± 6.1%. The mean aortic oxygen saturation was 94.3% ± 1.9%. Liver stiffness measurement (LSM), as determined using MRE, was elevated (5.3 ± 1.1 kPa). Concurrently measured, the longest diameter of the inferior vena cava (IVC) was expanded at 23.7 ± 5.0 mm. Liver biopsies were available for 13/22 patients. All cases that underwent liver biopsy exhibited liver fibrosis (CHFS stage 1 or higher). Among them, three (23.1%) were classified as Stage 4, indicating cirrhosis.

Correlation Between Magnetic Resonance Elastography‐Based Liver Stiffness Measurement and Pathological Liver Fibrosis

The mean LSM values obtained using MRE (n = 22) for CHFS Stages 1, 3, and 4 were 4.44, 4.67, and 6.46 kPa, respectively, as shown in Figure 1. The analysis revealed that as the histological severity of liver fibrosis increased, the LSM values obtained using MRE incrementally increased (p = 0.03, Kruskal–Wallis test). Correlation Between Pathological Liver Fibrosis and Other Findings

The correlation between pathological liver fibrosis and other factors was analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test (Figure 3). There was no significant correlation between the FIB‐4 index, general liver fibrosis markers including type 4 collagen 7 s, or platelet count. Furthermore, no significant correlation was observed with CVP or IVC diameter, which are indicators of congestion.

Discussion

Consistent with previous reports [27, 28, 29, 30, 31], we found no abnormalities in the general blood test data for FALD, except for high GGT levels. Cardiac catheterization tests previously performed on patients with FALD showed an average PAWP [32] and PAP [30, 33, 34], and persistent CVP elevation [14, 35, 36]. In this study, we also observed CVP elevation, but the PCWP and PAP were within their normal ranges.

Furthermore, the values for type IV collagen 7 s and PIII‐P, which are liver fibrosis markers, were higher than the reference values. Therefore, these markers may be useful for assessing the progression of FALD.

The most important histopathological feature assessed in biopsy specimens obtained from patients with chronic liver disease is the presence and severity of fibrosis. For liver fibrosis evaluation, we used the CHFS, which is also recommended in the position papers of Europe and the United States [37]. All cases were found to be CHFS stage 1 or higher, and we reconfirmed that more cases develop liver fibrosis after a Fontan procedure.

Our study also revealed that LSM using MRE correlated with liver fibrosis. These results are consistent with results obtained from previous studies that demonstrated a correlation between the LSM using MRE and the total fibrosis score measured via liver biopsy [18, 38]. Since the risk of bleeding is higher in Fontan patients receiving anticoagulant therapy, LSM using MRE can be expected to function as a non‐invasive examination that can replace pathological evaluation using liver biopsy. On the other hand, Serai et al. reported no correlation between the LSM using MRE and the liver fibrosis score using liver biopsy [39]. This report suggested that the presence of acute inflammation and hepatic congestion might affect the interpretation of LSM using MRE. Inconsistent interpretations of LSM using MRE across studies may be attributed to differences in the management and quality of life of patients after Fontan surgery in different countries and regions [40], as well as the small number of cases in each of these studies. Future studies involving a larger cohort, in multiple regions, need to be conducted to address these prevalent inconsistencies.

Sethasathien et al. set the cutoff value of LSM for diagnosing significant liver fibrosis (Stage ≥ 2) at 4.4 kPa [38]. LSM in CHFS stage 4 was 6.46, which is a very high value. However, the LSM in stages 1 and 3 was 4.44 and 4.67, respectively, which are so close that they cannot be reliably distinguished. Owing to the limited number of cases, the cutoff value was not analyzed; however, we speculate that a reliable cutoff value can be established by collecting more cases from multiple institutions.

Factors such as platelet count, FIB‐4 index, CVP, and others have been reported to correlate with liver fibrosis [41], but in our study, these factors were not significant as indicators of liver fibrosis. This may be due to the small number of liver biopsies performed (13 cases), likely resulting in insufficient statistical power. In the context of FALD, MRE has been shown to correlate with the time elapsed since the Fontan procedure [14, 18, 19, 42]. In this study, the lack of significant difference between MRE and the period after the Fontan procedure is attributed to the influence of selection bias. This is because the majority of patients assessed were aged 16 to 20 years with similar post‐procedure periods.

Previous reports indicate an association between increased liver stiffness assessed using MRE and GGT levels, creatinine levels, and MELD and MELD‐XI scores [18]. Furthermore, liver stiffness is known to correlate with central venous hypertension, increased Fontan pressure, pulmonary vascular resistance, and decreased cardiac output [14, 18, 20].

In our study, LSM using MRE showed a positive correlation with GGT, CVP, and FIB‐4 index (Figure 5); however, no correlation was observed with other parameters.

GGT is present in the bile duct endothelial cells and increases when there is bile duct obstruction or damage [43, 44]. Elevated GGT in Fontan‐related liver disease suggests hepatic congestion and venous outflow obstruction [45]. This study suggests that GGT levels may be very useful as a screening test for FALD. Additionally, LSM using MRE was related to the CVP and was reconfirmed as an indicator related to hepatic congestion. Elevated GGT and CVP levels suggest hepatic congestion, a unique and characteristic feature of hemodynamics following the Fontan procedure, and it is speculated that these factors may influence elastography values in LSM.

In this study, a correlation was observed between LSM using MRE and the FIB‐4 index, but no significant correlation was observed between liver fibrosis and the FIB‐4 index. A correlation between bridging fibrosis and the FIB‐4 index has been previously reported in patients with FALD [18], and while the FIB‐4 index may be useful as a screening test for FALD, we would like to gather more cases and clarify the role of the FIB‐4 index in the future.

One advantage of this study is that FALD was evaluated using both MRE and histology. Only two studies thus far have evaluated FALD, a rare disease, using both MRE and histology [18, 38]; however, this is the first such study conducted in Japan.

This study has some limitations. First, the case number (22) is small, mainly due to the low incidence of FALD and the fact that the study was conducted at a single medical institution, which limited the number of participants. Second, the association between MRE‐based LSM and pathological liver fibrosis was based on the statistical association of cross‐sectional observations. Therefore, the causal relationship between the two is unknown. Third, since patients with an implanted pacemaker or a pacemaker lead cannot undergo an MRI, cases with arrhythmia requiring pacemaker implantation may have been excluded from this study. Therefore, MRI is not universally available for FALD and is insufficient in terms of versatility. This may have led to selection bias in this study. Finally, the patients were enrolled from a single tertiary medical institution, so the participant enrollment process may have led to selection bias.

To summarize, MRE is a promising non‐invasive tool for evaluating liver fibrosis in FALD. However, further research is needed to establish its clinical usefulness and standard cutoff values. Further assessment of factors related to prognosis, in a large sample, would provide clarity on the role of MRE in FALD.
