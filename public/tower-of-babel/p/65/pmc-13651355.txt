Effectiveness of game-based learning in mental health nursing education on self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy among nursing students
Authors: Alireza Rafi, Maryam Heidari, Seyed Mohammad Salehi Behbahani, Hassan Bostan, Ali Tamimi, Mehrnaz Ahmadi, Samaneh Naeimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditional lecture-based teaching in nursing education often leads to limited student engagement, poor knowledge retention, and low self-confidence, particularly in complex fields such as mental health nursing, where students must navigate abstract clinical presentations and develop interpersonal skills that cannot be reduced to algorithmic protocols. Game-based learning (GBL) has emerged as an interactive strategy to enhance motivation, critical thinking, self-directed learning (SDL), and academic self-efficacy. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of GBL integrated into a mental health nursing internship on SDL and academic self-efficacy among undergraduate nursing students.MethodsA quasi-experimental, two-group, pretest–posttest design with a one-month follow-up was employed. Forty-six sixth-semester nursing students from Abadan University of Medical Sciences (Iran) were enrolled and allocated to an intervention group (n = 23) or a control group (n = 23) based on the pre-existing scheduling of their clinical internship rotations. After accounting for attrition, the final analytical sample comprised 40 participants (intervention: n = 20; control: n = 20). The intervention group received GBL integrated with standard instruction, while the control group received conventional internship training only. The intervention involved a custom-designed board game with scenario-based challenges delivered over 10 sessions (40 h total). Outcomes were measured using the Self-Directed Learning Readiness Scale (SDLRS) and the College Academic Self-Efficacy Scale (CASES) at pretest, posttest, and one-month follow-up. Data were analyzed using repeated measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons for within-group changes, independent t-tests for between-group comparisons, and Cohen’s d for effect sizes. A sensitivity analysis for attrition bias was conducted using a pattern-mixture model under best-case, worst-case, and intermediate imputation scenarios.ResultsGroups were homogeneous at baseline (P > 0.05 for all demographic and outcome variables). In the intervention group, repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of time for both SDL (F(2,38) = 38.74, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.67) and academic self-efficacy (F(2,38) = 42.51, P < 0.001, partial η²=0.69). Post-intervention, the GBL group showed significant improvements in SDL (mean difference: 16.25 ± 8.03, P < 0.001) and academic self-efficacy (mean difference: 12.50 ± 7.14, P < 0.001), with large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 1.25 and 1.56, respectively). These gains were maintained at the one-month follow-up, with no significant decline from posttest to follow-up (SDL: P = 0.784; self-efficacy: P = 0.821). The control group exhibited no significant changes across any time points (P > 0.05). Between-group differences were significant at posttest (SDL: P = 0.037, d = 0.68; self-efficacy: P = 0.029, d = 0.72) and at follow-up (SDL: P = 0.043, d = 0.66; self-efficacy: P = 0.034, d = 0.70). Sensitivity analyses confirmed that the findings were robust to plausible patterns of missing data due to attrition, with the treatment effect remaining significant even under worst-case imputation scenarios.ConclusionGBL integrated into a mental health nursing internship significantly enhanced SDL and academic self-efficacy, with effects sustained at one-month follow-up. These findings support the integration of game-based strategies into nursing curricula as a student-centered approach to bridge theory–practice gaps and foster durable self-regulatory capacities. Further research with larger samples, multi-site designs, and extended follow-up periods of six to twelve months is recommended to confirm the generalizability and long-term durability of these effects.

Introduction

In recent years, innovative teaching methods aimed at facilitating knowledge transfer, enhancing critical thinking skills, and promoting student-centered learning have gained increasing attention and support from nursing educators [1]. As higher education progressively shifts toward learner-centered approaches, nursing instructors are now expected to integrate diverse and interactive teaching strategies into their curricula [2]. Given the complexity of modern clinical environments, reforming nursing education to better prepare students for professional practice has become a pressing need [3]. One of the key reasons for inadequate knowledge retention and skill acquisition among nursing students may be the persistence of traditional lecture-based teaching and the limited use of modern learning theories and advanced instructional practices [4]. These conventional methods often fail to engage students, leading to rapid forgetting, insufficient opportunities for questioning and discussion, and declining motivation [5]. Furthermore, nursing students frequently experience high levels of anxiety in professional education settings, as they are often required to make rapid clinical decisions in stressful situations [6].

One active learning strategy that has received growing attention is game-based learning (GBL). Games represent a spectrum of intrinsically motivated and voluntary activities typically associated with enjoyment, engagement, and meaningful challenges that encourage active participation [7]. GBL provides immediate feedback, fosters collaboration among learners, and enhances motivation and engagement [8]. Studies have demonstrated that game-based learning can improve students’ academic self-efficacy and motivation [9], strengthen self-directed learning skills [10], and enhance critical thinking and decision-making abilities [11]. Additionally, by integrating interactive and competitive elements, GBL promotes both cognitive and affective learning outcomes, supporting deeper understanding and long-term retention of knowledge [12]. Effective teamwork—a crucial component of future nursing practice—also benefits from such learning experiences, as games encourage communication, creativity, and shared goals among participants [13].

Psychiatric nursing internships present unique educational challenges distinct from those in medical–surgical or critical care settings. While other placements rely on concrete physiological parameters, psychiatric nursing involves abstract, nuanced, and often ambiguous clinical presentations [14]. Students must master complex therapeutic communication skills and learn to make clinical judgments without laboratory values, imaging, or physical examination findings [15]. Additionally, many nursing students experience significant anxiety when interacting with patients exhibiting acute psychiatric symptoms such as psychosis, agitation, or suicidal ideation, due to unfamiliarity, societal stigma, and uncertainty about appropriate responses [16]. This anxiety can hinder learning and delay the development of essential competencies. As well, game-based learning offers a promising solution by providing a psychologically safe environment for students to practice therapeutic communication, clinical decision-making, and receive immediate feedback without risking patient harm or real-time performance pressure [17]. The narrative and role-playing elements of well-designed educational games align closely with the interpretive and interpersonal nature of psychiatric care, effectively bridging theoretical knowledge and clinical practice [18].

Despite the quantitative expansion of nursing education in Iran, the quality of teaching and learning remains a major challenge. Traditional, lecture-based instruction continues to dominate nursing curricula, resulting in limited student participation, surface learning, and weak clinical competence [19, 20]. The lack of skill-based training programs, the theory–practice gap, and inadequate feedback mechanisms often leave students anxious and unprepared for clinical decision-making [21]. Moreover, the absence of engaging, evidence-based teaching methods contributes to reduced motivation, low self-confidence, and declining professional interest among nursing students [22]. Therefore, revising educational strategies and adopting innovative, interactive approaches such as game-based learning, simulation, and experiential education have become essential to enhance the quality and relevance of nursing education [12].

Within this context, two learner attributes have emerged as critical determinants of academic success and clinical competence in nursing education: self-directed learning readiness and academic self-efficacy. Self-directed learning readiness refers to the degree to which an individual possesses the attitudes, skills, and personality characteristics necessary to take primary responsibility for their own learning—including the ability to identify learning needs, set goals, locate resources, implement strategies, and evaluate outcomes. Academic self-efficacy, grounded in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, denotes a student’s belief in their capacity to successfully perform academic tasks at designated levels [23, 24]. Academic self-efficacy, grounded in Bandura’s Social Cognitive Theory, denotes a student’s belief in their capacity to successfully perform academic tasks at designated levels [24]. Students with high academic self-efficacy are more likely to persist in the face of challenges, employ effective learning strategies, and achieve higher academic performance [25]. These findings are of particular concern given the robust evidence linking higher self-directed learning readiness and academic self-efficacy to improved academic performance, enhanced clinical decision-making, greater persistence in the face of academic challenges, and reduced burnout among nursing students and practicing nurses [26]. Within this framework, game-based learning serves as a dynamic environment that stimulates both self-regulation and self-belief. The immediate feedback, active participation, and sense of achievement embedded in games enhance students’ perceived competence (self-efficacy) and autonomy (self-directed learning). Consequently, integrating GBL in mental health nursing courses may not only promote active engagement but also strengthen learners’ confidence in their academic and clinical capabilities [27].

Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effectiveness of game-based learning in mental health nursing education on self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy among nursing students. To our knowledge, this is the first quasi-experimental study to examine the effects of a structured, multi-session board game intervention specifically designed for psychiatric nursing internship education, and to assess the durability of these effects through a one-month follow-up. By focusing on a specialty that has received limited attention in the GBL literature, employing validated instruments to measure theoretically grounded outcomes, and incorporating a longitudinal design to evaluate knowledge retention, this study addresses a critical gap in the evidence base. The results of this study can provide valuable evidence to inform curriculum development and support the implementation of innovative, student-centered teaching strategies in nursing education.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a quasi-experimental, two-group, pretest–posttest design with a one-month follow-up. Participants were allocated to the intervention and control groups based on the pre-existing scheduling of their clinical internship rotations. Students assigned to the Monday/Wednesday internship schedule were designated as the intervention group, while those assigned to the Tuesday/Thursday schedule were designated as the control group. This allocation was determined by the faculty’s administrative scheduling system prior to the commencement of the study and was entirely outside the control of the research team. No random sequence was generated, no allocation concealment mechanism was implemented, and no blinding procedures were employed at any stage. Accordingly, the study is classified as quasi-experimental. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Abadan University of Medical Sciences (Ethics code: IR.ABADANUMS.REC.1403.018). The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki regarding research with human participants [28].

Participants and setting

The study was conducted among sixth-semester undergraduate nursing students enrolled in the Faculty of Nursing, Abadan University of Medical Sciences, during the 2024–2025 academic year. All participants were completing their clinical internship in psychiatric disorders nursing. Sampling was performed using a census approach, and all eligible students from one cohort were invited to participate in the study.

Blinding

Blinding was not feasible in this study due to the nature of the educational intervention. Participants were necessarily aware of their group assignment, as the game-based learning activities were visibly distinct from conventional instruction. The instructor who delivered the intervention and facilitated game sessions was aware of group assignments. Outcome data were collected by a member of the research team who was not blinded to allocation. Statistical analyses were conducted by the principal investigator without masking of group codes. The absence of blinding at all stages is acknowledged as a methodological limitation.

Contamination control

To minimize the risk of cross-group contamination, several strategies were implemented. First, the clinical internship sessions for the intervention and control groups were scheduled on separate days of the week (intervention group: Mondays and Wednesdays; control group: Tuesdays and Thursdays), ensuring that students from different groups were not concurrently present in the clinical setting. Second, the game-based learning activities were conducted in a dedicated simulation laboratory accessible only to intervention group members during their scheduled sessions. Third, all game materials (board, cards, dice) were collected and securely stored by the research team at the conclusion of each session, preventing control group access. Fourth, students were explicitly instructed not to discuss educational activities with peers from the other group for the duration of the study. While complete elimination of contamination cannot be guaranteed in an educational setting with students from the same cohort, these measures substantially reduced the likelihood of systematic diffusion of the intervention to the control condition.

Intervention design

The intervention was designed to integrate game-based learning (GBL) into the Psychiatric Disorders Nursing Internship course. The course lasted 10 sessions, each lasting four hours, conducted over five weeks. Both groups received identical theoretical content, clinical exposure, and instructor guidance. However, the intervention group participated in a structured game-based educational approach, whereas the control group received conventional internship instruction. Game design and tools

The educational game, titled “PsychCare Quest,” was a newly developed, purpose-designed intervention created specifically for this study. It is not a standardized or commercially available tool; rather, it was designed de novo by the research team in collaboration with three experts in psychiatric nursing education and instructional design to align with the learning objectives of the Psychiatric Disorders Nursing Internship course [29].

Primary objective

The overarching educational goal of the game was to enhance students’ clinical reasoning skills in psychiatric nursing by simulating the progressive, stepwise nature of patient care—from initial assessment and diagnosis through treatment planning, therapeutic intervention, and discharge. The game was designed to promote self-directed learning by requiring students to independently retrieve and apply theoretical knowledge to solve clinically authentic problems, rather than passively receiving information from an instructor.

Core application

The game served as an active learning tool integrated into the internship curriculum. Specifically, it was designed to: (1) reinforce theoretical knowledge through repeated retrieval practice embedded in multiple-choice question cards across three difficulty levels; (2) stimulate higher-order clinical reasoning through open-ended scenario-based questions requiring synthesis of diagnostic, pharmacological, and nursing care knowledge; (3) foster self-directed engagement by shifting the locus of control from the instructor to the learner; and (4) promote cooperative and competitive motivation through small-group gameplay, peer interaction, and a ranking system within a supportive learning environment.

The spiral board design metaphorically represented the clinical journey of a psychiatric patient, with obstacles corresponding to critical decision points in the care pathway.

Content validation

Content validity of all game materials (150 multiple-choice questions, 20 scenario cards with 80 open-ended questions, and board design) was established through expert review. Three independent experts in psychiatric nursing and educational design evaluated each item for relevance, accuracy, clarity, and alignment with learning objectives. Items were revised or eliminated based on expert feedback until consensus on adequacy was achieved.

Game steps

Students in the intervention group were divided into groups of four players, each assigned a colored token (red, blue, yellow, or green). Every player received a scenario card at the beginning of the session, which they kept throughout the game.

The game began with the first player rolling the dice to determine question difficulty. A correct answer to the drawn question card allowed the player to advance six spaces on the board; an incorrect answer required the player to move one space backward (except when already positioned at the start or immediately following an obstacle).

Upon reaching an obstacle square, the player faced one of the open-ended questions from their assigned scenario card. A correct response enabled the player to proceed, whereas an incorrect answer required them to remain on the same square until successfully answering in a subsequent turn.

During gameplay, students were permitted up to three guided hints from the researcher to support problem-solving and maintain engagement without compromising autonomy. The atmosphere of the game promoted cooperation, healthy competition, and self-directed exploration of psychiatric nursing concepts.

The game concluded when one player successfully reached the final square (number 72) and correctly answered the last scenario question, thereby “completing” the simulated care journey. Other players continued until final rankings were determined. Each session ended with a brief reflective discussion facilitated by the instructor to reinforce key learning outcomes and connect game experiences to clinical practice.

Intervention fidelity

To ensure consistent implementation of the intervention across sessions, the following fidelity measures were employed: (1) a standardized session checklist was completed by the instructor after each session to document adherence to planned content and activities; (2) game sessions were conducted using a structured facilitator guide specifying the sequence, timing, and facilitation strategies for each game component; (3) a co-facilitator observed 20% of sessions (two out of ten) using a fidelity observation form and provided feedback to the primary instructor; and (4) any deviations from the planned protocol were documented and reviewed during weekly research team meetings.

Teaching materials

provided to the control group included the standard internship handbook, clinical assessment forms, and access to the same psychiatric nursing textbooks and online resources available to the intervention group. No game-based elements, structured competitive activities, or simulation scenarios (beyond routine clinical practice) were incorporated into the control condition. Control group participants were not exposed to the game board, question cards, scenario cards, dice, or any other materials developed for the intervention.

Both groups received equivalent contact hours, instructor attention, clinical exposure, and theoretical review. The sole substantive difference between conditions was the replacement of instructor-led case discussions and clinical debriefing (in the control group) with structured game-based learning activities (in the intervention group).

Data collection procedure

Data were collected at three time points: (1) before the start of the intervention (pretest) (2), immediately after completion (posttest), and (3) one month after the intervention (follow-up). Questionnaires were administered in-person under researcher supervision to ensure completeness and consistency. Participation was voluntary, and all students provided written informed consent prior to inclusion.

Implementation documentation

The implementation process was documented in a detailed study protocol that outlined all procedures, session content, game mechanics, and fidelity monitoring strategies. This protocol is archived at the Department of Psychiatric Nursing, Faculty of Nursing, Abadan University of Medical Sciences.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and all outcome variables met the assumption of normality at each measurement point (P > 0.05). Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables as mean ± standard deviation.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between the intervention and control groups using independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as some expected cell counts were < 5. To assess attrition bias, completers and dropouts were similarly compared on baseline characteristics and pretest outcome measures.

Changes in self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy across pretest, posttest, and one-month follow-up were examined within each group using one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA). Mauchly’s test was used to assess sphericity, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when necessary. Significant omnibus effects were followed by Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons. Effect sizes for time effects were reported using partial eta-squared (η²).

Between-group differences at each measurement point and in change scores (posttest–pretest, follow-up–pretest, and follow-up–posttest) were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests. Cohen’s d was calculated to estimate within-group and between-group effect sizes and interpreted according to conventional thresholds (0.20 = small, 0.50 = medium, 0.80 = large).

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to evaluate the impact of attrition. A pattern-mixture approach imputed missing data under best-case, worst-case, and intermediate scenarios, and treatment effects were re-estimated for each scenario. In addition, tipping point analyses identified the assumptions under which between-group differences became non-significant (P ≥ 0.05). Sensitivity analyses were performed for both outcomes at posttest and follow-up.

All tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 46 nursing students initially enrolled in the study. Of these, 6 participants were excluded due to the following reasons: failure to complete questionnaires (n = 2), absence from more than two internship sessions (n = 3), and voluntary withdrawal (n = 1). The final analytical sample comprised 40 participants who completed all sessions and assessments, with 20 in the intervention group and 20 in the control group, yielding an overall attrition rate of 13.04%. A comparison of completers (n = 40) and dropouts (n = 6) revealed no statistically significant differences in baseline age (P = 0.672), gender (P = 0.891), GPA (P = 0.543), or pretest self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy scores (P = 0.714 and P = 0.638, respectively), suggesting that attrition did not systematically bias the study findings.

The two groups were homogeneous at baseline, showing no statistically significant differences in age, gender, marital status, residence status, GPA, or level of interest in the nursing field and mental health courses (all P > 0.05). Baseline comparisons

No significant differences were observed between the intervention and control groups at baseline for either self-directed learning (138.50 ± 20.69 vs. 141.65 ± 18.20; t (38) = 0.51, P = 0.612) or academic self-efficacy (103.00 ± 19.99 vs. 102.25 ± 19.13; t (38) = 0.12, P = 0.904), confirming the equivalence of the groups prior to the intervention.

Within-group changes

In the intervention group, repeated measures ANOVA revealed significant main effects of time for both self-directed learning (F (2, 38) = 38.74, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.67) and academic self-efficacy (F (2, 38) = 42.51, P < 0.001, partial η² = 0.69). Pairwise comparisons with Bonferroni correction indicated that self-directed learning scores increased significantly from pretest (138.50 ± 20.69) to posttest (154.75 ± 19.34; mean difference = 16.25, P < 0.001), and this improvement was maintained at the one-month follow-up (153.95 ± 18.42), with no significant decline from posttest to follow-up (mean difference = 0.80, P = 0.784). Similarly, academic self-efficacy increased significantly from pretest (103.00 ± 19.99) to posttest (115.50 ± 17.23; mean difference = 12.50, P < 0.001), and gains were sustained at the one-month follow-up (114.85 ± 16.89), with no significant attenuation from posttest to follow-up (mean difference = 0.65, P = 0.821).

In the control group, repeated measures ANOVA showed no significant main effects of time for either self-directed learning (F (2, 38) = 0.67, P = 0.519, partial η² = 0.03) or academic self-efficacy (F (2, 38) = 0.58, P = 0.563, partial η² = 0.03), indicating that conventional instruction did not produce meaningful changes in either outcome.

Between-group comparisons at posttest and follow-up

Independent t-tests at posttest revealed significantly higher scores in the intervention group compared to the control group for both self-directed learning (154.75 ± 19.34 vs. 141.90 ± 18.28; t (38) = 2.16, P = 0.037, Cohen’s d = 0.68) and academic self-efficacy (115.50 ± 17.23 vs. 102.55 ± 18.84; t (38) = 2.27, P = 0.029, Cohen’s d = 0.72). These significant between-group differences were sustained at the one-month follow-up for self-directed learning (153.95 ± 18.42 vs. 141.70 ± 18.53; t (38) = 2.10, P = 0.043, Cohen’s d = 0.66) and academic self-efficacy (114.85 ± 16.89 vs. 102.40 ± 18.91; t (38) = 2.20, P = 0.034, Cohen’s d = 0.70).

Effect sizes for pre-post changes

The effect size (Cohen’s d) for changes from pretest to posttest within the intervention group was large for both outcomes: 1.25 for self-directed learning and 1.56 for academic self-efficacy. The effect sizes for pretest to follow-up changes in the intervention group were similarly large: 1.21 for self-directed learning and 1.47 for academic self-efficacy, indicating durable educational impacts of the game-based learning method.

Sensitivity analysis for attrition bias

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the robustness of the findings to participant attrition (6/46, 13.04%). Although completers and dropouts did not differ significantly in baseline characteristics or pretest outcomes (all P > 0.05), two complementary approaches were applied to evaluate the potential impact of missing data.

Using a pattern-mixture model, missing posttest and follow-up data were imputed under three scenarios: best-case, worst-case, and intermediate (mean imputation). Under the best-case scenario, between-group differences at posttest increased for both self-directed learning (mean difference = 15.21, P < 0.001, d = 0.82) and academic self-efficacy (mean difference = 14.33, P < 0.001, d = 0.92). Under the intermediate scenario, significant between-group differences were maintained for self-directed learning (mean difference = 11.87, P = 0.006, d = 0.61) and academic self-efficacy (mean difference = 12.04, P = 0.004, d = 0.66).

Importantly, under the worst-case scenario, which assumed no improvement among intervention dropouts and maximum improvement among control dropouts, between-group differences remained statistically significant at posttest for self-directed learning (mean difference = 8.92, P = 0.042, d = 0.46) and academic self-efficacy (mean difference = 9.11, P = 0.038, d = 0.48). At one-month follow-up, the differences remained marginally significant (self-directed learning: P = 0.049; academic self-efficacy: P = 0.047).

A tipping point analysis showed that the primary findings would become non-significant only under extreme assumptions inconsistent with the observed data. Specifically, intervention-group dropouts would need to experience substantial declines from baseline while control-group dropouts simultaneously achieved unusually large improvements. For self-directed learning, the threshold required a decline of more than 10 points in the intervention group and an improvement exceeding 15 points in the control group; corresponding values for academic self-efficacy were greater than 8 and 12 points, respectively.

Overall, the sensitivity analyses demonstrated that the beneficial effects of game-based learning on self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy were robust across a wide range of plausible missing-data assumptions, supporting the internal validity of the study findings.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the effectiveness of game-based learning (GBL) on self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy among nursing students. Consistent with previous evidence, the findings demonstrated that the use of GBL significantly improved both learning performance and academic self-efficacy. Earlier research has consistently highlighted the benefits of game-based education in enhancing students’ academic success [35–37]. Another study reported that GBL exerts a strong and positive effect on academic achievement across educational levels and disciplines, underscoring its stability and generalizability as an instructional approach [38].

In this study, an interactive strategy was introduced that integrated game-based learning into both clinical practice and theoretical teaching within the Mental Health Nursing practicum. The results revealed that students who participated in the GBL intervention achieved significantly higher scores in learning outcomes and academic self-efficacy compared to those in the control group. These findings suggest that engaging and interactive learning environments enhance nursing students’ ability to make decisions, solve problems, and manage their own learning processes [39, 40]. Integrating innovative methods such as game-based learning into traditional teaching frameworks appears to facilitate learning objectives, encourage self-regulation, and strengthen learners’ belief in their own competence.

This conclusion aligns with prior research showing that interactive and game-based environments stimulate active participation, encourage decision-making in the learning process, and promote motivation and academic confidence through immediate feedback, rewards, and a sense of achievement [41]. Moreover, game-based education provides a psychologically safe environment in which students can experience failure, reflect, and learn without facing the real-world consequences of clinical errors. Such experiential learning contributes to a deeper understanding and stronger retention of clinical concepts [42].

The findings of the present study provide robust evidence supporting the educational value of GBL in nursing education. A key outcome was the marked improvement in students’ learning performance after participating in the intervention. The integration of GBL with both theoretical and clinical components allowed students to connect abstract knowledge with practice-based decision-making. This approach fostered critical thinking, clinical reasoning, and problem-solving, while increasing engagement and motivation during the learning process. A study has confirmed that serious games and gamified mechanisms in nursing education produce strong effects on theoretical knowledge and moderate to large effects on practical skills [43]. These outcomes demonstrate that GBL is not only an innovative method for deepening theoretical understanding but also a tangible means of enhancing professional competence required in real clinical environments [44].

While many studies report positive effects of GBL in nursing education as mentioned earlier, some research reveals inconsistent or limited outcomes. For instance, in a randomized study comparing a serious-game simulation to traditional teaching for nursing students’ clinical reasoning, no statistically significant difference in reasoning outcomes was found between the game and traditional groups, despite higher motivation and satisfaction levels in the game group [45]. This suggests that although GBL may enhance engagement and affective outcomes, it does not always translate into measurable cognitive or clinical decision-making gains—potentially due to design, duration, or context of the intervention. Similarly, a systematic review of game-based teaching in undergraduate nursing reported improvements in learning satisfaction, critical thinking, decision-making and skill, but found no significant effect on problem-solving skills [17]. These mixed results highlight that GBL is not a universally effective solution; rather, its impact may depend on factors such as alignment between game complexity and learner level, the integration with curriculum, and the duration of intervention.

Another source of inconsistency arises from heterogeneous implementation and methodological limitations across studies. A meta-analysis on serious games in nursing found high heterogeneity (I² values up to 90%) and effect sizes that ranged from low to moderate for knowledge, while self-confidence and skills showed moderate effects [46]. The authors noted that variations in game types, participant characteristics, intervention length and outcome measures limit the generalisability of positive findings. In some cases, novice learners confronted with highly complex game scenarios may experience a so-called “expertise reversal” effect—where the rich interactive format may hamper rather than help learning due to cognitive overload [47]. Thus, the design of a game-based intervention must carefully match students’ prior knowledge and cognitive load capacity, especially in specialised domains such as mental health nursing.

A particularly noteworthy finding of the present study was the durability of the observed improvements. The intervention group’s gains in both self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy were not only evident immediately post-intervention but were robustly maintained at the one-month follow-up, with no statistically significant decline from posttest to follow-up on either outcome. The large effect sizes for pretest-to-follow-up changes indicate that the benefits of the game-based learning intervention were sustained beyond the immediate post-intervention period. This finding is particularly meaningful in the context of educational interventions, where novelty effects and initial enthusiasm may produce transient gains that rapidly decay once the intervention is withdrawn [48]. The retention of gains at one month suggests that the game-based learning experience may have catalyzed deeper, more enduring changes in students’ self-regulatory capacities and academic self-beliefs, rather than merely producing a temporary motivational boost. This interpretation is consistent with the theoretical mechanisms outlined by Knowles [23] and Bandura [24], both of which emphasize that sustained changes in self-directedness and self-efficacy require repeated, successful experiences of autonomous functioning and mastery—precisely the conditions that the structured, iterative game design provided over its ten sessions.

Furthermore, the present study confirmed that game-based learning significantly improved nursing students’ academic self-efficacy. Through successful problem-solving, immediate feedback, and reflection on strengths and weaknesses, students developed stronger self-beliefs and gained greater control over their own learning process [49]. This mechanism is consistent with Bandura’s Self-Efficacy Theory [50], which posits that self-efficacy is strengthened through three primary sources: mastery experiences, immediate feedback, and vicarious experiences. Well-designed educational games inherently incorporate these mechanisms by providing progressive challenges, instant performance feedback, and modeled professional behaviors, thereby enhancing learners’ confidence, motivation, and active engagement [51]. Evidence from a recent study supports this mechanism, showing that GBL interventions in nursing education significantly improve self-efficacy across domains such as academic integrity, communication, collaboration, and clinical safety [52].

Beyond academic self-efficacy, the results also contribute to the growing body of evidence on self-directed learning (SDL) as a key outcome of GBL. Self-directed learners take initiative, set goals, and evaluate their own progress, which are critical attributes for lifelong professional growth [53]. Recent studies have shown that game-based methods promote SDL by fostering intrinsic motivation, reflective competence, and autonomous decision-making [54, 55]. For example, structured learning games that include feedback cycles, reflective checkpoints, and peer collaboration have been shown to enhance learners’ metacognitive awareness and responsibility for their learning trajectories [56]. These mechanisms are particularly important in nursing, where continuous self-assessment and reflection underpin clinical judgment and safe patient care. In this context, the improvement observed in self-directed learning scores among students in the intervention group suggests that GBL not only engages learners cognitively and emotionally but also cultivates the self-regulatory capacities necessary for professional competence.

Another important implication of the present study concerns the contextual adaptability of GBL in nursing education. While game-based strategies have been predominantly developed in Western educational systems, their successful application in non-Western contexts, such as Iran, highlights the potential for cultural adaptation and curriculum innovation [57]. Integrating culturally relevant content, values, and professional scenarios into game design ensures that learning activities resonate with students’ clinical realities and ethical frameworks. Moreover, the emphasis on teamwork, respect, and communication within GBL aligns closely with the collectivist cultural orientations observed in many Eastern societies, thereby reinforcing social learning and empathy among nursing students [58]. Thus, localized implementation of GBL not only promotes academic outcomes but also supports the development of culturally sensitive nursing professionals capable of delivering holistic patient care.

The results of this study underscore the potential of GBL as a transformative educational strategy in nursing curricula. Incorporating structured and theory-driven game elements into clinical courses—especially in complex fields like mental health nursing—can enhance both cognitive and affective learning outcomes. GBL may serve as an effective bridge between theoretical content and clinical practice, fostering reflective learning, confidence, and engagement. Future studies with larger and more diverse samples, as well as longitudinal designs, are recommended to evaluate the sustainability of these effects and to refine game-based instructional models for broader use in nursing education.

Limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of a structured, scenario-based GBL intervention implemented in a real clinical environment. Conducting the intervention under comparable conditions with a single instructor minimized potential confounding variables. However, Some limitations should be acknowledged.

First, the study was conducted in a single educational institution with a relatively small sample size (N = 40), which may limit the generalizability of findings to other nursing programs, cultural contexts, or educational settings. Multi-center studies with larger and more diverse student populations are needed to confirm the replicability of the observed effects.

Second, although the one-month follow-up provided evidence of short-term retention of learning gains, it remains insufficient to establish the long-term durability of the intervention effects. Future research should incorporate extended follow-up periods of six months to one year to determine whether the gains in self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy persist over clinically meaningful timeframes and translate into improved clinical performance in subsequent placements.

Third, a significant limitation concerns the risk of educational contamination between study groups. Because both the intervention and control groups were drawn from the same academic cohort at a single institution, there was a possibility that control group participants could have been exposed to information about the game-based learning activities through informal peer communication during the five-week study period. Although several preventive measures were implemented—including scheduling group sessions on separate days, restricting game materials to a dedicated simulation laboratory, and instructing participants not to discuss educational activities across groups—complete elimination of contamination cannot be guaranteed in an unblinded educational trial conducted within a single cohort. If contamination did occur, it would likely have resulted in the control group adopting some elements of self-directed learning strategies or experiencing increased motivation from exposure to the novel intervention, thereby attenuating the observed between-group differences. Consequently, the effect sizes reported in this study may represent conservative estimates of the true intervention effect. Future research should consider employing a cluster design with randomization at the cohort or institutional level, or incorporating a crossover design with a washout period, to more effectively control for contamination.

Fourth, the absence of blinding of participants, the intervention facilitator, outcome assessors, and the data analyst represents a source of potential bias. Self-reported outcome measures may be susceptible to social desirability and expectancy effects, particularly among participants aware of receiving a novel educational intervention. Future research should, at minimum, employ blinded outcome assessment and, where possible, incorporate objective performance-based measures alongside self-report instruments.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides strong evidence that implementing game-based learning in a mental health nursing internship significantly enhances nursing students’ self-directed learning and academic self-efficacy. The intervention’s integration of interactive gameplay, scenario-based challenges, clinical practice and theoretical review enabled students to engage actively, receive immediate feedback, and build confidence in their academic and clinical capabilities. These findings affirm the potential of game-based strategies to shift traditional nurse education toward more student-centred, engaging, and competency-oriented methods.

Nevertheless, educators and curriculum planners should not assume game-based learning will automatically yield positive outcomes across all domains. Given the mixed evidence in the literature—especially regarding problem-solving skills and reasoning in some settings—and the high variability in effect sizes, careful attention to intervention design, fit with learners’ level and institutional context is essential. Future research should explore long-term follow-up, larger sample sizes, and standardised game design elements to optimise effectiveness and support scalable implementation in nursing education.
