Diagnostic value of automated breast volume scanner for breast ductal carcinoma in situ
Authors: Congliang Tian, Jialing Wu, Zinan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study explores the differential diagnostic value of conventional dimensional ultrasound (US) and automated breast volume scanner (ABVS) for breast ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) patients. A total of 986 female patients who underwent breast tumor surgery in our hospital from December 2019 to December 2022 were included. Clinical, US, ABVS, and pathological information were collected from all of the patients. Pathological results were used to separate patients into 3 groups: benign, DCIS, and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC). Single-factor and multivariate analyses were conducted to evaluate the characteristics of DCIS. Of the 986 patients with breast tumors included in this study, 498, 193, and 295 were diagnosed with benign, DCIS, and IDC tumors, respectively. Compared with benign tumors, DCIS tumors were characterized by higher age at onset and breast imaging-reporting and data system (BI-RADS) grades, together with high rates of extension to the nipple, microcalcification, convergence sign, abundant blood supply, and nipple discharge proportion. Relative to IDC patients, DCIS tumors exhibited lower BI-RADS grades, aspect ratio values, and Ki-67 index values together with lower rates of irregular morphology, unclear boundaries, posterior echo attenuation, convergence sign, enhanced peripheral echo, nipple discharge, and palpable masses, and higher rates of extension to the nipple. Higher microcalcification rates in DCIS tumors were observed than the proportion of non-calcification in IDC cases. Conventional US and ABVS images of DCIS tumors exhibit certain distinctive characteristics that can aid in the differential diagnosis of DCIS.

1. Introduction

Breast cancer is the most prevalent and a leading cause of cancer-related death among women globally, and breast cancer proportion continues to rise annually.[1] Pathologically, breast cancer can be roughly divided into invasive and noninvasive cancers, the majority of which are classified as ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) and invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), respectively.[2] The pathological classification of breast tumors has implications for tumor development, treatment, and prognosis.[3] Patients who plan to undergo surgery for breast tumors often require preoperative puncture biopsy for preoperative diagnosis.[4] Benign tumors can be treated via tumor resection and have a good prognosis.[5] DCIS cases are often treated through breast-conserving surgery and adjuvant endocrine therapy, thus resulting in a better prognosis.[6] However, 8 to 38% of patients diagnosed with DCIS via preoperative biopsy ultimately receive a pathological diagnosis of IDC after surgery and must subsequently undergo a second surgical procedure.[7] The treatment of IDC cases necessitates a combination of preoperative neoadjuvant chemotherapy, breast resection, axillary lymph node dissection, and postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy.[8] The prognosis varies depending on genotype, degree of disease progression, and patient condition.[9] Without treatment, approximately 25 to 60% of DCIS cases progress to IDC.[10] Therefore, early detection, diagnosis, and treatment of DCIS are essential approaches to reducing recurrence rates and improving patient prognosis.

Mammography is a effective method for early detection of breast tumor.[11] Most DCIS cases are detected through mammography during physical examination.[12] While X-ray examination can improve diagnostic rates, it also contributes to higher rates of overdiagnosis, resulting in unnecessary radiation damage and overtreatment.[13] The circular enhancement sign and apparent diffusion coefficient derived from magnetic resonance imaging can aid in distinguishing between DCIS and IDC lesions, but this approach is expensive and not suitable for routine screening.[14] Ultrasonography offers greater diagnostic utility than X-ray imaging for soft tissue lesions.[15] As it is not affected by dense breast tissue and does not require the use of ionizing radiation, ultrasonography plays an irreplaceable role in breast cancer screening and diagnosis.[16] DCIS lesions often lack typical malignant ultrasound (US) signs, and their lesions are often mixed with normal or other diseased tissues, making the conventional US-based diagnosis thereof more difficult.[17] However, the continuous development and application of US technologies has enabled the US-based diagnosis of DCIS lesions.[18] The automated breast volume scanner (ABVS) approach can scan the breast, and its coronal plane offers unique advantages for detecting the features of tumor edges.[19] ABVS approaches can also detect calcification and the long-axis orientation of the tumor, which can provide greater diagnostic insight to facilitate the differentiation between different types of breast tumors.[20] As such, this study aimed o explore the differential diagnostic value of conventional US and ABVS for DCIS patients.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Ethical approval

This retrospective study was approved by the review board of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University (PJ-KS-KY-2023-118). All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant clinical research ethics committee and with those of the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki). Informed consent was waived, which was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University because the present study is retrospective. All data were fully anonymized before we accessed them.

2.2. Patients

We collected 986 female patients (average age: 48.81 ± 13.97 years, range: 14–88 years) who underwent breast tumor surgery in our hospital from December 2019 to December 2022 for study inclusion. Patients eligible for inclusion were those that: underwent routine two-dimensional US and ABVS examination within 2 weeks before surgery, had clear and complete US images and reports, had an accurate and complete pathological diagnosis, and had complete clinical data. Exclusion criteria: inflammatory masses or abscesses, lobular tumors or other special tumors, radiotherapy and chemotherapy, and incomplete data. The case selection process is detailed in Figure 1.

Flow chart of the study population enrollment.

2.3. Data collection

Clinical, pathological, and ultrasonographic information from the included patients was collected through the hospital information system, including age, whether a palpable tumor was detected, whether nipple discharge was evident, estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2, and Ki-67 proliferation index status, breast imaging-reporting and data system (BI-RADS) grading, distance from nipple, size, aspect ratio, shape, boundary, internal echo, rear echo, calcification, whether tumor extend to nipple, duct dilation, peripheral echo, blood circulation, edge, and convergence sign findings for each tumor. Convergence sign refers to the radial distribution of linear, slightly hyperechoic and hypoechoic glands around the lesion, which are clustered and converged. Calcifications with a diameter <1 mm are called microcalcifications. Rich blood refers to the presence of 5 or more punctate or 2 or more longer blood vessels within a tumor. Enhanced peripheral echo is that the echo intensity around the tumor is higher than that of the tumor.

When conducting retrospective analyses of US images, experienced US physicians analyzed these images and recorded the relevant US features of breast tumors in a blinded manner. The 2013 version of the BI-RADS grading criteria was employed. Tumor size was determined based on the maximum diameter of each section. Abundant blood circulation refers to the detection of 5 or more punctate or 2 or more longer blood vessels within a tumor. ABVS observes the edge, calcification, and direction of the tumor, assessing the edges for burrs or angles. Calcifications with a diameter of <1 mm are referred to as microcalcifications. The direction of the tumor indicates whether its long axis points toward the nipple. Convergence sign refers to a band-shaped gland with slightly high and low echogenicity that gathers and converges radially around the lesion.

2.4. Data analysis

All of the statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk). In the single-factor analyses, categorical variables were reported as frequencies and compared using the chi-square test. According to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, if P > .05, it conforms to a normal distribution, otherwise it is a non-normal distribution for continuous variables. Normal distribution variables were reported as the mean ± standard deviation and were compared by one-way ANOVAs. Non-normal distribution were reported as the median (interquartile range) and compared using Kruskal–Wallis test. The Bonferroni correction adjusts p values because of the increased risk of a type I error when making multiple statistical tests. Multivariate analyses were performed using multi-classification logistic regression statistics. Performed automatic variable screening through stepwise regression analysis. The collinearity test is used to evaluate whether there is a high degree of linear correlation between data. The concordance proportion was calculated to determine how well the predicted model approximates the data.

4. Discussion

Breast DCIS lesions consist of breast cancer cells that are confined to the breast duct epithelium without penetrating the basement membrane, with no metastatic foci and no apparent clinical symptoms in most cases. DCIS cases are prone to misdiagnosis.[21] DCIS is considered to be an early-stage breast cancer lesion with the potential to develop into invasive breast cancer.[22] The prognosis of breast cancer patients is directly related to early detection, early diagnosis, and early treatment. It is thus important that the diagnostic accuracy for breast carcinoma in situ cases be improved in order to enable timely treatment and improved patient outcomes.

In clinical practice, most DCIS lesions are discovered through US or molybdenum target examination during routine physical examinations. Molybdenum target mammography can detect microcalcifications associated with breast cancer malignancy. While breast cancer screening sensitivity is approximately 85%, this rate falls to 47.8 to 64.4% for dense breast tissue.[23] Ultrasonography offers greater soft tissue resolution than molybdenum target mammography and is not affected by glandular density.[24] It also offers advantages including simple operating procedures, low cost, real-time imaging, a noninvasive approach, and repeatability. Therefore, US plays an important role in the diagnosis and treatment of breast tumors. Conventional US offers a high degree of diagnostic value in the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant breast diseases. However, the biological characteristics and ultrasonographic manifestations of DCIS of the breast are not typical, thus hampering the early diagnosis of carcinoma in situ of the breast. Kanako Ban reviewed and analyzed the US characteristics of 705 cases of BCIS, which mainly manifested as local ductal dilation and internal solid echo with wide base.[25] Through ABVS, Li Siqi found that the positive rate of convergence sign in IDC is higher than DCIS, and scattered microcalcifications are more common in DCIS.[26] Previous study have shown the ABVS detection rate of malignant tumors was higher than that of conventional US, and the tumor diameter detected using ABVS was also more accurate,[20] but it did not specify which US indicators are useful for diagnosing DCIS. Therefore, this study summarizes the characteristics of US and ABVS, and explores the diagnostic value of ABVS for DCIS.

The BI-RADS grading evaluation standard is commonly used when assessing breast lesions in order to evaluate the degree of malignancy based on specific characteristics in a standardized manner that can reduce errors and uncertainty associated with the interpretation of breast imaging reports, particularly for the diagnosis and review of breast cancer.[27] The main US features of benign tumors include regular morphology, clear boundaries, uniform internal echoes, no calcification, poor blood flow, and an aspect ratio <1 and mostly classified as 3-4a, while the main US features of malignant tumors include irregular morphology, unclear boundaries, uneven internal echoes, microcalcifications, abundant blood flow, and an aspect ratio >1 and are mostly classified as 4b-5.[28] This study found that the main US features of DCIS include irregular shape, clear boundaries, uneven internal echoes, microcalcifications, abundant blood supply, aspect ratio <1, and extend to nipple and the BI-RADS classification was primarily 4a-4c, which is consistent with the clinical understanding that the degree of malignancy of DCIS is between that of benign and IDC lesions. Previous studies have suggested that a breast tumor aspect ratio >0.7 can be used as a diagnostic indicator for malignancy.[29] In this study, DCIS lesions exhibited the lowest aspect ratio, which may be related to their lateral growth along the duct. Microcalcifications in breast tumors are closely related to tumor properties, forming as a result of intratumoral calcium deposition, necrosis, and vascular calcification.[30] The proportion of microcalcification in DCIS in this study was significantly higher than benign lesions, but similar to IDC lesions. The breast includes 15 to 20 glandular lobes, and the ducts of these glandular lobes eventually converge at the nipple.[31,32] As DCIS lesions are located in the duct and do not penetrate the basement membrane layer, the long axis of these tumors may point toward the nipple as the tumors grow. The proportion of DCIS extending to the nipple in this study was 2.19 times higher than that for benign tumors and 3.23 times higher than that for IDC tumors, with both of these differences being statistically significant. The convergence sign identified in ABVS coronal sections is a specific breast cancer-related sign that results from the fact that breast cancer cells can penetrate the basement membrane and infiltrate into the surrounding tissue, and the surrounding normal fibrous connective tissue is stretched after reactive hyperplasia, thus forming a radial gland contraction centered on the focus.[33] DCIS lesions are primarily confined in the breast duct and do not break through the basement membrane. Although some of them are accompanied by micro invasion, their ability to invade surrounding tissues is weaker than that of IDC cells. In this study, the proportion of convergence signs in DCIS similar to benign lesions, but lower than IDC significantly. This confirms that the ABVS convergence sign offers great diagnostic value for IDC. This study also found that DCIS lesions are also characterized by older age at onset, a richer blood supply, and a higher proportion of nipple discharge compared to benign tumors. Relative to IDC tumors, DCIS lesions also exhibit lower Ki-67 index values, blurred boundaries, attenuated posterior echoes, enhanced peripheral echoes, and a lower proportion of nipple discharge and palpability.

There were several limitations in this study. Firstly, bias can not be avoided as a retrospective study. Secondly, the data are still selective and cheap due to single-center design. Thirdly, we did not study the interobserver variability. A prospective study evaluating the diagnostic performance of the ABVS in addition to B-mode US is in progress, and a large-scale, multicenter study is required.

5. Conclusion

The main features of DCIS include nipple discharge, microcalcification, no convergence sign, aspect ratio <1, and extend to nipple. Although DCIS tumors lack in typical IDC US signs and exhibit some degree of overlap with benign tumor US signs, conventional US and ABVS are useful applications for assessing DCIS.
