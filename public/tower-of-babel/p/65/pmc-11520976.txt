Establishment of a new method to isolate viable x‐ray‐sensitive cells from planarian by fluorescence‐activated cell sorting
Authors: Miyuki Ishida, Yoshihito Kuroki, Kiyokazu Agata
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlanarians show outstanding regenerative ability due to the proliferation of neoblasts. Hence the method to isolate planarian neoblasts is important to understand the regeneration process. In our previous study, we reported a method to isolate planarian neoblasts of Dugesia japonica using fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS). However, we have not yet succeeded in cultivating these cells even under in vivo conditions after transplantation into x‐ray‐irradiated planarians. This suggests that dissociated cells might enter apoptotic or necrotic states in the process of fluorescent dye staining and sorting. Here, we developed a new method to isolate viable neoblasts, which can proliferate in the x‐ray‐irradiated planarians. First, the toxicity of various fluorescence dyes was investigated. All nuclear fluorescent dyes such as Hoechst 33342, DRAQ5, and DyeCycle, showed, more or less, toxicity to mammalian culture cells. In contrast, cytoplasmic fluorescent dye for live cells, calcein AM, was less toxic on these cells. Next, we stained the dissociated planarian cells with only calcein AM, and then collected the x‐ray‐sensitive fraction. Although the purity of neoblasts was slightly lower than that of the original staining method (ca. 97% → ca. 89%), the sorted cells could actively proliferate when they were injected into x‐ray‐irradiated planarians. This simple staining and sorting method will provide new opportunities to isolate viable neoblasts and understand regenerating processes.Keywords: calcein AM, fluorescence‐activated cell sorting, neoblasts, planarian, transplantation

INTRODUCTION

Planarians have high regenerative capacity, which is due to the presence of neoblasts (adult pluripotent stem cells, aPSCs) throughout their body (Agata et al., 2006; Agata & Watanabe, 1999). An understanding of the characteristics of aPSCs, such as motility, localization, and gene expression is important to understand the regeneration mechanisms of planarians. Our group established a method to purify neoblasts from planarian bodies using fluorescence‐activated cell sorting (FACS) (Hayashi et al., 2006; Higuchi et al., 2007). Generally, cells can be sorted by FACS using cell‐type‐specific surface markers (Hulett et al., 1969). However, such markers have not been identified in planarians. Therefore, we have focused on the cellular characteristics of planarian to purify the neoblasts. Our method is based on the fact that neoblasts are the only cells with proliferative activity in the planarian body and are very sensitive to x‐ray irradiation (Shibata et al., 1999, 2010; Wolff & Dubois, 1948). Also, neoblasts have scant cytoplasm and show typical undifferentiated cell morphology, without endoplasmic reticulum or Golgi apparatus (Pedersen, 1959). To purify neoblasts using these characteristics by FACS, three fluorescence dyes (PI, Hoechst 33342, calcein AM) are used. PI, Hoechst 33342, and calcein AM stain dead cells, nuclear DNA, and cytoplasm of live cells, respectively (Jones & Senft, 1985; Loken, 1980; Weston & Parish, 1990). The fraction of planarian neoblasts can be identified by comparing FACS profiles of x‐ray‐irradiated and nonirradiated planarians (Hayashi et al., 2006). Thus, the use of FACS is a powerful method to isolate planarian neoblasts.

Hayashi et al. obtained two x‐ray‐sensitive fractions (named X1 and X2) and one x‐ray‐insensitive fraction (named Xis). The X1 fraction contained proliferating cells (higher DNA content: high intensity of Hoechst 33342 nuclear dye staining, scant cytoplasm: low intensity of calcein AM cytoplasmic dye staining), suggesting that the X1 fraction might be composed of neoblasts (Hayashi et al., 2006). Our group's FACS protocol has been widely used for the purification of planarian neoblasts (Fernandéz‐Taboada et al., 2010; Reddien et al., 2005; Romero et al., 2012; Scimone et al., 2010). In fact, single‐cell gene expression analysis indicated that 87.6% of cells in the X1 fraction expressed DjpiwiA, a neoblasts‐specific marker in Dugesia japonica (Hayashi et al., 2010), supporting the high purity of our method. Thus, Hoechst 33342 staining is an excellent method for the purification of neoblasts. However, Hoechst 33342 is generally considered to have cytotoxicity for the cells (Siemann & Keng, 1986). For example, mouse leukemia and human lymphoma cell lines stained with Hoechst 33342 proliferated more slowly than unstained cells (Erba et al., 1988; Fried et al., 1982). Furthermore, UV light for the excitation of Hoechst 33342 damages DNA (Rastogi et al., 2010) and causes cell cycle disturbances and cell death in a variety of organisms, including bacteria, plants, and animals (Hall et al., 1996; Nawkar et al., 2013; Stein et al., 1989). Therefore, an improved method for the purification of planarian neoblasts with low toxicity and high purity is required.

To understand the proliferation of neoblasts and the following regeneration processes, the transplantation experiment of the purified neoblasts into the x‐ray‐irradiated neoblasts‐less planarians is a powerful approach. In the previous reports, wild type of neoblasts were transplanted into the x‐ray‐irradiated eye‐deficient mutants of Dugesia ryukyuensis and successfully induced the regeneration of the head, including the eyes (Kobayashi et al., 2008; Nodono et al., 2012). Furthermore, in another species of planarian, Schmidtea mediterranea, x‐ray‐irradiated planarians were rescued by transplantation of a single cell, indicating that all types of cells can be generated from a single cell (Wagner et al., 2011; Zeng et al., 2018). These experiments demonstrated that some neoblasts retain their pluripotency, and they are subsequently called aPSCs. However, although such transplantation experiments unraveled the role of aPSCs during regeneration processes, in vitro culturing of the purified aPSCs is still difficult. Moreover, there have been no reports showing that FACS‐purified neoblasts expanded in vivo in D. japonica, a related species of D. ryukyuensis. This lack of knowledge suggested that the neoblasts isolated using FACS might enter into apoptotic or necrotic phases during the process of staining and sorting. Here, we develop a new method to isolate viable planarian neoblasts using FACS with low toxicity. Furthermore, we demonstrate the proliferative potential of the purified cells in vivo by injecting them into x‐ray‐irradiated planarians.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

A clonal strain of the planarian D. japonica derived from the Iruma River in Gifu Prefecture, Japan was used in this study. Planarians were cultured in dechlorinated water at 21°C (Mori et al., 2019). Chicken liver was fed to them twice a week. Planarians were starved for 4 days or more before experiments. We used planarians that were 8 mm in length.

X‐ray irradiation

Planarians were irradiated with 100 R of x‐rays by using an x‐ray generator (SOFTEX B‐5) (Kato et al., 2001).

Cell culture

PC12 cells were derived from a pheochromocytoma of the rat adrenal medulla. The cells were maintained on collagen (Cellmatrix Type IV, Nitta Gelatin)‐coated plates in high‐glucose Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM; Wako) containing 10% horse serum (HS; SAFC Biosciences), 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest), 1× penicillin–streptomycin solution (penicillin: 100 units/mL; streptomycin: 100 μg/mL; Wako) at 37°C in 5% CO₂ (Yamamoto et al., 2016). Cell suspensions were prepared using trypsin–ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solutions (Nacalai Tesque). EB5 cells are a mouse ES (embryonic stem) cell line. The cells were maintained on gelatin (Sigma)‐coated plates in Glasgow's minimal essential medium (GMEM; Wako) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biowest), 100 μM 2‐mercaptoethanol (Nacalai Tesque), 1× nonessential amino acids solution (Nacalai Tesque), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Nacalai Tesque), 1,000 units/mL LIF (ESGRO, Sigma‐Aldrich), 1× penicillin–streptomycin solution (penicillin: 100 units/mL; streptomycin: 100 μg/mL; Wako) at 37°C in 5% CO₂. EB5 cells were cultured in the presence of 1 μM PD0325901 (Wako) as MEK inhibitor and 3 μM CHIR99021 (Wako) as GSK‐3β inhibitor (Ogawa et al., 2004). Cell suspensions were prepared using trypsin–EDTA solution (Nacalai Tesque).

Cell staining

In the toxicity test of previously reported staining methods (Figure 1), PC12 and EB5 cells were stained as follows. In the Hoechst 33342 staining method (Hayashi et al., 2006), cells (2 × 106 cells/mL) were stained with 32 μM Hoechst 33342 (Dojindo) and 0.5 μg/mL calcein AM (Invitrogen) for 2 hr at 37°C. In the DRAQ5 staining method (Peiris et al., 2016), cells (1 × 106 cells/ml) were stained with 5 μM DRAQ5 (BioStatus) for 20 min and 0.4 μg/mL calcein AM for 10 min at 37°C.

In the toxicity test of various nuclear dyes at different concentrations (Figure 2), PC12 and EB5 cells were stained with Hoechst 33342, DRAQ5, and DyeCycle (Invitrogen) at a concentration of 1, 3, 5, 10, or 32 μM/1 × 106 cells for 30 min at 37°C. At 1 hr (only for EB5 cells), and 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 days (for PC12 and EB5 cells) after culturing, the cytotoxicity was checked by using a Cell Counting Kit‐8 (Dojindo).

For the staining of cytoplasm (Figure S1), 2.5 μg of CellTracker Green CMFDA (Invitrogen) was added to 2 × 106 dissociated planarian cells in 1 mL suspension. The cells were stained at 20°C for 10 min. CellTracker‐positive cells were collected by FACS, suspended in Holtfreter's solution diluted 5/8 in distilled water to 20,000 cells/μL, and 69 nL of the cell suspension was injected into the region posterior to the pharynx.

Preparation of dissociated planarian cells and staining for isolating aPSCs

Dissociated cells were prepared based on the previous report (Hayashi & Agata, 2018; Hayashi et al., 2006). Ten planarians were chopped into small pieces on ice and transferred to 980 μL of Holtfreter's solution diluted 5/8 in distilled water. The samples were agitated in 0.1% trypsin solution (Nacalai Tesque) at 20°C for 5 min at 20 rpm by a rotator (MACSmix; Miltenyi Biotec). Cell suspensions were gently pipetted 60 times with a wide‐bore tip and centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 3 min. After washing two times, tissue fragments were removed by filtering through 30 μm and 20 μm filters (Sysmex). In the original Hoechst staining method, cell suspensions (2 × 106 cells/ml) were stained with 32 μM Hoechst 33342 and 0.1 μg/mL calcein AM for 2 hr and incubated at 20°C. X‐ray‐sensitive cells were isolated using violet and blue lasers of BD FACS Melody (Kuroki et al., unpublished). In the Hoechst low staining method, cells (1 × 106 cells/mL) were stained with 1–10 μM Hoechst 33342 for 30 min and incubated at 20°C. In the only calcein AM staining method, cells (2 × 106 cells/mL) were stained with 0.05 μg/mL calcein AM for 30 min at 20°C.

Immunostaining

Cells were fixed with 3.7% formaldehyde (Cica) in 45% of 1× phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Fixed cells were smeared on a slide glass with Smear Gell (GenoStaff). After washing three times with TPBS (PBS containing 0.1% Triton X‐100) for 5 min per wash, cells were blocked with 1% blocking reagent (Roche) and 20% FBS in MNT Buffer (0.1 M maleic acid, 0.15 M NaCl, 0.1% Triton X‐100) for 30 min at RT. Samples were incubated with rabbit anti‐DjPiwiA antibody (1/1,000) (Shibata et al., 2016) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with 1× PBS for 5 min per wash, cells were treated with the secondary antibody, CF 594 goat anti‐rabbit IgG(H + L) (1/1,000; BIOTIUM) for 90 min at RT. Whole‐mount immunostaining was carried out as previously reported (Tasaki et al., 2011). Mouse anti‐DjPiwiA monoclonal antibody (1/500) (Shibata et al., 2016) and rabbit anti‐pH 3 antibody (1/200; Sigma‐Aldrich, 06‐570) (Hendzel et al., 1997) were used as a primary antibody, and CF 488 goat anti‐mouse IgG(H + L) (1/1000; BIOTIUM) and CF 594 goat anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) (1/1000) were used as a secondary antibody. The images were captured using an FV10i microscope (Olympus).

Planarian cell culture and viability check

Ten thousand isolated cells were cultured in 200 μL of 2/3 diluted KnockOut DMEM (Gibco) with 5% FBS in the well of a 96‐well plate (Greiner Bio‐One) at 20°C in 5% CO₂. The viability of cultured cells was checked by staining with 0.05 μg/well calcein AM (Invitrogen) and 0.2 μg/well PI (Dojindo).

Cell transplantation

Cells isolated by the only calcein AM staining method were transplanted into x‐ray‐irradiated planarians using a microinjector (NANOJECTII; Drummond). Planarians were placed with their ventral side up on ice covered with a filter paper, and a capillary glass was inserted into the planarian at a 30° angle (Wang et al., 2018). To confirm the injection of the purified cells into the body, we injected a mixture of 20,000 cells/μL cell suspension with 20,000 fluorescent beads/μL (Fluoresbrite YG Carboxylate Microspheres 6.00 μm; Polysciences). Sixty‐nine nanoliters of cell suspension was injected into the anterior portions of both eyes or into the posterior region of the pharynx between the two main intestinal ducts using a siliconized (Fuji Systems Corporation) capillary glass (Drummond).

Statistics

All the data were expressed as the mean ± SEM and analyzed by Microsoft Excel. The statistical analysis was performed by means of one‐way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple‐comparisons test. Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Toxicity test of fluorescent dyes with the previously reported methods

To investigate the toxicity of fluorescent dyes that are used for the isolation of planarian neoblasts by FACS, we stained rat PC12 cells and a mouse ES cell line (EB5) as representative cells of committed and pluripotent stem cells, respectively. We stained these cells with Hoechst 33342 and calcein AM, which were used to isolate planarian neoblasts (Hayashi et al., 2006). Then we sorted the positive cells by FACS and checked their viability by in vitro cell culturing. When the cells were stained with 32 μM Hoechst 33342 and 0.5 μg/mL calcein AM, almost half of the PC12 cells survived compared with the nonstained cells at 5 days after culturing (Figure 1a, black line), while the mouse ES cells were completely unable to survive (Figure 1b, black line). This result suggests the high toxicity of co‐staining according to the previous method (Hayashi et al., 2006) (hereafter referred to as “the original staining method”). To investigate which dye was toxic to these cells, they were separately stained with Hoechst 33342 and calcein AM. The results clearly indicated that Hoechst 33342 showed high toxicity to PC12 and EB5, while calcein AM did not affect their viability (Figure 1a,b, green line). To avoid the toxicity of UV excitation for Hoechst 33342, we further investigated another nuclear dye, DRAQ5, which is excited by red laser light (Henderson et al., 2013) and was used for the isolation of planarian neoblasts (Peiris et al., 2016). However, the toxicity of DRAQ5 was higher than that of Hoechst 33342 (Figure 1a,c). These results again support the necessity of the improved isolation method.

Toxicity of various nuclear dyes at a range of concentrations

To optimize the staining condition with low toxicity, we tested lower concentrations of staining dyes. We also evaluated the toxicity of a nuclear dye, DyeCycle, which has not been reported for staining planarian cells. DyeCycle is excited by red laser light, like DRAQ5. The use of DyeCycle has been reported for staining cells for injection into ascidians and for the generation of sponge hybridomas (Kassmer et al., 2020; Pomponi et al., 2013).

In PC12 cells, Hoechst 33342 and DyeCycle were less toxic than DRAQ5 (Figure 2a,c,e). DRAQ5 showed the strongest toxicity among these dyes also in EB5 cells (Figure 2d), and DyeCycle showed intermediate toxicity among them (Figure 2f). Unexpectedly, Hoechst 33342 showed the lowest toxicity among the three dyes (Figure 2b), although its toxicity has been generally concerning (Erba et al., 1988; Fried et al., 1982; Siemann & Keng, 1986). EB5 cells showed higher sensitivity to all three nuclear dyes when compared with PC12 cells. However, when the cells were stained with low concentrations of Hoechst 33342 (<10 μM), not only PC12 but also EB5 cells could survive after dye staining and FACS sorting (Figure 2a,b). We concluded that to isolate viable stem cells based on their DNA content, Hoechst 33342 is the best dye, and its concentration should be less than 10 μM.

Isolation of the x‐ray‐sensitive cells after staining with Hoechst 33342 at lower concentrations

Next, we investigated whether the staining of Hoechst 33342 with lower concentrations can be applied to isolate the neoblasts in planarians using FACS. First, dissociated cells from intact and x‐ray‐irradiated planarians were stained with 1–10 μM and 32 μM Hoechst 33342. Then, we recorded the FACS plots of Hoechst 33342 versus FSC‐H (Figure 3a). By comparing the plots of intact and x‐ray‐irradiated planarians, the area which contains more neoblasts can be identified. In the original staining method (Hayashi et al., 2006), the X1 fraction consisting of neoblasts was found in Hoechst 33342 high region (Figure 3a, solid line box). However, no X1 fraction was identified in the 1–10 μM plots due to the weak intensity of Hoechst 33342 (Figure 3a). To acquire the weak signal, we increased the gain configuration of Hoechst 33342 to a position corresponding to the plot of 32 μM Hoechst 33342 (Figure 3b). Unexpectedly, in the 1–10 μM Hoechst 33342 plots, cells in the Hoechst 33342 low/FSC‐H high region disappeared (Figure 3b, dashed‐line region). We considered that this fraction contained a large number of neoblasts and collected these cells to determine the percentage of neoblasts. These cells were further stained by the original staining method and analyzed by FACS (Figure 4a).

We identified the X1 fraction by comparing x‐ray‐irradiated and nonirradiated cell populations in each condition (Figure 4b, solid line box). The percentage of the X1 fraction was calculated as a ratio of the X1 fraction in Hoechst 33342 and calcein AM double‐positive cells. The results showed that the sorted cells contained 75%–87% X1 fraction cells (Figure 4c), suggesting that this isolation method is useful for isolating viable neoblasts. We concluded that it is possible to isolate viable neoblasts after staining with a lower concentration of Hoechst 33342 than in the original staining method, although the purity of neoblasts is slightly lower than that of neoblasts in the original staining method. The lowest concentration, 1 μM, is considered the least toxic, hence we adopted this concentration for the subsequent analysis (hereafter referred to as “the Hoechst low” staining method).

An alternative method to isolate neoblasts using calcein AM cytoplasmic dye

In addition to “the Hoechst low” staining method, we tried to isolate neoblasts after staining with only calcein AM, because this dye did not show any toxicity to the mammalian cells (Figure 1). After staining with only calcein AM for 30 min, we obtained two fractions that showed high sensitivity to x‐ray irradiation (Figure 5a). One fraction was detected in the calcein AM‐low (scant cytoplasm)/FSC‐A‐low (small size) fraction (hereinafter referred to as XS). The other fraction was detected in the calcein AM‐low (scant cytoplasm)/FSC‐A‐high (large size) fraction (hereinafter referred to as XL). When these two fractions were collected and their cellular populations were analyzed by the original staining method in the same way as Figure 4b (Figure 5b), 53% of the XS cells and 92% of the XL cells corresponded to the X1 cells (Figure 5c). Therefore, we decided to use the XL cells in the following experiments. In this study, we define the isolation of the XL cells by staining with calcein AM as “the only calcein AM” staining method.

Comparison of purity and viability between “the Hoechst low” and “the only calcein AM” staining methods

As described above, we showed that two different methods could be used to isolate viable neoblasts using FACS. To determine which is better as a standard method for isolating viable planarian neoblasts, we compared the purity and viability of these cells between the two methods. First, the cell purity was assessed by immunohistochemistry using an anti‐DjPiwiA antibody, which is known to bind specifically to neoblasts (Figure 6a). The purity of neoblasts obtained by the original staining method was higher than 97% (Figure 6b). The purities of both “the Hoechst low” and “the only calcein AM” staining methods were almost 90% and were not significantly different. Thus, although the original method resulted in the highest purity, both of the new methods were sufficient for isolating neoblasts in healthy conditions.

Next, the viabilities of cells isolated by the two new methods were compared by culturing the planarian cells for 5 days in vitro. Figure 6c shows the survival rates of these cells in a diluted MEM‐based medium. Interestingly, more than 60% of the cells isolated by “the only calcein AM” staining method could survive for 24 hr, whereas the original staining method and “the Hoechst low” staining method showed 30% and 40% survival rates, respectively. These results suggest that “the only calcein AM” staining method was the most suitable among the three methods for isolating viable neoblasts with high purity at least for 1–2 days.

Proliferation and expansion of transplanted cells isolated by “the only calcein AM” staining method

To demonstrate that the isolated cells can be applied to further experiments, we transplanted the neoblasts purified into the x‐ray‐irradiated planarians. First, to count the number of injected cells, we stained the planarian cells with CellTracker Green CMFDA and collected stained cells by FACS (data not shown). The cell suspension was aspirated by the glass capillary (Figure S1a) and the number of the injected cells was estimated as 100 cells (Figure S1b). Next, the cells were injected into the tail region of the planarian (Figure S1c). The planarians were immediately fixed, and the injected cells were confirmed by fluorescence microscopy (Figure S1d–l). Finally, we purified the neoblasts using “the only calcein AM” staining method and injected them into the planarian based on the above injection system. Two weeks after injection, we examined the propagation of injected cells in the mesenchymal space by staining the cells with the anti‐DjPiwiA antibody. In nonirradiated planarians, there were many DjPiwiA‐positive cells distributed almost uniformly in the mesenchymal space of both the ventral and dorsal sides (Figures 7a and S2a). In contrast, in the x‐ray‐irradiated planarians, no DjPiwiA‐positive cells were observed (Figures 7h and S2h). When the isolated cells were injected into the tail region, many DjPiwiA‐positive cells were detected around the injected point in both the ventral and dorsal sides (Figures 7b–g and S2b–g), suggesting the propagation of the injected cells. However, these cells did not spread to the head side above the pharynx within 2 weeks after injection. Interestingly, DjPiwiA‐positive cells collectively migrated and formed branch‐like patterns, particularly on the ventral side; the cells aligned with the left–right and anterior–posterior axes (Figures 7b–g and S2b–g).

The sorted cells maintain proliferative activity

Injection of only about 100 sorted cells (Figure S1b) resulted in the production of many DjPiwiA‐positive cells (Figures 7b–g and S2b–g), suggesting that the transplanted neoblasts might proliferate after injection and spread out in the tail region. To investigate whether the transplanted cells maintained proliferative activity in vivo, we stained neoblasts undergoing proliferation using the M phase‐specific antibody phosphorylated Histone H3 (pH 3). In control experiments, nonirradiated planarians showed almost equally distributed pH 3‐positive cells in the mesenchymal space (Figure 8a,a’), and x‐ray‐irradiated planarians did not show any pH 3‐positive cells (Figure 8d,d’). On the other hand, pH 3‐positive cells were detected in the tail region when the neoblasts were injected into the tail region (Figure 8b,b’), suggesting that the injected neoblasts have proliferative activity. Next, to clearly show the injected point‐specific proliferation, we simultaneously injected neoblasts into the tail and eye regions (Figure 8c,c’,c”). As we expected, pH 3‐positive cells were detected around the tail and prepharyngeal regions. These results clearly demonstrated that the sorted cells using “the only calcein AM” staining method maintained proliferative activity. We also found that neoblasts injected around the eye region migrated to the prepharyngeal region (Figure 8c). Since neoblasts do not normally exist in the head, it is thought that the injected neoblasts migrated and proliferated according to the injected body's positional information. The success rates of injection and propagation of the injected cells are summarized in Table 1. Transplantation experiments were performed with single staining for DjPiwiA after injection into the tail, co‐staining for DjPiwiA and pH 3 after injection into the tail, and co‐staining for DjPiwiA and pH 3 after injection into the head and tail (Table 1). In each experiment, 7/21, 4/10, and 2/5 planarians showed the expected single or double signal, respectively. However, it should be noted that all of the injected planarians under the same conditions were dead within about 2 weeks after the injection, and that the tail pieces after amputation cannot regenerate without blastema formation. Collectively, these results show that neoblasts isolated by FACS from the planarian D. japonica proliferated and propagated in x‐ray‐irradiated planarian host bodies after transplantation.

DISCUSSION

In this study, to isolate planarian neoblasts with low toxicity and high purity, we developed two methods based on FACS. In the first one, “the Hoechst low” staining method, the cells were stained with 1 μM of Hoechst 33342 for 30 min while the original method used 32 μM of Hoechst 33342 for 2 hr to produce a saturated condition (Hayashi et al., 2006). This method improved the toxicity in culture cells (Figure 2a,b) and in dissociated planarian cells (Figure 6c). In the second one, “the only calcein AM” staining method, the cells were stained with 0.05 μg/mL of calcein AM for 30 min, enabling higher viability than that of “the Hoechst low” staining method (Figure 6c). Importantly, the purity of neoblasts in the two methods was nearly 90%, almost the same as the original staining method (Figures 4c, 5c, 6b). It is surprising that these simple staining methods could isolate neoblasts with low toxicity and high purity. Moreover, the utility of “the only calcein AM” staining method was confirmed by the transplantation experiments (Figures 7, 8, and S2).

In “the Hoechst low” staining method, X1 fraction cells were isolated as large cells (FSC‐H high) (Figure 3b). Moreover, in “the only calcein AM” staining method, we found two fractions that were specifically eliminated in the x‐ray‐irradiated planarians named XS and XL (Figure 5a). Interestingly, the XL fraction contained much X1 cells compared with the XS fraction (Figure 5c). These results suggest that cell size can be used as an indicator for the purification of the neoblasts. Although nuclear staining has been used as a conventional method, we believe that focusing on cell size and combining it with other fluorescent dyes for purification may also be useful.

Hayashi et al. reported that the X1 fraction can be found in the Hoechst 33342‐high region (Hayashi et al., 2006) (Figure 3a). Despite the differences in staining concentration, this is not consistent with our data because the X1 fraction was not found in Hoechst 33342‐high region (Figure 3b). We focused on this discrepancy and found that the fraction that showed relatively higher Hoechst 33342 signal in “the Hoechst low” staining method (Figure 3b, solid line boxes) contains unhealthy cell (data not shown); these cells contained less calcein AM‐positive cells. Although Hoechst 33342 is considered to stain both live cells and dead cells, the permeability of the plasma membrane can be changed depending on the cellular condition. Such a hypothesis is supported by the fact that PI selectively permeabilizes the plasma membrane of dead cells.

The injected neoblasts proliferated and formed branch‐like patterns (Figures 7b–g, 8b,c, and S2b–g). A similar phenotype was also observed in knockdown planarians of the MTA‐A or MTA‐B genes (Sato et al., 2022), whose mammalian homolog MTA1 is known to be associated with cell migration during cancer metastasis (Li & Kumar, 2015; Toh et al., 1994). MTA‐A (RNAi) and MTA‐B (RNAi) planarians exhibited notable defects in regeneration, although MTA‐A (RNAi) and MTA‐B (RNAi) planarians maintained the neoblasts in the branch‐like regions. In the RNAi planarians, the distribution pattern of the neoblasts was changed to a branch‐like pattern and the differentiation of the neoblasts tended to be inhibited. These results suggest that the branch‐like patterns could act as stem cell niches, maintaining the undifferentiated state, and that differentiation occurs upon detachment from the niche. The RNAi planarians cannot regenerate, although they maintained the neoblasts in the branch‐like regions. In this study, neoblasts were injected into x‐ray‐irradiated planarians to confirm whether they regenerated. However, all planarians died after about 2 weeks. The failure of regeneration may be caused by the inability to detach neoblasts from the branch‐like shape niche and failure to differentiate. A similar transplantation experiment was conducted using S. mediterranea (Wagner et al., 2011). However, branch‐like patterns were not observed 6 and 30 days after transplantation; the injected cells proliferated as colony‐like morphology. Also, when S. mediterranea were partially x‐ray irradiated, the neoblasts of S. mediterranea did not show any branch‐like structure during migration (Guedelhoefer & Sánchez Alvarado, 2012; Sato et al., 2022). The differences in the structure of stem cell niches between S. mediterranea and D. japonica may contribute to the regenerative ability. The identification of the factor that promotes detachment from the niche might be the key to understanding the regeneration process.

A previous report showed that the injection of the neoblasts into the x‐ray‐irradiated D. ryukyuensis enables it to regenerate from the amputated body (Kobayashi et al., 2008). Based on this report, we injected neoblasts into the x‐ray‐irradiated D. japonica and then amputated them. However, all the planarians (15/15) were dead after about 2 weeks (data not shown). The different number of injected neoblasts between these methods may have made these differences; ~10,000 cells in the previous report (Kobayashi et al., 2008) and ~100 cells in our experiments. An improved method to inject a high amount of the sorted cells is required. One more possibility is the species‐specific differences in the stem cell niche. Although no reports have observed the distribution of the neoblast after x‐ray irradiation in D. ryukyuensis, the molecular basis and structure of the stem cell niche could be different among planarian species.

In summary, we have developed a new purification method for planarian neoblasts with low toxicity and high purity. This method not only facilitates transplantation but also enables a wide range of experiments, including in vitro cultivation, single‐cell analysis, and drug screening. These approaches contribute to the understanding of the regenerative mechanisms of planarians and their potential applications in regenerative medicine and tissue regeneration.
