Prioritized barriers to implementation of personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients
Authors: Nynke Bos, Julie E M Swillens, Lisa Vandeberg, Jeroen A Schouten, Frank L van de Veerdonk, Mihai G Netea, Study group ESGBIES, Anke J M Oerlemans, Marlies E J L Hulscher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis remains a leading cause of mortality despite increased adherence to clinical guidelines. Personalized immunotherapy, tailored to individual immune profiles, holds promise as an adjunctive treatment. Understanding of barriers influencing decision-making is essential for developing appropriate implementation strategies to promote the uptake of personalized immunotherapy into routine practice. Identifying barriers early, before personalized immunotherapy is included in clinical guidelines, can help accelerate its implementation in the future. This study aims to prioritize these barriers.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among healthcare professionals involved in the care of patients with infectious diseases. Using maximum difference scaling, 29 barriers were ranked according to their perceived relevance. The mean relative importance score of each barrier was calculated using hierarchical Bayes estimation for the total sample, as well as separately for groups with low versus high levels of experience with immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases. These scores were used to rank barriers, with higher scores indicating higher relative importance.ResultsA total of 174 healthcare professionals completed the survey. The most relevant barriers identified were lack of protocols and insufficient scientific evidence. Regarding the remaining barriers, healthcare professionals with less immunotherapy experience prioritized individual-level barriers, such as insufficient domain knowledge, while those with more experience prioritized organizational-level barriers, such as insufficient priority from hospital management. Barriers related to team processes and patient outcome expectations were ranked low by both groups.ConclusionsThis study highlights the need for a multifaceted implementation strategy to effectively introduce personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients. Background

Sepsis, a life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to an infection [1], is currently treated with the use of antimicrobial agents targeting the pathogen, combined with intensive supportive care. Although the increasing adherence to clinical guidelines for sepsis, as promoted by the Surviving Sepsis Campaign [2, 3], has led to improved health outcomes for sepsis patients, it remains a prominent cause of mortality [4]. Recent literature suggests that sepsis may benefit from customized treatments based on the presenting phenotype of either hyperinflammation or immune paralysis [5–10]. Personalized immunotherapy, in which host-directed treatment is based on biomarker results from specific blood tests that require professionals interpretation to determine immune status, is emerging as a promising adjunctive therapy to improve patient outcomes [5–10].

The integration of a novel treatment, such as personalized immunotherapy, into routine sepsis care is a complex endeavor [11]. Several factors, for example those related to the healthcare professional (HCP) or the organization, may influence the (un)successful implementation of such a new treatment [12]. The inherent complexity of sepsis, including its rapid progression and variable presentation, may further complicate this process. Previous studies have shown that these factors commonly hinder the implementation of sepsis guidelines [13–16]. The introduction of a personalized treatment in sepsis care may pose additional challenges, as studies have indicated that HCPs' lack of familiarity with this approach and lack of knowledge to interpret biomarkers for individual immune profiling are important factors hindering implementation [17, 18].

Identifying such implementation challenges associated with novel treatments is essential for developing appropriate implementation strategies [12, 19, 20]. Since this is a time-consuming process, it is important to conduct such assessments at an early stage, alongside effectiveness studies [21–23]. This is particularly relevant in the context of sepsis, given the life-threatening nature of this condition [1, 4]. In this way, implementation delays can be minimized, thereby facilitating timely patient access to a potential new treatment, such as personalized immunotherapy, if ultimately recommended in clinical guidelines.

In a previous study, we qualitatively assessed factors influencing HCPs' decisions to prescribe personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients (Bos N, Swillens JEM, Vandeberg L, Janssen R, van de Veerdonk FL, Oerlemans AJM, et al. Exploring barriers and facilitators for the implementation of personalized immunotherapy for patients with severe infections: A qualitative study among healthcare professionals, under review). The findings indicated that HCPs were indeed concerned about the feasibility of implementing personalized treatments in practice due to the perceived complexity of personalized therapy for sepsis. However, many other factors were identified, the relative importance of which remains unclear. Identifying the most relevant influencing factors is essential to focus the development of implementation strategies aimed at promoting the uptake of personalized immunotherapy into routine sepsis practice [12, 19, 20]. Building on our previous findings, the current study aims to prioritize the most relevant hindering factors (barriers) for HCPs to consider personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients. Additionally, we describe the barriers’ prioritization by HCPs with either low or high levels of experience with immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases. This study was conducted during the ImmunoSep trial, a study on the effectiveness of personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients (See Table 1 [6]).

Methods

Design

This study used a cross-sectional survey design to prioritize the most relevant barriers for HCPs to consider personalized immunotherapy as an adjunctive treatment for sepsis. We used maximum difference scaling (MaxDiff), a method that simplifies ranking tasks for participants, to calculate the relative importance of barriers identified in previous interviews with HCPs (Bos N, Swillens JEM, Vandeberg L, Janssen R, van de Veerdonk FL, Oerlemans AJM, et al. Exploring barriers and facilitators for the implementation of personalized immunotherapy for patients with severe infections: A qualitative study among healthcare professionals, under review). The current study was preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to accessing the data (see https://osf.io/fjc67). This study is reported in accordance with guidelines for reporting observational studies (see Table A1 in Additional File 1 [24]).

Participants

We recruited hospital-based HCPs involved in the care of patients with infectious diseases from hospitals worldwide, with the focus on European countries. Experience with personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients was not a requirement for participation. Rather, the recruitment process was designed to include respondents with varying levels of experience with immunotherapy.

An open invitation was sent through scientific conferences, professional associations and project partners (ImmunoSep and HDM-FUN consortia). Our goal was to obtain a minimum of one hundred completed surveys, based on prior similar studies using MaxDiff [25–28] and consultation with a statistician. Given the exploratory nature of the research question, which did not aim to detect statistically significant differences between barriers or subgroups, a formal power calculation was not performed.

Survey development

We developed a survey in English, starting with information describing personalized immunotherapy as tailoring immunomodulatory treatment based on immune status using biomarkers. Our aim was to present the principle of personalized immunotherapy, but to provide respondents with a frame of reference, we illustrated this concept with examples from the ImmunoSep trial [6]: in this trial, patients with hyperinflammation, indicated by ferritin level, received anakinra to suppress their immune system and patients with immunoparalysis, indicated by human leukocyte antigen-DR (HLA-DR) level, received interferon-gamma to stimulate their immune system. This information was followed by a case description of a patient with a septic shock and organ failure admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU), with the aim to help HCPs in considering and prioritizing barriers in their decision-making process regarding personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients.

The barriers included in the survey were derived from our previous qualitative study, in which we identified 57 factors (i.e., barriers and facilitators) that influence HCPs' decisions to prescribe personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients (Bos N, Swillens JEM, Vandeberg L, Janssen R, van de Veerdonk FL, Oerlemans AJM, et al. Exploring barriers and facilitators for the implementation of personalized immunotherapy for patients with severe infections: A qualitative study among healthcare professionals, under review). To ensure consistency in the survey, facilitators were reframed as barriers, and these barriers were then rephrased into statements (e.g., the facilitator ‘Decision-making in multidisciplinary meetings’ was reversed into the barrier ‘insufficient multidisciplinary meetings to facilitate decision-making’ and rephrased as ‘In my hospital, there are not enough multidisciplinary meetings to facilitate decision-making). During this process, 28 factors were excluded based on the following criteria: 1) the factor was included in the case description since it is a perquisite for implementation (e.g., ‘the availability of the treatment’), 2) the reversed facilitator was already represented as a barrier (e.g., ‘belief that the treatment is effective’ vs. ‘concerns about the effectiveness of the treatment’), 3) there was insufficient distinction between two factors (e.g., ‘lack of robust evidence on the effectiveness’ and ‘previous trials on the effectiveness were negative’), or 4) the factor could not be transformed into a relevant statement for considering personalized immunotherapy that fits the case description (e.g., ‘willingness to transfer patients’).

The comprehensibility and interpretation of the treatment information, the case description and the remaining 29 statements were evaluated using cognitive interviewing [29], both in a group session with infectious disease researchers (n = 8), and in one-on-one sessions with infectious disease experts (n = 4) and professionals in training with relevant medical or research experience in infectious diseases (n = 4). The sessions included individuals with diverse mother tongues to evaluate the survey across linguistic backgrounds. Adjustments in the survey as a result of these sessions were discussed in the project team until consensus was reached. Although initially developed as a checklist in the chronic disease domain, it is presented as a comprehensive checklist with broad applicability across various health settings and practices, including infectious diseases [30–32].

Data collection

Sawtooth Software Lighthouse Studio (version 9.15.4) was used to create and conduct the survey. The static version of the survey can be accessed here: https://edu.nl/hbptt. After providing their consent to participate, participants were asked to complete the following characteristics: gender, age group, country, profession and years of clinical experience. Participants were also asked to rate their experience with immunotherapy for patients being treated for infectious diseases and the extent to which their hospital has experts available to consult about immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases, both on a scale from 1 to 10. Next, the participants were presented with the information on personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients and the case description. They were then instructed to complete the Maxdiff questions, selecting the statement they found most applicable in each set. The 29 barriers were presented in 22 sets of four statements, while following these principles: 1) item frequency balance: each item is shown approximately equally often (at least three times per participant), 2) orthogonality: two items appear together in a set an equal number of times, 3) connectivity: all items are directly or indirectly connected to each other, ensuring that every item can be compared with every other item across the sets, 4) within-set positional balance: each item occurs equally often at each position within a set, and 5) across-set positional balance: the order of sets is varied for each participant [33]. After completing the Maxdiff questions, participants were asked to identify any additional barriers they considered important and had not been included in the survey.

Data analysis

To determine the most relevant barriers, we retrieved the mean relative importance score (RIS) and overall rank of each barrier from Sawtooth Software Lighthouse Studio 9.15.4. In this software, importance scores were estimated per barrier per participant by combining individuals’ specific choices with the choices of other participants, using Hierarchical Bayes (HB) estimation [34]. These importance scores were then converted to a mean RIS for each barrier on a scale of 0 to 100, where a higher mean RIS indicates a more important barrier. This means that a barrier with a mean RIS of 4 is twice as important as one with a RIS of 2. If all barriers were equally important, each would have a RIS of 3.4 (i.e., 100 divided by the total number of barriers, [28]). The mean RIS of each barrier determined the overall ranking of the barriers.

Results

Participants

A total of 280 participants started the survey by selecting the option that indicated their consent to participate. Of these, 228 (81.4%) started with the MaxDiff statements, of which 174 (76.3%) participants completed the survey and were included in the data analysis. Table 3 shows the characteristics of the participants. The majority (72.4%) were intensivists, and some participants (19.0%) selected multiple professions. Participants reported varying levels of experience with immunotherapy for severe infectious diseases, with approximately half of the respondents (58.6%) rating their experience as 5 or lower on a 10-point scale. The highest percentage (14.4%) of participants rated their experience as a 1 (no experience with immunotherapy for infectious diseases), and the lowest percentage rated it at the highest levels, with 4.6% selecting a 9, and 5.7% selecting a 10 (specialized in immunotherapy for infectious diseases). Regarding the access to consulting experts in their hospitals, participants reported a wide range of availability, with the highest percentages at the extremes of the 10-point scale. Specifically, 19.0% rated their access to immunotherapy expert consultation as 1 (no experts to consult), and 19.5% rated it as 10 (easy access to an expert to consult).

Prioritized barriers

The rankings and mean RIS of the barriers are displayed in Fig. 1. Twelve barriers were identified as relatively most important, given their mean RIS > 3.5. These barriers were associated with four domains of the TICD checklist [11]. The highest ranked barriers occurred within the clinical innovation domain, including the subdomain clarity of the clinical intervention (items 1.5 and 1.4, ranked 1st and 4th), availability of scientific evidence (items 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, ranked 2nd, 3rd and 6th), and feasibility of the clinical intervention (item 1.7, ranked 9th), respectively. The barriers ranked next occurred in the individual HCP domain, including the subdomain awareness and familiarity with the clinical intervention (item 2.4, ranked 5th) and existing domain knowledge (item 2.1, ranked 8th). Next, a barrier in the incentives and resources domain involved the subdomain availability of necessary resources (item 4.1, ranked 7th). The final three barriers in the top twelve ranking were part of the capacity for organizational change domain, from the subdomains priority of necessary change (item 5.3, ranked 10th) and the relative strength of supporters and opponent (items 5.1 and 5.2, ranked 11th and 12th). Participants did not identify additional barriers beyond those included in the survey.

Prioritized barriers of HCPs with low and high levels of experience

Table A2 in Additional File 2 lists the barriers according to the ranking of the total group, along with the ranking and mean RIS for the two subgroups with low (score 1–5, N = 102) versus high (score 6–10, N = 72) levels of experience in immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases. Both groups prioritized barriers related to the subdomains of scientific evidence (items 1.1–1.3) and the clarity of the clinical intervention (items 1.4 and 1.5), with all five items appearing within the top six. However, two barriers within the domain of the individual HCP (items 2.4 and 2.1, regarding the subdomain awareness and familiarity with the clinical intervention) were relatively salient in the low experienced group, ranked 2nd and 7th, with the high experienced group ranked 7th and 15th. Conversely, participants with more experience prioritized barriers within the domain of capacity for organizational change (items 5.2, 5.3, 5.1, regarding the subdomains priority of necessary change and the relative strength of supporters and opponent) as relatively important (i.e., within the top 12), whereas those with less experience did not (i.e., ranked 12th, 13th and 15th).

Discussion

This study provides insight into the barriers that an implementation strategy should take into account to promote personalized immunotherapy for patients with sepsis. The highest-ranked barriers were mainly within the clinical innovation domain. Specifically, insufficient current evidence and a lack of a treatment protocol were identified as most relevant by both HCPs with low and high levels of experience with immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases. This shows that robust evidence and a clear protocol are crucial for HCPs to consider personalized immunotherapy in clinical sepsis practice, regardless of their own experience with the treatment. It is therefore important to first enhance the availability and awareness of scientific evidence regarding personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients, subsequently allowing to protocolize this treatment. At this point in time, the first step toward building evidence for personalized immunotherapy in sepsis has been taken, as the clinical trial conducted concurrently with this implementation study reported positive results (See Table 1; [6]).

Once sufficient evidence has been gathered to support the inclusion of personalized immunotherapy in sepsis guidelines, the other relevant barriers identified in this study should be addressed. Examples of potentially effective strategies are given below. In general, when developing strategies, it is important to select implementation strategies that are well aligned with local barriers. Various matching tools that link contextual barriers to implementation strategies can guide this process [35–39]. In practice, however, the relationship between determinants and strategies is not always consistent, as implementation takes place in dynamic contexts. To increase the consistency and transparency of complex strategies and their outcomes, strategy developers are advised to use the Implementation Research Logic Model (IRLM; [40]). The IRLM offers a structured way to make these relationships explicit by linking determinants, strategies, mechanisms of action, and implementation outcomes within a coherent and testable framework. The IRLM can also be used when executing, reporting on and synthesizing implementation strategies.

The lack of clarity regarding the optimal time to start the treatment and the perceived complexity of the patient eligibility process are two important barriers in the clinical innovation domain. These barriers could be addressed by conducting further research or expert opinion. The resulting recommendations regarding optimal treatment initiation and patient eligibility criteria should subsequently be included in the established protocol. Developing supporting materials, such as decision support tools, could further simplify the patient eligibility process [35, 36]. Additionally, the lack of timely availability of biomarker results, an important barrier within the incentives and resources domain, should be addressed. It is important to first determine the required speed of biomarker results to guide the application of personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients based on further research or expert opinion. In cases where biomarker results are delayed, the factors contributing to the delay should be identified, including whether these factors are related to the biomarker itself or to organizational processes. This assessment will help guide the selection of appropriate biomarkers based on required turnaround time or development of solutions to expedite the biomarker availability [12].

Other relevant barriers were insufficient knowledge of immunology and immunomodulation, and experience with personalized immunotherapy. Not surprisingly, these two barriers within the domain of the individual HCP were particularly relevant among HCPs with low levels of experience with immunotherapy. Developing educational materials and offering training on these areas would be effective strategies to address these barriers in this particular group [35, 36]. The HCPs with higher levels of experience could contribute to the development of these materials and provide support in training activities.

Three barriers within the domain of capacity for organizational change were identified at the bottom of the top twelve. Notably, these barriers were ranked higher by HCPs with more experience with immunotherapy for patients with infectious diseases. This may indicate that as less-experienced HCPs become more familiar with the treatment, they may also encounter similar organization-related barriers. In order to enhance the level of support and priority for personalized immunotherapy for sepsis within the ICU and at the hospital management level, it is important to start involving a diverse range of stakeholders in the hospital, including the management, at an early stage in the implementation process. These stakeholders can be engaged, for example, in consensus meetings on the impact of personalized immunotherapy for sepsis. Stakeholder engagement, in particular the hospital management, is essential to build support for novel evidence-based treatments [35, 36], which has also been shown to be important in the implementation of sepsis guidelines [14, 15, 41, 42].

It is also noteworthy that barriers related to the HCPs’ expectations of patient outcomes were ranked relatively low. Although these factors were identified as barriers in our prior qualitative study (Bos N, Swillens JEM, Vandeberg L, Janssen R, van de Veerdonk FL, Oerlemans AJM, et al. Exploring barriers and facilitators for the implementation of personalized immunotherapy for patients with severe infections: A qualitative study among healthcare professionals, under review), they are currently not perceived as highly relevant. This can be cautiously considered a promising indication, suggesting that the HCPs in this study had a relatively positive attitude toward the outcomes for sepsis patients treated with personalized immunotherapy. Additionally, barriers related to team processes were also ranked relatively low. However, these barriers may become more relevant at a later stage. It is reasonable to assume that once the most relevant current barriers have been addressed, or when HCPs begin to use the treatment in practice, they may encounter other barriers, such as those related to team processes. Poor teamwork and communication, and changes in team composition are frequently reported barriers for the implementation of sepsis guidelines [14–16, 41, 43, 44]. It would therefore be useful to re-evaluate barriers once the existing barriers have been addressed and the treatment becomes more mainstream in clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

A strength of this study is that we prioritized barriers at an early stage, enabling the development of strategies to address them and thereby contributing to the acceleration of the implementation once the treatment will be introduced into practice [21–23]. However, a limitation of this early stage setting may be the use of hypothetical scenarios, where participants were asked to assume availability and reimbursement of biomarkers and medication for personalized immunotherapy for sepsis. The consequence of this choice in operationalization may be that these prerequisites for implementation may lack once this treatment is introduced in practice. Our recruitment strategy also has both strengths and limitations. We used open invitations sent through diverse scientific channels. This allowed us to reach a broad range of HCPs with varying levels of experience with immunotherapy, providing a wide perspective on the identified barriers and enabling the development of implementation strategies that can be tailored to the different levels of treatment experience. However, this strategy may have introduced selection bias, potentially targeting participants with certain interests related to immunotherapy, limiting the diversity of perspectives. Additionally, our focus on European countries resulted in a higher representation from this region. Consequently, our findings may not be representative of other regions, where barriers regarding personalized immunotherapy for sepsis may differ. Taken together, these strengths and limitations indicate that our findings provide an initial, broad exploration of barriers to consider personalized immunotherapy for sepsis. It is important to re-evaluate these factors prior to actual implementation, including the actual conditions that apply in the specific context of interest (e.g., country, hospital, facilities) at that point in time for those involved (e.g., medical profession, experience level). The survey structure and statements used in this study could serve as a valuable point of reference in this process.

Another strength is of this study the use of MaxDiff to rank barriers. This method provides a clear understanding of which barriers HCPs perceive as most relevant when considering personalized immunotherapy for a patient with sepsis. However, our operationalization of the MaxDiff methodology also has some limitations. For instance, participants were only asked to indicate the most important barrier for each set, rather than the most and least relevant barriers. Consequently, the results of the lower-rated barriers are less accurate. This methodological choice was made because our primary aim was to identify the most relevant barriers, but also to reduce the cognitive burden on participants (from making two choices to one choice for each set). Additionally, a few participants reported that they found the MaxDiff questions challenging to respond to, citing a few instances where they did not perceive any barriers presented in the sets of four statements. This is an inherent aspect of the MaxDiff methodology, which forces participants to prioritize and select the most relevant option, even when some items may seem less applicable.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that effective implementation of personalized immunotherapy for sepsis patients requires a multifaceted implementation strategy. The first priority should be to increase the availability and awareness of robust scientific evidence, followed by the development of a clear protocol, including explicit patient eligibility criteria. Some important aspects, such as the optimal treatment timing and the required speed of biomarker results, require further assessment to be able to develop an appropriate strategy. Given that HCPs with different levels of experience with immunotherapy prioritize barriers differently, some strategies should be tailored to meet these diverse needs. For example, strategies for less experienced HCPs should focus on enhancing their domain knowledge and familiarity with the treatment, and thus educational programs are crucial for the implementation of personalized immunotherapy. In addition, early engagement and support from the ICU and hospital management are essential for successful implementation. Finally, once personalized immunotherapy will be introduced in clinical sepsis practice, re-evaluation of barriers while taking the specific context into account would be useful to ensure optimally effective implementation of this promising treatment into sepsis care.
