Complete Heart Block Related to Bicuspid Aortic Valve Calcification in a Triathlete
Authors: Abhishek Chandra, Michael C Downey, Matthew D Olson, Kevin M Harris
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile athletic patients may exhibit bradycardia, significant exertional symptoms in the setting of a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) warrant further investigation.Case SummaryA 36-year-old male triathlete presented with progressive exercise intolerance, fatigue, and bradycardia. Electrocardiogram demonstrated complete atrioventricular block (junctional ventricular escape: 52 beats/min). Echocardiography revealed a moderately calcified BAV with mild to moderate stenosis and preserved ventricular function. Cardiac imaging demonstrated a Sievers type 0 valve with asymmetric extravalvular calcification extending to the atrioventricular node and perimembranous septum near the penetrating His bundle, providing a mechanistic explanation for conduction block. A dual-chamber left bundle branch pacemaker was implanted, and the patient shortly returned to baseline performance.DiscussionThis athlete's case highlights a rare presentation of complete heart block secondary to BAV-associated calcification, demonstrating its conduction implications.Take-Home MessageBradycardia and decreased exercise tolerance in athletes warrant thorough evaluation for rare structural causes of conduction disease.Key words: arrhythmia, bicuspid aortic valve, congenital heart disease, endurance athlete

History of Presentation

A 36-year-old male ultramarathoner and Ironman triathlete presented with months of progressive exercise intolerance characterized by early fatigue, “heavy legs,” and needing to walk after 2 miles. He denied chest pain, dyspnea, or syncope, but reported occasional palpitations and exertional lightheadedness. His smartwatch documented a rise in nocturnal resting heart rate to over 100 beats/min from a baseline of 45 to 55 beats/min.

He had no significant medical history, no family history of cardiovascular disease, no significant supplement use, and no tobacco or illicit substance use. He consumed alcohol socially. Physical examination revealed normal perfusion, bradycardia, and a soft systolic ejection murmur at the right upper sternal border.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for exercise intolerance and bradyarrhythmia in an athlete includes physiologic sinus bradycardia with junctional escape, myocarditis, Lyme carditis, infiltrative cardiomyopathies, genetic and/or structural heart disease (eg, lamin A/C gene mutations). Given evidence of atypical calcification in the setting of Sievers type 0 bicuspid aortic valve (BAV) extending into the region of the penetrating His bundle, mechanical disruption of native conduction was also considered.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a mildly enlarged left ventricle with normal systolic function (ejection fraction: 62%), a moderately enlarged right ventricle with preserved function, and normal atrial sizes. The aortic valve was bicuspid, Sievers type 0 (anterior-posterior phenotype), with mild to moderate stenosis (peak velocity: 3.0 m/s, mean gradient: 20 mm Hg, valve area: 1.65 cm2) with significant calcification and trivial regurgitation. The sinus of Valsalva measured 4.3 cm (Figure 1).

Computed tomography angiography revealed moderate aortic root enlargement (44 mm) and confirmed a Sievers type 0 BAV with heavy calcification adjacent to the membranous septum, near the expected region of the atrioventricular (AV) node (Figure 2). No coarctation or coronary artery disease was seen.

Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging showed no late gadolinium enhancement, edema, or features of myocarditis or infiltrative disease. The left ventricle was moderately increased in volume with an ejection fraction of 54%, estimated end-diastolic volume of 259 mL, and a right ventricular ejection fraction of 53%. Exercise stress testing confirmed complete heart block (CHB) with acceleration of the junctional escape, yet persistent AV dissociation despite the atrial rate exceeding the ventricular rate (Figure 3). Thyroid function and Lyme serologies were normal.

The patient was admitted for further evaluation. Given the calcification overlying the conduction system and persistent, symptomatic CHB with chronotropic incompetence, a dual-chamber pacemaker was indicated. A formal electrophysiological study was not pursued at the time of device implantation, as initial atrial and ventricular electrograms from the leads prior to anchoring (and response to various device-programmed pacing) confirmed CHB and would not have changed clinical management.

A dual-chamber device was placed, with successful conduction system pacing. The atrial lead was positioned at the right atrial septum, and the right ventricular lead was placed intraseptally, demonstrating successful selective left bundle branch pacing (paced QRS duration: 115 ms, qR morphology in V1, V6 R-wave peak time: 72 ms, V6-to-V1 interpeak difference: 49 ms). Postimplant device interrogation showed normal function, with atrial pacing at 45% and ventricular pacing at 99% (Figure 4). There were no atrial or ventricular tachyarrhythmias detected. The patient's symptoms improved immediately after implantation.

Given his BAV and degree of aortic stenosis and dilation, the patient was counseled that endurance activity was reasonable and was advised to avoid high-intensity isometric exercise, maintain a blood pressure below 130/80 mm Hg, monitor symptoms, and complete outpatient genetic screening and annual surveillance imaging.1 Screening echocardiography for first-degree relatives was recommended.

Discussion

This case illustrates an unusual mechanism of CHB in a young endurance athlete with a Sievers type 0 BAV. While conduction abnormalities in BAV disease are uncommon, calcium extension from the annulus into the adjacent membranous septum can disrupt the AV node or His bundle.2,3 In this patient, the asymmetric calcium deposition seen on computed tomography was likely a manifestation of altered flow dynamics inherent to the anterior-posterior commissural orientation of the type 0 valve (explaining why more common morphotypes of BAV with earlier calcifications do not have the same impingement on their conduction system, a theoretical cause of this patient's unique calcification pattern; see Figure 5). Furthermore, with aortic root dilation, there is anterior displacement of the aortic annulus, which moves the valvular apparatus closer to the penetrating His bundle, as the bundle dives posteriorly toward the membranous septum.

Aortic Valve Commissure Displacement in the Current Patient

Side-by-side comparison of trileaflet aortic valve commissures to the current patient with Sievers type 0 bicuspid aortic valve. Note the rotational displacement (overlaid in third panel) of the commissural closures in the setting of differential leaflet morphology. As such, the type 0 (anterior-posterior) configuration has a commissural plane more anterior and leftward than trileaflet morphology, placing the focus of shear stress (and thus calcification) more directly adjacent to the conduction system in a type 0 configuration.

The association between bicuspid morphology, calcium distribution, aortic root dilation, and conduction system involvement has been rarely reported.3, 4, 5 Most cases of AV block in young adults are due to inflammatory or infiltrative causes, such as sarcoidosis or myocarditis.6 In the present case, the absence of fibrosis or enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging supported a localized structural mechanism rather than a diffuse myocardial process.

Although athletic remodeling commonly produces sinus bradycardia and even junctional rhythms with high vagal tone, distinctions must be made when evaluating athletes with conduction disturbances.6,7 The patient's persistent CHB and inadequate chronotropic response during exercise indicated pathologic conduction disease and was not attributable to “athlete's heart.”1,2,4,6,7

After developing CHB, the patient's resting ventricular rate was approximately 50 beats/min while awake, but it was over 100 beats/min during sleep. This results from nocturnal variations in autonomic tone. Non-REM (rapid eye movement) sleep is dominated by parasympathetic activity, causing vasodilation. This triggers sympathetic catecholamine release, with the reflex tachycardia suppressed by high vagal output acting on the sinoatrial and AV nodes.8 In CHB with an infranodal escape rhythm, vagal tone cannot slow the escape focus, allowing unrestricted stimulation of ectopic foci by catecholamines during sleep-induced vasodilation.8 Higher autonomic vascular tone while awake suppresses this sympathetic surge, leading to slower escape rates.

Special considerations are required for device selection and programming for permanent pacing of athletes with CHB. Strategies that reduce the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy include traditional therapy with right and left ventricular leads. For our patient, with an anticipated high burden of ventricular pacing, conduction system pacing was selected. For device programing in the athlete with CHB, permissive bradycardia via reductions in lower rate limits permits native atrial activity and reduces unnecessary atrial pacing (promoting battery longevity), but it can allow for the emergence of ventricular escape rhythms. As our patient's ventricular escape rhythm was near 50 beats/min, the device was programmed DDD-R with a lower rate limit of 50 beats/min to help suppress his underlying ventricular escape rhythm. Lower rate settings, or an atrial-sensed mode without atrial pacing, could result in reemergence of his escape rhythm, thereby suppressing ventricular pacing and leading to a loss of AV synchrony. This would functionally return the patient to CHB. Theoretically, loss of AV synchrony in this setting would result in a decrease in cardiac output, increasing sympathetic tone and accelerating the sinus rate above the ventricular escape rate. Thereby, ventricular pacing would resume and restore AV synchrony. As cardiac output then improves, sympathetic tone decreases, allowing the sinus rate to fall below the escape rate, resulting in a recurrent cycle of lost and restored AV synchrony. In athletes, after return to baseline level of athletic exertion, treadmill-guided device reprogramming is the clinical gold standard to ensure optimal chronotropic support while minimizing undesirable upper-rate device behaviors.

The Sievers type 0 anterior-posterior BAV has 2 cusps without a raphe and is the rarest form of BAV (2.6%) when compared with incidences of type 0 lateral (4%), type 2 (5%), and the rarest type 1, noncoronary to left cusp fusion (3%).1,6 This configuration can alter transvalvular flow and shear stress patterns, leading to eccentric calcification.3,4 When calcium deposits form adjacent to the membranous septum, the proximity to the AV node and His bundle poses risk for conduction disturbance.1,2,6,7,9 This case provides visual evidence supporting that anatomic relationship. The degree of calcification was significant for our patient's age (36 years); it is unknown if oxidative stress or other mechanisms invoked by endurance training contributed, similar to the paradoxical manner they contribute to coronary calcification in endurance athletes.9,10

To our knowledge, few other reports have described CHB directly attributed to localized calcium extension from a BAV in an athlete. The case underscores the importance of multimodality imaging in defining structural mechanisms of conduction disease.

Follow-Up

At the 6-week follow-up, the patient remained well and asymptomatic, had normal pacemaker function, and reported resuming high-intensity endurance activity without limitation. Long-term monitoring was established for aortic valve and aorta monitoring, with routine device surveillance.

Conclusions

Localized calcification from a BAV can rarely extend into the AV conduction system, leading to CHB even in young, structurally normal hearts. Recognition requires integration of clinical presentation, advanced imaging, and electrophysiologic findings. This case emphasizes the interplay between congenital valve morphology, conduction anatomy, and the value of cardiac imaging when conduction abnormalities arise in patients with BAVs.
