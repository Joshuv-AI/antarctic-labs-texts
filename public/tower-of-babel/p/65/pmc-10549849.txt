Mini‐open Pedicle Subtraction Osteotomy versus Standard Posterior Approach for Ankylosing Spondylitis‐related Spinal Kyphosis: A Comparative Study
Authors: Gao Si, Weipeng Qiu, Qixian Shen, Yongqiang Wang, Weishi Li, Miao Yu, Yu Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveSurgical strategy for spinal kyphosis in patients with ankylosing spondylitis (AS) has been challenging. Pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) through a minimally invasive (MI) approach has been developed with promising clinical outcomes. We aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of PSO via an MI approach and a standard posterior approach (SPA) for treating AS‐related spinal kyphosis.MethodsA total of 41 patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis who underwent PSO through an MI approach (MI surgery [MIS] group: n = 25) or SPA (SPA group: n = 16) between January 2015 and July 2020 were retrospectively included. Spinopelvic parameters were evaluated before the surgery, immediately after the surgery, and at the 2‐year follow‐up. Clinical data including operative time, estimated blood loss, blood transfusion, level of fusion, incision length, bed rest period, length of hospitalization, and surgical complications were compared between the two groups. The Scoliosis Research Society outcomes instrument‐22 (SRS‐22) was administered to assess patients' quality of life at the latest follow‐up. Comparisons between the two groups were performed using independent sample t‐test or Chi‐square test.ResultsCharacteristics and baseline kyphosis of the two groups were matched. At the 2‐year follow‐up, in the MIS group, the average correction values of the sagittal vertical axis and global kyphosis (GK) were 9.5 cm and 44.3°, respectively. Compared with the SPA group, the MIS group had similar correction values and correction losses after surgery. No obvious differences were observed in any radiographic parameters, except for GK, immediately after surgery and at the 2‐year follow‐up between the two groups (p > 0.05). The MIS group had a significantly shorter operative time, lesser blood loss, lesser transfusion volume, shorter fusion level, and lesser time to mobilization than did the SPA group. Higher average functional activity scores of SRS‐22 were obtained in the MIS group than in the SPA group.ConclusionMini‐open PSO may be an effective alternative to the SPA for treating AS‐related spinal kyphosis, with comparable correction effect, lesser surgical trauma and faster recovery. This comparative study may provide valuable guidance for surgical decision‐making and patient counseling.Keywords: Ankylosing Sspondylitis, Kyphosis, Minimally invasive surgery, Osteotomy

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic, progressive immune‐mediated inflammatory disease 1 , 2 , 3 predominantly affecting the axial skeleton, which may lead to severe thoracolumbar kyphotic deformities. 4 The prevalence of AS in the adult population is between 0.9% and 1.4%, with male predominance. 1 , 5 AS‐related spinal kyphosis is a debilitating condition that results in sagittal spinopelvic imbalance and impaired horizontal gaze 6 and affects more than 30% of patients with AS, thereby placing a large burden on their families. 7 , 8 , 9 The best treatment for AS‐related spinal kyphosis is corrective osteotomy, the most common types of which are pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO), Smith–Peterson osteotomy, and vertebral column decancellation. 1 , 3 , 7 , 10

With long incisions and operative times and substantial soft tissue retraction and blood loss, PSO performed via the standard posterior approach (SPA) is technically challenging owing to a high risk of intraoperative and postoperative complications. 7 , 11 In recent years, mini‐open PSO through a minimally invasive (MI) approach has been developed to reduce major complications of spinal surgery while maintaining operative goals similar to those of open surgery. 11 , 12 , 13 In a previous study, as an innovative surgical strategy, we performed one‐level PSO in patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis using a MI approach that improved the operating procedure and utilized a modified operating table. 7 The preliminary clinical results of mini‐open PSO were superior to those of traditional PSO in terms of cosmetic outcomes. However, as the entire spine in patients with AS is rigid with restricted spinal flexibility due to heterotopic ossification of the facet joints and paravertebral ligaments, performing PSO via a mini‐open incision is technically challenging. Additionally, the compensatory ability of the unfused segment is insufficient in patients with AS, which distinguishes their condition from other types of kyphosis. Inadequate correction or mechanical complications after PSO can lead to sagittal reconstruction failure and require reoperation. 14 Therefore, the effectiveness and applicability of PSO through a MI approach for AS‐related spinal kyphosis require further investigation.

Our study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of PSO via the MI approach and the SPA in treating patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis, and to identify the effectiveness and applicability of mini‐open PSO for AS‐related spinal kyphosis.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective controlled study included 41 patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis who underwent one‐level PSO at two hospitals between January 2015 and July 2020. This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (2021‐463). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosis of AS based on the Modified New York criteria (1984); 15 , 16 (ii) kyphosis in the thoracolumbar spine; and (iii) a minimum follow‐up duration of 2 years, with complete clinical records and radiographic follow‐up. The exclusion criteria were a history of spinal fracture or former spinal surgery.

A total of 41 patients who fulfilled the above criteria were recruited and divided into two groups based on surgical approaches. In our study, the indication for PSO through the MI approach was a required kyphosis correction of ≤60°. The numbers of patients in the MI surgery (MIS) and SPA groups were 25 and 16, respectively.

Surgical Technique

The entire MIS procedure was performed through a mini‐open incision measuring approximately 10 cm. L2 was usually selected as the osteotomy site. After positioning the patient, a midline skin incision was made from L1 to L3. The paraspinal muscles were detached to expose bilateral laminae. Four multiaxial and long‐arm pedicle screws were inserted at the L1 and L3 levels, and their positions were checked using fluoroscopy. If all screws appeared to be well placed, V‐shaped PSO was performed at L2. Next, two short rods were instrumented and locked onto the pedicle screws to prevent vertebral subluxation. After the PSO was performed, the nuts at the L1 level were loosened, and the modified operating table was gradually manipulated to correct the kyphotic deformity. The height of each support of the modified operating table could be adjusted separately to fit the ideal corrective spinal alignment of each patient. When the osteotomy was completely closed, the nuts were tightened. Consequently, the back of the spine was shortened, and surgical manipulation at T12 and L4 could be achieved through the existing incision since they were closer to the osteotomy site. Four pedicle screws were inserted at T12 and L4. The positions of all screws were checked using fluoroscopy. The existing short rods were replaced with two long rods (diameter 6.0 mm), and all nuts were locked. A drainage tube was placed, and the incision was closed. The somatosensory and motor‐evoked potentials were monitored throughout the procedure (Figure 1).

Four pedicle screws are instrumented at the levels of L1 and L3 before osteotomy (C). V‐shaped pedicle subtraction osteotomy (PSO) is performed at L2 (D). The osteotomy is closed, and four screws are inserted at the levels of T12 and L4 with two long rods instrumented (E, F).

In the SPA, an incision of an appropriate length was made along the midline. The posterior elements of the spine were exposed subperiosteally to the transverse processes. Pedicle screws were inserted at two or more adjacent segments above and below the osteotomy site. Fluoroscopy was performed to confirm the positions of the screws. After laminectomy and facetectomy, the pedicles and cancellous bone were removed from the osteotomy site. The operating table was gradually adjusted to a neutral position. The rods were then inserted using the cantilever technique. Instrumental compression was applied to close the osteotomy site. After kyphosis correction, the final rods were instrumented, and the nuts were tightened. Somatosensory and motor‐evoked potentials were monitored throughout the surgery.

Radiographic and Clinical Assessment

Posteroanterior and lateral whole‐spine standing X‐rays were taken before the surgery, immediately after the surgery, and at the 2‐year follow‐up. All patients were classified into four AS‐related kyphosis subtypes according to the location of the apex, as described in a previous study: 17 Type I (lumbar); Type II (thoracolumbar); Type III (thoracic); and Type IV (cervical or cervicothoracic junction).

The spinopelvic parameters were measured by two experienced orthopedic surgeons independently. The intraclass correlation coefficient for inter‐rater reliability of all the parameters were >0.9. Measured parameters included thoracic kyphosis (TK, the angle between the upper endplate of T5 and lower endplate of T12), lumbar lordosis (LL, the angle between the upper endplate of L1 and the sacral plate), thoracolumbar kyphosis (TLK, the angle between the upper endplate of T10 and lower endplate of L2), sacral slope (SS, the angle between the horizontal and the sacral plate), pelvic incidence (PI, the angle between a perpendicular from the midpoint of the upper endplate of S1 and a line connecting the center of the femoral head to the center of the upper endplate of S1), pelvic tilt (PT, the angle between the vertical and the line through the midpoint of the sacral plate to the femoral heads axis), proximal junctional angle (the angle between the lower endplate of the upper instrumented vertebra [UIV] and upper endplate of the UIV +2), sagittal vertical axis (SVA, the distance between the plumb lines dropped from the center of the C7 vertebral body and the posterior‐superior aspect of the S1 vertebral body), and global kyphosis 2 (GK, the angle between the superior endplate of the maximally tilted upper end vertebra and the inferior endplate of the maximally tilted lower end vertebra).

Clinical and surgical data such as operative time, estimated blood loss, blood transfusion, level of fusion, incision length, bed rest period, and length of hospitalization were recorded for all patients. Blood loss was estimated on the basis of aspirated blood and weighed sponges. Transfusion volume was determined by an anesthesiologist on the basis of each patient's intraoperative mean arterial pressure and blood loss. The Scoliosis Research Society outcomes instrument‐22 (SRS‐22) was used to assess health‐related quality of life at the latest follow‐up.

Statistical Analyses

The SPSS version 26.0 statistical software (IBM Corp. Armonk, NY, USA) was used for data analysis. Continuous variables of radiographic and surgical data were compared between the two groups using an independent‐sample t‐test. An analysis of variance was performed to assess differences in radiographic data between the two groups at three time points (before the surgery, immediately after the surgery, and at the 2‐year follow‐up). A chi‐squared analysis was performed to compare categorical data such as sex, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and surgical complications between the two groups. Statistical significance was set at a p value <0.05.

Results

We recruited 41 patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis, comprising 25 (two women; 23 men) who underwent MIS, with a median age of 41.1 ± 10.5 years, and 16 (three women; 13 men) who underwent SPA, with a median age of 39.4 ± 10.0 years. Type II kyphosis (n = 33) was the most common, followed by type I kyphosis (n = 5) and type III kyphosis (n = 3). The UIV was located in the lower thoracic spine for each patient. All osteotomy sites were located at the thoracolumbar junction based on the location of the apex and required correction of kyphosis. The two groups were matched for sex, age, BMI, and magnitude of preoperative kyphosis (Tables 1 and 2).

Radiographic Data

As shown in Table 2, PT, SS, LL, TLK, SVA, and GK significantly improved after surgery in both groups (all p < 0.05). At the 2‐year follow‐up, both groups maintained good correction, and no significant changes were observed in any of the aforementioned spinopelvic parameters. Compared with the SPA group, the MIS group had similar final correction values of SVA and GK at the 2‐year follow‐up. At the last follow‐up, in the MIS group, the average correction values of SVA and GK were 9.5 ± 6.0 cm and 44.3° ± 15.3°, respectively. Correction loss values of SVA and GK showed no significant differences between the two groups. In the MIS group, the average loss of SVA correction and GK correction were 1.0 ± 2.0 cm and 2.2° ± 8.6°, respectively. The two groups were matched for preoperative sagittal alignment. No significant differences were noted in PT, PI, SS, LL, TLK, TK, or SVA scores (p > 0.05) between the two groups postoperatively or at the 2‐year follow‐up; Significant differences were observed in GK between the two groups postoperatively and at the 2‐year follow‐up.

Clinical Outcomes

The surgical information of the two groups is presented in Table 3. A shorter average operative time (p < 0.001), lower average blood loss (p = 0.038), lower average transfusion volume (p < 0.001), shorter average fusion level (p < 0.001), shorter average length of incision (p < 0.001), and shorter average time to mobilization (p = 0.004) were observed in the MIS group than in the SPA group. The ICU admission rate of the MIS group was significantly lower than that of the SPA group. No significant differences were observed in the mean duration of hospitalization or complication rates between the two groups. The average functional activity scores of SRS‐22 were higher in the MIS group (p = 0.019) than in the SPA group, whereas pain, appearance, mental health, and satisfaction with the treatment were not significantly different between the two groups (Table 4).

Discussion

This study focused on the effectiveness and safety of one‐level mini‐open PSO compared with the conventional approach for AS‐related spinal kyphosis. We found that PSO through the MI approach could achieve comparable correction of kyphosis, lesser surgical trauma, faster recovery and better patient‐reported outcomes.

PSO, also known as closing wedge osteotomy, has been widely recognized as an effective method for correcting AS‐related spinal kyphosis. Corrections of 30° to 45° can be obtained by performing classical one‐level PSO. 18 , 19 , 20 Recently, PSO through a mini‐open approach has been developed with promising clinical outcomes. Although MIS techniques have been well described in many studies, most have not involved three‐column osteotomy. Voyadzis et al. 21 initially explored a new method for performing PSO using the MI approach in cadaver models. After continuous application in clinical practice and improvements, mini‐open PSO has been used to treat adult spinal deformities with improved sagittal alignment and reduced surgical trauma. 11 , 12 , 13 , 22 However, the effectiveness and applicability of PSO through a MI approach for AS‐related spinal kyphosis require further investigation.

Advantages of Mini‐open PSO for AS‐related Spinal Kyphosis

The advantages of MIS include a smaller incision, reduced blood loss, less soft tissue trauma, and earlier mobilization. Our comparative study showed that the patients in the MIS group had significantly lower operative blood loss, transfusion volume, and time to mobilization than did those in the SPA group. Mini‐open incisions allow more soft tissue to be preserved and reduce surgical trauma to facilitate early patient mobility. Additionally, open PSO for the correction of AS‐related spinal kyphosis tends to have a high rate of surgical complications, including wound infections. 23 Smith et al. 24 demonstrated that the MI approach may result in a lower rate of postoperative infections. In our study, three patients developed wound infection in the SPA group, whereas none in the MIS group developed wound infection. In open PSO, larger surgical incisions and longer wound exposure time may increase the risk of postoperative infections. Dural adhesion is a common phenomenon in AS surgery, and three patients in the MIS group had cerebrospinal fluid leakage. When the adhesions were severe, dural tears inevitably occurred despite using the MIS technique. Overall, no significant difference was observed in complication rates between the two groups, possibly because of the limited sample size of the two groups.

Sagittal imbalance in AS‐related spinal kyphosis is characterized by reduced lumbar lordosis, increased thoracic kyphosis, compensatory pelvic retroversion, and an anterior displacement of the SVA. 2 , 25 , 26 In the MIS group, the average PT, SS, LL, TLK, SVA, and GK improved immediately after surgery and at the 2‐year follow‐up. Moreover, the average corrections of SVA and GK at the 2‐year follow‐up were 9.5 cm and 44.3°, respectively, in the MIS group, which were similar to the values in the SPA group and those reported in previous studies. 18 , 27 , 28 Additionally, the pain, appearance, mental health, and satisfaction component scores of the SRS‐22 at the last follow‐up were not significantly different between the MIS and SPA groups. The SRS‐22 activity domain score in the MIS group was better than that in the SPA group, possibly because the SPA group had greater postoperative GK and higher levels of fusion. Thus, PSO through the MI approach can obtain a corrective effect comparable to that of the SPA (Figure 2).

The operation time was shorter in the MIS group than in the SPA group, presumably owing to a considerably shorter average fusion level of 4.1 segments. On the basis of a surgical incision length of approximately 10 cm used in the MIS group, the pedicle screws were usually instrumented at two segments above and below the osteotomy site. Generally, shorter fusions are not believed to improve the loss of spinal mobility due to spinal stiffness related to AS, and longer fusions are regarded as a method conducive to maintaining the correction. 29 , 30 However, a previous study demonstrated that both long‐ and short‐segment instrumentation can ensure maintenance of kyphosis correction. 27 In our study, no obvious correction loss were found in the MIS group, with an average fusion level of 4.1 segments during the follow‐up period. The patients in the MIS group maintained satisfactory correction. Hence, we assume that short‐segment instrumentation (two segments above and below the osteotomy site) is sufficient for AS‐related kyphosis with an ossified spine. Short‐segment instrumentation plays a substantial role in minimizing trauma.

Applicability of Mini‐open PSO for AS‐related Spinal Kyphosis

In our study, the indication for mini‐open PSO was that the required correction angle was less than 60°, for which global sagittal alignment could be significantly improved by one‐level PSO. If the kyphosis was severe and rigid, two‐level PSO was performed for better reconstruction, and the incision length could not be minimized in these cases.

For AS‐related kyphosis, the typical recommendation is that the osteotomy site is at or close to the kyphotic apex for successful sagittal reconstruction. 20 The patients in the MIS group mainly presented with lumbar kyphosis and an absence of obvious thoracic hyperkyphosis. Hence, the osteotomy points in the MIS group were mostly located in the lumbar region, predominantly at L2 and L3. This allowed for consideration of the correction of thoracolumbar kyphosis and reconstruction of the lumbar lordosis. Zhang et al. 31 demonstrated that PSO at L2/L3 achieved the greatest corrective success in patients with AS with lumbar kyphosis. We also assumed that PSO using the MI technique would be more suitable at the L2/L3 level. When the osteotomy is closed in MIS, the posterior part of the spine is shortened and more spinal segments can be reached through the existing incision. This allows for the entire surgical process to be performed through the mini‐open incision. When locating the osteotomy point at the lower thoracic region, owing to thoracic constraints, access to the proximal and distal fixation segments through the original small incision may be more restricted. This may affect surgery in the adjacent segments. Moreover, a level higher than L2 is located at the conus medullaris. Spinal osteotomy at the nonspinal cord regions is recommended to avoid the restriction of a bony thorax and the risk of a spinal cord injury.

Strengths and Limitations

Surgical strategy for AS‐related spinal kyphosis has always been a thorny challenge. This study further explored the differences in clinical outcomes between mini‐PSO and conventional open PSO in patients with AS‐related spinal kyphosis. Our study elaborated on the effectiveness and applicability of mini‐PSO, which might provide guidance for clinical practice. This study also had several limitations. First, this was a retrospective study; a higher level of evidence is needed to further verify the safety and efficacy of the mini‐open PSO. Second, although this was a multicenter study, which might have reduced selection bias, the sample size was small. Consequently, we were unable to perfectly match the two groups in terms of osteotomy site selection. Third, the follow‐up period was inadequate to evaluate the stability of this technique. Studies with a long‐term follow‐up period and a larger sample of patients who underwent PSO through a MI approach for treating AS‐related spinal kyphosis are needed to elucidate the occurrence of loss of correction and other complications.

Conclusions

PSO through the MI approach can achieve correction of kyphosis and maintenance of the correction comparable to those achieved with PSO through the SPA. With lesser surgical trauma and faster recovery, one‐level PSO through the MI approach may be an effective alternative to the SPA for treating AS‐related spinal kyphosis, especially for patients requiring correction of less than 60°. This comparative study may provide valuable guidance for surgical decision‐making and patient counseling.
