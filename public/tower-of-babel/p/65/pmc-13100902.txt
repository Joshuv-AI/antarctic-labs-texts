Pleiotropy and Facilitation of Local Adaptation in the Silverleaf Sunflower Helianthus argophyllus
Authors: Uzezi E Okinedo, Brook T Moyers, David Alvarez-Ponce
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLocal adaptation drives changes in population phenotypes that confer survival or reproductive success in specific environments. Local adaptation may be hindered or facilitated by pleiotropy, which is the control of multiple traits by a single genetic locus. In this study, we characterized the role of pleiotropy in local adaptation in the Texas endemic silverleaf sunflower, Helianthus argophyllus. Populations of H. argophyllus exhibit a bimodal life history strategy, consisting of tall, late-flowering forms and short, early-flowering forms that occur in close geographic proximity. The expression of life-history traits in H. argophyllus populations is linked to local adaptation and controlled by a highly pleiotropic locus. Still, we do not know how local adaptation and pleiotropy interact at the transcriptomic level. Here, we identify putatively locally adapted genes using whole RNA sequencing data and two selection outlier approaches. We assess transcriptomic pleiotropy by evaluating whether allelic variants within genes regulate the expression of other genes (an eQTL approach) and by assessing gene co-expression network connectivity. Candidate locally adapted genes show a modest enrichment for eQTL, which control the expression of more eGenes than non-candidate eQTL. Candidate locally adapted genes also exhibit significantly higher connectivity in gene co-expression networks, greater genetic differentiation and genetic diversity, and stronger selective sweep signals than the rest of the transcriptome. Gene co-expression networks enriched for candidate locally adapted genes show higher genetic differentiation and stronger signatures of soft selective sweeps than unenriched networks. Our results support a model of ecotypic divergence under gene flow and align with recent revisions of Fisher's geometric theory, wherein large-effect pleiotropic loci can facilitate local adaptation while polygenic soft-sweeps across functional gene networks fine-tune adaptive responses.Keywords: local adaptation, pleiotropy, H. argophyllus, trans-eQTL, gene co-expression network

Evolutionary theory predicts a “cost of complexity,” where evolutionary responses are less likely to involve genetic variation that affects many traits, because a response in one trait will change them all. We find the opposite pattern in gene expression during local adaptation in a sunflower species: the set of genes that have evidence for changing during local adaptation is enriched for genes that affect the expression of more, rather than fewer, other genes. This surprising result aligns with emerging evidence that sometimes evolutionary responses can be more efficient when they target multiple trait changes at the same time.

Introduction

Pleiotropy describes how a single genetic locus influences multiple phenotypic traits (Stearns 2010; Tenaillon 2014). Pleiotropy can arise in many forms, including shared regulatory elements that affect the expression of multiple genes or interactions among genes within regulatory networks (Wagner and Zhang 2011). The organization of such interactions could result from coordinated genetic regulation or shared biological role, but may also arise from features like shared genomic co-location (Kliebenstein 2011). One way we can assess these interactions is through gene co-expression networks, a kind of trait co-variance model where genes are nodes linked to each other by shared patterns of expression. Connections between genes can reveal pleiotropic influences of the expression of one gene on others (Wagner and Zhang 2011; Hämälä et al. 2020a). These networks are not inherently pleiotropic; rather, interaction network organization provides a structure in which pleiotropic effects can be expressed, concentrated within a coordinated program, or constrained from spreading across unrelated processes. Network associations can be used to generate hypotheses on how genetic variation drives coordinated gene expression responses to influence multiple downstream traits, including the expression of other genes. In this context, the position or connectivity of genes within networks can be used as an indirect proxy to assess its pleiotropy within a specific expression space: more central genes tend to covary with a larger set of transcripts and are more likely to be regulators with broader transcriptional influence than peripheral genes. Assessing how pleiotropy manifests within network organization is important for understanding how genetic changes translate into coordinated phenotypic evolution and how constraints or trade-offs shape evolutionary outcomes.

Fisher's geometric model explains how highly pleiotropic or large-effect loci may constrain adaptation by generating trade-offs that shift an organism from its fitness optimum (Fisher and Fisher 1930; Edwards 2000). Under this model, adaptation proceeds through small-effect, non-pleiotropic loci, which minimize detrimental consequences on an organism's fitness. This is consistent with some studies of the genetic architecture of species-wide adaptation (e.g. Nocchi et al. 2024). However, extensions of Fisher's theory propose that pleiotropic loci can accelerate adaptation, mainly when populations are far from their fitness optimum, such as during habitat expansion or environmental shifts or when experiencing maladaptive gene flow (Fisher and Fisher 1930; Tenaillon 2014). Experimental evidence supports this and shows that adaptive loci with larger effect sizes often correlate with increased distance from optimal fitness (Orr 1998; Hämälä et al. 2020a). Across deep time, plant families appear to repeatedly use orthologous, pleiotropic genes in adaptation to similar climatic environments (Whiting et al. 2024). In addition, recent studies show that divergent selection under gene flow facilitates the repeatability of large-effect, pleiotropic loci, leading to parallel genetic responses across populations (Rennison and Peichel 2022; Poore et al. 2023; Battlay et al. 2025 ). This suggests that when gene flow or migration is present, adaptation is less hindered by pleiotropic constraints (Battlay et al. 2024; Lai et al. 2025).

While some studies suggest that adaptation follows a polygenic model to minimize pleiotropic costs, others argue that pleiotropy drives correlated adaptive trait shifts (Smith 2016; Kinsler et al. 2020, 2024; Hämälä et al. 2020a). Few studies, however, directly test whether adaptive loci in natural diverging populations are broadly pleiotropic or smaller in scope. Two studies have found evidence for elevated pleiotropy in genes under selection during local adaptation. In common ragweed, Hämälä et al. (2020a) showed that loci under selection during range expansion exhibited pleiotropy, while in stickleback Rennison and Peichel (2022) showed that genomic regions under parallel ecotypic divergence contained more pleiotropic genes than non-parallel-divergent genomic regions. Deepening the evidence for these patterns across systems helps evaluate when selection may favor genetic architectures that minimize trade-offs versus those that integrate multiple adaptive changes through pleiotropy (Hansen 2003).

Helianthus argophyllus offers an ideal system for studying pleiotropy in adaptation, as the species includes clear ecotypic divergence with a known genetic basis. Endemic to Texas and parts of Florida and an annual in the Asteraceae family, it occupies sandy habitats and exhibits two striking ecotypes: a tall, late-flowering inland type and a short, early-flowering coastal type (Heiser 1951; Moyers and Rieseberg 2016). It shares morphological similarities with Helianthus annuus but is distinguishable by its dense, silvery-white pubescence on the leaves, stems, and phyllaries (Heiser et al. 1969; Gausman et al. 1977). A key feature of H. argophyllus is its bimodal life history strategy, which has led to the emergence of two distinct ecotypes: a tall, late-flowering ecotype with low branching found in drier inland Texas and a short, early-flowering, highly branched ecotype, distributed on barrier islands along the Gulf of Mexico (Moyers 2015; Moyers and Rieseberg 2016). Despite their narrow geographical range, these ecotypes coexist within the same populations but differ in flowering phenology (Moyers and Rieseberg 2016). The tall, late-flowering ecotype blooms from late August to early September and is the primary ecotype in inland populations. In contrast, the short, early-flowering ecotype blooms in June and is more common in coastal populations (Heiser 1951). This bimodal flowering strategy is controlled by HaFT1, a FLOWERING LOCUS T homolog introgressed from H. annuus. HaFT1 is in a 10Mb low-recombination region on chromosome 6 of H. annuus, and likely in the same position in H. argophyllus, although this chromosome has a reciprocal translocation with H. annuus chromosome 15. We will refer to this 10Mb region as the HaFT1 region throughout. The short, early-flowering ecotype is either homozygous or heterozygous for the presence of HaFT1, representing the ancestral allele introgressed from H. annuus. In contrast, the tall, late-flowering ecotype lacks HaFT1 entirely (homozygous for its absence, the derived allele original to H. argophyllus). The HaFT1 region is strongly associated with flowering time, which correlates with fitness-related traits such as seed size, seed mass, and plant height (Ziebell et al. 2013; Gnan et al. 2014; Moyers and Rieseberg 2016; Todesco et al. 2020; Osnato et al. 2022). Large haploblocks like the HaFT1 region can maintain clusters of adaptive alleles under gene flow by suppressing recombination of maladaptive alleles, thereby creating genomic regions with potential to link local adaptation with reproductive isolation (Todesco et al. 2020). Given that the HaFT1 region is a well-characterized pleiotropic locus and that ecotypic divergence in H. argophyllus involves multiple life-history traits (Moyers and Rieseberg 2016), we hypothesize that adaptive divergence in H. argophyllus might be driven by one or more pleiotropic regulatory hubs that maintain co-adapted alleles through reduced recombination, while their trans-regulatory effects across the genome might coordinate polygenic responses to selection.

In this study, we apply a transcriptomic and network-based approach to investigate how pleiotropy might facilitate local adaptation in H. argophyllus. Our goal is to determine whether putatively locally adaptive genes are more or less pleiotropic than non-adaptive genes using the proxy of gene co-expression networks. We sequenced the transcriptomes of individuals representing two divergent populations (inland, with only the tall, late-flowering ecotype, and coastal, with both ecotypes) and estimated genetic diversity indices. We identified candidate locally adapted loci using both environment-dependent and population structure-based models. We then assessed the pleiotropy of adaptive loci using two independent yet complementary approaches: (i) an eQTL framework that tested associations between genetic variants and the extent of their effect on gene expression, and (ii) gene co-expression network analysis to evaluate the centrality and connectivity of candidate locally adapted genes and determine whether adaptive variants cluster within networks annotated with specific biological processes. If adaptive loci are pleiotropic (a pleiotropic model), we expect them to include more eQTL that regulate more targets (eGenes) and display high network connectivity within gene co-expression networks. In contrast, adaptation might be driven by loci of narrower effect, depleted of eQTL or clustering within specific functional pathways, allowing for more independent evolution of traits.

Results

RNA Sequencing

We analyzed total RNA sequence data for 19 Helianthus argophyllus seedlings grown in a common environment from seed collected from two major sub-populations of its range in Texas (North inland: late flowering, n = 9, and coastal: early and late flowering, n = 10) to identify genomic footprints of local adaptation (Fig. S1). Hereafter, we refer to these populations as “north” and “coast”. A total of 6,779,134 SNPs and Indels were called from Freebayes (Garrison and Marth 2012). After filtering, we retained 113,626 biallelic SNPs in 16,622 genes.

Genetic Diversity and Population Structure

Transcriptome-wide nucleotide diversity (θπ) was 0.0014, and Watterson theta (θW) was 0.00088, indicating low levels of genetic diversity within H. argophyllus overall. Nucleotide diversity and Watterson's theta should be approximately equal in a population under equilibrium conditions (evolving neutrally with constant demographic size) (Cutter 2019). Differences between θπ and θW likely reflect changes in population size over time. For variable sites, Tajima's D was slightly negative (D = −0.13), possibly due to rare alleles or population expansion (Table S1). PCA (Fig. 1a) and FST (mean FST = 0.05) demonstrated genetic differentiation between the sampled sub-populations: PC1 (18.4 PVE) showed clear differentiation between north and coast samples. Two individuals (btm22 and btm25) showed high values along PC2 (13 PVE), potentially reflecting lower-level population differentiation in the central region of the species range or gene flow between the north and coast populations (Fig. 1a). Admixture analysis using ADMIXTURE suggests that the population is stratified into two distinct genetic groups at K = 2, representing the two sampled populations (Fig. 1b), with differences between the populations remaining at higher K values while the central samples in the range emerge as distinct.

Selection Scans

We used two methods to identify genes underlying the local adaptation of H. argophyllus: LFMM 2 and PCAdapt (Luu et al. 2017; Caye et al. 2019). LFMM 2 takes an environment-genotype association approach that combines population structure and environmental variables, whereas PCAdapt uses population structure information only. For our LFMM model, we controlled for population structure using K = 2 latent factors estimated from the scree plot of the genotype-based principal component analysis (PCA) (Fig. S2). We defined the principal axes of environmental variation using a PCA of 19 BIOCLIM variables. We used the first four environmental PCs, which explain 98.95% of the total variation (Fig. S2; Table S2). Out of all 113,626 biallelic SNPs, 4,497 distributed across 2,744 genes bore signatures of selection in the LFMM model (q < 0.05). BIOCLIM PC1 (78.1 PVE) was not associated with any SNP, while PC2 (14.2 PVE), PC3 (4.4 PVE), and PC4 (1.6 PVE) were associated with 3,706, 2,358, and 14 SNPs, respectively, with 1,581 SNPs associated with more than one PC. BIOCLIM PC2 largely represents precipitation (most strongly associated with BIO12, 18, and 19), PC3 is associated with precipitation (BIO18 and 19) and temperature (BIO3 and 9), and PC4 is aligned with latitude as well as precipitation (BIO13–16) (Fig. S3). In the PCAdapt analysis, 1,796 SNPs distributed across 1,115 genes were identified as selection outliers (q < 0.05). The two methods co-identified 435 SNPs in 315 genes, more overlap than expected by chance (P-value < 2.2 × 10−16, Fisher's exact test; Fig. 2a). Five outlier SNPs aligned within the HaFT1 region (Fig. 2b). For further analyses, we considered genes with outlier SNPs from either approach as candidates for local adaptation (“selection outliers” or “candidate adaptive genes”). Genes containing bi-allelic SNPs not identified as selection outliers (or as either eQTL or eGenes in the following section) make up our control comparison group.

Proportion of eQTL and eGenes in Selection Outliers

To investigate whether selection outlier loci were more pleiotropic than control genes, we tested associations between SNPs and gene expression to identify trans-regulatory variants that control the expression of other genes. Unlike cis-regulatory elements, trans-regulatory variants are identifiable from transcriptome sequence data; they influence many downstream targets, providing a broad measure of regulatory pleiotropy (Brynedal et al. 2017; Hämälä et al. 2020a; Josephs et al. 2020). We grew our plants in a common garden to minimize environmental variation so that expression differences and resulting eQTLs largely reflected genetic rather than environmental effects. Of the 30,809 genes in our expression dataset, 14,757 with variable sites and sufficient expression were used for the eQTL analysis (Fig. S4a). The model included the first two principal components of our expression data as cofactors to control for population structure (Fig. S5 to S6). A total of 24 (0.16%) eQTL genes (genes containing eQTL SNPs) were identified and associated with the expression of 13 (0.09%) genes (eGenes) (FDR < 0.05, Table S3; Fig. S4b). No gene was identified as both an eQTL and an eGene. Of the 2,744 LFMM outlier genes, 11 (0.40%) were eQTL, while 6 (0.53%) PCAdapt outliers were eQTL. One (0.036%) LFMM outlier gene and none of the PCAdapt outlier genes were eGenes. Compared to the rest of the transcriptome, both outlier gene sets harbored more eQTL (LFMM; P < 0.05, PCAdapt; P < 0.05, Fisher's exact test; Fig. S4b, c). We also examined whether the number of eGenes regulated differed between selection outlier and non-outlier eQTL. On average, selection outlier eQTL regulated more eGenes than non-outliers (LFMM = 1.86; PCAdapt = 1.20; non-outliers = 1.00; Table S4).

Evidence of Selection at Outlier Loci

To investigate the evolutionary forces experienced by our identified selection outliers, we estimated measures of genetic differentiation and divergence. We performed selection tests, including Fay and Wu's H and the composite likelihood ratio test (CLR). We compared these estimates for selection outliers, eQTL, and eGenes in relation to control genes. Because eQTL control gene expression, comparing their population genetic statistics with those of control genes (i.e. variable genes from our expression dataset, excluding selection outlier, eQTL, and eGenes) and eGenes allows us to examine how selection has targeted regulatory genes and their downstream targets. We would expect genes containing selection outlier SNPs to generally show stronger signals of selection than control genes. Genetic differentiation, as estimated by FST, was considerably higher for selection outlier genes than for control genes (LFMM; P = 3.5 × 10−2, PCAdapt; P = 8.7 × 10−14, Wilcoxon rank sum test; Fig. 3a). Outlier genes also had higher Dxy compared to control genes (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank sum test; Fig. 3b). These results provide additional evidence that our selection outliers might underlie population divergence. We also find that Dxy was significantly elevated for eQTL (Fig. 3b).

Within each population, we estimated two measures of genetic diversity at each gene, θπ (nucleotide diversity) and θW (Watterson's estimator), to explore additional evolutionary patterns in outlier genes. We would expect genes experiencing population-wide positive or purifying selection to exhibit reduced genetic diversity, while balancing selection, divergent selection, or a higher local mutation rate might result in increased genetic diversity. In both the North and Coast populations, outlier genes had higher θW and θπ compared to the control genes (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank sum test) (Fig. S7).

Finally, we further validated our outlier genes using Fay and Wu's H-neutrality measure and the Composite Likelihood Ratio (CLR) test for selective sweeps (Fig. 3c to f). CLR estimates at the selection outlier genes identified by the LFMM and PCAdapt methods, as well as eQTLs, were significantly more positive compared to the control genes in both populations (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank sum test) (Fig. 3e, f), while these selection outliers had more negative Fay & Wu's H estimates compared to control genes (LFMM-north; P = 4.62 × 10−11; PCAdapt-north; P = 5.27 × 10−5; PCAdapt-coast; P = 0.00160, Wilcoxon rank sum test (Fig. 3c, d). CLR was significantly reduced for eGenes in the coast population (Fig. 3e). These results suggest that candidate locally adapted genes are enriched for selective sweeps in both populations, a while the elevated CLR for eQTL is consistent with partial/soft selective sweeps on these regulatory variants.

We drilled down into these estimates within the HaFT1 region in the north and coast populations, revealing contrasting diversity and selection patterns (Fig. S8). Nucleotide diversity (θπ) the HaFT1 region was consistently higher for genes in the coast population, particularly near 13.9 Mb where selection outlier SNPs were detected. In the north population, a near-complete loss of nucleotide diversity in the region was observed. In contrast, θW showed similar levels between populations. Measures of differentiation and divergence, Dxy and FST, were moderate. Fay's H was negative in the coast but near zero in the north, pointing to an excess of high-frequency derived alleles in the coastal population, consistent with recent positive selection. Composite likelihood ratio (CLR) peaks aligned spatially with the diversity troughs in the coast, further validating the occurrence of a selective sweep acting on or near HaFT1. Our results indicate that the HaFT1 region has undergone recent positive selection in the coast population but not the north (Fig. S8).

Connectivity of Selection Outliers

We estimated gene co-expression networks to further evaluate the pleiotropy of genes harboring selection outlier SNPs. Since the co-expression networks were built from plants at the same age grown in a common garden, the resulting networks should largely reflect heritable gene co-expression common to both populations, although maternal effects are possible in our design. We used softConnectivity in WGCNA to measure the strength of correlation between each gene's expression pattern and those of all other genes. These correlations were adjusted using a soft-thresholding power, which gives more weight to strong correlations while still preserving weaker ones to maintain the overall network structure (Zhang and Horvath 2005; Langfelder and Horvath 2008). Our network, therefore, consisted of a few highly connected hubs, and the resulting connectivity score indicates the centrality or influence of each gene within the network. Our gene co-expression analysis identified 89 networks, with a median of 79 genes per network (range, 32 to 5,526). Five networks contained more than expected selection outlier genes (q < 0.01, Fisher's exact test). Permutation tests showed that these enrichments were not randomly distributed across co-expression networks but clustered in specific networks. The yellow, green, red, blue, and pink networks were 1.6 to 2.4X enriched for selection outliers compared to expectation (Fisher's FDR < 0.01; permutation FDR, n = 10,000; Table S5). We used the connectivity of our candidate adaptive genes to estimate transcriptomic pleiotropy. More highly connected genes are putatively more pleiotropic in that changes to them will have broader effects on the expression of other genes. Outlier genes had higher connectivity than control genes across all gene co-expression networks (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Fig. 4a) and had higher within-network connectivity than non-outliers in the yellow, green, and red networks (permutation FDR = 1.67 × 10−4; Fig. 4d to f; Fig. S9).

To assess the position of genes within the HaFT1 region relative to selection outliers, we examined their centrality within co-expression networks (Fig. 4). Four out of the five outlier-enriched networks contained selection outlier genes within the HaFT1 region. Genes with selection outliers in the HaFT1 region as well as other linked genes in the region were highly connected in these networks (Fig. 4b, c), suggesting that selection may be acting on key regulatory genes in the low recombination haploblock around HaFT1. Because recombination is low (Todesco et al. 2020), these connected variants can be inherited together even with gene flow, allowing coordinated changes in gene expression. Functional annotation of HaFT1-window genes in the five outlier-enriched networks showed a broad set of cellular and regulatory processes rather than exclusively canonical flowering-time functions. However, many genes show highest expression in reproductive tissues (e.g. flowers, pollen, carpels/ovules, or the shoot apex; Table S9 to S13), and the two ecotypes differ in growth as well as reproductive traits. Within this set, the HaFT1-region genes that are selection outliers were annotated as an RNA-binding/helicase-related factor (ATC3H31-like), an AAA-type ATPase, and a vesicle trafficking/cytokinesis component (KEU/SEC11), broad but consistent with roles in gene regulation and cellular organization during growth and reproductive development (Table S10 to S13) (Baek et al. 2011; Rameneni et al. 2018; Beuder et al. 2022).

Diversity declined with expression level variance and with increasing gene expression network connectivity, particularly for θπ (Table 1), suggesting a stronger purifying constraint on highly connected genes. However, these models explained only a low proportion of variance in connectivity (R2 ≤ 2%; Table 1). Because our outlier loci showed both higher connectivity than controls and enrichment for selective sweep signatures (P < 2.2 × 10−16, Wilcoxon rank-sum test), our data suggest that divergent selection in this system is targeting pleiotropic genes even if these genes are on average under purifying selection.

Annotation of Adaptation Loci and Gene co-expression Networks

Candidate adaptive genes were enriched in the biological functions of ADP binding, defense response, and stress-responses such as oxidoreductase and glucan synthase activities (Fig. S10). Gene co-expression networks with more than expected selection outlier genes were involved in core biological functions ranging from recognition of pollen, biotic defense, and cellular activities such as protein autophosphorylation and transmembrane receptor kinase activity (Table S6). Also, KEGG module annotations identified these five networks as predominantly involved in primary metabolic pathways, including the citrate cycle, de novo purine biosynthesis, gibberellin biosynthesis, acyl-CoA biosynthesis, and nitrate assimilation (Table S7). Three out of the five outlier-enriched networks had elevated FST compared to random sampling from non-enriched networks (the same three as in Fig. 4d to f), while all five outlier-enriched networks had significantly elevated Dxy (Fig. 5). However, CLR was more positive (Fig. 5e, f), Fay and Wu's H values were near neutral (Fig. 5c, d), and genetic diversity was elevated across outlier-enriched networks in both populations (Fig. S11). Similar patterns were observed for these gene co-expression networks even when outlier genes were removed (Fig. S12 to S13).

Discussion

Pleiotropy is a key factor in adaptive evolution, influencing how genetic loci contribute to phenotypic variation and shaping evolutionary trajectories (Stearns 2010; Tenaillon 2014). Fisher's geometric model posits that adaptation is more efficient through small-effect loci, as large-effect mutations may overshoot the fitness optimum and cause maladaptation (Wagner and Zhang 2011). However, Orr's extension of Fisher's model suggests that pleiotropic loci can facilitate rapid adaptation in novel environments, particularly when a population is far from its fitness optimum, such as following range expansion or under divergent selection with gene flow (Orr 1998, 2005; Battlay et al. 2024). The role of pleiotropy in adaptation has been debated, with some models suggesting that highly pleiotropic loci constrain evolution by generating trade-offs (Guillaume and Otto 2012; Mauro and Ghalambor 2020). In contrast, others propose that such loci can accelerate adaptation (Whiting et al. 2024); (Hämälä et al. 2020a; Battlay et al. 2024). As with many similar debates in biology, the answer likely depends on context.

In this study, we explored how pleiotropy facilitates local adaptation in Helianthus argophyllus populations. Our findings provide new insights into the interplay between pleiotropy and local adaptation in the silverleaf sunflower H. argophyllus, where divergent selection acts under gene flow (Melo et al. 2016; Collet et al. 2018; Battlay et al. 2024). We used gene co-expression networks as a proxy for gene pleiotropy. We find that candidate genes for local adaptation are under strong selection, enriched for eQTL that regulate more target eGenes, and highly connected within co-expression networks. These candidate genes may represent highly connected regulatory genes that facilitate adaptive divergence in H. argophyllus populations. Our results suggest that adaptation in H. argophyllus follows a pleiotropic model, in which genes under divergent selection are likely to have transcriptomic regulatory functions. This model fits best when the traits under selection have not yet attained optimum fitness, potentially due to adaptation to heterogeneous environments or ecotypic divergence under gene flow (Wagner et al. 2007; Wagner and Zhang 2011; Hämälä et al. 2020a; Battlay et al. 2024; Lai et al. 2025). At the same time, the enrichment of selection outliers within specific co-expression networks and the accompanying signatures of partial or soft sweeps indicate that adaptation may proceed in a polygenic manner within gene networks (Hämälä et al. 2020b). Together, this suggests that selection may be targeting specific functions through standing genetic variation, while ongoing gene flow or variable environments prevent populations from reaching their fitness optima.

Our identified candidate adaptive genes are supported by signals of positive selection, enriched for loci associated with the expression of other genes (eQTL), and have higher gene co-expression network connectivity relative to the transcriptome-wide control. Genetic diversity (θπ and θW), differentiation (FST), and divergence (Dxy) were significantly higher in selection outliers than in non-outliers, providing additional support for the hypothesis that these loci are under divergent selection between populations. Outlier loci also had higher CLR and more negative Fay & Wu's H values, indicative of strong selective sweeps (Beisswanger and Stephan 2008; Degiorgio et al. 2016). At the network level, gene co-expression networks enriched for these outliers showed elevated FST and Dxy, with Fay and Wu's H values closer to zero and a positive CLR, a signal of strong population differentiation accompanied by incomplete or soft sweeps. Importantly, the key patterns remained even after outliers were excluded: outlier-enriched co-expression networks continued to show elevated Dxy and CLR relative to control co-expression networks, suggesting that selection may be targeting genes with shared expression patterns beyond the outlier loci. These gene co-expression networks were also annotated with specific biological functions shared by many genes in each network. This pattern points to network-level pleiotropy or functional modularity in which selection on a set of highly connected regulatory genes drives coordinated allele-frequency shifts across their partners (Hämälä et al. 2020b). This pattern aligns with theoretical expectations for polygenic adaptation under pleiotropic constraint, where adaptation proceeds through changes across multiple loci and retains diversity within networks (Barghi et al. 2020). Taken together, these signatures indicate that population divergence in H. argophyllus is both pleiotropic and polygenic: pleiotropic on a few highly connected regulatory variants, and polygenic in how the response unfolds through network-wide shifts that tune network output.

Gene ontology analysis revealed that selection outliers were enriched in genes annotated with biological pathways related to broader regulatory functions, such as signal transduction, defense response, and protein serine/threonine kinase activity. The five gene co-expression networks enriched for selection outliers were additionally annotated with primary and secondary metabolic pathways using KEGG module analysis (Arnqvist and Sayadi 2022). These functional annotations are generic enough that specific interpretation is impractical, but suggest that broad aspects of biological function are under divergent selection between H. argophyllus populations. These annotations also generally support a functional modularity model, where the genetic targets of selection share functional roles or contribute to shared traits.

Across all co-expression networks, selection outliers were more centrally positioned than non-outliers. Zooming in on the HaFT1 region within co-expression networks, we observed a similar pattern: outlier nodes from within the HaFT1 region were highly connected. This 10 Mb low recombination region is implicated in ecotypic differentiation in H. argophyllus, likely driven by a present/absence polymorphism of the FLOWERING LOCUS T homolog HaFT1. This HaFT1 region-specific result is consistent with selection acting on highly pleiotropic hubs, potentially due to divergent selection under gene flow, which would be further facilitated by locally reduced recombination.

While our study provides new insight into H. argophyllus adaptive variation, some limitations are to be considered. First, our transcriptomic data were collected at a single developmental time point (3 weeks), and our analyses can only provide insight into genes expressed at this life stage. We assumed that a common environment allowed us to assess primarily heritable differences in gene expression, but maternal effects may have played a larger-than-expected role. Secondly, we focused on trans-eQTLs because of the data available to us: trans associations capture distant regulatory effects that can reveal pleiotropic interactions across gene networks. However, trans associations generally have smaller effect sizes, are more context-dependent, and show lower reproducibility than cis-eQTLs (Yao et al. 2017; Banerjee et al. 2021; Dutta et al. 2022). They may also reflect indirect regulatory cascades rather than direct variant-to-gene links (Yao et al. 2017). Future studies integrating both cis- and trans eQTLs might better clarify the combined roles of local and distant regulatory mechanisms in adaptation.

Our findings align with the (Hämälä et al. 2020a) study in common ragweed, where putative locally-adaptive loci were highly connected in co-expression networks and were enriched for eQTLs. Together, our results support the theory of pleiotropy facilitating adaptation, especially when populations experience environmental shifts (e.g. a range expansion in common ragweed). In our study, we extend this framework by investigating how candidate adaptive loci are distributed across functional networks to structure pleiotropic interactions. Our results are also consistent with the theory advanced by (Battlay et al. 2024), who showed that divergent selection in the presence of gene flow favors large-effect, pleiotropic loci as repeatable targets of local adaptation. Their results show that pleiotropic constraints do not strongly prevent large-effect loci from driving repeated adaptation when migration is present. Hence, repeatability is expected to be stronger in local adaptation with migration, because gene flow swamps small-effect changes, making large-effect loci the most likely targets (Battlay et al. 2024). In line with pleiotropic loci underlying divergent selection, our study parallels (Rennison and Peichel 2022) in stickleback which found that genomic regions under parallel ecotypic divergence contained more pleiotropic genes than non-parallel-divergent genomic regions. In our findings, gene flow between H. argophyllus ecotypes might favor selection on large-effect, pleiotropic loci that drive adaptive divergence despite ongoing introgression (Moyers and Rieseberg 2016; Hämälä and Savolainen 2019; Todesco et al. 2020). This aligns with emerging evidence that plant species re-use orthologous genes with higher pleiotropic effects during environmental, but not global, adaptation (Nocchi et al. 2024; Whiting et al. 2024).

Conceptually, our findings align with (Lotterhos et al. 2018) modular framework, which posits that climate adaptation in Pinus contorta involves a modular genetic architecture. We also find that local adaptation in H. argophyllus may be occurring through functionally integrated co-expression networks. Lotterhos et al. found modularity among loci responding to specific environmental pressures. Our findings show regulatory modularity driven by pleiotropic hub genes with specific biological functions, suggesting that selection is acting at the level of gene regulatory networks. In considering how outlier-enriched co-expression networks might evolve in response to selection, our results also suggest polygenic adaptation in agreement with the modular pattern of expression reported by (Hämälä et al. 2020b) in Theobroma cacao. As in their case, adaptive genes in H. argophyllus span multiple co-expression networks. The presence of these genes in networks most likely allows the evolution of other, more constrained functions through regulatory coupling, such that while pleiotropic constraint exists, it is feasible to achieve network-wide shifts in gene expression (Hämälä et al. 2020b).

Our study shows that local adaptation in H. argophyllus proceeds through broadly pleiotropic, highly connected regulatory variants, enriched within gene co-expression networks and carrying signals of polygenic, soft selective sweeps. Backed by genetic differentiation, selection, eQTLs, and gene ontology enrichment analyses, our findings demonstrate how pleiotropic loci and gene co-expression networks shape adaptive divergence in H. argophyllus. This research contributes to the ongoing discussion on how selection and pleiotropy interact to drive evolution in complex organisms.

Conclusions

This study provides new insights into the relationship between pleiotropy and adaptation in a wild plant species experiencing divergent selection under gene flow. Our findings suggest that candidate locally adaptive loci in Helianthus argophyllus are under strong selection, with stronger signals of hard selective sweeps and a greater likelihood of regulating gene expression than non-adaptive loci. At the same time, gene co-expression network organization enables population-specific divergence through polygenic, soft selective sweeps within gene co-expression networks enriched for selection outlier loci. This balance between pleiotropic and polygenic effects highlights how regulatory network architecture mediates adaptive divergence, offering insight into how selection acts through pleiotropic hubs and network organization to drive evolutionary change. Local adaptation may be facilitated and hindered by pleiotropy, which likely depends on the distance of the populations from fitness optima and the consistency of those optima. Understanding this dynamic helps us comprehend how genetic structures influence adaptation, with significant implications for evolutionary biology, conservation, and plant breeding. As genetic diversity declines, which is required to maintain additive genetic variation, these results highlight the possibility of targeting specific gene co-expression subnetworks for desired trait variation in natural or artificial populations.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and RNA Sequencing

Sample collection and sequencing are described in Renaut et al. (2013). Here, we include 19 individuals representing two distinct subpopulations of H. argophyllus, identified by previous research (Moyers and Rieseberg 2013). The populations include coast (n = 9), and north (n = 10). Briefly, individual seeds from the north and coast subpopulations of H. argophyllus collected by BTM in 2011 were grown for 3 weeks in a growth chamber with 12 h of daylight at 22 °C (Moyers and Rieseberg 2013). All above-ground tissue was then frozen and stored at −80 °C until RNA was extracted using a modified TRIzol Reagent protocol. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and sequenced on a GAII (paired-end sequencing, 2 × 100 bp reads). The average number of reads per individual was 1,864, 375, with an average read depth of 17.8.

Sequence Processing

We ran FastQC/0.11.5 (Andrews 2010) on the RNA sequencing reads to assess quality and Trimmomatic/0.39 (Bolger et al. 2014) to perform sliding window trimming with a 4-base window size. We removed reads with an average quality below 20 and reads shorter than 25 bases after trimming. We aligned reads to the Ha412HOv2 H. annuus reference genome (https://www.sunflowergenome.org/assembly-data/) (Badouin et al. 2017) using STAR (Dobin et al. 2013). The average mapping percentages for the sampled populations were 81.2% and 79% for coast and north respectively (Table S5). After alignment, we used Picard 2.23.3 (Ebbert et al. 2016) to add read group information and remove duplicate reads. We called variants on aligned reads using Freebayes/1.3.5 (Garrison and Marth 2012) and filtered the VCF file with vcftools 0.1.14 (Danecek et al. 2011). We applied the following filtering criteria: site quality ≥ 30, genotype quality ≥ 20, read coverage ≥ 6, and missing data < 20%.

Genetic Diversity and Population Structure

We estimated θW, θπ, Dxy, Tajima's D, and FST within each population and per gene with Pixy (Korunes and Samuk 2021) for all sampled individuals. We filtered for genotype variants with a Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) of ≥0.05 for subsequent analyses but not for diversity statistics. We included gene coordinates as intervals in our genetic analysis to compute all statistics per gene and extracted each gene set; selection outliers, eQTLs, eGenes, and control for comparison. Waterson's θ (θW) is an estimator of population mutation rate and can be compared to nucleotide diversity (π or θπ), a measure of within-population polymorphism, to identify genetic loci that have experienced non-neutral evolution or demographic changes (Tajima 1989; Watterson 1975). We identified admixture proportions with ADMIXTURE (Alexander et al. 2009; Skotte et al. 2013).

Selection Outliers

We used the LFMM 2 and PCAdapt R packages to identify selection outliers at a q-value threshold of <0.05. LFMM 2 is a genotype-environment association (GEA) method that incorporates more than one independent variable in a multivariate model. These variables can be climatic, methylation, or gene expression data (Caye et al. 2019). We included 19 BIOCLIM variables in our LFMM 2 model to identify genotype-environment associations (Noce et al. 2020). PCAdapt identifies locally adapted candidates after controlling for population structure. PCAdapt is optimized to handle admixed individuals, account for population divergence, handle large sequencing datasets, and has a relatively low FDR. This means PCAdapt tends to produce fewer false positives when identifying loci under selection, given the same nominal threshold, compared to some other genome-scan methods (Luu et al. 2017).

eQTL and eGenes

We counted the number of reads expressed for each gene using FeatureCounts in the Subread/1.6.2 package (Liao et al. 2014). We normalized the gene count file generated by Subread for our eQTL analysis using Variance Stabilizing Transformation (VST) with Deseq2 R package, which uses the median-ratio normalization method (Lin et al. 2008; Love et al. 2014; Evans et al. 2018). We used the MatrixEqtl R package to identify loci (eQTL) associated with changes in the expression of specific genes (eGenes) (Shabalin 2012). We defined trans-eQTLs as SNP–gene associations located more than 1 Mb apart or on different chromosomes, consistent with standard thresholds (Võsa et al. 2021; Dutta et al. 2022). We increased our filter MAF threshold from 0.05 to 0.1 to reduce spurious associations (Wang et al. 2021). To control for expression heterogeneity, we performed principal component regression on our expression data (Tian et al. 2014) and selected the first two principal components, which explained 92% of the variation. We included these principal components (Fig. S4) as covariates from the regression analysis using the first two principal components in our eQTL model to control for the effect of population structure (Tian et al. 2014).

Selective Sweeps

We subset the VCF file by the genomic coordinates of selection outlier genes, eQTL, eGenes, and control genes for each population. Control genes were the expressed genes that were not identified by any of the other analyses. We identified putative selective sweeps using two approaches: Fay and Wu's H (Fay and Wu 2000) and the Composite Likelihood Ratio (CLR) method using the SweepFinder2 package (Vy and Kim 2015; Degiorgio et al. 2016). We used the H. annuus reference as an outgroup when calculating Fay and Wu's H to estimate the proportion of derived alleles. To estimate CLR, we subset each VCF file by gene and used all positions within each gene to create grid files for the analysis.

Statistical Comparisons

We compared medians for population genetic estimators across selection outlier genes, eQTL, eGenes, and 1,000 bootstrapped control genes using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test in R (Rey and Neuhäuser 2011).

Gene co-expression Networks

We used the WGCNA R package to construct gene co-expression networks (Langfelder and Horvath 2008). We set the power threshold to 4 to obtain the adjacency matrix of the signed network using the “softConnectivity” function. In WGCNA, softConnectivity calculates how strongly each gene's expression pattern is correlated with all other genes. These correlations are adjusted using a soft-thresholding power, which gives more weight to strong correlations while still preserving weaker ones to maintain the overall network structure (Zhang and Horvath 2005; Langfelder and Horvath 2008). This creates a network where a few genes act as highly connected hubs, and the resulting connectivity score indicates the centrality or influence of each gene within the network (Zhang and Horvath 2005; Langfelder and Horvath 2008; Zeng et al. 2021). We compared the log-transformed connectivity of node genes that were selection outliers, eQTLs, or eGenes with that of node genes in the transcriptome-wide background using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (Rey and Neuhäuser 2011). To assess whether outlier genes were more central than expected within gene co-expression networks (Fuller et al. 2007), we calculated within-network soft connectivity using WGCNA's softConnectivity function for each outlier-enriched module and applied a size-matched permutation test in which the median connectivity of outlier genes was compared to a null distribution generated by repeatedly sampling the same number of non-outlier genes from the same module (10,000 permutations). We visualized gene co-expression networks using Cytoscape and igraph (Shannon et al. 2003; Nepusz and Csárdi 2006).

Annotation of Genes and Networks

We used the clusterProfiler R package for gene and KEGG module overrepresentation analyses of selection outlier genes and co-expression networks (Yu et al. 2012). Since VCF was aligned to the HA412-HO reference genome, but the HanXRQr2.0-SUNRISE genome is used in the KEGG database, we also aligned our transcriptome to the HanXRQr2.0-SUNRISE genome to obtain NCBI gene IDs for each gene sequence using the NCBI BLAST command-line tool (> 2008). We then used these gene IDs as queries in the gene overrepresentation analysis using the “enricher” function in the clusterProfiler package. We used gene IDs as queries for the KEGG module analysis using the “enrichMKEGG” function in the clusterProfiler package. H. annuus is the closest fully annotated relative, and the two species share high sequence similarity, allowing reliable transfer of functional information. This approach is widely used for functional annotation in non-model species where curated orthology databases are unavailable (Duarte et al. 2021). While tools like OrthoFinder can infer one-to-many orthologs across multiple taxa (Emms and Kelly 2019), BLAST-based mapping is appropriate and efficient for closely related species. It ensures accurate KEGG pathway assignment for our dataset (Conesa et al. 2005). Finally, we annotated genes in the HaFT1 region by first converting gene IDs from the HA412-HO format to the HanXRQr2.0-SUNRISE format. Because many sunflower genes are not fully annotated, we then identified the corresponding Arabidopsis orthologs and used the TAIR database to extract functional information, including gene descriptions, known functions, and the tissues with the highest expression (Garcia-Hernandez et al. 2002).
