Validation and clinical application of the Chinese version of the rapid response scale
Authors: Ziying Chen, Junhao Shen, Yifang Chen, Zerui You, Xiaoyu Chen, Fan Zhang, Xiaofeng Lan, Chenyu Wang, Yuping Ning, Yanling Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExisting depression assessment tools inadequately detect rapid symptom changes after antidepressant treatments. This study aimed to translate, validate, and explore the clinical application of the Chinese version of the McIntyre and Rosenblat Rapid Response Scale (CMRRRS) for use during the treatment of rapid-onset depression.MethodsThe McIntyre and Rosenblat Rapid Response Scale was translated and culturally adapted for use in Chinese settings. Briefly, 71 patients with major depressive disorder who were receiving intravenous esketamine were assessed using the CMRRRS and other validated scales. Properties were examined, including internal consistency, construct validity, and responsiveness to change. It allows real-time symptom monitoring and personalized treatment adjustments. Background

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is one of the most prevalent and disabling mental illnesses and is recognized by the World Health Organization as a leading cause of disability worldwide [1]. Traditional antidepressants, such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, are the first-line treatment for MDD. However, they are limited by a delayed onset of action. The worldwide prevalence of MDD rises, affecting global healthcare and socioeconomic systems. Therefore, there is an increasing need for therapeutic solutions that provide quick alleviation of symptoms and decrease suicidal ideations [2]. In response, rapid-acting antidepressants, such as ketamine [3] and psilocybin [4], as well as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), have been developed. Of these, esketamine has shown promising outcomes (5;6). Ketamine is a racemic combination consisting of equal amounts of R-ketamine and S-ketamine (esketamine). Esketamine exhibits a higher affinity for the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor and is usually administered through an intravenous (IV) route. This research concentrated on IV esketamine for managing MDD.

Measurement-based care (MBC) is a patient-centered, dynamic treatment model that uses sensitive and standardized assessment tools to monitor real-time symptom changes, optimize treatment strategies, and enhance therapeutic outcomes. MBC facilitates timely clinical decision-making with quantitative data, enabling rapid interventions and personalized treatment plans. Additionally, MBC considerably improves patient adherence, therapeutic efficacy, and satisfaction, compared with traditional medical care [7, 8].

Despite the advances in rapid-acting antidepressants, validated assessment tools for capturing their therapeutic effects are lacking. Commonly used tools, including the Montgomery-Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) [9, 10] and the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-17) [11, 12], have been validated and comprehensively describe depressive symptoms. However, the MADRS and HAMD-17 have been designed to examine symptom changes and their severity over 1 week. As such, they are less suitable for detecting the rapid symptom fluctuations characteristic of fast-acting antidepressant therapies [13]. The limitations of these tools to capture rapid changes in symptoms may negatively affect the precise evaluation of treatment outcomes, impeding prompt clinical decision-making. Therefore, developing specialized assessment tools tailored for fast-acting antidepressant treatments is crucial. These tools would improve the application of the MBC and facilitate a more adaptive, personalized approach to managing depression. The McIntyre and Rosenblat Rapid Response Scale (MARRRS) and its Chinese version (CMRRRS) have been designed to provide more sensitive measurements of fast-acting antidepressant treatment modalities.

Additionally, existing depression assessment scales require time-consuming evaluations conducted by clinicians. This presents a burden in clinical settings, particularly when using rapid-acting antidepressants that warrant constant assessment and monitoring. To alleviate the pressure while facilitating prompt and precise symptom monitoring, rapid self-assessment tools have become an important alternative [14].

Prior studies have attempted to improve upon standard depression scales and questionnaires by converting them into self-reported formats or restructuring them to relieve clinical burden and detect emotional experiences more accurately [15, 16]. Notably, the McIntyre team adapted the existing Questionnaire-16 Self-Rating Scale into the MARRRS. It adheres to MBC standards and can detect short-term changes in depressive symptoms [17]. It shows promise in capturing real-time, precise depressive symptoms while reducing redundant clinical assessments and facilitating continuous investigation into therapies with a quick mode of action.

Nonetheless, the MARRRS has yet to be translated into different languages or tested in diverse populations. Furthermore, there is a scarcity of empirical studies examining its clinical application and relevance. Because rapid-acting antidepressant treatments are increasingly adopted in China, there is an urgent demand for a suitable tool that can effectively monitor swift changes in symptoms. This study aimed to translate and culturally modify the MARRRS, originally developed by McIntyre and colleagues [17], into the CMRRRS; assess its psychometric characteristics, including validity, reliability, and sensitivity during intravenous (IV) esketamine treatment; and investigate its practical application in clinical settings, including grading symptom severity and determining the minimum clinically important difference (MCID).

Methods

Participant Recruitment

Participants were recruited from The Affiliated Brain Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University, where they completed the entire IV esketamine treatment course and clinical evaluations.

Translation of the MARRRS

Permission to translate the MARRRS was obtained through email communication. First, the English version of the MARRRS [17] was independently translated into Chinese by two bilingual psychiatric specialists and culturally adapted to the Chinese context. Then, the preliminary Chinese version (CMRRRS) was independently back-translated into English by two Chinese researchers with high English proficiency. They conducted a linguistic validation process and reported any inconsistencies or misinterpretations. After a consensus meeting, two senior clinicians conducted a final review and proofreading of the CMRRRS to confirm its adaptation to Chinese culture. No grammatical errors were reported. Any discrepancies in ambiguous concepts were adjusted to the cultural context, and the translation was revised. The complete CMRRRS was authorized for validation.

Data resource and screening criteria

This real-world observational study included adult patients receiving IV esketamine treatment for treatment-resistant depression (TRD) or severe suicidal ideations. Strict eligibility criteria were implemented because esketamine use in this setting is off-label. Adults who met the criteria listed in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders and had a confirmed diagnosis of MDD were selected. All participants had shown poor response to at least two prior antidepressant treatments administered at sufficient doses and for an appropriate duration; or had severe suicidal ideations. Depression severity was assessed using a HAMD-17 score of ≥ 17, and suicidal tendencies were evaluated with a score of ≥ 2 on the Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation Part I (SSI-I) during the initial screening. The exclusion criteria comprised any serious or unstable physical condition, as well as individuals with alcohol or substance abuse disorders. Individuals under psychiatric medications had to maintain a consistent dosage throughout the IV esketamine treatment phase. Before enrolling in the program, all participants provided written informed consent. Additional methodological information can be found in our previous publication [18].

Treatment Process

At baseline, demographic data, such as age, sex, height, and weight, were obtained. Patients underwent an overnight fast before receiving intravenous esketamine through a syringe pump at a dose of 0.25 mg/kg over a 40-min infusion period. Treatment responders were identified as patients who experienced a ≥ 50% decrease in their MADRS total score from baseline or had a post-treatment MADRS score ≤ 10. These patients could complete their treatment after the third infusion and were advised to begin maintenance therapy. Non-responders were identified as patients who experienced a ≤ 20% reduction in their MADRS total score at the third assessment; they were considered for early termination after the fourth or fifth infusion. Patients who exhibited intermediate responses (a 21–49% decrease in MADRS) were advised to complete all six infusions for further observation. Up to six infusions were administered on an alternating-day schedule, i.e., on days 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, and 12, depending on the response of each treatment. During and after each infusion, vital signs (blood pressure, pulse, and oxygen saturation) were monitored to ensure a return to baseline levels.

Assessment

Within 2 h after each infusion, patients completed self-assessments using both the CMRRRS and SSI-I to monitor symptom changes. Additionally, within 24 h after the third infusion (infusion 3–24 h) and again within 24 h after either completing or terminating the treatment (post-treatment), trained clinicians conducted comprehensive examinations. These included the MADRS, Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), and the Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale (SHAPS). During these sessions, patients were also instructed to repeat the CMRRRS and SSI-I.

Analysis

IBM Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26.0 was used for all statistical analyses. Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to determine the internal consistency of the CMRRRS. To determine convergent validity, a Spearman’s correlation was calculated between MADRS and CMRRRS total scores and score changes at pre-treatment, infusion from 3 to 24 h, and post-treatment. An exploratory factor analysis (EFA) of the 14 CMRRRS items was conducted. Principal component analysis with varimax rotation was conducted to determine the number of factors per an eigenvalue threshold of ≥ 1.0. The factor structure was then validated using a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) and tested for measurement invariance across baseline and first three infusions. In addition, a linear mixed-effects model was applied utilizing data from baseline and the first three infusions to evaluate CMRRRS’s ability to detect short-term symptomatic fluctuations as treatment could be discontinued after the third to sixth infusion depending on patient response. The model incorporated age, sex, body mass index, and baseline MADRS scores. A compound symmetry covariance matrix was utilized, and estimation was conducted using Restricted Maximum Likelihood, with the α-value set at 0.05.

To categorize patients according to their symptom progression, Latent Profile Analysis (LPA) with Mplus software was utilized. It capped the number of latent categories at five. The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT), and Bootstrapped Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) were used to assess model fit to identify the optimal number of categories.

To validate inter-group differences among the latent classes identified by LPA, the Kruskal-Wallis test was conducted in SPSS 26.0, evaluating scores on the CMRRRS, SSI-I, MADRS, HAMA, and SHAPS. To further explore the distributional characteristics among classes, Kernel Density Estimation (KDE) curves were created using the “density” function in R software. It illustrated symptom distribution across latent classes and helped identify the intersection points (thresholds) between severity groups.

Then, the MCID was used to identify the smallest significant score alteration suggesting clinical improvement. Here, the MCID was calculated using both anchor- and distribution-based methods in SPSS 26.0. In the anchor-based approach, mean score changes related to clinically defined response categories were utilized to estimate the MCID. In the distribution-based approach, the MCID was defined as 0.5 times the standard deviation of the CMRRRS score change. MCID results from both approaches were compared. Finally, cross-tabulation was conducted to assess whether the CMRRRS score changes after the first infusion indicated treatment responses, compared with the established MCID. ROC analyses were also used for cross-validation to consider the differences in MCID in different severity groups.

Among them, nine patients had comorbid disorders, including three with generalized anxiety disorder, four with obsessive-compulsive disorder, one with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, and one with another psychiatric condition. Internal Consistency and Validity

The CMRRRS exhibited robust internal consistency across all infusions (Supplementary Table S1), with Cronbach’s α = 0.94 at both baseline and post-treatment, and Cronbach’s α = 0.93 across all timepoints. Correlational analysis indicated convergent validity between the MADRS and the CMRRRS total scores (r = 0.77, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1. A.). Moreover, changes in MADRS scores were strongly correlated with changes in CMRRRS scores (r = 0.67, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1. B.).

Factor analysis

The Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy was 0.9, suggesting that the sample was adequate for factor analysis. Furthermore, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was highly significant (p < 0.001). EFA was conducted on the CMRRRS items, suggesting two factors for data collected at baseline and after treatment: vigor and distress (Supplementary Table S2). The vigor factor included 10 items as follows: happiness, concentration, general interest, motivation, pleasure and enjoyment, energy, social isolation, signs of positive emotion, restlessness, and worry. The distress factor included four items: unhappy, self-perception, suicidal thoughts, and hopefulness/hopelessness. Collectively, they accounted for 63.0% of the total variance. Three factors were identified from data across the complete infusion timeline: psychic anxiety, vigor, and distress (Supplementary Table S3). The vigor factor included six items: happiness, general interest, motivation, pleasure and enjoyment, social isolation, and positive emotion. The psychic anxiety factor included four items: concentration, energy, restlessness, and worry. The distress factor retained the same four items as mentioned previously. Collectively, they accounted for 68.2% of the total variance. Overall sample of CFA and the result of measurement invariance over the first three time points were tested, details see supplementary tables S4-S7.

Sample size

Data were collected from 71 patients who contributed to 368 responses across different time points using the 14-item CMRRRS. The sample size surpassed the recommended psychometric validation standard of 5 to 10 participants per item. Cronbach’s α ranged from 0.93 to 0.94, indicating excellent internal consistency. Additionally, the KMO value (0.9) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity validated the sample’s suitability for factor analysis. Therefore, the sample size was adequate to validate the scale, providing sufficient statistical power to support the findings.

Sensitivity

Results of the linear mixed-effects model demonstrated a significant main effect of time on CMRRRS total scores: F (1, 42.0) = 730.775, p < 0.001. This finding indicates the scale’s sensitivity to short-term clinical changes after esketamine infusions (Supplementary Table S8). Corrected pairwise comparisons indicated a significant reduction in symptoms from baseline to post-infusion timepoints 1 (p = 0.003), 2 (p < 0.001), and 3 (p < 0.001). Furthermore, symptom severity significantly decreased between post-infusion 1 and 2 (p = 0.001) as well as between infusion 1 and 3 (p < 0.001).

LPA Classification

LPA was utilized to detect various patient subgroups per their symptom changes. Several indices were used to assess model fit, including the BIC, AIC, adjusted BIC (aBIC), LMRT, and BLRT (Table 2). The AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased with an increase in the number of classes. Notably, the most significant enhancements in model fit were observed between the 2-class and 3-class models. They exhibited significant p-values for LMRT. All models showed BLRT p-values < 0.001, indicating strong model performance. Additionally, entropy values were > 0.8 across these models, indicating strong classification accuracy. Hence, the 3-class model was selected as the optimal solution. For clarity, the identified classes are named as Group 1, Group 2, and Group 3.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to systematically explore the CMRRRS tailored to detect the rapid antidepressant effects of IV esketamine. This study suggests that CMRRRS is a reliable, valid, and sensitive tool for monitoring changes in MDD symptoms during rapid-acting antidepressant treatment. The internal consistency of the CMRRRS remained high at all assessment intervals. The preliminary translation, back translation, and proofreading affirmed robust content validity [19, 20]. Additionally, convergent validity was demonstrated via correlation analyses comparing the MADRS and the CMRRRS, suggesting that the CMRRRS effectively reflects the severity of depressive symptoms, confirming its criterion validity.

Regarding structural validity, a two-factor structure was identified from baseline and post-treatment data, whereas a three-factor structure was obtained from infusion data. The CFA results suggested the three-factor model is a better factor structure for CMRRRS, with nearly identical parameters. Combining the result of measurement invariance over time and the discrepancies of EFA, it is suggested that symptom changes during ketamine treatment, which may encompass drug-induced anxiety. Several studies have identified possible subscales and symptom clusters in patients with MDD depending on the disease severity or time of the assessment [21, 22]. Therefore, the CMRRRS symptom clusters varying from McIntyre’s findings can be reasonable. Notably, the identified factors explain over 60% of the total variance, demonstrating a robust structure.

Furthermore, the CMRRRS demonstrated significant sensitivity and practicality. The linear mixed-effects model indicated that the CMRRRS successfully identified rapid symptom changes after IV ketamine treatment. Hence, it can effectively reflect fast-acting therapeutic responses in real-time treatment monitoring.

Additionally, this study analyzed the distribution characteristics and classification thresholds of CMRRRS scores. The LPA results showed that the AIC, BIC, and aBIC values decreased with an increase in the number of classes, indicating a progressive improvement in model fit. Furthermore, the largest changes in model fit indices occurred between the two- and three-class models. The three-class model exhibited a classification accuracy (E index) of 0.893, indicating high classification quality. Hence, it was selected as the optimal solution, and the optimal number of latent classes was set at three [23].

Kruskal-Wallis test results confirmed distinct hierarchical differences among the three LPA-derived groups with significant differences across all scales. Additionally, significant differences were observed across psychological dimensions, including depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation, and anhedonia, supporting the classification system’s integrity. Based on symptom severity across the five scales, the three groups were named as follows: “mild,” “moderate,” and “severe.”

The intersection points in the KDE curves for the three groups presented distinct peaks. Thus, the LPA classification results were reasonable, with significant differentiation in symptom severity among the groups. Additionally, the x-coordinate values of these intersection points (17 and 28) represented the threshold positions between the score distributions across different groups. They can be utilized to categorize patients into groups based on their symptom severity and facilitate more precise symptom assessment and decision-making, contributing to personalized treatment strategies.

The MCID is an essential metric for determining the clinical relevance of interventions [24]. The results from the combination of anchoring, distributional, and ROC analyses support the robustness of the MCID range (3.64–13.26). The applicability of MCID was validated by assessing the concordance between classification results and patient response outcomes. The AUC values were modest overall. However, the observed trend consistently indicated that patients with higher baseline depression severity required smaller absolute changes to achieve clinically meaningful improvement, in line with clinical expectations. Hence, 5 was selected as the MCID for the CMRRRS. This value enables clinicians to assess whether symptom improvements are both statistically significant and clinically relevant.

By optimizing the MCID, this study further strengthened the clinical application of the CMRRRS in fast-acting antidepressant treatments, enabling it to better identify thresholds for substantial symptom improvement. This optimization is crucial for initially evaluating the efficacy of fast-acting antidepressant treatments and provides a scientific foundation for subsequent treatment choices. Moreover, aligning the MCID with patient response outcomes establishes a foundation for the standardized evaluation of early treatment response. For instance, the MCID can identify patients with insufficient treatment response, facilitating dose adjustments or the inclusion of adjunctive therapies.

These findings further validate the scientific value of the CMRRRS for rapid symptom identification, classification, and monitoring treatment in the early stages. It may support healthcare professionals to assess the severity of symptoms more accurately and create personalized treatment plans based on established thresholds [25].

Despite the promising findings, there are several limitations. First, the research utilized a single-arm, open-label approach without a control group, which may have introduced expectation bias in outcomes reported by both clinicians and patients [26]. The lack of a randomized controlled trial (RCT) with placebo or standard treatment groups may limit rigorous assessment of the CMRRRS’s sensitivity to true treatment responses. Future investigations should consider RCTs that include placebo or standard treatment arms to better evaluate the scale’s responsiveness. Second, the limited sample size of unique patients from a single center restricted the generalizability of the results, particularly in subgroup analyses. External validation in independent samples will be essential to confirm and refine the results of factor structure and MCID thresholds. Future studies should consider larger and multicenter groups using standardized assessment procedures. Third, the absence of additional self-reported tools (e.g., Patient Health Questionnaire-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item) limited the ability to examine convergent and discriminant validity. Future studies should utilize established comparator scales and known-group designs to strengthen construct validation. Finally, this study did not collect long-term follow-up data. Although the MCID seems responsive to short-term changes, its effectiveness in monitoring long-term treatment responses and maintenance therapy requires further validation. Future studies should extend the follow-up to at least 6 months to analyze the scale’s performance during maintenance therapy and sensitivity to long-term treatment effects.

The MARRRS was initially developed for use during ketamine treatment, and the CMRRRS was validated for intravenous esketamine therapy. Nonetheless, the CMRRRS appears to have potential applications in various fast-acting antidepressant interventions, such as putatively mirtazapine and electroconvulsive therapy. Confirming the CMRRRS’s efficacy in these situations would broaden its applicability to a wider range of treatments and facilitate the comprehensive management of depressive symptoms.

Conclusions

The CMRRRS is one of the first scales validated specifically for Chinese patients receiving rapid-acting antidepressant treatment. It shows high internal consistency and validity, effectively reflecting short-term symptom changes in real-time after esketamine treatment. Additionally, it may support personalized treatment plans. This study proposed severity classification criteria based on LPA and KDE. Moreover, it introduced an MCID value of 5 based on distribution- and anchor-based approaches to indicate meaningful clinical improvement. These findings highlight substantial advancements in treatment guidance and facilitate the development of novel rapid antidepressant therapies in psychiatric practice.
