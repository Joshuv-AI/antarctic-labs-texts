New therapeutic hope for rare podocytopathies
Authors: Yanqin Zhang, Fang Wang, Jie Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Podocytopathies are kidney diseases caused by podocyte injury or dysfunction that drives proteinuria or nephrotic syndrome. A significant expansion in understanding of the complex causes and mechanisms of podocyte injury, as well as potential therapeutic approaches, has been achieved over the past two decades with rapid advances in genomics in both research and clinical practice. Podocytopathies associated with monogenic mechanisms account for approximately 30% of cases with steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) or focal segmental glomerulosclerosis lesions (FSGS), with even higher proportions in pediatric patients. Podocytopathies related to each gene mutation are rare. However, recent developments in these rare podocytopathies contribute to an optimistic outcome for the future to eventually avoid kidney failure. Introduction

Podocytes act as an important part of the glomerular filtration barrier, which is essential for maintaining normal kidney function and preventing proteinuria [1, 2]. Podocytopathies are kidney diseases caused by podocyte injury or dysfunction that drives proteinuria or nephrotic syndrome [3]. The typical kidney histopathological abnormalities in podocytopathies are minimal change lesions and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis lesions (FSGS) [4]. Autoimmunity, infection, drug, hyperfiltration, or genetic causes are associated with podocytopathies [4–8].

More than 60 genes including both podocyte-expressed genes and syndromal non-podocyte-specific genes have so far been identified to cause monogenic and rare podocytopathies associated with FSGS or steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome (SRNS) [9]. The primary mechanism of monogenic podocytopathies is arising from pathogenic variants generally localized in genes that encode podocyte proteins involved in slit diaphragm components and regulators (NPHS1, NPHS2, CD2AP, TRPC6, etc.), actin cytoskeleton architecture and regulation (ACTN4, INF2, MYH9, PLCE1, etc.), nucleoporins and nuclear function (WT1, LMXIB, PAX2, SMARCAL1, etc.), cellular metabolic pathways (COQ2, COQ6, COQ8B, DGKE, etc.), and focal adhesions (ITGA3, ITGB4) [10–14]. Aberrant structure and function of these podocyte proteins lead to podocyte injury, foot process effacement, disruption of the glomerular filtration barrier, and subsequent renal fibrosis, with clinical presentation of proteinuria and kidney failure. In addition, podocyte damage can also be caused by pathogenic variants in genes that encode proteins in glomerular basement membrane (GBM) (COL4A3, COL4A4, COL4A5, etc.) [15, 16]. Pathogenic variants in COL4A3–5 genes cause Alport syndrome due to abnormal α3, α4, or α5 chains of type IV collagen in GBM which are exclusively derived from podocytes. Podocyte damage, including accelerated podocyte detachment and progressive podocyte loss from glomeruli with age in Alport syndrome, has been reported [17]. Animal models of Alport syndrome also demonstrate decreased mechanical stability and splitting of the GBM, as well as aberrant collagen-mediated signaling contributes to defective podocyte–GBM adhesion and podocyte injury [18, 19]. Therefore, X-linked Alport syndrome (XLAS) and autosomal recessive Alport syndrome (ARAS) in particular are rare podocytopathies.

In patients with SRNS, the likelihood of detecting a pathogenic variant in genes resulting in direct or indirect podocyte injury or dysfunction decreases with age. In European children with nephrotic syndrome manifesting in the first year of life, 66.3% of them were detected with pathogenic variants in 4 genes (NPHS1, NPHS2, WT1, and LAMB2). NPHS2 variants were the most frequent cause in those children [20]. An international cohort study confirmed that 61.3% of children with SRNS in the first year of life were identified with a pathogenic variant. Furthermore, the fraction decreased to 25.3% at age of 2 to 6 years, to 17.8% at age of 7 to 12 years, and to about 10.3% at the age of 13 to 18 years [21]. Approximately 12 to 37.5% of patients with SRNS were detected with a pathogenic variant according to the reports all over the world [22, 23]. Distribution of causative genes may vary among different ethnic populations, e.g., WT1, NPHS1, NPHS2, and ADCK4 (COQ8B) are the 4 most frequently mutated genes in Chinese children [23]. Thus, genetic analysis as early as possible is critical for an etiological diagnosis in rare podocytopathies.

Patients with a monogenic cause are a rare subset of podocytopathies, but a majority of the patients will eventually develop stage 5 chronic kidney disease (CKD 5). Proteinuria and poor response to immunosuppressive treatments are common clinical characteristics. Some patients have extrarenal manifestations, such as hearing loss and eye abnormalities. Proteinuria is not only a marker of podocyte damage but also a trigger of a series of mechanisms leading to the worsening of the damage in both podocytes and tubulointerstitium [24]. Therefore, to identify and develop new therapeutic drugs to reduce proteinuria is a promising area of therapeutic research in monogenic podocytopathies. In addition, managing extrarenal symptoms and maintaining a healthy lifestyle can improve the quality of life for patients with monogenic podocytopathies.

The advances in genomics and the application of genetic testing in clinical practice are accelerating a better understanding of the molecular foundations of podocytopathies and access to personalized, mechanism-based care [25, 26]. This review therefore focuses on aspects of new advances of therapies in rare podocytopathies with monogenic causes.

Therapeutic approaches

Most patients with monogenic podocytopathies are less likely to respond to immunosuppressive therapy and are associated with rapid progression to kidney failure. In recent years, therapeutic drugs for common chronic kidney diseases (CKD) that act by reducing proteinuria, attenuating kidney inflammation and fibrosis, have increasingly been shown to improve prognosis of monogenic podocytopathies, for example, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers, sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i), and endothelin receptor A antagonists. Before gene-specific therapies are available for patients with monogenic podocytopathies, the comprehensive treatment strategy for CKD recommended by KDIGO should be adopted [27]. Firstly, a healthy lifestyle including a healthy and diverse diet, moderate physical activity, an optimal body mass index, no use of tobacco products, and withdrawal of nonessential, potentially nephrotoxic medications is recommended. Secondly, first-line drug therapy to reduce risks of progression of CKD and its associated complications is recommended. Thirdly, targeted therapies for complications such as managing hyperkalemia, anemia, and CKD–mineral bone disorder are recommended. Meanwhile, novel personalized treatments targeting pathogenic genetic variants or specific signaling pathways are in development, offering new hope for the management of rare monogenic podocytopathies, such as coenzyme Q10 supplementation, TRPC6 inhibition, and gene therapy. Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system blockers

Renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers, including angiotensin converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEi), angiotensin receptor blockers (ARB), and aldosterone/mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRA), are effective drugs in treating hypertension, diabetic nephropathy, and non-diabetic CKD [28, 29]. In addition, a growing number of studies demonstrate the renoprotective effects of ACEi and ARB in patients with SRNS by reducing proteinuria and decelerating the progression of kidney disease [30, 31].

The ESCAPE Trial investigated the nephroprotective effect of strict blood-pressure control with ACEi in children with proteinuric CKD and enrolled 385 children with stage II to IV CKD randomly assigned to a fixed high dose of ramipril and followed for 5 years. The results showed that median urinary protein-to-creatinine ratio (UPCR) was reduced during the first 6 months and then gradually increased again to a similar level of proteinuria at baseline after 36 months [32]. The post hoc analysis of the ESCAPE Trial demonstrated an early antiproteinuric effect of ACEi was independently associated with a reduced risk of kidney failure progression [33]. To evaluate the effects of addition of an aldosterone antagonist to an ACEi or ARB (or both) in adults who had CKD with proteinuria, a meta-analysis involving 44 studies (5745 participants) demonstrated aldosterone antagonists may reduce proteinuria, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), and systolic blood pressure, and probably increase the risk of hyperkalemia and gynecomastia compared to placebo or standard care [34]. Finerenone is a selective, non-steroidal MRA. A phase 3 clinical trial FIONA (NCT05196035) and open-label extension study in children with CKD and proteinuria aimed at demonstrating that combining finerenone with an ACEi or ARB is safe, well tolerated, and effective in reducing proteinuria, is currently ongoing [35].

In children with Alport syndrome, a retrospective cohort study demonstrated that proteinuria decreased significantly in the first 2 years of treatment with RAAS blockers and gradually returned to its level before the onset of treatment after 3 years of treatment [36], which was in agreement with the findings in the ESCAPE Trial. A multicenter, randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind phase 3 trial (EARLY PRO-TECT Alport trial) demonstrated that ramipril therapy was safe in children with Alport syndrome aged 2 years and older even before kidney damage occurs. Moreover, ramipril therapy decreased the risk of disease progression by almost 50% [37].

In adult patients with Alport syndrome, several retrospective cohort studies showed RAAS blocker therapy delayed the onset of CKD 5 in males with XLAS and genotype–phenotype correlations influenced the response to angiotensin-targeting drugs [38–40]. Nephroprotective effects of RAAS blockers both in patients with ARAS and in heterozygous carriers of XLAS and ARAS were confirmed [41, 42]. Recently, analysis of the natural history of disease in patients with Alport syndrome (NCT02136862) revealed 81% of patients received RAAS blocker therapy. RAAS blockers have been recommended in the guidelines for management of Alport syndrome [43, 44].

Therefore, ACEi and ARB are effective treatments and recommended at the time of diagnosis for both children and adults with rare podocytopathies, such as monogenic SRNS and Alport syndrome, especially for males with XLAS and patients with ARAS.

Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors

Sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) lower serum glucose by enhancing urinary glucose excretion and have shown additional kidney-protective effects in patients with CKD with or without type 2 diabetes. In the DAPA-CKD Trial, the nephroprotective effect of dapagliflozin was compared with placebo in 4304 participants with CKD and proteinuria. The incidence of primary composite outcome (including a sustained decline of ≥ 50% in eGFR, CKD 5, or death from kidney or cardiovascular causes) was significantly lower in the dapagliflozin group than in the placebo group [45] and dapagliflozin significantly reduced urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) compared with placebo [46]. A recent report on the long-term effects of dapagliflozin in CKD using the data from the DAPA-CKD trial demonstrated dapagliflozin delayed time to kidney failure [47]. Empagliflozin, another SGLT2i drug, has been proven to reduce the risk of kidney disease progression or death from cardiovascular causes among 6609 patients with or without diabetes in the EMPA-KIDNEY trial [48]. The additional cardiorenal benefits among patients in the empagliflozin group appeared to last for up to 12 months after it was discontinued in the post-trial follow-up [49]. In a prospective, randomized, open-label crossover clinical trial, the efficacy and safety of combined SGLT2i (dapagliflozin) and MRA (eplerenone) was investigated in adult patients with CKD and proteinuria. The results showed a superior UACR-lowering effect from baseline after 4 weeks of combination treatment with dapagliflozin–eplerenone, and lower incidence of hyperkalemia was found with combination treatment [50]. These studies provided an important basis for recommending SGLT2i in the management of CKD.

Among DAPA-CKD participants, 104 participants were confirmed with FSGS. Further analysis showed that dapagliflozin reduced the rate of eGFR loss compared with placebo in the subgroup of patients with FSGS, although the frequency of monogenic FSGS among these patients was not specified [51]. The protective effects of SGLT2i in non-diabetic CKD are associated with decreasing glomerular hyperfiltration, decreasing inflammatory cytokines and reactive oxygen species, improving lipid profile, reducing body weight, and so on [52].

Data from a case series of 6 adult patients with Alport syndrome and FSGS who underwent SGLT2i therapy revealed the mean UACR decreased by almost 40% from 1827 mg/g before SGLT2i initiation to 1127 mg/g after a mean treatment duration of 4.5 months, with stable levels of serum creatinine and eGFR [53]. The potential effect on reducing the amount of albuminuria by SGLT2i added to ACEi/ARB in patients with Alport syndrome was identified in an international, multicenter, observational study (NCT02378805) [54]. Recently, the effect of combination of SGLT2i dapagliflozin and ARBs was investigated in Col4a5 G5X Alport mice and the results showed dapagliflozin enhanced the nephroprotective effect of losartan but not of olmesartan, indicating that the combination therapy approach needs careful evaluation for maximum benefit [55]. In addition, a preclinical randomized controlled trial in Col4a3-/- mice on triple RAAS/SGLT2/MR blockade demonstrated that average survival time was prolonged significantly compared with the dual RAAS/SGLT2 blockade group and the ramipril group, suggesting an additive renoprotective effect of a triple RAAS/SGLT2/MR inhibition therapy in Alport syndrome [56]. Therefore, SGLT2i is recommended as an additional therapy for adults with Alport syndrome on ACEi/ARB therapy [57].

However, the beneficial effects of SGLT2i on proteinuric CKD were only demonstrated in adults aged 18 years or older in either DAPA-CKD or EMPA-KIDNEY trials, as well as in the observational studies on Alport syndrome. At present, there is no evidence supporting the efficacy or safety of SGLT2i for pediatric monogenic podocytopathies. The ongoing DOUBLE PRO-TECT Alport (NCT05944016) is a multicenter, randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial to assess the efficacy and safety of dapagliflozin in children and young adults with early-stage Alport syndrome [58]. There is another ongoing trial (DAPA-PedHKD; NCT06890143) in children with hereditary kidney disease with proteinuria aiming to evaluate the efficacy and safety of dapagliflozin. These clinical trials will provide more direct evidence for expanding the application of SGLT2i in pediatric patients with monogenic podocytopathies.

Endothelin receptor A antagonists

Endothelin-1 (ET-1) is a vasoactive peptide that is overexpressed and contributes to the pathophysiology of various chronic kidney diseases by binding to endothelin A (ETA) receptor [59, 60]. In experimental mice with adriamycin-induced nephropathy and cultured mouse podocytes, activation of ETA receptor resulted in podocyte damage, slit diaphragm dysfunction, and disruption of the cytoskeleton, and the selective ETA receptor antagonist sitaxsentan reduced proteinuria, ameliorated podocyte foot process architecture, and normalized podocyte number [61, 62].

In the double-blind, randomized, active-controlled, phase 3 study, PROTECT (NCT03762850), the long-term nephroprotective potential of treatment with sparsentan in adult patients with IgA nephropathy and proteinuria was investigated. Sparsentan is a single molecule that can inhibit both the ETA receptor and angiotensin II type 1 (AT1) receptor. The 406 participants were enrolled and randomly assigned to receive sparsentan or irbesartan. Over 110 weeks, treatment with sparsentan versus irbesartan resulted in significant reductions in proteinuria and preservation of kidney function [63].

In patients with FSGS, the efficacy and safety of sparsentan was investigated in a randomized, multicenter, double-blind, parallel, active-control study, DUPLEX. The 371 patients with biopsy-proven FSGS or a pathogenic variant causing FSGS, aged 8 to 75 years old, with UPCR ≥ 1.5 g/g and eGFR ≥ 30 ml/min/1.73 m2 were enrolled and randomly assigned to receive either sparsentan or active control (irbesartan). At 36 weeks, 42% of patients in the sparsentan group and 26% of patients in the irbesartan group showed partial remission of proteinuria. The reduction of proteinuria was sustained throughout the trial period. At week 108, no significant difference in eGFR slope was observed between the two treatment groups [64]. This study indicates that ETA receptor antagonists are effective in reducing proteinuria in FSGS, in both adult and pediatric patients, including those with rare monogenic podocytopathies.

For Alport syndrome, ETA receptor antagonist (sitaxsentan) treatment in Alport mice demonstrated the nephroprotective effects of delayed onset of proteinuria, attenuated kidney histological injury, reduced proinflammatory cytokines, and increased lifespan [65]. The effects of sparsentan, a dual ETA receptor and AT1 receptor antagonist, on kidney and auditory function were studied and compared with those of losartan (ARB) in Alport mice. In addition to its nephroprotective effects, sparsentan reduced the susceptibility to hearing loss. Sparsentan increased lifespan more than losartan did [66]. An observational study on the effect of ambrisentan treatment in 12 adult patients with Alport syndrome showed a significant reduction of 24-h urinary protein after 2 months from baseline [67]. Taken together, these findings indicate an additional antiproteinuric effect of ETA receptor antagonists on top of RAAS blockers in podocytopathies. Two ongoing clinical trials in children (NCT05003986) and in adults (NCT04573920) are assessing the effect of ETA receptor antagonist treatment in patients with proteinuric glomerular diseases including FSGS and Alport syndrome.

Coenzyme Q10 supplementation

Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10) is a key component of the mitochondrial inner membrane respiratory chain [68]. Primary CoQ10 deficiency due to pathogenic variants in genes involved in CoQ10 biosynthesis, mainly COQ2, COQ6, COQ8B, and PDSS2, accounts for about 6% of genetic SRNS cases [69, 70]. A retrospective study on CoQ10 supplementation in SRNS caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in COQ2, COQ6, or COQ8B genes enrolled 116 patients. All of them received oral CoQ10 supplementation. The proteinuria-lowering effect was observed after initiation of CoQ10 supplementation, with an 88% reduction at 12 months. During 5 years of follow-up, proteinuria stayed at about 40% of the baseline level. Moreover, the 5-year CKD 5-free survival rate was 62% in the CoQ10-supplemented patients versus 19% in the untreated patients [71]. However, the 5-year kidney survival rate was not improved in the analysis of a subgroup of 60 patients with COQ8B deficiency, even though a lower annual eGFR decline rate of 7.2 ml/min/1.73 m2 in the treated group versus 17.7 ml/min/1.73 m2 in the untreated group was identified among the 32 pre-CKD 5 COQ8B patients. In addition, the earliest age at CoQ10 treatment initiation was 1 year old and the dose of CoQ10 was between 20 and 30 mg/kg per day in this retrospective study, providing an important reference for the clinical application of COQ10 supplementation. The response to CoQ10 therapy can be also influenced by diet and the intestinal capacity for lipid absorption. Oral CoQ10 therapy was generally safe and well tolerated in this study, with 4% of treated children experiencing gastrointestinal symptoms, which were the main reported adverse effects. These findings highlight that early diagnosis and timely full target dose of CoQ10 supplementation are crucial for patients with SRNS due to primary CoQ10 deficiency.

TRPC6 inhibition

Recently, a multicenter observational study on characteristics of TRPC6-associated podocytopathies showed that 48.9% of patients progressed to kidney failure by median age of 33 years. In addition, in familial patients, gain-of-function variants in TRPC6 were more common (70.8%) [12]. Gain-of-function TRPC6 variants cause overexpression and/or overactivity of TRPC6 leading to podocyte injury [72]. Therapies targeting TRPC6 are currently under development. BI 764198 is a novel, potent and selective small-molecule TRPC6 inhibitor. Phase 1 trials of BI 764198 in healthy volunteers and participants with kidney impairment included 4 clinical trials: a single rising dose (SRD) trial (NCT03854552, n = 54), a multiple rising dose (MRD) trial (NCT04102462, n = 44), relative bioavailability trial (NCT04656288, n = 28), and a single-dose trial in participants with moderate or severe kidney impairment compared with individuals with normal kidney function (NCT04176536, n = 28). The drug-related adverse event (DRAE) rate was 4/54 (7.4%) including headache (n = 3) and diarrhea (n = 1) in the SRD trial. In the MRD trial, DRAE occurred in 5/32 (15.6%) participants receiving BI 764198 and 1/8 (12.5%) receiving placebo. To date, the results of Phase 1 trials have demonstrated BI 764198 is well tolerated [73]. A Phase 2 study assessing the efficacy and safety of BI 764198 administered for 12 weeks in patients aged 18 to 75 years with biopsy-proven primary FSGS or TRPC6 gene pathogenic variants associated with FSGS, UPCR ≥ 1000 mg/g and eGFR ≥ 30 ml/min/1.73 m2, is ongoing (NCT05213624).

Gene therapy

Gene therapy is a highly promising therapeutic strategy for curing monogenic podocytopathies through multiple approaches, including gene replacement, gene editing, and RNA interference. However, several significant challenges for its clinical translation remain. First of all, developing efficient and specific delivery systems for gene therapy targeting podocytes is challenging, because of the restrictive nature of the glomerular filtration barrier. Other challenges are common to all gene therapy applications, such as ethical and safety concerns including off-target effects and unintended immune responses. With the continuous advancement in gene delivery systems and editing technologies and a more profound understanding of kidney biology, gene therapy offers the possibility of avoiding kidney transplantation for patients with progressive rare monogenic podocytopathies, marked by a rising number of preclinical studies in this area.

A study using adeno-associated virus (AAV)–based gene therapy to rescue SRNS caused by pathogenic variants in NPHS2 demonstrated AAV-LK03-mediated transduction of podocin resulted in the expression of human podocin and functional rescue in vitro in mutant podocin R138Q human podocytes [74]. The AAV 2/9 on mouse podocytes showed a superior transduction efficiency in vitro when compared with AAV-LK03. Furthermore, the effect of intravenous injection of AAV 2/9 gene transfer was investigated in the conditional NPHS2 knockout nephrotic mice; both a prophylactic approach before induction of disease and a therapeutic approach 2 weeks after onset of proteinuria demonstrated a significant reduction in albuminuria and amelioration of histological kidney injury, as well as improvement of long-term survival [74]. In another study using a mouse model (Sgpl1-KO mice) characterized by SRNS and FSGS with multisystemic manifestations, administering a single dose of AAV9 containing the human SGPL1 in the first days of life as a single treatment in newborn Sgpl1-KO pups prevented nephrosis and prolonged survival; 137 days compared with 11 days in the untreated group. Anti-AAV antibodies were detected in treated mice in this study [75]. These findings demonstrate that AAV-based gene therapy could be a potential therapeutic approach for SRNS associated with monogenic podocytopathies, although several challenges remain, including delivery, immunogenicity, and safety concerns [76].

Exon skipping therapy has emerged as a promising approach for Alport syndrome, which can bypass the mutant exon inducing a premature stop codon and produce a shorter but in-frame mRNA transcript. The basis of this treatment strategy is that patients with COL4A5 gene splice mutations leading to an in-frame deletion have milder symptoms and slower progression to CKD 5 than patients with truncating variants, e.g., nonsense variants or frameshifts [77]. The efficiency of antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)–mediated exon skipping was evaluated firstly in a mouse model carrying a nonsense variant in exon 21 in the COL4A5 gene [78]. At 21 weeks of age, compared with the vehicle-treated mice, ASO-treated mice showed remarkably lower levels of UACR, serum creatinine, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN), restored α5 (IV) expression in the glomeruli, and a significantly prolonged survival period [78]. Recently, researchers further investigated whether exon skipping therapy after disease onset has renoprotective effects by developing a tamoxifen-inducible, podocyte-specific exon 21-skipping model, which carried a nonsense variant in exon 21 in the COL4A5 gene. Their findings emphasized that exon skipping had therapeutic potential in progressive stages of Alport syndrome, providing a more appropriate time window for therapeutic interventions in clinical translation [79].

Nonsense readthrough therapy is another promising approach to restore the expression of full-length proteins. Omachi et al. [80] explored the feasibility of premature termination codon (PTC) readthrough for Alport syndrome and identified eleven of the 49 nonsense variants in COL4A5 reported in patients were susceptible to PTC readthrough using various readthrough drugs. They tested a set of next-generation PTC readthrough drugs, among which ELX-02, a designer aminoglycoside, showed the highest PTC readthrough activity and is expected to exhibit reduced toxicity. Currently, the safety and efficacy of ELX-02 in patients with Alport syndrome harboring nonsense pathogenic variants is being evaluated in an ongoing phase 2 study (NCT05448755).

A recent study by Omachi et al. evaluated the therapeutic potential of anticodon-edited transfer RNAs (ACE-tRNAs), known as nonsense suppressor tRNAs, for PTC readthrough of COL4A5 nonsense pathogenic variants. They found that ACE-tRNAs mediated significantly greater PTC readthrough efficiency (about 60%) than the results shown in the previous study conducted under the same experimental conditions using various readthrough drugs (about 30%) and induced full-length COL4A5 protein expression in cultured cells containing nonsense mutations [81]. These findings indicate that PTC readthrough therapy has become an increasingly realistic approach in nonsense variant-driven Alport syndrome.

The gene-editing technology mediated by CRISPR/Cas9 or adenine base editors (ABEs) is a revolutionary tool for research on gene therapy, offering potential clinical applications [82]. A preclinical study evaluated the feasibility of using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to correct two disease-causative variants (c.1871G>A, p.G624D in COL4A5 and c.2567G>A, p.G856E in COL4A3) in urine-derived podocyte-lineage cells from two patients with Alport syndrome. The results demonstrated the correction efficiency was very high, 58.8% in reverting the causative variant in COL4A5 and 44.2% for COL4A3 variant with a small percentage of off-target indels (8.8% for COL4A5 and 10.4% for COL4A3) [83]. Recently, ABE therapy repaired 13% of the pathogenic alleles and effectively reduced urinary oxalate levels and kidney crystal deposition in AgxtQ84X rats, a model of primary hyperoxaluria type 1 (PH1) [84]. These findings raise new hope for personalized medicine and highlight both the prospect and the challenges of base editing at its present stage of development.

Additionally, microRNA 21 (miR-21) which is a small non-coding RNA has been reported to be elevated in kidney and positively correlated with 24-h urine protein, serum creatinine, BUN, and severity of kidney pathology in Alport mice as well as in patients with Alport syndrome [85]. Anti-miR-21 on top of ACEi showed an additive effect to reduce proteinuria and fibrosis, to preserve kidney function and prolong survival time in Alport mice [86]. Based on these promising findings, a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase 2 trial (the HERA clinical trial, NCT02855268) aimed to assess the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of lademirsen (anti-miR-21) therapy and enrolled 43 patients with Alport syndrome who were at risk of rapidly progressive disease (UACR > 1000 mg/g, eGFR between 35 and 90 ml/min/1.73 m2 and an annual eGFR decline of ≥ 4 ml/min/1.73 m2). However, the study was terminated after a planned interim analysis of 28 participants who completed 48 weeks of double-blind treatment due to no significant improvement in kidney function [87]. There are other clinical trials (NCT04536688, NCT05521191) investigating the safety and tolerability of anti-miR-17 oligonucleotides in autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD). Therefore, antisense oligonucleotides targeting specific microRNAs that post-transcriptionally regulate gene expression represent a potential strategy for individualized therapy in rare monogenic podocytopathies.

Although basic research on gene therapy for kidney diseases has achieved promising results, the translation into clinical application still faces many difficulties [88–91]. Taking Alport syndrome as an example: First, a significant hurdle that must be addressed is to design and screen AAV vectors with capacity for packaging the COL4A3/4/5 genes and podocyte-specific targeting. Second, it is important to evaluate the transduction efficiency of podocytes by different gene delivery routes, such as intravenous injection, local injection via the renal artery or renal pelvis, or subcutaneous administration. Third, assessments for acute and long-term safety of gene therapy continue to face challenges, such as the immunogenicity of AAV, immune responses against the type IV collagen produced by delivered genes, hepatotoxicity, and potential oncogenic risks. In addition, clinical translation involves regulatory hurdles, including establishing links between animal model experiments and clinical applications in terms of dosage and timing of treatment, to integrate clinical trial costs while ensuring efficacy and safety, and so on.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The field of podocytopathies that result from podocyte injury and loss has been advancing rapidly over the last several decades. In particular, about 30% of SRNS cases are currently diagnosed based upon the genetic variants as monogenic podocytopathies. Despite patients associated with each gene pathogenic variant being rare, the continued case accumulation and improved understanding of the disease course and disease pathogenesis enable less toxic and more personalized podocyte-targeted treatments, such as coenzyme Q10 supplementation for mitochondrial podocytopathies or exon skipping therapy for males with Alport syndrome with truncating variants in COL4A5, although the efficacy of the latter has been proven in mouse model only. The remarkable progress in the field of treatment options for Alport syndrome demonstrated in Fig. 1 may serve as a template for other rare podocytopathies [92–94], with the Alport Syndrome Alliance uniting patients, physicians, basic scientists, and pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies to advance both Alport research and its clinical translation. There is still an urgent need to identify specific, early, and prognostic risk markers, as well as predictive biomarkers of drug response. In the future, both clinical trials exploring the utility of agents like glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists for anti-inflammation and anti-fibrosis targeting the final common pathway for CKD, and preclinical and clinical trials targeting molecules or proteins encoded by specific mutational genes will hopefully lead to reduce the risk of kidney failure for patients with rare monogenic podocytopathies.Fig. 1The illustration of therapeutic pathways in Alport syndrome.
