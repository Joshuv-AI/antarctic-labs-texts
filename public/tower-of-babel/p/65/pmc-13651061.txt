Artificial intelligence for detection, prediction, and severity classification of diabetic peripheral neuropathy: a systematic review
Authors: Jia Ying Tay, Yee Gary Ang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN) is a common complication of diabetes and an important contributor to foot ulceration and lower limb amputation. Early detection remains challenging because conventional screening methods are often subjective, resource-intensive, and insensitive to subclinical disease. Artificial intelligence (AI) has increasingly been applied to support DPN assessment, but its applications across different clinical tasks have not been clearly synthesised. This systematic review evaluates AI applications for DPN detection, prognostic prediction, risk stratification, and severity classification.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore for studies published from January 2021 to 1 September 2025. Studies developing or validating AI models for DPN assessment using patient datasets were included. Study selection was performed by one reviewer, with a second reviewer contributing to uncertain inclusion decisions. Data extraction and risk-of-bias assessment were performed by one reviewer and verified by another using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST). Due to heterogeneity in clinical tasks, data types, outcome definitions, and validation approaches, findings were synthesised narratively.ResultsTwenty-six studies were included and stratified by clinical task: diagnostic detection of existing DPN, prognostic prediction or risk stratification, and severity classification. Diagnostic detection studies mainly used corneal confocal microscopy, plantar pressure or gait-based assessments, nerve conduction studies, electromyography, and selected clinical variables. Prognostic prediction and risk stratification studies relied mainly on electronic medical record variables, including age, diabetes duration, glycated haemoglobin, renal markers, inflammatory markers, and cardiovascular risk factors. Severity classification studies used electrophysiological measures, structured clinical examination findings, and symptom-based scores. Across tasks, age, diabetes duration, and glycaemic control were recurrent predictors, while imaging, gait, plantar pressure, and electrophysiological features were more task-specific. Reported internal performance was often high, but most studies used small or single-centre datasets, heterogeneous reference standards, and limited reporting of predictor importance. External validation was uncommon, and risk of bias was frequently high, particularly in the analysis domain.ConclusionsAI approaches for DPN assessment show promise, but interpretation depends on task. Future studies should develop task-specific models, report predictor importance transparently, and undertake external and prospective validation before routine clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03676-x.Keywords: Diabetic peripheral neuropathy, Artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Risk stratification, Prognostic prediction, Severity classification

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) is a prevalent disease worldwide, projected to affect over 1.3 billion people by 2050 [1]. It leads to macro and microvascular complications with high morbidity and burden of care. Diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DPN), typically diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy, is a common microvascular complication affecting 1 in 3 persons with diabetes [2]. Peripheral nerve damage results in loss of sensation, pain and impaired gait. If untreated, it can lead to foot ulcers and eventual amputation. Tight glycaemic control can delay neuropathy onset [3] and 85% of amputations can be avoided through regular foot screening [4], thus screening for DPN is crucial for timely intervention via patient education and footcare.

The American Diabetes Association, similar to Singapore’s Agency for Care Effectiveness guidelines, recommends annual screening for DPN, comprising history and physical examination for symptoms and signs [5, 6]. Tests include vibration perception, pinprick or temperature perception and protective sensation. However, these methods are subjective and unreliable in early and subclinical DPN despite up to 50% of DPN being asymptomatic [5]. Nerve conduction study is the gold standard for diagnosis, it reliably detects large fibre damage but not small fibre damage which precedes it [7, 8]. Furthermore, it is resource intensive and reserved for diagnosis of complex cases. Given the high patient load and frequency of screening, diabetic foot screening faces the challenge of being a manpower and time-consuming process.

Artificial intelligence (AI) has been applied to aid diabetic complication screening. In particular, studies have established strong performance in image-based detection of diabetic foot ulcers and diabetic retinopathy [9, 10]. Machine learning models can analyse large-scale data and identify complex patterns, drawing useful predictions that enable risk stratification and personalised intervention [11]. Leveraging on physiological data facilitates automated and accurate disease detection, improving screening efficiency and reducing manpower burdens. As healthcare moves beyond detecting diabetic foot ulcers towards upstream prevention, literature targeting DPN, the precursor to ulcers, have emerged. Systematic reviews have broadly covered diabetic foot complications or microvascular complications, focusing on diabetic foot ulcers and retinopathy but there has yet to be a review comprehensively covering the various aspects of diabetic neuropathy screening [10, 12].

Therefore, this review aims to summarise AI applications for DPN according to clinical task, including diagnostic detection, prognostic prediction, risk stratification, and severity classification. Its objectives are to evaluate model performance, input features, reported predictor importance, validation methods, and task-specific implications for clinical translation.

Methods

Study design

Following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [13], a systematic review of literature on AI use for DPN detection, prognostic prediction, risk stratification, or severity classification was conducted. Ethics approval was not required since data was publicly available. The research question was crafted using population, intervention, control, and outcomes (PICO) format as shown in Table 1.

Data sources and search strategy

A literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, and IEEE Xplore up to 1 September 2025. The first author performed the search, and the second author supported study selection for uncertain studies and inclusion decisions. Additionally, backward and forward citation searching were conducted by examining the citation list of included studies and their ‘cited by’ records. Search strategy was constructed using medical subject headings (MeSH) and keywords combined using Boolean operators. Keywords included “diabetes, diabetic foot, diabetic neuropathy, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, artificial intelligence, machine learning, deep learning, screening, early detection, risk stratification, risk assessment, prediction” alongside their variations. The full search strategy was translated across databases (Appendix A). The selection of databases was intended to capture both clinical and technical literature relevant to artificial intelligence applications in diabetic peripheral neuropathy screening. PubMed was used for comprehensive coverage of biomedical research [14], while Scopus provided broad multidisciplinary indexing [15]. IEEE Xplore was included to identify engineering and machine learning studies that may not be indexed in traditional biomedical databases. Other sources, including trial registries and grey literature, were not systematically searched. This may have resulted in omission of unpublished or ongoing studies.

Only studies in English language were included and results were exported to Zotero (version 6.0.37) [16].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were included if they involved adults aged 18 years and above with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, used AI-based tools for DPN detection, prognostic prediction, risk stratification, or severity classification, reported model performance using patient datasets, and were published in English.

To capture contemporary developments in artificial intelligence methodologies and recent model development relevant to current clinical and technical practice, only studies published from 2021 onwards were included. This restriction may have excluded earlier foundational studies and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Exclusion criteria were studies involving only non-diabetic participants, not using AI, involving only monitoring, treatment or outcomes of diabetic neuropathy without features of screening. Studies involving diabetic foot screening without neuropathy-specific outcomes were excluded. Case reports, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, letters, books, purely theoretical or computational models and papers with unavailable full-text were also excluded.

Study selection

Literature screening was undertaken by TJY. Duplicates were first removed via Zotero (version 6.0.37) [16], after which the remaining records were screened based on the relevance of titles and abstracts. Full texts of potentially eligible articles were then reviewed. AYG contributed to decisions on uncertain studies and inclusion eligibility. Where disagreement remained, discussion was used to reach consensus.

Data extraction and analysis

Data were recorded by one reviewer and verified by another reviewer. Information extracted from each study included author, publication year, study design, participant characteristics, data source, sample size and input modality. Key AI model types, performance metrics, ground truth reference, final input variables and validation methods were also recorded where available. Data were grouped primarily according to clinical task, namely diagnostic detection, risk prediction, and severity classification, with modality-specific features described within each category where relevant. Due to heterogeneity in methodology and outcomes reported, a meta-analysis was not performed.

For the purpose of synthesis, each included study was mapped to its primary clinical task before analysis and this task classification was used to structure data tabulation, narrative synthesis and interpretation.

Where studies addressed more than one task, the dominant task was identified based on the study objective, outcome definition, and reference standard; secondary tasks were noted separately. Predictors and input features were then analysed within each clinical task, including their frequency of use, reported importance where available, overlap across tasks, and relationship with established clinical knowledge.

Quality assessment

Risk of bias and applicability of each study was assessed using the Prediction Model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST) for diagnostic and prognostic prediction model development or validation [17]. This was assessed by one reviewer and verified by a second reviewer. Any inconsistencies were discussed to reach consensus. PROBAST assesses risk of bias across four domains (participants, predictors, outcomes, and analysis), and applicability to the review question across three domains (participants, predictors, and outcomes). Each study was rated as high, low, or unclear risk according to 20 signalling questions, and overall risk was evaluated. High overall risk was assigned if one or more domains had high risk of bias.

Results

Study selection

The search strategy captured 503 articles in total, of which 44 duplicates were removed. 347 out of 459 articles were excluded from titles and abstracts screen. 110 full-text articles were retrieved and assessed using eligibility criteria. 84 articles were excluded for wrong intervention not involving screening DPN, wrong outcomes, wrong patient population and published before 2021. 26 studies were included in the final review (Fig. 1). The included studies addressed three conceptually distinct tasks: diagnostic detection of existing DPN, prognostic prediction of future DPN or elevated risk, and severity classification. Because these tasks differ methodologically and clinically, findings are interpreted within task type rather than treated as directly comparable.

PRISMA flow diagram showing study selection process

Study characteristics

Prior methodological work has suggested that conventional machine learning algorithms can classify diabetic sensorimotor polyneuropathy severity using nerve conduction study data [18]. Study characteristics are summarised in Table 2 according to primary clinical task. Of the 26 included studies, most addressed diagnostic detection of existing DPN or prognostic prediction / risk stratification, while fewer focused primarily on severity classification. Several studies addressed more than one task and were labelled accordingly. Diagnostic detection studies used corneal confocal microscopy, plantar pressure or gait-based assessments, movement sensors, microcirculatory parameters, electrophysiological measurements, and electronic medical record variables [19–29, 39, 40, 42, 43]. Prognostic prediction or risk stratification studies mainly used electronic medical record variables, biomarkers, clinical characteristics, and diabetes history [30–37]. Severity classification studies used MNSI variables, nerve conduction studies, electromyography, and plantar pressure or gait characteristics [38–41]. Study designs were mostly retrospective or cross-sectional, with few prospective studies and limited external validation [30, 31, 33, 34, 38, 44].

Risk of bias

All 26 studies were assessed using PROBAST: 23 were assessed as model development studies and three were additionally assessed for external validation [31, 34, 38]. Overall, risk of bias was frequently high (Fig. 2), while applicability concerns varied across studies (Table 3). Nineteen studies were rated as having high risk of bias, six as low risk of bias, and one as unclear risk of bias. For applicability, 15 studies were rated as having low concern, 10 as high concern, and one as unclear concern. The main contributor to high risk of bias was the analysis domain, particularly small sample size, exclusion of participants with missing data, and inadequate handling of missing data. Several studies also had concerns in the participants domain because data were derived from existing trials, hospital inpatients, or specialised diabetes clinics, which may limit applicability to community or primary care populations. Outcome-related concerns were identified where DPN definitions were unclear, non-standardised, or incorporated predictor information.

Results analysis by clinical task

To address heterogeneity in intended clinical use, the core synthesis was organised by clinical task rather than modality alone. This allowed predictors and model performance to be interpreted in relation to the intended use of each model: identifying existing DPN, estimating future or elevated risk, or classifying established disease severity.

Diagnostic detection of existing DPN

Studies in this category used AI models to identify existing DPN or distinguish DPN from non-DPN at or near the time of assessment. Diagnostic detection models used a range of inputs, including CCM images, plantar pressure and gait characteristics, movement sensor data, microcirculatory parameters, EMG, NCS, and selected clinical or electronic medical record variables [19–29, 39, 40, 42, 43]. CCM-based models used images of the corneal sub-basal nerve plexus and reported high internal discrimination, with AUC values around 0.94–0.95 [19–21]. This is clinically plausible because corneal nerve fibre changes may reflect small-fibre neuropathy, which can occur early in diabetic neuropathy [8, 45–48].

Plantar pressure, gait, and movement-based diagnostic models used features such as pressure distribution, pressure-time integral, reaction time, centre-of-pressure features, movement spectral features, and temperature distribution [22–24, 42, 43]. These features reflect functional consequences of neuropathy, including altered weight-bearing, impaired proprioception, and abnormal gait. Microcirculatory models used post-occlusion reactive hyperaemia, local thermal hyperaemia, transcutaneous oxygen pressure, and vascular indices, reflecting the vascular contribution to diabetic neuropathy [25]. Electrophysiological and EMG-based models used nerve conduction velocity, amplitude, latency, and muscle signal features, which are clinically proximal to large-fibre nerve dysfunction [39, 40, 44].

Across diagnostic detection studies, overlap with prognostic models was strongest for age, diabetes duration, and HbA1c, particularly in models that incorporated clinical or EMR variables [26–29]. However, the most task-specific diagnostic features were imaging, plantar pressure, gait, microcirculatory, and electrophysiological measurements. This pattern is clinically coherent because detection of existing DPN relies on markers of current structural or functional nerve impairment rather than systemic risk factors alone [7, 8, 45–47].

Prognostic prediction and risk stratification

Studies in this category used AI to estimate future DPN occurrence or identify patients at increased risk. Most relied on electronic medical records or routinely collected clinical variables [30–37]. Commonly used predictors included age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, renal function markers, albuminuria, lipid profile, uric acid, blood pressure, inflammatory markers such as NLR and CRP, and liver-related markers such as GGT [27, 28, 30, 32–36].

Where predictor importance was reported, age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, serum creatinine, albuminuria, uric acid, CRP, GGT, NLR, and urinary albumin-creatinine ratio were recurrently identified as influential predictors [27–29, 32–34, 36]. Vitamin D was reported as protective in some models, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously because of potential confounding and inconsistent measurement across populations [32, 33].

These predictors are broadly consistent with established clinical knowledge. Diabetes duration and HbA1c reflect cumulative glycaemic exposure, while renal markers and albuminuria may reflect broader microvascular disease burden [2, 3, 5, 45]. Inflammatory markers are biologically plausible because chronic inflammation and oxidative stress have been implicated in diabetic neuropathy [2, 33, 45, 46]. The overlap between prognostic and diagnostic tasks was strongest for age, diabetes duration, and HbA1c, whereas prognostic models relied less on direct neurophysiological or imaging features. This distinction is clinically important because prognostic models estimate future risk using upstream systemic risk factors, while diagnostic models detect existing nerve damage using more proximal physiological markers.

Severity classification

Studies in this category used AI to classify DPN severity or neuropathy subtype rather than simply identify disease presence or future risk. Severity classification models commonly used MNSI variables, nerve conduction parameters, EMG signals, vibration perception, monofilament findings, foot appearance, deformities, fissures, and plantar pressure or gait characteristics [38–41].

Reported performance was generally high, particularly when electrophysiological variables were used [39]. This is expected because nerve conduction and EMG features directly reflect the degree and type of peripheral nerve dysfunction [7, 47]. However, models based on clinical signs and symptoms may be less sensitive for mild or early DPN. Haque et al. reported strong performance using MNSI data but noted misclassification of mild DPN, suggesting that early disease remains difficult to classify using bedside features alone [38].

Predictor overlap between severity classification and diagnostic detection was greater than between severity classification and prognostic prediction. Both diagnostic and severity models used direct measures of nerve dysfunction, including nerve conduction parameters, pressure or gait abnormalities, and structured clinical examination findings [38–43]. Severity classification models differed from prognostic models because they focused on staging established disease rather than estimating future onset.

Cross-task interpretation

Across all tasks, age, diabetes duration, and HbA1c were the most consistent cross-task predictors [26–28, 32–34, 36]. These variables are clinically plausible because they reflect cumulative exposure to diabetes-related metabolic injury [2, 3, 5, 45]. However, the most informative features differed by task. Diagnostic detection relied on contemporaneous physiological and imaging markers, prognostic prediction relied on systemic metabolic, renal, inflammatory, and vascular risk factors, and severity classification relied on markers of established neuropathic dysfunction [18–44].

This task-specific pattern indicates that AI models for DPN should not be interpreted as interchangeable screening tools. A model developed to detect existing DPN may not be suitable for predicting incident DPN, and a severity classification model may not be appropriate for population-level screening. Future studies should therefore clearly define the intended clinical task before model development, select predictors aligned with that task, and validate performance in the corresponding clinical setting [11, 17].

Reported predictor importance was available mainly in studies using explainability or feature-ranking methods such as SHAP, ELI5, LIME, or model-based importance scores. In diagnostic detection studies using clinical or EMR variables, important predictors included UCr, disease duration, D-dimer, albumin, CRP, GGT, age, lipoprotein(a), HbA1c, UACR, and serum creatinine [26–29]. In prognostic prediction or risk stratification studies, important predictors included comorbidities, glycaemic control, physical activity, HbA1c, diabetes duration, vitamin D, cystatin C, CRP, NLR, albuminuria, uric acid, GGT, serum creatinine, hypertension, and PNI [30, 32–36]. In severity classification studies, important predictors were more often direct markers of neuropathic impairment, including MNSI examination features, NCV variables, EMG features, and plantar pressure features [38–41].

Discussion

This review demonstrates that AI applications in DPN are heterogeneous and should be interpreted according to their intended clinical task. The included studies addressed three distinct tasks: diagnostic detection of existing DPN, prognostic prediction or risk stratification, and severity classification.

These tasks differ in clinical purpose, predictor requirements, reference standards, and implementation pathways. Therefore, direct comparison of model performance across tasks is inappropriate [11, 17].

Diagnostic detection models generally used features biologically close to existing neuropathy, including CCM-derived nerve fibre patterns, plantar pressure or gait abnormalities, movement sensor features, microcirculatory parameters, nerve conduction parameters, EMG signals, and structured clinical examination findings [19–29, 39, 40, 42, 43]. These features are clinically plausible because they measure structural, vascular, or functional consequences of neuropathy [7, 8, 45–48]. The high internal performance reported by many diagnostic models is therefore not surprising, but it should be interpreted cautiously because of small sample sizes, internal validation, and heterogeneous reference standards.

Prognostic prediction and risk stratification models used a different set of predictors. They relied mainly on systemic risk factors available in electronic medical records, such as age, diabetes duration, HbA1c, renal function, albuminuria, blood pressure, lipids, inflammatory markers, and metabolic markers [30–37]. This aligns with the pathophysiology of DPN, where chronic hyperglycaemia, microvascular disease, oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction contribute to nerve injury over time [2, 3, 5, 45, 46]. However, these models remain insufficiently validated for clinical deployment. Their role is likely to be population-level risk stratification, identification of patients needing intensified follow-up, or prioritisation for confirmatory assessment rather than standalone diagnosis.

Severity classification models should be interpreted separately from both diagnostic and prognostic models. These models classify the extent or subtype of established neuropathy and frequently use nerve conduction, EMG, MNSI variables, vibration perception, monofilament findings, and symptom scores [38–41]. Their clinical value may lie in staging, monitoring, or specialist triage. However, they should not be conflated with screening models for asymptomatic patients, particularly because conventional bedside tools may have limited sensitivity for early or small-fibre neuropathy [7, 8, 46, 47].

Across tasks, age, diabetes duration, and HbA1c were the most consistent overlapping predictors [26–28, 32–34, 36]. Their recurrence across diagnostic and prognostic models is consistent with established clinical knowledge and supports their role as general markers of cumulative neuropathy risk [2, 3, 5, 45, 49, 50]. However, the most discriminative or task-specific predictors differed. Imaging and electrophysiological features were more relevant to diagnostic detection and severity classification, while renal, inflammatory, and metabolic markers were more relevant to prognostic prediction [18–44]. This distinction is important because predictor importance should be interpreted in relation to the model’s intended clinical task.

The included studies were limited by small and single-centre cohorts, with data collected from hospital inpatient records and specialised diabetes clinics. Few studies included only patients without DPN diagnosis at baseline [30, 31, 34, 44]. Patients with more severe or longer diabetes duration were likely overrepresented, thus less representative of a primary care setting. Furthermore, reference outcomes used varied and could not be adequately accounted for when comparing model performance. Six studies used the gold standard, nerve conduction study, while nine studies used the Toronto consensus criteria which also included nerve conduction study. Other studies relied on physical examination and symptoms which are less sensitive, possibly underestimating DPN prevalence [47, 51]. Establishment of a comprehensive and standardised diagnostic protocol that captures different DPN stages could be helpful. A further limitation is that some included studies did not clearly distinguish between diagnostic detection, risk prediction, and severity classification, requiring task assignment based on stated objectives, outcome definitions, reference standards, and timing of predictor and outcome measurement.

A further methodological limitation relates to the use of the original PROBAST tool for risk-of-bias assessment. Although PROBAST is an established framework for assessing diagnostic and prognostic prediction model studies, recent methodological guidance has recognised the need to extend PROBAST for studies using artificial intelligence and machine learning methods. PROBAST+AI was developed to update PROBAST-2019 and provides a more explicit framework for assessing quality, risk of bias, and applicability across prediction models using either regression-based or artificial intelligence methods [52]. Therefore, while PROBAST provided a structured and transparent approach for this review, it may not fully capture AI-specific methodological concerns, including data leakage during model development, preprocessing and augmentation choices, hyperparameter tuning, model architecture selection, class imbalance handling, calibration, explainability, fairness, reproducibility, dataset shift, and deployment-related performance drift. The PROBAST findings in this review should therefore be interpreted alongside these AI-specific considerations.

Lastly, reporting of performance metrics was inconsistent. Different combinations of discrimination metrics were reported, commonly AUC, sensitivity and accuracy but not specificity. Few studies reported 95% confidence intervals, limiting fair comparison. In addition, the search strategy was limited to selected biomedical and engineering databases and did not include trial registries or grey literature, which may have resulted in incomplete capture of relevant studies.

Future studies could therefore include multi-centre studies with larger and more heterogeneous populations. Established models could be externally validated in outpatient community settings to assess model generalisability and applied to prospective studies in the long-term. To balance model performance with real-world feasibility and cost-effectiveness, integration of multimodal clinical data may be a useful direction for future research.

A meta-analysis was not performed because of substantial methodological heterogeneity across studies, including differences in model objectives, outcome definitions, predictor sets, and validation approaches. While this review focused primarily on model performance, other aspects such as explainability, clinical utility, implementation feasibility, and impact on patient outcomes remain to be assessed before clinical translation. Nevertheless, this review highlights both the potential and the current methodological limitations of AI-based tools for DPN assessment.

Conclusion

This systematic review summarises AI applications for DPN across three distinct clinical tasks: diagnostic detection, prognostic prediction or risk stratification, and severity classification. Diagnostic detection models mainly used direct physiological or imaging markers of neuropathy, prognostic models relied on systemic metabolic, renal, inflammatory, and vascular risk factors, and severity classification models used features reflecting established neuropathic dysfunction [18–44]. Age, diabetes duration, and HbA1c were the most consistent cross-task predictors and align with established DPN risk factors [2, 3, 5, 45]. Current evidence remains limited by methodological heterogeneity, high risk of bias, small single-centre datasets, inconsistent reference standards, and reliance on internal validation. Larger, externally validated, and prospectively evaluated task-specific studies are required before these models can be considered for routine clinical implementation [11, 17] in DPN care pathways.
