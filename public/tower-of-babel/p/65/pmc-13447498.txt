Supplementary Motor Area Meningioma and Postoperative Hemorrhage Presenting With Acquired Narcissistic Personality Traits: A Case Report
Authors: So Jin Kwak, Sangwoo Kim, Dae Hyun Kim, Won Hyuk Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMeningiomas involving the supplementary motor area (SMA) are known to cause motor deficits, but acquired narcissistic personality changes following such lesions rarely described. We report a rare case of a 63-year-old woman who developed acquired narcissistic personality traits following the resection of a right frontal parasagittal meningioma involving the SMA, complicated by postoperative hemorrhage. Although previously cooperative, she manifested transient grandiosity, entitlement, and lack of empathy, temporarily meeting Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 criteria. Her symptoms improved with pharmacotherapy and rehabilitation. We hypothesize that while the SMA is not a canonical hub in the triple network model, this focal damage induced connectional diaschisis, disrupting functional connectivity to distant Salience and Default Mode Network hubs implicated in social cognition. This case suggests that acquired narcissistic personality traits can emerge from lesions in non-hub regions through remote network dysfunction, highlighting the importance of evaluating functional connectivity in acquired personality disorders.Keywords: Narcissistic Personality Disorder, Supplementary Motor Area, Triple-Network Model, Connectional Diaschisis

INTRODUCTION

The supplementary motor area (SMA), is classically associated with motor initiation, volitional control, and effortful action [1]. The classic postoperative presentation following SMA lesion, known as SMA syndrome, is characterized by contralateral akinesia and mutism with preserved muscle strength, resolving completely within weeks to months [2]. Beyond these well-recognized motor deficits, however, lesions involving SMA and adjacent medial frontal regions have been associated with behavioral and personality changes, including disinhibition, impulsivity, and alterations in social conduct [3,4].

Among the behavioral sequelae reported following medial frontal lesions, narcissistic personality features, involving disturbance in self-perception and a disruption in the regulation of self-esteem, have rarely been described [5]. Recent neuroimaging studies have identified distinct structural and functional neural correlates that underpin the core phenotypic traits of the disorder. Schulze et al. [6] were the first to report gray-matter volume reductions in fronto-paralimbic circuits, including the anterior insula, anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), and dorsolateral prefrontal cortices (DLPFC) in patients with narcissistic personality disorder (NPD) via voxel-based morphometry. Menon [7] subsequently proposed the triple network model, in which the disruption of switching among the Default Mode Network (DMN; self-referential thought), Central Executive Network (CEN; executive control), and Salience Network (SN; salience detection and dynamic gating between DMN and CEN) underlies a variety of psychiatric disorders, including NPD.

However, the precise relationship between these neural correlates and narcissistic personality traits remains to be fully elucidated, and individual variations in their expression are expected. This report details a patient who manifested acquired narcissistic personality traits following surgical intervention for a brain tumor in the SMA.

CASE DESCRIPTION

A 63-year-old female patient was admitted to the hospital with a complaint of headaches and underwent a brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), which revealed a 2.7 cm extra-axial enhancing mass in the right parasagittal frontal region (Fig. 1A). This finding was consistent with a meningioma involving the SMA. The patient exhibited no focal neurological deficits. Consequently, a craniotomy and tumor removal procedure was performed. Immediately after the operation, there was a significant decline in the strength of her left lower extremity, as indicated by a decrease from a manual muscle test (MMT) trace grade to complete paralysis (zero grade). This resulted in the urgent need for a hematoma evacuation on the next day. A brain computed tomography scan revealed a right frontal intraparenchymal hematoma involving the SMA (Fig. 1B). Subsequent MRI scans taken after the hematoma was evacuated showed resolution of the hematoma, along with postoperative changes in the same region (Fig. 1C).

At two weeks after the 1st operation, the patient was transferred to the department of Physical and Rehabilitation Medicine (PRM). The patient remained consistently alert and oriented to time, place, and person throughout the postoperative period. A physical examination revealed that her upper extremities and right leg exhibited normal strength, while her left hip flexors demonstrated poor grade, her knee extensors exhibited fair grade, and her ankle dorsiflexors exhibited trace grade by MMT. She demonstrated an excessive degree of confidence, asserting that she would be walking soon, often voicing dissatisfaction and engaging in interpersonal conflict.

According to the patient’s family, she had previously worked as a civil servant in the education sector and was described as a calm, quiet person who rarely spoke about herself. She reportedly had no prior history of interpersonal difficulties with others or within the family and was instead considered to be thoughtful and considerate toward others. However, it was noted that postoperatively, she began to display prominent narcissistic personality traits. Initially, these traits were most prominently directed toward the caregiver, including her husband and son. She frequently engaged in loud verbal altercations with her spouse in the hospital room, to the extent that a roommate requested transfer to another room due to noise, which did not appear to concern the patient. After her son became the primary caregiver, the patient’s aggressive behaviors escalated. During transfers to the therapy room, she threw objects at her son and bit him, necessitating intervention by security staff and administration of sedative medication. The patient also exhibited significant conflict with the specific medical staff members who did not comply with her demands. When her demands were not immediately met in the therapy room, she spat on the floor and verbally abused the therapist by name, which severely interfered with the provision of therapy. On the ward, she repeatedly pressed the call bell without a clear medical need and persistently complained at the nurses’ station that her feelings were not being acknowledged. She persistently claimed that, as a paying patient, she was in the position of a “gap (甲)”—a term in Korean culture referring to the party with superior status in a contractual relationship—and that she was not being treated accordingly. Her adherence to both medication and rehabilitation therapy was poor; she wrote down the components of each prescribed medication, selectively chose which medications to take, and likewise insisted on picking specific rehabilitation interventions. Of note, the patient demonstrated well-preserved attention, maintaining focus for over 30 minutes during her preferred rehabilitation programs. Furthermore, while formal cognitive evaluation was precluded by the patient’s uncooperative attitude, her cognitive function was sufficiently preserved to allow for the reading and comprehension of complex medication instructions.

A consultation with a psychiatrist resulted in the conclusion that the patient's behavior temporarily met the criteria for NPD, as outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders-5 (DSM-5) [5]. This determination was made when the patient met a minimum of five out of nine criteria in Table 1. Initially, escitalopram 10 mg daily was prescribed for intermittent depressed mood; however, this was discontinued due to worsening aggressive behavior. The medication was then switched to quetiapine 6.25 mg once a day in the evening, but it was discontinued owing to poor adherence secondary to its sedative effect and the lack of an appropriate indication for the patient’s symptoms. As there was no significant improvement in her verbal aggression and disruptive behaviors, a psychiatric consultation was obtained, and risperidone 0.5 mg once daily and valproate 250 mg twice daily were started. After 5 days without notable adverse effects, the doses were increased to risperidone 1 mg once daily and valproate 250 mg twice daily. The same regimen was continued at discharge for an additional 2 weeks. At the beginning of treatment, the patient showed selective medication intake; however, medication adherence improved progressively over time. In addition to medication, we minimized unnecessary contact, as her problematic behaviors tended to intensify during interpersonal conflicts with staff. Also, we instructed the treatment team to maintain a consistent approach with clear interpersonal boundaries when interacting with the patient. In parallel, she continued a rehabilitation program focused on left lower extremity strengthening exercises and gait training for left leg weakness during her inpatient stay.

At approximately 1 month after symptom onset, the patient was discharged from the department of rehabilitation medicine and transferred to a community hospital. At the follow-up visit to the PRM outpatient clinic at approximately 2 months after symptom onset, her mood appeared stable, and her left leg strength had markedly improved, evaluated as MMT grade good. As no caregiver stayed with her at the local hospital, detailed observation of behavioral changes during that period was limited. However, according to family reports, her verbal aggression and entitlement behaviors gradually improved after transfer, and there were no significant conflicts reported with the medical staff or family members at the community hospital. In the absence of formal neuropsychological testing, narcissistic traits were assessed based on DSM-5 criteria [5], and the patient no longer met the threshold of five or more criteria at this visit.

All clinical and imaging data were fully anonymized prior to analysis.

DISCUSSION

In this particular instance, prominent narcissistic personality traits manifested following a meningioma in the right frontal parasagittal region, which included the SMA, and its postoperative hemorrhagic complication. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of acquired narcissistic personality traits arising from a lesion in this region. The behavioral changes were remarkable not only for their clinical severity but also for their phenotypic resemblance to primary NPD, emerging de novo in a patient.

Several alternative postoperative neurobehavioral syndromes warrant consideration before attributing the observed behavioral changes to an acquired personality disturbance. Postoperative delirium was excluded because the patient maintained stable clinical course without the diurnal fluctuations or attentional deficits characteristic of DSM-5 criteria [5]. Additionally, her symptoms were distinct from frontal disinhibition syndrome due to their social selectivity. Unlike generalized disinhibition [4], her outbursts were target-specific, directed at certain medical staff, who refused her demands. This preserved social monitoring suggests a structured personality shift rather than a global loss of restraints. Furthermore, while SMA syndrome [2] classically presents with contralateral akinesia and mutism—reflecting motor hypoactivity—oura patient exhibited the opposite pattern of behavioral hyperactivity, with grandiosity, aggression, and entitlement. This divergence aligns with the concept of ‘pseudopsychopathy’ described by Blumer and Benson [8], in which orbitofrontal and medial frontal damage produces disinhibited, socially inappropriate behavior, rather than the motor suppression characteristic of classical SMA syndrome [2]. Notably, her presentation extended beyond generic disinhibition to temporarily fulfill specific DSM-5 criteria [5] for narcissistic personality traits, underscoring the clinical specificity of this case.

Having excluded alternative neurobehavioral syndromes, it is nonetheless important to clarify the conceptual distinction between the observed presentation and a primary personality disorder. Although these symptoms do not meet the longitudinal criteria for a primary personality disorder, their phenotypic expression was remarkably similar to that of developmental NPD. The phenotypic similarity suggests that primary NPD and acquired narcissistic traits may share a common neuroanatomical substrate. Therefore, we focus on the anatomical basis of this sudden behavioral shift as a structural analogue to the psychiatric disorder.

Although the SMA is not a canonical hub of the triple network, its dense functional connections may allow focal lesions to propagate network-level dysfunction. We hypothesize that connectional diaschisis selectively weakened SMA's functional and structural edges to SN and DMN hubs, disrupting network switching and interactions, thereby precipitating narcissistic personality features [9]. This hypothesis is supported by neuroimaging studies of primary NPD, which share overlapping neural substrates. Schulze et al. [6] demonstrated that reduced gray matter volume in the left anterior insula—a region critical for interoceptive awareness and affective stimulation—correlates with emotional empathy deficits in NPD, suggesting that callousness and exploitative behavior may stem from a structural incapacity to generate affective resonance. Nenadic et al. [10] reported reduced gray matter volume in the right DLPFC and the mPFC, regions integral to top-down inhibitory control, which likely underpins the failure of self-regulatory mechanisms manifesting as narcissistic rage and maladaptive aggression.

To elaborate on this mechanism, Menon's triple network model posits that impaired switching among three core networks—the DMN, CEN, and SN—constitutes a shared pathophysiological mechanism underlying various psychiatric disorders [7,11,12,13,14]. Jankowiak-Siuda & Zajkowski [15] proposed that dysfunction of the SN hubs (anterior insula, ACC) leads to DMN hyperactivity and CEN disengagement, resulting in impaired empathy and excessive self-focus [16]. However, given that the SMA has not traditionally been regarded as a canonical hub for these three networks, the observed behavioral changes are better understood as a consequence of how damage to a non-hub connection region can propagate network-level dysfunction through remote effects.

The application of the concept of connectional diaschisis-functional disconnection (i.e., edge weakening) between the lesioned region and its connected nodes may result in network-level dysfunction at remote sites [9]. According to seed-based resting-state functional MRI analyses by Zhang et al. [16], the SMA demonstrates positive connectivity with the anterior insula (SN hub) and negative connectivity with the posterior cingulate cortex and mPFC (DMN hubs). Consequently, damage to the SMA may result in a reduction of its connectivity with SN hubs (anterior insula–ACC), thereby impairing the switching function between DMN and CEN. The reduction of inhibitory coupling with DMN nodes could then lead to DMN hyperactivity, manifesting as impaired empathy and heightened self-referential processing—core features commonly associated with NPD (Fig. 2). Taken together, the integration of the concepts of connectional diaschisis and the triple network model provides a speculative but potentially valuable framework for understanding acquired personality disturbances and warrants validation through future neuroimaging studies in patients with SMA lesions.

Beyond its theoretical implications, this case carries important clinical relevance for neurorehabilitation. Patients exhibiting acquired narcissistic personality traits—characterized by grandiosity, entitlement, and lack of empathy—frequently result in therapeutic resistance, interpersonal disruptions, and countertransference reactions among healthcare providers [17,18]. This relational strain compromises the therapeutic alliance and can cause splitting among medical teams. Rigid boundary management is necessary to prevent staff burnout and treatment fragmentation [19]. Furthermore, patients may view repetitive rehabilitation exercises as beneath them, refusing participation or devaluing the rehabilitation team when their exacting standards are not met. These behaviors have been demonstrated to impede functional prognosis and discharge planning significantly [20,21]. Therefore, it is imperative for neurorehabilitation specialists to possess a comprehensive understanding of the lesion characteristics that may manifest as acquired narcissistic personality traits. Moreover, a multidisciplinary approach by an appropriate rehabilitation team for patients exhibiting such traits is deemed necessary.

Limitations of this report include its nature as a single case and the absence of direct measures of functional connectivity. Accordingly, the proposed mechanism remains hypothetical and should be interpreted with caution. Future studies should longitudinally examine patients with SMA lesions using multimodal neuroimaging to quantitatively assess changes in SN–DMN–CEN connectivity.

This case demonstrates how a meningioma involving the SMA and subsequent hemorrhage may induce newly developed narcissistic traits by disrupting functional connectivity to salience and DMN hubs via connectional diaschisis. This distinctive case suggests the need to consider the functional connectivity of the damaged area with other brain regions when it comes to acquired personality changes following focal brain injury.
