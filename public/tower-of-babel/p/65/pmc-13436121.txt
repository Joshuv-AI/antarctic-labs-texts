The Anatomage table in medical education: perceptions and associated factors among students in Rwanda
Authors: Abebe Muche, Abebe Bekele, Patrick Orikiriza, Daniel Seifu, Derbew Fikadu Berhe, Charles Okot Odongo, Equlinet Misganaw Amare, Olayinka Rasheed Ibrahim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGross anatomy is foundational in medical education and is traditionally taught through cadaveric dissection. However, post-COVID-19 technological advancements have introduced digital tools, such as the anatomage table (AT), which offers interactive 3D virtual dissection to enhance learning outcomes and overcome logistical challenges.ObjectiveThis study assessed the perceived educational benefits of AT and associated factors among medical students at the University of Global Health Equity (UGHE), Rwanda.MethodsA cross-sectional analytical study using a structured, validated questionnaire was conducted among 148 first- to third-year Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery students. Data were collected using the KoboToolbox and analyzed using SPSS. Chi-square tests and logistic regression were used to identify factors associated with perceived educational benefits of AT. A p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval was considered statistically significant.ResultsOf the 135 participating medical students (response rate of 91.2%), 83.0% perceived AT as beneficial, while 83.7% demonstrated good practice and a favorable attitude toward its use. Additionally, 65.9% of the participants reported improved learning due to the use of AT. Logistic regression identified female sex (AOR = 3.51, CI: 1.26–9.76), good practice (AOR = 3.05, CI: 1.26–7.36), and perceived learning improvement (AOR = 4.45, CI: 2.26–9.13) as significant predictors of perceived benefits.ConclusionThis study shows that students perceived AT as a beneficial educational tool for learning gross anatomy and associated it with a positive attitude and improved learning experience.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09569-x.

Introduction

Gross anatomy remains a fundamental component of medical education and provides the basis for clinical reasoning, diagnostic interpretation, surgical practice, and safe patient care [1, 2]. Traditionally, the modality of teaching gross anatomy relies on cadaveric dissection, which is often considered the gold standard because it promotes hands-on learning and spatial understanding [3, 4]. However, maintaining cadaver-based anatomy education has become increasingly challenging because of high operational costs, shortages of trained anatomy educators, limited laboratory infrastructure, ethical concerns, and health risks associated with embalming chemicals such as formaldehyde [1, 5]. These challenges are more prevalent in many low- and middle-income countries, including Rwanda, where anatomy education is affected by limited access to cadavers, constrained laboratory resources, increasing student enrollment, and scarcity of specialized teaching personnel. In addition, socio-cultural perceptions surrounding cadaveric dissection also influence cadaver procurement and utilization. Consequently, there is growing interest in innovative and sustainable approaches that can complement conventional anatomy teaching while maintaining the quality of education.

One important innovative advancement in anatomy education is the Anatomage Table (AT), a life-sized virtual dissection table designed to provide an immersive learning experience for students. Using high-resolution three-dimensional images reconstructed from CT, MRI, X-ray, and ultrasound data, the AT allows students to explore and virtually dissect anatomical structures in remarkable detail [5]. Its touch-interactive design encourages active participation, helping students better understand complex spatial relationships while supporting independent learning. Studies from different countries have shown that incorporating the AT into anatomy teaching can enhance student engagement, improve spatial comprehension, increase learner satisfaction, and positively influence perceived academic performance [6–8]. Despite the increasing global adoption of the AT, evidence of its effects on students’ learning perspectives and outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa remains limited [9, 10]. Therefore, evaluating students’ learning perspectives is important as medical schools in the region increasingly integrate AT into anatomy education. Furthermore, using Kirkpatrick’s framework for evaluating educational interventions, this study would allow for the evaluation of learner reactions (Level 1) and perceived learning gains (Level 2), which have remained underexplored in low-resource settings such as Rwanda. Therefore, this study aimed to assess medical students’ perceptions of the educational value of the AT and identify factors associated with these perceptions at the University of Global Health Equity (UGHE).

Materials and methods

Study design and settings

A quantitative cross-sectional analytical study was conducted between April 01 and May 30, 2025, with three groups of medical students upon completion of the body structure and function module. The Body Structure and Function module is the introductory course in the pre-clinical sequence, providing students with their first engagement in UGHE’s integrated curriculum. Throughout the module, students participated in interactive learning experiences, including virtual dissections using AT, and developed hands-on skills with a range of medical equipment and technologies.

The study was conducted among medical students at the University of Global Health Equity (UGHE), a private, not-for-profit institution of higher learning established in 2020 and owned by Partners In Health (PIH). UGHE’s campus is in Butaro, a sector within Burera District in Rwanda’s Northern Province.

As a pioneering academic institution in Rwanda, the UGHE is committed to training the next generation of global health professionals. It offers competitive scholarships to aspiring medical students from Rwanda, neighboring countries, and three other African nations—Liberia, Lesotho, and Sierra Leone—where PIH collaborates with governments to promote equitable healthcare. To address gender disparities in the health workforce, the UGHE’s admissions policy mandates that two-thirds of its scholarships be awarded to female students.

Study participants

The target population for this study comprised students enrolled in the Bachelor of Medicine, Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) program at UGHE, the university’s flagship undergraduate medical program. Eligible participants included first-, second-, and third-year medical students who had prior exposure to the AT as part of their anatomy learning. According to the records from the Registrar’s Office, these three cohorts comprised 148 students, all of whom were invited to participate in this study. Fourth- and fifth-year medical students were excluded because they had progressed beyond the basic medical sciences phase of the curriculum and had limited recent exposure to anatomical instruction using the Anatomage Table, which could affect the accuracy and relevance of their responses.

Sample size estimation and sampling technique

The minimum sample size was estimated using Yamane’s formula for a finite population. Based on a total eligible population of 148 students, a 95% confidence level, and a 5% margin of error, the required minimum sample size was 108 students [11]. However, to maximize representativeness and reduce selection bias, all 148 eligible students were invited to participate using a census sampling approach. A total of 135 students responded, exceeding the minimum required sample size and yielding a response rate of 91.2%.

Data collection and tools development

Data were collected using a pretested, structured questionnaire developed by the research team based on an extensive review of the relevant literature. To ensure both contextual and content validity, a draft version of the instrument was evaluated by a panel of five experts in the field. Their feedback was incorporated to refine the tool, enhancing its relevance and clarity. The data collection tools included sections covering key areas, including socio-demographic characteristics, attitudes, perceived learning improvements, and perceived benefits of AT use. A pilot test involving 20 students from Year 5 and Year 6 medical students was conducted to assess the questionnaire’s clarity, comprehensibility, and consistency of responses. The results of this pilot study confirmed acceptable levels of face and content validity.

The data collection tool consisted of four fundamental constructs that play a crucial role in influencing the benefits of AT use. Each construct was measured using a specific number of items: practice, six items; attitude, five items; perceived improvement of learning, seven items; and perceived educational benefit, seven items. All 25 items within the four constructs were assessed using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1(strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) (Supplementary File 1).

The finalized questionnaire was administered electronically using the KoboToolbox platform via the Open Data Kit (ODK) Collect Android application, an open-source tool widely used for mobile and web-based data collection [12].A pre-tested, structured, closed-ended questionnaire was distributed to eligible students via their official WhatsApp group using a secure KoboToolbox survey link. The WhatsApp group was used solely for survey distribution and was not involved in data collection or storage. Upon accessing the link, participants were redirected to an anonymous online survey platform where detailed study information was provided, and electronic informed consent was obtained prior to participation. Only students who provided consent were allowed to proceed with the questionnaire; those who declined were automatically excluded from the study. No personally identifiable information, including names, phone numbers, or WhatsApp identifiers, was collected, and all responses were submitted confidentially and stored securely to ensure participant privacy and data protection throughout the study.

Data analysis

Upon completion, the collected data were exported from KoboToolbox to Microsoft Excel and then to SPSS version 25 for statistical analysis. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the participants’ characteristics. For the Likert-scale responses, a composite median score was calculated and used to categorize the responses. For attitude, the composite median score was 4.10, which was used to distinguish between good and poor attitudes. Participants who scored above this threshold were categorized as having a positive attitude. For perceived learning improvement resulting from the use of the AT as an anatomy teaching resource, a composite median score of 3.83 was used as the threshold to differentiate students who perceived an improvement in their anatomy learning from those who did not. To assess the perceived benefit of AT in the teaching and learning process, the composite median score of 4.36 served as the cut-off for categorizing responses. Students who scored above the median were considered to perceive AT as beneficial. On the other hand, for practice towards AT, we used an average response of 4.18 as the cut-off point to classify participants into “Good Practice” and “Poor Practice” categories.

Additionally, a Chi-square test for independence (with Yates’ Continuity Correction) was conducted to determine whether there was a significant association between the perceived benefit of AT and various factors. Furthermore, a binary logistic regression model was used to assess the impact of various factors on the respondents’ likelihood of reporting a perceived benefit of AT. A p-value < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was considered significant.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the UGHE Institutional Review Board (UGHE-IRB/2024/010). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All collected data were kept confidential, and the participants’ right to withdraw from the study was fully respected. To maintain confidentiality, personal identifiers were omitted, unique codes were used, and access to the data was restricted to authorized personnel. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association).

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

Of the 148 eligible students, 135 participated in the study, yielding a response rate of 91.2%. Among the respondents, 68.1% (n = 92) were female, while 60% (n = 81) reported growing up in urban areas. The median age of the respondents was 21 years. Based on the year of study, 60 (44.4%) were in year 1, 42 [31.1%] were in year 2, and the remaining 33 [24.4%] were in year 3.

Discussion

This research is the first of its kind in the country to generate empirical data on the perceived benefits of the AT and its associated factors in understanding body structure and function among medical students in Rwanda. These findings show students’ endorsement of the Anatomage table, its perceived educational value, and support for its integration into anatomy instruction. Before the COVID-19 pandemic, cadaveric dissection was the primary method used in practical sessions. The COVID-19 pandemic caused a paradigm shift in the implementation of virtual learning [13]. Many studies have highlighted the benefits of hands-on dissection and its importance in learning anatomy [14]. However, other studies advocate for integrative, innovative educational technologies, such as the Anatomage Table, to complement or even replace traditional cadaveric dissection [15] and enhance the effectiveness of anatomical education. In our study, most students demonstrated good practice (83.7%) and a positive attitude (83.7%) toward AT, reflecting institutional or curricular factors such as laboratory integration and faculty encouragement. Research has demonstrated that students using the AT in anatomy education achieve academic outcomes comparable to those utilizing conventional dissection techniques [16, 17]. However, a randomized controlled trial by Boscolo Berto et al. (2021) found that virtual dissection enhanced learning outcomes threefold compared to traditional cadaveric dissection and textbooks [18].

Our findings indicated that 65.9% of students reported perceived learning improvement and 83.0% perceived benefits of AT compared to other technology-enhanced learning tools in anatomy education. They also noted that AT is a novel and engaging learning tool in anatomy education that sparks interest and motivates frequent use. Additionally, it plays a key role in sustaining student engagement in anatomy education. Despite the high cost of approximately $80,000 per AT in Africa, many medical institutions have adopted its use in teaching. The shift in preference towards AT is primarily due to strong cultural and religious beliefs that discourage human body dissection, considering it an offensive and sacrilegious act of mutilation [19]. A cross-sectional study conducted in Ghana indicated that 60% of medical students agreed that operating and using the AT was relatively easy compared to traditional human body dissection [4]. Similarly, a study of Jordanian medical students found that 56.5% enjoyed learning anatomy through virtual dissections, while 18.8% remained neutral on the subject.

Additionally, 63.3% of the students stated that the AT enhanced their understanding of anatomy lectures, and 65.2% recommended virtual anatomy dissections for other students and courses. The study also found that the AT had a greater positive impact on the memorization and comprehension of anatomy lectures among male students than among female students [1]. The findings of the present and related previous studies suggest that medical students, particularly those aged 16–25 years, are inclined to use the Anatomage virtual dissection table. This preference aligns with the characterization of 21st-century students as the “joystick generation,” known for their exceptional ability to navigate and utilize technology [20].

The present study found a statistically significant association between students’ attitudes toward the AT and their perceived benefits of utilizing it. However, regression analysis revealed that attitude was not a meaningful predictor of perceived benefits, even after controlling other independent variables. Therefore, although attitude is associated with the perceived benefit of AT, it may not exert a significant independent impact once the model incorporates additional variables, such as practice, perceived learning improvement, or sex. In addition, the results may indicate that attitude alone is inadequate for assessing the perceived value of sophisticated educational technologies such as AT. Our findings align with those of previous studies, suggesting that behavioral engagement and perceived benefits typically have a more immediate influence than attitudes in shaping students’ perceived educational advantages [21, 22]. These findings emphasize the significance of student engagement approaches beyond merely developing a positive attitude. Educational interventions could be more successful if they focus on improving hands-on use, learning outcomes, and confidence in technology utilization.

A meta-analysis found that female students frequently show greater appreciation for visually engaging, interactive, and structured learning environments [23]. The AT, featuring 3D visualization and an interactive interface, likely aligns well with these preferences, facilitating enhanced spatial orientation, improved understanding, and heightened satisfaction, particularly among learners favoring visual and kinesthetic learning styles. According to Felder and Silverman’s learning style model, female students prefer sensing, visual, and sequential learning styles, whereas males often gravitate towards abstract, intuitive, and global learning approaches [24]. This report may clarify why female students benefit more from detailed step-by-step visualization tools, such as the AT. Studies have reported inconsistent findings regarding sex differences in the use of anatomy learning technologies. A study involving 236 premedical students using tablet-based 3D visualization and augmented reality tools found that male students reported more positive learning experiences than female students [25]. Similarly, research among Jordanian medical students found significant gender differences in preference for AT [1]. In contrast, a recent study involving 96 medical students reported that female students achieved medium-to-large learning gains when using virtual avatars and prosected specimens, while male students showed only minor gains with virtual avatars but large gains with prosections [26]. Consistent with these findings, the present study found that female respondents were more likely to perceive the advantages of the AT. These variations may be related to differences in educational settings, participant characteristics, learning environments, and the type of technology used.

According to experiential learning, active engagement and repeated task practice enhance comprehension and strengthen knowledge retention [27]. In this scenario, learners who used the AT more often likely developed better operational skills, explored more features (e.g., 3D dissection and cross-sectional analysis), and consequently derived greater educational benefits from the tool. Moreover, Bandura’s social cognitive theory indicates that self-efficacy, a belief in one’s ability to accomplish tasks, increases with consistent exposure and achievement [28]. Good practice with AT probably improved students’ confidence and satisfaction, strengthening their trust in its benefits. Similarly, a study conducted elsewhere found that hands-on engagement affects the perception of learning tools. For example, Barmaki et al. and Maurer et al. found that students who actively engaged with 3D visualization tools for anatomy education considered it more effective for learning anatomy [25, 26].

Furthermore, Brown et al. reported that students who used virtual dissection tools more frequently had more positive perceptions than their counterparts [22].In line with this study, our findings also showed that students who engaged in effective AT practice were more likely to recognize its benefits than those who engaged in poor AT practice. This significant association highlights the crucial role of engagement and familiarity in shaping students’ evaluation of educational technology. These findings indicate that greater exposure to and effective use of AT improves learning outcomes and positively affects students’ perceptions of the technology’s usefulness. These findings emphasize the need to provide structured practice opportunities and guided applications within the curriculum to enhance the educational benefits of digital anatomy platforms.

Constructivist learning theory suggests that students learn more effectively when they actively participate in the learning process [29]. The Anatomage Table, an interactive 3D virtual dissection tool, allows students to explore anatomical structures in detail, which may improve their understanding, retention, and spatial ability [5]. Students who experience such improvements are more likely to view the tool as useful and beneficial for their learning. Kirkpatrick’s Model of Training Evaluation, particularly the “Reaction” and “Learning” levels [30], indicates that improved learning outcomes are associated with more positive perceptions of the learning experience. In the present study, students who reported positive learning improvement from AT use were 4.54 times more likely to perceive the tool as highly beneficial, indicating a significant association between perceived learning improvement and a positive evaluation of the technology. Similar findings were reported by Brown et al. (2015) who observed a positive relationship between perceived learning improvement and satisfaction with virtual anatomy platforms [22]. These findings suggest that students who perceive educational benefits from the AT are more likely to value its use in anatomy education and support its structured integration into anatomy teaching.

This study had a few limitations. Firstly, the cross-sectional nature of this study inherently limits the ability to establish causal or temporal relationships among perceived benefits, the AT, and the evaluated explanatory variables because exposure and outcome data were collected at a single time point. Secondly, this study relied primarily on self-reported Likert-scale responses, which may introduce bias, including social desirability bias and subjective interpretation of questionnaire items, potentially failing to fully capture its actual educational benefits. Thirdly, the absence of objective academic performance measures, such as examination scores or practical assessment outcomes, limited the evaluation of measurable learning outcomes associated with the use of AT. Fourthly, this study did not incorporate qualitative methods, which would have enabled a deeper exploration of students’ experiences and the contextual factors influencing their perceptions and perceived benefits. Fifthly, this study was conducted at a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other medical schools and educational settings because the university has its unique curricular, demographic, and institutional characteristics, including its anatomy teaching approach and technological infrastructure, which may differ from those of other institutions within Rwanda. We also acknowledged that dichotomizing continuous variables using median cut-off values may have reduced data variability and resulted in some loss of information. This approach also carries the risk of misclassification, particularly among participants with scores close to the median threshold. Consequently, the findings from this study should be interpreted with caution and as an association rather than causation. Although the study targeted approximately 90% of the eligible population, a larger sample would have strengthened statistical power and enhanced the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, while the study evaluated students’ reactions and perceived learning gains at Kirkpatrick Levels 1 and 2, it did not assess behavioral change (Kirkpatrick Level 3), particularly the extent to which learning acquired through the AT translated into changes in learning practices or application in academic and clinical contexts.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study shows that students perceive AT as a beneficial educational tool for learning gross anatomy and associate it with a positive attitude, good practice, and improved learning experience. We recommend that future studies employ mixed-methods designs and objective academic performance indicators to provide a more comprehensive assessment of the educational impact of the Anatomage Table.
