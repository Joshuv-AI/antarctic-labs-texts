Effect of season, age and physiological status on body weight and blood metabolites of communal sheep
Authors: Mthi Siza, Louis T Tyasi, Jean Rust, Segun Ikusika, Mpisana Zuko, Sive Tokozwayo, Qokweni Luxolo, Francisca Rumosa-Gwaze, Mandla Yawa, Soul Washaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Nutritional status of grazing animals is difficult to determine.•Blood biochemical properties are biological markers in animal nutrition.•Seasonal variations will inform sheep feeding to optimise production.•Blood parameters are correlated.Keywords: Extensive production grazing ewes, Serum biochemistry, Seasonal variation

Introduction

It is naturally difficult to measure dry matter intake (DMI) in grazing animals compared to experimental or animals raised under intensive production systems (Mapfumo et al., 2025). Given this fact, metabolic proxies have been proffered to estimate the nutritional status of grazing animals. Substantial work has been conducted on blood and milk biomarkers to infer feed efficiency (Huhtanen et al., 2015; Cantalapiedra-Hijar et al., 2018; Cheng et al., 2021; Duarte et al., 2019). However, simultaneous evaluation of body weight and multiple blood metabolites across seasons, age groups, and physiological stages under true communal rangeland conditions has scarcely been done. This integrated approach provides novel baseline data specific to Southern African production systems, and highlights critical seasonal nutritional gaps that may inform targeted supplementation and management strategies for improved flock productivity. Hematological and serum biochemical values are considered useful for the evaluation of body condition, the nutritional and immune status in animals, where measurements are not available (Amuda & Okunlola, 2018). These parameters are good indices of the physiological status and perturbations are indicative of response of animals to various physiological situations. In addition, Gorkhali et al. (2017) posit that these parameters are essential for clinical examination, differential diagnosis or suggesting a prognosis, whereas productivity and reproductive efficiency of sheep correlate with these parameters. Studies to ascertain metabolic profiles on grazing sheep have been sporadic, there is great interest in increasing the knowledge of the metabolic and biochemical adaptation of ewes to such processes like pregnancy and lactation in terms of protein and energy metabolism. For example, forage quality or supplementation affects the intake of essential amino acids necessary for protein synthesis, thereby altering the albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio (Washaya et al., 2019). Heightened metabolic activity is closely linked to changes in glucose, total protein, and minerals such as calcium and phosphorus (Ahmed et al., 2021). Serum glucose levels are indicative of liver function, while blood urea nitrogen reflects kidney function; reductions in total protein and albumin levels can signal significant hepato-renal damage (Akanmu et al., 2020). Seasonal fluctuations in temperature and physiological status also influence metabolic rates and nutrient requirements in grazing animals. During pregnancy, ewes preferentially utilize lipid reserves for energy, thereby sparing glucose for fetal growth (Varanis et al., 2021). When energy demand exceeds dietary intake, animals may enter a state of negative energy balance, which can progress to pregnancy toxemia. Metabolic requirements rise further during pregnancy and lactation, and feed shortages exacerbate the situation, forcing animals to mobilize body reserves, ultimately affecting body weight and reproductive performance (González-García et al., 2014; Tavirimirwa & Washaya, 2022). These challenges are characteristic of low-input grazing systems typical of Southern Africa, highlighting the importance of monitoring blood metabolites over time. In contrast, commercial and semi-intensive systems often synchronize breeding so that pregnancy occurs during periods of low forage availability (Maguraushe et al 2023; Washaya et al., 2023), whereas lactation coincides with peak pasture growth, thus supporting sustainable production (Storer et al., 2025). Unfortunately, this may not be practical due to such factors as hormonal, nutritional and environmental conditions, as well as uncontrolled breeding which is common in communal areas. Blood and biochemical parameters can be affected by breed, age, sex, season, physiological status and nutrition (Abdel-Lattif and Al-Muhja, 2021). Because these are rangeland animals, their efficiency in utilizing grazing varies with seasons and is intimately related to their adaptive capacity to variable nutritional quantity and quality (González-García et al., 2014). The mechanisms underlying the outstanding individuals in converting food into metabolites are still not well known, however we hypothesize that animals which are able to maintain body weight, at the same time remaining productive, maximize their relative feed intake’s (RFI) capability throughout the year (Mapfumo et al., 2023). Therefore, the study was aimed to investigate temporal changes in body weight and blood profiles of related metabolites in rangeland sheep raised under communal grazing.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

Ethical clearance was obtained from the Dohne Research Ethical Committee (Ethical clearance reference number: 04/2012) to conduct the study. All trials were conducted per the ethical standards of experimentation by the Committee of Ethics on Animal Use of the Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (SPCA) (Constitutional Court of South Africa, 2013). Oral informed consent was obtained from each participant after telling them about the purpose of the study and the study participants' risks, benefits, and rights. All the information obtained from the study participants was kept confidential.

Study site description

The study was conducted at Ntseshe village under Mnquma Local Municipality, Amathole District, in Eastern Cape Province (Fig. 1). Ntseshe village is situated at 32°05′59.50″S; 28°05′20.93″E with an elevation of 739 m, approximately 28 km north-east of Ngqamakhwe town. The area is found in the semi-arid region of the Eastern Cape Province with a mean annual precipitation of 739 mm, with average annual temperatures reaching a high of 25.3 °C (summer) and a low of 7.2 °C (winter).

South African map showing the study site in the Eastern Cape Province.

Ntseshe village falls under grassland biomes and Mthatha moist vegetation type (GS 14). Mthatha moist is found between two vegetation types, namely Tsomo grassland and coastal tropical forest (Mucina and Rutherford, 2011). Mthatha moist vegetation is characterized by undulating plains and hills supporting different kinds of livestock such as goats, sheep, cattle, donkeys, and horses. The vegetation of Mthatha moist grassland is dominated with moderately and poorly grazed species due to uncontrolled grazing. Unplanned grazing management has led to the dominance of unpalatable grasses and the encroachment of Acacia mearsii along the riverbanks. The recommended grazing capacity for Ntsheshe village ranged from 5 to 6 hectares per large stock unit. Soil parent material is driven mudstone of the Tarkastad and Adelaide subgroups with highly leached soils (Mucina and Rutherford, 2011).

Experimental animals and data collection

A total of 262 South African Merino (SAM) ewes, averaging 30.9 ± 0.81 kg live weight, were selected for the study based on age, health status, and the willingness of participating sheep farmers. The age of the ewes was determined using dentition, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The animals grazed natural pasture, which served as their primary feed source, with ad libitum access to water from various community sources. Grazing occurred daily from 08:30 to 17:00, after which the ewes were kraaled overnight. Animal health care was provided by local paraveterinary technicians, while complicated cases were referred to the Dohne Research Station for veterinary attention. Due to limited infrastructure, uncontrolled natural mating was the predominant breeding practice, with SAM rams running continuously with the ewes throughout the year. For accurate identification and record keeping, all animals were fitted with plastic ear tags. Data were collected between March 2012 and December 2013, covering all four seasons (autumn, winter, spring, and summer).

Age determination of sheep using dentitions.

Pregnancy diagnosis and body weights measurements

Sheep underwent an ultrasonic scan (Sheep Pregnancy Tester 200,350, SWAVET, SA) once every three months with a 5 MHz abdominal probe to establish pregnancy status, however, this could not fully capture early embryonic losses or more dynamic reproductive changes. Scanning and weighing were done in the morning before the sheep went out to graze to minimize the effect of postprandial variations. Body weight measurement was taken every month using a sheep crate electronic weighing scale. This was done in the middle of each month and pooled to represent each season,

Blood collection and analysis

Blood samples were collected between 07:00 and 10:00 before sheep were released from their kraal/paddock to graze freely. This was performed in each season directly before body weight measurements were taken. A 5 ml blood sample was collected from each sheep via the jugular vein into a plain test tube (no anticoagulant) for biochemical assays. A clean, sterile needle was used for each animal to avoid cross-contamination between the samples. The blood was centrifuged (Benchmark Scientific C3100-E-UK) at 1000 × g for 10 min to obtain serum, which was subsequently stored at -20 °C until analysis. The serum samples were analyzed using commercially available kits from Siemens (South Africa) and a Next/Vetex Alfa Wasseman Analyzer (Woerden, Netherlands). The serum was analyzed enzymatically for glucose and spectrophotometrically for total protein (TP) using the methods of Wechselbaum (1946) and albumin as described by Doumas (1972). Globulin concentrations were calculated as the difference between TP and albumin, while the albumin/globulin (A/G) ratio was determined by dividing the albumin value by the globulin concentration. Serum was also used to determine calcium (Cali et al., 1972), phosphorus (Bansal, 1990) and magnesium (Tietz, 1976) using the colorimetric technique. Due to the nature of the production system, factors like disease burden, parasitism, and genetic variation within the flock were not explicitly controlled for although they can influence both body weight and blood metabolites.

Glucose levels were determined following the method described by Gochman and Schmitz (1972), using reagent NAE2-27 after enzymatic oxidation with glucose oxidase. For cholesterol determination, serum cholesterol esters were completely hydrolyzed into free cholesterol and free fatty acids using pancreatic cholesterol esterase, as per Calame et al. (1975). The total protein content was measured using Lowry et al. (1951), and the albumin concentration was determined by the method of Pinnell and Northam (1978).

Statistical analysis

Repeated measures data were analyzed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SAS (SAS Institute, 2016), with year, age, physiological status and season included as fixed factors. Since repeated measures ANOVA assumes sphericity (the equality of variances of the differences between time points), the Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity was performed to assess this assumption. Based on the test results, if the assumption of sphericity had been violated, a more complex mixed model would have been considered. As sphericity was not violated (see Results), the repeated measures GLM was deemed appropriate. Differences between means were tested using Tukey’s post-hoc comparisons, with statistical significance declared at P<0.05. The partial correlation method in R using “ppcor” package was used estimate the relationships among serum components and physiological status, significance was declared at P< 0.05.

Results

The overall Mauchly’s Test of Sphericity indicated that the assumption of sphericity was met for both transformed variates (Mauchly’s W = 0.424, χ² = 4.29, p = 0.117) and orthogonal components (Mauchly’s W = 0.763, χ² = 1.35, p = 0.508), confirming that the variances of the differences between time points were approximately equal. Therefore, the repeated measures GLM was considered appropriate. The effect of year, age and season on body weight and serum biochemistry constituents of sheep grazing on communal rangeland are depicted in Table 1. The body weight, glucose, globulin and albumin: globulin (A/G) ratio levels varied (P<0.05) between years. A decrease in body weight gain and A/G ratio concentration levels (P<0.05) were observed in the year 2013. There was an increase in glucose and globulin concentration levels in the year 2013 compared to 2012. There were no significant differences observed between total protein, albumin, cholesterol, urea, calcium, phosphorus and magnesium in years 2012 and 2013. The age of the experimental animals significantly (P<0.05) influenced BW and glucose levels whilst other serum biochemistry parameters were non-significant. A higher BW of 31.8±0.71 was observed in sheep at the age of 6 years compared to 30.7±0.65 of 8 and 30.1±0.81 at 4 years of age, respectively. The blood glucose levels were observed to be significantly higher in sheep at the age of 8 years compared to those at 4 and 6 years respectively. The body weight performance of sheep was significantly (P<0.05) different across the sampling season. The BW, glucose, TP, albumin, globulin, urea, calcium and phosphorus levels all varied with season. The highest BW was recorded in summer and autumn and the least in spring and winter (P<0.05). Sheep blood glucose levels were higher (P<0.05) during the winter and spring seasons and least in the summer and autumn seasons. The TP, albumin and globulin calcium and phosphorus contents were all higher in winter and spring compared to autumn or summer, while urea was high in spring and summer (P<0.05). The magnesium, potassium and cholesterol content were not subject to season, year or age of the animals (P>0.05). The effects of pregnancy and lactation on serum biochemistry of sheep is presented in Table 2. The physiological status significantly (P<0.05) influenced BW, glucose, TP, albumin, globulin, cholesterol, calcium and phosphorus levels of female sheep. NPNL ewes had higher BW and cholesterol levels compared to P, and NPL (P<0.05). Pregnant as well as NPL ewes exhibited the least BW, highest glucose, albumin, globulin, cholesterol, calcium and phosphorus level compared to NPNL. Pregnancy (P) or not pregnant but lactating (NPL) has similar effects on blood serum components which is consistent when ewes are neither pregnant nor lactating (NPNL) that is protein, energy and calcium levels are elevated in PL and NPL than NPNL. In this study there is a week positive correlation between BW and A/G ratio, otherwise, BW is negatively correlated to most serum components. The A/G showed moderate to weak positive correlations with the rest of the biochemical, while significantly positive correlations were recorded among K, P, Ca, Mg, glucose and cholesterol.

Discussion

Age and seasonal effects on serum parameters

The effect of year on BW, glucose, globulin, and the albumin/globulin ratio (A/G ratio), was expected. This could be due to changes in the availability and composition of dietary protein and energy sources across different years. For example, variations in forage quality or supplementation practices can affect the intake of essential amino acids necessary for protein synthesis, thereby altering the A/G ratio (Cannas et al., 2019). Autumn and summer are typically periods of abundant forage growth and availability that provided sheep with sufficient nutrients for growth and maintenance, leading to optimal weight gain in the current study. These results were similar to those reported by Nedeva et al. (2022) in which blood serum metabolites changed among seasons. The current study revealed that the TP, albumin and globulin decreased from summer to winter respectively, while urea was high in spring and summer, similar result were reported by Nedeva et al. (2022). This could be due to several factors such as the physiological demand of animals and seasonal changes in temperature and daylight hours that can affect metabolic rates and nutrient requirements. For instance, animals may require more proteins for thermoregulation in colder months or during periods of increased activity in spring. The influence of age on BW and glucose levels in the current study highlight several important aspects of sheep physiology and management. Younger animals require diets rich in energy and protein to support growth, whereas older sheep may require adjustments in their diet to maintain optimal body condition and metabolic health. Blood glucose levels did not show a linear increase or decrease as animals aged. The lack of a linear relationship could be due to various factors such as, animals may exhibit fluctuations in glucose metabolism over their lifespan due to changes in diet, activity levels, hormonal balance, and overall health status. In agreement Oramari et al. (2014) and (Rahman et al., 2018), showed that older animals had higher glucose, albumin, cholesterol and blood urea levels than younger ones. The effects observed in this study are likely influenced by seasonal fluctuations in rainfall, temperature, and forage availability, which are inherent to communal rangeland systems. These factors act as potential confounders and should be considered when interpreting our results. Our findings therefore reflect the complex interaction between environmental variability and animal physiology (Mapfumo et al., 2023, 2025, Washaya et al., 2024) emphasizing the need for context-specific management strategies for communal sheep production systems in Southern Africa

Effects of physiological status

The body weight of ewes depended on physiological states in the current study, because resources allocation in empty ewes favours growth and reproduction above maintenance requirements, similarly blood cholesterol was high in lactating than pregnant ewes. The physiological status also influenced some biochemical parameters, similar results were reported by Varanis et al., (2021), where urea, cholesterol and glucose varied widely in pregnant sheep. This could be due to different physiological states such as pregnancy, lactation, and growth imposing varying metabolic demands on female sheep. Additionally, during pregnancy, there is an increased need for nutrients to support fetal growth and development. This heightened metabolic activity can lead to changes in glucose levels (reflecting energy metabolism), total protein (indicating protein synthesis and turnover), and minerals like calcium and phosphorus (essential for bone development). According to David et al. (2020) glucose levels were highest during lactation than pregnancy in Santa Ines ewes, while urea decreased with advancing pregnancy and increased with lactation days, and similar trends were observed for albumin and globulin. The effects of pregnancy on glucose are linked to the fact that the growing foetus demands glucose as the primary source of energy consuming approximately 70 % of maternal production (Varani et al., 2021). The Ca levels for NPL ewes was high in the current study, however this is in contrasts to David et al. (2020) where Ca increased during the peri-partum to lactation stages.

Correlation

The findings that body weight of experimental sheep was positively correlated to A/G ratio (r=0. 0280) could be explained by malnutrition that is exacerbate by low quality feed under communal farming system (Maguraushe et al., 2023; Mapfumo et al., 2025, 2023; Mapfumo and Muchenje, 2015). Moreover, negative relationship between body weight and glucose observed in this study can be contributed by increase in maintenance requirements of an animal. These findings are in line with the previous results reported by Carlos et al. (2015) that nutrition and health condition play an important role on the changes of animal performance and blood metabolites. Serum protein profiling is the essential tool for diagnosing of many diseases and conditions. The positive correlation in serum protein concentration with other blood metabolites such as albumin, globulin, calcium, phosphorus, and glucose concentration observed in this current study are in agreement with Xulu et al. (2022) who found increase in total protein, albumin and globulin with serum concentration in lambs and indigenous Zulu goats. Positive correlation on albumin concentration with A/G ratio and calcium concentration could be reflected by sensitivity and specificity of hormonal balance, nutritional status, water balance and state of health of an animal. The albumin serum concentration is produced in the liver has its synthesis influenced by nutrition, general liver condition, stress and extravascular concentration. Moreover, albumin is the indicator of an animal’s protein nutritional status (Soloiman et al., 2010). The positive correlation on urea with total protein, albumin and globulin found in this study agrees with earlier report by Legendre et al. (2017). The observed negative correlation between A/G ratio and globulin serum concentration in communal sheep agrees with results reported by Shelke and Dhami (2002) who reported that globulin revealed negative correlations with A/G ratio in both Gir (Bos indicus) and Jafarabadi bulls Thailand. Phosphorus concentration showed positive correlation with total protein, albumin, urea, potassium, calcium, cholesterol, phosphorus and magnesium concentration. These results are in line with Umar et al. (2017) who reported positive correlation between calcium and phosphorus serum concentration in Teddy goats.

Conclusion and recommendation

The study highlights significant seasonal and physiological variations in the biochemical and physiological parameters of sheep, such as glucose, total protein, albumin, and globulin levels, with notable fluctuations across different years and ages. Seasonal changes, particularly during autumn and summer, are associated with improved body weight gains, likely due to increased forage availability and quality. Conversely, higher levels of total protein, albumin, and globulin are observed in winter and spring, reflecting the animals' metabolic adaptations to varying environmental conditions. Additionally, age-related variations in glucose levels suggest altered glucose metabolism in older sheep, potentially indicative of metabolic issues like insulin resistance. Given these findings, it is recommended that sheep management practices be tailored to seasonal forage availability and the physiological state of the animals. Ensuring appropriate nutritional adjustments and monitoring for age-related metabolic changes can enhance overall sheep health and productivity. Future research should further explore the implications of age-related glucose metabolism and seasonal dietary impacts to refine management strategies and improve sheep welfare.
