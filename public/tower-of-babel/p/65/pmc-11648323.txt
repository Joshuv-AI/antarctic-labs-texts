Rare Intravascular Involvement of Pleomorphic Dermal Sarcoma Seen During Mohs Micrographic Surgery
Authors: Cristo Armando Carrasco Mendoza, Jason Robert Castillo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPleomorphic dermal sarcoma (PDS) and atypical fibroxanthoma (AFX) are rare mesenchymal tumors that share similar clinical, histological, and immunohistochemical characteristics. Careful histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen that includes subcutaneous fat remains the preferred way to differentiate between these tumors. AFX is limited to dermal invasion, whereas PDS demonstrates deeper invasion. Moreover, PDS may present with tumor necrosis and high-grade histological findings, such as lymphovascular and perineural invasion, features absent in AFX. However, like PDS, AFX is a diagnosis of exclusion, and an exhaustive immunohistochemistry panel is recommended to distinguish these tumors from other spindled cell tumors in the differential diagnosis.The authors present the case of an 86-year-old man with biopsy-suspected AFX who was referred for Mohs micrographic surgery for tumor excision. During Mohs, the tumor was observed to have invaded deeply into the subcutaneous tissue and galeal aponeurosis, aligning more closely with a PDS. The diagnosis of PDS was confirmed using en face processing during Mohs surgery, which captured the intravascular involvement of a solitary vessel. Differentiating between PDS and AFX is important because PDS is a more aggressive tumor, with a higher rate of local recurrence and metastasis, and requires closer monitoring.

Introduction

Pleomorphic dermal sarcoma (PDS) is a rare, fibrohistiocytic cutaneous malignancy.1

It usually presents as a rapidly growing ulcerated subcutaneous nodule on sun-exposed skin. The condition most commonly occurs in individuals in their 7th or 8th decade of life.2,3 The scalp is involved in more than half of cases, and there is a strong male predominance with a male-to-female ratio of 7:1.2,3

The diagnosis of PDS is primarily one of exclusion. It is crucial to differentiate PDS from similar tumors such as atypical fibroxanthoma (AFX), spindle cell melanoma, angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, or squamous cell carcinoma, as they may exhibit similar clinical and histopathological features.1,4 Immunohistochemistry plays a vital role in distinguishing PDS from these similar tumors. Moreover, AFX, a neoplasm of mesenchymal origin, cannot be distinguished from PDS based on clinical presentation or immunohistochemistry alone as it is based on depth of invasion of the tumor.

Differentiating PDS from AFX is challenging and typically requires careful histopathological review of a biopsy specimen that includes subcutaneous fat.4 This distinction is crucial because the final diagnosis of PDS affects patient management.5 PDS is generally considered more aggressive than AFX, with a higher rate of local recurrence and metastasis. Consequently, it necessitates more aggressive treatment, collaborative approach across multiple specialties, and more frequent follow-up.1,2

The authors present the case of an 86-year-old White man referred for Mohs micrographic surgery for a biopsy-proven AFX of the scalp. During surgery, histopathological examination revealed diffuse atypical, spindled cells infiltrating the dermis, subcutaneous fat, and galeal aponeurosis, suggestive of PDS. Notably, during stage 2 of Mohs micrographic surgery, a solitary vessel with intravascular involvement of PDS was identified in 1 tissue section of the block. The entire block was sent for permanent histopathological analysis, which did not show intravascular involvement with breadloafing of the block. The Mohs slide with the intravascular invasion was sent in for rereview with pathology, which did indeed show a solitary transected vessel with intravascular involvement. This case involving a rare tumor highlights the utility of Mohs micrographic surgery with en face processing. The patient’s tumor was successfully cleared after 4 stages of Mohs micrographic surgery. He was referred to the tumor board for a multidisciplinary discussion and advised to receive adjuvant radiation. He declined radiation and has been managed with regular follow-ups and scouting biopsies, showing no evidence of local recurrence or metastases.

Informed consent has been provided for the purposes of this case report.

Case Patient Presentation

An 86-year-old White man with an extensive history of non-melanoma skin cancer presented to the dermatology clinic for evaluation of a lesion on the scalp. The patient reported that the lesion had been present for 3 months and had gradually increased in size. Additionally, the lesion was painful and bled daily. A shave biopsy was performed, revealing AFX. A referral was then made for Mohs micrographic surgery to remove the tumor. On the day of the Mohs procedure, physical examination was notable for a 2 × 2 cm friable plaque on the right crown, with no regional lymphadenopathy appreciated (Figure 1). Review of systems revealed no fever or unintentional weight loss.

Marked preoperative ulcerated plaque on mid-parietal/right crown of scalp with extensive actinic damage.

During stage 2 of Mohs micrographic surgery, atypical, spindled cells were noted infiltrating the dermis and subcutaneous fat, suggesting the diagnosis of PDS over AFX. In 1 tissue section of the block obtained during stage 2 of Mohs, a solitary vessel with intravascular involvement of the atypical cells, surrounded by a dense lymphocytic infiltrate, was observed, consistent with PDS with intravascular involvement (Figure 2). This finding was detected using horizontal en face tissue processing. The block was then sent for permanent histopathological analysis and immunohistochemistry. The block did not show intravascular involvement on breadloafing of the specimen. The Mohs slide with the intravascular invasion was sent in for review with the regional expert in dermatopathology and did indeed show a solitary transected vessel with intravascular involvement highlighting the utility of en face processing. Stage 3 of Mohs surgery revealed focal atypical, spindled cells involving the galeal aponeurosis, accompanied by surrounding inflammation. After 4 stages of Mohs surgery, the tumor was successfully cleared, resulting in a final defect size of 3 × 3 cm. (Figure 3). The defect was closed with partial intermediate layered closure and second intent wound healing.

The final histopathological analysis confirmed the diagnosis of PDS with intravascular involvement of a solitary vessel. Immunohistochemistry revealed strong and diffuse positivity for CD10 and CD68, although SOX10, S100, CD31, CD34, Desmin, and smooth muscle actin (SMA) were negative, further confirming the diagnosis and ruling out similar tumors, such as spindle cell melanoma, angiosarcoma, and leiomyosarcoma. A multidisciplinary approach was adopted, and consultations with head and neck surgery and radiation oncology were sought for management advice. Computed tomography scans of the head, neck, and chest with contrast were performed, and the patient’s case was presented for discussion at the head and neck surgery and radiation oncology tumor board. The computed tomography scans revealed no evidence of metastatic spread or involvement of the nodal basin. Based on the tumor board’s recommendation, the patient was diagnosed with cT1N0M0 PDS and was offered adjuvant radiotherapy to reduce the anticipated chance of local recurrence of 15% due to intravascular involvement. However, the patient declined radiotherapy at this time.

Follow-up in Mohs clinic 1 month after surgery revealed no signs of local recurrence or regional lymphadenopathy. The patient was placed on regular 3-month follow-ups, all of which were subsequently unremarkable. However, 11 months after surgery, the patient presented to the dermatology clinic with a painful sore on the scalp. Physical examination revealed a friable 1-cm erythematous nodule near the surgical scar. Due to the chance of PDS recurrence, a shave biopsy was performed, which showed squamous cell carcinoma in situ. The patient underwent scouting biopsies of the new scalp lesion due to the history of PDS with intravascular involvement. The scouting biopsies confirmed the presence of squamous cell carcinoma and squamous cell carcinomas in situ, but no evidence of local recurrence of PDS was found. Mohs surgery was subsequently performed to remove the squamous cell carcinoma, with no spindle cell neoplasm identified on histopathology. As of the time of writing, the patient showed no evidence of local recurrence or metastasis and was scheduled to undergo close observation with Mohs follow-ups at 3-month intervals.

Differential Diagnosis

PDS and AFX are rare mesenchymal tumors that share similar clinical, histological, and immunohistochemical characteristics.2 Some authors suggest that these tumors are thought to be a similar entity on a spectrum, with PDS being the more aggressive and deeper variant.2 Both conditions typically present clinically on sun-exposed areas of the head and neck in elderly patients. Both are described as rapidly growing ulcerated nodules with bleeding.2 Careful histopathological examination of a biopsy specimen that also includes subcutaneous fat remains the recommended approach for distinguishing between the two.

Both tumors are composed of a variable admixture of epithelioid, histiocytoid, giant multinucleated, and/or spindle cells with pleomorphic nuclei and atypical mitotic figures.4,5 In AFX, tumor cell invasion is limited to the dermis.1 PDS has deeper invasion beyond the dermis into subcutaneous tissue, fascia, skeletal muscle, or galeal aponeurosis.1,5 Additionally, PDS can be distinguished from AFX by the presence of tumor necrosis and high-grade histologic features, including lymphovascular or perineural invasion.1,5

On immunohistochemistry, both types of tumor can exhibit strong and diffuse positivity for CD10, although this marker is nonspecific, and CD68.5 Moreover, AFX, like PDS, is a diagnosis of exclusion, and an exhaustive immunohistochemistry workup is recommended to distinguish these tumors from other spindled cell tumors in the differential diagnosis.5 Spindle cell/desmoplastic melanoma can be ruled out by negative staining for melanocytic specific markers, such as SOX10 and S100.5 Negative staining for the vascular markers CD31 and CD34 can be used to rule out angiosarcoma.5 Negative staining for desmin and smooth muscle actin can help in ruling out leiomyosarcoma.5 Squamous cell carcinoma is a common cutaneous malignancy that can present similarly clinically and histologically to AFX and PDS but can be excluded by negative staining for pancytokeratins.4,5

Clinical Reasoning

PDS was the final diagnosis based on clinical, histopathological, and immunohistochemical findings. Initially, the patient was referred for Mohs micrographic surgery for suspected AFX. During the Mohs procedure, an atypical, spindled cell tumor was noted invading the dermis, subcutis, and galeal aponeurosis, which was consistent with PDS. Although tumor necrosis and lymphatic and perineural invasion were absent, 1 tissue section of the block during stage 2 of Mohs showed a solitary transected vessel with spindle cell involvement in the lumen, consistent with PDS with intravascular invasion. The blocks from stage 1 and 2 of Mohs were sent to the regional expert in dermatopathology for permanent histopathological analysis and immunohistochemistry. Histopathological review confirmed the diagnosis of PDS and excluded other spindled cell tumors in the differential diagnosis. The intravascular involvement was seen on only 1 tissue section of Mohs micrographic surgery, highlighting the utility of en face processing.

Treatment Pathway

The standard of care for PDS is Mohs micrographic surgery or a wide local excision with 1- to 2-cm margins.1,4 After successful Mohs surgery of this patient’s tumor, an interdisciplinary tumor board convened to discuss the best management plan. PDS is an exceedingly rare tumor; therefore, there is a paucity of evidence-based recommendations on management after surgical removal.2 The patient was diagnosed with cT1N0M0 PDS. The chance of local recurrence was estimated to be at least 15%. Adjuvant radiotherapy, historically used to treat PDS, was offered but declined by the patient.1 The patient is currently being managed with frequent, 3-month follow-ups to evaluate for local recurrence and metastasis, as recommended by the literature.1,2 The follow-up consists of careful physical examination, including lymph node examination, to evaluate for local recurrence and regional metastasis. Scouting biopsies are performed by Mohs micrographic surgery to evaluate worrisome lesions for local recurrence.

Outcomes

As of the time of writing, the patient was 2 years post Mohs micrographic surgery for PDS with intravascular involvement, with no evidence of local recurrence or metastasis. The patient declined radiotherapy but was being closely followed by dermatology and Mohs micrographic surgery, with regular follow-up and scouting biopsies performed when necessary to evaluate for local recurrence and metastasis.

Key Learning Takeaways

PDS can be very difficult to diagnose due to its shared clinical, histologic, and immunohistochemical similarities with several tumors, including AFX, spindle cell/desmoplastic melanoma, angiosarcoma, leiomyosarcoma, and squamous cell carcinoma.
