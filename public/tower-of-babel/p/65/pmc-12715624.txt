Enhancing Gene Delivery and Immune Modulation in Primary Dendritic Cells by Utilizing Composites of Iron Oxide Nanocubes and Biocompatible PLGA–PDMAEMA Polymers
Authors: Chonnavee Manipuntee, Chalathan Saengruengrit, Kasirapat Ariya-anandech, Warerat Kaewduangduen, Areerat Kunanopparat, Amanee Samaeng, Asada Leelahavanichkul, Patcharee Ritprajak, Numpon Insin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Dendritic cells (DCs) represent pivotal targets in immunotherapy, gene therapy, and vaccine delivery for major diseases including cancer, autoimmune diseases, and transplant rejection. Overcoming the challenge of efficient gene delivery via conventional nonviral molecules into primary DCs has been a persistent obstacle. Within this research, we introduce an innovative gene delivery system utilizing magnetic iron oxide nanocubes (MCs) encapsulated with a biocompatible polymer, poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PL), and a cationic polymer, poly­(2-(dimethylamino)­ethyl methacrylate) (PD), facilitated by a magnetic field. Positively charged MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD (double coating of PD) composites were synthesized, and plasmid DNA (pMAX-GFP) attachment ensued. The nanocomposite with double layers coated with PD displayed increased positive charge despite the incorporation of plasmid DNA. In addition, these nanocomposites exhibited superparamagnetic properties with a saturation magnetization of 4.5 emu/g. At concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 μg/mL, the nanocomposites demonstrated minimal toxicity on bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) and exhibited efficient cellular uptake under the influence of a magnetic field. Composites with higher positive charges and increased amounts demonstrated enhanced plasmid transfection efficiency without activating BMDCs. These findings suggest that using MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD nanocomposites as carriers holds promise as a viable and effective gene delivery platform to primary DCs, revealing their potential in advancing gene-based therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Dendritic cells (DCs) represent the most effective antigen-presenting cells (APCs) capable of bidirectionally initiating and programming the immune system for both immune tolerance and immune activation. − Consequently, the employment of primary DCs for therapy based on DCs has become a crucial cornerstone in the fields of vaccinology and immunotherapy. To manipulate DC properties, gene delivery to the cells provides several potential strategies for clinical medication in several diseases like autoimmune diseases, cancer, and transplant rejection. However, achieving high transfection efficiency in gene delivery to the cells has been a challenge in several studies. Currently, gene delivery systems based on viral vectors are commonly employed due to their high transfection efficiency. Nonetheless, concerns about the risk of viral vectors have been raised for in vivo applications. − Hence, the development of gene delivery system based on nonviral vectors for DCs with high transfection efficiency, low cytotoxicity, and ability to manipulate the functional properties of DCs is fascinating and challenging.

Recently, nonviral gene delivery technologies such as lipofection, , electroporation, , and magnetic induction have gained significant attention as safer alternatives to viral vectors for research and therapeutic applications. Lipofection involves the use of cationic lipids that form lipoplexes to facilitate the entry into cells. This method is user-friendly and cost-effective; however, it often faces challenges such as limited internalization in diverse cell types, low in vivo efficiency, and rapid degradation of the liposomal vehicles, which can hinder sustained gene expression. Electroporation, on the other hand, employs a high-voltage electric field to induce membrane permeabilization. While it achieves high transfection rates across various cell types, including nondividing cells, its application is limited by significant cytotoxicity and potential tissue damage in vivo. In contrast, magnetic induction utilizes magnetic nanoparticles or nanocomposites to transport genetic material, which is then concentrated on target cells by an external magnetic field. This method enables rapid and efficient transfection in vitro, improved targeting capabilities in vivo, and maintains high cell viability, effectively addressing some of the drawbacks associated with lipofection and electroporation. Therefore, a nonviral gene delivery system based on magnetic induction represents a promising strategy for further investigation and optimization.

Magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles have garnered substantial attendance, particularly applications in biomedical field such as cancer therapeutics, hyperthermia, and drug and gene delivery. − Their superparamagnetic property not only ensures colloidal stability but also enables them to be manipulated with an external magnetic field for targeted drug or gene delivery to specific sites. , A common approach to enhance biocompatibility for drug and gene delivery involves encapsulating spherical magnetic nanoparticles with biocompatible polymers like poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA). This method offers the flexibility to load both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, depending on the synthesis strategies employed. , However, a drawback of this approach is a significant decrease in the magnetization of the composites compared to the bare particles prior to modification. , This reduction stems from a decline in the magnetic response and delivery system efficiency. In addressing this issue and improving the delivery system, magnetic nanocubes (MCs) emerge as promising candidates. Their exhibited higher magnetizations have been reported when compared to spherical counterparts of similar sizes. Despite their potential, MCs have been relatively unexplored in the realm of drug and gene delivery applications.

In recent times, the integration of polymer-encapsulated magnetic nanoparticles into the gene delivery system based on nonviral vectors has proven to be an appealing alternative due to its heightened safety margin and the rapid cellular uptake facilitated by an induced magnetic field. ,, Our previous publication revealed that a combination of mesoporous carriers (MCs) with the positively charged polymer poly­(2-(dimethylamino)­ethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA) displayed a preference for binding plasmid DNA (pDNA). In our research, we developed nonviral carriers in the form of cationic MC–PDMAEMA nanocomposites to deliver a model plasmid DNA, specifically pMAX-GFP, into primary dendritic cells (DCs). Additionally, PDMAEMA has been shown to suppress the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in macrophages. , Given the unique properties of MC and PDMAEMA, these nanocomposites proved effective as vectors for plasmid delivery without stimulating bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs). However, at high concentrations, the nanocomposites exhibited cytotoxicity, which could compromise the transfection efficiency. To address this challenge, we proposed the use of copolymers of PDMAEMA with poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) to enhance the biocompatibility of dendritic cells. This combination aims to mitigate cytotoxicity issues and improve the transfection efficiency. In this study, we developed a nonviral carrier consisting of a combination of mesoporous carriers (MCs), PLGA, and PDMAEMA, resulting in cationic MC–PLGA–PDMAEMA nanocomposites. This formulation effectively delivered the pMAX-GFP gene or a model gene into primary DCs, leading to an improved transfection efficiency.

In this context, combining the superparamagnetic nature of MC with PLGA and PDMAEMA facilitated gene delivery to targeted primary DCs and minimized inflammatory responses. This suggests a feasible and effective platform for gene delivery into primary immune cells with our carrier.

Material and Methods

Materials

Poly-l-lysine hydrobromide powder (MW 30,000–70,000, cell culture grade), poly­(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA, MW 38,000–54,000, Resomer RG 504H), poly­(2-(dimethylamino)­ethyl methacrylate) (PDMAEMA, MW 10,000), iron­(III) chloride (anhydrous), poly­(vinyl alcohol) (PVA, 80% hydrolyzed, MW 9000–10,000), oleic acid (90%), and sodium oleate (≥99%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). Sodium hydroxide was obtained from QRC (New Zealand).

Biological Reagents

Lipofectamine 2000, a commercial transfection reagent, was sourced from Invitrogen (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 medium, penicillin–streptomycin, fetal bovine serum (FBS) GlutamaxTM were obtained from GIBCO (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Fluorescein 5(6)-isothiocyanate (FITC, ≥90%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (MO, USA). ELISA kits, purified antimouse CD16/32 monoclonal antibody, and fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against mouse CD11c+, CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC class II were obtained from Biolegend (CA, USA). The model plasmid DNA, pMAX-GFP, was obtained from Addgene (USA).

Synthesis of the Magnetic Nanocube

The magnetic nanocubes (MCs) were prepared using a modified thermal decomposition based on previous reports. , In brief, an iron­(III) chloride solution (8 mmol in 10 mL of Milli-Q water) was combined with a mixture of a sodium hydroxide solution (24 mmol in 10 mL of Milli-Q water) and oleic acid (24 mmol). This mixture was then added to a combination of ethanol, hexane, and Milli-Q water (16 mL:28 mL:12 mL) and refluxed at 70 °C. The organic phase was collected to obtain an iron­(III) oleate solution. The obtained iron precursor (1.034 g) was mixed with 0.168 g of oleic acid, 0.0475 g of sodium oleate, and 7.89 g of 1-octadecene. The resulting mixture was evacuated at 80 °C for 1 h to degas and remove the residue of moisture. Then, the reaction mixture was heated and maintained at 320 °C for 60 min under a nitrogen atmosphere. Subsequently, the mixture was rapidly cooled to ambient temperature. Finally, the synthesized particles were washed with 2-propanol and redispersed in hexane.

Fabrication of MC–PLGA–PDMAEMA Nanocomposites

The MC–PLGA–PDMAEMA nanocomposites (MC–PL–PD) were fabricated via a modified single emulsion technique coupled with solvent evaporation, adapted from our previous work on PLGA nanocomposites. To elaborate, dried MC (2.5 mg) was redispersed in 10 mL of dichloromethane. Subsequently, PLGA (100 mg) was introduced into the suspension. This mixture was then added to a solution containing 120 mg of PVA and 10 mg of PDMAEMA in 30 mL of Milli-Q water, followed by homogenization (D-Lab D-160 Homogenizer) at 22,000 rpm for 2 min. Post-homogenization, the suspension underwent a 2 h stirring period in a fume hood to facilitate dichloromethane evaporation. The resulting suspension was centrifuged at 500 rcf to eliminate excessively large composites. The supernatant was subjected to further centrifugation at 2000 rcf to collect the precipitate, yielding composites of a larger size (MC–PL–PD (L)). Subsequently, the remaining supernatant underwent an additional 6000 rcf-centrifugation to collect smaller-size composites (MC–PL–PD (S)). Following lyophilization, the obtained nanocomposites were maintained at 4 °C until required for subsequent experiments.

Plasmid DNA (pMAX-GFP) Culture and Extraction

For plasmid DNA (pDNA) culturing, pMAX-GFP (pDNA) underwent transformation into Escherichia coli (XL-1 Blue) using a calcium chloride treatment. After transformation, the cells were plated on Luria–Bertani agar fortified with kanamycin (50 mg/mL), and the plates were maintained at 37 °C for 16 h. Subsequently, the obtained colonies resulting from the transformation underwent inoculation via incubation on 5 mL of Luria–Bertani broth supplemented with kanamycin (50 mg/mL) in an orbital shaker at 37 °C for an additional 16 h. Following the incubation period, the medium was subjected to centrifugation at 14,000 rpm, and the obtained cell pellet was then processed with the EndoFree Plasmid Maxi Extraction Column (Qiagen, MD, USA) to extract pDNA.

Preparation of pDNA/MC–PL–PD/PD

The MC–PL–PD NCs were dispersed in Milli-Q water. Subsequently, pDNA was introduced into the MC–PL–PD suspension, maintaining a pDNA:composite w/w ratio of 1:100. The mixture was promptly vortexed for 30 s and incubated at ambient temperature for 10 min to obtain pDNA-incorporated MC–PL–PD nanocomposites (p/MC–PL–PD). Following this, PDMAEMA was added to the mixture, maintaining a PDMAEMA:composite w/w ratio of 5:100. The combined mixture underwent another vortexing session for 30 s and was then incubated at room temperature for an additional 10 min to yield the p/MC–PL–PD/PD nanocomposites.

The pDNA-loading capacity of MC–PL–PD nanocomposites (NCs) was assessed by mixing a suspension of MC–PL–PD NCs (100 μg) with a pDNA solution (1 μg), as described above. Following a 10 min incubation, the magnetic NCs were separated from the suspension using an external magnetic field. The residual pDNA remaining in the supernatant was quantified at 260 nm with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, DE, USA). The loading efficiency was calculated according to the following equation: Loading Efficiency (%) = (Amount of pDNA associated with MC–PL–PD/Amount of used pDNA) × 100%.

Characterization

The synthesized nanoparticles were observed with morphology and size using a Talos F200X G2 transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). The crystalline phase of the nanoparticles was analyzed by using X-ray diffractometry (XRD) (Smart-Lab, Rigaku, Japan). Verification of the polymer coating was conducted through Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) (Nicolet 6700 FT-IR spectrometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). For the nanocomposites, morphology and size were examined using a Zetasizer ZSP particle size analyzer (Malvern, UK) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (JSM-IT100, JEOL, Japan). A custom-built vibrating sample magnetometer (developed by KURDI, Thailand) was utilized to assess the magnetic response at room temperature.

Primary DC Culture

Mouse bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were prepared in accordance with established protocols. , The experimental procedure is thoroughly described in the Supporting Information.

Cytotoxicity Assay

The cytotoxicity of p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (25–100 μg/mL) on BMDCs was assessed using the cytotoxicity assay, followed by previous reports. Briefly, a 96-well plate was used for BMDC seeding in 200 μL of RPMI complete media at a concentration of 1 × 105 cells/well. The cells were then incubated for 24 h to obtain well cell adhesion. Subsequently, the cells were treated with variations of quantities and sizes of p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (25–100 μg/mL) under application of cylindrical neodymium magnets positioned 4 mm under the plate (260 mT) for 1 h. After incubation for 24 and 48 h, the culture medium replacement was then performed with fresh media, and 12 mM MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide, Invitrogen, CA, USA) was filled to the cultured wells and incubated for 1 h, followed by washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and dissolving the formazan crystals using DMSO. The microplate reader (EPOCH2C, Biotek, Agilent Technologies, CA, USA) was used to measure absorbance of the mixture at 570 nm.

Cellular Uptake

For composite preparation, FITC was incorporated into the MC–PL–PD composites. In brief, MC–PL–PD (10 mg) was dispersed in 8 mL of Milli-Q water. Subsequently, 1 mL of FITC solution at a concentration of 2 mg/mL in ethanol was mixed with the prepared particle suspension for 2 h in the dark. Free FITC was removed from the resulting mixture by washing with Milli-Q water until there was loss of fluorescence in the supernatant. Ultimately, the FITC-labeled MC–PL–PD composites were obtained. For MC–PL–PD/PD composites, PDMAEMA was added to the mixture at a PDMAEMA:composite w/w ratio of 5:100. The mixture underwent vortexing for 30 s and incubating for 10 min to yield FITC-labeled MC–PL–PD/PD nanocomposites.

For cellular uptake studies, coverslips were poly-l-lysine coated in 12-well plates via incubation for 18 h and subsequently rinsed with PBS to eliminate unbound poly-l-lysine. Next, 1 mL of BMDCs in RPMI media at a concentration of 5 × 105 cells/mL was added to each well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C under a 5% CO2 atmosphere to obtain well cell adhesion. Then, the cells were treated with various types of FITC-labeled composites (100 μg/mL) under application of cylindrical neodymium magnets positioned 4 mm under the plate (260 mT) for 1 h. After the incubation period and following PBS washing, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 30 min. Nuclei were stained by using a 30 nM solution of DAPI for 5 min. For 10 min, cell membranes were labeled with a deep-red plasma membrane marker (Invitrogen CellMask, Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). After washing with PBS, the stained cells were visualized on a FLUOVIEW FV10i laser scanning confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

In a parallel experiment, 1 mL of BMDCs (5 × 105 cells/mL in RPMI media) was seeded in 24-well plates and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO2. Subsequently, the cells were treated with FITC-labeled composites under a magnetic field for durations ranging from 15 min to 3 h. After being washed with PBS, followed by the incubation period, the cells were labeled with fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against mouse CD11c+, used as a marker for dendritic cells. For the cellular uptake analysis, the fluorescence intensities were performed by flow cytometry analysis with a CytoFLEX instrument (Beckman Coulter, CA, USA). For particle uptake in BMDCs, the percentages were determined using Kaluza analysis software.

Transfection and Maturation in Primary DCs

For evaluation of the transfection efficiency of plasmid DNA into BMDCs, the composites with pDNA combination (p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD with concentration between 25 and 100 μg/mL) were assessed to compare with naked pDNA without any transfection reagent and pDNA-formulated Lipofectamine 2000, a commercial transfection reagent. Initially, BMDCs (1 mL, 2 × 105 cells/mL) were seeded and allowed 24 h of incubation for cell adherence and stabilization. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to treat with p/MC–PL–PD or p/MC–PL–PD/PD in RPMI media under application of magnets (260 mT) under the plate for 1 h. After magnet removal, the cells were further incubated for 24 and 48 h post-treatment. For commercial transfection reagents, pDNA-formulated Lipofectamine 2000 kit (Lipof) was transfected into the cells. As a control condition, the cells were stimulated with naked pDNA. Following transfection, flow cytometry was employed to evaluate the pDNA expression and DC maturation.

To analyze the gene expression and DC maturation, a cell scraper was used to collect BMDCs, whereas the supernatants were subsequently collected and stored separately for cytokine quantification. For the evaluation, the Fc receptor on cells was blocked using the antimouse CD16/32 monoclonal antibody. Subsequently, the cells were stained with fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies against mouse CD11c+, CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC class II. Then, the stained cells were evaluated by using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX) and processed by using Kaluza analysis software.

To assess pDNA transfection efficiency, green fluorescence protein (GFP) expression derived from naked pDNA and pDNA incorporated with the several carrier types was observed via the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI). Subsequently, the MFI of interference was subtracted from the MFI of the cells before incubation.

Cytokine Quantification

The levels of cytokine secretion from BMDCs after transfection were assessed in the supernatant using ELISA, following the manufacturer’s instructions. In brief, a sandwich ELISA was conducted utilizing capture and detection antibodies specified to each cytokine alongside a system of streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase. To access cytokine levels, 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) served as the substrate. A microplate reader (EPOCH2C) was used to quantify the absorbance at 450 nm.

Results and Discussion

Characterization of Particles

The magnetic nanocube (MC) was fabricated by using the thermal decomposition method. Iron­(III) chloride served as the iron precursor, and its ligands were then exchanged with oleic acid under basic conditions to obtain an iron–oleate complex. Subsequently, the iron complex underwent thermal decomposition, leading to the nucleation of Fe–O–Fe clusters before growing into the final MC structure. TEM images showcased cubic shapes with a side length of 12.3 ± 1.2 nm, as illustrated in Figure . For crystalline-phase analysis of the MC, the XRD pattern of the MC revealed dominant peaks at two theta values (30, 35, 43, 57, and 63°), corresponding to the diffraction patterns of γ-Fe2O3 (maghemite, ICSD No. 9006316) and Fe3O4 (magnetite, ICSD No. 9005838) (Figure A). The well-matched pattern confirmed that the MC exhibited a ferrimagnetic character with a strong magnetic response. ,

XRD pattern of MC compared with JCPDS of magnetite (Fe3O4) and maghemite (γ-Fe2O3) (A). FT-IR spectra of magnetic iron oxide nanocubes (MCs), PLGA (PL), PDMAEMA (PD), and as-synthesized MC–PL–PD composites (B).

To achieve encapsulation with biocompatible polymers, the fabrication of MC–PLGA–PDMAEMA nanocomposites (MC–PL–PD) was undertaken through a single emulsion/solvent evaporation method. In the organic phase, the as-synthesized MC and PLGA were dissolved in dichloromethane, while in the aqueous phase, PVA and PDMAEMA were dissolved in water. These phases were homogenized to form an emulsion, and subsequent solvent evaporation within a microcavity resulted in the formation of MC–PL–PD. The resultant MC–PL–PD underwent further size separation into two fractions with estimated sizes of 500 nm (termed MC–PL–PD (L)) and 300 nm (termed MC–PL–PD (S)), achieved through centrifugation. SEM images of MC–PL–PD (L) and MC–PL–PD (S) revealed spherical morphologies with diameters of 505 ± 26 nm and 297 ± 18 nm, respectively (Figure ). TEM images of MC–PL–PD confirmed the encapsulation of MC within the polymer particles, as depicted in Figure S1. The characterization of functional groups on the MC–PL–PD was conducted using FTIR spectra (Figure B) to affirm the surface modification of MC–PL with PDMAEMA. The FTIR spectrum of the as-synthesized MC–PL–PD exhibited a distinct peak at 2773 and 2823 cm–1 corresponding to the symmetric C–H stretching on the tertiary amine of PDMAEMA, indicative of the successful coating of PDMAEMA onto the MC–PL surface.

To facilitate the attachment of plasmid DNA (pDNA) to MC–PL–PD, pDNA was loaded onto the surface of MC–PL–PD through electrostatic interactions between the negatively charged pDNA and the positively charged PDMAEMA on the surface of MC–PL–PD. To confirm the successful attachment of pDNA to MC–PL–PD, the surface charge was assessed by using Zeta-sizer analysis (Table ). In Milli-Q water at pH 5, the zeta potentials of MC–PL–PD were measured at +31.5 and +36.8 mV for MC–PL–PD (L) and MC–PL–PD (S), respectively. The positive zeta potential of MC–PL–PD indicated the presence of positively charged PDMAEMA on their surfaces, contrasting with the negative zeta potential of PLGA composites in our previous work.

After the binding of pDNA, the zeta potentials of p/MC–PL–PD (L) and p/MC–PL–PD (S) notably turned down to +20.8 and +20.0 mV, respectively. This reduction in particle charge confirms the proposed binding interaction between pDNA and the composites through electrostatic interaction, given the negatively charged nature of pDNA and the positively charged nature of MC–PL–PD. As positively charged particles are known to exhibit enhanced internalization by BMDCs, , p/PC–PL–PD was enveloped with PDMAEMA through electrostatic interactions to yield p/PC–PL–PD/PD. Following PDMAEMA coating, the zeta potentials of the larger and small sizes of p/MC–PL–PD composites significantly increased to +29.8 and +34.4 mV, respectively. This augmentation in surface charge corresponds to the binding of p/PC–PL–PD and PDMAEMA through electrostatic interactions. Moreover, considering the hydrodynamic size of p/MC–PL–PD/PD in comparison to MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD, a noticeable increase was observed, indicating the successful completion of surface modification.

The pDNA binding capacity of the nanocomposites was evaluated using an indirect quantification method, whereby the amount of unbound pDNA in the supernatant was measured and subtracted from the initial input. The results showed that MC–PL–PD (L) and MC–PL–PD (S) nanocomposites exhibited pDNA-loading efficiencies of 35.6 ± 4.3% and 40.6 ± 2.01%, respectively, as shown in Table . Notably, the slightly higher efficiency observed for MC–PL–PD (S) suggests that the smaller nanocomposites may provide a more favorable surface area for pDNA binding.

Magnetic Properties

A Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (VSM) was employed to evaluate the magnetic properties of MC and its composites (MC–PL–PD), as illustrated in Figure . The MC displayed a notable high saturation magnetization (Ms) of 11.3 emu/g. Additionally, the MC exhibited superparamagnetic behavior, evidenced by the presence of a narrow hysteresis loop (2.1 Oe). Upon encapsulation into the polymer, MC–PL–PD maintained superparamagnetic behavior with a further reduction in the hysteresis loop, now measuring less than 0.9 Oe. The decrease in the Ms of MC–PL–PD from the initially synthesized MCs to 4.5 emu/g corresponded with a significant reduction in the magnetic response of MC–PL–PD. However, in comparison to the saturation magnetization of magnetic iron oxide nanospheres (MSs) and their composites (MS–PL–PD) of similar size (Figure S2), both MC and its composites (MC–PL–PD) demonstrated 1.5 times increase. These results affirm the enhanced magnetic response of our gene delivery system.

Cytotoxicity

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of the composites, primary mouse dendritic cells (BMDCs) were cultured with plasmid DNA interacting with MC–PL–PD (p/MC–PL–PD) and plasmid DNA interacting with MC–PL–PD/PD (p/MC–PL–PD/PD) at concentrations ranging from 10 to 100 μg/mL (Figure ). At 24- and 48 h postincubation, the MTT assay was employed to measure the cell viability of BMDCs, as depicted in Figure . After incubation for 24 h, all types of composites at concentrations between 10 and 50 μg/mL exhibited higher than 90% of cell viability, showing no notable difference compared to the control and indicating noncytotoxicity. The small size of the composites produced lower toxicity when compared with the larger size. The treatment with both p/MC–PL–PD (L) and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (L) at 100 μg/mL showed a reduction in the cell viability (70–75%) at 24 h. Additionally, p/MC–PL–PD/PD (L) at concentrations of 10 and 50 μg/mL demonstrated increased cytotoxicity at 48 h, when compared with p/MC–PL–PD (L), while p/MC–PL–PD (L) at a concentration of 100 μg/mL appeared to be more toxic. The observed increase in toxicity at higher concentrations of the composite particles may be attributed to the augmented particle size (L) and the elevated content of PDMAEMA. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating that PDMAEMA could induce cytotoxicity in various cell types at different concentrations. Despite the reduction in cell viability observed in the dendritic cells exposed to the larger-sized composites, the cell viability remained approximately 70–80% at both 24 and 48 h, indicating favorable cell viability.

PDMAEMA is known to exhibit cell-type- and condition-dependent cytotoxicity, with higher molecular weight (MW) polymers showing increased toxicity. For instance, PDMAEMA with MW ranging from 43 kDa to 915 kDa was reported to cause cytotoxicity in human brain microvascular endothelial cells. In contrast, magnetic nanocubes coated with low-MW PDMAEMA showed favorable cell viability on L929 and Raw264.7 cells in our previous study. Subsequently, PDMAEMA (∼73 kDa)-coated magnetic nanocubes displayed low toxicity toward dendritic cells (DCs) while achieving high transfection efficiency and immune suppression. These findings suggest that the nanocomposite architecture likely shields the cationic charge of PDMAEMA, thereby reducing immune activation compared with free PDMAEMA. To further address residual cytotoxicity, we propose copolymerizing PDMAEMA with PLGA, which has been shown to reduce charge density and improve dendritic cell compatibility, while promoting tolerogenic immune responses. Although elucidating the precise molecular mechanisms underlying DC inactivation is beyond the scope of the current study, our data support the conclusion that both polymer design (i.e., low MW, reduced charge density) and nanocomposite structure contribute to the observed immune-suppressive effects. BMDCs were incubated with the composites labeled with a green fluorescent dye or FITC for 1 h under application of an external magnet (260 mT). The green fluorescence from MC–PL–PD/PD (L) and (S) was discovered within the cell membrane (on red region), whereas the green fluorescence from MC–PL–PD (L) and (S) was not clearly evident.

This suggests that MC–PL–PD/PD (L) and MC–PL–PD/PD (S) were effectively taken up into the BMDCs and confined to the area around the nuclei (on the blue region) within boundaries of the cell membrane. Flow cytometric analysis was employed to quantify the uptake of FITC-labeled MC–PL–PD (100 μg/mL) by BMDCs exposed to an external magnetic field during incubation times between 15 min and 3 h (Figure B). The MC–PL–PD exhibited over 25% internalization after a 15 min incubation, with a significant increase up to 66% observed at the 3 h mark. Comparatively, when assessing the cellular uptake of MC–PL–PD/PD, their internalization demonstrated a significant difference, reaching 97% after just 15 min of incubation.

The enhanced cellular uptake of MC–PL–PD/PD (L) and MC–PL–PD/PD (S) is likely attributed to the PDMAEMA coating, which contributes to an increased surface charge (Table ), thereby enhancing internalization into BMDCs for this nonviral gene delivery system. Despite the substantial increase in the internalization of MC–PL–PD with an extended incubation duration from 1 to 3 h, we have selected an incubation time of 1 h under existence of applied external magnetic field for subsequent experiments of gene transfection and DC maturation to minimize the incubation duration.

To confirm the superior efficiency of magnetic nanocomposites in delivering cargo into primary DCs, we evaluated the effect of external magnetic induction on the uptake of MC–PL–PD/PD composites by BMDCs, thereby validating the magnetofection concept. Internalization of FITC-tagged MC–PL–PD/PD composites was quantified under conditions with (MC–PL–PD/PD) and without (MC–PL–PD/PD) an applied magnetic field, as illustrated in Figures S3 and S4. In the absence of magnetic induction, internalization rates of MC–PL–PD/PD (L) and MC–PL–PD/PD (S) were 2.55% and 2.59%, respectively, after 15 min of incubation. By contrast, application of a 260 mT magnetic field markedly enhanced uptake, reaching 19.3% and 22.4% for MC–PL–PD/PD (L) and MC–PL–PD/PD (S), respectively, at the same time point. The sustained superparamagnetic behavior of the magnetic nanocubes (MCs) within the composites accounts for this increased cellular uptake, thereby supporting the magnetofection concept and highlighting their potential as an efficient nonviral gene delivery system for BMDCs.

In Vitro Transfection in Primary DCs

The pMAX-GFP (pDNA) was used as a model gene to transfect into the BMDCs to generate green fluorescence protein (GFP). The expression level of GFP was measured to assess the transfection efficiency. GFP expression from pDNA cooperated with p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD was compared with bare pDNA and pDNA complexed with a commercial transfection reagent, Lipofectamine 2000 (Lipof). The Lipof is recognized for its effectiveness as a nonviral carrier, yielding high transfection efficiency. Before conducting studies on BMDCs, we performed a preliminary assessment of transfection efficiency with variation of concentrations of p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (10–100 μg/mL) on HEK 293T cells in the presence of an external magnetic field (Figure S5). Composites at 10 μg/mL showed no significant increase in transfection efficiency compared to naked pDNA, while composites at concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 μg/mL were able to deliver pDNA into HEK 293T cells. Consequently, composite concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 μg/mL were selected for further transfection efficiency studies in BMDCs. Additionally, we examined transfection efficiency with various amounts of pDNA (0.2–1 μg/100 μg composites) on HEK 293T cells in the presence of an external magnetic field (Figure S6). The composites with a pDNA amount of 1 μg showed a greater transfection efficiency than Lipof. Therefore, composites with a pDNA-loading amount of 1 μg were chosen for subsequent transfection efficiency studies in BMDCs.

In Vitro Maturation of BMDCs

The maturation process of DCs represents a critical profile in controlled drug delivery, gene delivery, and vaccination. Given the focus on biocompatible composites or carriers of this study, it was crucial that the composites should either induce minimal stimulation or not provoke an immune response during the gene delivery process. The influence of p/MC–PL–PD and p/MC–PL–PD/PD on BMDC maturation was assessed by incubating BMDCs with these composites under the influence of an external magnetic field and evaluated in comparison to treatments with bare pDNA and Lipof. To evaluate DC maturation, the surface levels of specific DC maturation markers such as CD40, CD80, CD86, and MHC class II on DC marker, CD11c+, and cell were subsequently assessed using fluorescence-conjugated monoclonal antibodies and flow cytometry. The investigation focused on pDNA-complexed composites at a 48 h incubation time, as illustrated in Figure . As opposed to the negative control, naked pDNA did not induce the expression of all surface markers, possibly due to poor immunogenicity and low internalization of naked pDNA into BMDCs. Both 25 and 50 μg of pDNA-complexed composites did not lead to any further enhancement of BMDC maturation at 48 h. The p/MC–PL–PD (L) and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (L) composites (100 μg) exhibited a persistent DC maturation. Meanwhile, 100 μg of p/MC–PL–PD (S) and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (S) composites had no effect on DC maturation after 48 h of incubation time. Therefore, MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD appear to be biocompatible for in vivo delivery of BMDCs, with minimal concerns regarding DC stimulation.

Dendritic Cell Cytokine Production

For investigation of the effect of composites onto the production of cytokines from BMDCs, the inflammatory mediators: IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, categorized as proinflammatory cytokines, alongside IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, were assessed in the supernatant of BMDCs incubated with composites (50 and 100 μg/mL) under the application of external magnets for 1 h. Following the removal of the magnetic field, incubation was additionally continued for 48 h. As illustrated in Figure , the cells with transfection of pDNA-incorporated composites displayed reduced cytokine production levels contrasted with the control. Specifically, the BMDCs transfected with all types of composites showed no alteration in the production of IL-6. However, BMDCs transfected with p/MC–PL–PD (S) and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (S) demonstrated reduced IL-1β production, whereas the larger composites did not have the same effect. Additionally, all types of composites interfered with the TNF-α production. Notably, p/MC–PL–PD (S) and p/MC–PL–PD/PD (S), and to a lesser extent p/MC–PL–PD/PD (L), significantly diminished IL-10 production in BMDCs.

Previous studies have also demonstrated the anti-inflammatory activity of PDMAEMA. , Nanoparticles coated with PDMAEMA were shown to decrease the activation of BMDCs and reduce cytokine production in fibroblasts, macrophages, and BMDCs. , Consistently, our results demonstrated that single and double coatings with PDMAEMA exhibited an anti-inflammatory effect in BMDCs. Moreover, the smaller size of the particles produced a greater anti-inflammatory effect compared with larger sizes. This effect may be attributed to the lower cytotoxicity (Figure ) and the reduced BMDC stimulation capability of the smaller particles (Figure ). Despite the anti-inflammatory effect of MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD, these nanocomposites retained a significant ability for gene transduction (Figure ). Therefore, MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD, especially in smaller sizes, have the potential to serve as effective carriers for the introduction of genetic material into primary dendritic cells without adversely affecting the activation of dendritic cells.

Conclusion

Nanocomposites comprising magnetic nanocubes and biocompatible polymers, PLGA and PDMAEMA (MC–PL–PD), were successfully synthesized by using the single emulsion/solvent evaporation method. Both MC and MC–PL–PD exhibited superparamagnetic behavior, with a modest increase in magnetization (Ms) approximately 1.5 times compared to that of MS and MS–PL–PD. The resulting MC–PL–PD, featuring a positive surface charge, effectively incorporated negatively charged pDNA through electrostatic interaction. Furthermore, p/MC–PL–PD demonstrated an increased surface charge through recoating with positively charged PDMAEMA. At concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 μg/mL, all composites demonstrated minimal toxicity toward bone-marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs), and under a magnetic field, all composites were actively captured by BMDCs. Notably, MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD composites demonstrated superior gene transduction efficiency into BMDCs compared to that of the lipofection system. Additionally, these composites displayed an anti-inflammatory effect and low immunostimulatory activity, potentially facilitating DC manipulation. Consequently, MC–PL–PD and MC–PL–PD/PD present themselves as an effective nonplatform for delivering genes during the in vivo transfection process of primary DCs, offering the potential to silence immune responses effectively.
