Changing patterns of education, marriage and fertility in Chinese adolescents: a systematic synthesis of national and subnational population data from 1990 to 2020
Authors: Dongmei Luo, Xiaojin Yan, Tianyu Huang, Jiajia Dang, Shan Cai, Yi Song, Susan M Sawyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundSecondary education, and early marriage and childbirth are major determinants of health and wellbeing. We aimed to describe the long-term trends and inequalities of these three indicators in Chinese adolescents over three decades of dramatic social and economic change.MethodsUsing data from Chinese population censuses and 1% population sample surveys from 1990 to 2020 (the most recent survey), we calculated the senior secondary school participation rate in 20–24-year-olds, the ever-married rate in 15–19-year-olds, and the age-specific fertility rate in 15–19 year-old females, by urban-rural location and province.FindingsIn urban areas, the secondary school participation rate substantially increased, from 34.7% in 1990 to 82.4% in 2020 for females and from 38.7% to 77.0% for males. The participation rate in rural areas also greatly increased, from 5.0% in 1990 to 55.7% in 2020 for females and from 9.1% to 51.0% for males, but remained markedly lower than in urban areas at all time points. The ever-married rate decreased dramatically from 1990 to 2005, rebounded between 2005 and 2015, and then dropped so that the rate in 2020 was at a level comparable to that in 2005 (0.7% [95% CI 0.7–0.7] for urban females, 2.2% [2.1–2.2] for rural females, 0.2% [0.2–0.2] for urban males, and 0.5% [0.5–0.5] for rural males). In urban areas, the fertility rate decreased from 10.1 per 1000 in 1990 to 2.4 (2.3–2.4) per 1000 in 2000, but then largely stabilised between 2000 and 2020. For rural adolescents, the fertility rate also decreased dramatically from 25.9 per 1000 in 1990 to 8.5 (8.2–8.8) per 1000 in 2005. The fertility rate rebounded to 15.4 (14.9–15.9) per 1000 in 2015 and decreased to 12.5 (12.3–12.7) in 2020 in rural areas. These three indicators greatly varied by province in 2020, with more negative patterns in western provinces.InterpretationChina has seen dramatic changes in adolescent education, and early marriage and fertility over the past three decades but large inequities persist between urban-rural areas and by province. Government investments are needed to promote education in rural areas and western regions to promote more equitable health, development and wellbeing in Chinese adolescents.FundingNational Nature Science Foundation of China.Keywords: China, Adolescents, Social determinants of health, Participation in education, Early marriage, Adolescent childbirth

Research in context

Evidence before this study

In China, rapid demographic, social, and economic changes over the past three decades have major implications for participation in education, early marriage and childbirth in adolescents. We searched PubMed with terms (“China” [Mesh]) AND ((“Adolescent” [Mesh]) OR (“Young Adult” [Mesh])) AND ((“Educational Status” [Mesh]) OR (“Marriage” [Mesh]) OR (“Birth Rate” [Mesh]) OR (“Fertility” [Mesh])) and searched Wanfang (a Chinese database) with terms “zhongguo” (China) AND (“qingshaonian” (adolescent) OR “qingnian” (young Adult)) AND (“shoujiaoyuchengdu” (educational status) OR “jiehun” (marriage) OR “zaohun” (early marriage) OR “shengyulv” (birth rate) OR “zaoyu” (early parenthood)) on May 23, 2025, for articles published from January 1, 1990, without language restrictions. Despite its association with health, we found no comprehensive reports of trends and inequalities of educational status in Chinese adolescents. A report on the trend of marriage and fertility rates in 15–19-year-old adolescent females in China was found that used data from the Chinese National Population Census and 1% National Population Sample Survey from 1990 to 2015. Concerningly, this report found that after a period of steady decline, there was an unexpected increase in marriage and childbearing in adolescent females from 2005 to 2015. Access to the most recent surveys provides the opportunity to understand if this rebound phenomenon has been sustained in the new decade.

Added value of this study

We used data from the Chinese National Population Census and the Chinese 1% National Population Sample Survey from 1990 to 2020 (the most recent survey) to analyse long-term trends and subnational disparities of participation in secondary education, early marriage and childbirth among adolescents over the past three decades. Over this period, the senior secondary school participation rate dramatically increased in urban and rural males and females (from 38 to 51 percentage points). Our data reveals that the rebound in early marriage and adolescent fertility from 2005 to 2015 in the previous report has not been sustained, with a decrease in marriage and fertility rate in 15–19-year-olds from 2015 to 2020, which is likely to have been driven by the increased participation in secondary education. However, urban-rural and subnational disparities persist in secondary school participation, early marriage, and fertility in adolescents, with a substantially more negative pattern in rural areas and western provinces.

Implications of all the available evidence

Economic growth and education policies have contributed to dramatic improvements in the educational participation of Chinese adolescents over the past three decades. These improvements are likely to have overtaken the effect of a male-biased sex ratio that appeared to have halted previous declines in early marriage and adolescent fertility. However, substantial urban-rural and subnational gaps in education, early marriage and fertility persist, consistent with China's uneven economic and social development. Given the significance of education for health and the context of China's ageing society, these findings suggest that greater investment in education is particularly needed in rural and western regions in China.

Introduction

Education, early marriage, and early childbirth are among the headline indictors for tracking the health and wellbeing of adolescents.1,2 Participation in education is the strongest determinant of health in adolescents, associated with future economic opportunities, as well as adolescent births and mortality.1, 2, 3 Beyond greater risks for pregnancy complications, maternal death and intimate partner violence, early marriage and early childbirth (before 18 years of age) can profoundly effect young women's lives and that of their children by limiting educational opportunities and future employment prospects, among other factors.4,5 Early marriage may also signal early fatherhood in boys which can result in additional economic pressures that may similarly constrain their access to ongoing education.6 Over the past two decades, there has been marked improvement in adolescent participation in education, accompanied by a decline in early marriage and adolescent livebirths, although this greatly varies by country as well as subnationally.2,6,7

Since 1986, Chinese education policy has promulgated nine years of free compulsory education. China has also experienced unprecedented economic growth over the past few decades, with its gross domestic product (GDP) increasing 14 fold from 1990–2020.8 This economic growth has been accompanied by a rapid increase in city living, with the urbanisation rate increasing from 26% in 1990 to 64% in 2020.9 Altogether these shifts in educational policy, wealth and urbanisation have contributed to higher rates of educational participation. Yet while a Chinese government report estimated a 95.5% completion rate of nine years of education, it is uncertain what factors are responsible for this.10

Economic and demographic changes might each be expected to shape attitudes towards decision-making around marriage, childbirth and parenting in Chinese adolescents. Improvement in educational participation in China over recent decades is likely to have reduced early marriage by keeping adolescents in school. Economic growth and urbanisation are also expected to increase access to health services, including contraception, and reduce the rate of early childbirth as well as the demand for abortion. Beyond these factors, China's birth-limiting policies—the 1980 one-child policy, replaced in 2015 by a universal two-child policy and then a three-child policy in 202111—are also likely to impact the “marriage market” and subsequent rates of childbirth.

In China, we previously found that in 2015, the ever-married rate in 15–19 year old girls was 2.4% with an age-specific fertility rate of 9.2 per 1000.12 These rates had decreased in the decade between 1990 and 2000 but both indicators unexpectedly increased between 2000 and 2015. We proposed that this fertility rebound might reflect China's male-biased sex ratio, its large population of migrant workers, as well as unmet need for contraception. That report was based on data spanning 1990 to 2015 from the Chinese National Population Census and the Chinese 1% National Population Sample Survey.12 New data from the Chinese National Population Census for 2020 (the most recent survey) enables us to understand more recent trends to determine if the increase in adolescent fertility that we previously described has been sustained.12

Understanding inequalities is an important consideration in China where marked urban-rural divisions have signaled significant inequities.13 The urban-rural dual household registration (hukou) system was first implemented in the late 1950's to limit population growth in China's large cities. People with urban and rural household registration continue to differ remarkably in their access to quality education, medical care and job opportunities.14 Geographical difference is another aspect of inequality, with faster economic growth in China's southeast regions compared with its western regions.13 Understanding changing patterns in urban-rural and regional differences in adolescent health and wellbeing, including educational participation, is needed to inform future policies that are intended to reduce any persisting differences.

In this study, using nationally representative data from the Chinese National Population Census and the Chinese 1% National Population Sample Survey from 1990 to 2020, we aimed to describe long-term trends in participation in secondary education, early marriage and early childbirth for Chinese adolescents, and to then analyse disparities across these three indicators with respect to urban-rural and geographical locations.

Methods

Study design and participants

Data were extracted from the Chinese National Population Census in 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020 and the Chinese 1% National Population Sample Survey in 1995, 2005, and 2015.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 (Fig. 1) The sampling and data collection procedures have been previously described.12 These censuses and 1% sample surveys were designed with comparable sampling frameworks, questionnaires, and quality control procedures, enabling consistent estimation of the demographic indicators across time. The 1% sample surveys provide interim estimates between censuses, with smaller sample sizes but similar methodology. Specifically, these censuses included all individuals of Chinese nationality who resided in China at the time of the survey. International in-migration (<0.1%) is minimal in China. For the 1% sample survey, communities/villages were randomly selected using the probability-proportion-to-size method. All residents of Chinese nationality in the selected communities/villages were included in the survey, which represented 1.03%, 1.33%, and 1.55% of the whole population in 1995, 2005, and 2015, respectively.19, 20, 21

Flow chart of Chinese National Population Census and 1% National Population Sample Survey, 1990–2020.

Survey forms for both the national census and the 1% national sample surveys were completed and verified by trained enumerators at the household residence based upon responses provided by the householder or others in the household who were familiar with the family. The same short and long forms were used for both the census and the 1% sample survey. The short form included information on basic demographic characteristics (sex, date of birth, ethnicity, residency, as well as education level). The long form covered additional information including marital status (unmarried, married, divorced, or widowed), whether a child had been born during the last year (date of birth and sex of the child), occupation, etc. In the 1990 census, all individuals completed the long form. In 2000, 2010, and 2020, a 10% sample of randomly selected houses completed the longer survey. For the 1% national sample surveys, all participants completed the long form of the survey. Post-enumeration surveys estimated that the undercount rates (the percentage of individuals missed in the enumeration process) for each of the four censuses was 0.07% in 1990, 1.81% in 2000, 0.12% in 2010, and 0.05% in 2020.15, 16, 17, 18 The undercount rate for the 1% sample surveys was 1.72% in 2005 and 0.54% in 2015 (no rate was reported in 1995).19, 20, 21

Data on education, marriage, and childbirth were obtained from aggregated tabulations published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China, which are based on these population censuses and 1% sample surveys, and include both national- and provincial-level data.15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 The population of people living in ethnic minority autonomous areas in a province was obtained from China's Ethnic Statistical Yearbooks, which was also based on the censuses and sample surveys.22 We obtained the nominal gross domestic product (GDP) per capita for provinces from 1990 to 2020 from the Chinese Statistical Yearbook.9 Relevant data for Xinjiang in 2020 had not been released by the local government at the time of this analysis. The sample size for each sex (males or females) and residency group (urban or rural) in our education analyses ranged from 9,636,685 to 46,583,712 for the population census, and from 152,979 to 506,034 for the sample surveys.

Statistical analysis

In China, compulsory education covers primary school (grades 1–6) and junior secondary school (grades 7–9). Senior secondary school comprises grades 10–12, which may be academic or vocational. The senior secondary school participation rate (SPR) was calculated in 20–24-year-olds, defined as the number who had ever attended senior high school (regardless of completion)/the total population of this age group × 100%. This age group (20–24 years) was chosen in order to accurately ascertain participation in senior secondary education. The ever-married rate (EMR) and age-specific fertility rate (ASFR) were calculated for 15-19-year-olds. The EMR (%) was defined as (1 – the number of those never married/the total population of this age group) × 100%. The ASFR (per 1000) was defined as the number of births last year/the total number of females for the age group × 1000. We displayed the SPR, EMR and ASFR by sex, urban-rural location (communities/villages located in urban or rural areas), and province. The EMRs by urban-rural location in 2000 were not reported by the National Bureau of Statistics and so are missing for this analysis. The EMRs are also reported by the highest level of education that the individual had ever attended (regardless of completion), which were primary school (grades 1–6) or no schooling, junior secondary school (grades 7–9), senior secondary school (grades 10–12) or higher.

We built multivariable random-effects generalised least squares regression models on panel data to test if adolescent marriage or childbearing were correlated with education level, sex ratio, and proportion of ethnic minority populations in a province. We assessed the appropriateness of the random-effects specification by comparing random-effects and fixed-effects models using the Hausman test. In those models, the group variable is province while the time variable is year. The dependent variables were provincial EMR or ASFR, which were examined separately. The proportion of the population from an ethnic minority was defined as the proportion of people living in ethnic minority autonomous areas in a province. This refers to administrative areas where one or more ethnic minorities live and are legally able to execute regional autonomy. Eleven of 31 provinces have no ethnic minority autonomous areas. These random-effects generalised least squares regression models included provincial data in 1990, 2000, 2010, and 2020. In 1990 Chongqing was excluded from the models because it was not considered as a municipality until 1997. In 2020 Xinjiang was excluded because of missing data. A Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) was used to guide the selection of covariates for each regression model. All analyses were conducted with Stata version 18.0 and R version 4.1.0. We used the packages ggplot2 and sf in R to display the subnational patterns of adolescent education, marriage and childbearing.

Ethical approval

This study used publicly available aggregated data from the Chinese National Population Census and the 1% National Population Sample Survey, published by the National Bureau of Statistics of China. As these are de-identified, with aggregated statistics in the public domain, neither ethical approval nor a formal waiver was required.

Role of funding source

The funder of the study (the National Nature Science Foundation of China) had no role in the design of the study, data collection, analysis, interpretation, or writing of the manuscript.

Results

Fig. 2 and Supplement Table S1 show the national trends in adolescent education, marriage, and fertility from 1990 to 2020. The SPR for 20-24-year-olds greatly increased over these three decades for both sexes. In urban areas, the SPR increased from 34.7% in 1990 to 82.4% in 2020 for females and from 38.7% to 77.0% for males. While the SPR was lower in rural than urban areas at all time points, the SPR in rural areas had also increased from 5.0% in 1990 to 55.7% in 2020 for females and from 9.1% to 51.0% for males. This resulted in a persistently large urban-rural gap over this period, with urban females and males having 26.0–38.9 percentage point higher SPR rates than their rural counterparts. In 1990, males had around 5 percentage point higher SPRs than females in both urban and rural areas. A reverse of this sex difference was first noted in 2015 in urban areas but not until 2020 in rural areas, with females ending up with around a five percentage point higher rate than males in both urban and rural areas in 2020.

SPR = senior secondary school participation rate (20–24 years), EMR = ever-married rate (age 15–19 years), ASFR = age-specific fertility rate (age 15–19 years).

For those aged 15–19 years in 1990, the EMR was over two times higher in rural females compared with urban females (5.5% vs. 2.0%) and in rural males compared with urban males (2.1% vs. 0.8%) (Fig. 2). These rates decreased greatly between 1990 and 2005 and then rebounded between 2005 and 2015 in both urban and rural areas. In 2020, the rates decreased to a similar level to that in 2005, with an over two-fold urban rural gap (0.7% [95% CI 0.7–0.7] for urban females, 2.2% [2.1–2.2] for rural females, 0.2% [0.2–0.2] for urban males, and 0.5% [0.5–0.5] for rural males).

In urban areas, there was a 76% decrease in the ASFR for females aged 15–19 years between 1990 (10.1 per 1000) and 2000 (2.4 per 1000 [95% CI 2.3–2.4]) (Fig. 2). The rates then fluctuated slightly between 2000 and 2020, ranging from 2.4 (2.3–2.4) to 4.1 (3.9–4.3) per 1000. In rural areas, the ASFR decreased by 67% between 1990 and 2005, from 25.9 to 8.5 (8.2–8.8) per 1000. The rate rebounded to 15.4 per 1000 (14.9–15.9) in 2015 and then decreased slightly to 12.5 (12.3–12.7) in 2020. There has also been a persisting urban-rural gap in fertility; in 1990 there was nearly a 3-fold increase between the ASFR in rural vs. urban females, which had increased to a difference of around 4-fold by 2020.

Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6 and Supplement Tables S2–S3 show the three indicators (SPR, EMR, ASFR) by China's 31 provinces in 2020. Across these provinces, the SPR in 2020 ranged from 45.6% in Tibet to 92.6% in Beijing in females, and from 41.7% in Tibet to 85.2% in Beijing in males (Fig. 3). The rates were generally lower in western and southern areas for both females and males. The SPR differences between urban and rural areas were generally high (Fig. 3). Nine provinces had an urban-rural difference in SPR that was greater than 30 percentage points in females and 11 provinces had a similar difference in males. In 2020 across provinces, the EMR in females ranged from 0.3% (95% CI 0.3–0.4) in Tianjin and Shanxi to 4.4% (4.1–4.7) in Qinghai (Fig. 4). The EMR in rural females was >3% in most western provinces (Gansu, Guizhou, Ningxia, Qinghai, Tibet, Yunnan), Guangxi, Hainan, and Shanghai (Fig. 6). In these nine provinces, the rate in rural areas was 2.2 (in Hainan) to 5.8 percentage point (in Ningxia) larger than that in urban areas in females. The overall EMR was low in males in most provinces. The overall ASFR in females by province ranged from 1.0 per 1000 (0.6–1.3) in Beijing to 24.60 per 1000 (23.7–25.5) in Guizhou in 2020 (Fig. 5). Higher rates (>10 per 1000) were seen in most western provinces (Gansu, Guizhou, Ningxia, Qinghai, Tibet, Yunnan), Guangxi, and Hainan. Large rural-urban differences were also found in these eight provinces.

The EMR differed dramatically by education level (Fig. 7 and Supplement Table S4). In 2020, the EMR was 13.0% (12.7–13.4) for females with an education level of primary school or no schooling, three times that of females who attended only junior secondary school (3.9% [3.9–4.0]), and 43 times that of females who attended senior secondary school (0.3% [0.3–0.3]). For males, the rate was 2.0% (1.8–2.1), 0.8% (0.8–0.9), and 0.1% (0.1–0.1) for these three education levels, respectively. The rebound in EMR since 2000 was apparent in females with lower education levels. Specifically, the EMR rebounded nearly three-fold, from 4.9% (4.9–5.0) in 2000 to 13.6% (13.5–13.8) in 2010 in females with a primary school education level. There was no evidence of returning to the previous trajectory, as it remained relatively stable at 13.0% (12.7–13.4) in 2020. For girls with junior secondary school education, the rate also rebounded three-fold, from 0.9% (0.9–0.9) in 2000 to 3.3% (3.3–3.3) in 2010. However, there was a further increase in EMR to 3.9% (3.9–4.0) in 2020 in females having junior secondary school education.

Discussion

Using nationally representative data from China, this study of trends and inequalities in education, early marriage and childbirth in adolescents shows dramatic improvements in these three indicators across the past three decades. Large disparities persist between urban and rural areas and across provinces and the early marriage rate was particularly high in girls who did not attend secondary school. The rebound in early marriage and childbirth from 2000 to 2015 that we previously reported12 has not persisted, but while the early marriage rate and adolescent fertility decreased from the unexpectedly rising rates seen during that period, the most recent rates are generally comparable to the levels seen in 2005.

Senior secondary school participation has strikingly increased over the past three decades, with a two-fold improvement in urban areas (from ∼37% to ∼80%) and eight-fold improvement in rural areas (from ∼7% to ∼53%). These improvements compare favorably to other high income countries (∼61%),7 although rates are not directly comparable due to differences in reporting. In China, rather than reporting completion of 12 years of education, participation (enrolment) is reported in senior secondary schooling (years 10–12), which highlights that drop-out may be important to consider. For example, a survey of 7414 students from the upper-secondary technical and vocational education and training schools in two provinces of China (Shaanxi and Zhejiang) in 2011–2012 showed that 10.7% students dropped out in their first academic year (year 10 of senior secondary schooling).23 The extent of progress in educational participation in China most likely reflects rapid economic growth, as China's GDP has increased 14 fold over the past three decades from 1030 billion US dollars in 1990 to 14,620 billion US dollars in 2020 (in constant 2015 prices).8 Investment in education has concurrently improved; in 1992, government expenditure in education was only 1.7% of GDP but this gradually increased to meet the global average proportion in 2012 (4.3%) where it has remained.8 The labour force participation rate among Chinese youth aged 15–24 years has also declined from 78% in 1990 to 57% in 2010 (the most recent available data).24 This decline reflects a growing prioritization of education, which in turn contributes to delay in marriage and childbearing.

China's ASFR in 2020 was relatively low in 15–19 year olds (3.4 per 1000 in urban areas and 12.5 in rural areas) compared with high-income countries (9.6 livebirths per 1000 in 2021).25 We previously attributed the rebound in early marriage and adolescent fertility between 2000 and 2015 to a male-biased sex ratio in the so-called marriage market, more sexual activity without reliable contraception, and the large number of internal migrant workers (as many of them are poorly educated, with little sexuality education and poor access to contraception) during this period.12 In the current study, reductions in early marriage and adolescent fertility between 2015 and 2020 in both urban and rural areas might reflect the strong impact of continuing improvement in educational participation. The updated regression analysis in the current study highlights the importance of education and signaled less influence of a male-biased sex ratio at birth. However it is noteworthy that in contrast to these overall trends, early marriage in girls with only primary schooling (13.0%) or junior secondary schooling (3.9%) remained unchanged from the rebound seen in 2010. This suggests that further efforts are needed to keep girls at school, and that comprehensive sexuality education might be particularly beneficial in adolescent girls with low levels of education.

Although the overall pattern is positive, there is no evidence that the stark urban-rural gap in education, early marriage, and adolescent fertility has lessened over the past three decades. Moreover, the provincial differences in these three indicators, that were also apparent in the 2020 census, are consistent with persisting relative disadvantages in China's western provinces. Differences in senior secondary education participation might reflect the uneven social and economic development between rural and urban areas and across provinces. Although China achieved >90% completion rate for nine years of compulsory education,10 the quality of this education may not be ideal in rural and less developed regions due to inadequate funding provided by local governments, compounded by poorer quality teaching due to urban and more developed regions commonly attracting better teachers.26,27 These factors may limit the academic achievements of students in rural and less developed regions and restrict them from participating in senior secondary education. Compared with urban adolescents, parents of rural adolescents with lower socioeconomic status may also have lower expectations of their children's education and may have less capacity to directly and indirectly support the education of their children.28

In this study, rural girls were more likely to be married and have children compared with their urban counterparts. This difference can largely be explained by the lower educational participation in rural adolescents and the gender norms in rural areas in less developed provinces which shape expectations that girls who are not at school get married and have children at an early age.29 Provision of comprehensive sexuality education and access to contraception may also be more challenging in rural areas and be another contributing factor.30 Accompanied by low rates of senior secondary educational participation, the marriage and fertility rates in 15–19-year-old girls were extremely high in the rural areas in Guizhou, Ningxia, Qinghai, and Yunnan provinces. Greater investment from the Chinese Government is especially needed in these areas to promote greater participation in schooling for disadvantaged girls. These same provinces also have a high proportion of ethnic minorities.22 Consistent with our regression analyses, an alternative explanation for these differences in rates are that cultural norms in ethnic minorities shape more positive attitudes towards early marriage and childbirth.31 Depending on local customs, ethnic minorities in China have generally been allowed to marry two years earlier (18 years for women and 20 years for men) compared with their Han counterparts. In rural minority communities, strong gender norms, family expectations, and community pressure may outweigh the benefits of schooling in shaping the timing of marriage or childbearing in adolescents.32 To address this, multi-level interventions beyond school-based programs, including community engagement and initiatives that challenge gender norms might be required.

This systematic study of long-term trends, urban-rural gaps, and subnational differences in education, early marriage and child-bearing substantially extends our previous study12 by including data from the most recent census, including data on secondary education, and by urban-rural locations within a province. There are also some limitations, some of which were noted in our previous report.12 Importantly, marriage and childbirth may be under-reported in these Chinese censuses and sampling surveys, due to factors such as cohabitation or unions before the legal age, and stigma around unplanned births. An analysis of the 2000 census indicated that around 19% of children at ages 0–4 were unreported, particularly those born outside legal frameworks and to younger mothers.33 Thus, our study likely underestimates the true prevalence and disparities of early marriage and adolescent fertility in China. In addition, this report relied on the aggregated tabulations published by the National Bureau of Statistics, but these indicators are not completely consistent with global practice.7 Specifically, we report the secondary education participation rate rather than the completion rate, and ever-married rate in 15–19-year-olds rather than the rate of marrying before 18 years of age. Furthermore, relying on provincial rather than individual measures in the correlation analyses could raise a possibility of an ecological fallacy. Finally, these correlations do not imply causality, as unmeasured confounding may exist.

As a result of economic growth and education policies, China has seen dramatic improvement in adolescent education over the past three decades. These improvements look like they have overshadowed the effect of a male-biased sex ratio that appeared to have previously halted declines in early marriage and adolescent fertility. However, substantial urban-rural and subnational gaps in education, early marriage and fertility persist, consistent with China's uneven economic and social development. Government investments are particularly indicated to promote access to quality secondary education in rural areas, which may include subsidies to support disadvantaged students, incentives and professional development for rural teachers, and improvements to educational infrastructure. Given the strength of education as a social determinant of health, these efforts are expected to further decrease early marriage and adolescent fertility in China, and promote more equitable development, health and wellbeing.
