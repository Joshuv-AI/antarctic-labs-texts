Identification of circulating biomarkers and immune subtypes for diabetic retinopathy through multi-cohort integrative analysis
Authors: Jiwei Zhang, Linglin Zhang, Zhihong Yu, Gaojie Yang, Liling Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo elucidate the dynamic molecular evolution and immune heterogeneity in diabetic retinopathy (DR) and identify diagnostic biomarkers.MethodsMulti-omics integrative analysis was performed on peripheral blood transcriptomic data from diabetic patients with and without DR. Techniques included differential expression analysis, immune infiltration profiling, unsupervised clustering, and LASSO regression for biomarker selection. Findings were validated in an independent high-glucose-treated endothelial cell model.ResultsDR progression exhibits staged molecular evolution from diabetes (immune suppression) to early DR (cell cycle inhibition, immune burst) and advanced DR (metabolic stress, persistent immune dysregulation). Six co-downregulated immune modules indicated systemic immune suppression. Patients were classified into three immune subtypes: vascular-inflammatory, metabolic disorder, and oxidative stress-tissue remodeling. A diagnostic model based on IK, PLBD2, FLNA, H6PD, and ZMAT2 achieved an AUC of 0.876 for distinguishing DR from diabetes alone.ConclusionThis study delineates the dynamic molecular landscape and immune heterogeneity of DR, providing a potential multi-gene diagnostic signature and a framework for patient stratification, which may facilitate early detection and personalized management.Keywords: diabetic retinopathy, peripheral blood transcriptome, molecular typing, immune microenvironment, biomarkers

1. Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a microangiopathy caused by the chronic effects of diabetes mellitus (DM), Global estimates suggest that approximately 35% of people with diabetes have some degree of diabetic retinopathy, with around 10% developing vision-threatening disease. 1 DR is a highly prevalent retinal vascular disease and ranks as the fifth leading cause of moderate to severe visual impairment in individuals aged 50 and above. With the increasing lifespan of DM patients, the number of individuals with DR and associated visual impairments is expected to rise rapidly. 2 In Taiwan, the prevalence of DR ranged from 3.75% to 3.95% between 2005 and 2014. In South Korea, the prevalence rate increased from 14.3% to 15.9% between 2006 and 2013. While the prevalence was higher among women, the disease progression tended to worsen more easily in men. 3 Risk factors associated with DR include blood glucose concentration, 4 baseline blood glucose, 6-year glycemic exposure and hypertension, 5 dyslipidemia, 6 duration of diabetes, 7 nephropathy, 8 higher body mass index, 9 and smoking, 3 among others. DR can be determined through biochemical indicators, such as HBA1C (Hemoglobin A1c), which is a major risk factor for proliferative DR (PDR). 10 In the study of protein spots in DR patients, the levels of RBP1 (retinol-binding protein 1), NUD10 (Diphosphoinositol polyphosphohydrolase 3 alpha), and NGB (neuroglobin) were decreased, while HBG2 (hemoglobin) and BY55 (CD160 antigen) were upregulated in the diseased group. 11 Metabolomic studies found elevated expression of indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase as well as kynurenine, kynurenic acid, and 3-hydroxykynurenine in NPDR (non-proliferative DR) and PDR patients. 12 However, current research lacks appropriate, independent, and unified biological markers that can indicate the presence and progression of DR in advance.

The pathogenesis of DR involves capillary occlusion, leading to retinal ischemia and demonstrable leakage on fluorescein angiography. The development of DR is influenced by multiple biochemical signaling pathways, including protein kinase C and protein glycosylation. 13 Therefore, the diagnosis of DR primarily relies on retinal examination and imaging, including fundus photography, optical coherence tomography (OCT), and fluorescein angiography where indicated. 12 . Since the effective intervention window for DR occurs before the onset of severe vision loss, systematic screening and regular monitoring are crucial. In the UK, for instance, all diabetic patients aged 12 and above are included in the annual retinopathy screening program. 14 While this system has significantly reduced the incidence of diabetes-related vision impairment, the comprehensive ophthalmic examination heavily relies on the operator’s skills and specialized equipment. Moreover, patients’ access to medical institutions with screening capabilities is often hindered by factors such as willingness to seek medical care, geographical distance, time costs, and financial burdens. Consequently, the participation rate in annual regular screenings struggles to reach the desired level.

The molecular mechanisms driving microvascular dysfunction have not yet been precisely elucidated, and there is a lack of effective early-warning tools and precise molecular subtyping methods. To address this, this study aims to systematically analyze the molecular characteristics of diabetic retinopathy (DR) and identify key biomarkers by integrating multi-omics data with in vitro experiments. First, we obtained DR-related transcriptomic data from public databases and performed differential expression analysis, functional enrichment, and protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis to identify hub genes. Second, we characterized the immune microenvironment of DR and conducted molecular subtyping of patients through immune infiltration analysis and unsupervised clustering. Furthermore, we utilized LASSO regression to screen diagnostic biomarker combinations from the hub genes and validated their efficacy in independent datasets. This study aims to provide new clues and potential targets for the early diagnosis, classification, and mechanism research of DR.

2. Methods

2.1. Dataset acquisition and preprocessing

The GSE221521 dataset (peripheral blood RNA-seq) was used as the primary cohort, including 50 healthy controls, 74 DM patients, and 69 DR patients. Validation used GSE304690 (retinal pigment epithelial cells treated with high glucose, 5 samples per group). Expression matrices were extracted, log2-transformed, and low-expression genes filtered. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using limma with thresholds: DM vs control |logFC|≥0.2, adj.P≤0.2; DR vs control |logFC|≥0.3, adj.P≤0.1; DR vs DM |logFC|≥0.25, adj.P≤0.1. Venn diagrams and box plots visualized overlaps and expression patterns.

2.2. Enrichment pathway and PPI network construction

GO enrichment (clusterProfiler) was performed on DEGs (DR vs control; DR vs DM) with q<0.2, p<0.05. To ensure sufficient enrichment results, a lenient threshold for selecting input genes was applied: |logFC| ≥ 0.3 and adj.P ≤ 0.1. For each comparison, the top 5 terms from each GO category (biological process, molecular function, cellular component) were extracted, merged, and visualized as a combined bar plot with distinct colors differentiating the two datasets. PPI networks were built from STRING v11.5 (confidence ≥0.900). Hub genes were identified by centrality measures (degree, betweenness, closeness) and visualized in circular layouts.

2.3. Immune signature module and immune typing

To evaluate immune infiltration, ssGSEA was performed on log2(FPKM+1) expression matrices using the GSEABase and GSVA packages. The reference gene set was c7.immunesigdb.v2024.Hs.symbols.gmt. Based on differential expression results, immune-related genes consistently altered in DR versus both Control and DM were selected. Hierarchical clustering was applied, and ssGSEA enrichment scores were calculated for each sample across immune features, visualized as a heatmap.

For immune molecular subtyping, unsupervised K-means clustering (k=3, nstart=25) was applied to the top 200 most variable genes (based on Z-score normalized expression matrix) to classify samples into three immune subtypes. Marker genes for each subtype were identified by differential expression (logFC ranking). Heatmaps of the top 50 marker genes and PCA plots were generated. The top 5 marker genes per subtype were shown in bar charts. GO enrichment analysis (q<0.05, p<0.2) was performed on marker genes of each subtype; the top 10 enriched pathways per subtype were combined into a dot plot (dot size = gene count, color =enrichment significance).

2.4. Diagnostic biomarker screening and model construction

Using an AUC > 0.75 threshold, we performed preliminary screening of these differentially expressed genes, limiting the number of genes for subsequent analysis to no more than 100. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated for each candidate gene, which were then ranked according to their diagnostic performance. Based on this, we generated a distribution plot of AUC values, a scatter plot showing the correlation between AUC and log2FC, and separately plotted ROC curves for the top 10 genes ranked by AUC to visually demonstrate their classification performance. LASSO regression was further employed to screen for key multi-gene biomarker combinations. The expression matrix was transformed into a format suitable for LASSO modeling, with the optimal regularization parameter λ selected through ten-fold cross-validation. Key genes with non-zero coefficients were extracted. These genes were used to construct a multi-gene predictive model, and the ROC curve of this model was plotted. Subsequently, Reactome pathway enrichment analysis was performed on the top 100 genes ranked by AUC, and a gene-pathway interaction network was constructed to functionally analyze the biological background of high-frequency biomarker genes.

2.5. Independent dataset validation and pathway analysis

To validate the reliability of key molecules identified from clinical data (GSE221521), we further conducted independent analysis using the transcriptomic dataset of high glucose-treated retinal pigment epithelial cells (GSE304690). Preprocessing was performed according to the standardized pipeline consistent with the GSE221521 dataset. After preprocessing, samples were divided into high-glucose treatment and normal-glucose control groups based on sample descriptions. Differential expression analysis was conducted using the limma package. Significant differentially expressed genes were identified by fitting linear models with empirical Bayes moderation, using thresholds of |logFC| ≥ 0.5 and adjusted p-value (adj.P.Val) < 0.05, with separate identification of up- and down-regulated genes in the high-glucose group. To focus on core changes, we selected the top 30 most significantly differentially expressed genes ranked by adj.P.Val as the core gene set. For each gene in this set, Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to validate its expression differences between the two groups, with expression level boxplots generated for visualization. Furthermore, we conducted Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) to reveal global functional alterations. First, the expression matrix was processed by removing duplicates and merging gene symbols to construct a gene list ranked by logFC as input for GSEA. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was initially performed with parameters set as p-value <0.5, no upper limit for adj.P.Val, minimum 3 and maximum 800 genes per pathway. GO biological process (BP) enrichment analysis was performed using identical parameters. Finally, the top three most significant pathways from the KEGG enrichment results were selected, and their GSEA enrichment curves were plotted separately to visually demonstrate the coordinated changes of genes under high-glucose conditions in these pathways.

3. Results

3.1. Global differential expression profile of diabetic retinopathy

Using the GSE221521 dataset, we mapped the transcriptomic landscape of DR across different stages. In DM vs control (Figure 1(a)), IGHV4-31 was downregulated while AC020656.1, GAPT and CARD8-AS1 were upregulated. The alterations in the DM genome suggest that immunoglobulins have been suppressed during diabetes, while the inflammation-related pathway (CARD) becomes activated. In early DR (DR vs control), IK and SYF2 (cell cycle/splicing) were downregulated, whereas IFITM3P1 and H6PD (immune/metabolic stress) were upregulated (Figure 1(b)). pathway enrichment showed leukocyte-mediated immunity and inflammatory activation (Figure 1(d)). In advanced DR (DR vs DM), PLEKHG2, PIEZO1, and PLBD2 were upregulated, and ARHGDIB downregulated (Figure 1(c)); pathways included T cell activation and lysosomal function (Figure 1(e)). Comparing early and advanced DR, early stage featured interleukin-1 production, while advanced stage exhibited sustained T cell activation along with cellular stress and metabolic dysregulation (Figure 1(f)). Nine genes (ribosomal pseudogenes, non-coding RNAs, and TOE1) were consistently altered across control, DM, and DR, as shown by Venn diagram and box plot (Figure 1(g) and (h)), indicating that dysregulation at transcriptional and translational levels underlies DM-to-DR progression. Overall, DR evolves from immune suppression and initial inflammation (DM) to immune-inflammatory burst with cell cycle arrest (early DR), and finally to sustained immune activation with multi-dimensional cellular dysfunction (advanced DR). 3.2. Protein-protein interaction network and hub genes of differentially expressed genes

In the early stage of DR (DR vs control), the PPI network was highly interconnected, comprising 200 nodes and 1,533 edges (Figure 2(a)). The top 25 hub genes were almost exclusively ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPS11, RPL11, RPS6) and associated factors (e.g., UBA52) (Figure 2(b)), indicating that global reprogramming of protein synthesis is a central cellular stress response during early DR. In the advanced stage (DR vs DM), the node count remained similar (185), but edges drastically decreased to 457, reflecting a shift toward more specific and modular connectivity (Figure 2(c)). Hub genes showed greater diversity: ribosomal proteins (e.g., RPS6, RPL5, RPL36, IMP3) still predominated, but key functional genes such as EP300 (transcriptional regulation), TPT1 (apoptosis/protein synthesis), PLEC (cytoskeleton integrity), HSPA5 (ER stress), and SNW1 (splicing) emerged (Figure 2(d)). Ribosomal proteins consistently occupied pivotal positions throughout disease progression. Overall, the DR molecular network evolves from a dense “stress response” network centered on protein synthesis in early stages to a “dysfunctional-type” network integrating transcriptional regulation, ER stress, and cytoskeletal remodeling in advanced stages.

3.3. Systemic suppression of immune function modules in DR

To systematically analyze the co-regulatory patterns of immune-related genes in DR, we performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) on differentially expressed genes, identifying six immune signature modules (M1-M6) significantly associated with disease status (Figure 3(a)). These modules exhibited overall downregulation in both DM and DR patients compared to controls, indicating coordinated suppression of immune-related gene clusters during DR progression. Further analysis revealed high positive correlations among these six modules (correlation coefficients ranging from 0.79 to 1.00) (Figure 3(c)). Among them, modules M1, M2, M3, and M4 exhibited the highest mutual correlations (r > 0.95), forming a highly synergistic core immunosuppressive network. M5 and M6 also showed significant positive correlations with the core network (r > 0.79), serving as functional extensions or complementary components that collectively participate in constituting a broad immunosuppressive program. Differential module score analysis (Figure 3(b)) was employed to quantify the activity changes of each module across different groups. Statistical results showed that compared with the control group, DR exhibited significant decreases in scores across all six immune modules (p < 0.001). The DM group also demonstrated a declining pattern, though with less significance than the DR group. This reflects a progressive and global suppression of immune module activity during the progression from DM to DR. Based on keyword extraction, these modules encompass adaptive immunity (T-cell receptor signaling, B-cell activation), innate immunity (pattern recognition receptor signaling, interferon-gamma response), immune migration, and cytokine-mediated pathways (Figure 3(d)). Thus, DR exhibits a progressive, global suppression of immune module activity, reflecting a complex immunopathology where systemic immune functions are suppressed despite local inflammatory activation.

3.4. Immunophenotype-based classification of DR patients

To investigate the inhibitory patterns within the DR patient population, we performed unsupervised clustering analysis on DR patient samples based on the expression profiles of immune-related genes from the whole transcriptome. Three immunologically distinct molecular subtypes (Subtype_1, Subtype_2, Subtype_3) with significant differences were successfully identified. PCA results demonstrated a clear separation trend among the three subtypes in the dimensionality-reduced space (Figure 4(a)), indicating that immune feature-based classification can effectively distinguish DR patient populations with different molecular backgrounds, confirming the intrinsic immune heterogeneity in DR. Fourteen marker genes that most effectively distinguish the three subtypes were further identified (Figure 4(b)), with their expression patterns showing significant differences among subtypes. Subtype_1 was characterized by high expression of GP1BB and non-coding RNAs (MIR1244-1, RN7SL1); GO-BP enrichment revealed pathways related to platelet activation and protein targeting to membrane, suggesting a vascular-inflammatory phenotype (Figure 4(c) and (d)). Subtype_2 showed elevated expression of mitochondrial genes (e.g., MT-ATP6, MT-TM) and was enriched in energy metabolism pathways (oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial ATP synthesis), indicating a metabolic dysfunction type. Subtype_3 specifically expressed DNAJC25-GNG10 and TXNP4, with enrichment in type II hypersensitivity and antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, pointing to an oxidative stress-tissue remodeling type. Thus, DR patients can be classified into three immune subtypes: Vascular-Inflammatory Type (Subtype_1), Metabolic Dysfunction Type (Subtype_2), and Oxidative Stress-Tissue Remodeling Type (Subtype_3).

3.5. Screening of diagnostic biomarkers and construction of combination models

To identify molecules distinguishing DR from DM, we performed LASSO regression on DEGs between DR and DM. Ten-fold cross-validation selected an optimal λ, identifying 8 key genes with non-zero coefficients (Figure 5(a)). To evaluate the diagnostic potential of individual genes, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated for the top 100 most significantly differentially expressed genes in the DR vs DM comparison (Figure 5(b)). Further analysis of the relationship between gene expression changes (Log2 Fold Change) and diagnostic performance (AUC) revealed (Figure 5(d)) a positive correlation trend, indicating that genes with greater expression differences exhibited stronger potential for distinguishing DR from DM. This statistically validates the rationale for screening biomarkers based on differentially expressed genes. Among these, the top 10 single-gene AUC-ranked genes (including PLBD2, H6PD, PLXND1, etc.) (Figure 5(h)) were integrated with the expression levels of 8 key genes selected by LASSO to construct a multi-gene combination diagnostic model. This model demonstrated excellent discriminative ability: the area under the ROC curve (AUC) reached 0.876 (Figure 5(e)), along with high specificity (0.973) and acceptable sensitivity (0.652). Model scores were significantly separated between DR and DM groups (Figure 5(f)). The calibration curve showed excellent agreement between predicted and actual probabilities (mean absolute error = 0.023) (Figure 5(c)). Finally, an interaction network of the eight key marker genes and their associated pathways revealed collective involvement in immune response, cell adhesion, and angiogenesis (Figure 5(g)).

3.6. Validation and pathway mechanism of the high glucose in vitro model

The reliability of in vivo findings was validated through an independent dataset and in vitro cell experiments. A high-glucose-treated human retinal microvascular endothelial cell model was employed to simulate the stress state in a diabetic environment, followed by transcriptomic analysis. Consistent with the in vivo study, the in vitro high-glucose model also induced significant transcriptomic alterations, and a set of core genes (e.g., PPP1R3D, SCIN, FCGR2B) differentially expressed between the HG group and control group were identified (Figure 6(d)). To systematically understand the biological processes under high-glucose stress, we performed Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA). GSEA of KEGG pathways showed significant enrichment of immune-related pathways, including PPAR signaling, RIG-I-like receptor signaling, autoimmune thyroid disease, and allograft rejection (Figure 6(a)). The top three enriched pathways were allograft rejection, autoimmune thyroid disease, and nucleotide metabolism (Figure 6(b)). GO biological process enrichment revealed that high glucose significantly suppressed T cell tolerance induction, negative regulation of respiratory burst, and negative regulation of macrophage activation (Figure 6(c)), indicating a release of inhibitory controls on immune cell activation. Additionally, enrichment of gluceraldehyde-3-phosphate metabolic process and peroxisome organization reflected metabolic reprogramming and oxidative stress. Thus, the in vitro model recapitulated key features of DR: activation of immune/autoimmune pathways, release of immunosuppressive regulation, and metabolic-oxidative stress, supporting the “hyperglycemia-immune dysregulation-tissue damage” mechanism.

4. Discussion

This study utilized peripheral transcriptome sequencing data from the GSE221521 dataset and adopted a multi-omics integrated analysis strategy to systematically delineate the complete dynamic molecular landscape of DR from DM onset to progression. Key diagnostic biomarkers were identified and validated in cellular models. The research revealed the dynamic molecular evolution patterns of DR from DM to advanced stages. The DM phase was characterized by suppression of immunoglobulin genes such as IGHV4-31 and initial activation of the CADR inflammatory pathway, indicating early disruption of immune homeostasis.15–17 Early DR manifested as downregulation of cell cycle/splicing genes (IK, SYF2) and upregulation of interferon response (IFITM3P1) and metabolic stress (H6PD) genes, with pathway enrichment showing comprehensive leukocyte-mediated immunity activation. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported that metabolic dysregulation and inflammatory cell adhesion play crucial roles in DR-related microvascular dysfunction, including altered metabolic pathways in vascular formation, 18 leukocyte stasis mediated by adhesion molecules, 19 FTO-driven neovascularization, 20 and clinical correlations between leukocyte indices and DR severity on imaging. 21 In advanced DR, genes associated with metabolic/mechanical stress (PLEKHG2, PIEZO1) became central, while immune regulation (e.g., T-cell activation) remained persistently active. Oxidative stress was identified as both driver and consequence of hyperglycemia-induced metabolic dysregulation. 22 Importantly, the nine consistently differentially expressed core genes across the three groups (such as TOE1 and RPL series pseudogenes) that we identified are involved in RNA translation and protein processing. 23 This highlights that fundamental, global regulation at the transcriptional and translational levels serves as the intrinsic driving force behind the progression from DM to DR, while stage-specific activation of immune or metabolic pathways represents specific manifestations of this underlying dysregulation under different pathophysiological stresses.

The immune microenvironment of DR exhibits a complex and paradoxical state characterized by systemic suppression and localized heterogeneous activation. On one hand, WGCNA analysis identified six functionally coordinated immune modules (M1-M6) that were consistently downregulated in DR, spanning multiple levels including adaptive immunity and innate immunity. These findings suggest the potential existence of systemic immune suppression or exhaustion during disease progression. In chronic metabolic diseases, persistent antigen stimulation and inflammation lead to functional exhaustion of immune cells, representing an adaptive response of the immune system. Sustained hyperglycemia may suppress immune cell functions - for instance, high fructose can inhibit microglial phagocytosis. 24 Additionally, diabetic patients demonstrate immunodeficient phenomena. 25 This apparent paradox of systemic immune suppression coexisting with inflammatory activation can be reconciled as a hallmark of chronic metabolic dysregulation. Sustained hyperglycemia and metabolic stress may lead to the functional exhaustion or suppression of systemic adaptive immunity over time, while simultaneously triggering innate inflammatory pathways and oxidative stress responses that contribute to vascular damage. On the other hand, clustering analysis based on immune genes distinctly classified DR patients into three subtypes: “Vascular-Inflammatory” (Subtype-1, enriched in platelet activation), “Metabolic Dysfunction” (Subtype-2, enriched in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation), and “Oxidative Stress-Tissue Remodeling” (Subtype-3). Therefore, we speculate that the immune microenvironment in DR is not simply activated, but rather constitutes a complex imbalance system characterized by intense local retinal inflammation alongside diminished peripheral or systemic immune response capacity. This “paradoxical” state may reveal the spatiotemporal heterogeneity inherent in DR immunopathology. The high expression of mitochondrial genes in Subtype-2 (metabolic dysfunction type) strongly supports the central role of metabolic-immune crosstalk in this subtype.

Through screening based on a multivariate diagnostic model, we successfully identified five potential target genes that play a central role in distinguishing diabetic retinopathy (DR) from diabetes mellitus (DM) alone: IK, PLBD2, FLNA, H6PD, and ZMAT2. These genes not only form a statistically high-precision diagnostic combination but also functionally cover multiple key aspects of the DR pathological process. As a critical immune regulation factor, IK serves as the specific ligand for lymphotoxin beta receptor (LTβR) and plays a central role in regulating the migration and localization of immune cells. 26 Its altered expression in DR suggests an imbalance in the homeostasis of systemic immune cell trafficking and local recruitment. As a lysosomal phospholipase B, PLBD2 participates in the metabolism and remodeling of membrane phospholipids, and its upregulation may be closely associated with the activation and tissue infiltration processes of immune cells under pathological conditions. 27 The FLNA gene encodes a widely expressed actin-binding protein that plays crucial roles in maintaining cytoskeletal dynamics and integrating membrane receptor signaling, particularly in platelet activation and megakaryocyte development. 28 Abnormalities in this gene may directly affect the integrity of retinal microvasculature and thromboinflammatory responses. H6PD is a key enzyme in endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress response. By regulating ER redox homeostasis, it can significantly suppress the excessive accumulation of mitochondrial and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby protecting cells from oxidative stress-induced damage and apoptosis. 29 ZMAT2, as a highly conserved yet insufficiently studied gene, has been reported to play important roles in keratinocyte differentiation and precursor mRNA splicing regulation, 30 suggesting its potential involvement in broader post-transcriptional gene expression regulatory networks in DR. These five targets—spanning diverse dimensions including immune regulation (IK), lipid metabolism and inflammation (PLBD2), cytoskeleton and vascular function (FLNA), metabolism and oxidative stress (H6PD), and RNA processing (ZMAT2)—collectively delineate a multi-layered molecular interaction network driving the onset and progression of DR. Their discovery not only provides a solid biological foundation for the diagnostic model but also points to potential new directions for multi-target interventions addressing the complex pathological mechanisms of DR. From a clinical perspective, while DR is currently diagnosed through established retinal imaging modalities and structured screening programs, the proposed circulating biomarker model is not intended to replace these gold standards. Instead, its potential role is clearly positioned within a complementary clinical context, such as risk stratification, early detection, or as an initial screening tool in resource-limited settings where specialized retinal imaging equipment and expertise are not readily available.

To validate the reliability of the aforementioned targets and pathways at the cellular level, we established an in vitro model of human retinal microvascular endothelial cells treated with high glucose. Transcriptomic analysis of this model revealed that its gene expression profile under high-glucose stress exhibited high concordance with the data obtained from peripheral blood of DR patients, particularly showing consistency in immune activation and metabolic reprogramming-related pathways. This finding provides supporting context for the core pathological mechanism hypothesis proposed in this study: sustained hyperglycemia actively creates a pro-inflammatory local microenvironment by inducing significant metabolic reprogramming and cellular stress (such as endoplasmic reticulum stress) in retinal microvascular endothelial cells. This microenvironment facilitates the adhesion, infiltration, and activation of immune cells, ultimately leading to a series of characteristic pathological changes in DR, including retinal vascular barrier damage, neovascularization, and neural tissue injury.

This study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inference, and the peripheral blood transcriptome may not directly represent retinal tissue changes, thus cautious interpretation is required. Additionally, the validation relied on a small in-vitro model; independent, multicenter, large-sample prospective cohorts are needed to confirm the diagnostic model and subtypes. Future research will include longitudinal tracking, single-cell sequencing of blood and retinal tissues, and multi-omics integration.
