Women’s decision-making autonomy and early antenatal care initiation in Ghana: a propensity score-matched analysis
Authors: Delight Mawufemor Agbi, Gideon Ikemdinachi Nwankwo, Courage Venunye Mawuko, Daniel Thoya Iha, Josias Ounsinli Djossou, Edson Mwebesa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionWomen’s decision-making autonomy is widely recognized as an important determinant of maternal health behaviors such as the timing of antenatal care (ANC) initiation in low- and middle-income countries. However, empirical evidence from Ghana on this relationship, particularly using causal inference approaches, remains limited. This study investigates the effect of women’s decision-making autonomy on the early initiation of ANC visits in Ghana.MethodsData were sourced from the 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS), including 4,314 women aged 15–49 years with a birth in the preceding three years before the survey. Autonomy was measured as a composite index from household decision-making participation. Propensity Score Matching (PSM) was used to match women with High and None/Low autonomy, based on covariates like age, education, wealth, and residence. The average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) was estimated to assess the impact of high autonomy on early ANC initiation (within the first trimester), compared to the counterfactual scenario in which the same women lacked such autonomy.ResultsAmong the women included in the study, 25.3% reported high decision-making autonomy, and 66.4% initiated ANC early. Women with high autonomy were more likely to be older (30–49 years), educated (secondary or higher), married, employed, Christian, insured, urban residents, and from wealthier households. After matching on socio-demographic covariates, no statistically significant effect of high autonomy on early ANC initiation was observed [-0.029; 95% CI: -0.081 to 0.022].ConclusionWomen’s decision-making autonomy, when measured through household decision-making participation and within the Ghanaian context, does not independently drive early ANC initiation, suggesting that broader structural and contextual barriers may constrain the translation of autonomy into early care-seeking.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04374-6.

Introduction

The initiation of antenatal care (ANC) within the first trimester is globally recognized as a cornerstone of maternal and neonatal health, substantially reducing morbidity and mortality risks for both mother and child [1]. Early ANC enables critical interventions such as nutritional counselling, screening for infections, and health education that improve pregnancy outcomes [2]. Despite global recommendations of at least eight ANC contacts [1], delayed initiation remains prevalent in many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [2, 3]. Access to ANC is influenced by multiple factors, including distance to facilities, financial constraints, socio-cultural beliefs, and health system readiness [4–6]. Among these factors, women’s decision-making autonomy, defined as the capacity to make independent or joint decisions regarding health, finances, and mobility, has been identified as an important determinant of maternal health-seeking behavior in LMICs, where socio-cultural and systemic barriers often impede access to care [7, 8]. Research reveals that women with greater autonomy are more likely to prioritize their health, allocate resources efficiently, and overcome structural barriers to care [9, 10].

Studies across LMICs consistently associate women’s empowerment with improved maternal health outcomes, including higher ANC attendance, early initiation, and facility-based delivery [9, 11]. However, the magnitude and direction of these effects vary depending on the context and measurement approaches. Studies controlling for education, wealth, and residence often find attenuated associations, suggesting that autonomy’s influence is partially mediated by socioeconomic status [3, 12]. Moreover, the mechanisms linking autonomy to early ANC initiation remain unclear, as many studies use general autonomy indices that do not capture health-specific decision-making or rely on cross-sectional data that limit causal inference [7, 11].

Ghana provides a relevant case study due to persistent disparities in ANC utilization despite notable declines in maternal mortality [13, 14]. The Ghana Demographic and Health Survey (GDHS) reports indicate that the proportion of sampled respondents who received an ANC visit in the first trimester increased from 55% in 2008 to 64% in 2014. Recent studies in Ghana reveal that, on average, 65–66% of pregnant women initiated their first ANC visit within the first trimester, though much lower rates are observed in some regions and districts [3, 6]. Regional inequalities, transportation barriers, and out-of-pocket costs contribute to delayed ANC initiation [15]. These disparities are particularly salient given Ghana’s maternal health targets and ongoing policy commitments to improve equitable access to skilled maternal care, underscoring the urgency of identifying determinants of early ANC initiation.

While women’s decision-making autonomy has been linked to improved maternal service utilization in Ghana, findings remain inconsistent. Some studies report positive associations, whereas others document negligible or even inverse relationships once confounding factors are considered [5, 6]. For example, Ameyaw et al. [16]found that autonomy in health-related decisions increased facility-based delivery, while other autonomy dimensions, such as control over household purchases, were associated with lower utilization of health services. These mixed findings suggest that the effects of individual empowerment may be contingent upon, or mediated by, broader socioeconomic and structural conditions. In particular, evidence from Ghana demonstrates that household wealth remains a strong and persistent predictor of maternal health service utilization, including ANC attendance, even in the context of policies aimed at improving access and equity [17]. This highlights the possibility that economic and structural constraints may override or condition the influence of women’s decision-making autonomy on health-seeking behavior. Consequently, this study hypothesizes that higher decision-making autonomy independently increases the likelihood of early ANC initiation, though its effect size is constrained by household socioeconomic status.

To test this, this study addresses these gaps by employing propensity score matching (PSM) on nationally representative data from the 2022 GDHS to estimate the causal effect of women’s decision-making autonomy on early ANC initiation. The significance of this study lies in its potential to clarify whether autonomy independently influences early ANC initiation within the current Ghanaian policy and socioeconomic context, while accounting for observed confounding factors. By reducing the imbalance between women with high and no/low autonomy, PSM provides a more rigorous counterfactual comparison than conventional regression approaches [18]. Focusing on early ANC initiation, a key indicator of maternal health service utilization, aligns with global commitments under Sustainable Development Goal 3 (SDG 3), particularly targets aimed at reducing maternal mortality and ensuring universal access to quality maternal health services, as well as Ghana’s national maternal health priorities [1, 19, 20]. The findings are therefore positioned to inform policy by highlighting whether empowerment-focused interventions alone are sufficient, or whether they must be complemented by strategies addressing structural and wealth-related barriers to care.

Materials and methods

Data source and study population

This study utilized secondary data from the 2022 GDHS. The GDHS is a nationally representative survey conducted by the Ghana Statistical Service and the Ghana Health Service, providing detailed information on fertility, family planning, maternal and child health, and women’s empowerment. The 2022 GDHS employed a two-stage stratified cluster sampling design to select households across all regions of Ghana [21]. The study sample consisted of 4,314 women aged 15–49 years who had a live birth in the three years preceding the survey and had complete data on decision-making autonomy and the timing of their first ANC visit. Sampling weights, primary sampling units (clusters), and strata were incorporated in all descriptive and analytic estimations to account for the complex survey design and ensure nationally representative inference. Analyses were restricted to women with complete data on the exposure, outcome, and covariates included in the propensity score model.

Outcome variable (Y)

The primary outcome was the early ANC Visit, a binary variable derived from the woman’s self-reported gestational age at the time of her first antenatal appointment for the most recent birth. Following standard public health definitions, the outcome was categorized as:

The binary operationalization was adopted to align with World Health Organization (WHO) recommendations and national maternal health indicators, which emphasize initiation of ANC within the first trimester as a clinically meaningful threshold [22]. This categorization facilitates policy interpretation and comparability with prior DHS-based studies, where early versus late ANC initiation is the standard outcome definition. Although gestational age at first ANC could be analyzed as a continuous or ordinal measure, such approaches are more susceptible to recall error and digit preference in self-reported data. Dichotomization, therefore, reduces measurement noise and improves robustness of inference in the presence of recall bias.

For each domain, responses indicating that the woman decided alone or jointly with her partner were coded as evidence of participation in decision-making, while decisions made solely by the partner or others were coded as no participation. The responses across the four domains were aggregated to construct an autonomy index reflecting the breadth of a woman’s involvement in household decision-making. Women were classified as having high decision-making autonomy if they participated in decision-making across all four domains. Women who participated in fewer than four domains were classified as having none or low autonomy. This binary categorization (High Autonomy = 1; None/Low Autonomy = 0) was adopted to identify a policy-relevant threshold distinguishing women with consistently high decision-making power from those with partial or limited autonomy.

Dichotomization was chosen to enhance interpretability and comparability with prior DHS-based studies examining maternal health behaviors, where decision-making autonomy is frequently operationalized as a binary exposure [23]. This approach facilitates communication of findings to policy and programmatic audiences by focusing on a clear contrast between high and low levels of household decision-making power. We acknowledge that this operationalization may reduce variability and obscure potential dose-response relationships; this limitation is noted in the manuscript. Joint decision-making was treated as indicative of autonomy because, within the Ghanaian context, shared decision-making represents a meaningful expression of women’s agency and a departure from unilateral male dominance in household decisions. We recognize that the interpretation of joint decision-making varies across settings and discuss this contextual dependence as a limitation. Finally, decision-making autonomy was measured at the time of the survey and may not perfectly reflect autonomy during pregnancy. This temporal mismatch may introduce non-differential misclassification, which could bias estimated associations toward the null.

Propensity Score-Matched Analysis (PSMA)

The vector X includes the full set of confounding covariates listed in the study variables section. We used logistic regression as the estimation algorithm for the propensity scores.

The analysis focused on estimating the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT), the effect of having high autonomy among women who actually possess it. To ensure appropriate comparisons, we applied nearest neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.03 to restrict matches to women with closely similar propensity scores. The caliper ensures that matches are made only within an acceptable range of propensity score distances, thereby improving covariate balance and reducing the likelihood of poor matches [27, 28]. Matching was based on age, education level, number of children ever born, occupation, media exposure, religious affiliation, place of residence, wealth index, sex of household head, current marital status, and health insurance coverage.

where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Y}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document}(1) is the outcome (Early ANC Initiation) for woman i under the condition of high autonomy, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{Y}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document}(0) is the counterfactual outcome (Early ANC Initiation) for the same woman i under the condition of none/low autonomy. The ATT, therefore, represents the expected difference in the proportion of Early ANC Initiation between women with high autonomy and the same women had they not possessed high autonomy [26].

To estimate ATT, we first selected appropriate observed covariates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{i}}$$\end{document}, then estimated the propensity score, checked for overlap and balance in the propensity scores, and finally computed the ATT [26, 29, 30]. The counterfactual scenario was established by matching women with high autonomy to women with none/low autonomy based on the estimated propensity scores. We assessed the overlap and balance using propensity score graphs (Fig. 1). One woman who fell outside the region of common support was excluded from the analysis to avoid biased parameter estimates [18]. The robustness of the ATT estimate was ensured by using 1,000 bootstrap replications to derive the standard errors and 95% confidence intervals. To assess the robustness of the estimated ATT to potential unobserved confounding, sensitivity analysis was conducted using Rosenbaum bounds, implemented through Hodges–Lehmann (HL) bounds for matched observational data. This approach helped to evaluate how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to change the study’s conclusions.

Results

Prevalence of women’s decision-making autonomy and background characteristics of the sample

Among all women surveyed, 25.3% [95% CI: 23.4–27.3] exhibited high decision-making autonomy. Also, 66.4% [95% CI: 64.5–68.3] of women initiated antenatal care (ANC) early, while 33.6% [95% CI: 31.7–35.5] began late. Women with high autonomy had a slightly higher rate of early ANC initiation [68.8% versus 65.6% among those with low or no autonomy], representing a 3.2% point difference, though this was not statistically significant (p = 0.157).

Maternal characteristics

Women with high autonomy tended to be older, particularly in the 30–34 years [27.2%, 95% CI: 23.8–30.9] and 35–49 years [32.5%, 95% CI: 28.5–36.6] age groups. Most had attained at least a secondary education [53.0%, 95% CI: 48.8–57.1], with 12.6% [95% CI: 10.0–15.8] holding higher education degrees. The majority were married [76.1%, 95% CI: 72.2–79.6], had one to two children [45.7%, 95% CI: 41.7–49.7], and were currently employed [89.0%, 95% CI: 86.1–91.3].

Socioeconomic and health characteristics

A substantial proportion of women with high autonomy were Christians [73.3%, 95% CI: 68.2–77.7] and had health insurance coverage [96.4%, 95% CI: 94.4–97.7]. Interestingly, most were non-users of contraceptives [59.2%, 95% CI: 54.6–63.6]. Media exposure was common, with 85.0% [95% CI: 81.4–87.9] regularly accessing mass media. Nearly half resided in households with 1–4 members [45.6%, 95% CI: 41.3–49.9] and lived in urban areas [50.7%, 95% CI: 46.3–55.0]. Economically, a large share of these women belonged to the richer [18.4%, 95% CI: 15.4–21.9] or richest [24.1%, 95% CI: 20.3–28.4] wealth quintiles.

Regional distribution

Regionally, women with high autonomy were concentrated in the Ashanti [19.7%, 95% CI: 16.1–24.0], Greater Accra [14.3%, 95% CI: 11.4–17.8], and Upper East [6.3%, 95% CI: 4.9–8.0] regions, highlighting notable geographical differences across Ghana.

Effect of women’s decision-making autonomy on the early ANC visits

The propensity score distributions were examined to assess matching quality and the region of common support between women with high decision-making autonomy (exposed) and those with low or no autonomy (unexposed). Substantial overlap in propensity scores was observed across groups within the common support region (Fig. 2), indicating adequate comparability after matching. One observation was excluded due to a lack of common support. Following matching, ATT for early initiation of the first ANC visit was estimated. The descriptive ATT was − 0.029 (SE = 0.024), as shown in Table 2.

Discussion

This study examined the effect of women’s decision-making autonomy and early initiation of ANC in Ghana using a robust PSM applied to data from the 2022 GDHS. By addressing confounding factors inherent in observational studies, this research seeks to clarify the role of decision-making autonomy in maternal health-seeking behavior, a critical issue in LMICs where socio-cultural and systemic barriers often impede access to care.

The prevalence of high decision-making autonomy in this study was 25.3%, relatively low compared to other LMICs as reported in a systematic review by Gebeyehu et al. [9], which found substantial variation in women’s decision-making autonomy regarding maternal health services, with reported prevalence ranging from 22% to 82.4%. For instance, India exhibited a prevalence of 82.4% [31], Ethiopia ranged from 41.1% to 81.6% [25, 32], and Nigeria reported 40.7% [23], while prior Ghanaian studies showed variability (21.9%; 75%) [9, 33]. Women with high autonomy were more likely to be older (30–49 years), educated (secondary or higher), married, employed or working, Christian, insured, and urban residents, often from wealthier households. These characteristics align with findings from Ameyaw et al. [16] and Shibre et al. [24]. This pattern suggests that autonomy is embedded within broader socio-economic advantage, reinforcing the need to distinguish the independent role of autonomy from correlated structural resources.

After adjusting for confounders (age of respondents, parity, occupation, education level, marital status, religion, wealth index, type of place of residence, region, exposure to media), the PSM analysis indicated that women with higher autonomy were 2.9% points less likely to initiate ANC early compared to their none/low-autonomy counterparts (ATT = -0.029), importantly, this estimate was not statistically significant, and therefore no definitive directional effect of autonomy on early ANC initiation can be inferred. The robust covariate balance, evidenced by substantial propensity score overlap and reduced biases, ensures the reliability of this finding with respect to observed confounders. While contrary to some prior findings [25]. The result resonates with evidence from Senegal and Malawi showing that autonomy alone may not guarantee improved maternal health service utilization [34, 35]. This finding invites a more critical reflection on how autonomy is measured and operationalized. The GDHS autonomy indicators capture reported participation in household decision-making but may not fully reflect women’s ability to translate decisions into action in constrained environments.

Several mechanisms may explain why higher decision-making autonomy does not necessarily translate into earlier ANC initiation. First, financial autonomy may be constrained by out-of-pocket costs, transportation expenses, and opportunity costs, limiting the ability to act on health decisions despite nominal decision-making power. Notably, although 96% of high-autonomy women in this study were insured under the National Health Insurance Scheme (NHIS), the lack of a significant effect suggests that insurance coverage alone does not resolve the autonomy-access gap. This highlights that even women with the authority to make health decisions are restricted by residual costs not covered by the NHIS, such as laboratory fees, diagnostic scans, or transportation expenses [36, 37]. Second, mobility-related autonomy may be insufficient in contexts where distance to facilities, poor road infrastructure, or safety concerns restrict physical access to care [38]. Third, negotiation power within households may not fully offset partner influence or prevailing social norms that deprioritize early preventive care [39]. Finally, prior negative experiences with health services or perceptions of poor quality may lead autonomous women to delay ANC despite having the authority to seek care [40]. Together, these mechanisms support the concept of “bounded agency,” whereby women’s decision-making power is constrained by systemic and structural barriers such as distance to facilities, cost of care, transportation challenges, and service quality [41]. Parallel evidence from Ghana demonstrates that wealth differentials and geographical location remain strong determinants of maternal and child health outcomes, even after accounting for government interventions [17, 42]. This suggests that in the absence of accessible, affordable, and high-quality services, autonomy alone may not translate into timely health-seeking behavior. Thus, the null finding should be interpreted as evidence of limited independent influence of autonomy under prevailing structural constraints, rather than the absence of agency altogether.

The slightly negative but non-significant point estimate observed in this study should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Rather than indicating a true adverse effect, it may reflect selective or strategic care-seeking among autonomous women, such as delaying ANC when perceived pregnancy risk is low or when anticipated service quality is poor. Moreover, the multidimensional nature of autonomy implies that domains such as financial control, mobility, and healthcare decision-making may exert heterogeneous and context-dependent effects. As noted by Achana et al. [43], the relationship between autonomy and maternal health outcomes is highly sensitive to contextual norms, service accessibility, and perceived quality of care. The PSM methodology strengthens the study’s capacity to estimate adjusted associations consistent with a causal interpretation under standard assumptions by mimicking randomization through careful matching on observed confounders. The use of nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.03 ensured high-quality matches and minimized bias due to measured confounding. Nonetheless, the findings should not be interpreted as definitive causal effects, given the cross-sectional design and the possibility of residual unmeasured confounding.

From a policy perspective, these findings suggest that interventions focused solely on enhancing women’s decision-making autonomy may be insufficient to improve early ANC initiation in Ghana. Empowerment efforts must be integrated with specific national frameworks, such as the Free Maternal Health Care Policy (FMHCP) [44] and the Community-Based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) initiative [45]. While the FMHCP removes basic user fees, policy integration should focus on expanding the NHIS benefit package to cover indirect costs that currently restrict the agency of even the most autonomous women. Empowerment strategies are likely to be more effective when combined with efforts to reduce financial barriers, improve physical access to facilities, enhance service quality, and address transportation constraints. Integrated approaches such as transportation vouchers, respectful maternity care initiatives, community outreach, and partner-inclusive ANC promotion may better enable women to translate autonomy into timely care-seeking behavior. Recent evidence from Ghana and Kenya supports this integrated approach, demonstrating that gains in autonomy yield greater maternal health benefits in enabling service environments [46, 47]. The observed heterogeneity in autonomy’s influence, also reported by Budu et al. [48], underscores the need for context-sensitive policies that address both individual agency and structural constraints simultaneously.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis is based on cross-sectional data from the 2022 GDHS, which limits the ability to establish temporal ordering between women’s decision-making autonomy and early ANC initiation. Consequently, although PSM was employed to improve comparability between exposure groups, the results should not be interpreted as definitive causal effects. Second, the PSM approach balances only observed covariates, and residual confounding due to unmeasured factors such as partner support, household power dynamics, gender norms, perceived quality of care, and prior experiences with health services may still influence the estimated association. Third, women’s decision-making autonomy was operationalized using a composite index derived from DHS questions. While this measure is widely used and policy-relevant, it may have limited conceptual breadth, as some components, such as decision-making over husband’s earnings, may not directly reflect health-specific autonomy. Additionally, dichotomizing autonomy may mask heterogeneity across dimensions such as financial control, mobility, and healthcare decision-making, and may obscure potential dose-response relationships. Fourth, the inclusion of variables such as media exposure as confounders reflects their documented influence on health knowledge, norms, and service utilization; however, residual measurement error in these covariates may persist. Fifth, ANC initiation timing was self-reported and may be subject to recall bias, particularly among women with longer recall periods. Finally, the analysis did not explicitly account for supply-side constraints such as facility density, transportation availability, or service quality, which may independently shape timely ANC initiation and constrain women’s ability to act on their decisions. Despite these limitations, the use of a nationally representative dataset and rigorous covariate balancing enhances confidence in the internal validity of the matched estimates.

Implications for policy and practice

The findings of this study have important implications for maternal health policy and practice in Ghana and other LMICs. Although women’s decision-making autonomy is widely promoted as a pathway to improved maternal healthcare utilization, the results suggest that autonomy alone may be insufficient to ensure timely initiation of ANC in settings where structural and health-system barriers persist. Interventions that focus exclusively on enhancing women’s decision-making power may therefore have limited impact if financial constraints, geographic access, transportation challenges, and service quality issues remain unaddressed. These findings are also relevant to global health initiatives, particularly SDG 3, which aims to reduce maternal mortality and ensure universal access to reproductive health services [20]. Early initiation of ANC is a key pathway through which SDG 3 targets can be realized. The results of this study suggest that progress toward these targets in Ghana and similar LMICs will require not only empowerment-focused strategies but also sustained investments in health system strengthening, poverty reduction, and geographic equity in service delivery. Policies and programs that combine empowerment with improved accessibility, affordability, and quality of maternal health services may contribute more effectively to progress toward SDG 3 targets.

Recommendations for future research

Future research should consider a mixed-methods approach to better understand the reasons behind the observed null association. Qualitative studies could investigate the lived experiences of women with high decision-making autonomy to understand context-specific and social barriers that may hinder early ANC initiation. In addition, longitudinal study designs are recommended to examine how women’s autonomy evolves and how these dynamics influence ANC-seeking behavior across pregnancy and successive births. Quantitative research could further investigate the role of unobserved confounders, such as spousal involvement and social support networks. Finally, future causal studies should assess the impact of supply-side factors, including health facility density and transportation availability, to empirically test whether systemic constraints are the primary determinants of early ANC initiation in Ghana.

Conclusion

This study provides an evidence-based reassessment of the assumption that women’s decision-making autonomy necessarily leads to early initiation of antenatal care in Ghana. Using a robust propensity score matching approach applied to nationally representative data from the 2022 GDHS, we estimated the effect of women’s autonomy decision-making and early ANC initiation while accounting for key socio-demographic confounders. The findings indicate that higher decision-making autonomy was not significantly associated with early ANC initiation. Although the point estimate was slightly negative, the effect was imprecisely estimated and not statistically significant. These results suggest that decision-making autonomy alone may be insufficient to ensure early ANC initiation in contexts where structural barriers to care persist.
