Clinical Reasoning‐Led Physiotherapy Intervention for Biceps Tendinopathy: A Case Report
Authors: Siddiqua Syeda Ummul, Kazi Md Azman Hossain, Fabiha Alam, Saleha Fazal, Farzana Sharmin, Md. Mafrohi Sattar, Md. Ershad Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLong head of biceps tendinopathy (LHBT) is a common source of anterior shoulder pain, often misdiagnosed due to overlapping symptoms with other shoulder disorders. This case report presents a 56‐year‐old female with chronic shoulder pain managed through a clinical reasoning‐guided physiotherapy program. Assessment using clinical reasoning, including the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework, confirmed LHBT. The intervention included pain management, manual therapy, range of motion exercises, strengthening exercises, and patient education over 6 weeks. Outcomes showed marked improvements: pain decreased from 7/10 to 1/10, muscle strength improved from 4/5 to 5/5, and functional disability scores (SPADI, DASH) reduced by over 80%. These findings demonstrate that structured, reasoning‐based physiotherapy can facilitate accurate diagnosis and effective recovery in LHBT. This approach provides a model for evidence‐informed, individualized clinical reasoning‐based rehabilitation in similar musculoskeletal conditions.

Introduction

Long head of the biceps tendinopathy (LHBT) is a prevalent musculoskeletal disorder characterized by persistent anterior shoulder pain, localized tenderness, weakness, and functional impairment. The long head of the biceps originates from the supraglenoid tubercle and superior glenoid labrum of the scapula and passes through the bicipital groove before inserting at the radial tuberosity [1]. Due to its anatomical course and biomechanical role, the tendon is susceptible to overload during repetitive activities such as lifting, pulling, overhead movements, or throwing, which may lead to tendon degeneration, inflammation, or partial tearing. Clinically, patients commonly report pain during shoulder flexion or abduction, weakness with lifting, and discomfort during overhead or rotational activities [2, 3]. Identified risk factors include age between 18 and 65 years, repetitive upper limb activity, overuse among athletes and manual workers, and anatomical variations of the bicipital groove. Although LHBT accounts for approximately 5% of proximal biceps pathology, it frequently occurs secondary to other shoulder disorders and has been reported in up to 90% of rotator cuff tears, with 45% demonstrating associated LHBT instability [4, 5]. The underlying pathomechanics involve traction, friction, and compressive forces within the constricted bicipital groove; however, LHBT often coexists with or mimics other shoulder conditions, including rotator cuff pathology, labral tears, and glenohumeral instability, complicating accurate clinical diagnosis [1, 4, 6].

Diagnosing overlapping shoulder disorders is clinically challenging and requires a structured clinical reasoning approach. Clinical reasoning is a systematic process in which physiotherapists collect patient history, perform physical examinations, interpret findings, and develop individualized management plans. Several reasoning models support this process, including hypothetico‐deductive reasoning, pattern recognition, three‐track reasoning, and narrative reasoning. When combined with the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework, these approaches enable comprehensive evaluation of body functions and structures, activity limitations, participation restrictions, and contextual factors, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy and supporting personalized treatment planning [7, 8, 9, 10].

Evidence‐based physiotherapy interventions for LHBT emphasize a multimodal approach that includes patient education, activity modification, exercise therapy, manual therapy, and adjunctive pain‐relief modalities. Progressive loading exercises aim to restore tendon integrity, improve neuromuscular control, and facilitate shoulder mobility, while manual therapy addresses soft tissue and joint mobility impairments. Structured treatment protocols guided by clinical reasoning have been shown to reduce pain, minimize disability, and promote a return to functional activities by addressing both primary pathology and contributory biomechanical and psychosocial factors [7, 11, 12].

This case report highlights the clinical importance of appropriate LHBT diagnosis and demonstrates a structured, clinical reasoning–led physiotherapy approach to managing anterior shoulder pain. Rather than emphasizing intervention novelty, the focus is on the reasoning process used to address diagnostic uncertainty. By integrating hypothesis‐driven assessment, differential diagnosis, and repeated reassessment within the ICF framework, this report illustrates how clinical reasoning can guide safe and effective physiotherapy decision‐making in the absence of imaging confirmation.

Case Presentation

Patients' Description

A 56‐year‐old female housewife presented with a 7‐month history of anterior shoulder pain. The pain began after a minor incident while lifting household items weighing 3–4 kg with her right hand. Initially, the pain was intermittent and sharp, but gradually progressed to a constant dull ache. At presentation, the patient rated her pain as 7/10 on the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS). Pain worsened during overhead activities, lifting, and reaching, limiting daily household tasks. On examination, there was localized tenderness along the bicipital groove extending approximately 2 cm proximally and distally, with mild swelling and warmth. Pain was reproduced with palpation and resisted elbow flexion with forearm supination. Special tests were positive, including Speed's, Yergason's, and Upper‐Cut tests. Shoulder girdle and scapular assessment revealed mild scapular dyskinesis, reduced upward rotation (~8° deficit), and side‐to‐side differences > 1.5 cm on the Lateral Scapular Slide Test (LSST). The Scapular Dyskinesis Test (SDT) and Scapular Retraction Test (SRT) also indicated weak stabilizer function. Manual muscle testing showed mild weakness in shoulder flexion, abduction, and external rotation (4/5), confirmed by handheld dynamometry (~12.5 kg for shoulder flexion). Active and passive range of motion limitations were also recorded (Table 1). Neurological examination was normal. Written informed consent was obtained, and all procedures followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Differential Diagnosis and Diagnostic Reasoning

Anterior shoulder pain can result from multiple conditions. Rotator cuff tendinopathy was considered unlikely because resisted abduction and external rotation strength were preserved, a painful arc was absent, and tests, including drop arm and impingement‐oriented assessments, were negative. Subacromial pain syndrome was excluded due to negative Neer and Hawkins–Kennedy tests and absence of pain in sustained overhead positions. SLAP lesions were considered less likely because the patient reported no catching, locking, instability, or pain during overhead or throwing activities.

Diagnostic certainty for LHBT tendinopathy was strengthened through multiple findings. Positive special tests (Speed, Yergason, Upper‐Cut), localized tenderness along the bicipital groove, and symptom reproduction with resisted elbow flexion supported the diagnosis. Objective scapular assessment further supported the clinical impression. Serial reassessments confirmed consistent findings over time. Imaging was not performed as there were no red flags, and the patient responded favorably to conservative management.

Clinical Reasoning Process

The assessment and management were guided by a structured clinical reasoning framework combining Pattern Recognition, Hypothetico‐Deductive Reasoning, Three‐Track Reasoning, and Narrative Reasoning. Pattern recognition helped identify symptom clusters typical of LHBT tendinopathy. Hypothetico‐deductive reasoning systematically excluded alternative diagnoses. Three‐Track Reasoning integrated physical, functional, and psychosocial information. Narrative reasoning ensured that the intervention addressed the patient's daily activity limitations, goals, and lifestyle. This approach allowed precise identification of the primary pathology and informed an individualized physiotherapy program. Table 2 represents a brief description of the clinical reasoning process. Clinical Reasoning‐Led Physiotherapy Intervention

The patient underwent a progressive, evidence‐based physiotherapy intervention program guided by clinical reasoning principles. The intervention aimed to alleviate pain, restore ROM, improve muscle strength, and enhance shoulder function. A total of 18 supervised sessions were delivered over 6 weeks (3 sessions per week), beginning with pain control and tendon protection, followed by ROM restoration, strengthening, and proprioceptive/functional retraining. Patient education on activity modification and nutrition was also incorporated to support tendon healing and prevent recurrence [7, 8]. Outcome Measurements

Outcomes were measured using validated and reliable tools. Outcome measurements were conducted at three time points: baseline (pre‐intervention), mid‐intervention (3 weeks), and post‐intervention (6 weeks). Pain intensity was evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) [13], pressure sensitivity was assessed using a pressure algometer [14], and range of motion was measured using a standard goniometer [15]. Muscle strength was measured manually by manual muscle testing as well as to ensure more accuracy with a handheld digital dynamometer [16], while functional disability and activity limitations were assessed using the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH) questionnaire [17], and the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI) [18]. Mid‐intervention assessment allowed evaluation of immediate treatment effects and informed ongoing intervention adjustments.

Results

The patient demonstrated progressive improvement across all outcome measures over the 6 weeks intervention. Pain intensity (VAS) decreased from 7/10 at baseline to 4/10 at 3 weeks and 1/10 at discharge. Pressure tolerance/pain sensitivity improved from 3.5 to 5.8 kg at mid‐intervention and 8.2 kg at 6 weeks. Active and passive shoulder and elbow range of motion gradually recovered, with restricted baseline movements achieving near‐full functional range by discharge. Muscle strength increased from 4/5 (≈12.5 kg) at baseline to 4+/5 (≈16.2 kg) at 3 weeks, reaching 5/5 (≈21.4 kg) at discharge. Functional disability (as measured by SPADI and DASH) declined from 62.3% and 55.6/100 at baseline to 41% and 32.4/100 at 3 weeks, respectively, and then reached 12% and 9.7/100 at discharge, reflecting a substantial restoration of function. Overall, these findings indicate clinically meaningful improvements in pain, range of motion, muscle strength, and functional ability over the course of the intervention, with brief outcomes summarized in Tables 4 and 5. Discussion

This case illustrates how a structured clinical reasoning process can guide physiotherapy decision‐making in a patient with suspected LHBT and overlapping shoulder symptoms. Outcomes are interpreted within the context of hypothesis‐driven assessment, ongoing reassessment, and symptom‐guided progression, rather than being attributed solely to specific interventions. The improvements observed align with previous research showing that multimodal, individualized physiotherapy reduces symptoms while addressing biomechanical and functional deficits associated with tendon pathology [3]. Tailored interventions combining progressive manual therapy, structured exercise, scapular stabilization, and activity modification consistently demonstrate significant clinical benefits.

Pain intensity decreased from 7/10 to 1/10, an 86% reduction. Comparable studies on multimodal physiotherapy for biceps and rotator cuff tendinopathy have reported similar or slower pain reductions over longer periods [7, 12]. Early pain relief in this case likely reflects tendon unloading through education, short‐term analgesic modalities, and the long‐term desensitization effects of eccentric strengthening and stabilization exercises. Pressure pain threshold improved from 3.5 kg to 8.2 kg (134%), indicating tendon adaptation and reduced peripheral sensitization via progressive loading. Evidence suggests that combining manual therapy with eccentric exercise promotes tissue remodeling and enhances load tolerance [19].

Shoulder ROM approached full functional mobility within 6 weeks, likely driven by concurrent pain reduction, manual therapy, and neuromuscular retraining targeting scapulohumeral rhythm [7, 11]. Muscle strength improved from 4/5 (~12.5 kg) to 5/5 (~21.4 kg), a 71% gain, reflecting the importance of combining eccentric loading, scapular stabilization, and kinetic chain exercises [19]. Functional disability also improved markedly, with SPADI decreasing from 62.3% to 12% and DASH from 55.6% to 9.7%, over an 80% reduction, highlighting the effectiveness of a multimodal, patient‐centered approach [2, 7].

Overall, this case demonstrates that structured, reasoning‐guided physiotherapy can achieve meaningful improvements in pain, mobility, strength, and function within a relatively short period. While typical tendinopathy programs report gains over 8–12 weeks, the 6‐week recovery here underscores that customized, well‐structured interventions can accelerate progress safely. These results suggest that integrating diagnostic reasoning, progressive tendon loading, and patient‐centered strategies offers synergistic benefits beyond symptom relief, emphasizing physiotherapy as a process for restoring holistic function, addressing biomechanical deficits, and enhancing patient confidence and quality of life [4].

Strengths and Limitations

A major strength of this case was the structured use of multiple clinical reasoning strategies, which enhanced diagnostic precision and guided individualized intervention. The incorporation of the ICF framework further allowed holistic assessment, ensuring that both impairments and participation restrictions were addressed. Close supervision and strong patient adherence likely accelerated recovery compared to standard timelines.

However, the case is limited by its single‐patient design, which restricts generalizability. Objective imaging or long‐term follow‐up was not performed, preventing confirmation of structural tendon changes or sustainability of improvements. Additionally, psychological outcomes were not formally measured, despite being targeted through narrative reasoning.

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that a clinical reasoning‐led physiotherapy intervention program can result in rapid and meaningful improvements in pain, muscle strength, mobility, and functional ability in LHBT. The outcomes were consistent with and, in some domains, exceeded results reported in prior research, supporting the rationale that structured reasoning, progressive tendon loading, and holistic patient engagement can optimize short‐term recovery. These findings add to the evidence base advocating for individualized physiotherapy in tendon rehabilitation.

Implications for Practice

This case underscores the critical role of clinical reasoning in managing tendinopathies and complex musculoskeletal conditions. Integrating accurate diagnosis, gradual exercise progression, and patient‐centered strategies can improve functional outcomes. Multimodal interventions combining pain management, manual therapy, tendon loading, and functional retraining are most effective when applied within a reasoning‐guided framework. For clinical practice, providers should prioritize structured, individualized approaches over protocol‐driven models to optimize functional recovery, with broader applicability across complex musculoskeletal disorders.
