Specific of mechanical alloying of solid-liquid binary system Fe-Ga and effect of different process control agents
Authors: V.A. Milyutin, R. Bures, M. Faberova, F. Kromka, B. Kunca
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mechanical alloying allows obtaining nonequilibrium structures in various systems, often possessing unique properties, including magnetic ones. Considering the unusual structural features of the magnetostrictive Fe-Ga alloy, this approach may be promising for this system. In this work, extensive experimental studies were carried out aimed at studying the features of mechanical alloying of Fe-Ga. The object of the study was the system Fe-20 wt% Ga in which disordered solid solution α-Fe(Ga) is formed. It was shown that high-intensity milling is an effective tool for mechanical alloying of solid-liquid binary system Fe-Ga, but a serious problem is a low powder recovery, less than 50 %. To solve this problem, various process control agents were tested. Their influence on powder recovery, process kinetics, particle size, carbon contamination, and magnetic properties was studied using a large set of techniques such as XRD, SEM, EDS, VSM, LIBS, and others. It has been shown that, based on a combination of factors, the optimal process control agent for this system is ethanol in an amount of 1 wt%

Introduction

In modern electrical engineering, there is a steady trend towards minimizing the use of rare earth elements. The main reasons are their extremely high price, as well as the enormous damage that their extraction causes to the environment [1]. Therefore, increased attention is being paid to rare earth-free materials in an attempt to replace traditional alloys and compounds with high rare earth content. One such example is Fe-Ga, which has abnormally high magnetostriction and is intended to replace the widely used magnetostrictive material Tb-Dy-Fe. In addition to high magnetostriction, the alloy also has a large set of unique characteristics such as good damping properties [2], strong auxetic behavior in a certain crystallographic direction [3], giant Nernst effect [4], the high-temperature stability of magnetization [5]. All this opens up great prospects for the practical application of the alloy in various fields of engineering. A wide range of potential applications involves the use of the alloy in various forms: sheets [6], rods [7], wires [[4], [8], [9]], thin films [10], and powder [11]. There are distinctive features in the manufacture and operation of all these Fe-Ga products [10,9,12]. This work is devoted to the issue of obtaining powder from Fe-Ga alloy. The powder can be obtained either from precursors, which are obtained by pre-melting the elements and then crushed in some way, for example, milling [13] or gas atomization [14], or directly from pure elements by mechanical alloying (MA). All these methods have their advantages, for example, in the case of milling, it is possible to preliminarily implement heat treatment of ingot (quenching), which, as is known, increases magnetostriction [15] and thus obtain a powder with higher magnetostriction. On the other hand, MA as a nonequilibrium process allows to overcome some metallurgical limitations and obtain unique structures. For example, using MA, it is hypothetically possible to obtain single-phase FeGaZn and FeGaCu alloys (solid solution α-Fe(Ga, Zn) and α-Fe(Ga, Cu) correspondingly), which, according to calculations, have increased magnetostriction [16,17]. They are difficult to obtain using traditional methods from the liquid phase due to the formation of a second parasitic phase or technical problems [18,19]. In addition, MA may be suitable for the obtaining of Fe-Ga alloys with small additions of rare-earth elements (<0.5 at.%). Due to MA, it is possible to enhance the solubility of rare-earth elements in α-Fe(Ga) lattice, which, as shown earlier, leads to a significant increase in magnetostriction [20]. Thus, MA opens up broad prospects in obtaining FeGa alloys with unique properties.

MA is usually defined as a solid-state powder processing technique that involves repeated cold welding, fracturing, and rewelding of powder particles in a high-energy ball mill [21]. As a result, there is an accumulation of crystal lattice defects in the particles and grain refinement. In combination with constant mixing and local temperature increase, this promotes diffusion and the formation of various phases. There are many examples of successful obtaining of solid solutions, including supersaturated, intermetallic, and amorphous phases, using MA in various systems [22,23]. In addition, many results have been obtained in the production of functional magnetic alloys such as Al-permalloy [24], high entropy alloys [[25], [26], [27]], Finemet-type alloys [28], binary and ternary Fe-based alloys such as Fe-Al-B [29], Fe-Al [30], Fe-Si [31], Fe-Si-B [31], etc. In general, the features of MA in Fe-based alloys have now been studied in sufficient detail, which makes it possible to obtain the required structures and properties in these alloys.

However, the problem is that the MA of even the binary Fe-Ga system has been studied very little, which does not allow developing modes for obtaining binary and ternary alloys based on Fe-Ga. The MA process of Fe-Ga is complicated by two factors: the high plasticity of pure Fe and the low melting point of pure Ga (29.76 °C). Due to the mechanical impact of intensive milling, the powder temperature inevitably exceeds 30 °C, which leads to the melting of Ga. In general, solid-liquid reactions of metallic materials during MA are much less common than solid-solid ones and have been studied much less. Because of this, it is difficult to predict the outcome of the MA process in advance based on previous results in similar Fe-based binary systems, for example, Fe-Ge [32] and Fe-Al [30]. In addition, this, combined with the high ductility of iron, can lead to the problem of powder sticking to the milling media, which often occurs in the case of MA of ductile elements, for example in Fe-Al [30]. To solve this problem, process control agents (PCAs) are typically used. Their main task is to prevent excessive welding of the particles with each other and with milling media, but at the same time maintain a balance between cold welding and fracturing of particles [22]. There is no universal PCA suitable for all systems and MA regimes, and it is extremely difficult to predict the optimal amount of PCA to achieve the desired effect. In addition to controlling powder sticking, the type and amount of PCA have a strong influence on the possibility of phase formation and the kinetics of this process [30], particle size and morphology [33], as well as the contamination of the powder [34]. As a rule in MA, compromises and optimal solutions are always required. This also applies to selecting the type and quantity of PCA for specific processes. This work aims to study the effect of different process control agents on the mechanical alloying of Fe-Ga. The range of possible phases in Fe-Ga alloys according to the phase diagram is quite wide [35]. In this work, we focused on the formation of the A2 phase, which is a disordered solid solution of Ga in the α-iron lattice. This phase on a macro scale provides increased magnetostriction in compositions up to 20 at.% Ga and, as a consequence, has practical significance.

Materials and methods

Mechanical alloying process

As starting materials Fe with 99.95 % purity supplied by Höganäs (commercial name ABC 100.30) and Ga with purity 99.9999 % supplied by CMK s.r.o. were used. The median particle size of Fe is about 100 μm. Ga in a solid state was mechanically crushed onto particles under 1 mm. Powders in proportion Fe-20 % Ga (weight percentage is used in this paper unless otherwise specified) were mixed in a Resodyn acoustic mixer for 3 min to achieve a homogeneous distribution of Fe and Ga powder before MA. This mix (50 g) is then placed in a 125 ml milling jar with 15 steel balls of 15 mm diameter each, giving a ball to powder ratio of 4:1. High intensity ball mill Retsch Emax with temperature control was used for MA. The milling intensity was set at 1000 rpm, the temperature range from 30 to 42 °C. When the temperature reaches 42 °C, the process automatically stops to cool the milling jar using the built-in liquid cooling system. This temperature limit was chosen as a compromise between a high enough temperature to carry out long MA processes in a sufficient time (too low upper temperature is reached very quickly during high-intensity process, so much time is spent on cooling rather than on the process itself) and a relatively low temperature to minimize the risk of changing the aggregate state of the PCA inside the jars. Effective milling time (excluding cooling) was 12 h, with sampling (2 g) each 4 h. The direction of movement of the milling jars was reversed every 10 min. Most of the processes were implemented without a protective atmosphere. However, the milling jars are equipped with gas-tight lids, which prevents any contact with the atmosphere during the process. In several cases, MA was implemented under an Ar atmosphere without sampling. Various PCAs were used, both solid and liquid, in amounts from 1 up to 3 %. The list was adjusted during the experiment according to the intermediate results. Characterization

The structure and morphology of the powder was studied using SEM Tescan Vega 3. For the additional chemical analysis, a digital microscope with an Integrated Laser Induced Breakdown Spectroscopy (LIBS) analyzer Keyence VHX-970FN was used. Analysis of carbon content was carried out using the volumetric method. To do this, the powder was thoroughly washed in an ultrasonic bath to remove organic residues and control the amount of only the carbon that entered the powder structure. XRD analysis was carried out using Philips X'Pert Pro with Сu Kα radiation. For post-processing of the XRD data, namely the calculation of lattice parameters, microstrains, and crystallite size, Maunt and FullProf software were used. A Malvern Mastersizer 2000M laser analyzer was used to analyze the particle size distribution. The density of powder samples was measured using a helium pycnometry system (AccuPyc II 1340). The magnetic properties of the powder were studied using VSM MicroSence EV9 at ambient temperature and magnetic field up to 1250 kA/m. The powder recovery value was calculated as the proportion of powder that can be extracted from the milling jar after the end of the process without additional mechanical action.

Results and discussion

The process without PCA

As a starting point, the process without any PCAs was carried out. The powder after MA was studied by several different methods. Since Ga atoms are larger than Fe, substitution with Ga increases the lattice parameter and shifts the XRD peaks towards smaller 2θ angles. It is known that composition Fe-20 at.% Ga has lattice parameter (a) 2.907 Å [36], while pure iron used in this work has 2.864 Å. Thus, XRD analysis is an effective tool for monitoring solid solution formation during MA of Fe and Ga. First of all, it should be noted that neither the superstructure reflections of the ordered structure D03 or B2 nor the peaks from other ordered phases that may occur in Fe-Ga, such as L12 [35], were observed in XRD profiles corresponding to all process durations (Fig. 1a). It is known that ordered nanoclusters with different types of ordering in a disordered matrix can play an important role in the formation of magnetoelastic properties of FeGa [37]. Due to their small size (usually up to 10 nm) and small volume fraction, high-resolution methods such as HRTEM [38], SANS [39,40] and other are used to study them in cast alloys. The question of their formation during MA remains open to date. The fact that we did not detect ordered phases using the XRD method does not indicate their unequivocal absence in the structure. This issue requires additional study of the powder using high-resolution methods.Fig. 1XRD profiles of the powder after 4, 8, and 12 h of MA process without PCA (a) and 110 peak after 4 h of MA.Fig. 1

XRD profiles of the powder after 4, 8, and 12 h of MA process without PCA (a) and 110 peak after 4 h of MA.

In addition, no peaks corresponding to oxides were observed. Partial oxidation of powder is a common phenomenon during MA. However, in this case, the oxide content is small and not detected by the XRD method. Even though the MA process was carried out without a protective atmosphere, the amount of oxygen inside the milling jars is negligible, and the gas-tight lid prevents the ingress of additional air. From a thermodynamic point of view, the most probable oxidation reactions are the formation of Ga2O3 and Fe3O4 as can be seen from the result of ΔG calculation using HSC Chemistry software (Fig. S1 supplementary material). The potential amount of oxide, e.g. Ga2O3 that could be formed by reaction with available oxygen can be easily calculated. For approximate calculations air volume of 125 ml (total volume of milling jar) can be used, not taking into account the fact that a significant part of the jar (up to 2/3) is occupied by milling balls and powder so the actual free volume is much less. At standard pressure and temperature, the mass of oxygen in 125 ml of air is 37 mg. If this entire amount reacts with gallium, 148 mg of Ga2O3 will be formed, which is approximately 0.3 % of the total weight of the powder. Even considering the two openings of milling jars for sampling, the total amount of possible oxides, if any, is too small to be detected by the XRD method, which usually has a threshold of phase detection of about 1–2% [41]. A similar situation is true for iron oxides. A direct comparison of the result indicating the absence of a noticeable amount of oxides with the literature data is difficult, since, to our knowledge, the issue of oxidation during the milling process in Fe-Ga has not been studied. However, it can be compared with information on similar systems, such as Fe-Cr, given that Cr and Ga have very similar affinities for O2. In the work [42] the MA process of FeCr-based alloy was experimentally investigated. The oxide (Fe, Cr)2O3 was detected by XRD only after 38 h of milling, with a shorter process time, oxides were not observed. In our work, we are dealing with a process with a maximum duration of 12 h with only two lid openings, so it can be assumed that the presence of oxides is minimal. Moreover, taking into account the previous results of the study of the influence of the milling atmosphere on the magnetic properties of Fe-based powder obtained using MA [43] it can be assumed that the possible influence on the properties is also insignificant and we do not consider it further.

Only a shift of peaks is observed in the XRD profiles. This indicates the formation of a solid solution α-Fe(Ga), so it can be concluded that the MA process is effective with the selected parameters. In addition, a broadening of the peaks compared to the initial state can be observed, which is associated with the accumulation of defects during the milling process. It is known, that in the case of the Fe-Al alloy, which is close to the Fe-Ga system two mechanisms of solid solution formation during MA take place. They are gradual diffusion of Al into the Fe lattice and the rapid formation of Fe(Al) solid solution coexisting with other phases [30,44]. In the case of Fe-Ga, we also observe both mechanisms. At the initial stage of MA (4 h), asymmetric peaks are observed. It can be fitted by assuming that the two overlapping peaks correspond to the α-Fe and A2 solid solution (Fig. 1b). After 8 h, the asymmetry of the peaks becomes almost imperceptible, and after 12 h h it disappears completely, which indicates the completion of the solid solution formation. Another parameter that confirms the successful formation of the A2 solid solution is the density. As the duration of the process increases, its value is 7.51, 7.58, and 7.66 g/cm3 after 4, 8, and 12 h respectively. Since the density of pure Ga (5.9–6.1 g/cm3 depending on the aggregate state) is significantly lower than that of Fe and Fe-Ga, a decrease in the amount of free gallium due to its dissolution in the iron lattice leads to an increase in the density of the powder.

During the early stages of milling, particles are crushed to the size of about 10 μm or less. Then cold welding of the particles occurs, which leads to the formation of agglomerates. After 4 h of milling, agglomerates are formed by a smaller number of larger particles. The surface of the agglomerates is covered with smaller fragmented particles. After 8 h of milling, the agglomerates grow and their overall shape remains irregular. After 12 h of milling, the agglomerates were compacted, and the surface of the agglomerates was smoothed. This can be seen in SEM images (Fig. 2) and particle size distribution (Fig. 3). Agglomerates are visible after 4 h of MA, and as milling continues, their number and size increase, leading to an increase in the average powder size. After all durations of MA, the powder has granular irregular morphology.Fig. 2SEM images of Fe-Ga powder after 4, 8, and 12 h of the MA process without PCA.Fig. 2Fig. 3Particle size distribution of Fe-Ga powder after 4, 8, and 12 h of the MA process without PCA.Fig. 3

The problem with the process without PCA is the extremely low powder recovery, which is 42 % of the initial amount. Even the rather frequent change of movement direction of milling jars (every 10 min) does not help to avoid this. Most of the powder sticks to the walls of the milling jar. In addition to the loss of the material, this can lead to a violation of the initial ratio between Fe and Ga and, as a consequence, the impossibility of obtaining an alloy of a given composition. To solve this problem, several PCAs were tested in this work.

Selection of PCAs and effect on powder recovery

First of all, stearic acid (C18H36O2), which is probably the most widely used solid PCA for MA of various metal systems, was tested as PCA. It is a relatively stable compound with a melting point of about 70 °C and a boiling point of 361 °C. It has proven to be an effective PCA for mechanical alloying of Fe-based alloys such as Fe40Co30Ni30 [43], Fe-Zr-based alloy [45], and Fe-Al [46]. Two MA processes with different amounts of stearic acid (1 and 3 %) were realized. The addition of PCA prevented powder sticking to the milling media and significantly increased powder recovery. The process with 1 % stearic acid went smoothly. However, we encountered some problems when the amount of stearic acid was increased to 3 %. When the first sample was taken after 4 h of the powder with 3 % stearic acid, an intense exothermic chemical reaction was detected and the process was interrupted. To avoid this, a second attempt was made where the process with 3 % stearic acid was carried out in an Ar atmosphere without sampling. However, opening the jar after 12 h again found a chemical reaction that is accompanied by the release of heat and a characteristic smell. Powder analysis revealed the presence of large amounts of oxygen in many particles (Fig. S2), indicating that the observed exothermic reaction represents the combustion of Fe powder. The use of 3 % stearic acid as PCA at the selected MA parameters results in a strong reduction in particle size while imparting a flaky morphology to the particles (Fig. S2), which promotes the pyrophoricity of iron and leads to its combustion upon contact with air. Another disadvantage of using stearic acid as a PCA for MA in the Fe-Ga system is the possible formation of Ga stearate during milling according to the reaction:2Ga +3(C18H36O2) → 2(Ga(C18H35O2)3) + 3H2.

Experiments on the reaction of pure metal powders with stearic acid showed that gallium is one of the three metals with the lowest reaction onset temperature and the fastest formation kinetics [47]. Such a reaction is possible with a local increase in temperature, specific surface area, and accumulation of defects during the MA process. Although no problems were found with small amounts of stearic acid (1 %), we conclude that the use of stearic acid as a PCA in the MA of the Fe-Ga system is undesirable. For this reason, it is necessary to search for other optimal PCAs. In this work, we chose zinc stearate, polyvinyl alcohol (both are solid-state PCA), and ethyl alcohol for this role. Zinc stearate Zn(C18H35O2)2 is a promising alternative to pure stearic acid in this case because this compound is expected to not react with pure gallium. Moreover, possible zinc contamination in the case of Fe-Ga alloys does not have a negative effect on the functional properties, on the contrary, by theoretical calculations, it is expected that partial replacement of gallium with zinc can lead to an increase in magnetostriction [16]. In turn, polyvinyl alcohol is a polar adhesive and film-forming polymer with a very high melting temperature (more than 200 °C), which is expected to be an effective PCA in this case. In addition, liquid PCA, namely ethyl alcohol in amounts 1, 2, and 3 % was used. Ethyl alcohol is also a widespread PCA for MA of many metallic systems [30,48]. Experiments with liquid PCA were carried out without sampling to avoid evaporation of alcohol when the lid was opened.

The addition of any PCA significantly increases powder recovery compared to the process without PCA (Table 2). However, the effect depends greatly on the type and amount of PCA. The exception is ethyl alcohol, any amount of it leads to approximately the same result. Of course, it is impossible to select the optimal PCA based on powder recovery information alone. It is necessary to carefully study the influence on the kinetics of the process, the size, and morphology of the powders, as well as the contamination (primarily carbon), which inevitably occurs in the presence of organic compounds during milling.

Particle size and structure of the powder

The addition of any PCA results in a significant reduction in particle size compared to the process without PCA. During milling of metallic systems grinding of particles and their cold welding repeat many times. PCA usually limits the possibility of cold welding because it acts as a surfactant, and therefore reduces the resulting particle size. This work shows that the addition of any PCA to the Fe-Ga system leads to a decrease in particle size by approximately an order of magnitude (Fig. 4). All images at different magnifications along with other experimental data can be found in the raw data repository [49]. In general, SEM images of the powder after MA are similar regardless of the type and amount of PCA. All powder samples contain fine particles ranging in size from 1 to 5 μm and agglomerates ranging in size from approximately 10 to 20 μm. Their ratio and size are approximately the same in all cases. In terms of morphology, there are minor differences in the powder obtained by MA with different PCA. Individual particles in all cases have a predominantly irregular granular morphology. At some stages, particles with flaky-like morphology can be observed (4 h of MA with 1 % of zinc stearate). Agglomerates at intermediate stages of the process (4 and 8 h) have spherical or ellipsoidal morphology with smooth (PVA, ethanol) or irregular surface (zinc stearate and stearic acid). After 12 h in all cases, agglomerates have a mostly spherical morphology with a smooth surface, their number and size decrease.Fig. 4SEM images of the powder after 12 of MA with 1 % (a) and 3 % (b) of polyvinyl alcohol, 1 % zinc stearate (c), and 1 % stearic acid (d).Fig. 4

SEM images of the powder after 12 of MA with 1 % (a) and 3 % (b) of polyvinyl alcohol, 1 % zinc stearate (c), and 1 % stearic acid (d).

However, the representative sample of SEM data is too small to draw a definitive conclusion regarding particle size. This information can be obtained from particle size distribution. Distributions for several processes are shown in Fig. 5. All distributions with the addition of PCA, regardless of its type and quantity, are of the bimodal type. The first peak is typically in the range of 1–10 μm, and the second peak is in the range of 40–60 μm. The reason for the presence of a second peak corresponding to larger particles is the formation of agglomerates due to cold welding. Although the tendency for their formation is reduced by the addition of PCA (compared to the process without PCA), it does not disappear completely under the milling parameters used. The evolution of particle size distribution with milling time varies slightly for different PCAs. For example, in the case of 1 % PVA (Fig. 5a), a logical and regular behavior of the particle size distribution is observed. With increasing MA time, the proportion of small particles increases and the proportion of large particles decreases (Fig. 5a). This occurs because as defects accumulate and the possibility of plastic deformation is exhausted, individual particles cease to form agglomerates. The same trend is also true for the late stages (8 and 12 h) of the process with 1 % stearic acid (Fig. 5b), although the distribution after 4 h is more complex. This is because the different properties of the compounds used as PCA have a greater influence at the initial stage of the process. A clear effect of the amount of PCA is seen in the example of ethanol (Fig. 5c). An increase in its amount leads to a shift of the first peak towards smaller particle size, while the right peak remains in the same position, only the intensity changes. Thus, it can be concluded that in the Fe-Ga system, the type and amount of PCA (up to 3 %) has virtually no effect on the size of the agglomerates; with an MA duration of up to 12 h, only their ratio changes.Fig. 5Evolution of particle size distributions along with milling time for MA with 1 % PVA (a), 1 % stearic acid (b), and after 12 h of MA with different content of ethanol (c).Fig. 5

Effect of PCA on the kinetic of the solid solution formation

To study the influence of PCA on the kinetics of solid solution formation, XRD analysis of the powder was carried out at different stages of MA. It is shown that during the MA process, two phenomena occur simultaneously: an increase in the lattice parameter of α-Fe and an increase in the intensity of the peaks belonging to the solid solution A2, corresponding to the composition Fe80Ga20 with a gallium content at the boundary of its solubility limit in α-Fe. The change in both of these parameters with increasing MA time for five processes with different PCA is shown in Fig. 6. At the initial stages (4 and 8 h), the simultaneous presence of two phases is observed. However, as the peaks corresponding to α-Fe(Ga) shift towards smaller angles (increase in the lattice parameter), due to the increase in the number of dissolved gallium atoms, the boundaries between them disappear. Therefore, the volume fraction of the A2 phase for samples after 12 h of MA is not given.Fig. 6Results of XRD analysis of the MA samples with different PCA. Results of XRD analysis of the MA samples with different PCA. The lattice parameter of α-Fe phase (a) and a fraction of A2 solid solution corresponding to the Fe80Ga20 composition (b).

The specificity and kinetics of formation of the target phase depending on the PCA used can be estimated based on the data presented in Fig. 6. The no-PCA process, as expected, shows the fastest increase in both lattice parameter and A2 phase amount. This is because in this case, there is nothing to prevent cold welding and thus more Fe/Ga interfaces are formed, which in turn promotes interdiffusion and solid solution formation. However, in this case, it is also necessary to take into account that the initially specified Fe-Ga ratio may be disturbed since a significant part of the powder adheres to the grinding media (mainly in the case of the no-PCA process). Using LIBS analysis it was shown that residuals on milling media contain predominantly iron (Fig. S3). Thus, the original Fe-Ga ratio is disturbed due to the increase in the amount of gallium in the final composition, resulting in the largest lattice parameter resulting from the non-PCA process (Fig. 6a). In other cases, this effect is less pronounced, since powder recovery increases significantly. In terms of process kinetic, almost all PCAs have similar effects. Different behavior was observed in experiments with PVA. According to the kinetics of the process, MA with the addition of 1 % PVA is the closest to no-PCA MA. This information, as well as analysis of powder recovery, leads to the conclusion that 1 % PVA does not reduce the cold welding ability of the Fe-Ga powder in the MA process. At the same time, the addition of 3 % PVA significantly reduces the rate of solid solution formation; moreover, even 12 h are not enough to complete this process. In the case of MA processes with the addition of 1 % zinc stearate and stearic acid, almost identical behavior is observed (Fig. 6), which is associated with the similar properties of these PCAs. In both cases, 12 h of milling with the selected parameters is sufficient to complete the MA process.

The effect of the amount of ethanol as PCA was also studied. The following α-Fe lattice parameters were obtained for the samples after 12 h of milling: 2.894, 2.892, and 2.888 Å for the processes with 1, 2 and 3 % ethanol, respectively. From the point of view of XRD analysis, there is no noticeable difference between 1 and 2 %, in both cases there are symmetrical peaks corresponding to a 100 % Fe(Ga) solid solution with a close lattice parameter. However, the addition of 3 % results in a pronounced asymmetry, which is a consequence of the incomplete MA process. Thus, it can be concluded that 3 % of ethanol, as well as 3 % of PVA, significantly slows down the kinetics of the formation of a solid solution in the Fe-Ga system, which leads to the need to increase the process time and/or its intensity.

Сarbon contamination

The presence of organic compounds acting as PCA can result in some amounts of carbon being introduced into the powder. An additional possible factor is contamination from steel milling media (milling jar and balls). In magnetic materials, it is especially important to control the level of carbon contamination, since even small amounts have a significant impact on magnetic properties. Ethanol shows the best results in terms of contamination since the smallest amount of carbon is found in the powder after MA when 1 % is added.

Magnetic properties

The magnetic properties of the Fe-Ga alloy are of practical importance, and their analysis can serve as an additional method for controlling MA processes.

In the example of Fig. 7a, which shows the change in hysteresis loops over time during MA without PCA, it is evident that as the solid solution is formed, the saturation magnetization of the MS powder increases. This is because the measured magnetization is normalized to the mass. In the initial state, there is a powder mixture of 80 % pure iron, which is ferromagnetic, and 20 % gallium, which is diamagnetic. Therefore, as gallium atoms enter the iron lattice and the content of the non-magnetic component in the powder mix decreases, an increase in magnetization is observed, although the saturation magnetization of A2 Fe(Ga) is lower than that of pure Fe. The amount of free gallium present in the powder at a given MA stage has a decisive influence. Therefore, the experiments with 3 % PVA and ethanol lead to the lowest MS value (Table 4), because even after 12 h the formation of a solid solution is not finished. Discussion

In this paper, the influence of different PCAs on the formation of Fe(Ga) solid solution under high-intensity MA is investigated. It has been shown that the use of various PCAs in some cases leads to slight differences in the kinetics and prevailing mechanisms of solid solution formation, particle size evolution, powder recovery, and the degree of carbon contamination. Stearic acid undergoes thermal decomposition commencing at 232 °C. Differential thermal analysis (DTA) indicates a broad exothermic peak between 360 °C and 380 °C, followed by a minor peak at 515 °C [54]. The decomposition of stearic acid proceeds in two distinct steps: initially, it decomposes into smaller radicals such as C2H4, ∗C2H3, and ∗C2H5. The second step involves the formation of final reaction products including H2O, CO2, and CO [55].

Zinc stearate, an ester of stearic acid and zinc, begins to decompose at a lower temperature of approximately 200 °C. Beyond 550 °C, no significant mass loss is observed, indicating the completion of its decomposition process [52].

PVA exhibits different thermal decomposition behaviours in molten and solid states. In the molten state, decomposition primarily involves the elimination of water and chain scission, resulting in volatile products such as aldehydes and ketones. Conversely, in the solid state and in the absence of air, the thermal decomposition of PVA involves water elimination, which diminishes hydrogen bonding within the polymer matrix and consequently lowers its melting temperature [56].

Ethanol, when subjected to thermal decomposition in the presence of air, oxidizes to produce CO2, and H2O [57]. The flash point of ethanol is approximately 13 °C.

This overview highlights the different thermal characteristics and decomposition pathways of various PCAs, which are critical for understanding their stability and reactivity during MA processes. At the same time, the question of which properties of PCA have a predominant influence on the various characteristics of the milling product in different cases remains open. For example, recent work analyzed correlations between the properties of liquid PCAs and the results of the milling of oxides [58]. It has been shown that in two different materials (Bi2O3 and CuO) different parameters have a key effect on particle size, in particular proton affinity and surface tension of the PCA, respectively [58]. Whereas crystallite sizes in different cases were most influenced by dynamic viscosity and density. In the case when milling is accompanied by the formation of a new compound from the original powders (mechanical alloying), finding such correlations and establishing their mechanisms is a much more difficult task. In addition, it is necessary to take into account the peculiarity of the studied process of Fe-Ga MA. Even though in the initial state, Ga is a powder that was pre-mixed with Fe powder (see section 2), even with slight heating, which inevitably occurs during the high-intensity milling process, it turns into a liquid state. A very high surface tension of liquid Ga [59] leads to the fact that both iron and PCA are pushed out of it, and the droplets merge while moving in the milling jar. However, over time, high-intensity milling results in the gradual formation of more Fe/Ga interfaces due to the high-energy impact of milling balls. This in turn promotes the diffusion of Ga into Fe and, as a consequence, the formation of a solid solution. It can be assumed that at low MA intensity, the Fe-Ga process will not be effective even with increasing time. This is confirmed by our unpublished results obtained using a vibratory mill (Fig. S4).

Among the processes with the addition of solid PCA (in the initial state), a different behavior was found in the case of PVA. Small amounts of PVA are not very effective for the process control. The high surface tension energy of this compound (Table 6) is useless in this case since it is valid for the liquid state, the transition to which occurs only at temperatures above 200 °C, which is hardly achievable inside the milling jar during our experiment. On the contrary, a large amount of PVA leads to an excessive reduction in cold welding ability. This is because this amount is enough to evenly cover almost all iron particles in their initial state. This, in turn, leads to a noticeable slowdown in the kinetics of the process. Moreover, judging by the XRD analysis data, this occurs because the mechanism of rapid formation of the solid solution practically stops working (Fig. 6b), while the gradual dissolution of Ga in Fe remains at the same level as at lower PCA contents (Fig. 6a).

Small additions of other PCAs that are either initially liquid (ethanol) or capable of at least partially becoming liquid during milling (stearic acid, zinc stearate) perform significantly better in terms of the combination of powder recovery and solid solution formation kinetic. The problem with stearic acid and zinc stearate is a high degree of carbon contamination of the Fe-Ga powder (Table 3). In the case of 1 % of ethanol, the amount of carbon entering the solid solution lattice is two times lower. The different degrees of carbon contamination appear to be associated with different patterns of decomposition of PCA used. Although the initial decomposition temperature of the compounds used is higher than the maximum external temperature of the milling jar (42 °C), it can be assumed that partial decomposition may occur in places of local heating during high-intensity exposure to the powder. Residues resulting from the decomposition of PCA can lead to the formation of new compounds and contamination of the alloy. PCA-forming residues with lower molecular weight and lower boiling (and/or flash) point temperature generally result in lower contamination. The decomposition of residual hydrocarbons into CO2 and H2O (as in the case of ethanol) without a solid residue leads to the lowest contamination of the mechanically alloyed alloy.

Finally, it was shown that the addition of 3 % of not only polyvinyl alcohol but also other PCAs to Fe-Ga MA leads to undesirable effects such as a significant slowdown in the kinetics of solid solution formation without improving powder recovery (3 % ethanol) or overmilling leading to spontaneous combustion of the iron powder upon contact with air (3 % stearic acid). In addition, stearic acid can react with pure gallium, making it an undesirable PCA for use in the MA of the Fe-Ga system.

Conclusions

The work showed that a high-intensity (1000 rpm) milling process leads to the formation of α-Fe(Ga) solid solution in a fairly limited time (less than 12 h). It was found that the formation of the solid solution occurs through two mechanisms: gradual dissolution of Ga in the Fe lattice and local sudden formation of a solid solution phase with a high gallium content, which coexists with other phases upon further milling. MA of the Fe-Ga system from high-purity chemical elements without any additives is complicated by excessive cold welding, which leads to very low powder yield (less than 50 %) and disruption of the original Fe-Ga ratio. To solve the problem, various PCAs have been tested, including stearic acid, polyvinyl alcohol, zinc stearate, and ethanol. Based on a combination of factors (powder recovery, solid solution formation kinetics, carbon contamination), it was found that the best PCA in this case is 1 % of ethanol. It has been shown that any of the PCA used in an amount of 3 % leads to undesirable effects such as a strong deterioration in the kinetics of solid solution formation or overmilling compared to 1 %.
