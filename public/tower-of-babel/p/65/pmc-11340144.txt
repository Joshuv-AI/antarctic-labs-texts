Psoas attenuation is associated with early postoperative complications in geriatric patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis
Authors: Xiaofei Hou, Hailiang Hu, Chao Kong, Sitao Zhang, Wei Wang, Shibao Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMorphometric analysis of the psoas major muscle has shown utility in predicting postoperative morbidity in various surgical fields, but its usefulness in predicting complications in elderly patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery has not been studied. The study aimed to investigate if psoas major parameters are independent risk factors of early postoperative complication among elderly patients.MethodsPatients who underwent multilevel lumbar fusion for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) were included. The psoas major was measured at the lumbar 3/4 intervertebral disc level in three ways on computed tomography image: psoas muscle mass index, mean muscle attenuation, and morphologic change of the psoas major. Early complications were graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system and the Comprehensive complication index (CCI). A CCI ≥ 26.2 indicated severe complications. Logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors.ResultsThis retrospective study reviewed 108 patients (mean age 70.9 years, female to male ratio 1.8:1). Complications were observed in 72.2% of patients, with allogeneic blood transfusion being the most frequent (66.7%), followed by wound infection, acute heart failure (2.8% each). Severe complications occurred in 13.9% of patients. After multivariable regression analysis, those in the lowest psoas muscle attenuation tertile had higher odds of experiencing early postoperative complications (OR: 3.327, 95% CI 1.134–9.763, p = 0.029) and severe complications (OR: 6.964, 95% CI 1.928–25.160, p = 0.003).ConclusionThe psoas muscle attenuation can be used as a predictor of early postoperative complications in elderly patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery for DLSS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-024-07779-2.

Background

With the aging of population, degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) has become one of the conditions seen most frequently in orthopaedics and neurosurgical practice [1]. DLSS is the primary reason for spine surgery in those over the age of 65 years, and complex fusion procedures have risen significantly in recent years [2]. However, this trend is associated with higher rates of postoperative complications [3], yet the risk factors have not been clarified thoroughly [4]. Timely identifying risk factors for postoperative complications can assist both surgeons and patients in making informed decisions together. This information can be used to determine whether or not to proceed with surgery, or to guide the implementation of perioperative interventions designed to enhance postoperative outcomes, such as prehabilitation [5].

The psoas major muscle obtained through computed tomography (CT) is highly correlated with whole body muscle mass [6]. The morphological features of the psoas major muscle, specifically its muscle mass and density at the L3 vertebra level, have been utilized to anticipate the onset of postoperative complications in patients undergoing major general, vascular, and transplant surgeries [7–9]. However, it remains unclear whether morphometrics of psoas major can be used as a reliable predictor of postoperative complications in patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery.

The aim of this study is to investigate the correlation between the psoas major muscle mass (measured as the psoas muscle index [PMI]), radiation attenuation (measured as the mean Hounsfield Units [HU]), morphologic changes of the psoas muscle (measured as MPM), and perioperative morbidity in patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery for DLSS. Our hypothesis is that in older patients who undergo multilevel lumbar fusion surgery for LSS, these easily measured psoas major parameters represent independent risk factors for early postoperative complications.

Materials and methods

Participants

After obtaining approval from the Ethical Committee of our hospital, the records of patients (over the age of 65) who underwent multilevel lumbar instrumented fusion (≥ 3) for degenerative lumbar spinal stenosis (DLSS) from January 2018 to December 2021 in Xuanwu Hospital were screened for study eligibility. The diagnostic criteria for DLSS were the presence of intermittent claudication and imaging features of multilevel spinal stenosis on lumbar MRI and CT. Patients without available radiological findings within 2 years of surgery were excluded. We also excluded patients with previous lumbar spinal surgery history. Patients with cachexia due to infectious diseases or cancer, myopathies, and dyskinesia were also excluded.

Preoperative and intraoperative data for each patient were collected with use of the electronic medical records. Baseline characteristics were documented for each patient, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes, and history of cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular disease, Parkinson’s disease, and history of spinal surgery (cervical and/or thoracic spine).

Preoperative Charlson comorbidity index was calculated for each patient and used to assess baseline comorbidity burden and health status [10]. The 17 comorbidities used for assessment were presented in supplementary Table 1.

The type of surgery was analyzed using the surgical invasiveness index (SSI), which was developed to rate spine surgical procedures based on the type and complexity of intervention involved in the procedure. The index is the sum of six weighted surgical components: anterior decompression, anterior fusion, anterior instrumentation, posterior decompression, posterior fusion, and posterior instrumentation. The weights for each component represent the number of vertebral levels at which each is performed. This index ranges from 0 to 48 points, and a higher score indicates a greater surgical invasiveness [11].

The severity of the complications within the 30-day postoperative period was graded using the Clavien-Dindo classification system (CD) [12] and comprehensive complication index (CCI) [13], respectively. The CD classification is graded from 1 to 5 according to increasing severity and the type of intervention required to treat the complication (Supplementary Table 2). In this study, grades II, III, IV, and V were considered as the presence of postoperative complications [14]. The CCI was based on the CD classification. Complications of individual patients were first graded by the CD classification, and the weighted sum of different grades of complications was calculated, with a final index ranging from 0 (no complications) to 100 (death from complications). A calculation of CCI can be obtained free of charge at http://www.assessurgery.com [13]. According to relevant studies, a CCI of 26.2 was set as the cut-off point and patients with complications were divided into a major complication group (CCI ≥ 26.2) and a minor complication group (CCI < 26.2) accordingly [15].

Analytical morphometrics

The morphometry of psoas major was measured according to the previously reported three methods [6, 16, 17]. In details, an axial image from preoperative CT scan corresponding to the L3/4 intervertebral disc level was imported to the measuring software (Advanced Visualization Workspace 2.0, Neusoft, Shenyang, Liaoning, China). The cross-sectional areas (CSA) of total lean psoas area were quantified within predefined validated boundaries of -29 to + 150 Hounsfield units (HU) to exclude non-muscular tissues and areas of intramuscular fatty infiltration [18]. Total psoas area was measured by manually tracing and adding left and right psoas area. To reduce potential bias, our measurements included only the psoas major muscle, excluding the psoas minor muscle, which is present in approximately 50% of the population [19]. The psoas major muscle mass index (PMI) was calculated by normalizing the CSA for height (cm2/m2) (Fig. 1) [6]. Average lean muscle attenuation was calculated automatically outlined on the images (Fig. 1) [16]. Morphologic change of the psoas muscle (MPM) was defined as the mean of the respective ratios of the short to the long axis of the right and left psoas muscles (Fig. 1) [17]. For a reliability assessment of the freehand identification of the region of interest, a random sample of 40 patients (20 males and 20 females) was selected and measured by 2 observers independently.

Statistics

SPSS 22.0 statistical software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to assess the distribution of continuous data. Continuous data with a Gaussian distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Data with a non-Gaussian distribution were presented as median and interquartile range. Categorical data are described as frequency (percentage). To assess differences in demographic and surgical information and morphometric measurements associated with complications, chi-square tests were performed for categorical/binary variables. For continuous data that followed a Gaussian distribution with homogeneity of variances, we employed the standard T-test. For data with unequal variances, the Welch T-test was used. For non-Gaussian distributed data, a non-parametric test (bootstrap) was applied. Chi-square was performed to assess the association between the tertiles measurements and any perioperative complications or major complications. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analysis were used to examine the relationship between complications and variables studied. All variables associated with complications (with a p value of < 0.1 on univariate analysis) were examined consecutively by multivariable logistic regression analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were computed to assess the reliability among observers of the morphometric measurements of the PMI, as well as muscle attenuation and MPM. A p value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Descriptive data

A total of 108 patients were identified and their data entered in the database. A summary of descriptive information can be found in Table 1. The mean age of all patients was 70.9 years (SD = 4.5). Of the all patients, 63.9% (n = 69) were female, 36.1% (n = 39) were male, mean BMI was 26.6 (SD ± 4.0, female was 26.7 ± 3.9, male was 26.3 ± 4.3). Of the 108 patients, 64.8% (n = 70) had hypertension, 30.6% (n = 33) had diabetes. Of the all patients, 17.6% (n = 19) with cardiovascular disease, 11.1% (n = 12) with cerebrovascular disease, 3.7% (n = 4) with Parkinson’s disease. 13% of patients (n = 14) with history of spinal surgery (Cervical or thoracic spinal surgery). The mean preoperative serum albumin level was 39.4 g/L (SD = 3.0 g/L). The median Charlson Comorbidity Index was 1, with an interquartile range of 0 to 1.

The ICCs for the PMI and muscle attenuation measurements were high, ranging from 0.95 to 0.99. The ICCs for MPM were lower than those for PMI and muscle attenuation but still excellent, with a range of 0.85 to 0.89. Patients were categorized into tertiles based on their PMI, muscle attenuation, and MPM scores. The highest tertile had the largest muscle size, muscle attenuation or MPM score, while the lowest tertile had the smallest. To reduce statistical bias, the highest and middle tertiles were combined for analysis since there were few serious complications in the highest tertile. The median muscle attenuation was significantly lower in patients with postoperative complications (36.6 [32.8, 40.6]) compared to those without complications (39.8 [36.4, 42.0]), with a p-value of 0.01 and an effect size η² = 0.039. Patients in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation were significantly more likely to experience postoperative morbidity (p = 0.02, V = 0.219). Patients with severe complications had a significantly lower PMI (5.5 [4.1, 6.0] vs. 6.0 [5.5, 7.3], p = 0.007, η² = 0.065) and muscle attenuation (31.7 [30.1, 36.5] vs. 38.1 [35.5, 41.4], p < 0.001, η² = 0.126). Patients in the lowest tertile of PMI and muscle attenuation were significantly more likely to experience postoperative severe morbidity (PMI: p = 0.02, V = 0.253; muscle attenuation: p < 0.001, V = 0.341). However, no significant difference was found in terms of MPM in patients with any complications or severe complications (all p > 0.05).

Odds ratio and multivariate analysis of morphometrics

To determine the risk factors associated with postoperative complications, both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted and the results are presented in Table 4. Univariate analyses revealed that the surgical invasiveness index and being in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation were significantly associated with the occurrence of postoperative complications. The surgical invasiveness index was found to be an independent risk factor for postoperative comorbidity, with an OR of 1.367 (95% CI 1.034–1.808, p = 0.028). Furthermore, patients in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation had an OR of 3.327 for experiencing postoperative complications compared to the other two tertiles (95% CI 1.134–9.763, p = 0.029). The Hosmer and Lemeshow Test indicated a good fit for the multivariable logistic regression model (p = 0.295).

The results of univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses for severe postoperative complications was presented in Table 5. Univariate analyses revealed that being in the lowest tertile of PMI, and being in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation were significantly associated with the occurrence of severe complications. These two variables and albumin (p = 0.064) were subsequently included in the multivariable analysis. Notably, patients in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation had an odds ratio of 6.964 for experiencing perioperative complications when compared to the other two tertiles (95% CI 1.928–25.160, p = 0.003). The Hosmer and Lemeshow Test confirmed a good fit for this multivariable logistic regression model as well (p = 0.651).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the relationship between the morphology of the psoas major muscle and early postoperative complications in patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery for DLSS. Our findings revealed that the overall incidence of early postoperative complications was 72.2%, with 13.9% of cases classified as severe according to the CCI classification system. Multivariable regression analysis demonstrated that being in the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation was associated with an odds ratio of 3.327 for experiencing postoperative complications and an odds ratio of 6.964 for experiencing severe postoperative complications. Furthermore, the surgical invasiveness index was identified as an independent risk factor for perioperative comorbidity with an odds ratio of 1.367.

Skeletal muscle accounts for approximately 40% of the body’s total weight and contains 50–75% of all body proteins [18]. Not only does skeletal muscle help maintain the body’s motor function, it also secretes various active peptides and cytokines (such as interleukin-6, interleukin-15, myostatin, etc.) that participate in the metabolism and function of body organs [20]. Patients with decreased skeletal muscle volume and function are more likely to experience postoperative complications and reduced resistance to adverse events [21]. Psoas major muscle evaluated at the L3 vertebral level is linearly related to whole-body muscle mass [6, 22]. The postoperative complications of various diseases can be predicted by using PMI, muscle attenuation, and MPM assessed from CT images [7–9, 17]. In our study, multivariable logistic regression analysis showed that the lowest tertile of muscle attenuation was an independent risk factor for postoperative complications and severe complications in patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery. However, PMI was not found to be associated with any early postoperative complications. The attenuation values of muscle on CT, which vary greatly among individuals, provide quantitative data on muscle composition and are superior to muscle quantity in evaluating muscle function [23] (Fig. 2). Psoas density better predicted poor outcomes and complications compared to cross-sectional area in a cohort of trauma patients [24], a cohort having pancreatectomy [25], a cohort of patients having cardiac surgery [26]. Our findings, along with these studies, suggest that muscle quality may be more important than muscle quantity in predicting postoperative comorbidity.

Our study revealed that the surgical invasiveness index can predict the occurrence of overall postoperative complications, but it is not effective in predicting the occurrence of severe postoperative complications. The possible reason is that the most commonly recorded complication in our study was postoperative allogeneic blood transfusion resulting from anemia. Previous studies have demonstrated that the surgical invasiveness index is significantly associated with intraoperative blood loss [11].

In this study, we observed an overall perioperative complication rate of 72.2%, which is higher than that reported in previous literature [27]. This may be attributed to the fact that all patients underwent multilevel lumbar fusion surgery, which can cause significant trauma and increase the risk of postoperative complications [28]. Interestingly, the Charlson comorbidity index did not show a significant impact on the occurrence of postoperative complications. This may be because that all cases in our study were elective operations, and the patients underwent thorough systemic evaluation and received standard perioperative management, allowing their bodies to be better prepared for surgery [29].

The current study underscores the importance of assessing psoas major muscle quality measurements to select high risk patients that might benefit from preoperative prehabilitation to prevent postoperative complications. Therefore, a well-designed prehabilitation program containing physical exercise and adequate protein intake to improve psoas major muscle quality before the selective lumbar fusion surgery may have important clinical implications [30].

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, with a retrospective cohort design, this study’s analysis is inherently limited by the availability and quality of patient records in the database. Secondly, this study reports on a single, academic institution and may not be reflective of other centers, especially about perioperative patient evaluation and management. Future research should include larger prospective multi-center investigations to further validate the efficacy and feasibility of using psoas muscle attenuation to predict early postoperative complications following multilevel lumbar fusion surgery.

Conclusion

Identifying elderly patients with high risk for postoperative morbidity is of great clinical significance. Our study has found that psoas major muscle attenuation collected on preoperative lumbar CT scan is an independent risk factor for early postoperative complication in elderly patients undergoing multilevel lumbar fusion surgery. We thus propose routine assessment of psoas muscle attenuation as a sensitive predictive tool for risk stratification and resource utilization planning in these patients.
