Impact on daptomycin resistance using combination of daptomycin plus ceftaroline in daptomycin-susceptible and -resistant Staphylococcus aureus
Authors: Nagendra N Mishra, Cody A Black, Wessam Abdelhady, Sabrina Farah, Naidelyn A Rodas, Josue Brizuela, Clarissa Bustamante Lopez, Jorge N Artaza, George Sakoulas, Warren E Rose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPersistent Staphylococcus aureus infections treated with prolonged daptomycin (DAP) can select for DAP resistance (DAP-R), reinforcing the need for combination strategies that both improve killing and constrain resistance evolution. Prior work suggests the DAP+ceftaroline (CPT) combination can deliver synergistic killing, prevent emergence of DAP-R, and resensitize DAP-R subpopulations toward a DAP-susceptible (DAP-S) phenotype. Here, using a clinically derived, MSSA isogenic DAP-S (616)/DAP-R (703) strain pair, we evaluated DAP+CPT activity across in vitro assays, ex vivo models, and an in vivo experimental infective endocarditis (IE) model, and integrated ultra-deep targeted sequencing to link phenotypic responses to genomic adaptation. DAP+CPT produced enhanced killing of both strains in vitro and ex vivo, improved target-tissue clearance of the DAP-R strain in vivo, prevented emergence of DAP-R in the DAP-S parental strain in vitro and ex vivo, and resensitized the DAP-R strain toward a DAP-S phenotype ex vivo. Genomically, ultra-deep sequencing of resistance loci (thousands-fold coverage) identified fixed background divergence versus the N315 reference and revealed regimen-dependent selection in membrane-stress pathways, including a high-frequency mixed mprF subpopulation consistent with DAP-driven heterogeneity under monotherapy, contrasted by distinct locus-level changes under combination exposure. In addition, coverage profiling detected a large, combination-associated mobile-element/prophage gene-content event in the 703 background affecting an immune-evasion/β-hemolysin–converting region, highlighting that antibiotic pressure can couple resistance dynamics with pathogenesis-relevant genome remodeling. Together, these data show that DAP+CPT provides potent activity beyond synergistic killing—improving clearance while constraining or reshaping resistance evolution—and they define genomic signatures that help explain divergent evolutionary trajectories under DAP alone versus DAP+CPT.

Introduction

Staphylococcus aureus endovascular infections are prevalent in both hospital and community-acquired settings, causing ∼20 000 deaths in the US annually (Antimicrobial Resistance Collaborators 2022). Antibiotic resistance and treatment failure to standard of care (SOC) agents (antistaphylococcal β-lactams, vancomycin, and DAP) have compromised the efficacy of current therapeutic strategies translating to worse clinical outcomes (Adamu et al. 2024). Despite progress in many fields of medicine, mortality in S. aureus bacteremia in the most vulnerable populations remains stubbornly high (Sinclair et al. 2022).

Use of DAP monotherapy for methicillin-susceptible and -resistant S. aureus (MSSA, MRSA) bacteremia, endocarditis, and orthopedic infections may lead to the emergence of DAP-resistant (DAP-R), resulting in treatment failures (Lamp et al. 2007, Sakoulas et al. 2008, Zainah et al. 2013, Keino et al. 2015, Barreau et al. 2016). The common genetic mutations associated with DAP-R in S. aureus are single point mutations in multiple and specific regions of the multi-peptide resistance factor (mprF) open reading frame (ORF) (Friedman et al. 2006, Mishra et al. 2009). MprF principally facilitates the relative positive surface charge via lysinylation of cell membrane (CM) phosphatidylglycerol (PG), forming lysyl-PG (L-PG) in S. aureus (Friedman et al. 2006, Mishra et al. 2009). In addition, DAP-R has also been associated with polymorphisms in other genes involved in PG content (cls, dlt) and the histidine kinase of the system (YycG) (Koprivnjak et al. 2011).

This has prompted clinicians to utilize DAP-combination regimens with β-lactams like ceftaroline (CPT) to improve treatment outcomes based on small studies and case series. DAP + CPT has been used effectively as “salvage” therapy for persistent bacteremia (McCreary et al. 2020); importantly, this was found to significantly reduce in-hospital mortality vs SOC monotherapy in a recent, small randomized prospective open-label trial (Geriak et al. 2019). Thus, clinical evidence is emerging that DAP combination regimens may represent a future paradigm shift in S. aureus bacteremia therapy. However, the optimal DAP combination regimen remains unclear and may be organism-specific.

DAP + β-lactams demonstrate potent “synergistic” killing in vitro versus S. aureus, particularly with β-lactams that inhibit PBP (penicillin-binding protein)-1 (Berti et al. 2016). Similar to other β-lactam antibiotics, CPT inhibits cell wall synthesis by binding to PBPs, specifically PBP2a in MRSA, that is linked with methicillin resistance (Long et al. 2014). This binding perturbs the cross-linking of peptidoglycan, a crucial step in cell wall formation. DAP+CPT combination is a proven potential strategy against DAP treatment failures, including DAP-R in S. aureus.

The DAP-CPT combination was investigated in a retrospective cohort study in adults with persistent S. aureus bacteremia (Johnson et al. 2021). Interestingly, DAP+CPT exhibited significantly lower rates of clinical failure vs. SOC (Johnson et al. 2021). Additional clinical success of DAP + CPT vs SOC has been reported using DAP+CPT as a salvage therapy in treating refractory MRSA bacteremia (Sakoulas et al. 2014, Geriak et al. 2019). Reversion of DAP-R to DAP-S has been described clinically with DAP+CPT (Rose et al. 2012); however, studies investigating the effect of CPT on DAP-R are limited overall. Therefore, the aims of the current study were to elucidate (i) the multimodal impact and potential benefits of DAP+CPT combination therapy, (ii) deep genomic changes associated with prolonged treatment, and (iii) assessment of these impacts in MSSA.

Note: We have employed the term, “DAP-R,” instead of “DAP-nonsusceptibility” for easier presentation.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains used in this study

We focused our studies on a well-characterized clinical bloodstream isogenic strain pair of S. aureus (Yang et al. 2013). The DAP-S 616 was initially isolated from a patient before DAP therapy, while the DAP-R 703 strain was subsequently isolated from the same patient post-DAP therapy (Jones et al. 2008). The isogenicity of this pair was confirmed by pulsed-field gel electrophoresis and clonal complex typing, as described (Jones et al. 2008).

Minimum inhibitory concentration

DAP and CPT analytical powder were obtained from Merck & Co., Inc. (Whitehouse Station, NJ) and AbbVie (North Chicago, IL), respectively. DAP minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was evaluated by CLSI-recommended broth microdilution (CLSI 2025), adjusted with cation-adjusted (50 mg calcium/L and 12.5 mg magnesium/L) Mueller–Hinton broth (ca-MHB; Difco, Detroit, MI). DAP MICs were also determined by standard Etest (bioMérieux) on Mueller-Hinton agar (MHA) plates (Difco). CPT MIC testing was performed by standard broth microdilution assay (CLSI 2025). The breakpoint distinguishing DAP-S and DAP-R S. aureus is considered DAP MIC ≥ 2 mg/L (Jones et al. 2008). Initial bacterial inoculum size of 1 × 105 CFU/mL (standardized from a McFarland unit of 0.5) were used, and bacteria were incubated at 35°C. The lowest concentrations of antibiotics that prevented visible growth represented the MIC.

In vitro time kill cuve

For bactericidal and synergistic assessments, we performed time-kill assays in ca-MHB to detect in vitro bactericidal synergy. We utilized MIC concentrations of each antibiotic (Yang et al. 2010a, Zapata et al. 2018), and employed a standard initial inoculum of ∼ 1 × 105 CFU/mL. If this resulted in bactericidal activity with either agent alone (≥3 log10 killing), then sub-MIC concentrations were used. The extent of killing was determined by sampling at pre-assigned time-points (i.e. 0–8 h) and counting surviving CFUs on MHA at following incubation at 35°C for 16–24 h (Yang et al. 2010a, Zapata et al. 2018). At least three biological replicates were performed on separate days. Data from each time-point assessment was expressed as mean log10 CFU/mL (+/− SD) of surviving counts. This method were determine whether a specific regimen exerts: (i) a bactericidal effect (defined as ≥3 log10 CFU/mL reduction in colony counts vs the initial inoculum); (ii) a “synergistic effect” (defined as ≥ 2 log10 CFU/mL reduction in counts at the 0–8 h sampling, when comparing the antibiotic-combination-of-interest vs. the most-active single agent; or (iii) both effects. “Indifference” or “antagonistic” interactions were defined as previously cited (Zapata et al. 2018).

Prevention of emergence of DAP-R

For prevention of emergence of DAP-R, S. aureus 616 (DAP-R) was serially passaged in vitro in DAP alone or the combination of DAP+CPT. DAP concentrations initially range from 0.25 to 0.5× MIC of DAP-S 616, then increased serially by two-fold on passage days 2–4 as described before (Reed et al. 2019, Mishra et al. 2022). The concentrations of CPT were held constant during passage studies at 0.125 µg/mL (Berti et al. 2015, Reed et al. 2019). Samples after each day’s passage were obtained and stored at −80°C for further in vitro assays. The proportion of S. aureus strains evolving a DAP-R phenotype were determined by parallel plating onto MHA, with or without the respective antibiotic, encompassing the concentration range of DAP MICs commonly found in DAP-R MRSA strains (2–4 µg/mL) (Bayer et al. 2015). Colonies from the screening plates were evaluated for DAP susceptibility by broth microdilution and Etest®; any strains that display DAP MICs of ≥2 µg/mL were considered DAP-R (2025). Stability of DAP-R was confirmed by growing and passaging strains for 5 days in antibiotic-free ca-MHB (Mishra et al. 2017, Reed et al. 2019). The DAP MIC of these “antibiotic-free post-passage” strains were then determined as before (Yang et al. 2010).

Resensitization of DAP-R to DAP-S phenotype

To determine the ability of CPT to resensitize DAP-R to DAP, we employed DAP-R 703 for in vitro serial passaging. DAP concentrations were initiated at 0.5×MIC of DAP-R 703, then increased serially by two-fold on days 2–4 of passage. DAP concentrations were not increased beyond 4 µg/mL to maintain optimum selective pressure of antibiotics (Reed et al. 2019) and ensure DAP MICs do not exceed those most commonly found in clinical DAP-R S. aureus (2–4 µg/mL) (Bayer et al. 2015). CPT was maintained as constant at 0.125 µg/mL throughout the daily passage. As above, the MICs of the serially passaged DAP-R 703 were followed every two days during passage by standard Etest (CLSI 2025). “Resensitization” to the DAP-S phenotype were defined as post-passage reduction in DAP MICs to ≤ 1 µg/mL. Whole genome sequencing with comprehensive bioinformatics analysis described below was performed on resulting isolates following in vitro passage.

Ex vivo model

The human component-derived SEVs (consisting primarily of human fibrinogen/fibronectin/fibrin/platelets) were prepared as described elsewhere (Kang and Rybak 1995, Steed et al. 2012, Werth et al. 2014, Rose et al. 2020). The model mirrors experimental and human IE in terms of vegetation bacterial burden and antibiotic PK-PD. Moreover, the model recapitulates two distinct contexts of human IE: (i) the “fluid phase,” simulating the bloodstream milieu during both hematogenous seeding from the vegetation); and (ii) the “tissue phase,” mimicking the microenvironment of infected vegetations.

We assessed the efficacy of DAP, alone and in combination with CPT in synergy, preventing evolution of DAP-R and/or resensitizing DAP-R strains to DAP-S over a 96-h treatment period. This exposure duration is adequate for antibiotic resistance to emerge during clinical treatment (Kang and Rybak 1995, Steed et al. 2012, Werth et al. 2014, Rose et al. 2020). The chamber fluid phase PK-PD mimics the respective total drug exposures in human serum over variable dosing ranges. Monotherapy: Computer-generated drug 2-minute administration at simulated dose-regimens of (i) DAP—6 mg/kg, once daily to achieve an AUC0-24 630 mg*h/L (PK targets of Cmax 75.0 mg/L, Cmin 9.0 mg/L, half-life 7.9 h) (Butterfield-Cowper et al. 2018, FDA 2025); and (ii) CPT—600 mg every 8 h (PK targets of Cmax 21.3 mg/L, Cmin2.2 mg/, half-life 2.3 h, AUC0-24 140 mg*h/L) (Matzneller et al. 2016, EMA 2025). This was compared to combination therapy with these regimens using principles for simulating the PK of drugs with different half-lives (Blaser 1985). These respective doses represent those commonly employed for treatment of S. aureus bacteremia/IE (Fowler et al. 2006, Geriak et al. 2019, McCreary et al. 2020).

Antimicrobial PK/PD in the ex vivo SEV model

Pre-therapy, and at the same time-points as seen in Fig. 2, two SEVs each were removed from the chambers, weighed, homogenized, diluted in cold saline, plated onto MHA, then incubated at 35°C for 48 h (to maximize S. aureus survival). Surviving colonies were quantified, and those in each treatment therapy group calculated as mean log10 cfu/g (± SD). Models were performed in duplicate for each antibiotic regimen (monotherapy vs. combination therapy), providing four SEVs per time-point per drug exposure for PK/PD assessment. Antimicrobial synergy, resistant development (DAP and CPT), and DAP-R resensitization were assessed using methods described above.

For PK/PD analysis, central compartment (“fluid”) samples were obtained for verification of target antibiotic levels when given in monotherapy and combination regimens. DAP and CPT concentrations were determined by HPLC/MS (via the Analytical Instrumentation Center at UW-Madison Pharmacy (Gregoire et al. 2016). We determined the following PK-PD metrics: half-life, Cmax and Cmin concentrations (relative to MIC), clearance, drug AUC and time-above-MICs (T>MICs), (GraphPad Prism Software) to validate human-like exposure metrics and define PK/PD target optimization for each regimen.

In vivo IE model

The in vivo experiments were critical to fully confirm translatability of in vitro and ex vivo studies above in terms of preventing and resensitizing DAP-R in a relevant endovascular scenario. For this purpose, we used the well-characterized rabbit IE model. It recapitulates human IE pathogenesis, microbiology, immunology, histopathology, and target organ seeding. The model reflects a composite of acute (bacteremia) and subacute infection (organ abscesses), with high target organ S. aureus densities (Chambers et al. 2009). Thus, it is ideal for quantifying the in vivo effects of distinct antibiotics alone or in combination, on prevention of DAP-R and resensitization of DAP-R MSSA strains. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Animal Research Committee (IACUC) of The Lundquist Institute.

In brief, the left-sided aortic valve IE model involves placement of a polyethylene catheter retrograde via the right carotid artery in anesthetized animals across the aortic valve, into the left ventricular apex, where it remains indwelling. The presence of the indwelling catheter ensures that robust biofilms will occur at this site of vegetation formation. Catheter trauma of valvular and mural endocardium yields platelet–fibrin-rich “sterile” vegetations. Subsequent intravenous (IV) inoculation of the prioritized MRSA strains of interest was carried out at an ID95 inoculum established in prior IE studies [∼5 × 105 CFU/mL] (Kennedy and Chambers 1989, Chambers et al. 2009, Garcia-de-la-Maria et al. 2013). This achieved reliable colonization of vegetations, microbial persistence and proliferation within vegetations, vegetation growth, hematogenous seeding to other target organs (kidneys; spleen) and re-seeding of endocardial lesions.

Antibiotic regimens in the IE model

For DAP dose-ranging protocols, we have used doses of these agents in rabbits which are equivalent to human-simulating doses that prevent emergence of DAP-R and are able to resensitize such DAP-R strains to DAP. These dose estimations were derived from results in both the in vitro and ex vivo models above. For DAP, we have employed regimens of 10 mg/kg IV once daily in rabbits; this dose is the human equivalents of DAP at 6 mg/kg IV, when given once daily (Chambers et al. 2009). For CPT, 10 mg/kg bid IV was employed; these regimens generate human-like PK-PD (Jacqueline et al. 2011). All DAP and CPT regimens were given for 3 days, starting at 24 h post-infection to maximize the potential for both development of antibiotic resistance, as well as for resensitizing the DAP-R strains to a DAP-S phenotype in vivo (Reed et al. 2019).

Sacrifices

Animals were sacrificed at least 12 h after the last antibiotic doses to minimize antibiotic carryover (based on the respective half-lives of these agents in rabbits). If antibiotic carryover was suspected, log2 dilutions were performed to dilute the antibiotic(s) allowing for bacterial growth. Cardiac vegetations, kidneys, and spleens were excised, weighed, and homogenized in entirety. These were quantitatively cultured and the whole sample was parallel-plated onto antibiotic-free MHA or MHA supplemented with DAP, using the same subculture concentrations of these agents as above for the in vitro and ex vivo studies above. The limit of detection in target organ cultures in this model, based on average target tissue weights, is ≤2 log10 CFU/g. Individual colonies from organ cultures growing on DAP-containing plates were subjected to formal MIC determinations by both Etest and broth microdilution, as per CLSI methods (CLSI 2025); this confirmed the evolution of resistant subpopulations. Colonies were subsequently tested for the stability of the DAP-R phenotypic by serial in vitro passages in DAP-free media. The primary outcome metrics of interest was resensitization of DAP-R MSSA to DAP from the same organs.

Statistical analysis

Analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was used to determine bacterial density differences among control, single, and combination regimens in vitro/ex vivo. Student’s t test was used for continuous data (e.g. bacterial densities in SEVs and within vivo target organs, presented as mean log10 CFU per gram of tissue ± SD). P values of ≤0.05 were considered significant (GraphPad Prism software).

Genomic analysis of resistance loci and regimen-associated variation

Reference-based mapping

Reads were mapped to the S. aureus N315 reference genome (accession: NC_002745.2) and processed into coordinate-sorted, indexed BAM files. Downstream variant and coverage analyses were performed on the main reference contig, which contains the resistance loci examined here.

Ultra-deep allele counting at mprF codon 826

To quantify within-sample heterogeneity at a focal resistance site, ultra-deep pileup-based allele counting was performed at mprF codon 826. The mprF coding sequence spans NC_002745.2:1 363 612–1 366 134 on the positive strand, and the focal position NC_002745.2:1 366 087 corresponds to a C>T substitution that changes CTT>TTT (predicted amino acid change p.L826F). Per-base allele counts were computed from pileups under minimum mapping quality and base quality thresholds of 20. Reads flagged as unmapped, secondary, QC-failed, duplicate, or supplementary were excluded using a combined flag mask of 3844. Pileup iteration parameters were configured to permit ultra-deep depth without downsampling so that allele frequencies could be estimated at several thousand-fold coverage. For each sample, the major non-reference allele and its frequency were reported.

Sensitivity analysis excluding chimeric/supplementary alignments

Because ultra-deep sequencing can amplify alignment artifacts, allele counting at the focal mprF site was repeated after excluding reads carrying supplementary alignment tags (e.g. SA: Z) and/or supplementary alignments. “All reads” and “noSA” allele counts and allele frequencies were recorded per sample, enabling assessment of whether mixture signals persisted after removal of potentially chimeric reads.

Window-based SNP discovery and QC metrics around mprF

To characterize additional variation surrounding the focal site and to contextualize subclonal patterns, a ±10 kb window around NC_002745.2:1 366 087 (NC_002745.2:1 356 087–1 376 087) was scanned for SNP candidates using pileup-based calling with bcftools mpileup/call. For each candidate site, allele frequency (AF), depth (DP), and strand-resolved allele counts were extracted along with multiple site-level bias and call-quality metrics, including read position bias (RPBZ), mapping quality bias (MQSBZ), base quality bias (BQBZ), and variant distance bias (VDB). Two reporting tiers were generated: (i) all biallelic SNP candidates and (ii) a stringent “clean” subset applying AF/depth requirements and bias-metric cutoffs calibrated for ultra-deep targeted data. A separate “fixed-alt clean” mode was used to retain near-fixed alternate alleles even when some bias statistics were undefined (e.g. when reference-supporting reads were absent).

Functional annotation and predicted amino acid changes

For variants retained in the high-confidence set, predicted amino acid changes were derived by intersecting variant coordinates with coding sequence features in N315.gff3 and applying the alternate allele to the corresponding codon using the N315 reference FASTA. Amino acid changes were reported in protein-level notation, including synonymous substitutions (p.X###=), missense substitutions (p.X###Y), and stop-gain variants (p.X###*). This procedure was applied to both the main-text high-confidence table and the broader pileup-derived candidate call set used for supplemental reporting.

Prophage/mobile element dropout analysis in the 2.0 Mb region

To evaluate regimen-specific gene-content differences and to distinguish true biological presence/absence events from callability artifacts, coverage depth was summarized across a candidate region on NC_002745.2 spanning 1 990 000–2 050 000. For each sample, mean depth and the fraction of positions with zero coverage (fraction_zero_depth) were computed using per-base depth over all positions (samtools depth -aa). Contiguous zero-coverage runs were identified, and DAPCPT-specific runs were defined as those present in strain 703_at day 10 of DAP+CPT passage (703 Day10 DAP+CPT) after subtracting zero-coverage positions already present in the 703 Day 0 baseline (untreated control) or 703_Day 10_DAP passage alone. Overlaps between DAP+CPT-specific zero-depth intervals and annotated genes were identified using genome feature coordinates from N315.gff3 to determine whether dropout corresponded to prophage/mobile element neighborhoods.

Assembly-based similarity and content QC (Mash distance)

To distinguish large gene-content events from technical noise, contamination, or sample swaps, assembly-level metrics and genome similarity were assessed for the 703 strain timepoints. Total assembly size and contig fragmentation were summarized, and pairwise genome distances were computed using Mash sketches built from assemblies/contigs. This enabled an empirical definition of a within-experiment “effectively identical” distance floor (replicate/timepoint comparisons within the same isolate) and identification of outliers consistent with discrete presence/absence events while still remaining closely related to the experimental background.

Results

In vitro killing by time-kill curve analysis

In vitro growth kinetics were similar between the strains, thus suggesting equivalent overall fitness (data not shown). The DAP-S/DAP-R strain pair was exposed to sub-lethal concentration of CPT, DAP alone or in relevant combinations; early timed-killing was assessed over 8 h. CPT substantially enhanced early killing of DAP in the DAP-R strain vs either drug alone (Fig. 1). In DAP-S 616, DAP 0.5×MIC resulted in moderate, early bacterial killing (< 2 log10 CFU/mL); the addition of CPT to DAP enhanced bacterial killing resulting in synergistic, bactericidal activity at 24 h (Fig. 1). In DAP-R 703, DAP or CPT alone (0.5×MIC) did not reduce bacterial burden and resulted in bacterial stasis or growth. The combination of DAP+CPT at these concentrations produced synergistic bactericidal activity noted in Fig. 1.

Sublethal concentrations of DAP (0.5xMIC) in combination with CPT (1×MIC) significantly enhance the killing of DAP-S/DAP-R strains in vitro versus untreated and either DAP or CPT alone. **P value <0.05 for mean killing versus control (growth) and single antibiotic treatment (ANOVA).

Resistance prevention and resensitization during in vitro serial passaging

To determine whether DAP+CPT could mitigate in vitro emergence of DAP-R, DAP-S 616 and DAP-R 703 were passaged for 14 days in DAP alone, or in combination, with CPT; MICs were serially assessed. The DAP + CPT combination was able to prevent the DAP-S evolving to DAP-R. DAP-S 616 exposed to DAP + CPT remained susceptible to DAP (1 µg/mL at Day 14: Table 2); in contrast, DAP-S 616 passaged in sublethal DAP concentrations alone became highly and stably DAP-R (4 µg/mL at Day 14; Table 2). DAP-R 703 strain passaged in DAP+CPT further prevented the increase in DAP MIC; however, failed to resensitize DAP-R 703 to a DAP-S phenotype. Further, 703 strain passaged in DAP alone exhibited increased DAP MICs (4 µg/mL at Day 14; Table 3).

Because the observed regimen- and lineage-specific phenotypic divergence after 10 days of sub-MIC exposure implies selection on discrete genetic mechanisms, we next linked phenotype to genotype by profiling canonical resistance loci at ultra-deep depth and by screening for larger genomic events that can alter both callability and biology (e.g. mobile elements).

Across the six paired baseline and day-10 genomes, the genomic signal separated cleanly into (i) fixed background differences versus the N315 reference that were shared across lineages/timepoints, and (ii) treatment-associated heterogeneity that emerged in a regimen- and strain-dependent manner (Table 4, Supplemental Data S1). The most prominent example of within-sample genetic heterogeneity occurred under DAP monotherapy, where isolate 703 developed a high-frequency mixed population at mprF codon 826 (L826F), consistent with strong selection on membrane-stress adaptation pathways. In contrast, other treatment arms showed either lower-frequency subclones (e.g. additional mprF mixtures) or arm-specific fixed changes in loci that plausibly interact with antibiotic stress physiology (e.g. gdpP, pbp2), supporting distinct evolutionary routes under DAP alone versus DAP+CPT. Of note, all samples across the entire 2,407 bp window, lacked reads which mapped to mecA.

Importantly, selection was not limited to SNP-level changes. Coverage profiling revealed a large, regimen-specific gene-content event in the 703 DAP+CPT day-10 genome, consistent with loss/replacement/excision of a mobile element in the 2.0 Mb region that is enriched for prophage-associated functions. This structural signal explains clusters of missing or uncallable sites in that interval and indicates that, under combination exposure in the 703 background, adaptation may include mobile-element dynamics in addition to canonical resistance-locus variation.

High-confidence ultra-deep calls capture both background divergence from N315 and regimen-associated within-sample heterogeneity

Across ultra-deep targeted resistance loci (typically several thousand-fold coverage per locus), multiple near-fixed alternate alleles were detected in both isolate backgrounds relative to the N315 reference (Table 4). These fixed-alternate differences were consistent across isolates and timepoints and were therefore interpreted as background divergence from N315 rather than treatment-emergent evolution. In contrast, treatment-associated heterogeneity was most prominent in mprF under DAP monotherapy, where mixed allele frequencies suggested emergence of dominant subclones in a regimen- and isolate-dependent manner. Combination DAP+CPT showed a distinct pattern characterized by additional fixed changes in specific loci and, in one sample, evidence for a large regional coverage dropout consistent with a prophage/mobile element event.

Daptomycin selection in isolate 703 is associated with a high-frequency mprF p.L826F mixture

Ultra-deep pileup quantification at NC_002745.2:1 366 087 demonstrated a strong within-sample mixture in DAPR_703_DAY10_DAP, with the alternate allele comprising ∼74% of reads at high depth. Functionally, this C>T change at codon 826 converts CTT>TTT and corresponds to the predicted amino acid change p.L826F. In contrast, baseline DAP-R_703 showed only low-level non-reference observations, and 703 Day10 DAP+CPT showed no meaningful non-reference support at this position, indicating regimen-specific emergence and/or maintenance of the L826F-associated allele under DAP monotherapy in the 703 genetic background.

The p.L826F mixture is robust to exclusion of supplementary/chimeric alignments

703 Day10 DAP+CPT contained substantial evidence of supplementary/chimeric alignments near the focal locus, raising the possibility that apparent mixtures could be driven by mapping artifacts in ultra-deep data. However, allele-frequency estimates at NC_002745.2:1 366 087 were unchanged when allele counts were recomputed after excluding reads with supplementary alignment tags and/or supplementary alignments (“noSA” analysis). This supports the interpretation that the observed p.L826F mixture reflects genuine within-sample genetic heterogeneity rather than an alignment-driven artifact.

Additional mprF window variation is isolate- and regimen-specific and includes strain-fixed polymorphism and a secondary emerging subpopulation

Window-based scanning around mprF identified additional SNPs consistent with both background differences and secondary subpopulations. In the 703 background, a near-fixed change at NC_002745.2:1 364 495 (C>T) was present at baseline and in the day-10 DAP+CPT sample, corresponding to the predicted amino acid change p.S295 L and supporting its interpretation as a strain-703-specific fixed polymorphism relative to N315. In the 616 background, a lower-frequency mprF subpopulation emerged after DAP monotherapy at NC_002745.2:1 364 621 (C>T), corresponding to p.S337 L and consistent with a treatment-associated subclone present at modest frequency. Together with the dominant p.L826F mixture in 703 under DAP, these patterns indicate distinct mprF evolutionary trajectories determined by isolate background and regimen.

Combination DAP+CPT is associated with distinct fixed changes in resistance-linked loci in each isolate background

Beyond mprF, DAP+CPT showed additional fixed changes that were not shared across backgrounds. In isolate 703 at day 10 under DAP+CPT, two gdpP variants were near-fixed at high depth, including a stop-gain (p.K563*) and a nearby missense change (p.H566N), consistent with a combination-associated fixed genotype in this sample. In isolate 616 at day 10 under DAP+CPT, a near-fixed pbp2 variant (p.V390L) was detected, consistent with a β-lactam-linked target locus change under combination exposure. A low-frequency but balanced gdpP mixture (p.F217L) was also observed in 616 under DAP+CPT, indicating minor subclonal diversity in addition to the fixed pbp2 change.

DAP+CPT in isolate 703 is associated with a large prophage/mobile element dropout in the 2.0 Mb region

Coverage profiling revealed a pronounced dropout specific to 703 Day10 DAP+CPT across NC_002745.2:1 990 000–2 050 000 (Fig. 2). In this window, 703 Day10 DAP+CPT exhibited a markedly elevated fraction of zero-coverage positions compared with other samples, which clustered near a lower baseline consistent with known low-mappability segments. Mean depth in the window was also substantially reduced in 703 Day10 DAP+CPT relative to its paired 703 baseline and 703_Day10_DAP samples. In a targeted subset of the region (28,452 bp), coverage collapsed completely in 703 Day10 DAP+CPT (mean depth ≈ 0; fraction_zero_depth = 1.0) while remaining extremely high in 703 Day10 DAP, demonstrating that the earlier “missing sites” signal in this region was driven primarily by absence of coverage (i.e. ungentoypable sequence) rather than variant-calling or VCF emission behavior.

Overlap analysis with N315 gene annotations showed that the dropout region contained hallmark prophage and immune evasion cluster genes, including the β-hemolysin converting prophage neighborhood (e.g. hlb region), immune evasion factors (e.g. scn and sak), enterotoxin-associated genes (e.g. sep), holin, and numerous phage structural genes (tail/capsid/portal/terminase). This gene-content signature strongly supports a prophage/IEC-associated event rather than a SNP-only adaptive process.

Assembly metrics and Mash distances support a genuine gene-content event rather than sample mix-up or contamination

Assembly-based QC supported the coverage-based inference of a discrete gene-content event in 703 Day10 DAP+CPT. The 703 Day10 DAP+CPT assembly size was reduced compared with 703 baseline and 703 Day10 DAP, consistent with loss of tens of kilobases of sequence. Mash distances further separated 703 Day10 DAP+CPT from the otherwise tight within-isolate cluster: replicate/timepoint comparisons within isolates were near the empirical floor (∼10^-6), whereas distances involving 703 Day10 DAP+CPT were orders of magnitude higher (on the order of 10^-4), consistent with a large presence/absence event. Importantly, distances to N315 remained substantially larger than within-experiment distances, indicating that 703 Day10 DAP+CPT remained closely related to the 703 background overall while differing in a discrete genomic region, arguing against contamination or sample swap and supporting a true mobile element/prophage-associated genomic change under DAP+CPT in isolate 703.

Resistance prevention and resensitization in ex vivo-derived subpopulations

We further analyzed SEV-derived isolates from the above model. The DAP-S strain treated by either DAP + CPT remained susceptible to DAP (DAP MIC 0.5 µg/mL), thus preventing the emergence of DAP-R. In contrast, the DAP-S strain treated with DAP alone became DAP-R at 72–96 h (MIC 4 µg/mL). Further, the DAP-R strain treated by DAP + CPT was resensitized to DAP-S (MIC 1 µg/mL) at 72–96 h, while the DAP-R strain treated with DAP alone remained DAP-R (MIC 2–4 µg/mL).

In vivo IE model

Discussion

The current study provides a novel platform for evaluating antibiotic combinations and defining the synergy, prevention, and resensitization that have been noted clinically (Rose et al. 2012, Sakoulas et al. 2014, Sakoulas et al. 2014, Geriak et al. 2019). It evaluated how DAP and CPT could provide enhanced bactericidal therapy against MSSA in vitro, ex vivo, and vivo while possibly mitigating the emergence of DAP resistance. Prior data on DAP+CPT combination has focused on MRSA (Rose et al. 2012, Sakoulas et al. 2014, Sakoulas et al. 2014, Geriak et al. 2019). This study is the first to evaluate this capacity in MSSA, which has noted to develop resistance to DAP (Fowler et al. 2006). DAP-R development remains uncommon in clinical practice; however, this occurrence has devastating consequences for patients as limited therapeutic options exist.

Data from the current study using a prototypical DAP-S/DAP-R isogenic strain pair, supports the notion that strategic combinations of DAP+CPT can exert increased bactericidal impacts in vitro. Moreover, selected combinations may be quite effective in preventing the emergence of DAP-R in S. aureus. These results mirror findings with DAP plus fosfomycin (FOF) combination, an antibiotic affecting an earlier step in cell wall synthesis (Reed et al. 2019, Garcia-de-la-Maria et al. 2020, Pujol et al. 2021, Mishra et al. 2022). We reported this phenomenon may be linked to genetic changes S. aureus induced by FOF (Rose et al. 2025), which appear to be distinct changes to those observed in this current study with DAP+CPT. Further investigation into these differences may provide precision approaches with these combinations in future patients. Overall, these data provided a solid foundation upon which to assess enhanced bactericidal efficacy and resistance prevention with DAP+CPT and denote peptidoglycan synthesis as a key target for DAP combinations.

Paralleling the in vitro studies, the ex vivo SEV modeling demonstrated enhanced bactericidal impacts of DAP+CPT combination vs both the DAP-S and DAP-R strains over monotherapy alone. The combinatorial interactions of DAP+CPT generally mirrored our in vitro outcomes, with one important exception. Within SEVs ex vivo, these combinations resensitized the DAP-R strain, an effect previously noted with DAP+CPT in another DAP-R strain (Rose et al. 2012). These latter results suggest that the microenvironment within SEVs (e.g. platelets; fibrin; fibrinogen, etc.) may amplify the collective impacts of these antibiotic combinations. In addition, the concentrations used that mimic human therapeutic concentrations may impact DAP resensitization. These factors are likely to help explain the clinical report of DAP-R resensitization with the addition of CPT (Rose et al. 2012).

Next our in vivo data underscore the potential clinical translatability of DAP combination strategies against DAP-R S. aureus strain. The differences in resensitization found between the ex vivo SEV model and the in vivo IE model demonstrate that the resensitization effect may be variable and hinges of subtle nuances in antibiotic exposure and environment effects that may lead to reverting to DAP-S with CPT exposure. In addition, although resensitization in the ex vivo model occurred, the resulting MIC was only a log2 dilution lower (1 µg/mL versus 2 µg/mL) compared to the rabbit model following DAP+CPT. We note that although a comprehensive evaluation of DAP-R development and the effect of CPT was performed in this study, these results should be interpreted with caution since only a single strain pair was evaluated and require follow-up confirmation studies. These findings also support the need for more extensive evaluation of similar combination regimens in this model vs. larger S. aureus strain sets to identify predictive organism and drug exposure impacts leading to changes in DAP susceptibility.

The mobile-element dropout in the 2.0 Mb region is notable because β-hemolysin-converting prophages often carry the immune evasion cluster (IEC)—including factors such as complement inhibition (scn), staphylokinase (sak), and enterotoxin genes (e.g. sep)—and insert into/alter hlb biology. A regimen-associated gain/loss or replacement of this element, therefore, has implications beyond “callability”: it can plausibly reshape host adaptation and virulence potential by modulating immune evasion functions while simultaneously changing β-hemolysin context. While the selection here occurred under in vitro antibiotic pressure (and thus may reflect stress-driven prophage instability, bottlenecking, or compensatory tradeoffs), the finding underscores that antibiotic exposure can couple resistance evolution with pathogenesis-relevant gene-content shifts, and it motivates follow-on work to (i) confirm the structural event with orthogonal mapping/assembly evidence and (ii) test whether IEC/hlb-associated functional phenotypes (e.g. immune evasion readouts or hemolysis-related traits) are altered in the DAP+CPT-selected population.

In conclusion, the data from this study, together with prior work, support the rationale for a future randomized clinical trial evaluating combination therapy for MRSA and MSSA bacteremia with an emphasis on endocarditis. Repurposing strategies that pair DAP with a β-lactam such as CPT may extend the clinical lifespan of key standards-of-care (e.g. DAP and vancomycin [VAN]) by improving bacterial killing while reducing the probability of resistance amplification during therapy. Antimicrobial peptide resistance is a common phenotypic feature of endocarditis-associated S. aureus, and high-inoculum infections create a setting in which VAN or DAP monotherapy may be vulnerable to failure. Moreover, prior observations that VAN exposure can promote cross-resistance relevant to DAP before DAP is even deployed are consistent with the emergence of DAP-R reported in pivotal clinical experience. Collectively, these considerations argue that, in patients at high risk for treatment failure (e.g. high burden disease and membrane stress–adapted phenotypes), adjunctive CPT may be a pragmatic strategy to mitigate resistance trajectories and improve therapeutic response. Since this study focused on a single MSSA pair, comparative effects with β-lactams targeted MSSA are warranted in future studies.

More broadly, our integrated in vitro, ex vivo, and in vivo platform provides a generalizable framework to evaluate antimicrobial efficacy and resistance suppression in gram-positive pathogens and potentially other clinically important bacteria. While this study was not designed to resolve every mechanistic pathway, pairing phenotypic readouts with ultra-deep targeted sequencing added a critical translational layer: we observed regimen-associated genetic heterogeneity in canonical membrane-stress loci (including mprF) under DAP monotherapy, distinct locus-level changes under DAP+CPT, and a combination-associated mobile-element/prophage gene-content event affecting an immune-evasion/β-hemolysin-converting region. Together, these findings support a model in which DAP+CPT not only enhances antibacterial activity but also reshapes evolutionary trajectories of both resistance-associated and pathogenesis-relevant genomic features, providing a mechanistically grounded basis for future clinical trials and for identifying biomarkers that could guide patient-level deployment of combination therapy.
