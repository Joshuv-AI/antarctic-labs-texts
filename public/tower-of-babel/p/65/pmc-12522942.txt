FAM19A4 and miR124-2 methylation status in human papillomavirus-driven and human papillomavirus-negative oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas
Authors: Maria Gabriella Donà, Eugenia Giuliani, Valentina Laquintana, Renato Covello, Raul Pellini, Silvia Moretto, Alessandra Latini, Maria Benevolo, Francesca Rollo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScarce data exist on host gene methylation in oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas (OPCs), which are caused by human papillomavirus (HPV) in approximately 40% of cases. We analyzed the methylation status of two host genes involved in cervical carcinogenesis, FAM19A4 and miR124-2, in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues of primary HPV-driven OPCs (i.e., HPV-positive and p16INK4A-positive), HPV-negative OPCs, head and neck (HN) squamous cell papillomas, and oral rinse-and-gargles (ORGs) from individuals without HN lesions.MethodsA multiplex real-time methylation-specific PCR on bisulfite-converted DNA was employed (PreCursor-M+, Fujirebio). Hypermethylation for each target was expressed as negative/positive based on the ΔΔCt, as well as by the ΔΔCt ratio (2-ΔΔCt).ResultsA total of 70 HPV-driven OPCs (66 HPV16+, 94.3%), 71 HPV-negative OPCs, 12 HN papillomas, and 20 ORGs were evaluated. Six of the 153 FFPE-purified DNA samples (3.9%) yielded invalid results. Hypermethylation for at least one target gene was observed in 56 of 69 valid HPV-driven OPCs (81.2%) and 33 of 67 valid HPV-negative OPCs (49.2%; p < 0.0001). None of the papillomas or ORGs were hypermethylated. HPV-driven OPCs showed a significantly higher methylation level compared with HPV-negative OPCs for both FAM19A4 (median ΔΔCt ratio 11.95 vs. 5.49; p = 0.0001) and miR124-2 (median ΔΔCt ratio 15.65 vs. 8.27; p < 0.0001).ConclusionsOur findings indicate that FAM19A4/miR124-2 hypermethylation occurs exclusively in tumor samples and that methylation levels are significantly higher in HPV-driven OPCs than in HPV-negative OPCs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-025-00697-5.

Background

Oropharyngeal squamous cell carcinomas (OPCs) are rare neoplasms that, besides being associated with classical risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption, may be caused by carcinogenic human papillomaviruses (HPVs). The HPV prevalence in OPCs varies from 20% to over 70% [1]. The proportion of OPCs attributable to HPV infection has been estimated to be 45% in Europe [2], but significant geographical differences exist [3]. The substantial rise in OPC incidence observed in recent years is attributable to the contribution of the HPV-driven fraction [4]. A large body of data has demonstrated that HPV-driven OPCs are biologically distinct from HPV-negative OPCs, and substantial differences exist in terms of prognosis [5, 6].

Recent data suggest that host gene methylation may serve as a diagnostic and prognostic marker of HPV-associated cancers and may also provide novel therapeutic strategies [7–10]. Indeed, epigenetic changes in key cancer-associated genes are hallmarks of cancers. Human foreskin keratinocytes immortalized by several high-risk HPV types (i.e., HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, and HPV45) show significantly increased methylation levels in several host genes, including family with sequence similarity 19 (chemokine (C–C motif)-like) member A4 (FAM19A4) and microRNA 124-2 (miR124-2) [11]. FAM19A4 was identified through a genome-wide analysis of DNA methylation changes associated with transforming HPV16 infections [12], whereas miR124 is a tumor suppressor gene silenced by methylation in several types of cancers [13], including cervical cancer [14]. Both genes have been recognized as methylation markers of cervical carcinogenesis, with their methylation increasing alongside cervical disease severity, reaching the highest levels in invasive cervical cancer [12, 15–17]. For this reason, they are currently being explored as possible tools to triage high-risk HPV-positive women [7, 18–23].

Host gene methylation has also been investigated in OPCs. Methylation analysis of several genes, mostly selected based on methylome studies or previous validation in cervical cancers, has revealed different methylation profiles between HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs [10, 24–27]. However, none of the previous studies have investigated FAM19A4 and miR124-2 methylation status in OPCs.

The above findings have fostered interest in evaluating epigenetic changes in HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs. In the present study, we evaluated the methylation status of FAM19A4 and miR124-2 in OPCs to assess whether (i) their methylation is associated with OPCs and (ii) differences exist between HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs.

Materials and methods

Study population

The specimens analyzed in this study were obtained from three groups of individuals: (1) patients with primary, untreated OPC; (2) patients with head and neck (HN) squamous cell papillomas; and (3) individuals without HN lesions. OPCs and HN papillomas were selected among the cases diagnosed at the Pathology Department of the Regina Elena National Cancer Institute between 2013 and 2023.

Consecutive cases with a histologically confirmed OPC diagnosis (tonsils, base of the tongue, soft palate, uvula, Waldeyer’s ring, and other parts of the oropharynx, as defined by the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision [ICD-10]) were selected based on the following criteria: (1) availability of results for both HPV testing and p16 immunohistochemistry; (2) residual DNA purified from the formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections, stored at − 80 °C and sufficient for methylation analysis.

HN papillomas were selected among the cases included in a previous study that evaluated HPV prevalence in these benign lesions [28], if residual DNA purified from the FFPE sections was stored at − 80 °C and was sufficient for methylation analysis.

Oral rinse-and-gargles (ORGs) had been collected from participants in a longitudinal study on oral HPV infection (OHMAR study) [29]. Inclusion criteria for the OHMAR participants have been previously described [29]. Briefly, participants were men with no clinically evident HN lesions, as confirmed through a full otolaryngologic examination. Control cases from the OHMAR participants were selected based on the following criteria: (1) availability of HPV test results; (2) presence of ORG-purified DNA stored at − 80 °C, sufficient for methylation analysis; (3) signed informed consent for the future research use of the sample. A convenience control group of 12 papillomas (six HPV-positive and six HPV-negative) and 20 ORGs (10 high-risk HPV-positive and 10 HPV-negative) was selected for methylation analysis.

The medical or research records were reviewed for each study subject to collect relevant data, including sex, smoking habits, age at diagnosis, and primary tumor subsite (for OPC patients). The study was approved by the CET Lazio Area 5 Ethics Committee (IFO/51/23, 25 Oct 2023).

Study samples

FFPE blocks from OPCs and HN papillomas had been sectioned as previously described [28, 30]. Briefly, up to 3 × 5 μm sections were cut for DNA extraction. For cancer tissues, an additional 3-µm section was obtained for p16 staining. ORGs had been collected using a commercial mouthwash [29]. Based on the HPV test results, a convenience sample of 10 HPV-negative and 10 high-risk HPV-positive cases with sufficient residual DNA was selected.

DNA purification

DNA extraction and purification from FFPE sections (deparaffinized using a Deparaffinization solution, Qiagen), had been performed using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was eluted in 100 µl of water. DNA purification from the ORGs had been performed with the Amplilute Liquid Media Extraction Kit, as previously described [31].

HPV testing and p16 immunohistochemistry

OPCs and HN papillomas had been tested using the INNO-LiPA HPV Genotyping Extra/Extra II Kits (Fujirebio), both based on the SPF10 primer set and capable of detecting all 12 high-risk HPV types (i.e., HPV16, HPV18, HPV31, HPV33, HPV35, HPV39, HPV45, HPV51, HPV52, HPV56, HPV58, and HPV59) [32]. ORGs had been tested using the Linear Array HPV Genotyping Test (Roche Diagnostics).

The CINtec® Histology Kit (Roche Diagnostics) had been used for p16 immunostaining of OPC samples, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples with strong nuclear expression in > 75% of tumor cells were classified as p16 positive [33]. OPCs positive for both high-risk HPV DNA and p16 expression were considered HPV-driven.

FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation analysis

After DNA quantification using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Qiagen), 300 ng of DNA was used for bisulfite conversion in a final volume of 50 µl using the EZ DNA Methylation™ Kit (Zymo Research). Samples were incubated at 50 °C for 16 h in the dark. Desulfonation and recovery of bisulfite-treated DNA in 10 µl of M-elution buffer were performed following the manufacturer’s protocol. Quantitative methylation-specific PCR (qMSP) was performed using 2.5 µl of bisulfite-converted DNA and 17.5 µl of the ready-to-use master mix of the PreCursor-M + assay (Fujirebio). A Magnetic Induction Cycler (MIC, Fujirebio) was used for qMSP.

The cutoff Cq value for the ß-actin quality control was set at 30; samples with Cq > 30 were considered invalid. Since the cutoff values provided by the manufacturer for the two target genes have been established for cervical samples, in this study we identified new thresholds, to optimize discrimination between HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Youden index were used to identify optimal cutoff values. The FAM19A4 promoter was considered hypermethylated when ΔΔCq for FAM194A was ≤ 3.47, whereas the miR124-2 promoter was considered hypermethylated when ΔΔCq for miR124-2 was ≤ 3.27. Samples were considered methylation-positive if FAM19A4 and/or miR124-2 (FAM19A4/miR124-2) were hypermethylated. The methylation level for each target gene was also expressed as the ΔΔCt ratio (2-ΔΔCt), where 0 indicates no methylation, and increasing values indicate higher methylation levels [34]. The median ΔΔCt ratio was calculated for each group of specimens.

Statistical analysis

The appropriate sample size for detecting a difference between two proportions was calculated based on our preliminary results obtained from 20 HPV-driven and 20 HPV-negative OPCs, which displayed FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation in 17/20 (85.0%) and 12/19 (63.2%, one sample was invalid), respectively. Setting the alpha level at 0.05, the power at 80%, and the ratio of the two groups at 1, the required sample size was 63 per group. This sample size was increased by 10% to compensate for any invalid results.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the main characteristics of the study groups. For the analysis purposes, cancer subsite was classified as tonsils, base of the tongue, or other oropharyngeal sites; smoking habits was categorized as never, former and current smoker; tumor status (T) was classified as T1–T2 and T3–T4; nodal status (N) was categorized as with nodal involvement (N+, regardless of the number and size of involved lymph nodes) or without nodal involvement (N0), to mitigate discrepancies resulting from pathologic classification changes during the study period. Differences between proportions were tested using the chi-square test. The Mann-Whitney test was used to compare median values. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc® Statistical Software version 23.0.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2024).

Study population

A total of 173 samples were included in the analysis: 70 HPV-driven OPCs, 71 HPV-negative OPCs, and a convenience set of 12 HN papillomas (six HPV-positive) and 20 ORGs (10 HPV-positive). The main characteristics of the study subjects are shown in Table 1. The majority of OPCs were diagnosed in men (74.3% for HPV-driven and 81.7% for HPV-negative OPCs). The most frequent cancer subsite was the tonsils (51.4% for HPV-driven and 39.5% for HPV-negative OPCs). The HPV genotypes detected in the different study samples are shown in Supplementary Table 1. HPV16 was the most frequent genotype among HPV-driven OPCs (66/70, 94.3%), followed by HPV35 (2/70, 2.9%), HPV18 (1/70, 1.4%) and HPV33 (1/70, 1.4%).

FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation analysis

All the samples were analyzed by qMSP. Non-neoplastic specimens were used as controls to investigate whether methylation of the two host genes was associated with the cancer status. Six of the 153 FFPE-purified DNA samples (3.9%)—specifically one HPV-driven OPC, four HPV-negative OPCs, and one papilloma—showed invalid results in qMSP and were excluded from the analysis.

Hypermethylation for at least one target gene was observed in 56 of the 69 valid HPV-driven OPCs (81.2%) and 33 of 67 valid HPV-negative OPCs (49.2%; p < 0.0001) (Table 2). Two HPV-driven OPCs positive for high-risk HPV types other than HPV16/18 were methylation-positive for both genes (data not shown). None of the papillomas or ORGs were hypermethylated.

Examining the two target genes individually (Table 2), methylation of FAM19A4 was detected in 60.9% of HPV-driven OPCs and 28.4% of HPV-negative OPCs, respectively (p = 0.0001). Similarly, the methylation positivity rate for miR124-2 was significantly higher in HPV-driven OPCs than in HPV-negative OPCs (73.9% vs. 38.8%, p < 0.0001).

DNA methylation levels of HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs were also compared in terms of ΔΔCt ratio. HPV-driven OPCs exhibited significantly higher methylation levels than HPV-negative OPCs for both FAM19A4 (median ΔΔCt ratio 11.95 vs. 5.49; p = 0.0001) and miR124-2 (median ΔΔCt ratio 15.65 vs. 8.27; p < 0.0001) (Fig. 1).

FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation status by OPC patient characteristics

Since individual characteristics are known to influence epigenetics, we evaluated the methylation status of FAM19A4/miR124-2 by stratifying OPC patients based on their characteristics. The methylation positivity rate for FAM19A4/miR124-2 was higher in men than women among both HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (Table 3). For both HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs, no significant differences were observed based on sex, smoking status, T, N, or cancer subsite. In fact, the methylation positivity rate of T1-T2 OPCs was similar to that of T3-T4 OPCs. The proportion of FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylated cases was also comparable between patients with and without nodal involvement, although the latter group was very small. For HPV-negative OPCs, tumors located in sites “other” than the tonsils and base of the tongue exhibited a higher methylation positivity rate, although this difference did not achieve statistical significance (p = 0.126).

Discussion

The methylation status of FAM19A4 and miR124-2 host genes has never been investigated in OPCs. Our findings demonstrate that methylation analysis of these genes can fully discriminate OPCs from non-neoplastic HN lesions. In fact, none of the papillomas were hypermethylated, whereas approximately 50–80% of the OPCs were methylation-positive for at least one gene. In addition, FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation was absent in ORG samples from HN lesion-free individuals, whereas others have observed background methylation in normal oropharyngeal tissue for different methylation markers [26]. The complete absence of methylation in high-risk HPV-positive papillomas and ORGs suggests that FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation is not driven by high-risk HPV infection itself but is instead associated with transforming high-risk HPV infections and malignancy.

The correlation between the methylation status and expression of FAM19A4 and miR124-2 in OPC has not been evaluated. Nonetheless, increase of miR124-2 methylation is related to its reduced expression in high-risk HPV-immortalized keratinocytes [14]. In addition, according to data from the MethHC, a database of DNA methylation and gene expression in human cancer, there is a negative correlation between FAM19A4 methylation level and expression in head and neck cancers [35, 36].

Noteworthy, both the methylation positivity rate and methylation levels were significantly higher in HPV-driven OPCs compared with HPV-negative OPCs. This observation suggests an additional biological difference between HPV-driven and HPV-negative OPCs. However, it must be noted that FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation is less frequent in HPV-driven OPCs than in cervical cancers, where positivity rates exceeding 90–95% have been reported [15, 16]. This difference between cervical cancers and HPV-driven OPCs has also been observed for other methylation markers, suggesting that HPV-driven oropharyngeal and cervical carcinogenesis have distinct characteristics. These differences likely result from variations in the epithelium, carcinogenic exposure, and immune surveillance, among other factors [26].

Among the HPV-driven OPCs, two cases harboring high-risk HPV types other than HPV16/18 (one HPV33-positive and one HPV35-positive) exhibited methylation for both target genes. This finding indicates that methylation of FAM19A4 is not exclusive of HPV16- or HPV18-driven OPCs, despite initial reports of FAM19A4 methylation in HPV16- and HPV18-immortalized keratinocytes and HPV16- or HPV18-positive cervical cancer cell lines [12].

It is well known that epigenetic changes are associated with individual characteristics, such as sex, and lifestyle, such as smoking history and alcohol consumption [37–39]. In our study, stratified analysis showed no significant differences in FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation between men and women in either HPV-driven or HPV-negative OPCs, consistent with previous findings [26]. In addition, the methylation status of these genes did not vary significantly based on smoking status, T, N, or cancer subsite for HPV-driven as well as HPV-negative OPCs. In this latter group, the proportion of FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylated cases was higher in tumors located in sites “other” than the tonsils and base of the tongue. Nevertheless, the sample size for these “other site” HPV-negative OPCs was quite limited, preventing firm conclusions regarding this finding.

Notably, methylation results were invalid for fewer than 4% of cases where DNA was purified from FFPE samples. This indicates that DNA extracted from FFPE tissues of HN lesions is suitable for methylation analysis using the assay employed in this study. The proportion of samples with invalid methylation analysis is comparable to that in a study involving both cytological and histological cervical samples [15], but more than twice as high as in an investigation that analyzed only cytological cervical samples [20].

Some limitations of this study must be acknowledged. The vast majority of our OPC patients were men, and sex may influence epigenetics; however, this reflects not only the characteristics of our case series [40], but also the global sex distribution of OPCs [41]. Additionally, we lacked tissue samples from the normal oropharynx as controls. ORGs are cytological specimens and do not strictly represent the oropharyngeal site, as they contain cells derived from both the oral cavity and the oropharynx. Moreover, ORGs were not collected from OPC patients. Therefore, a comparative analysis between ORGs from OPC patients and healthy individuals could not be performed. Finally, the lack of complete data on alcohol consumption did not allow us to perform the analysis involving this variable. The strength of this study lies in the use of a commercially available assay that includes a built-in check for sample quality and is based on a standardized procedure, ensuring reliable results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found that the methylation status of FAM19A4/miR124-2, methylation markers of cervical carcinogenesis, enables the discrimination of OPCs from non-neoplastic HN lesions, as well as HPV-driven from HPV-negative OPCs. HPV-driven OPCs exhibited significantly higher FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation rates and levels than HPV-negative OPCs. Given the need for novel prognostic biomarkers to improve risk stratification of HPV-driven OPCs, the clinical significance of this elevated FAM19A4/miR124-2 methylation warrants further investigation, particularly regarding its impact on survival outcomes.
