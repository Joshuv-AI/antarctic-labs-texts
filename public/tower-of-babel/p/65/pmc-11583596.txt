Quantifying skin permeation of a novel metformin lotion using modified hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography
Authors: Derek J Maloney, Jianying Zhang, Satyaj Bhargava, MaCalus V Hogan, James H-C Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim: This study aimed to quantify the permeation of metformin (Met) lotion through pig ear skin using high-performance liquid chromatography, specifically hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), to separate Met from biological contaminants and effectively measure its permeation through skin similar to human skin.Materials & methods: A Franz cell permeation assay was used to assess the permeation kinetics of 6% Met lotion through pig ear skin. Samples were collected at various time points and prepared for high-performance liquid chromatography analysis by removing large biological contaminants. The permeated Met was quantified by monitoring its retention time (RT) at 9 min using HILIC, with an acidic, polar mobile phase and a normal-phase column.Results: A distinct Met peak with a RT of approximately 9 min was observed in the 6% Met lotion, which was absent in the permeation samples from the 0% Met lotion. This peak (RT 9 min) was distinct from the ‘biological-contaminants’ peaks at RT 2–3 min and increased linearly over time, reaching 36.8% of the total applied Met at 24 h.Conclusion: These findings demonstrate that the HILIC method effectively separates Met from biological components in pig ear skin, allowing accurate quantification of Met despite the presence of skin lipids and proteins.

Introduction

Metformin (Met), a widely used drug for managing Type II diabetes, exhibits therapeutic benefits across a range of diseases, including cardiovascular disorders, neurodegenerative conditions, cancer and degenerative osteoarthritis [1]. Notably, Met possesses anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic properties [2,3]. Research into tendinopathy – a chronic and painful tendon disorder characterized by inflammation – has identified HMGB1, a key tissue damage-signaling molecule, as central to the development of this condition [4]. It was also demonstrated that intraperitoneal (IP) injections of Met can reduce inflammation in injured and aging tendons by decreasing extracellular HMGB1 and PGE2 production, markers of tendinopathy [5,6].

While Met is typically administered orally to diabetic patients, its strong alkaline properties can lead to adverse effects on the stomach, liver and kidneys [7]. Additionally, IP injections may cause unwanted systemic side effects. Oral administration also fails to deliver effective drug concentrations to specific local tissues, such as tendons [8]. Consequently, a topical application of Met is being explored in our laboratory as a viable alternative for treating joint pain and inflammation in tendon tissues.

To determine appropriate dosages for clinical application, it is crucial to understand the diffusion kinetics of Met through skin. Permeation tests, which assess how a drug interacts with and penetrates the skin, are essential for this purpose. These tests also allow for the quantification of drug permeation. Animal skin tissues, particularly using pig ear skin, which closely resembles human skin, are commonly used for these assessments [9]. However, detecting Met in biological samples from permeation tests remains a significant challenge.

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) is instrumental for separating analytes at extremely low concentrations based on their polarity. Nonetheless, the detection of Met in permeation samples using HPLC is complicated by the presence of various biological “contaminants” such as fats and proteins that diffuse from the pig ear skin. During sample preparation, there is a high likelihood of contaminants appearing in the HPLC chromatogram, which complicates the extraction and analysis of compounds from biological samples.

In this study, we adopted a simple, cost-effective and robust method to quantify the amount of Met that permeates through the skin. Using the classical Franz cell, our permeation test replicates human skin conditions – the Met diffuses from the top of the skin, through its layers, and into the receptor chamber fluid below. For the quantification of permeated Met, we utilized a novel approach involving hydrophilic interaction liquid chromatography (HILIC), which capitalizes on Met’s small, polar and basic characteristics [10].

Materials & methods

Chemicals

Metformin hydrochloride (C4H11N5 ·HCl) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Catalog No. D150959-5G). Ammonium acetate (CH3COONH4) was sourced from Sigma (Catalog No. A1542-500G). Acetic acid (CH3COOH) was acquired from Sigma (Catalog No. A-6283). Acetonitrile (CH3CN) was purchased from Honeywell (Catalog No. 34851-4X4L). All chemicals used were of HPLC-grade purity, and solutions were prepared using deionized water filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to minimize contamination.

Met lotion formulation

Met lotion was formulated in our laboratory. It was produced in-house using a proprietary process under optimized conditions. This process yielded a Met lotion, with a concentration of 6% of Met. For the control lotion, the same formulation was followed without the addition of Met (0% Met lotion).

Permeation test

A 2 cm2 section of skin was excised from pig ears sourced from Strip District Meats in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania. Pig skin was used as it is commonly used as a surrogate for human skin permeability testing [11,12]. This skin sample was positioned on a 9 mm jacketed Franz Cell with a 5 ml receptor volume (Figure 1) obtained from PermeGear (Catalog No. 4G-01-00-09-05). The Franz cell was filled with 5 ml of distilled water and maintained in a large water bath set to 37°C. A stir bar was used within the Franz cell to ensure continuous mixing of the receptor chamber contents.

Schematic of the Franz cell used in the permeation test. The pig ear skin is positioned between the donor chamber (A) and remains in continuous contact with the fluid in the receptor chamber (B). Samples are extracted through the sample port (C) using a long pipet tip. The entire cell is situated within a water bath. The water jacket (D) is filled with water maintained at 37°C (arrows) to simulate the core body temperature. A stir bar (E) rotates throughout the test to ensure even mixing of the receptor chamber's contents. The receptor chamber opening has an area of 2.54 cm2.

A quantity of 24.0 mg of 6% Met lotion was applied to one pig ear skin flap, while 24.4 mg of 0% Met lotion was applied to another in a separate Franz cell. Applied amounts were calculated to maintain a constant layer of thickness of the lotion [13]. Samples of 200 μl were extracted from each Franz cell and transferred into glass test tubes at predefined time points: 0, 5, 15, 30 min and 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 24 h post application. An additional 200 μl of distilled water was added to each Franz cell at each time point. All collected samples were immediately frozen at -20°C after collection. This experimental setup was validated based on methods used in similar transdermal permeation studies [14].

Sample preparation

For HPLC analysis, permeation samples were prepared according to a published method [15] with slight modification by mixing each 200 μl sample with 600 μl of ice-cold acetonitrile, followed by vigorous vortexing. The samples were then stored at -20°C for 1 h before being centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 10 min. Subsequently, 700 μl of the clear supernatant was carefully transferred to a new glass test tube. These samples were then placed in a 70°C incubator overnight to facilitate evaporation. The dried residues were reconstituted in 200 μl of the HPLC mobile phase and vortexed vigorously to ensure uniform mixing. Each sample solution (20 μl) was injected into the column for detecting the concentration of Met by HPLC. This sample preparation procedure was modified from previous studies on Met transdermal delivery [16].

HPLC method

The analyses were conducted using an Agilent 1100 series HPLC system, which included a G1322A Degasser, G1311A Quaternary Pump, G1329A Automatic Liquid Sampler, G1330A Automatic Liquid Sampler Thermostat and G1316A Column Compartment, all acquired from Marshall Scientific. The analytical column used was a Supelco Supelcosil LC-Si (4.6 mm × 250 mm, 5 μm) from Sigma-Aldrich (Catalog No. 58295). The mobile phase consisted of 40 parts 0.01 M ammonium acetate, adjusted to pH 5.0 with acetic acid and 60 parts acetonitrile (v/v). The operational parameters included a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min, an injection volume of 20 μl, and a detection wavelength of 235 nm. Each sample underwent a 15 min analysis at room temperature. The system was calibrated using standard Met solutions to ensure accurate and reproducible measurements. This HPLC method was optimized for both sensitivity and specificity, allowing for detection limits as low as 2 μg/ml, with the ability to have a much lower detection limit if needed.

Determination of Met quantities

The standard curve for Met was generated using a procedure identical to that described in the Sample Preparation section, involving the addition of 600 μl of ice-cold acetonitrile to each 200 μl of Met standard solution, followed by centrifugation and the extraction of 700 μl of clear supernatant, which was carefully transferred to a new glass test tube. These samples were placed in a 70°C incubator overnight to facilitate evaporation. The dried residues were then reconstituted in 200 μl of the HPLC mobile phase and vortexed thoroughly to ensure uniform mixing. A 20 μl aliquot of each sample was injected into the column for Met concentration analysis by HPLC. The resulting standard curve facilitated the quantification of Met permeation through the skin at each time point.

To determine if the quantification of Met was altered by biological components found in solution, we tested the permeation samples of Met standard solution penetrated through the pig ear skin using the same protocol for Met lotion analysis. We also combined a Met standard solution and “contaminated” solution that contained biological components. This control was implemented to account for matrix effects from biological components, as recommended in transdermal permeation studies [13]. To achieve this, we prepared a Franz cell permeation test and added a pig ear skin, ensuring the skin was in contact with the receptor chamber liquid, and let sit for 24 h. After 24 h, 200 μl of the receptor chamber liquid was added to 50 μl of a Met standard solution. Seven hundred fifty microliter of acetonitrile was then added, followed by centrifugation, and the extraction of 875 μl of clear supernatant which was carefully transferred to a new glass test tube. These samples were then placed in a 70°C incubator overnight to facilitate evaporation. The dried residues were reconstituted in 200 μl of the HPLC mobile phase and vortexed vigorously to ensure uniform mixing. Each 20 μl of the sample solution was injected into the column for detecting the concentration of Met by HPLC.

To calculate the total percentage of Met that permeated through the skin at each time point, the equation C1 + C2 + C3 + … + Cn-1 × 0.2 + Cn × 524000 × 0.06 × 100% was used. Here, C1, C2,…, Cn-1 represent the Met concentrations at successive time points, with Cn being the specific time point of interest. The factor 0.2 represents the sample volume in milliliters taken from the Franz cell, and the receptor chamber contained a total of 5 ml of ddH2O. The total amount of Met initially applied in the 6% lotion (24,000 μg) is multiplied by 6% (0.06) to determine the total Met content in the applied sample.

Results

To determine the retention time (RT) of Met under our test conditions, we initially injected a pure Met solution directly into the HPLC system without any prior treatment. This approach resulted in a single peak at an RT of 9.3 min for a 50 μg/ml Met solution, indicated by a black arrow in Figure 2A. In contrast, additional peaks appeared at RTs of 2–3 min when Met passed through pig ear skin and underwent a sample preparation process before HPLC injection, as shown by the dashed blue arrow in Figure 2B. At an RT of 9 min, Met was clearly separated (solid blue arrow in Figure 2B) from undesirable components such as proteins and lipids from pig ear skin and other potential contaminants that might have accumulated during sample preparation, which eluted at RTs of 2–3 min (Figure 2B, C & D). The acidic, polar conditions of our HILIC method effectively bound Met, allowing it to elute at a more favorable RT, distinct from those of other substances. This clear separation facilitated accurate quantification of Met permeation using HILIC.

Met demonstrated an elution at an RT of approximately 9 min. The detection limits for Met were defined at a lower limit of 40 ng when 20 μl of 2 μg/ml Met solution was injected, eluting at 9.13 min (red arrow in Figure 2C), and an upper limit of 4 μg when 20 μl of 200 μg/ml Met solution was injected, eluting at 8.85 min (green arrow in Figure 2D). As Met concentration increased, the RT slightly decreased, likely due to the substantial quantity of polar Met interacting with the polar stationary phase of the normal phase column. The elution peak of Met displayed excellent symmetry with minimal tailing or shouldering, ensuring its isolation for analysis. Peak area integration and calculation were performed using ChemStation software.

The calibration curve was constructed by plotting the peak area against the Met concentration, ranging from 20 to 200 μg/ml. The biological components entering the sample did not significantly alter the RT or quantifiability of Met, nor did they significantly affect the coefficient of determination of the curve (Figure 3A & B). The data revealed that the peak area of Met increased proportionally with its concentration. A strong correlation was obtained (R2 = 0.9969), and the resulting regression equation was y = 86.19x – 149.6, where ‘y’ represents the peak area and ‘x’ denotes the Met concentration in μg/ml.

For the permeation samples from both lotion groups, a consistent group of peaks elutes at RT 2–3 min at every time point, including at 0 min (black arrow labeled peak in Figure 4A & B). These peaks likely represent a variety of compounds accumulating in the solution as the method progresses. Since these peaks are not of interest and their composition is unclear, they can be disregarded, allowing focus to remain solely on the RT of 9 min for Met determination (Figure 4B). No peak at RT 9 min was observed in the permeation samples from the 0% Met-lotion group (Figure 4A).

In the 6% Met lotion group, the area of the peak at RT 9 min increases linearly over time, indicating that Met permeation through the skin is time-dependent (Figure 4B). No peak was found at RT 9 min in the initial permeation samples at 0 min (circled area in Figure 4B). However, by 5 min, a measurable peak appears on the chromatogram (red arrow labeled peak in Figure 4B), corresponding to 9.87 μg of Met permeated through the skin (Figure 5A), which is 0.7% of the total Met in the lotion (Figure 5B). At 24 h, a prominent peak at approximately RT 9 min was detected (Figure 5B), with a total of 529.37 μg of Met having passed into the receptor chamber of the Franz cell (Figure 5A). At 8 h, 365.87 μg of Met had permeated (Figure 5A). Calculations from the Methods section suggest that 36.8% of the 6% Met lotion had permeated through the skin at the 24 h mark, and 25.4% at the 8 h mark (Figure 5B). A notable increase in Met permeation occurred between the 1 h and 2 h time points (Figure 5).

No significant peaks were observed at RT 9 min in the 0% Met lotion group (Figure 4A). The only difference between the two lotions is the presence of Met in the 6% formulation, confirming that Met is the exclusive compound eluting at RT 9 min. Similar peaks at RT 2–3 min in all permeation samples, including the Met standard solution and both lotion groups, suggest these could be biological components of the pig ear skin.

Discussion

Permeation tests are crucial for evaluating any topical drug formulation, as they help to determine diffusion rates, efficacy and appropriate dosages for clinical use. In our study, we utilized HILIC to quantify the levels of Met that had permeated through pig ear skin from a lotion formulation. We observed that Met was effectively separated from the proteins and lipids in the skin samples, eluting distinctly from their peaks. Notably, a small peak at an RT of 9 min was detected within just 5 min in the permeation sample of the 6% Met lotion group, a pattern that was consistent at all tested time points. This peak continued to increase linearly, underscoring the method’s effectiveness and reproducibility for studying Met lotion permeation kinetics.

HILIC, which is akin to normal phase chromatography but uses a polar mobile phase, is particularly suited for analyzing highly polar compounds like Met. This method enhances Met’s RT, allowing for better separation from substances like proteins and lipids that diffuse from the skin [10]. In contrast, reversed-phase HPLC (RP-HPLC) employs a nonpolar column, which significantly reduces the RT of Met, resulting in co-elution with other undesired analytes [17].

Some researchers reported a RT of 2.5 min for Met when detected using RP-HPLC [18]. Our results confirmed this finding, as Met eluted at 2.3 min when tested using a reversed-phase column (data not shown).

This study also demonstrated the importance of the mobile phase in Met determination in biological samples. It was found that Met eluted at 1.18 min when methanol-water (30:70; v/v) was used as the mobile phase [19]. However, when 0.1% formic acid was added to the methanol-water (30:70; v/v) solution, the RT of Met increased to 1.37 min [19]. These results indicate that the RT of Met lengthens as the polarity of the mobile phase increases.

We utilized ammonium acetate and acetic acid in our mobile phase, which affects metformin’s elution, causing it to elute later than other low-polarity compounds on a normal column. While these unwanted peaks elute at RT 2 min, Met elutes distinctly at RT 9 min. The necessity for a lengthy run sequence for each sample, however, underscores the potential for optimizing the mobile phase to reduce the run time and save resources, making the process more efficient in terms of time and cost [20].

Despite the prevalence of RP-HPLC in the field, only a few studies have utilized HILIC to quantify Met in solutions, and none have applied this method to study Met lotion permeation rates [21,22]. Other methods like ion pair chromatography or coupling HPLC with mass spectroscopy, though effective, are often more time-consuming, costly and error-prone [23,24]. HILIC offers an effective alternative by leveraging Met’s polarity to separate it cleanly from other components in the solution.

Current research is focused on understanding the disposition of the 64.1% of Met that remains unaccounted for 24 h after application – specifically, whether it is retained within the skin or localized to superficial layers [25]. Furthermore, prolonging the observation period to 48 and 72 h could reveal whether Met continues to permeate, albeit at a diminished rate, after 3 days. Additionally, variations in the quantity of Met lotion applied (e.g., 5 vs 50 mg) and the thickness of the skin utilized in experiments could markedly affect the Met lotion permeation dynamics [26].

Conclusion

Met can be effectively separated from biological contaminants with a short retention time (RT). This approach is both time- and cost-efficient for acheiving a clean separation of Met.
