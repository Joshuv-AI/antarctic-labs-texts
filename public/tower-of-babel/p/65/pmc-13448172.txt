Sugar reduction in complex food systems: linking metabolic mechanisms, matrix engineering, and safety evaluation
Authors: Huimin Xue, Kexin Hong, Xiping Kang, Yunxiao Gao, Yunyu Tang, Weidong Huang, Jicheng Zhan, Jia Liu, Yilin You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Excessive intake of rapidly absorbable sugars challenges metabolic regulation beyond caloric imbalance. Glucose and fructose differ in absorption kinetics and hepatic metabolic pathways, leading to distinct effects on glycemic dynamics, de novo lipogenesis, insulin signaling, and microbiota–host interactions. Emerging evidence further indicates that sugar bioavailability and metabolic outcomes are strongly influenced by food matrix structure, physicochemical interactions, and processing-induced microstructural changes. This review synthesizes recent advances in understanding these metabolic distinctions and examines how matrix-level reformulation strategies can modulate metabolic responses while preserving functional and sensory performance. Contemporary technological approaches—including non-sugar sweeteners, structural and bulking redesign, multisensory sweetness modulation, and digital formulation tools—are evaluated with attention to ingredient interactions, product stability, and safety profiles. Regulatory and risk-assessment frameworks are discussed in relation to ingredient deployment and translational feasibility. By integrating molecular metabolism with food engineering and safety evaluation, this review proposes a mechanistically grounded framework for the rational design of reduced-sugar foods within complex food systems.

Introduction

Sweetness is among the earliest gustatory qualities perceived by humans. During lactation, infants are exposed to sweet taste through lactose, the principal sugar in human milk (~6–7%), which may help shape early sweet preference1,2. During childhood, consumption of energy-dense foods helps meet the high energetic demands of growth, and the reward value of sweetness further reinforces such ingestive behavior. Sweet taste not only underpins palatability, but also activates brain reward circuits, eliciting pleasurable responses that promote continued consumption of sweet foods3. Carbohydrates are a fundamental energy source in the human diet and serve as the primary metabolic fuel for blood and tissues. Although glucose (Glu) and fructose (Fru) share the same chemical formula (C₆H₁₂O₆) and gross energy content (≈400 kcal/100 g), they differ markedly in perceived sweetness, glycemic index, satiety effects, and in absorption and metabolic pathways4. Sucrose (Suc), a disaccharide composed of Glu and Fru, is a principal dietary source of Fru alongside high-Fru corn sirup (HFCS), honey, whole fruit, and fruit juices. In whole foods, sugars are typically embedded within a matrix of dietary fiber, vitamins, and minerals and are released more slowly; even diets high in fruits and vegetables rarely contribute fructose energy exceeding ~5% of total intake5. Consequently, moderate consumption of sugars within intact food matrices is generally not associated with adverse health outcomes in otherwise healthy adults.

By contrast, structural changes in modern food systems have substantially increased the intake of free sugars. Sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB), i.e., beverages to which caloric sweeteners such as HFCS (≈55% Fru, 42% Glu) or fruit juice concentrates have been added, have become major contributors to population-level free sugar intake6. Global SSB consumption has risen rapidly over the past three decades, increasing by >40% between 1990 and 20167. In the United States, roughly 6.5% of adult energy intake (≈145 kcal/day) is derived from SSB, exceeding recommended limits from national dietary guidelines and the World Health Organization‌ (WHO)8. In China, about 50% of adults consume SSB; per-capita SSB consumption reached 119 kg in 2014 (~300 mL/day), nearly a tenfold increase relative to a decade earlier, and the prevalence of SSB consumption among children rose from 7.8% to 18.9%9. The mean sugar concentration of SSB is ~10.7 g/100 mL; a single 500-mL bottle thus commonly exceeds the WHO recommended daily limit for free sugars (50 g). Rapid absorption of free sugars from SSB can provoke large postprandial glycemic excursions and increase metabolic burden. A large body of evidence links chronic high SSB intake to increased risks of obesity, type 2 diabetes (T2DM), cardiovascular disease (CVD), and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)10,11. The population health burden attributable to SSB consumption has risen concomitantly. In 2020, approximately 2.2 million new cases of T2DM and 1.2 million new cases of CVD were attributed to SSB consumption worldwide, accounting for 9.8% and 3.1% of all cases, respectively. SSB were responsible for 12.5 million disability-adjusted life years11. Of additional concern, higher SSB intake has been associated with adverse mental-health correlates, including depressive and anxiety symptoms and increased suicidal ideation among adolescents, indicating potential impacts on psychological well-being12.

As mechanistic and epidemiological evidence of SSB-associated harms has accumulated, reducing free sugar content in the food supply has emerged as a priority for public health agencies, the food industry, and consumers. In recent years, the WHO and many national authorities have issued policy recommendations and implemented interventions, such as sugar taxation, front-of-pack nutrition labeling, and marketing restrictions, aiming at reducing population sugar consumption13. From a food-science perspective, however, consumer dependence on the characteristic flavor and hedonic texture of sweetened beverages makes sugar reduction a multidimensional challenge that spans sensory science and formulation engineering. Research and industry practice are therefore shifting from single-axis sugar replacement toward integrated, “smart” sugar-reduction technologies that combine precision use of non-caloric and low-caloric sweeteners, food-structure optimization, and multisensory enhancement to realize “less sugar, same sweetness” product profiles.

Against this background, this article was developed as a narrative review informed by a structured literature search covering the major metabolic, technological, sensory, and public health dimensions of sugar reduction. It examines the metabolism of free sugars in foods and beverages and their implications for body-weight regulation, carbohydrate and lipid metabolism, cardiovascular function, hepatic health, and intestinal homeostasis, thereby clarifying the scientific basis and health relevance of sugar reduction. It also synthesizes current advances in formulation technologies, sensory modulation approaches, and international policy measures, with the aim of constructing an integrated framework that links mechanistic understanding, product innovation, and policy intervention. By bringing together evidence from nutritional metabolism, sensory psychology, food engineering, and public health policy, this review seeks to provide a multidisciplinary evidence base to inform the development of lower-sugar beverages and more effective population-level sugar-reduction strategies.

Metabolism of free sugars

WHO defines free sugars as monosaccharides and disaccharides added to foods and beverages, as well as sugars naturally present in honey, syrups, fruit juices, and fruit juice concentrates14. The principal free sugars in SSB are Suc, Glu, and Fru. Suc is hydrolyzed in vivo to yield Glu and Fru. This section focuses on the metabolic distinctions between Glu and Fru in the body. Sweet taste receptors

The gustatory system in the oral cavity evaluates the chemical composition of foods via sensory properties: food chemicals bind to specific receptors, transducing chemical energy into electrical signals that inform the brain about nutrient content, palatability, and potential toxicity15. The brain uses this molecular recognition information to guide subsequent ingestive behavior and to trigger downstream physiological processes that optimize nutrient absorption and metabolic regulation15. Sweet and umami/salty taste signaling are mediated by three members of the class C G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) family, namely taste receptor type 1 member 1, 2, and 3 (T1R1, T1R2, and T1R3), which form heterodimeric receptors. The T1R2–T1R3 heterodimer functions as the canonical sweet receptor; sugars bind with differing affinities to elicit receptor responses16. Activation of the sweet receptor by sweet compounds engages G-α-gustducin and downstream cAMP/protein kinase A signaling, increases intracellular Ca²⁺, and activates TRPM5, leading to ATP release as a neurotransmitter and subsequent excitation of cranial nerve afferents that project to central gustatory regions17.

Beyond the oral cavity, T1R2/T1R3 are also expressed in the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, bladder, adipose tissue, and brain; their activation can facilitate intestinal Glu transport into enterocytes and onward into the circulation. Stimulation of intestinal taste receptors (T1R2/T1R3) promotes the release of incretin hormones, including glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) and Glu-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), both of which potentiate insulin secretion in the setting of elevated glycemia and modulate appetite and gastrointestinal motility. Free Glu and Fru in SSB are rapidly absorbed after ingestion and can activate sweet receptors to elicit these downstream physiological responses.

Fructose metabolism

After gastric digestion, chyme enters the small intestine, where Fru is taken up across the apical enterocyte membrane via the facilitative transporter 5 (GLUT5) and is then exported across the basolateral membrane into the portal circulation primarily via Glu transporter 2 (GLUT2). Fru metabolism bypasses the key regulatory steps of glycolysis and gluconeogenesis (phosphofructokinase and pyruvate kinase) and proceeds predominantly via the ketohexokinase (KHK) pathway18. KHK phosphorylates Fru to form Fru-1-phosphate (F1P), a potentially reactive intermediate, which is further cleaved to yield D-glyceraldehyde and dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP). From this point, Fru metabolism converges with glycolytic intermediates. Under conditions of excess Fru intake, glyceraldehyde is phosphorylated by triose kinase to produce glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, which can be oxidized via the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle for energy or converted to acetyl-CoA and then into triacylglycerol (TAG). DHAP can also be enzymatically converted to glycerol-3-phosphate and esterified with long-chain fatty acids (C16–C18) derived from citrate-driven de novo lipogenesis (DNL) to form TAG4. Unlike canonical glycolysis, intestinal Fru metabolism can circumvent major rate-limiting checkpoints and is less subject to feedback inhibition; consequently, fructolysis can be rapidly initiated, and excessive Fru intake promotes gluconeogenesis and DNL.

Following intestinal absorption, Fru is delivered via the portal vein to the liver, where GLUT2 facilitates rapid and efficient hepatic uptake of most ingested Fru. Unlike Glu, Fru has a relatively small acute effect on blood Glu and insulin concentrations; substantial fractions of absorbed Fru are oxidized (approximately 25% to lactate, ~15% to glycogen)19. In the liver, Fru metabolic fates include20: (1) catabolism to pyruvate for subsequent energy production; (2) conversion to lactate; (3) incorporation into glycogen for storage; (4) esterification and storage as hepatic lipid droplets; and (5) conversion to Glu, which can be metabolized in hepatocytes or released into the systemic circulation. Hepatocellular lipid droplet accumulation predisposes to NAFLD, which can progress to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis when inflammatory processes intensify. Excessive Fru intake also induces endoplasmic reticulum oxidative stress, exacerbating hepatic steatosis.

The kidney is another organ involved in sugar metabolism: proximal straight tubules are a principal site of renal Fru handling, where filtered Fru is reabsorbed via membrane GLUT5 and metabolized through KHK-mediated pathways21. Recent evidence indicates that the kidney can generate endogenous Fru via the polyol pathway. Whether dietary or endogenously produced, Fru excess can lead to intracellular accumulation of uric acid22. Elevated uric acid exerts multiple pathogenic effects, including endothelial dysfunction, vascular injury, and contributions to NAFLD and other components of the metabolic syndrome23. Glucose metabolism

Most dietary Glu reaches the intestinal brush border in the form of α-limit dextrins and disaccharides (e.g., maltose, Suc), is cotransported across the apical membrane into enterocytes by the Na⁺-dependent Glu cotransporter 1 (SGLT1), and then exits the basolateral membrane via GLUT2 into the systemic circulation24. Approximately 20% of absorbed Glu is taken up by the liver via GLUT2, while the remaining ~80% enters the peripheral circulation, raising plasma Glu and stimulating insulin secretion25. Insulin binding to hepatic receptors promotes tyrosine phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrate-1 and subsequent activation of the serine/threonine kinase Akt. Akt phosphorylates FOXO1, downregulating gluconeogenic gene expression and helping to maintain euglycemia. Through downstream signaling, Akt activates glycogen synthase to convert a substantial portion of Glu into hepatic glycogen for energy storage4. In parallel, Akt increases sterol regulatory element-binding protein-1c (SREBP-1c) activity, which promotes acetyl-CoA conversion to free fatty acids (FFA) and ultimately their packaging into very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) for export and storage in adipose tissue26. Compared with Fru, Glu exerts a relatively weaker effect on CVD and metabolic syndrome development. Only approximately 5% of filtered Glu undergoes phosphorylation within proximal tubular cells; the majority of Glu is released into the peritubular capillary circulation27.

The necessity of sugar reduction

Weight management

Mechanistic and neuroimaging studies suggest high dietary sugar intake may enhance the brain’s preference for sugar and thereby stimulate appetite28. The linkage between sensory feedback and the energetic properties of foods is central to understanding how foods contribute to obesity and metabolic dysregulation28. Interoceptive signals generated during nutrient ingestion are transmitted to the central nervous system, enabling the brain to infer the nutritional value of foods and to form predictive associations that guide future ingestive behavior29. Thanarajah et al.30 conducted a randomized controlled trial in normal-weight participants in which subjects, in addition to their habitual diets, received either high-fat/high-sugar snacks (n = 26) or low-fat/low-sugar snacks (n = 23) for eight weeks. Although the intervention did not produce detectable changes in body weight or standard metabolic parameters, it altered neural responses to food cues and consumption, including increased activation of dopaminergic regions implicated in motivation and reward30. These findings indicate an unconscious preference for high-fat/high-sugar foods and a diminished responsiveness to low-fat foods, which may help explain vulnerability to overeating and future weight gain. These results are consistent with prior neuroimaging studies showing that habitual SSB consumption modulates frontostriatal responses to food cues31.

Fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF21) is a hepatically derived hormone whose mRNA expression in the liver and circulating levels increase after sugar ingestion, with Suc producing particularly strong induction32. Circulating FGF21 can act on the brain to modulate neuronal networks that influence food and beverage choice33. In experimental models, FGF21 suppresses the intake of monosaccharides and alcohol in mice and reduces the consumption of the artificial sweetener saccharin in nonhuman primates. Human genetic studies have linked variation in FGF21 and its co-receptor β-klotho (KLB) to patterns of Fru and alcohol consumption, suggesting that interindividual differences in FGF21 signaling may partly determine sugar preference34. Collectively, these data suggest that the reinforcing properties of sugar derive not only from orosensory pleasure but also from post-ingestive signals that shape neural circuits controlling intake. Chronic consumption of sugar-rich SSB may therefore establish persistent brain activation patterns that heighten sugar preference and elevate the risk of overweight and metabolic dysfunction.

Sugar consumed as part of foods or as SSB may also alter intestinal absorptive capacity and thereby promote obesity. In one animal study, mice were assigned to three diets: a Fru-free control diet, a standard high-fat diet containing glu (45% of calories from fat), and an isocaloric high-fat diet in which Glu was replaced by Suc (providing both Glu and Fru)35. Despite equal energy intakes across groups, mice fed Suc exhibited significantly greater increases in body weight, fat mass, and serum total cholesterol compared with the Fru-free high-fat group35. The Suc-fed animals showed increased enterocyte survival, a 25–40% increase in small-intestinal villus length, and expanded intestinal surface area, which enhanced nutrient absorption from the high-fat diet and contributed to greater weight gain and adiposity.

Carbohydrate metabolism

Risk of T2DM

T2DM develops over many years, and evidence linking dietary sugars to T2DM is largely observational. A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis of prospective cohort studies identified through MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, and Cochrane databases found that total dietary sugar and Suc intake were not consistently associated with incident T2DM risk. Specifically, among 29 cohorts, each additional 20 g/day of total sugar and Suc intake was associated with slightly lower T2DM risk (total sugar: RR 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–0.98; low certainty; Suc: RR 0.95, 95% CI: 0.91–<1.00; moderate certainty), whereas no significant association was observed for Fru (RR 0.98, 95% CI: 0.83–1.15; very low certainty) or added sugar (RR 0.99, 95% CI: 0.96–1.01; low certainty)36. Using Burden of Proof meta-regression methods, Haile et al.37 estimated that consumption of 1.5–390 g/day of SSB was associated with an approximately 8% higher risk of T2DM across the modeled exposure range. Choo38 conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 155 controlled intervention comparisons (n = 5086) and found that Fru-containing sugars did not show harmful effects on HbA1c, fasting Glu, or fasting insulin when exchanged isocalorically for other macronutrients. In contrast, when these sugars provided excess energy, adverse effects were observed, particularly on fasting insulin, and SSB were among the food sources associated with harmful effects. However, the overall certainty of the evidence was low. These findings imply that positive energy balance, rather than dietary sugar, is the primary driver of T2DM development. Pregnant and breastfeeding women consume, on average, more than three times the recommended amount of added sugar, exceeding 80 grams per day, and most infants and young children consume sugary foods and drinks every day. In a historical natural experiment, Gracner et al.39 reported that early-life sugar rationing was associated with approximately 35% and 20% lower risks of T2DM and hypertension, respectively, and delayed disease onset by 4 and 2 years.

Hormones related to carbohydrate metabolism

GLP-1 and GIP are incretin hormones that act on multiple cell types (including pancreatic β-cells, adipocytes, immune cells, endothelial cells, and neurons) to regulate postprandial metabolism40. Experimental and translational studies suggest that GIP may contribute to insulin resistance, fatty liver disease, and low-grade inflammation, whereas GLP-1 generally exerts protective metabolic effects41. In healthy individuals, Glu robustly stimulates secretion of both GIP and GLP-1, whereas Fru does not acutely stimulate GIP release. Interestingly, in streptozotocin (STZ)-induced models of T2DM, glu-induced GIP secretion appears augmented, and Fru can markedly stimulate GIP secretion. In dietary intervention studies comparing high-starch and high-Suc diets in mice, both diets were isocaloric with respect to carbohydrate content. Mice fed the high-starch diet gained significantly more weight, whereas the high-Suc diet (38.5% Suc by weight) paradoxically prevented obesity in that model; however, Suc feeding reduced hepatic glucokinase activity, downregulated sweet taste receptors, decreased GLP-1 secretion, and induced glu intolerance42.

Hypertension and CVD

In an observational study of adults without prior hypertension, higher Fru intake was also associated with elevated blood pressure42. Another study reported an independent association between high Fru intake and elevated blood pressure in adults without a prior history of hypertension. As summarized in prior mechanistic reviews, multiple mechanisms have been proposed by which free Fru in SSB may induce systemic hypertension42: (1) Fru stimulates intestinal sodium absorption (via cooperative actions of putative anion transporter 1 and sodium/hydrogen exchanger 3) and impairs renal sodium excretion; (2) Fru is currently the only dietary sugar known to increase uric acid production, and hyperuricemia can precipitate renal injury and endothelial dysfunction; (3) Fru-driven increases in insulin and metabolic intermediates alter endothelial function through diverse pathways; and (4) Fru promotes chronic activation of the sympathetic nervous system, contributing to blood-pressure elevation.

High intake of free sugars and SSB has been associated with several established CVD risk factors, including insulin resistance, postprandial hyperglycemia, oxidative stress, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction43. Moreover, high postprandial glycemia increases oxidative stress and inflammation, perturbs lipid metabolism and total cholesterol synthesis, and induces endothelial dysfunction and β-cell stress. These pathophysiological processes contribute to both T2DM and atherogenesis, thereby raising CVD risk43. A meta-analysis of seven prospective cohort studies found that, compared with participants consuming SSB less than once per month (≤1/month), those with higher intake (>1/day) had a 9% greater risk of CVD (RR 1.09; 95% CI: 1.01–1.18)44. A prospective study from the Harvard School of Public Health that followed 80,647 women and 37,716 men for >30 years reported that, compared with individuals consuming <1 SSB per month, those consuming two SSB per day had a 21% higher all-cause mortality risk and a 31% higher risk of CVD mortality45. Analyses of UK Biobank data (n = 110,497) by Kelly et al. showed no significant association between total carbohydrate intake and CVD, but a 5% increase in free sugar intake was associated with a 7% higher risk of CVD, a 6% higher risk of coronary heart disease, and a 10% higher risk of stroke46. A US study reported that consuming >1 serving of SSB per day (237 g) was associated with a 42% increased CVD risk (HR 1.42; 95% CI: 1.00–2.01), and >1 serving of sugar-containing soft drinks per day (355 g) with a 23% increased risk (HR 1.23; 95% CI: 1.05–1.44)47. The prevention of CVD should emphasize obesity control and overall energy balance in addition to reducing sugar intake perse.

Liver health

Hepatic lipid metabolism

Fru ingestion upregulates hepatic ketohexokinase and lipogenic enzymes, accelerating Fru metabolism and fatty acid synthesis and creating a vicious cycle of intrahepatic lipid accumulation4. Comparative studies have revealed sugar-type–dependent differences in hepatotoxicity. Softic et al.19 compared 30% (w/v) Fru and 30% (w/v) Glu solutions and observed distinct effects on body weight, metabolic phenotype, and gene expression. When combined with a high-fat diet (60% kcal from fat), Fru more potently induced obesity, Glu intolerance, and insulin resistance than Glu. Excess Fru elevated Srebp1c mRNA, whereas excess Glu preferentially increased Chrebp mRNA, suggesting Fru more strongly drives DNL while Glu influences both lipogenesis and glycolysis. The physical form of dietary sugar also modulates visceral fat accumulation: Mastrocola et al.48 found that, under equal Fru intake, liquid Fru (60% w/v) produced more pronounced hepatic steatosis and fibrosis over 12 weeks, whereas solid Fru (60% kcal) elicited stronger hepatic inflammatory responses. These findings imply that liquid forms of sugar (as in SSB) may be more deleterious than solid-food sugar, and that combining solid and liquid sugars could better model human exposure in experimental studies of metabolic dysfunction.

Prevalence of NAFLD

Researchers at Boston University reported a dose–response relationship between SSB intake and NAFLD incidence, with habitual SSB consumers exhibiting a 2.53-fold higher NAFLD prevalence49. Analysis of NHANES dietary data (n = 3739) showed that, compared with non-consumers, daily intakes of ≥72 g sugar in men and ≥50 g in women were associated with a 60% increased risk of NAFLD50. Harvard investigators using Cox proportional hazards models found that, compared with individuals consuming ≤ 3 servings/month of SSB, those consuming ≥ 1 serving/day had an 85% higher risk of liver cancer (HR 1.85; P = 0.02) and a 68% higher risk of chronic liver disease mortality (HR 1.68; P = 0.04)51. A meta-analysis by Chiu et al.52 demonstrated that high Fru intakes (104–220 g/d) raised hepatic lipid content and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) in healthy adults; however, isocaloric substitution trials indicate that swapping Fru for other carbohydrates at equal energy does not necessarily induce NAFLD, suggesting that caloric excess rather than Fru underlies some adverse effects of SSB.

Gut health

The human gastrointestinal tract harbors over 1014 microorganisms, an ecosystem that can outnumber human cells by an order of magnitude, and plays central roles in fermenting undigested carbohydrates, bile acids, and cholesterol, synthesizing vitamins (e.g., biotin and vitamin K), modulating host energy storage hormones, and resisting pathogens through competitive exclusion53,54. Critically, the gut microbiota interacts with the immune system to shape immune cell maturation and host susceptibility to diverse pathophysiological states55.

Recent work has sought to link high Fru intake with specific alterations in the gut microbiome that may mediate host pathology. Importantly, this relationship is physiologically complex, because free Fru is absorbed primarily in the small intestine rather than being directly delivered to the colon under normal conditions56. However, Fru absorption is saturable and highly variable among individuals, and high intake, particularly in liquid forms such as SSB, may exceed absorptive capacity and lead to partial malabsorption57. Under these conditions, unabsorbed Fru can reach the colon, where it may directly alter substrate availability for resident microbes58. In addition, Fru may influence the gut microbiota indirectly through effects on bile acid metabolism, intestinal permeability, mucosal immune function, and hepatic metabolites that secondarily reshape the intestinal environment59.

Bacteroidetes and Phylum Firmicutes constitute the dominant bacterial phyla in healthy adult microbiomes, and the balance between them influences mucosal immune homeostasis and epithelial integrity. Ramne et al. 60 analyzed blood, urine, and fecal samples from participants in the Malmö Offspring Study using 16S rRNA sequencing and reported a significant negative association between SSB intake and the abundance of Bacillus species, as well as a positive association with the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio. Diets rich in Fru and Glu have been shown to suppress expression of key colonization factors such as Roc, reducing the fitness and abundance of beneficial commensals like Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron61. Kawano et al. 62 demonstrated that Suc and maltodextrin in high-fat diets increased growth of taxa in the family Enterobacteriaceae, disrupted microbial homeostasis, and significantly reduced the proportion and number of Th17 cells in the small intestinal lamina propria, changes linked to obesity, metabolic syndrome, and prediabetes. Removal of dietary sugar from high-fat diets ameliorated murine obesity and reduced intestinal lipid uptake and absorption via an IL-17-dependent mechanism, thereby preventing metabolic syndrome phenotypes. Excessive sugar intake also increases susceptibility to colitis. Khan et al.63 found that high dietary Fru, Glu, or Suc increased relative abundances of mucin-degrading species (e.g., Akkermansia muciniphila) and reduced beneficial Firmicutes such as Lachnospiraceae and Lactobacillaceae in murine models.

Microbial metabolites may further mediate these host effects. Chronic high-Fru diets induce dysbiosis, compromise gut barrier integrity, and increase permeability, facilitating bacterial translocation. Microbial trimethylamine (TMA) is transported to the liver via the portal vein, where hepatic enzymes generate trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), a metabolite implicated in heightened cardiovascular risk. Using isotopic tracers, Wu et al.64 showed that gut microbes can promote hepatic lipogenesis via acetate derived from Fru through pathways independent of ATP-citrate lyase (ACLY).

Thus, high-sugar, SSB-rich diets may disrupt gut homeostasis through multiple mechanisms65,66: (1) altering mucosal architecture. High Fru intake reduces colonic mucus thickness and defensin secretion and elevates local reactive oxygen species; (2) compromising barrier function—hyperglycemia induced by high sugar intake increases osmotic stress and susceptibility to infection, thereby perturbing the microbiota and promoting translocation of bacteria and endotoxin into the circulation, which may exacerbate colorectal carcinogenesis; and (3) reshaping the gut microenvironment—changes in sugar availability drive microbial transcriptional, compositional, and adaptive genetic responses that reduce SCFA production and elevate proinflammatory signaling. Collectively, these findings support the existence of a complex sugar–gut microbiota–metabolic axis, in which Fru may influence host metabolic homeostasis through both direct colonic exposure under conditions of malabsorption and indirect effects on intestinal and hepatic physiology. Much of the mechanistic evidence in this area currently derives from animal models, whereas human data remain more limited and largely associative. Technologies for sugar reduction

Reducing sugar in foods and beverages remains technically challenging because sugar contributes not only to sweetness and caloric load, but also to multiple functional properties, including texture, viscosity, bulk, color development, stability, and flavor release. Sugar reduction strategies can be broadly categorized into three complementary approaches: compositional replacement, structural redesign, and multisensory modulation. Non-sugar sweeteners (NSS)

Among current reformulation strategies, replacement of sugar with NSS remains the most widely used approach because it can preserve sweetness while minimizing changes in other sensory attributes. This strategy has become increasingly common in response to growing consumer demand for low-sugar and sugar-free products, particularly in ultra-processed foods such as diet beverages, snacks, and other reformulated products. NSS are also widely incorporated into weight-management programs and products intended for individuals with Glu intolerance or T2DM67. Survey data indicate that NSS exposure is already substantial at the population level. China has among the highest per-capita consumption of sugar and NSS, followed by India and the United States. Approximately 48% of US adults reported consuming NSS from dietary sources between 2007 and 201268. Approved NSS have been evaluated by regulatory authorities and scientific risk-assessment bodies, including the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Commission, and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA). These evaluations have concluded that, when consumed within accepted daily intake limits, such sweeteners are considered safe and non-carcinogenic69.

A binary classification of sugar alternatives as either artificial or natural is overly simplistic. In reformulation, these compounds are more appropriately categorized as artificial high-intensity sweeteners (AS), natural high-intensity sweeteners (NS), and rare sugars or other bulk sweeteners70. AS are chemically synthesized sugar substitutes, whereas NS are generally derived from plant or microbial sources71. Rare sugars, however, constitute a distinct category: although compounds such as D-allulose and D-tagatose occur naturally in small amounts, they are typically produced for food applications through enzymatic or biotechnological conversion rather than direct extraction72. Moreover, unlike most high-intensity sweeteners, rare sugars generally exhibit lower sweetness potency while contributing to bulk, texture, and other physicochemical properties in reformulated foods.

To date, the FDA has approved 7 AS (aspartame, sucralose, neotame, saccharin, acesulfame K, advantame, and sucralose variants) and 2 NS (steviol glycosides and mogroside V) for food use73. China has approved a broader range of sweetening agents, including artificial sweeteners, polyols, natural sweeteners, and other sugar substitutes. Canada authorizes aspartame, sucralose, neotame, acesulfame K, steviol glycosides, and monk fruit extracts74. This heterogeneity is important because the technological success of a sweetener does not necessarily translate into favorable physiological or behavioral outcomes. Despite their widespread use and general regulatory acceptance, the broader health implications of NSS remain controversial. In principle, replacing sugar with NSS should reduce energy intake and improve glycemic control. However, evidence for sustained benefits in body weight regulation and metabolic health remains inconsistent. One explanation for this inconsistency is that sweet taste is not merely a sensory signal, but is closely linked to anticipatory metabolic, endocrine, and reward-related responses. Emerging mechanistic evidence also suggests that the metabolic and behavioral consequences of NSS may depend on their interactions with central and peripheral signaling pathways. For example, Yunker et al.77 examined neural and metabolic responses to high-energy food cues in healthy young adults and reported that, after ingestion of artificially sweetened beverages, participants with obesity showed greater prefrontal responses to palatable food images than after Suc intake. Female participants also exhibited increased activation in reward-related brain regions and subsequently consumed more calories following artificially sweetened beverage intake77. Some other findings also suggest that, at least under some conditions, NSS may alter the relationship between sweet taste perception and neural reward processing78,79. Such effects could contribute to persistent sweet craving, reduced satiety, or compensatory food intake, thereby limiting the anticipated benefit of sugar replacement. Chronic exposure to highly intense sweeteners has been hypothesized to modify sweetness preference thresholds, attenuate sweet taste sensitivity, or alter cephalic-phase and gut-brain signaling, although the long-term relevance of these mechanisms in habitual human consumption remains incompletely understood80.

In 2023, the WHO issued a guideline advising against the use of NSS for weight control or for reducing noncommunicable disease risk81. Importantly, this recommendation was based not on definitive evidence of harm, but on the limited and inconsistent nature of the available evidence for long-term benefit. A narrative review published in The BMJ, which informed the WHO guidance82, found only small average improvements in body weight, body mass index, fasting Glu, and blood pressure in adults when NSS were compared with sugar or placebo. In children, NSS showed little consistent effect on body weight, blood pressure, or satiety, although some analyses suggested modest attenuation of BMI gain or weight gain under specific conditions82. However, these findings were constrained by short intervention duration, small sample sizes, methodological heterogeneity, and low or very low certainty of evidence. Taken together, these limitations make it difficult to draw robust conclusions regarding the long-term clinical value of NSS substitution82.

Although some observational studies have associated long-term NSS intake with obesity, T2DM, inflammatory bowel disease exacerbation, and increased all-cause mortality50,83–88, such findings require cautious interpretation because of potential reverse causality, residual confounding, and differences in underlying dietary patterns and health status. In addition, marked heterogeneity among individual sweeteners limits the validity of broad conclusions across all NSS. Overall, current evidence supports neither a uniformly beneficial nor a uniformly harmful interpretation of NSS use. Instead, NSS appear to function as a context-dependent sugar-reduction tool whose effects are influenced by the specific compound, food matrix, target population, and duration of exposure; thus, they should be viewed as one component of a broader reformulation strategy rather than a universal solution.

Spatial distribution technologies and structural innovation

Historically, attempts to reduce sugar in processed foods have prioritized maintaining sensory attributes89. In addition to direct sugar replacement, structural and spatial reformulation strategies have emerged as promising approaches for reducing sugar while preserving perceived sweetness. Early sugar-reduction efforts in processed foods often focused on maintaining sensory acceptance, and several studies suggest that moderate reductions in sugar can be achieved without noticeable declines in consumer liking, particularly when implemented gradually. In liquid beverages, reductions of approximately 6–11% have been reported to remain below sensory detection thresholds in some settings84, indicating that stepwise sugar reduction may be a feasible initial strategy for certain product categories. Oliveira et al.90 reported difference thresholds for added sugar in chocolate-flavored milk of 6.4–8.3%, and sequential reduction to 6.7% did not noticeably change consumer perception. Another work suggests stepwise reductions of orange juice sugar to 7.2% and 5.5% are feasible91. A recent study examined consumer rejection thresholds and acceptability for six strawberry yogurts with Suc reduced from 12 to 5 g/100 g and found that, despite physicochemical changes, Suc could be reduced to 5.25 g/100 g without impairing consumer preference92. Collectively, these findings suggest that consumer sensitivity to sugar reduction is product-dependent and that moderate reformulation may be possible before sweetness loss becomes perceptually salient.

Beyond simple reduction, increasing attention has been given to spatially heterogeneous sugar distribution as a means of enhancing sweetness perception without increasing total sugar content. This approach is based on the principle that sweetness intensity is influenced not only by the absolute concentration of sugar, but also by its temporal and spatial delivery during oral processing93,94. In gel-based systems, layered structures with concentration gradients can enhance early sweetness perception compared with homogeneous formulations, likely because of differences in receptor stimulation dynamics, fracture behavior, and the nonuniform release of Suc during consumption95. Spatial approaches in gel systems can reduce Suc by up to ~20% without perceptible loss of flavor. Texture also plays an important role in this process. Because oral processing depends on the interaction between food structure and salivary transport, modifications in texture can influence the release of both tastants and aroma compounds96. For example, van der Sman et al.97 proposed a physics-based sugar-replacement concept using material plasticization and water sorption applicable to baked goods. These findings indicate that sweetness perception depends not only on sugar concentration, but also on food microstructure and oral processing behavior.

Evidence from solid and semi-solid foods further supports the potential of spatial design as a sugar-reduction strategy. Traditional Indonesian layered dessert kue lapis exemplifies how nonuniform sugar distribution enhances perceived sweetness. Dadali and Elmaci98 produced three- and four-layer chocolates with varied sugar concentrations per layer and found that chocolates with reduced sugar in internal layers (while top and bottom layers matched reference sugar levels) tasted sweeter than uniformly reduced formulations, indicating spatial heterogeneity as a novel sugar-reduction tactic. Dadali and Elmaci99 also demonstrated that uneven Suc distribution in cake formulations allowed a 15% reduction in Suc without altering perceived sweetness. This suggests that spatial heterogeneity may serve as an effective alternative or complement to direct sweetener substitution in structurally controlled products.

However, the applicability of these approaches is highly matrix-dependent. Spatial sugar distribution is easier to implement in solid or semi-solid foods, whereas its use in beverages is limited by rapid mixing and the lack of a stable internal structure. Additional approaches, such as enzymatic sugar conversion, spatial heterogeneity, and cross-modal enhancement, are emerging technologies for lowering sugar while maintaining sweetness. Enzymatic hydrolysis of lactose in dairy yields sweeter Glu, potentially replacing added Suc100. Overall, structural innovation appears most effective when tailored to specific food matrices and combined with other reformulation strategies.

Multisensory integration technologies for sugar reduction

Sweetness perception in daily life is not a purely gustatory phenomenon but a complex, cross-modal interaction among taste, olfaction, vision, audition, memory, and emotion. Olfactory–gustatory integration is critical for primary sweetness perception and relies on neural processing within chemosensory brain regions101. The human brain combines olfactory and gustatory inputs; this cross-modal interaction highlights the role of odor information in modulating multisensory sweetness experiences. Cultivating odor–sweetness associations provides feedforward cues and memory traces such that re-exposure to olfactory cues amplifies sweet experience, supporting durable sweetness perceptions and memories102. Based on customary flavor pairings, incorporation of natural aromas in reformulated products may heighten sweet perception and permit partial reduction of sugar, fat, or salt informed by flavor-memory research103. This principle has provided the basis for multisensory sugar-reduction strategies that seek to preserve sweetness perception through sensory modulation rather than direct compositional replacement.

Among the available approaches, odor-induced taste enhancement (OITE) has emerged as one of the most promising. Specific volatiles such as strawberry, vanilla, caramel, passion fruit, and lychee notes enhance perceived sweetness104. However, the magnitude of this effect is not uniform and depends on aroma identity, concentration, baseline sugar level, and food matrix. Low-concentration aromas often produce stronger sweetening effects, and aroma-induced sweetening does not necessarily increase with higher sugar concentrations105. In low-to-moderate Suc solutions, vanilla aroma yields particularly strong synergistic effects; tomato and strawberry volatiles may enhance sweetness more than single aroma use. When consistent, congruent aromas are added to certain foods, perceived sweetness increases. Gloria Wang et al.106 using ABX testing, compared congruent (vanilla) and incongruent (beef) aromas paired with Suc and observed sweetness enhancement from both, with a greater effect for vanilla. Compounds such as ethyl 2-butyrate, furaneol, and γ-decalactone have been shown to increase perceived sweetness in reduced-sugar juices107. Chen et al.108 reported that geraniol enhanced sweetness in black tea infusions. Nonetheless, the magnitude of sugar reduction achievable via aroma enhancement is generally smaller than that achievable with non-nutritive sweeteners. Sweetness enhancement through aroma has also been demonstrated in reduced-sugar juices and tea-based beverages, supporting its potential as an adjunctive tool in reformulation. Nevertheless, compared with NSS, aroma-based enhancement generally provides a more modest degree of sugar reduction and is therefore more likely to function as a complementary rather than stand-alone strategy.

Visual and textural cues can also support sweetness enhancement. Sweetness perception engages language and memory networks; olfactory associative neural signals resemble those evoked by consciously imagining odors109. Industry can exploit multimodal sensory patterns—integrating intrinsic product attributes with packaging or tableware features, to develop holistic reformulation technologies. Velasco et al.110 reported associations between red color and curvilinear shapes with sweetness; circular plates and food displays can elevate sweetness ratings by ~17%. Differences in color–aroma pairing can alter the perceived balance between sweetness and acidity even when the underlying formulation is unchanged. In an experiment where participants consumed two beverages with identical Suc and citric acid but differing in color and aroma, the red/strawberry drink was rated sweeter and less acidic than the green/lemon drink111.

Similar cross-modal effects have been reported for auditory cues, with certain phonetic characteristics and sound patterns being consistently associated with sweetness. Growing evidence highlights multimodal links between taste and sound: front vowels and voiceless consonants are perceived as sweeter than back vowels and voiced consonants112, and long vowels are anticipated to sound sweeter than short vowels113. Collectively, these studies reveal the multidimensional nature of sweetness perception—encompassing affect, memory, and cross-modal interactions—and inform a cognitive–sweetness framework to explain how cognitive styles shape food choice and health outcomes. From an industrial perspective, understanding multisensory integration and its metabolic implications will guide the design of reduced-sugar products via aroma, color, labeling, and other cues.

From a technological perspective, multisensory integration offers a distinctive advantage because it can enhance sweetness perception without necessarily altering the core composition of the product. This makes it particularly attractive for applications in which direct sugar replacement may compromise texture, flavor balance, or consumer familiarity114. At the same time, the effectiveness of multisensory strategies is inherently context-dependent. Responses to aroma, color, sound, and other sensory cues can vary according to age, culture, prior experience, dietary habits, physiological state, and individual differences in sensory sensitivity115. As a result, although multisensory technologies hold considerable promise, their practical implementation requires careful product-specific validation and methodological standardization.

Overall, the evidence reviewed in this section indicates that no single technological approach is sufficient to achieve effective sugar reduction across all foods and beverages. Instead, current strategies differ substantially in how they preserve sweetness, maintain product functionality, and perform under real-world formulation constraints. National sugar reduction strategies

Achieving healthier and more sustainable dietary patterns requires a comprehensive food systems approach that addresses the structural drivers underlying the production, promotion, and consumption of high-sugar products. In this context, sugar reduction cannot rely on a single intervention, but instead requires coordinated action across reformulation, labeling, taxation, education, and broader environmental change. Voluntary reformulation initiatives

Food reformulation, improving the nutritional quality of processed foods by replacing problematic fats and reducing salt and sugar, has been identified as a key intervention priority116. To reduce population-level sugar intake and mitigate diet-related health risks, many countries have introduced reformulation initiatives targeting processed foods and beverages. Unlike mandatory nutrient limits defined by regulatory authorities, voluntary reformulation has emerged as a widely adopted strategy among the WHO, national governments, and the food industry. Such voluntary approaches include individual corporate commitments or negotiated agreements between governments and industry with varying levels of specificity and accountability.

The United Kingdom provides one of the most prominent examples. In the United Kingdom, Public Health England launched a gradual sugar-reduction program in 2015, aiming to reduce children’s sugar intake by 20% by 2020117. A meta-analysis by Kawther et al. demonstrated that the UK’s sugar reduction strategy significantly reduced sugar consumption, body weight, and disease risk, underscoring its public health relevance118. Germany similarly introduced a National Strategy for the Reduction of Sugar, Fat, and Salt in Processed Foods119, under which beverage manufacturers committed to reducing the average sugar content of soft drinks by 15% between 2015 and 2025120. However, progress has been relatively modest: between 2015 and 2021, the sales-weighted average sugar content of soft drinks declined by 2%, while per capita daily sugar sales from soft drinks decreased from 22.4 g to 21.6 g, corresponding to a 4% reduction and remaining below the interim target of 9%121. In Brazil, the government signed reformulation agreements with 68 food manufacturers in 2018, targeting a reduction of 144,000 tons of sugar consumption by 2022122.

Collectively, these experiences indicate that voluntary reformulation can support sugar reduction at the national level, but its effectiveness is highly dependent on the ambition of targets, the extent of industry participation, and the robustness of monitoring and accountability mechanisms. Thus, while voluntary approaches may facilitate early progress and stakeholder engagement, their impact is likely to remain limited in the absence of stronger implementation frameworks.

Front-of-pack labeling and nutrient warnings

The WHO recommends implementing front-of-package nutrition labeling (FOPL) as part of a broader strategy to improve consumer understanding of nutritional quality and encourage healthier purchasing and dietary behaviors, thereby helping to prevent diet-related noncommunicable diseases (NCDs)123. One of the most widely discussed models is the Nutri-Score, developed in France in 2014, which uses a summary label ranging from A (green) to E (red) based on the content of energy, saturated fats, sugars, salt, fiber, protein, and beneficial components such as fruits, vegetables, legumes, nuts, and specific oils124. In 2023, Benedikt Merz et al. updated the Nutri-Score algorithm to better align with Food-Based Dietary Guidelines (FBDG), strengthening its potential as an internationally applicable public health tool125.

Nutrient warning labels have also been implemented in several countries to identify ultra-processed foods high in sugar, fat, and sodium, which are major contributors to obesity and other NCDs126. Studies show that such warnings effectively increase consumer awareness of the risks of excessive sugar intake and reduce purchase intentions for high-sugar, high-fat, and high-sodium products127. Chile provides a notable example, having combined nutrient warnings with marketing restrictions targeting children and banned the sale of foods and beverages high in sugars or saturated fats in schools. Evaluations following implementation showed a marked decline in purchases of labeled products, including a 36.8% reduction in sugar content (-30.4 kcal per capita per day, 95% CI: −34.5, −26.3)128.

However, in highly diversified food markets, the extent to which sugar reduction leads to product substitution across brands or categories remains unclear. This uncertainty highlights the need for stronger evidence on consumer demand responses, manufacturer incentives for reformulation, and compliance with voluntary policy frameworks129. One study using a random-coefficient logit approach found that sugar content strongly influenced consumer choice in children’s cereals, implying that reformulation may lead to some loss of market share129. Importantly, simultaneous reformulation across brands minimized this loss and was identified as the most effective strategy for reducing total sugar sold while limiting substitution effects. Overall, current evidence suggests that front-of-pack labeling and nutrient warnings can influence consumer behavior and support reformulation, but their effectiveness is shaped by market context, policy design, and industry response.

Fiscal measures and taxation

In recognition of the health and economic burden associated with SSB consumption, over 40 countries have introduced sugar-based excise taxes, either by volume, sugar content, or as ad valorem taxes (a percentage of wholesale or retail prices), to reduce obesity, overweight, and diabetes prevalence130. Modeling studies show significant benefits. In Brazil, a 20% tax could reduce obesity prevalence by 6.7%, preventing millions of cases and saving substantial healthcare costs131. In the UK, mandatory taxation decreased total sugar sales by 34.3%, whereas voluntary reduction programs achieved only a 3.5% decrease (despite a 20% target)132. In Canada, implementing mandatory FOPL led to daily sugar reductions of 2.3–8.7 g per person, potentially preventing 2183–8907 diet-related NCD deaths (approximately 70% from CVD)123. German modeling suggests that a 20% ad valorem tax could yield 106,000 quality-adjusted life years and reduce national NCD burdens133, while in Brazil, a 20–30% SSB tax could lower obesity prevalence by 6.7–9.1% and reduce obesity-related healthcare costs by USD 257–346 million over ten years133. Simulations in Hong Kong indicate that even a 10% tax could significantly reduce obesity prevalence over time134.

The impact of sugar taxation across socioeconomic groups is multifaceted. Although such taxes may be financially regressive, as lower-income households often spend a larger share of their income on taxed beverages or foods, these groups are also disproportionately affected by diet-related non-communicable diseases and are generally more responsive to price changes135,136. Consequently, sugar taxation may lead to greater reductions in sugar consumption and larger health gains in these groups, suggesting that, despite its potentially regressive economic burden, it may still confer progressive benefits from a public health perspective137,138. Nevertheless, such outcomes are highly contingent on policy design, implementation, market response, and the presence of complementary interventions139. Overall, sugar taxation should not be viewed as a stand-alone remedy, but rather as one component of a broader and equity-oriented strategy for reducing sugar consumption at the population level.

Dietary guidelines and public education

In addition to fiscal and labeling policies, many national dietary guidelines have incorporated sugar reduction recommendations across life stages. Nearly all guidelines advise checking food labels, choosing low- or no-sugar foods, substituting sugary drinks with water, and limiting the portion size and frequency of SSB consumption140. When consuming juice, 100% fruit juice without added sugar is recommended, along with unsweetened canned fruits, fruit purees, and yogurts141. In cooking, recipes often advise halving added sugar or using natural fruit sweetness in jams, meringues, and ice creams142. Notably, these recommendations are not fully consistent across countries: whereas the United Kingdom supports the use of sweeteners in products such as beverages and breakfast cereals, Italy discourages the use of AS143.

Because early-life dietary exposure strongly influences lifelong taste preferences, limiting sugar intake during childhood and adolescence is crucial. Data from the UK’s National Diet and Nutrition Survey show that free sugar intake exceeds recommendations across all age groups144. In the United States, only 33% of children and adolescents (aged 2–19 years) meet the dietary guidelines for sugar intake145. However, education alone is unlikely to produce sustained behavioral change unless it is reinforced by supportive environments in schools, communities, and workplaces that make healthier choices easier and more accessible.

Intervention studies provide some support for this view. The “DRINK” trial demonstrated that school-based interventions promoting hydration, providing educational materials, and encouraging reusable drinkware effectively reduced SSB consumption and increased water intake, highlighting the importance of environmental modification146. In the UK, Public Health England’s national campaign Sugar Smart successfully reduced sugar intake among children aged 5–11 by an average of 6.2 g per day (SD = 43.8), though the effect was not sustained over time147. These findings suggest that public education can contribute to short-term improvements in sugar-related behaviors, but may have limited long-term effectiveness when implemented in isolation.

Governments have also targeted sugar reduction in children’s breakfast cereals, which remain among the highest-sugar products148. A 2023 analysis of UK cereals (n = 133) found that 81% still exceeded the 12.3 g/100 g target, and nearly 40% contained one-third of the daily recommended free sugar intake (19 g/day) for children aged 4–6 years in a single 30 g serving149. These findings highlight the urgent need for mandatory and comprehensive sugar reduction policies to effectively lower sugar consumption among children149. Regulatory frameworks should ensure that child-directed marketing practices are preferentially applied to the healthiest products, thereby helping to shape healthier food preferences and dietary behaviors from an early age. Dietary guidelines and public education remain essential components of national sugar-reduction strategies, but their long-term impact depends on the extent to which they are supported by structural, environmental, and regulatory measures.

Conclusions

SSB represents a major and multifactorial challenge to public health. As discussed throughout this review, the adverse effects of excessive SSB consumption cannot be explained solely by excess caloric intake, but are also shaped by the distinct metabolic handling of Fru, its capacity to promote hepatic lipogenesis, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and ectopic fat deposition, and its interaction with neurobehavioral pathways that reinforce sweet preference and habitual intake. Emerging evidence further suggests that the consequences of high sugar exposure may extend beyond classical metabolic disorders to include alterations in gut microbiota and broader mental and behavioral outcomes. Taken together, the current literature indicates that the health burden associated with SSB consumption arises from the interaction of metabolic, sensory, behavioral, and environmental factors, underscoring that excessive sugar intake is not merely a nutritional issue, but a broader food-system and public health challenge.

This complexity also explains why sugar reduction remains both technically and biologically challenging. Sugar contributes not only to sweetness, but also to texture, bulk, stability, flavor release, and overall consumer acceptance. Accordingly, no single reformulation strategy appears sufficient across products, food matrices, and population groups. NSS remains the most widely used tool for reducing sugar, yet its long-term metabolic and behavioral effects remain incompletely resolved. Structural approaches, including gradual sugar reduction and spatial distribution technologies, as well as multisensory approaches based on aroma and other perceptual cues, provide additional opportunities to reduce sugar while maintaining sweetness. However, many of these strategies have shown matrix-specific or short-term benefits, whereas their broader translational value and long-term effectiveness remain uncertain. Overall, the evidence reviewed here suggests that the most promising path forward lies not in any single replacement approach, but in the integration of compositional, structural, and perceptual strategies within product-specific reformulation frameworks.

Because the drivers of excessive sugar intake operate across both product design and consumer environments, technological innovation alone is unlikely to achieve sustained reductions in sugar intake without supportive policy and environmental change. Voluntary reformulation, front-of-pack labeling, nutrient warnings, taxation, and public education can each contribute to reducing sugar exposure, but their effects are often partial and less durable when implemented in isolation. In general, stronger and more sustained public health benefits are more likely when these interventions are combined within a broader food-systems framework that aligns regulation, market incentives, consumer information, and healthier food environments. At the same time, the effectiveness and equity of such measures remain context-dependent and are shaped by socioeconomic conditions, local dietary patterns, industry structure, and implementation strength. Thus, effective sugar reduction should be understood not as a single technological fix or policy action, but as a coordinated, multilevel strategy.

Important gaps nevertheless remain. In particular, the long-term health consequences of sugar replacement, the durability of sensory adaptation, and the real-world equity effects of policy interventions remain insufficiently resolved. Future research should therefore prioritize long-term human evidence capable of clarifying causal pathways, population heterogeneity, and the health impacts of both sugar reduction and sugar replacement strategies. Greater mechanistic integration is also needed to understand how metabolic responses, sensory perception, learned preference, and food environments interact over time. At the same time, future sugar-reduction strategies are likely to move toward more intelligent and sustainable design, incorporating advances in sweetener discovery, controlled flavor delivery, precision reformulation, and behavioral interventions that support long-term adaptation to lower sweetness levels. Ultimately, progress in this field will depend on closer integration of nutrition science, food technology, sensory science, behavioral research, and public health policy. Such an interdisciplinary and context-sensitive approach will be essential for developing sugar-reduction strategies that are not only effective, but also equitable, acceptable, and sustainable.
