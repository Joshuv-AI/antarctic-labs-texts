Refining surveillance of hepatocellular carcinoma in chronic hepatitis B through biomarker-based risk stratification
Authors: Tai-Chung Tseng, Shang-Chin Huang, Jia-Horng Kao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) accounting for more than half of the cases. Regular surveillance, including abdominal ultrasonography with alpha-fetoprotein testing every 6 months, enables early tumor detection and curative treatment; however, current guideline recommendations based mainly on age, sex, and race/ethnicity insufficiently capture individual risk variation, particularly among patients with CHB who do not have cirrhosis. In addition, real-world adherence to HCC surveillance is suboptimal—fewer than 40% of eligible patients receive surveillance at the recommended 6-month intervals. To improve both efficiency and impact, surveillance resources should be reallocated to patients whose estimated risk exceeds the cost-effective surveillance threshold. Recent studies support biomarker-based risk stratification as a more precise approach. For example, in untreated CHB, HBeAg-negative patients meeting inactive disease criteria with HBsAg <100 IU/mL have an annual HCC incidence below the 0.2% cost-effectiveness threshold, suggesting that routine surveillance may be unnecessary. In antiviral-treated CHB, models such as PAGE-B and its derivatives can accurately identify low-risk patients who may safely forgo HCC surveillance. Conversely, for patients at extremely high risk, including those with cirrhosis, intensified strategies—such as abbreviated MRI or combined biomarker algorithms—may enhance early detection. Collectively, these findings support a transition from a categorical, one-size-fits-all approach toward a precision surveillance paradigm that tailors the need for and intensity of HCC surveillance to biomarker-defined risk, thereby optimizing resource use, improving adherence, and maximizing clinical benefit. Nevertheless, further large-scale, prospective studies across diverse populations are needed to validate biomarker thresholds and confirm the long-term safety of exempting very-low-risk patients with CHB from HCC surveillance.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a major cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with chronic hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection accounting for at least half of the cases globally and up to 70%–80% in endemic regions.123 In addition to lowering HCC risk by long-term antiviral treatment with nucleos(t)ide analogue (NA),4567 surveillance for early detection of HCC is a cornerstone of HBV management, as curative HCC therapies are most effective at an early stage and have been shown to improve survival.123 However, the current paradigm of HCC surveillance in HBV infection has important limitations, particularly in patients without cirrhosis.

Existing international guidelines, including those from the American Association for the Study of Liver Diseases (AASLD),8 the European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL),9 the Asian Pacific Association for the Study of the Liver (APASL),10 and the World Health Organization (WHO),11 recommend semi-annual surveillance with ultrasound and serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), for patients with increased HCC risk. In patients with chronic hepatitis B (CHB) and cirrhosis, this recommendation is well justified by consistent evidence of elevated annual HCC risk. For patients with CHB who do not have cirrhosis and are not receiving antiviral treatment, however, guidance relies heavily on broad demographic factors such as race/ethnicity (Asian or African individuals), sex, and age thresholds, rather than individualized risk. These categorical recommendations, though pragmatic, do not fully capture the heterogeneity of HCC risk within the HBV population without cirrhosis. As a result, some patients undergo surveillance despite having HCC risk well below the cost-effectiveness threshold (estimated at ~0.2% per year),8912 while others who exceed this risk threshold do not meet current eligibility criteria and therefore often remain unscreened.

This review will examine current evidence and emerging tools that can refine HCC surveillance strategies in patients with CHB. We will first summarize the limitations of current guideline criteria, then discuss novel HBV biomarkers and non-invasive fibrosis tests that improve HCC risk prediction. Finally, we will outline the key evidence gaps that need to be addressed to translate these findings into clinical practice and propose how such biomarkers can be integrated to identify individuals with minimal HCC risk who may not require HCC surveillance, as well as those at very high risk who may benefit from enhanced strategies. Our goal is to provide a framework for shifting from categorical, one-size-fits-all recommendations toward a precision surveillance paradigm that optimizes efficacy, cost-effectiveness, and patient burden in the management of CHB.

THE CURRENT RECOMMENDATION CRITERIA AND ADHERENCE

All the clinical practice guidelines recommend semi-annual surveillance with ultrasound and AFP for all adults with cirrhosis and for selected patients with CHB who do not have cirrhosis at increased HCC risk (Table 1). Increased HCC risk in patients without cirrhosis has different definitions among different guidelines, including Asian men older than 40 years, Asian women older than 50 years, individuals from Africa (often beginning in their 20s), those with a first-degree family history of HCC, or persons with HDV infection.813 Similarly, the WHO 2024 HBV guidance recommends ultrasound with AFP every 6 months, emphasizing programmatic feasibility and integration into primary care. The highest-risk groups are those with cirrhosis or a family history of HCC, while people older than 40 years (a lower age threshold may apply depending on regional HCC incidence) and those with HBV DNA levels >20,000 IU/mL may also be considered for surveillance.11

Despite these clear guideline recommendations, underuse and poor adherence to HCC surveillance remain widespread globally. Multiple studies have shown that fewer than 40% of eligible patients undergo surveillance at the recommended 6-month intervals.1415 Barriers exist at multiple levels, including patient-level factors (such as limited awareness, logistical challenges, and financial constraints), provider-level factors (such as subspecialty and knowledge gaps), and system-level barriers (including inadequate insurance coverage).11617 Furthermore, significant global heterogeneity exists regarding these barriers; for instance, varying reimbursement policies and resource availability across the Asia-Pacific region create unique challenges for surveillance implementation.18 Given these complex hurdles, efforts must extend beyond simply increasing patient and physician awareness; reallocating surveillance resources to patients who truly need HCC surveillance is a critical step toward improving both efficiency and clinical impact.

LIMITATIONS OF APPLYING THE CURRENT CRITERIA TO PATIENTS WITHOUT ANTIVIRAL TREATMENT

Although sex and age are simple and practical screening criteria for patients with CHB not receiving treatment as recommended by the AASLD, APASL, and WHO guidelines,81011 several concerns remain regarding their clinical applicability.

First, age and sex are not the only risk factors for HCC development in patients without cirrhosis. Viral factors, such as HBV DNA and hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) levels, play important roles in disease progression.1920 For example, the HCC risk is heterogeneous even for older patients recommended for HCC surveillance by current guidelines.81011

A second challenge concerns age-based surveillance criteria. The AASLD and APASL guidelines, for instance, recommend surveillance for Asian men over 40 years and Asian women over 50 years.810 However, maintaining long-term adherence to HCC surveillance is a universal challenge in clinical practice. Relying solely on age thresholds creates a specific logistical hurdle, as patients who are currently excluded from surveillance based on age must be successfully retained in care until they reach the age cutoff to initiate surveillance. Thus, an “ideal” criterion for exempting patients from HCC surveillance should ensure that their annual HCC risk remains consistently below 0.2% even after reaching currently recommended age thresholds (see Section “Application of HBsAg criteria to identify individuals with minimal HCC risk among patients with inactive CHB” for a detailed discussion).

HBV risk scores in treatment-naïve patients with CHB

Since HCC development is influenced by host, viral, and hepatic factors, several risk calculators have been developed to predict HCC risk in untreated patients with CHB by integrating readily available clinical and virologic variables, including age, sex, serum alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and HBV DNA levels, hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg) status, and the presence of cirrhosis.2122 These risk calculators are valuable in 2 domains: guiding the timing of antiviral therapy and informing the need for HCC surveillance. Conceptually, when the predicted annual HCC incidence is higher than 0.2% per year, the conventional cost-effectiveness threshold, surveillance becomes justifiable.

The REACH-B score is one of the widely validated tools.23 Developed from community-based Asian cohorts, it estimates 3-year, 5-year, and 10-year HCC risks based on age, sex, ALT, HBeAg, and HBV DNA. It showed that even young patients without cirrhosis but with viremia may accumulate substantial long-term risk, highlighting the importance of earlier treatment and the need for HCC surveillance. The CU-HCC score integrates liver function reserve markers (albumin, bilirubin), cirrhosis, and HBV DNA, and has particular strength in stratifying HCC incidence.24 An evolution of this model, the LSM-HCC score, replaced the binary assessment of cirrhosis with liver stiffness measurement (LSM) by transient elastography, which improved discrimination and enabled more accurate identification of treatment-naïve patients with low risk.25 The GAG-HCC score adds viral genome information (basal core promoter mutations) to age, sex, HBV DNA, and cirrhosis, highlighting the role of viral genetics in augmenting oncogenic risk.26 These tools consistently emphasize that high HBV DNA and cirrhosis dominate risk stratification, while demographic factors further influence the overall risk level. However, it is important to note that the predictive performance of these risk scores can be variable. External validation studies have frequently shown reduced accuracy when these models were applied to cohorts different from the derivation populations, limiting their universal applicability and underscoring the need for caution in routine clinical practice. For a detailed review of the advantages and limitations of prediction models applied to patients with CHB who are not receiving antiviral therapy, we refer readers to the work by Hsu et al.21

Clinically, HBV risk calculators support two decision points in patients not receiving treatment. First, for treatment initiation, patients with high predicted HCC risk often have high HBV DNA levels, elevated ALT, or significant liver fibrosis, and should receive antiviral therapy immediately. Once treated, however, the performance of these treatment-naïve models declines,212227 since viral suppression and ALT normalization fundamentally alter risk trajectories. This observation highlights the importance of separate treatment-based risk scores, which will be addressed in subsequent sections.

Second, for HCC surveillance: if the calculated annual risk is lower than 0.2%, surveillance is not cost-effective. However, applying this threshold in practice remains challenging. Risk prediction relies on different scoring systems, each yielding point estimates with 95% confidence intervals (CI). If the 0.2% benchmark falls within the CIs, it is uncertain whether a patient’s true risk lies below the threshold, even if the point estimate itself is <0.2%. For example, the annual HCC risk of patients with inactive CHB and HBsAg >1000 IU/mL is 0.17% per year (95% CI: 0.12%–0.24%). In such grey-zone situations, excluding patients from surveillance may be premature, or at least shared decision-making should be employed, balancing potential benefits and potential financial and psychological harms of surveillance.28 Only patients whose estimated HCC risk, including the upper bound of the 95% CI, falls below the surveillance threshold could reasonably be exempted from HCC surveillance. However, few prediction models have specifically examined this criterion with independent validation.

Application of HBsAg criteria to identify individuals with minimal HCC risk among patients with inactive CHB

A considerable overlap exists between HCC surveillance criteria and antiviral treatment indications. A common concern regarding exempting patients from HCC surveillance is whether they might still require monitoring to identify future treatment indications. In Asia, patients with CHB typically undergo follow-up every 6 months, including blood tests for viral load, AFP, and liver function, as well as abdominal ultrasonography. Therefore, if patients who meet the criteria for exemption from HCC surveillance have a very low likelihood of subsequently developing treatment indications, they can safely forgo regular follow-up without compromising clinical safety.

Recent evidence from large, long-term Taiwanese cohorts provides critical insights into this issue.29 Instead of analyzing the overall CHB population, this study specifically focused on patients with inactive CHB, who are characterized by normal ALT, low HBV DNA levels, and minimal fibrosis, and consequently low HCC risk according to the current HCC risk calculators.2122

In the ERADICATE-B and REVEAL-HBV studies, a subgroup comprising 2674 patients with inactive CHB who were followed for a median of more than 26 years, lower serum HBsAg levels were associated with decreased HCC risk.29 Among 989 patients with inactive CHB and HBsAg <100 IU/mL, the annual incidence of HCC was 0.08% (95% CI: 0.05%–0.13%), well below the 0.2% threshold, and the HCC risk was comparable to individuals without chronic viral hepatitis after propensity score matching. By contrast, patients with HBsAg ≥100 IU/mL had increased HCC risk, with an age-adjusted and sex-adjusted hazard ratio (HR) up to 2.8 (95% CI: 1.6-4.8) compared with the low-HBsAg group. Importantly, these findings were validated in an independent hospital-based cohort, where patients with HBsAg <100 IU/mL had a similarly low annual incidence of 0.09% (95% CI: 0.05%–0.15%). These results address the primary concern by showing that the annual HCC risk was validated to be below 0.2% among Asian patients with inactive CHB and HBsAg <100 IU/mL.

The clinical implications could be substantial. Approximately 40% of patients with inactive CHB have HBsAg levels below 100 IU/mL, corresponding to roughly one-fifth of the total CHB population, given that inactive carriers constitute about half of the community-based cohorts.19 This subgroup maintained a very low annual HCC incidence of 0.11% (95% CI: 0.06%–0.19%) even among older patients who would otherwise meet AASLD and APASL HCC surveillance criteria,81029 suggesting that HBsAg <100 IU/mL represents a more biologically grounded marker than age alone. Moreover, HBsAg levels remained relatively stable over time, with more than 90% of patients persisting within the same category.29 Among those who did change, most showed a further decline in HBsAg, while only a very small fraction transitioned from non-candidates to candidates for HCC surveillance. Collectively, these findings provide strong evidence that patients with inactive CHB meeting this criterion can be exempted from HCC surveillance according to the current threshold, addressing concerns about initiating surveillance in the future as the patients age.

Finally, lower serum HBsAg levels are not only associated with lower HCC risk but also with a better clinical outcome.30313233 For example, in patients with inactive CHB, HBsAg level <1000 IU/mL has been linked to a lower risk of immune-active phase transition and disease progression in both Asian and Caucasian patients.303132 Similarly, in 482 patients with inactive CHB with HBsAg <100 IU/mL from China, only 4 patients (0.8%) progressed phase transition to HBeAg-negative immune-active phase after a median follow-up of 2 years.33 Taken together, these data indicate that patients with low HBsAg levels face both negligible risk of HCC and minimal risk of entering the immune-active phase, suggesting that the likelihood of missing a treatment indication in the absence of regular follow-up is exceedingly low. However, further long-term data are needed to fully validate the safety of this monitoring-free approach. Therefore, shared decision-making could be recommended to determine whether regular clinical monitoring and HCC surveillance can be safely discontinued.

In summary, among patients with HBeAg-negative CHB who fulfill the following criteria—normal ALT, HBV DNA <2000 IU/mL, minimal liver fibrosis, and HBsAg <100 IU/mL (based on at least one set of laboratory tests, although documentation of persistence provides higher confidence)—their annual HCC risk remains below the established surveillance threshold even as they age. These patients also show a low likelihood of transitioning to active CHB and have stable or declining HBsAg levels over time, indicating that their HCC risk remains consistently low. Although “inactive” CHB does not imply an absolute absence of risk, the residual HCC risk in this subgroup is comparable to that of the general population.29 However, further external validation in diverse populations with longer follow-up is required to fully confirm the safety of this strategy. Shared decision-making between physicians and patients is therefore recommended to determine whether continued HCC surveillance is necessary when HBsAg levels are <100 IU/mL.

HCC RISK STRATIFICATION IN NA-TREATED CHB

With the advent of potent NAs, the outcome of CHB has changed. Long-term viral suppression reduces inflammation and cirrhosis progression, thereby lowering the risk of HCC.567 Under these circumstances, HCC prediction models in untreated patients become inaccurate because HBV DNA levels and HBeAg are no longer discriminative. To address this, treatment-based risk scores were developed. For a detailed review of the pros and cons of prediction models for patients with CHB receiving NA therapy, we refer readers to the work by Hsu et al.21

The PAGE-B score is the most established tool, using only platelet count, age, and gender. It was originally developed to predict 5-year HCC risk among Caucasian patients on NA therapy, has been validated in Asian patients, and retains good discriminative performance during longer-term follow-up beyond 5 years.34353637 Its simplicity and excellent negative predictive value make it attractive for identifying low-risk patients (PAGE-B <10) who may not require HCC surveillance, and it has been adopted by EASL and AASLD guidelines.89 The CAMD score integrated age, sex, cirrhosis, and diabetes mellitus (DM), because diabetes is an important risk factor for HCC development.38 The REAL-B score incorporated seven variables, including age, alcohol use, diabetes, baseline cirrhosis, platelet count, and AFP levels, reflecting the contribution of metabolic and lifestyle factors.39

Since the PAGE-B score is the most widely used model, several modified versions have been proposed in Asia to improve its prediction performance. For example, the modified PAGE-B model incorporates albumin.40 While albumin levels are usually normal in patients without cirrhosis, they may help identify patients with cirrhosis who are at the highest risk of HCC. While this model cannot exempt patients from surveillance—given that all patients with cirrhosis require monitoring—it is valuable for identifying extremely high-risk individuals who may benefit from intensified surveillance strategies, as discussed in the section “Patients with extremely high HCC risk.”

Another model, PAGED-B, incorporates HBV DNA and DM as additional factors and is specific for patients with HBeAg-positive CHB. This model has been validated in both Asian and European cohorts.41 In this model, extremely high HBV DNA levels (>100 million IU/mL) were associated with a lower HCC risk than moderately elevated levels. This counterintuitive observation may reflect that extremely high baseline viremia is generally seen in patients with early-stage HBeAg-positive CHB—close to the immune-tolerant phase with limited liver fibrosis and low HCC risk.

Fibrosis parameters have also been integrated into PAGE-B. For example, adding Mac-2 binding protein glycosylation isomer (M2BPGi) to PAGE-B improved risk stratification: in 2 independent Taiwanese cohorts, an M2BPGi cutoff of 1.73 successfully differentiated HCC risk within the intermediate PAGE-B group.42 This suggests that some patients classified as intermediate risk by PAGE-B may not need continued HCC surveillance. The SAGE-B and CAGE-B models, which incorporate age and liver stiffness measurement by vibration-controlled transient elastography after 5 years of NA therapy,37 may outperform the PAGE-B model in predicting HCC development beyond 5 years of treatment.43 However, unlike routine parameters such as albumin, M2BPGi, and liver stiffness measurement are not universally available, limiting the widespread implementation of such modified scores.

Collectively, these treatment-based models provide a pragmatic framework for risk stratification in the NA era. One of their main values lies in distinguishing very-low-risk patients, whose annual HCC risk falls below the 0.2% cost-effectiveness threshold for HCC surveillance, from those whose risk remains above 0.2% warranting lifelong surveillance. Notably, unlike the original PAGE-B model, which has been validated in both Caucasian and Asian cohorts, many of the modified PAGE-B models have primarily been evaluated in Asian populations. Therefore, further validation in non-Asian cohorts is required before these modified scores can be widely recommended for global use.

HCC SURVEILLANCE AFTER HBsAg SEROCLEARANCE

Whether HCC surveillance should continue after HBsAg loss remains controversial. Evidence suggests that patients with CHB who achieve HBsAg seroclearance still carry a residual risk of HCC, particularly when seroclearance occurs later in life, in the presence of cirrhosis, family history of HCC, alcohol consumption, or in men. For instance, a large Korean cohort study showed that even after HBsAg loss, the annual HCC incidence remained above the conventional surveillance threshold.44 Similarly, a cohort study showed that among 4568 patients with CHB who cleared HBsAg, cumulative 5-year HCC incidence reached 1.5%, with age >50 years (adjusted HR ~4.3) and male gender (HR ~2.5) being independent predictors of HCC post-HBsAg seroclearance. In this cohort, females ≤50 years had essentially zero HCC incidence over 5 years.45 Another cohort study of 9769 patients who lost HBsAg found that over a median follow-up of 4.6 years, 106 (1.1%) developed HCC, and the annual HCC incidence after HBsAg seroclearance remained stable over time.46 It should be noted that all studies included patients with cirrhosis, who are recommended to undergo HCC surveillance regardless of their HBsAg status. Additional data are needed to clarify whether patients without cirrhosis who have achieved HBsAg clearance and have normal ALT levels and minimal liver fibrosis have HCC risk below the threshold for surveillance.

HCC SURVEILLANCE IN PATIENTS WITH RESOLVED HBV INFECTION

Individuals with resolved HBV infection (HBsAg-negative, anti–HBc-positive) without a documented history of chronic HBV infection generally display relatively low HCC incidence. A meta-analysis indicated a modest increase in HCC risk compared with uninfected controls, with a pooled relative risk of 1.67 (95% CI: 1.44–1.95);47 however, absolute rates typically fall below surveillance cost-effectiveness thresholds, in the absence of additional risk factors. For example, in Taiwan, where anti-HBc positivity is prevalent, the incidence of HCC in individuals with no ongoing HBV or HCV infection was ~47.2 per 100,000 person-years.48 Crucially, even in the highest-risk age group (60–70 y), the incidence was 120.2 (95% CI: 91.3–155.4) per 100,000 person-years, well below the cost-effectiveness threshold (200 per 100,000 person-years).48 Importantly, alcohol consumption, heart disease, and DM increased the risk of HCC. Based on these results, HCC surveillance is not universally recommended unless additional risk factors exist.

EVIDENCE GAPS AND PERSPECTIVES

Patients with inactive CHB who may not require HCC surveillance using HBsAg as a criterion

Although evidence suggests that patients with inactive CHB and HBsAg <100 IU/mL have an annual HCC incidence below 0.2%,29 these findings are currently based on limited studies. They have not yet been validated in purely community-based populations or across different racial/ethnic groups (eg, Western cohorts). Therefore, further external validation in independent and diverse cohorts is essential to confirm the generalizability of this strategy before broad clinical implementation.

Studies indicate that patients with CHB who achieve HBsAg <100 IU/mL after NA therapy, compared with those with HBsAg >100 IU/mL, are not only at lower risk of clinical relapse but also have a higher likelihood of HBsAg clearance after treatment discontinuation.4950 These patients may represent a state of sustained viral control similar to those with inactive CHB. However, whether their HCC risk approaches that of patients who spontaneously achieve HBsAg <100 IU/mL—and whether they can likewise be exempted from HCC surveillance—remains to be determined.

Finally, for patients attaining HBsAg <100 IU/mL through novel antiviral therapies that directly suppress HBsAg production,5152 additional studies are needed to determine the durability of treatment-induced HBsAg <100 IU/mL, and to clarify whether these patients share the similarly minimal HCC risk observed in those who achieve it spontaneously.

More tools are needed to predict the long-term HCC risk

More viral biomarkers

In patients with CHB, hepatocarcinogenesis is likely driven by a combination of host, viral, and liver factors. Among the liver factors, the role of liver fibrosis has been extensively studied. However, 2 additional contributors also play a role in carcinogenesis. The first one is the accumulation of DNA mutations resulting from repeated hepatocyte injury and regeneration. However, directly assessing cumulative DNA damage in hepatocytes is not feasible without access to liver tissue.

Another contributor is HBV integration, which promotes genomic instability and contributes to carcinogenesis.5354 Since HBsAg can be derived from both covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) and integrated HBV DNA, HBsAg level may potentially serve as a surrogate for the burden of viral integration, particularly in patients with HBeAg-negative CHB and low HBV DNA levels, in whom HBsAg production is primarily integration-driven.55 To achieve a more precise assessment, additional biomarkers such as hepatitis B core-related antigen or HBV RNA can be used to gauge cccDNA transcriptional activity, thereby allowing for the estimation of the specific HBsAg fraction derived from integrated HBV DNA.

AI-based tools to stratify HCC risk

With so many factors contributing to HCC risk, AI-based models might predict HCC risk more accurately than conventional models.56 In addition to baseline biomarkers, biomarker kinetics may offer additional insights; however, incorporating these dynamic changes also increases model complexity, further underscoring the need for AI-based approaches.

Global validation

Most risk scores have been derived from specific ethnic cohorts; global validation across diverse populations (Asian, Western, and African) is essential before they can be universally adopted.

Different HCC surveillance strategies in patients in different clinical settings

The current surveillance strategy is abdominal ultrasonography with AFP semiannually for patients who need HCC surveillance. However, the most important issue for surveillance is adherence, and the surveillance strategy should be adjusted according to different clinical settings and risk.

Patients in the community

In patients with CHB managed in community settings, access to abdominal ultrasonography is often limited, and adherence to image-based surveillance is challenging. Therefore, alternative surveillance strategies should be considered. Composite scores such as GALAD [integrating gender, age, AFP, AFP-L3, and des-g-carboxyprothrombin (DCP)] and ASAP (incorporating age, sex, AFP, and DCP) have shown superior sensitivity, specificity, and cost-effectiveness compared with AFP alone.575859 In a meta-analysis predominantly comprising phase 2 case–control studies, their pooled sensitivity for detecting early HCC was found to be comparable to that of abdominal ultrasonography.60 Therefore, prospective validation comparing biomarker panels (such as GALAD) versus ultrasound-based surveillance is currently underway in a large randomized trial to confirm their clinical utility in patients with cirrhosis or chronic HBV infection.61

Beyond protein biomarkers, liquid biopsy techniques utilizing circulating tumor DNA methylation signatures are emerging as powerful tools for early HCC detection. For instance, a multi-target blood test based on methylated DNA markers has demonstrated high sensitivity for detecting early-stage HCC, outperforming AFP alone.62 These molecular assays may offer a highly sensitive, non-invasive alternative to current surveillance tests in the future.

Blood-based surveillance offers several advantages: it does not require hospital visits, and test results are consistent regardless of who performs the tests.63 Thus, patients in the community could adopt a sequential surveillance strategy, whereby blood biomarkers are monitored regularly, and imaging studies are arranged only when “red flags” arise from abnormal biomarker results. These advantages provide a strong rationale for improved adherence and cost-effectiveness in community-based HCC surveillance programs. However, we acknowledge that the sensitivity of AFP alone is suboptimal, and access to advanced biomarkers (eg, AFP-L3, DCP, GALAD, and ASAP) is currently limited in many resource-constrained areas. Therefore, while blood-based surveillance offers logistical advantages, its successful implementation in community settings depends on further evidence showing its clinical benefit in real-world populations, as well as the future availability of cost-effective assays.

Patients with extremely high HCC risk

Another important clinical setting involves patients with CHB and cirrhosis. Although NA therapy can reduce the incidence of HCC by approximately half, these patients remain at substantially elevated risk. For example, patients with cirrhosis and low albumin levels (as reflected in the modified PAGE-B score) or with higher M2BPGi levels have a higher HCC risk than other patients with cirrhosis.4042 In addition, the recently described PAaM score (prognostic liver secretome signature with AFP plus age, male sex, albumin–bilirubin, and platelets) has shown promise in stratifying HCC risk in patients with liver cirrhosis.64 However, extensive global validation of such novel biomarkers in patients with CHB and cirrhosis is still required.

Optimizing surveillance in this extremely high-risk population is therefore crucial to enable early diagnosis and curative treatment. Several strategies have been proposed to improve surveillance effectiveness in this group.

Surveillance tool: The first-line modality remains abdominal ultrasound combined with serum AFP measurement. However, the diagnostic accuracy of abdominal ultrasonography may be compromised in patients with obesity, marked nodular cirrhosis, or steatosis.

In such patients, an abbreviated MRI can be considered as an alternative or complementary modality.6566 Simulation studies and recent EASL policy statements suggest that MRI-based precision surveillance provides favorable cost-effectiveness when reserved for extremely high-risk groups.67 In fact, physicians appear receptive to precision-based MRI surveillance over the current “one-size-fits-all” strategy,68 supporting a shift toward risk-stratified surveillance.

Use of biomarker panels: Integrating biomarker algorithms can further enhance early detection in extremely high-risk patients. The combination of GALAD + ultrasound provides the highest diagnostic accuracy and may represent the optimal surveillance strategy where resources permit,69 if further studies confirm their clinical benefit and cost-effectiveness.

Surveillance interval: Current guidelines recommend a 6-month interval for HCC surveillance. This is based on the randomized trial by Trinchet et al.,70 which showed that shortening the ultrasound surveillance interval to 3 months did not improve the detection of small HCC or survival compared with the standard 6-month interval. However, this finding was specific to ultrasound-based surveillance. Given the superior sensitivity of new serum marker panels, future studies are warranted to investigate whether a 3-month interval utilizing biomarker panel-based surveillance could provide a clinical benefit in these extremely high-risk populations.

Identification of patients who may not need surveillance: Complementary to identifying those with extremely high HCC risk, a critical future goal is to identify patients with “low-risk cirrhosis” (eg, those with regression of fibrosis) who might safely be exempted from HCC surveillance. However, robust data are currently lacking to define this subgroup safely.

Summary of unmet needs and proposed strategy

In summary, realizing the full potential of precision-based HCC surveillance requires addressing specific limitations in current knowledge, particularly regarding patients with inactive CHB, the application of novel biomarkers, and the optimal surveillance strategies for patients with extremely high HCC risk. CONCLUSIONS

HCC remains a major cause of mortality among patients with CHB, underscoring the need for more precise surveillance strategies. Current age-based and sex-based guidelines insufficiently capture the heterogeneity of HCC risk among patients without cirrhosis. Emerging evidence supports biomarker-driven approaches that identify individuals with truly minimal or exceptionally high HCC risk. In patients not receiving treatment, those who meet criteria for HBeAg-negative inactive phase and have HBsAg <100 IU/mL have an annual HCC incidence below the 0.2% cost-effectiveness threshold, suggesting that routine surveillance and follow-up may be unnecessary. In NA-treated CHB, models such as PAGE-B or PAGE-B-based models effectively identify low-risk individuals who may also be exempted from HCC surveillance. For very high-risk patients, abbreviated MRI or combined biomarker algorithms may enhance early detection. Collectively, these data support a paradigm shift toward precision surveillance that aligns surveillance intensity with individualized, biomarker-defined HCC risk.
