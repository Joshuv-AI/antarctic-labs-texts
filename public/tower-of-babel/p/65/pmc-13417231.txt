Effects of Outer Helmholtz Plane-Located Anions on the Oxygenate Adsorption and Oxygen Reduction Catalysis on Pt(111)
Authors: Yilin Zhao, Mingchuan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The efficiency of electrocatalysis, a key factor in integrating renewable electricity into energy systems, is fundamentally influenced by the distribution of ions within the electric double layer. While the effects of specifically adsorbed ions (SAIs) at the inner Helmholtz plane (IHP) and nonspecifically adsorbed ions at the outer Helmholtz plane (OHP) are well-established, the role of OHP-located SAIs has remains unexplored. In this study, we probe the unknown by constructing a model Pt(111) interface where sulfate anions are exclusively confined to the OHP within the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) potential region, achieved through their distinct pH dependencies. Strikingly, these OHP-located sulfates exert an asymmetric influence on the *O/*OH redox, which impedes the reduction of *O to *OH but not its reverse oxidation. This asymmetry is explained via a place-exchange model for the *O/*OH redox, in which the OHP-located sulfates primarily influence the electron-transfer-free process. The established OHP-anion behaviors are further correlated with ORR rates on Pt(111) in various electrolytes. Our study elucidates the effects of OHP-residing SAIs, expanding the knowledge boundary of ion effects on electrocatalysis.

Introduction

The catalyst–electrolyte interfacial double-layer microenvironment is a complex and dynamic entity, exerting a profound influence on both the reaction kinetics and the mechanistic pathways of electrocatalytic reactions. − Under an applied potential, solvated ions are electrostatically driven toward and accumulate within the interfacial region, thereby modulating local electric fields, solvation structures, and intermediate adsorption. − Consequently, elucidating the roles of cations and anions in electrocatalysis is essential for rational catalyst design. For instance, in CO2 reduction, the apparent reaction rate toward C2+ products systematically increases in the order Li+ < Na+ < K+ < Cs+. − Analogous cation-dependent effects have been documented in other key electrochemical reactions, including the hydrogen oxidation and evolution reactions.

The influence of anions has likewise attracted considerable attention. For the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), the Pt–electrolyte interface serves as a prototypical system for probing anion effects, particularly regarding site blocking and the modulation of intermediate adsorption energies. − Notably, even nonspecifically adsorbed anionsthose confined to the outer Helmholtz plane (OHP) on Pt(111)have notable impacts on adsorption and catalysis. , For example, Koper and Luo systematically investigated the impact of ClO4 – and CH3SO3 – anions on ORR kinetics in acidic media and related the ORR rate on Pt(111) to the rate of the *O to *OH transition, in addition to the thermodynamic *OH binding energy descriptor. , Moreover, nonspecifically adsorbed alkali cations (e.g., K+ and Na+) can interact with surface *OH species and adsorbed H2O. , The ORR kinetics have been reported to correlate with the hydration energies of these cations (Figure a). On the other hand, specifically adsorbed anions (e.g., SO4 2–, Cl–, and Br–) that reside in the inner Helmholtz plane (IHP) are well-known for their distinct electrochemical signatures and role as site-blocking species − (Figure b). For example, Markovic and co-workers systematically investigated the influence of adsorbed sulfates on ORR kinetics on Pt(111) in acidic media and quantitatively characterized their adsorption effects through kinetic modeling, thereby providing a foundational link between IHP-located sulfates and ORR kinetics.

To date, research on ion effects has primarily focused on two categories: IHP-located specifically adsorbed ions (SAIs) and OHP-located nonspecifically adsorbed ions. However, a third, unexplored category likely exists: SAIs that reside at the OHP (Figure b). These OHP-located SAIs have been previously overlooked, as their effects could be masked by their IHP counterparts.

Herein, we address this gap by employing specifically adsorbed sulfates as a model system to probe the effects of OHP-located sulfates on the kinetics of oxygenate interconversions and ORR rates on Pt(111). Since sulfates and oxygenates share distinct pH dependencies, we are able to confine the former to OHP within the ORR potential region in alkaline electrolytes. Notably, the OHP-located sulfates decelerate the reduction of the *O intermediate while exerting negligible influence on the oxidative kinetics of *OH. Using a place-exchange model for the *OH/*O redox, we attribute such an asymmetric effect to the impact of sulfates on the electron-transfer-free process. Finally, by correlating this probing of the asymmetric *OH ↔ *O transformation with measured ORR activities on Pt(111) in various electrolytes, we highlight the significant, yet hitherto undervalued, impact of OHP-residing SAIs on electrocatalysis.

Results and Discussion

OHP-Located Sulfates Retard the *O-to-*OH Transition on Pt(111)

Pt­(111) is the most widely studied surface for the mechanistic understanding of ORR. The ORR rate is typically related to adsorbed oxygenates (most commonly *OH and *O), which are assumed to either share the same energetics or directly interact with ORR intermediates. , However, the situation becomes more complicated when SAIs are present in the electrolyte, which typically block the surface sites at potentials prior to the adsorption of oxygenates.

Of the various SAIs, sulfates receive the most attention possibly due to the ease of obtaining pure sulfuric acid and its practical relevance to proton exchange membrane fuel cells. Experimentally, sulfates adsorb onto Pt(111) at ∼0.36 V vs. the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which proceeds through the non-proton-coupled electron transfer mechanism , (Figure S1). Since it differs from the proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) behavior applied to oxygenate adsorption, one would expect a Nernst shift of 59 mV per pH unit for the adsorptive potential gap between sulfates and oxygenates. Following this, the specific adsorption of sulfates would occur more positively than that of oxygenates on Pt(111) in alkaline media (Figure a). Therefore, sulfate-containing alkaline electrolytes can serve as a model system for probing the effects of OHP-located SAIs on the oxygenate adsorption and further ORR kinetics on Pt(111). Attenuated total reflection surface-enhanced infrared absorption spectroscopy (ATR-SEIRAS) results provide additional evidence that sulfate species do not adsorb onto the Pt film surface under alkaline conditions (Figure S2). Note that we measured the pH of the alkaline electrolyte (0.1 M KOH) we used to be 12.74 ± 0.12, showing negligible difference from the theoretical value.

Blank cyclic voltammetry (CV) of Pt(111) in 0.1 M KOH shows the characteristic regions of hydrogen underpotential deposition (Hupd), double-layer, and hydroxide adsorption/desorption within 0.07–0.35, 0.35–0.6, and 0.6–0.85 V vs. RHE, respectively (Figure b). After the addition of K2SO4 (Figure b and Figure S3), no additional voltammetric signals are observed. This observation strongly suggests that in this electrolyte system, sulfates are not specifically adsorbed on the Pt surface but are instead located outside the IHP across the recorded potential range, given the well-known fact that sulfate adsorption on Pt(111) exhibits distinctly sharp redox peaks. While negligibly influencing the H2O/*OH redox, the OHP-located sulfates notably impact the *OH/*O redox, as shown by the CV of Pt(111) between 0.6 and 1.15 V (Figure c). Specifically, the current profile associated with the reduction of *O to *OH shifts negatively upon the introduction of sulfates (Figure c). This observation indicates that the OHP-located sulfates slow *O reduction, resulting in the partial overlap of two reduction peaks (Figure c). In contrast, the reverse oxidation of *OH to *O has no clear dependence on the sulfates. Considering that the upper-limiting potential (ULP) of 1.15 V closely approaches the adsorption potential of sulfates, we further confirmed a consistent trend in CVs with a lower ULP of 1.1 V (Figure d and Figure S4). The potential was held at 1.1 V vs. RHE for 200 s (Figure d), followed by a negative-going scan. We observed that with a prolonged holding time, a similar phenomenon of retarded *O-to-*OH transition occurs. The asymmetric effect of OHP-located sulfates on the *O/*OH redox process is unprecedented and warrants further investigation.

Probing the Asymmetric Impact of OHP Anions on the *O-to-*OH Transition at Pt(111)

To better visualize the effects of OHP-located sulfates on the *O/*OH redox, we experimentally leveraged different scan rates to deconvolute two reductive processes (i.e., H2O/*OH and *O/*OH, Figure ). The two reduction processes gradually separate with decreasing scan rate and become fully decoupled at scan rates below 20 mV s–1. Clearly, the presence of OHP-located sulfates further retards the reduction of *O, as evidenced by the negative shift of the reduction peak (Figure ).

CVs of Pt(111) in Ar-saturated 0.1 M KOH solutions with 5 mM K2SO4 recorded at different scan rates. Vertical dotted lines are drawn to facilitate the comparison of peak potentials at different scan rates.

The observed asymmetric effect of sulfates on the *OH/*O redox strongly indicates that the physical picture underlying this redox is more complicated than a single elementary-step surface process. In this context, it is necessary to refer to the so-called “place-exchange” model that describes the early oxidation state, i.e., the formation of *O, of Pt(111). Briefly, this model assumes that once *OH is oxidized to *O, it subsequently exchanges with the underneath Pt and triggers deep oxidation. − Reversely, the reduction of *O on Pt(111) can be decomposed into two distinct elementary steps: Pt−O(sublayer)⇋O*(surface)+Pt 1 O*(surface)+e−+H+⇋O*H 2 Equation describes the reverse place-exchange process, in which sublayer *O atoms exchange positions with surface Pt atoms prior to being electrochemically reduced. This is a potential-dependent process and has been clearly observed through surface X-ray diffraction (SXRD). Equation represents an electrochemical proton-coupled electron transfer (PCET) reduction process, which can be reflected in voltammetric profiles. Considering CV observations that the OHP-located sulfates influence the *O reduction process but not the reverse *OH oxidation (Figure c), it is reasonable to deduce that the OHP-located sulfates mainly retard the electron-transfer-free eq rather than the PCET-involved eq .

Along this line of reasoning, it is helpful to compare the above effects with those of nonspecifically adsorbed ClO4 –, assumably also located at OHP. In contrast, the ClO4 – primarily affects the oxidation of *OH during the positive scan (with a scan rate of 50 mV s–1), and *OH oxidation is accelerated with decreasing ClO4 – concentration (Figure a and Figure S5). However, the same trend is not notable for the reverse *O reduction process, again suggesting an asymmetric impact of ClO4 – on the *OH/*O redox (Figure a and Figure S6). The same phenomenon was also observed in methanesulfonic acid, the anion of which also belongs to the nonspecifically adsorbed group. These observations suggest both ClO4 – and CH3SO3 – mainly affect the PCET-involved *OH oxidation (the reverse process of eq ). Other underlying factors in Figure a, attributed to the different interfacial electric fields induced by varying pH values, were ruled out in previous work. It was determined that the transformation of the *OH/*O adlayer is primarily influenced by the anion concentration. In the case of alkaline conditions (Figure b), ClO4 – does not exert a significant impact on the transformation of oxygen species on Pt(111), which may be attributed to a shift in the potential of zero charge (PZC) and a concomitant weakening of the ion–Pt interaction. We also emphasize that, based on the CVs in Figure b, the effect of [K+] on the *OH/*O redox is negligible. This confirms the dominant role of anions when K2SO4 is used as an additive in alkaline solutions (Figures c and c).

The observed inverse effects between OHP sulfates and perchlorates/​methanesulfonates on the *OH/*O redox behavior are intriguing, yet the underlying microscopic mechanism remains unknown. Our findings not only elucidate the asymmetric role of OHP anions (ClO4 –, CH3SO3 –, and SO4 2–) but also better support the place-exchange (PE) model, indicating that the *OH ↔ *O transformation on Pt is not a simple elementary step.

Implication of Asymmetric Effect of OHP-Located Sulfates on ORR Catalysis

It is well-known that adsorbed oxygenates dictate the ORR kinetics by acting as site blockers or energetic regulators for reaction intermediates following the Frumkin isotherm. Therefore, the asymmetric effects of OHP-located sulfates must have distinct implications for ORR catalysis. More quantitatively, for a Pt(111) surface, the coverages of oxygenates are variables that influence both the pre-exponential factors and the energetic terms in the kinetic formulation , (Figure a). This explains the potential gap observed between the positive and negative scans of the ORR polarization curves due to the scanning-direction variations in surface *O coverage. A similar trend is evident under both acidic and alkaline conditions when the upper potential limit reaches 1.07 V vs. RHE (Figure b,c). In contrast, when the upper limit is restricted to 0.9 V vs. RHE, the potential gap between the forward and reverse scans becomes negligible, as *OH oxidation does not occur within this potential range (Figures S7 and S8). As evidenced by the blank CVs, sulfates do not exhibit specific adsorption on Pt(111) within the potential range of 0.07–1.07 V vs. RHE in an alkaline medium at pH 13 (Figure ), thus excluding the possibility of IHP-located sulfates. Nevertheless, OHP-located sulfates can still impact the ORR on the Pt(111) surface through affecting the kinetics of *OH/*O redox. As observed in Figure d, the presence of sulfates increases the potential gap between the anodic and cathodic ORR curves. The gaps of the ORR curves exhibit a dependence on the K2SO4 concentration (Figure S9). This can be attributed to a larger difference in *O coverage compared with the case in 0.1 M KOH. Specifically, sulfates located at the OHP slow the reduction of *O, while the reverse oxidation of *OH to *O shows no clear dependence on sulfates (Figure c). Note that the control experiment of ORR in KClO4-containing 0.1 M KOH electrolyte excludes the influence of KClO4 on ORR performance (Figure S10).

Moreover, our findings of the OHP-located SAI effects provide plausible explanations for some puzzling phenomena in ORR catalysis. A notable one is that the potential gap between anodic and cathodic ORR curves is more pronounced in sulfuric acid than in perchloric acid (Figure S11). This gap was normally ascribed to the difference in anodic and cathodic oxygenate coverage, originating from the slow kinetics of *OH/*O redox. Specifically, ORR processes in sulfuric acid are generally recognized to involve the saturation adsorption of sulfates, which significantly suppresses surface oxygenate adsorption and would therefore yield a smaller difference in oxygenate coverage (Figure S11). Following the conventional understanding, one would predict a smaller potential gap in sulfuric acid than in perchloric acid, which is opposite to the experimental trend. This strongly suggests that an analysis based solely on the specific adsorbates on the surface is insufficient to fully account for the ORR catalysis on Pt. When further incorporating the asymmetric effect of OHP-located sulfates on the kinetics of *OH/*O redox, we can reconcile this puzzling phenomenon.

Conclusions

In summary, we have for the first time unraveled how OHP-located specifically adsorbed ions (SAIs), exemplified by sulfates, affect surface adsorption and catalysis. Leveraging the different pH dependencies, we have created an electrified Pt(111) interface where sulfates exclusively locate at the OHP within the potential region of ORR. The OHP-located sulfates exhibit an asymmetric effect on the kinetics of *OH/*O redox, which is explained via the place-exchange (PE) model: sulfates slow the PE process but negligibly influence the charge-transfer process. Such an asymmetric effect further correlates with the rates of ORR on Pt(111), unprecedentedly uncovering the effect of OHP-located SAIs on electrocatalysis. This work goes beyond the conventional electrolyte effects of IHP-located SAIs or OHP-located nonspecifically adsorbed ions and contributes to a holistic picture of Helmholtz-plane-tuning electrocatalysis.
