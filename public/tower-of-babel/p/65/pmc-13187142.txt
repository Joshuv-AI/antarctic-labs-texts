Calcium sulphate-based soybean salting-out urease purification for enzyme-induced carbonate precipitation in road base stabilisation
Authors: Kehinde Lemboye, Mohamed A. Shahin, Liang Cheng, Hayder H. Abdullah, Anant Aishwarya Dubey, Mohamed Arab, Jayantha Kodikara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant-based biocementation via enzyme-induced carbonate precipitation (EICP) presents a promising alternative to conventional soil stabilisation. Crude urease extracted from soybeans contains substantial organic matter, which can reduce its effectiveness for soil stabilisation. This study investigates the extraction and purification of soybean urease for stabilising crushed rock base (CRB) material in road construction. A calcium sulphate (CaSO₄) salting-out method was proposed and compared with conventional purification techniques, including filtration and centrifugation, in terms of organic matter removal, turbidity reduction and calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) precipitation behaviour. The purified enzyme was subsequently applied to CRB under varying soybean concentrations, cementation solution (CS) concentrations, and treatment cycles. Mechanical performance of CRB-treated samples was assessed using unconfined compressive strength (UCS) testing, while microstructural characteristics were examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Results show that CaSO₄ salting-out effectively removes excess organic matter while maintaining enzymatic activity for EICP, enabling enzyme recovery without energy-intensive centrifugation at practical soybean concentrations. Although the CaSO₄ salting-out enzyme produced lower total CaCO₃ mass than less-refined extracts, it achieved more uniform precipitation and improved strength efficiency per unit of CaCO₃. The controlled addition of skim milk further enhanced the compressive strength without affecting urea conversion. SEM revealed irregular and spherical CaCO₃ particles from all urease extracts, while skim milk addition promoted spherical deposition around and in between the CRB particles. XRD confirmed calcite as the main phase governing strength development.

Introduction

Enzyme-Induced Carbonate Precipitation (EICP) is a novel bio-mediated cementation technique that has attracted significant attention in recent years for its potential to stabilise soils for a range of geotechnical applications1–6. EICP presents a more sustainable and environmentally friendly alternative to conventional soil stabilisation techniques that rely on traditional chemical additives (e.g. lime or cement), which are known for their substantial carbon footprint during manufacture and potential toxicity after mixing with soils7. EICP leverages the catalytic action of urease enzymes, typically derived from plants, to hydrolyse urea into ammonium and carbonate ions. The subsequent reaction between carbonate ions and calcium ions from calcium chloride leads to the precipitation of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), which acts as a binding agent to improve the mechanical properties of soils8,9. This soil stabilisation technique has been shown to increase soil strength, reduce soil permeability, mitigate the risk of soil liquefaction, and reduce volumetric changes associated with soil swelling, making it a promising solution for various geotechnical engineering applications10–14.

Bio-mediated CaCO₃ precipitation has been widely investigated through microbial-induced carbonate precipitation (MICP) technique, in which urea hydrolysis depends on the metabolic activity of urease-producing bacteria. Although MICP has proven effective for soil improvement, its application is often constrained by sensitivity to environmental conditions, limited bacterial transport, and non-uniform cementation due to microbial localisation15–19. In contrast, EICP is preferred to MICP because it eliminates the need for bacterial cultivation, enhances biological safety, and lowers monitoring demands, enabling treatment under a wider range of conditions and offering more uniform precipitation behaviour20,21. Although MICP and EICP have been widely studied for stabilising sands and fine soils, their use in stabilising unbounded granular materials for road base layers remains underexplored. MICP has been successfully used to stabilise road pavement subgrade soils, improving strength and reducing swelling22–24. Studies, such as Naeem et al.25, have investigated the use of EICP to stabilise sand as a subgrade material and reported reductions in the required base layer thickness, with satisfactory rutting and fatigue performance. However, few published investigations examine the performance of EICP-treated crushed rock base (CRB) material as a pavement base layer.

Crude urease enzymes obtained without purification contain substantial amounts of organic matter, including proteins and fats. While crude urease enzymes are more cost-effective, their application in soil biocementation without prior purification can lead to the flocculation and co-precipitation of substantial amounts of organic matter alongside CaCO₃ crystals within soil pores in the presence of calcium salts26. This clogging effect can hinder subsequent treatment cycles, resulting in an uneven distribution of CaCO₃ precipitates within the soil matrix and compromising the uniformity and effectiveness of the biocementation process27. Therefore, undertaking comprehensive research is essential to efficiently extract and purify the urease enzyme from plant seeds, thereby improving the efficiency of the EICP technique while reducing costs and simplifying processing requirements.

Common techniques used for the purification of crude urease enzyme for biocementation applications include filtration, centrifugation, and salting out of organic matter by adding a precipitating agent before filtration or centrifugation26,28. Each of these purification techniques has distinct advantages and limitations, depending on the specific requirements of the process. The filtration technique is often used as an initial step in the purification process, in which the crude enzyme extract is passed through fine filters to remove large impurities and debris. It is simple, cost-effective, and scalable for various volumes, making it adaptable for small- to large-scale applications28,29. However, filtration is inefficient at removing smaller organic impurities.

Centrifugation helps remove suspended particles and smaller organic matter from the crude urease enzyme based on density differences by spinning the extract at high speeds30; however, it requires specialised equipment. In general, more efficient removal of fine particles requires higher centrifuge speeds and, therefore, higher energy consumption. The salting-out extraction method involves adding a precipitating agent, such as ethanol, a polar organic solvent or a calcium salt, to the crude extract, which reduces the solubility of organic matter and proteins, causing them to precipitate from the solution and to be further removed by low-speed centrifugation or filtration. Ethanol is widely used due to its polarity and exceptional capacity to dissolve a diverse range of compounds31,32. However, since ethanol is miscible in water at all concentrations33,34, trace amounts may remain in the purified enzyme solution, potentially interfering with the EICP process. The use of polar organic solvents in large-scale applications presents significant safety concerns due to their highly flammable nature, necessitating stringent safety measures to mitigate the risk of fire or explosion.

Recently, a few studies have reportedly used calcium sulphate (CaSO₄)35–37 for salting out the excess organic matter during the purification process. However, these studies have failed to conduct a detailed strategic study to identify the optimal CaSO₄ concentration for varying seed concentrations, accounting for its impact on enzyme activity, organic matter concentration removed, and turbidity. Nevertheless, the salting-out extraction method effectively removes excess organic matter and is simple and cost-effective. It is commonly used in combination with other methods, such as filtration or centrifugation, to improve purification efficiency. It should be noted that the process may lead to a partial loss of enzyme activity, which varies depending on the type of precipitating agent used and the level of the precipitation of organic materials, as reported by Tirkolaei et al.38, Pawar et al.39 and El-Hefnawy et al.40. This requires careful assessment of the salting-out agent concentration. The addition of an organic stabiliser (protein source) can help offset the loss of organic matter during purification and enhance CaCO₃ precipitation by providing nucleation sites for crystal growth. These improvements can significantly influence EICP performance in geotechnical applications.

The performance of flexible pavements is critically dependent on the stability and durability of the base layer, which functions as the primary load-distributing component41,42. Crushed rock, limestone or gravel materials are widely employed in road construction as aggregate base43. However, as an unbound material, their strength is governed mainly by aggregate quality, interparticle friction, and compaction. These properties can deteriorate over time under the combined effects of cyclic traffic loading, moisture ingress, and environmental stressors, resulting in reduced stability, progressive deformation, and premature pavement distress44,45. Stabilisation of CRB using EICP provides a sustainable approach to address these limitations. By promoting CaCO₃ precipitation within the aggregate matrix, EICP enhances interparticle bonding, improves stiffness, and increases resistance to moisture-induced weakening.

Addressing the research gaps identified above, this study aims to advance knowledge of EICP by systematically investigating soybean urease extraction and purification using the CaSO₄ salting-out technique, explicitly benchmarked against conventional filtration and centrifugation methods. The CaSO₄ was selected for its low solubility and moderate ionic strength, which allows effective precipitation of impurities while maintaining enzyme stability. Unlike previous studies that have applied salting-out qualitatively or empirically, this work identifies optimal CaSO₄ dosages across varying soybean concentrations. It quantifies their effects on enzyme activity and organic matter removal. Importantly, the proposed approach has the potential to eliminate the need for energy-intensive high-speed centrifugation under practical operating conditions, thereby providing a more practical and scalable purification pathway for EICP applications. Notably, few studies have investigated the application of EICP to well-graded crushed aggregates, highlighting the need for further research in this area. The performance of each purified enzyme was evaluated by analysing turbidity, organic matter content, urease activity, and CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency across a range of soybean and cementation solution (CS) concentrations.

Additionally, the efficacy of the crude urease obtained via the CaSO₄ salting-out method was further enhanced by incorporating skim milk, which compensates for the loss of organic matter during purification and provides nucleation sites to improve CaCO₃ precipitation. Unconfined Compressive Strength (UCS) tests were conducted to assess strength development in the EICP-treated CRB samples under varying treatment conditions. Microstructural characterisation using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and X-ray Diffraction (XRD) was employed to elucidate the effects of EICP on the stabilised CRB. Collectively, the findings of this study provide a foundation for the development of biocementation techniques to improve the engineering performance of base course materials and offer a basis for future research in sustainable pavement construction.

Materials and methods

Materials

The soil used in this study is CRB, which is commonly employed as a base layer in flexible pavement construction throughout Western Australia. An initial evaluation of the CRB was performed, including a particle size distribution test to determine its suitability for use as a base layer for road construction, following the guidelines set by Main Roads Western Australia43. Figure 1 indicates that the used CRB meets the grading range between the upper and lower limits specified by Main Roads Western Australia (MRWA) and based on the Unified Soil Classification System (USCS), the used CRB is classified as well-graded gravel (GW).

Fig. 1Particle size distribution of CRB.

Particle size distribution of CRB.

Locally sourced soybeans were used to extract crude urease enzymes for EICP due to their economical and abundant supply of urease protein. The soybeans used were manually sorted to remove any foreign materials before utilisation, as a precautionary measure to ensure enzyme quality. The biocementation solution used for EICP consists of a mixture of laboratory-grade urea and calcium chloride (purity > 99%), both obtained from Chem Supply. Pure food-grade CaSO₄ obtained locally was utilised in this study as the salting agent for the purification of soybean crude urease enzyme (SCUE).

Preparation of SCUE

This paper prepared SCUE using different extraction and purification methods, including filtration, centrifugation and the CaSO₄ salt-out precipitation method. The initial phase of the extraction process involved pulverising approximately 150 g of soybeans using an electric grain grinder for 4 min. Subsequently, a specific mass of pulverised soybeans was combined with deionised water at the designated mass concentration and allowed to stand for 30 min to ensure complete hydration of the soybean particles. Following soaking, the soybean suspension was manually mixed until a homogeneous mixture was obtained. To obtain the first purity level of SCUE, referred to as F-urease, the resulting admixture underwent filtration through cheesecloth to separate the solid particulates. The filtrate was further refined by passing it through a nylon filter mesh with a pore size of 43 microns to reduce the concentration of suspended coarse particles. In the second purification method, in which the obtained SCUE was designated as Ce-urease, the physical filtration steps were omitted, and the soybean admixture was subjected directly to centrifugation. This approach achieved a higher degree of purification by efficiently removing suspended solids (SS) and excess organic matter, resulting in a more refined enzyme extract. The procedure involved placing the mixture in 50 mL tubes and centrifuging at 4,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, following the conditions reported in previous studies35,46,47. The supernatant collected post-centrifugation constituted the Ce-urease. The third purification method involved incorporating various concentrations of CaSO₄ salt ranging from 0 to 0.44 M into the deionised water during the initial soaking stage, and a clear solution at the top after phase separation was obtained as the Ca-urease. Additionally, the CaSO₄ concentration required for effective salting-out of the excess organic content in the SCUE admixture was investigated. Figure 2 illustrates a typical SCUE extract at a concentration of 125 g/L obtained after filtration, centrifugation and CaSO₄ salting-out purification process.

EICP solution

Soybean concentrations ranging from 25 to 300 g/L were investigated to capture a spectrum of urease activities (approximately 3–31 U/mL), as typically reported in the literature48–50. This range was critical in the CaSO₄ salting-out purification, as it enabled assessment of the impact of varying CaSO₄ dosages on turbidity reduction and enzyme activity across different soybean concentrations. For subsequent evaluations, the soybean concentration was limited to 200 g/L. In parallel, CS containing equimolar urea and calcium chloride dihydrate were prepared at concentrations ranging from 0.25 to 2 M. This range was informed by preliminary trials and previous studies49,51, and was varied to determine the concentration that yielded the most favourable CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency. The selected optimal CS concentration was subsequently applied to the CRB material.

Preparation of EICP-treated CRB samples

Cylindrical moulds made from polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipes were used to prepare EICP-treated CRB-treated samples. These moulds had a diameter of 105 mm and a height of 115.5 mm, in accordance with Australian Standard52. Before sample preparation, the CRB material was rinsed with water to avoid contamination with fine particles, then dried in the oven at 105 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{\circ}{\mathrm{C}}$$\end{document}53. The CRB material was then mixed with water to achieve a moisture content of approximately 5% and lightly compacted in the moulds to achieve a void ratio of 0.37. The addition of water was solely aimed at promoting adhesion between the fine particles and the larger ones, thereby preventing segregation of the large and small particles. Subsequently, a specific volume of EICP solution equivalent to 1.2 times the pore volume of the CRB sample was introduced at the top and allowed to flow through to the bottom under the effect of gravity. The solution was maintained at a height of 1 cm above to prevent accumulation of CaCO₃ precipitates at the surface of the sample. The EICP treatment process involved the application of one EICP cycle (SCUE and CS), followed by the application of CS for subsequent second, third and fourth treatment cycles as applies. Each treatment solution was allowed to react for 24 h. Multiple treatment cycles were conducted on each sample to achieve the desired cementation level in the matrix and assess their influence on the strength of treated CRB material. After treatment, the samples were washed with two pore volumes of distilled water to remove the ammonia by-product. The samples were then extracted and stored for subsequent testing. The flushing process was intended to remove soluble residual salts and by-products, as suggested by Almajed et al.54. The two pore volumes applied correspond to the initial pore volume of the sample, ensuring sufficient water to remove soluble precipitates and by-products while keeping the solid CaCO₃ precipitates within the soil matrix.

The selection of the EICP process parameters was informed by the in vitro CaCO₃ precipitation tests reported in Sect. 3.3.1 and the preliminary compressive strength test. Soybean enzyme concentrations of 75, 125, and 200 g/L were combined with a cementation solution (CS) concentration of 1.0 M. The in vitro precipitation tests revealed that 1.0 M consistently produced higher CaCO₃ yields, with higher CS resulting in reduced or negligible CaCO₃ formation, particularly at lower soybean concentrations. The chosen soybean concentrations represented varying levels of urease activity and, consequently, different cementation potentials, allowing a comparative assessment of their effectiveness. From the compressive strength results discussed in Sect. 3.4.2, 200 g/L was identified as the most effective soybean concentration for CRB stabilisation due to its superior conversion efficiency and CaCO₃ precipitation mass. To further enhance CaCO₃ precipitation, prior in vitro tests indicated that a CS concentration of 1.5 M at this high soybean concentration could increase the precipitation mass by 42–54% for all urease enzyme types. Treatment cycles of 2 and 4 were selected to examine the cumulative effect of repeated CS applications on strength gains. Skim milk was added only to the Ca-urease treatments to offset the loss of organic matter caused by CaSO₄ salting-out. The F-urease and Ce-urease extracts retained sufficient organic content, and therefore, additional organic stabilisation was not required.

Methods

The experimental investigation program employed in this research includes an examination of the following:

Activity of urease enzyme solutions

The urease activity is defined as the amount of SCUE required to catalyse the hydrolysis of one micromole (µmol) of urea per minute under standardised conditions55. The activity of the urease enzyme solutions was evaluated by measuring the rate of ammonia production using the electrical conductivity method, as described by Whiffin56. This involved monitoring a reaction mixture consisting of 9 mL of approximately 1.67 M urea substrate and 1 mL of SCUE over 5 min. The urease activity was determined, taking into consideration the dilution factor of the SCUE during the measurement process. According to Whiffin56, a 1 mS/min increase in conductivity corresponds to the hydrolysis of 11 mM urea per minute.

Turbidity and organic content measurement

The quality and effectiveness of the SCUE solution are greatly affected by its SS and organic content. High SS can cause bio-clogging, which may reduce the efficiency of the EICP process. Similarly, excessive organic content may interfere with the precipitation of CaCO₃ crystals needed to effectively cement soil particles. Therefore, it’s essential to accurately measure and control these parameters to optimise SCUE performance for effective biocementation applications. To determine the turbidity, 100 µL of the SCUE solution was pipetted into deionised water at a 1:301 ratio. Then, about 20 mL of the diluted SCUE solution was placed in cylindrical glass vials, and turbidity was measured with a Hach 2100Q portable turbidimeter using a medium-range turbidity sensor (0–1000NTU). The organic content removed from the SCUE following the different purification processes was determined according to the procedure outlined by Liu et al.26. The mass of organic matter was measured as the oven-dry mass of the retentate obtained after filtration or centrifugation. To ensure the repeatability and reliability of the results, three independent replicate samples were prepared and tested following identical procedures, and the average value was reported.

Precipitation efficiency

A test-tube experiment was conducted to investigate the precipitation efficiency of the SCUE using the different extraction methods considered in the study. The investigation focused on the impact of varying CS concentrations in combination with different SCUE concentrations. Additionally, an optimised SCUE, selected based on its purity level, was further evaluated to determine the impact of reaction time on both precipitation mass and efficiency. Each test tube was prepared with 10 mL of CS and 10 mL of SCUE. The EICP solutions were gently mixed and subsequently allowed to stand undisturbed for a reaction period of three days. Following this, the precipitates were washed with deionised water to remove the ammonium chloride by-product, then passed through filter paper. The precipitates were subsequently dried at 30 °C and stored for further analysis.

Using a different method, a beaker experiment was conducted to compare the kinetics of the EICP process catalysed by the SCUE extracted in the study. In addition, the optimised SCUE, based on the effective extraction method, was further investigated to determine the effects of varying CS and soybean concentration on reaction kinetics. Each beaker contained 300 mL of the EICP solution, consisting of 150 mL each of CS and SCUE. At a specified interval, 100 µL of the EICP solution was sampled for ammonia measurement.

The influence of adding organic matter (skim milk) as a stabilising agent on the amount of CaCO₃ precipitated, and the precipitation efficiency, was also examined. Organic matter, particularly proteins, plays a crucial role in CaCO₃ precipitation by facilitating nucleation and crystal growth, enhancing structural integrity and mechanical properties, and regulating or stabilising the chemistry of the EICP solution38,54,57. Therefore, skim milk was added to the EICP solution to provide a pure organic protein source. The EICP solution was allowed to react for up to seven days in the test tube and beaker experiments. UCS test

The UCS tests were performed under strain-control conditions to evaluate the strength of the EICP-treated material, following the guidelines outlined in the Australian Standard52. The test was conducted at a displacement rate of 1.0 mm/min.

CaCO3 content in treated CRB

After UCS testing, triplicate samples of EICP-treated CRB, each weighing approximately 3–5 g, were randomly selected to quantify CaCO₃ content (CC) and assess its influence on strength development. The CC was determined by measuring the volume of carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas released when the CaCO₃ reacted with concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl). The experiment was conducted using a U-tube manometer filled with water, where the evolved CO₂ displaced the water, allowing the gas volume to be read from a graduated burette. Each EICP-treated sample was digested in 3 mL of 1 M HCl and left to react until gas evolution ceased, indicating complete dissolution of CaCO₃. The measured CO₂ volume was then correlated to CaCO₃ mass using a standard curve prepared from pure CaCO₃ of known mass.

Chemical conversion efficiency

The effluent from each sample was collected after each treatment cycle to assess the urea conversion efficiency (UCE) of the EICP reaction. For each treatment condition, three independent replicate samples were prepared and tested using identical procedures to ensure the repeatability and reliability of the results. Ammonia concentrations, resulting from urea hydrolysis, were quantified using the Nessler reagent method56. The moles of urea consumed during the EICP reaction were backcalculated from the observed ammonia concentration. The UCE was then determined by calculating the percentage of the initial urea in the EICP solution that was converted during the reaction.

Microstructural analysis

To provide more insights into the strength development of treated CRB, the morphological characteristics of the precipitated CaCO₃ were analysed using a Tescan Clara-made Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM). The samples for the SEM analysis were dried at room temperature for 48 h and then coated with a 10 nm carbon layer to dissipate charge. This analysis encompassed various purification levels of SCUE, as well as different concentrations of soybeans and CS. Additionally, the bonding pattern between the host CRB material particles and the precipitated CaCO₃ crystals was examined. The crystalline phases of the CaCO₃ precipitates obtained from the test tube experiment and the stabilised CRB were examined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 Advance with a copper X-ray source. The CaCO₃ precipitates were collected and dried before being ground in a porcelain mortar. Subsequently, the prepared test samples were placed in a standard-depth sample holder for analysis.

Results and discussion

The subsequent part investigates the characteristics of the extracted SCUE, EICP solution and CRB EICP-cementation using the three SCUE, including F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease. Before comparing different SCUE extracts, the CaSO₄ salt-out precipitation method was optimised for CaSO₄ content.

Optimal CaSO4 Concentration for Ca-urease Extraction Method

To determine the optimal CaSO₄ concentration in the salting-out purification method, it is essential to assess its effects on both turbidity (a relative measure of purity defined as the concentration of organic particles in the extract) and enzyme activity. Figure 3 shows this impact across a range of soybean concentrations from 75 to 300 g/L. The results indicate that increasing CaSO₄ concentration significantly reduces the turbidity of the Ca-urease up to an optimal threshold (Fig. 3a). For instance, at a soybean concentration of 75 g/L, the turbidity of the SCUE was notably reduced by approximately 92%, as the CaSO₄ concentration was increased from 0 to 0.024 M. Beyond this point, no further reduction in turbidity was observed. A similar trend was observed at a higher soybean concentration of 300 g/L, where turbidity decreased by nearly 100% as the CaSO₄ concentration increased from 0.00 to 0.20 M, with no further decrease at higher concentrations. However, increasing the CaSO₄ concentration adversely affected Ca-urease activity, particularly at low soybean concentrations (Fig. 3b). At a soybean concentration of 75 g/L, the activity decreased by approximately 34% as the CaSO₄ concentration increased from 0.000 to 0.024 M, with no further decrease in activity at higher concentrations. As the soybean concentration increased, the adverse effect of CaSO₄ on urease activity diminished. For instance, at a soybean concentration of 125 g/L, urease activity decreased by only 16% as the CaSO₄ concentration increased from 0.0 to 0.1 M, with no further impact observed at higher concentrations. At the highest soybean concentration of 300 g/L, the effect of CaSO₄ on urease activity was negligible. These results also demonstrate that CaSO₄ has a comparatively lower impact on the urease activity of the Ca-urease than other salts, such as calcium chloride. Yan et al.27 reported a 57% reduction in activity when urease was extracted at a soybean concentration of 80 g/L using 0.024 M calcium chloride as the salting agent. This is 68% higher than the reduction observed for the same CaSO₄ molar concentrations, even though Yan et al.27 used a slightly higher soybean concentration (6.67%, 80 g/L vs. 75 g/L), as depicted in Fig. 3b. This finding further supports the suitability of CaSO₄ for preserving urease activity during extraction processes. It should be noted that the range of CaSO₄ concentrations tested varied with soybean concentration. At lower soybean concentrations (e.g., 75 g/L), a wide range of CaSO₄ dosages was examined to establish the effective precipitation range for organic matter removal. Based on these preliminary results, higher soybean concentrations were tested only with higher CaSO₄ dosages, since lower levels had already been shown to be inadequate.

Characteristics of the SCUE Solution

Figure 5 investigates the purity and activity of the SCUE solution prepared using different extraction methods, including F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease. The investigation was conducted across a range of soybean concentrations of 25 to 200 g/L. The concentration of the CaSO₄ salt utilised in the experiment was determined based on the optimal values previously recommended in Fig. 4. Figure 5a presents the average turbidity of all the SCUE extracts at varying soybean concentrations. Regardless of soybean concentration, the turbidity of the Ca-urease was the lowest, followed by the Ce-urease and F-urease. Across all three SCUE extract types, turbidity increased with increasing soybean concentration. For instance, at a soybean concentration of 25 g/L, the turbidity of the SCUE was observed to be: 67 × 102, 16 × 102 and 2 × 102 FNU for the F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively. At a higher soybean concentration of 200 g/L, the turbidity increased to 768 × 102, 173 × 102 and 11 × 102 FNU for the F-urease, Ce-urease, and Ca-urease, respectively. The Ca-urease yielded approximately 97% and 98% reductions in turbidity at soybean concentrations of 25 and 200 g/L, respectively, compared to the F-urease.

Figure 5b presents the average organic matter concentration removed by the different purification methods. For instance, at a soybean concentration of 25 g/L, the amount of organic matter removed was 5.6, 10 and 18 g/L for F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively. At a higher soybean concentration of 200 g/L, the mass concentration of organic matter removed was 69.8, 115 and 146 g/L, respectively. These findings indicate that the CaSO₄ salting technique is more efficient at reducing turbidity and removing more organic matter from the extract, which might positively affect the quality of the CaCO₃ crystals precipitated compared to other methods.

The SCUE activity obtained using the three purification methods is illustrated in Fig. 5c. A linear correlation exists between soybean concentration and average urease activity across the three SCUE extracts. The urease activity of the Ca-urease was about 7–30% lower than that of the F-urease and Ce-urease, depending on the soybean concentration. The reduction in the urease activity was notable at soybean concentrations of 25–125 g/L, and its magnitude decreased as the concentration increased to 200 g/L. For example, at a soybean concentration of 25 g/L, the activities were 3.5, 3.43 and 2.46 U/mL for F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively. This represents reductions of about 30% and 28% compared to the F-urease and Ce-urease, respectively. However, at a higher soybean concentration of 200 g/L, the activities were 24.04, 23.62, and 22.05 U/mL, respectively, indicating approximately 8% and 7% reductions compared to F-urease and Ce-urease, respectively. The addition of CaSO4 was observed to decrease the urease activity of the Ca-urease due to an increase in the ionic strength. An increase in ionic strength can alter the enzyme’s conformation and stability, potentially leading to partial or complete denaturation and reducing its activity. It is important to note that the purification process inherently involves a trade-off between removing organic matter and retaining urease activity. While increasing the CaSO₄ dosage enhances the removal of suspended organic matter, excessive addition can lead to a measurable reduction in enzyme activity, particularly at lower soybean concentrations of 75 and 125 g/L. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies, where multiple purification steps often resulted in partial loss of urease activity38,40,58. Therefore, the selected purification conditions represent a balanced compromise that ensures sufficient enzyme activity is retained while achieving adequate removal of organic matter for subsequent EICP applications.

Characteristics of EICP Solution

Impact of SCUE extraction method and CS concentration on CaCO3 precipitation yield

Visual observations from the test tube test indicated that the CaCO₃ precipitates obtained from the EICP process occur in two distinct forms, slurry and crystal form, based on the type of extraction technique, soybean and CS concentration. The EICP process, catalysed by F-urease and Ce-urease enzymes, tends to precipitate the CaCO₃ in a slurry form rather than the typical CaCO₃ crystals for most of the CS ranges. This typically occurred at lower CS concentrations for soybean concentrations exceeding 50 g/L. Specifically, for F-urease, slurry precipitation was observed at CS ranging between 0.25 and 0.50 M, 0.25–0.75 M, 0.25–1.00 M, 0.25–1.25 M, 0.25–1.50 M and 0.25–1.75 M for soybean concentrations of 75, 100, 125, 150, 175 and 200 g/L, respectively. For Ce-urease, the slurry precipitation was detected at CS of 0.25 M, 0.25–0.50 M and 0.25–0.75 M for soybean concentrations of 75, 100 and 125–200 g/L, respectively. The prominent slurry precipitation at lower CS concentrations can be attributed to calcium ions in the EICP solution, which primarily interact with soybean protein and fat. Consequently, the slurry effect diminishes at a given soybean concentration as the CS concentration increases. This diminishing effect was observed at higher CS concentrations as the soybean concentration increased from 75 to 200 g/L. Notably, the slurry effect was absent with the Ca-urease.

Figure 6 illustrates the precipitated mass and efficiency of CaCO₃ for the EICP solution catalysed with varying concentrations of CS and soybeans for the three types of SCUEs: F-urease, Ce-urease, and Ca-urease. Figure 6a shows that the precipitated CaCO₃ mass induced by F-urease and Ce-urease initially increased with increasing CS concentration before stabilising at higher concentrations. This was detected for soybean concentrations up to 125 g/L. However, for Ca-urease, the precipitated CaCO₃ mass also increased with CS concentration, reaching a plateau at a threshold before declining with higher CS concentrations. This trend was observed for soybean concentrations ranging from 25 to 125 g/L. However, beyond 125 g/L soybean concentration, no significant reduction in the precipitated CaCO₃ mass was observed. The reduction and stabilised precipitation of CaCO₃ in all three SCUEs can be attributed to the retardation of enzyme activity due to the elevated ionic strength of the EICP solution. However, at soybean concentrations exceeding 125 g/L, the mass of CaCO₃ increased continuously with the CS concentration, indicating that the reaction kinetics were not limited by enzyme availability at higher CS concentrations. It is worth noting that the highest precipitated CaCO₃ mass was recorded for F-urease, followed by Ce-urease, and the lowest with Ca-urease. For instance, at a soybean concentration of 200 g/L and CS concentration of 2.00 M, the precipitated mass of CaCO₃ was 4.8, 4.3 and 3.4 g for the F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively. This indicates a high impact of the SCUE extraction technique and purification level.

Fig. 6Effect of varying CS concentrations for the SCUE after a reaction period of three days on: (a) precipitation mass; and (b) precipitation efficiency.

Effect of varying CS concentrations for the SCUE after a reaction period of three days on: (a) precipitation mass; and (b) precipitation efficiency.

The observation in Fig. 6a showed that increasing CS concentration, up to an optimal level, had a positive impact on the precipitated CaCO₃ mass in the EICP solution. However, as shown in Fig. 6b, increasing CS concentration led to a decline in precipitation efficiency across all SCUE types and soybean concentrations. This suggests that although more CaCO₃ mass may be produced at higher CS concentrations, the conversion efficiency of calcium and carbonate ions into solid precipitates diminishes with increasing CS concentration. For example, at a soybean concentration of 25 g/L, the precipitation efficiency decreased by approximately 80% for both F-urease and Ce-urease and 93% for the Ca-urease when the CS concentration increased from 0.25 to 2.00 M. In contrast, at a soybean concentration of 200 g/L, the precipitation efficiency decreased by approximately 39, 15 and 15% for F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively, over the same CS concentration range.

In addition to the CS concentration dependency, the overall precipitation efficiency is strongly influenced by soybean concentration. In general, increasing the soybean concentration in the EICP solution from 25 to 200 g/L increases the precipitation efficiency from approximately 100 to 200%, as shown in Fig. 6b. For instance, at a soybean concentration of 25 g/L and a CS concentration of 0.25 M, the precipitation efficiencies were approximately 108% and 106% for F-urease and Ce-urease, respectively. When the soybean concentration was increased to 200 g/L, the precipitation efficiency rose to approximately 196% and 124% for F-urease and Ce-urease, respectively. However, for the Ca-urease, 100% precipitation efficiency was observed at both 25 and 200 g/L soybean concentrations in a solution containing 0.25 M CS, indicating that excessive precipitation of soy organic matter did not occur during the process. In addition, at a higher CS concentration of 2.00 M, the precipitation efficiency increased with increasing soybean concentration from 25 to 200 g/L. The precipitation efficiency was observed to increase by approximately 6-, 5- and 11-fold. Interestingly, at specific soybean concentrations, the precipitation efficiency exceeded the theoretical value at specific CS concentrations. For example, in the EICP reaction catalysed by F-urease, the precipitation efficiency aligned with or fell below the theoretical values at CS concentrations of 0.50, 1.00, 1.25, 1.75 and 2.00 M for soybean concentrations of 25, 50, 75, 100 and 125 g/L. Similarly, for Ce-urease, the precipitation efficiency matched or fell below the theoretical values at CS concentrations of 0.50, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50, 1.75 and 2.00 M for soybean concentrations of 25, 75, 100, 125, 150 and 175 g/L. However, the precipitation efficiency consistently exceeded the theoretical value at higher soybean concentrations. This apparent precipitation efficiency exceeding 100% at higher soybean concentrations can be attributed to the higher organic matter content in the crude urease extract. As a result, in the presence of calcium ions, organic matter from the soybean extract can undergo flocculation59 and co-precipitate alongside CaCO₃. This additional mass contributes to the measured precipitate, giving an apparent efficiency greater than the theoretical stoichiometric value. In contrast, the precipitation efficiency for Ca-urease remained ≤ 100% across all CS and soybean concentrations, indicating the absence of organic matter precipitation, a phenomenon characteristic of F-urease and Ce-urease. The precipitation efficiency trend of the Ca-urease aligns with those reported by Ahenkorah et al.49.

Generally, the precipitation mass and efficiency of CaCO₃ decreased as the purity of the SCUE extract increased. Such a decrease in precipitation yield should not be viewed as a drawback, as the higher precipitation observed with the F-urease and Ce-urease results from CaCO₃ co-precipitation with organic matter rather than purely stoichiometric CaCO₃ precipitates. Notably, the presence of fat in the soybean used in the study can affect the quality of the CaCO₃ precipitate. Overall, across the three SCUEs, the precipitated CaCO₃ mass increased with increasing CS concentration. According to Almajed et al.54, the strength of EICP-treated soil is influenced not only by the mass of CaCO₃ precipitation but also by the quality of the precipitate. Based on these results, the Ca-urease extract was selected as the optimised SCUE due to its purity. However, further evaluation is required on Ca-urease to investigate the effect of reaction time on CaCO₃ precipitation and the kinetics of the EICP chemical reaction, which are discussed in the subsequent section.

The CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency for all SCUE extracts was plotted against the corresponding urease activity at varying soybean and CS concentrations in a three-dimensional plot. Nonlinear regression in MATLAB R2025b was used to fit the data, and the four best-fitting equations, along with their goodness-of-fit values, are presented in Table 2. Among the response surface functions evaluated, the rational surface function provided the best fit to the experimental data, as shown in Fig. 7a–c. The 3D plots revealed a decreasing trend in CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency with increasing CS concentration, most pronounced at lower urease activity and gradually diminishing as urease activity increased. At a constant CS concentration of 0.25 M, CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency for F-urease increased with increasing urease activity, whereas Ce-urease and Ca-urease exhibited minimal changes. The enhanced precipitation observed for F-urease likely reflects higher organic content associated with increased soybean concentration. At higher CS concentrations of 2.00 M, CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency increased across all SCUE types, indicating that elevated CS levels promote carbonate formation regardless of SCUE type or purification method.

While the following conventional exponential model (Eq. 3) given in Table 2 treats urease activity (x) and CS concentration (y) as independent variables, CaCO₃ precipitation efficiency is primarily governed by their relative proportions.3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z=a\exp (bx - cy)$$\end{document}

Using a ratio in the exponent reflects a scaled or normalised effect, in which the output depends on the relative abundance of enzyme to CS rather than on the individual variables. The balance between urea hydrolysis and calcium availability governs precipitation efficiency. Urease activity controls carbonate generation, while CS concentration influences calcium supply and ionic conditions that may inhibit enzyme effectiveness at high levels. Accordingly, the ratio of urease activity to CS concentration captures this kinetic balance, explaining why precipitation efficiency scales more consistently with the enzyme activity–CS ratio than with either parameter alone. Thus, a custom exponential model incorporating the ratio of urease activity to CS concentration was applied.4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$z=a\left[ {1 - \exp b\left( {\frac{x}{y}} \right)} \right]$$\end{document}

Fitting with the inverse ratio (y/x) produced negligible predictive power (R² ≈ 0). However, the proposed ratio-based approach more accurately captures the observed trends and improves goodness-of-fit compared to both the standard model and the inverse ratio. Using this model, the experimental data were plotted as precipitation efficiency versus the urease activity-CS concentration ratio (Fig. 8). For Ce-urease and Ca-urease, precipitation efficiency increased with the urease to CS ratio up to a threshold of approximately 15 kU/mol, beyond which further increases produced no significant enhancement. In contrast, F-urease exhibited higher thresholds at around 30 and 65 kU/mol. This behaviour is attributed to the higher organic content associated with F-urease, which can co-precipitate with CaCO₃. The presence of organic matter provides additional nucleation sites, leading to apparent precipitation efficiency values exceeding the theoretical maximum based solely on CaCO₃ formation. The fitting equations and goodness-of-fit metrics for the exponential ratio-based model are presented in Table 3.

Impact of reaction time (Ca-urease extract) on CaCO₃ precipitation yield

Analysing the influence of reaction time on the EICP process can provide valuable insights into the kinetics of CaCO₃ formation and the rate at which the desired cementation level is achieved. Figure 9 presents the precipitated mass and efficiency of CaCO₃ at varying reaction times for the EICP process, catalysed with varying concentrations of CS and soybean, using the optimised SCUE (Ca-Urease extract). In general, the precipitation mass of CaCO₃ across all soybean concentrations increased with the increase of reaction time until equilibrium was reached, as shown in Fig. 9a. However, the time to reach equilibrium decreased with increasing soybean concentration from 75 to 200 g/L. For instance, the time required to reach the equilibrium mass of precipitated CaCO₃ was 48 h for 125 g/L and 12 h for 200 g/L at a CS concentration of 1 M. This can be attributed to the higher urease activity associated with the increased soybean concentration, which accelerates urea hydrolysis and carbonate ion generation, thereby promoting faster CaCO₃ nucleation and reducing the time required to reach precipitation equilibrium. Interestingly, the EICP reaction with a soybean concentration of 75 g/L and a CS concentration of 1 M did not reach equilibrium within seven days. This can be attributed to the lower urease enzyme concentration at 75 g/L soybean concentration, which may prolong the reaction kinetics due to a reduced rate of urea hydrolysis.

Effect of the reaction time on Ca-urease induced CaCO₃ precipitation: (a) mass; and (b) efficiency.

After a reaction time of 12 h, the percentage mass of CaCO₃ precipitates was approximately 47, 74, and 100% for soybean concentrations of 75, 125 and 200 g/L, respectively, at a CS concentration of 1.0 M (see Fig. 9a). Furthermore, the results indicated that increasing the CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M, while maintaining the soybean concentration at 200 g/L, results in an approximate 40% increase in the mass of precipitated CaCO₃ at equilibrium. However, the time to reach equilibrium increased from 12 to 48 h. Consequently, the percentage mass of CaCO₃ precipitation after a 12-hour reaction time was reduced to 90%. Yet the precipitated mass of CaCO₃ at a 12-hour reaction time was 25% higher at a CS concentration of 1.5 M than at 1.0 M. The increased time required for the reaction to reach the equilibrium mass of precipitated CaCO₃ can be attributed to the inhibition of the urease enzyme due to the increased ionic strength of the EICP solution following the rise in CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M. These results indicate the potential for EICP application for soil biocementation at a reaction interval of 12 h. Similarly, the precipitation efficiency of the EICP solution catalysed with varying soybean concentrations increased with reaction time until equilibrium was reached, except at 75 g/L, where equilibrium was not reached within the timeframe considered in the study (Fig. 9b). At a CS concentration of 1.0 M, the time to reach equilibrium efficiency decreased as the soybean concentrations increased from 75 to 200 g/L. However, at a specific soybean concentration of 200 g/L, increasing CS concentration decreased precipitation efficiency from 100 to 93% and increased the time required to reach equilibrium from 12 to 48 h.

Ammonia generation

Monitoring ammonia production from the EICP process resulting from urea hydrolysis can provide insights into the kinetics of CaCO₃ precipitation. In this study, the amount of ammonia generated from the EICP solution was measured using a beaker CaCO₃ precipitation experiment for different SCUE extracts at extended reaction times of up to 168 h and for different CS and soybean concentrations, as shown in Fig. 10a. In general, ammonia production increases with increasing reaction time for all SCUE extracts. A notable difference can be detected between different SCUE extracts. The F-urease and Ce-urease show the highest ammonia production rate and total ammonia concentration compared to the Ca-urease. The increased purification level following CaSO4 salting-out appears to reduce both the rate and the total amount of ammonia generated. Such a decline in ammonia production might suggest that ureolysis has slowed, potentially impacting the overall rate of CaCO₃ precipitation, as shown earlier in Fig. 6.

Figure 10b depicts the effect of varying CS and soybean concentrations on the amount of ammonium ion generated in the EICP reaction catalysed with the Ca-urease enzyme. An increase in soybean concentration (a proxy for urease activity) led to higher rates and final ammonium ion concentrations. At a CS concentration of 1.0 M, the final ammonium ion concentration measured after 168 h showed a slight increase with higher soybean concentrations. Specifically, the ammonium concentration rose from 1.63 to 1.84 and 1.97 M as the soybean concentration increased from 75 to 125 and 200 g/L, respectively. Furthermore, at a constant soybean concentration of 200 g/L, an increase in the CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M resulted in an increase in the amount of final ammonium concentration from 1.97 to 2.87 M. In addition, the time required for the ammonium ions generated to reach optimum increased from 12 to 24 h. This phenomenon can be attributed to the retardation in enzyme activity because of an increase in the ionic strength of the EICP reaction solution. It is essential to note that adding 12 g/L of skim milk to the reaction did not affect the production rate or the final ammonium concentration, irrespective of CS concentration.

CRB EICP-Cementation

Urea conversion efficiency (UCE)

UCE of the three SCUE extracts during the stabilisation of the CRB was examined after each treatment cycle at a reaction time of 24 h. The UCE is defined as the percentage of urea that was converted into carbonate ions necessary for the precipitation of CaCO₃ in the presence of sufficient calcium ions. Figure 11 illustrates the UCE for the EICP treatment process using varying SCUE types based on extraction methods, with soybean concentrations of 75, 125 and 200 g/L and 1.0 M CS after the first EICP treatment cycle. In general, UCE increased with increasing soybean concentration from 75 to 200 g/L across all extraction methods. This can be attributed to increased urease activity resulting from higher soybean concentration. The three SCUE extracts exhibit approximately similar UCE after the first treatment cycle. This can be attributed to the fact that after each purification, the SCUE was sufficient to catalyse urea hydrolysis and drive CaCO₃ precipitation. During the first cycle, enzyme activity is generally at its peak, and inhibitory or denaturation effects have not yet accumulated. Consequently, the reaction conditions were nearly identical across all SCUE extracts and soybean concentrations, resulting in comparable UCE values. The results from precipitation yield and UCE analyses within this study indicate that a soybean concentration of 200 g/L offers the most effective conditions for EICP treatment, achieving the highest CaCO₃ precipitation and urea conversion.

Fig. 11Impact of soybean concentrations on UCE for the three SCUE types at 1.0 M CS after one cycle of treatment.

Impact of soybean concentrations on UCE for the three SCUE types at 1.0 M CS after one cycle of treatment.

Figure 12 shows the impact of treatment cycles on UCE for CS concentrations of 1.0 and 1.5 M, using an optimum soybean concentration of 200 g/L for all SCUE extract. Each treatment cycle was allowed to react for 24 h. It is apparent from Fig. 12a that, with a CS of 1.0 M, the UCE of the three SCUEs did not differ significantly at the first EICP cycle of treatment. However, the increase in treatment cycles led to a significant decrease in UCE across all SCUE extracts. The reduction in UCE, irrespective of the SCUE extract, can be attributed to enzyme denaturation due to pH shifts or the accumulation of ammonium ions within the soil matrix6. A similar trend was observed for 1.5 M CS, with a more pronounced reduction, as depicted in Fig. 12b. This indicates inhibition of urease activity due to increased CS concentration. An increased CS concentration increases the ionic strength of the EICP medium. High ionic strength can alter the enzyme’s tertiary structure, reducing activity60. This structural change may hinder urea binding to urease, decreasing the conversion efficiency. Regardless of the CS concentration, increasing the treatment cycles from 1 to 4 resulted in the highest UCE for F-urease, followed by Ce-urease, while Ca-urease exhibited the lowest UCE. The lower UCE recorded for Ca-urease can be attributed to the loss of stabilising organic matter, which may enhance enzyme stability and increase its susceptibility to deactivation.

Mechanical property of EICP-treated CRB

The UCS of EICP-treated CRB material for the three SCUE extracts, using varying soybean concentrations of 75, 125 and 200 g/L, as well as 1.0 M CS after two treatment cycles, is presented in Fig. 13. In general, the UCS of the EICP-treated CRB material increased with increasing soybean concentration across all SCUE extraction methods. This can be attributed to an increase in the CC, altering their structure (see Fig. 13). At a high soybean concentration of 200 g/L, there were no significant differences in measured UCS values, which ranged from 0.24 to 0.25 MPa across the three extraction methods. At a concentration of 125 g/L, a slight reduction in the UCS was observed for the CRB material treated with Ca-urease compared to those treated with F-urease or Ce-urease. However, at a soybean concentration of 75 g/L, variations in strength response become more prominent. At this soybean concentration, the CRB samples treated with the Ca-urease performed worse than the F-urease and Ce-urease CRB samples. At lower soybean concentrations, the enzyme is more prone to denaturation due to the higher ionic strength of the EICP solution, which is caused by calcium ions. The reduced amount of organic matter at this concentration (as shown in Fig. 5b earlier) exacerbates enzyme instability, leading to a faster loss of activity. Consequently, this deactivation leads to less effective CaCO₃ precipitation, accounting for the lower UCS values observed in the CRB samples treated with the Ca-urease.

Fig. 13UCS and CC of EICP-treated CRB material using 1.0 M CS with varying soybean concentrations for all the SCUE after two treatments.

UCS and CC of EICP-treated CRB material using 1.0 M CS with varying soybean concentrations for all the SCUE after two treatments.

Although the F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease CRB-treated samples in Fig. 13 exhibited almost similar strength performance at the optimum soybean concentration of 200 g/L, there was a significant change in performance with the increase of treatment cycles from 2 to 4. Figure 14 shows the impact of treatment cycles on UCS and CC performance for samples treated with 2 or 4 cycles at soybean and CS concentrations of 200 g/L and 1 M, respectively. The results demonstrated an increase in UCS values with increasing treatment cycles across all extraction methods. The UCS values increased by approximately 22%, 16%, and 57% for F-urease, Ce-urease and Ca-urease, respectively. Notably, the Ca-urease performed better than both the F-urease and Ce-urease. This notable strength gain can be attributed to the ability of the treatment solution to percolate through the voids, enabling an even distribution of CC precipitation within the treated CRB sample. In the case of the F-urease and Ce-urease, the co-precipitation of CaCO₃ alongside excess organic matter resulted in increased plugging of soil pores, hindering the even distribution of CaCO₃ precipitates and thereby hampering further strength gain with increased numbers of treatment cycles26,27.

In terms of CC, Fig. 14 shows an increase in CC values with increasing treatment cycles from 2 to 4. Notably, CC values were lower for CRB samples treated with Ca-urease than with F-urease or Ce-urease, especially at high treatment cycles. This difference can be attributed to the superior urease retention capabilities of the F-urease and Ce-urease treatments, which effectively sustained enzymatic activity and enhanced CaCO₃ precipitation during subsequent applications of the CS following the initial EICP treatment cycle. An increase in CC typically correlates with enhanced material/soil strength54. However, in the case of CRB samples treated with F-urease and Ce-urease, the substantial CC did not result in a significant increase in strength. This discrepancy can be attributed to the formation of weaker CaCO₃ precipitates, likely due to the presence of organic matter. The organic matter may have promoted floc formation and compromised the structural integrity of the precipitates.

Figure 15 shows the impact of CS concentration on the UCS and CC for samples treated at four treatment cycles. As shown, increasing the CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M negatively affects UCS in CRB samples treated with F-urease and Ce-urease. Despite the observed increase in the CC, the UCS decreased by approximately 17% and 13%, respectively, as the CS concentration was increased from 1.0 to 1.5 M. In contrast, the CRB sample treated with Ca-urease exhibited an increase in UCS by approximately 16% achieving a value of 0.44 MPa CS concentration was increased from 1.0 to 1.5 M. This suggests that the process is less influenced by the presence of organic matter present in the soybeans, which can otherwise promote the co-precipitation of CaCO₃ and soybean organic matter in the presence of calcium ions. It is worth noting that the observed increase in UCS for the CRB samples treated with the Ca-urease, corresponding to higher CS concentrations, was positively correlated with an increase in CC. Furthermore, the UCS observed for the CRB samples treated with the Ca-urease was achieved at a lower CC than that attained for the CRB samples treated with the F-urease and Ce-urease. This indicates more strength gain per CaCO₃ precipitated unit, supporting the notion that Ca-urease produces more effective cementation despite its low observed CC.

Based on the UCS, Austroads (2019)61 classifies cemented pavement materials as: modified (< 1.0 MPa), lightly bound (1.0–2.0 MPa), and bound (> 2.0 MPa). As such, all EICP-treated CRB samples so far fall within the modified material.

Failure propagation and distribution of CaCO3 in biocemented CRB

The distribution of CaCO₃ within the EICP-treated CRB material plays a critical role in the mechanical performance of EICP-treated soil62. Figure 16 illustrates the UCS failure propagation and CaCO₃ distribution within EICP-treated CRB material using the SCUE with a CS concentration of 1.5 M after four treatment cycles. As shown in Fig. 16a, the CRB material treated with F-urease exhibited a localised failure mode, with the weaker zone at the bottom of the sample. Such a failure pattern might be related to the uneven distribution of CaCO₃ precipitation, with more CC near the injection point (see Fig. 16a). The CC was observed to decrease towards the bottom of the CRB sample. Such an uneven distribution of CC might be caused by the impediment of the flow of the EICP solution after a few minutes of treatment due to the flocculation of the soybean organic matter within the pores of the treated CRB material. The CRB material treated with Ce-urease shows a minor localised failure zone near the bottom of the tested sample, as well as a crack in the second third of the sample (see Fig. 16b). This indicates a slightly improved distribution of CC within the treated CRB sample. The centrifugation process likely enhanced the removal of organic matter, resulting in a more uniform distribution of CaCO₃ compared to the CRB samples treated with F-urease. However, the presence of localised weak zones in the EICP-treated CRB material indicates that further optimisation of the SCUE purification process may be required to achieve a uniform distribution of CaCO₃.

The CRB sample treated with Ca-urease (Fig. 16c) shows a well-defined failure, with no minor weak zones. This indicates a further improved distribution in CC and demonstrates the ability of the EICP solution to penetrate the pores to the bottom of the sample. Similar failure observations have been reported by Shu et al.36, Zhang et al.63, and Arab et al.64 for EICP-treated sand, as well as by Devrani et al.65 for MICP-treated sand. Shu et al.36 and Zhang et al.63 reported that EICP-treated sand samples exhibited localised failure in regions having relatively low CC. However, Devrani et al.65 reported localised failure at the top and bottom of treated sand and attributed it to the heterogeneous distribution of CaCO₃ within the soil matrix. In addition, Shu et al.36 indicated that EICP-treated sand with a more uniform distribution of CC developed a split tensile failure mode. Arab et al.64 observed a shear and split failure pattern in EICP-treated sand, with no reference to the impact of the CaCO₃ distribution on the failure pattern.

Fig. 16Propagation of failure zone and CaCO₃ distribution within the EICP-treated CRB material after four treatment cycles with a CS concentration of 1.5 M and soybean concentration of 200 g/L for: (a) F-urease; (b) Ce-urease; and (c) Ca-urease.

Propagation of failure zone and CaCO₃ distribution within the EICP-treated CRB material after four treatment cycles with a CS concentration of 1.5 M and soybean concentration of 200 g/L for: (a) F-urease; (b) Ce-urease; and (c) Ca-urease.

The salting-out extraction method appears to effectively remove organic impurities, promoting consistent nucleation and growth of CaCO₃ crystals across the sample, as evidenced by the failure plane distributed throughout the entire sample. These results highlight the significant impact of enzyme purification on the distribution of CaCO₃ within the EICP-treated CRB material. Optimising the purification process ensures more uniform precipitation of CaCO₃, thereby improving the overall performance of the treated material.

In addition to the differences in failure location, the EICP-treated CRB samples exhibited distinct failure modes, as reflected in their stress–strain responses shown in Fig. 17. The CRB sample treated with the F-urease and Ce-urease exhibited a gentler pre-peak stress–strain slope and a slower decline in stress after the peak. This response indicates progressive bond degradation before the ultimate failure. In contrast, the CRB samples treated with the Ca-urease exhibited a more brittle failure response, evidenced by a steeper pre-peak stress–strain slope, attainment of the peak stress at lower axial strain, and a more abrupt post-peak stress drop. These observations demonstrate that enzyme purification influences not only the magnitude of the strength gain but also the deformation behaviour and failure mechanism of the EICP-treated CRB.

Enhancement of the biocementation process catalysed with the Ca-urease enzyme

Among the SCUE extracts evaluated in the study, Ca-urease demonstrated high UCS performance and a uniform distribution of CaCO₃ within the treated CRB samples. This can be attributed to the fact that F-urease and Ce-urease retain some organic matter with high protein and fat content, which form flocs in the presence of a calcium source, negatively impacting the EICP process. To further improve the performance of the EICP stabilisation process using the Ca-urease, this study suggests incorporating a controlled organic source of protein with minimal fat content (i.e. non-fat skim milk), following research done by Almajed et al.54. The addition of non-fat skim milk to the biocementation process catalysed by the Ca-urease may compensate for the loss of organic matter during enzyme purification. Organic matter acts as a protector for the urease enzyme and helps promote the nucleation and growth of CaCO₃ crystals38. This section discusses the role of skim milk in enhancing the mechanical properties of EICP-treated CRB material and its influence on the urea consumption rate during the chemical process. Skim milk concentrations ranging from 0 to 20 g/L were tested, and their effects on the UCS and the UCE were investigated.

Figure 18 presents the UCS and UCE for CRB material treated with 200 g/L of Ca-urease, with skim milk concentrations of 0, 4, 8, 12, and 20 g/L, and CS concentrations of 1.0 and 1.5 M, after two treatment cycles at a reaction time of 24 h per cycle. The results indicate that the UCS increased with increasing skim milk concentration, as illustrated in Fig. 18a. At a CS concentration of 1.0 M and skim milk concentration of 0 g/L, the UCS of the treated CRB was 0.195 MPa. However, as the skim milk concentration was varied to 4, 8, 12, and 20 g/L, the UCS increased significantly by 44%, 54%, 102%, and 145%, respectively, relative to the CRB sample with a skim milk concentration of 0 g/L. Similarly, at a CS concentration of 1.5 M, the UCS of the treated CRB material with 0 g/L skim milk was 0.241 MPa. The incorporation of skim milk at concentrations of 4, 8, 12, and 20 g/L led to increases in UCS of 43%, 66%, 109%, and 186%, respectively.

Fig. 18Effect of varying skim milk concentrations on CRB treated with Ca-urease at a soybean concentration of 200 g/L after two treatment cycles on: (a) UCS; and (b) UCE.

Effect of varying skim milk concentrations on CRB treated with Ca-urease at a soybean concentration of 200 g/L after two treatment cycles on: (a) UCS; and (b) UCE.

In contrast, the enhancement offered by skim milk addition to EICP on the UCS of the CRB is not evident in the case of the UCE, as shown in Fig. 18b. The corresponding UCE values did not vary significantly with increasing skim milk concentration. This indicates that adding skim milk does not affect the kinetics of the EICP process. The improvement in UCS performance can be attributed to the presence of proteins (e.g. casein) introduced by the addition of skim milk. The presence of casein, which makes up about 80% of the proteins in milk, stabilises the urease enzyme in the EICP process26. Also, it provides a strong binding affinity for calcium ions54,64,66,67. This unique ability enhances nucleation and growth of CaCO₃ crystals, resulting in more uniform and stronger precipitates. Studies by Aryal68 and Baffoe, et al69. have highlighted the significant influence of protein inclusion in the EICP treatment process on the soil-CaCO₃ interface. They indicated that protein inclusion was found to potentially enhance adhesive bonding, further improving the structural integrity and strength of the treated soils.

The addition of skim milk to the EICP process was optimised at 12 g/L to enhance strength while avoiding potential clogging issues at higher concentrations. Figure 19 compares the UCS and UCE of the EICP-treated CRB after four treatment cycles using the conventional EICP process (0 g/L skim milk) and the modified EICP process incorporating 12 g/L skim milk. The result indicated that the modified EICP process, utilising 1.5 M CS catalysed with 200 g/L of Ca-urease, achieved approximately a 2.4-fold increase in the UCS compared to the conventional process, even at the same CC of approximately 2.18% (Fig. 19a), transforming the CRB material from a modified material to a lightly bound material. These results are consistent with those reported by Almajed et al.54, Arab et al.64, and Martin et al.57, demonstrating greater strength improvement when the EICP treatment solution was modified with non-fat powdered milk. In addition, the inclusion of skim milk did not significantly affect the UCE as the number of treatments increased (Fig. 19b). This result corroborates the findings in Fig. 8b, indicating that adding 12 g/L of skim milk did not affect the rate of urea hydrolysis.

Figure. 20 illustrates the CaCO₃ precipitates obtained using F-urease, Ce-urease, and Ca-urease across three soybean concentrations: 75, 125 and 200 g/L, with a constant CS of 1.0 M in the test tube experiment. A distinct difference in the morphology of the precipitated CaCO₃ precipitates was observed for all the SCUE in the test tube experiment. SEM micrographs confirmed the co-precipitation of the organic matter in the soybeans alongside CaCO₃ for the F-urease and Ce-urease, as evidenced in Fig. 20a-b with the white arrows. The co-precipitation effect can be observed to reduce as the purification level of the SCUE improves. From the representative micrographs of the CaCO₃ obtained, the F-urease and Ce-urease enzymes were observed to induce spherical CaCO₃ with rough surfaces, with few smooth-surfaced spherical shapes observed. However, the CaCO₃ precipitated using the Ca-urease predominantly exhibited a smooth, spherical surface, with a few rough-surface and rhombic shapes (see Fig. 20c). Across all SCUE types, SEM micrographs indicate the precipitation of more irregular, non-uniform spherical CaCO₃ as the soybean concentration increased from 75 to 200 g/L. Low urease activity has been reported to favour the formation of larger CaCO₃ precipitate clusters70–72. However, an increased urease activity accelerates urea hydrolysis, thereby increasing the rate of carbonate ion production. This rapid supersaturation of carbonate ions favours nucleation over growth, ultimately resulting in smaller CaCO₃ crystals73. Higher precipitation rates and CS concentrations above 0.5 M can also result in spheroid-shaped vaterite, a metastable polymorph of CaCO374, which eventually gets stabilised to calcite75. Moreover, recent studies have shown that organic macromolecules, including carbohydrates and proteins, promote vaterite formation76–78, which may explain the absence of rhomboid calcite crystals for the CaCO3 precipitates induced by the F-urease and Ce-urease at a CS concentration of 1 M (see Fig. 20a & b).

Figure 21 illustrates the impact of increasing the CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M, while maintaining a constant soybean concentration of 200 g/L, across the different SCUE types after 3 days of reaction. The SEM micrographs indicate the precipitation of smooth and rough spherical CaCO₃ precipitates for all the SCUE used in the study, following an increase in the CS concentration to 1.5 M. Similarly, the co-precipitation of CaCO₃ and organic matter can be observed with the F-urease and Ce-urease, as shown in Fig. 21a & b. The modification of the EICP process, catalysed by the Ca-urease by the addition of 12 g/L of skim milk to replace the organic content lost during the purification process and serve as a stabiliser for the EICP reaction, did not alter the shape of the CaCO₃ precipitates (see Fig. 21d). In all SCUE types with a CS concentration of 1.5 M, varying small-sized spherical CaCO₃ particles were observed, with fewer larger ones.

To assess the formation of new crystalline phases within the biocemented material, XRD analysis was also conducted on CRB samples treated with the EICP process. Role of organic matter

The presence of organic matter in the F-urease and Ce-urease, as well as its removal during CaSO₄ salting-out purification, had a pronounced effect on the EICP process. In the F-urease and Ce-urease extracts, residual organic matter originating from soybean, comprising proteins and fats, tended to cause clogging and poor percolation during treatment. This hindered uniform infiltration of the CS and limited the development of strength in the treated CRB samples. The clogging and percolation issues reflect the adverse impact of uncontrolled organic matter content on the permeability and distribution of CaCO₃ in the EICP-treated CRB material. Following purification of the crude urease extract using CaSO₄, the issues of clogging of pore spaces and poor percolation of the CS were largely minimised. The Ca-urease exhibited improved clarity and efficiently percolated the CRB matrix, facilitating a more uniform distribution of CaCO₃ within the treated CRB matrix. However, the Ca-urease showed a lower UCE, particularly after the first treatment cycle, compared to the F-urease and Ce-urease extract, suggesting that the removal of organic components may also have eliminated stabilising and protective substances beneficial to urease performance, as suggested by Valles et al. (2022)79.

Interestingly, when a controlled quantity of organic matter, specifically skim milk, composed primarily of pure proteins without fats, was reintroduced into the Ca-urease enzyme, the resulting biocemented samples exhibited improved strength. This enhancement can be attributed to the protein content (casein) in skim milk, which may provide active nucleation sites and promote the formation of a more rigid and interconnected CaCO₃ precipitate network. These findings indicate that, while organic matter can be detrimental to the uniformity of CaCO₃ treatment and to the development of strength, a controlled addition of specific organic compounds can enhance the nucleation and bonding characteristics of the biocemented material. It is important to note that the incorporation of skim milk in the Ca-urease treatment had no observable effect on the reaction kinetics of the EICP process or on the crystalline phase of the resulting CaCO₃ precipitates.

Overall, this highlights a dual role of organic matter in the EICP process. It can be detrimental when present in crude enzyme extracts because organic matter, particularly fats, tends to form hydrophobic films and flocculate, blocking pore spaces, hindering percolation, and reducing treatment efficiency. However, when a purified organic additive such as skim milk protein without fat is deliberately introduced into the highly purified enzyme, it can enhance CaCO₃ nucleation and improve interparticle bonding.

Practical considerations for field implementation

The laboratory preparation procedures adopted in this study, including aggregate washing and oven drying, as well as the use of laboratory-grade urea and calcium chloride, were primarily implemented to ensure experimental consistency and to investigate the effects of EICP treatment on the mechanical response of the CRB. Such controlled conditions are commonly adopted to minimise the influence of uncontrolled material variability and to ensure that observed changes in mechanical behaviour can be attributed directly to the stabilisation process. It is important to note that the surface percolation method adopted in this study represents a controlled laboratory condition for the EICP treatment. However, it does not necessarily replicate the standard construction practice used in pavement construction. Thus, in field-scale implementation, mixing and compaction would likely be required.

Also, laboratory-scale pretreatment of CRB, such as washing and oven drying, would be difficult to replicate at a large scale in field applications. To evaluate the necessity of this pretreatment, a comparative investigation was conducted using washed and unwashed CRB samples treated under identical EICP conditions. These samples were prepared using Ca-urease with a soybean concentration of 200 g/L and a CS concentration of 1.5 M, with a total of 2 treatment cycles comprising one EICP application followed by one CS application. The resulting UCS values, shown in Fig. 27a, reveal only a modest difference between pretreated (0.49 MPa) and untreated (0.45 MPa) samples, indicating that cementation can be achieved without laboratory pretreatment of the CRB material. This suggests that the EICP process is relatively tolerant of the presence of fines or residual dust, which are typically encountered in field CRB materials.

Comparison of: (a) washed and unwashed CRB treated with Ca-urease at a soybean concentration of 200 g/L and a 1.5 M CS using two treatment cycles; and (b) laboratory-grade and industrial-grade chemical reagents for CRB treated with Ca-urease at the same soybean concentration and CS using four treatment cycles.

Furthermore, the use of laboratory-grade urea and calcium chloride in this study was intended to prevent potential interference from impurities during the controlled experimental phase. To assess the relevance of reagent purity for practical applications, an additional comparison was performed between laboratory-grade and industrial-grade chemical reagents. These samples were prepared using Ca-urease with the same soybean concentration (200 g/L) and CS concentration (1.5 M), but with a higher treatment level comprising four cycles (one EICP application followed by three CS applications). The UCS results shown in Fig. 27b indicate that slightly higher compressive strength was achieved using laboratory-grade reagents, with a UCS of 1.13 MPa, compared with 0.94 MPa obtained using industrial-grade chemicals. Despite this reduction in absolute strength, the use of the industrial-grade reagent still resulted in a substantial strength gain attributable to CaCO₃ precipitation. This indicates that the biocementation mechanism remained effective and that the overall trend of strength enhancement associated with CaCO₃ bonding was preserved even with lower-grade reagents.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that although controlled laboratory conditions were employed to establish fundamental behaviour, the EICP stabilisation process is robust to variations in pretreatment conditions and chemical grade. This supports the feasibility of extending the technique to field-scale applications using industrial-grade reagents and in situ CRB, without the need for extensive preprocessing or high-purity reagents.

Furthermore, the Ca-urease produced through CaSO₄ salting-out demonstrated superior cementation efficiency per unit mass of CaCO₃ precipitated, indicating a greater potential for improving the mechanical performance of the CRB material compared with the F-urease and Ce-urease extracts. In contrast, centrifugation is associated with higher energy requirements and specialised equipment, which may limit its practicality for large-scale implementation. For example, the energy consumption of stacked-disk centrifuges has been reported to reach approximately 3.3 kWh/m380,81, corresponding to an additional energy requirement of about 0.54 kJ per kU of urease activity. To illustrate the potential energy implications on a large scale, treating 1 m³ of pavement base material with a void ratio of 0.37 using the CaSO₄ salting-out purification method instead of centrifugation would yield an estimated energy savings of approximately 0.611 kWh. This reduction reflects the elimination of the centrifugation step while maintaining comparable urease activity during cementation, thereby supporting the practical scalability of the proposed purification approach. It should be noted that the efficiency of phase separation during CaSO₄ salting-out was influenced by soybean concentration. At lower concentrations (25–125 g/L), sedimentation of organic residues occurred rapidly, and the supernatant containing active urease could be readily recovered by decantation without centrifugation. However, at higher soybean concentrations, increased organic matter content resulted in slower phase separation. Under such conditions, centrifugation was required to ensure full recovery of the purified enzyme. Despite this limitation, the salting-out approach remains advantageous because it substantially reduces centrifugation requirements and enables enzyme purification with significantly lower energy input than conventional centrifugation-based methods.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to evaluate the influence of SCUE type, soybean concentration, treatment cycles, cementation solution (CS) concentration, and skim milk incorporation on the unconfined compressive strength (UCS) and CaCO₃ content (CC) of EICP-treated CRB. Two-way ANOVA revealed that both SCUE type and soybean concentration significantly affected UCS (p < 0.001), with soybean concentration exhibiting the strongest effect (F = 143.05, η² = 0.97), followed by SCUE type (F = 51.47, η² = 0.92). At the same time, a significant interaction was also observed (F = 17.99, p < 0.001). Both factors also significantly influenced CC (SCUE type: F = 8.53, p = 0.008, η² = 0.65; soybean concentration: F = 9.92, p = 0.005, η² = 0.69), although their interaction was not significant (p = 0.994). Tukey comparisons indicated that UCS increased significantly across soybean concentrations, whereas CC showed no significant difference between 125 and 200 g/L. Normality assumptions were satisfied (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.268). Similarly, SCUE type and treatment cycles significantly affected UCS, with treatment cycles having the strongest influence (F = 119.56, p < 0.001, η² = 0.95), followed by SCUE type (F = 19.14, p < 0.01, η² = 0.86), while their interaction was also significant (F = 19.31, p < 0.01, η² = 0.87). Increasing treatment cycles from 2 to 4 significantly increased UCS (p < 0.001), with Ca-urease producing higher UCS than Ce-urease (p = 0.002) and F-urease (p = 0.039). For CC, both SCUE type (F = 20.44, p = 0.002, η² = 0.87) and treatment cycles (F = 69.62, p < 0.001, η² = 0.92) were significant with a significant interaction (F = 7.97, p = 0.020, η² = 0.73). CC increased significantly when treatment cycles increased from 2 to 4 (p < 0.001), although F-urease and Ce-urease produced significantly higher CC than Ca-urease (p < 0.01) without corresponding strength gains. After four treatment cycles, SCUE type significantly influenced UCS (F = 60.24, p < 0.001, η² = 0.95), whereas the effect of CS concentration was not significant (F = 0.58, p = 0.476, η² = 0.09), although their interaction was significant (F = 8.59, p = 0.017, η² = 0.74). In contrast, both SCUE type (F = 31.11, p = 0.00068, η² = 0.91) and CS concentration (F = 12.45, p = 0.012, η² = 0.67) significantly affected CC, while their interaction was not significant (p = 0.696). Increasing CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M significantly increased CC (p = 0.012), but the corresponding increase in UCS was not proportional. Pearson correlation analysis confirmed a significant negative relationship between UCS and CC (r = − 0.754, p = 0.0046; R² = 0.569), indicating that higher CC obtained with F-urease and Ce-urease did not translate into greater strength. For the optimised Ca-urease treatment process, both skim milk concentration (F = 237.8, p < 0.001, η² = 0.98) and CS concentration (F = 170.3, p < 0.001, η² = 0.89) significantly influenced UCS with a significant interaction (F = 12.7, p < 0.001, η² = 0.72). Tukey comparisons showed significant UCS improvement across most skim milk levels (p < 0.001), except between 4 and 8 g/L (p ≈ 0.052), while increasing CS concentration from 1.0 to 1.5 M significantly increased UCS (p < 0.001). Assumption testing indicated homogeneity of variance (Bartlett’s test, p = 0.989) and a minor deviation from normality (Shapiro–Wilk’s test, p = 0.047); however, given the balanced design and large effect sizes (η² = 0.72–0.98), the ANOVA results remained robust. Increasing treatment cycles to four at 1.5 M CS and 12 g/L skim milk significantly increased UCS (p < 0.01). In contrast, skim milk concentration did not significantly affect CC (p = 0.784, η² = 0.05), indicating that skim milk improved bonding efficiency rather than increasing CaCO₃ quantity, reinforcing that carbonate quality rather than quantity governed strength development.

Conclusion

This study investigated the feasibility of stabilising a well-graded CRB material commonly used in road construction utilising EICP. The study assessed the extraction of crude urease enzyme from soybeans using a CaSO₄ salting-out technique and compared its performance with that of conventional purification methods, including filtration and centrifugation. The obtained urease enzymes were termed Ca-urease, F-urease and Ce-urease, respectively. A comprehensive experimental program was carried out to characterise the crude urease extracts, assess the performance of these enzymes in stabilising the CRB material, and conduct microstructural analysis. Based on the experimental program, the key outcomes of the study can be summarised in the following points:

This study underscores the limitation of relying on multiple treatment cycles for strength development, which presents a challenge for field application. While higher dry densities and the use of pure casein may provide more effective pathways to achieve sufficient strength, further investigations are recommended. Specifically, exploring alternative enzyme sources with lower organic matter content could reduce purification demands and enhance the quality and binding capacity of CaCO₃ precipitates. In addition, future studies should employ a formal design of experiments and optimisation algorithms to evaluate the interactions among enzyme concentration, CS concentration, and organic additives, and to identify the global optimum conditions for efficient CaCO₃ stabilisation. Future work should also assess the durability of EICP-treated CRB under wetting and drying conditions to evaluate its resistance to moisture-induced degradation and long-term pavement performance. Lastly, evaluating the performance of EICP-treated CRB under simulated traffic loading and conducting preliminary field-scale trials would help validate laboratory findings.
