Solid-Phase Synthesis of Guanine Derivatives with Four Diversity Elements via AgNO3‑Mediated Guanidine Formation
Authors: Jimin Moon, Jimin Lee, Hyojin Lee, Geonho Yoon, Taeho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We constructed a guanine derivative library with four diversities through efficient solid-phase synthesis. In this reaction, thiourea was transformed into guanidine through a guanylation reaction using AgNO3, and the guanine core was synthesized by cyclization using a base. After oxidation using mCPBA, several nucleophiles were substituted to construct 36 derivative libraries through solid-phase synthesis. The reaction involved 7 steps in total, showing a good yield of over 57–82%, but it also showed a difference in cyclization reaction selectivity between solid-phase synthesis and solvent-phase synthesis. We will use the synthesized chemical library to find hit compounds for various diseases.

Introduction

A wide variety of structurally diverse compounds are being synthesized as potential drug candidates for the treatment of diseases such as inflammation, cancer, and viral infections. It is well-known that the drug development process, from early discovery to commercial launch, typically takes around 10 years and costs approximately 2.6 billion dollars. In recent years, to reduce both the time and cost of identifying new drug candidates, research efforts have increasingly focused on drug discovery based on chemical libraries. As a result, an increasing number of studies have utilized these libraries to identify potential drug candidates. − Several approaches have been proposed to efficiently construct chemical libraries, including molecular editing, the synthesis of novel scaffolds, and structural modifications. − Among these efforts, our research group is focusing on the development of efficient chemical libraries through solid-phase synthesis, specifically targeting the synthesis of novel scaffolds and the efficient construction of heterocyclic compounds. Since the solid-phase synthesis method eliminates the need for workup and separation processes after the reaction, it is a suitable method for quickly synthesizing a large number of compounds, and various chemical libraries have been built using solid-phase synthesis so far. Among various molecular structures such as aromatic, acyclic, pericyclic, and heterocyclic, the heterocycle must be a structure that is in the spotlight in the development of new drugs. Among the drugs approved by the FDA, 30% of the compounds have five-membered heterocycles, and 62% of them have aromatic heterocycles. − It is also possible to choose a structure similar to that of existing bioactive structures and quickly find new drug candidates for rapid new drug development. Therefore, we conducted research focusing on the five-membered heterocyclic compound among the heterocycles with various structures. , Among the thiazole, oxazole, pyrrole, thiophene, and imidazole, imidazole is a structure contained in various physiologically active structures in nature, such as purine, xanthine, caffeine, guanine, and adenine. In addition, the nitrogen of imidazole has the advantage that a substituent can be introduced, unlike other five-membered heterocyclic compounds. Purine or guanine derivatives with imidazole are being investigated for various therapeutic applications, such as anti-inflammatory agents, treatment for genetic diseases, TLR (toll-like receptor) 7 and TLR 9 antagonists, antimicrobials, anticonvulsants, eIF4E (eukaryotic initiation factor 4E) inhibitors, and anticancer agents. , In several studies, a method for introducing various substituents has not yet been well suggested. However, most of the studies have focused on two to three diversities in derivative studies, such as purine and guanine. In addition, many studies have started with purine as the base structure to synthesize various guanine derivatives. While this approach can shorten the overall synthetic steps, it may be somewhat limited in terms of structural diversity. −

Therefore, our team conducted a study to build a library through efficient solid-phase synthesis of guanine derivatives with substituents at C-2, N-1, N-7, and C-8 (Figure ). So, to introduce various substituents into guanine derivatives, we developed a guanine derivative synthesis route using a guanylation reaction. However, some synthesis conditions were not suitable for solid-phase synthesis. − Due to the nature of solid-phase synthesis, the reaction must be carried out under optimized reaction conditions after testing various synthesis conditions. , Therefore, synthesis was proceeded through a mild guanylation synthesis method that can be applied to the solid-phase synthesis method. Among these methods, the Ag atom is known to react with the sulfur group, so it is used for guanylation. In this study, a chemical library was constructed using the guanylation reaction using AgNO3 in the solid-phase synthesis method for the synthesis of guanine derivatives.

Results and Discussion

For a successful solid-phase synthesis method, we started by optimizing the solution-phase synthesis method (Scheme ). Imidazole derivatives were synthesized using a simple Thorpe–Ziegler reaction. In order to synthesize the imidazole derivative, methyl N ′-cyano-N-phenylcarbamideothioate 2a was used to synthesize 2-int by reacting with ethyl bromoacetate under K2CO3/DMF conditions. The imidazole derivative 3 was then synthesized through a Thorpe–Ziegler reaction using DBU as a base. Imidazole derivative 3a and phenylisothiocyanate were reacted in pyridine to obtain thiourea 4aa. Several reaction conditions were explored to optimize the effective guanylation reaction. Initially, guanylation was performed through a desulfurization reaction using TBHP in toluene and chloroform, but the starting materials were not consumed, and only a small amount of products could be obtained (Table , entries 1–2). Strangely, when iodine was used, it was possible to synthesize a compound in the form of thiohydroxylamine. (entry 3). In addition, although the reaction was carried out using CuCl, guanylation could be successfully carried out with a 39% yield, but further optimization of reaction conditions was required due to the low yield (entry 4). Next, when the reaction was performed using an iron catalyst, the starting materials were used, but only some desired compounds could be found (entries 5–6). So, when we performed the reaction using BOP and HATU coupling agents, the desired compound was synthesized with a 59% yield in HATU/DBU conditions, whereas BOP did not proceed (entries 7–8). During additional optimization, guanylation was performed by using desulfurization using AgNO3. The reaction was performed using DBU as a base, and desired compound 5aaa could be obtained with a 95% high yield. In addition, similar results were obtained when Et3N, DIPEA, and pyridine were used as bases (entries 9–12). When the solvent was optimized for solid-phase synthesis after optimizing the base, CH2Cl2, THF, DCE, and toluene were selected, considering the swelling effect and reaction efficiency, and similar results were obtained (entries 13–16). Furthermore, elimination of the base, which effectively increased the equivalents of butylamine, did not result in any change in the reaction yield.

Finally, we were able to synthesize compound 5aaa with 81% yield on a gram scale by performing the synthesis under DBU/DMF conditions using AgNO3. Thereafter, the intramolecular cyclization reaction was performed using NaOEt to obtain guanine core 6aaa in 90% yield. In NaOEt/EtOH conditions, only the aromatic amine among the aromatic and aliphatic amines of guanidine reacted as a nucleophile. Next, we optimized the selectivity of the intramolecular cyclization in solid-phase synthesis by adjusting the solvent. EtOH alone showed poor resin swelling, resulting in inefficient reactions (Table ). Although EtOH gave a high product-to-byproduct ratio of 37:1, it cannot be used as the sole solvent. Thus, solvents enhancing swellingDMF, EtOH, CHCl3, and THFwere tested in 1:1 mixtures. The product ratio increased in the order CHCl3 ≥ THF > DCE > DMF (entries 1–5). Compound 5aaa was mostly unreactive except in DMF. The poor solubility of NaOEt in CHCl3, DCE, and THF caused precipitation, leading us to select DMF as the cosolvent. As the ratio of DMF increased, the ratio of byproducts increased, and when the ratio of EtOH was increased, the ratio of products increased relatively (entries 6–11). Therefore, we proceeded with a ratio of EtOH:DMF = 2:1, considering the swelling effect of the resin and the solubility of NaOEt (entry 6). Using DBU as the base, reactions proceeded with lower selectivity (entries 12–18). When DBU was used as the base, complete conversion was not observed in most solvents. However, in EtOH, a 13:1 ratio indicated a significantly higher proportion of 6aaa. In DMSO, 6aaa was also favored with a 1.43:1 ratio, but compared to other conditions, the proportion of 8aaa was the highest. Under these conditions, 8aaa could be isolated in 30% yield (entry 17). When using Brønsted acids and Lewis acids, the reaction did not proceed (entries 19–21). Under optimized EtOH:DMF (2:1) conditions, 6aaa was obtained in 94% yield (Table , entry 6). After the oxidation of sulfide to sulfone with mCPBA, guanine derivative 1aaaa was synthesized using benzyl alcohol. mCPBA gave a 60% yield of sulfone, while Na2WO4·2H2O improved it to 81% for sulfone 7aaa. In total, a six-step synthesis of guanine derivatives with four diversity points was optimized, achieving overall yields of 44–65%. Chemical libraries were constructed through solid-phase synthesis using optimized conditions in solution-phase synthesis. Four diversity factors were selected to build a guanine derivative library (R1 = aryl, alkyl, R2 = arylamine, R3 = primary, secondary amine, and R4 = alcohol, amine, thiol). Starting material resin 9a was synthesized by swelling Merrifield resin with sodium N’-cyano-N-phenylcarbamidothioate in DMF. In order to synthesize imidazole derivative resin, resin 10a was reacted with ethyl bromoacetate to synthesize 10a-int (Scheme ). In FT-IR, it was confirmed that CN stretching shifted to 2173 cm–1, and weak NH stretching disappeared, and the reaction was terminated by confirming the ketone peak of 1745 cm–1.

Solid-Phase Synthesis of Guanine Derivatives 1

Intermediate 10a-Int was used to synthesize imidazole derivative resin 11a through the Thorpe–Ziegler reaction. In this process, it was confirmed that 1745 cm–1 of ketone stretching shifted to 1686 cm–1, and the end of the reaction was confirmed after seeing that 2173 cm–1 of CN stretching disappeared. The synthesized amino ester resin 11a was stirred with the diversity element phenylisothiocyanate in DMF/pyr (2:1) to synthesize thiourea resin 12aa. In solid-phase synthesis, the swelling effect is different for each solvent, so when pyridine is used as solvent, the swelling effect is weak, and it was confirmed that the reaction did not proceed, so DMF was added as the cosolvent. At the end of the reaction, the stretching peak disappeared at 3496 and 3379 cm–1 of the amine, and a new amine stretching of 3396 cm–1 and thio stretching of 1568 cm–1 were confirmed. Guanylation was performed using the diversity elements amine and AgNO3. In this process, it was confirmed that the thio stretching of 1568 cm–1 disappeared. However, the silver salt left after the reaction could not be removed, so it was not possible to accurately measure the density and weight of resin 13. Guanidine resin 13 reacted with NaOEt as a base to undergo an intramolecular cyclization reaction, resulting in the synthesis of 14aaa. A new cyclic amide bond stretching was observed at 1698 cm–1. The guanine derivative resin 14aaa and mCPBA were reacted to oxidize the sulfide to sulfone. When hydrogenperoxide was used, it reacted with silver salt that could not be removed. So, we could not use the oxidation reaction with Na2WO42H2O as a catalyst. In this process, only 1698 cm–1 of cyclic amide stretching was shifted to 1711 cm–1. Finally, benzylalcohol was reacted with resin 15, and guanine derivative 1aaaa having four diverse elements could be obtained with a 16% yield. Synthesis of guanine derivatives using solid-phase synthesis showed a high yield of 77% per step in 7 steps. However, during the cyclization process, the cyclization selectivity of amine was poor, and compound 16aaaa showing a 4:1 ratio was also synthesized. Comparison of the reaction conditions in the solution-phase synthesis and solid-phase synthesis showed different results. This phenomenon suggests that, even with the introduction of a cosolvent, solvents with low swelling effects like EtOH exert less influence on the reaction in solid-phase synthesis compared to solution-phase synthesis. However, the synthesis of two different derivatives in a single reaction also offers the advantage of increasing the diversity of compounds in the chemical library construction. When R1 = Ph, and R2 = Ph, the cyclization selectivity was synthesized at a ratio of 1:1 when propyl thiol was substituted. In the case of propyl thiol, the reaction proceeds very well with high nucleophilicity when resin deprotection is performed in solid-phase synthesis (Table , and Figure , entries 1–5, 1 = 4–25%, 16 = 4–14%). This result was speculated that when alkyl groups such as butyls were substituted at N-1 of pyrimidinone, the electrophilicity of C-8 was lowered, and the reaction did not proceed relatively better than when N-1 was Ph. In addition, the phenol-based nucleophile reacted better than the nucleophile of benzylalcohol. In the case of benzyl amine in R3, it was shown that selectivity was reversed. When benzyl amine was substituted with a nucleophile rather than arylamine, a cyclization reaction was performed, and propyl thiol was substituted, side products could be obtained at a ratio of 2:3. However, when benzylamine of N-1 was substituted, the benzylalchol-based nucleophile could not be substituted for C-8 due to insufficient electrophilic properties, and 3,5-dimethoxyphenol with high nucleophilic properties showed very good yield (entries 6–10, 1 = 7–12%, 16 = 1–13%). Next, piperidine was substituted into R3, and the final compound could be obtained with a mild yield as a whole (entries 11–15, 1 = 7–16%). When 4-methylaniline was substituted with R2, morpholine was substituted with R3 to obtain a normal yield (entries 16–20, 1 = 12–23%). However, when there was a large donating group such as 4-methoxybenzylalcohol, a compound in which the hydroxyl group was substituted by debenzylation was synthesized at a yield of 18% (entries 18 and 19). This result is presumed to proceed via the Julia reaction mechanism. When R1 = p-toluene, R2 = 4-fluoroaniline, and R3 = 1-phenylpiperazine, the final compound was obtained with a low yield (entries 21–25, 1 = 2–4%). When pyrrolidine was substituted with R3, a final compound was synthesized with a high yield in the case of phenol with high nucleophilicity (entries 26–30, 1 = 9–23%). Even when the amine was substituted with R4, the Julia-type reaction byproduct was obtained (entries 26, 27). Such a result was presumed to occur because the nucleophilicity was too poor for the amine to be substituted. In addition, when the alkyl group of R1 was substituted, it was confirmed that only the arylamine-substituted compound in R2 was synthesized, and the resin deprotection process required a higher temperature than when R1 was aryl (entries 31–35, 1 = 3–8%). Strangely, when R1 was propyl, during the cyclization reaction, the aliphatic amine acted as a nucleophile in a 5.6:1 ratio. This result was also observed when the propyl thiol of R4 was substituted (entry 33, 1 = 4%, 16 = 26%). Additionally, when the R1 substituent was an aliphatic group, nucleophiles with relatively weak nucleophilicity, such as benzyl alcohol derivatives, showed poor reactivity. However, for nucleophiles with stronger nucleophilicity, such as thiol groups, two isomers were obtained with a combined yield of 30%. Next, we propose a plausible mechanism for the AgNO3-mediated guanylation reaction (Figure ). First, under basic conditions, the thiourea moiety in compound 4aa may undergo tautomerization to generate a thiolate anion (S–), which can coordinate with Ag+ to form a reactive 4aa–Ag complex. Subsequently, nucleophilic substitution by the amine occurs via attack on the electrophilic carbon center, displacing the Ag–S moiety and affording the desired guanidine product 5aaa. The resulting AgS– species likely undergoes spontaneous dimerization or condensation to form insoluble Ag2S, which precipitates from the reaction mixture, thereby driving the reaction to completion.

Conclusion

In summary, AgNO3 was used to successfully synthesize imidazologuanidine 6aaa through the guanidine formation reaction. The aryl amine of guanidine derivative 5aa selectively cyclized with a high yield of 94%, thereby oxidizing sulfide into sulfone using mCPBA. Several nucleophiles were used to synthesize a guanine derivative with 4 diversities by substituting the C-8 position of guanine. In addition, a total of 36 derivative libraries were constructed by applying solid-phase synthesis. However, the difference in selectivity between solution-phase synthesis and solid-phase synthesis was confirmed in the cyclization reaction, and it was confirmed through additional experiments that there was no problem with the reaction conditions. It is assumed that this is a problem related to the swelling effect, despite the mixing of solvents in solid-phase synthesis. This synthetic route achieved a high yield of 57–82% in a total of 7 steps. Finally, we built a 4 diversity of guanine derivative library with 36 compounds in 7 steps, and this synthesis method will be used to build an additional library for finding a hit compound.
