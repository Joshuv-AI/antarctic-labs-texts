The mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between university students’ attitudes towards violence against women and cognitive flexibility levels
Authors: Özlem Şahin Altun, Duygu Özer, Mustafa Akyüz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study investigated the mediating effect of self-efficacy in the relationship between university students’ attitudes toward violence against women and their cognitive flexibility levels.MethodThis cross-sectional study was conducted with 655 students studying at the Faculty of Health Sciences and Vocational School of Health Services of a university in the Eastern Anatolia region of Türkiye between January 1–20, 2025. The ISKEBE Attitude Scale, Cognitive Flexibility Scale (CFS), and General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSS) were used for data collection.ResultsIt was determined that 52.4% of the students were undergraduate students and 71.6% were female. It was determined that the total score of ISKEBE was 125.18 (24.07), the total score of CFS was 53.89 (8.18), and the total score of GSS was 60.74 (10.98). While there was a positive and very weakly significant relationship between the mean total score of the students’ ISKEBE and the mean total score of the students’ CFS (r = 0.160; p < 0.001), there was a positive and very weakly significant relationship between the mean total score of the students’ GSS (r = 0.133; p < 0.001). It was determined that self-efficacy had a mediating role in the effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes toward violence against women (R2 = 0.0887; p < 0.001).ConclusionIt was found that students had negative attitudes (i.e., rejecting violence against women) and high levels of cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy. As the cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels of the students increased, it was found that they exhibited negative attitudes (i.e., rejecting violence against women). It may be recommended to develop and implement training programs to improve students’ cognitive flexibility levels and feelings of self-efficacy, and to conduct awareness studies on violence against women.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24291-x.

Introduction

Today, there is a significant increase in violence globally [1]. One of the most concerning forms of this violence is violence against women, which remains a widespread public health and human rights issue [1, 2]. The primary source of such violence often lies in the power imbalance between men and women, perpetuated by patriarchal social structures [2]. Women are reported to be the primary victims, with approximately 1 in 3 women worldwide experiencing physical or sexual violence in their lifetime, especially by their partners [1]. Türkiye ranks second among OECD countries, with 38% of women exposed to violence [3]. These alarming figures underline the urgent need to investigate factors that may help prevent violence against women.

Violence against women has profound negative consequences for both physical and psychological health [4]. Research shows that women suffer psychological harm even more profoundly than physical harm following exposure to violence [4, 5]. This often manifests as decreased self-efficacy, low self-esteem [6, 7], negative self-concept [8], and body dissatisfaction [9], along with serious mental health issues such as post-traumatic stress disorder, depression, suicidal ideation [4, 5].

Given these harmful consequences, it is essential to identify psychological factors that may serve a protective role against violence. One such factor is cognitive flexibility, defined as an individual’s ability to adapt to new situations, consider alternative perspectives, and generate effective solutions to problems [10–13]. Individuals with high cognitive flexibility are better equipped to cope with complex, stressful, or unpredictable situations by evaluating multiple behavioral options and anticipating consequences [13–16]. Furthermore, cognitive flexibility may indirectly influence individuals’ belief in their own abilities, which is crucial in coping with challenging situations.

According to social cognitive theory and empirical studies, cognitive flexibility forms a crucial cognitive foundation for the development of self-efficacy beliefs [10–12]. Individuals with greater cognitive flexibility are more likely to perceive themselves as capable of handling problems and achieving goals, thereby enhancing their self-efficacy [13, 14, 17]. This positive relationship between cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy has been demonstrated across various contexts, particularly in demanding situations such as healthcare education and professional environments [17–19].

Self-efficacy, in turn, plays a key role in shaping individuals’ attitudes and behaviors towards social issues, including violence against women [6, 7, 20]. El-Etreby et al. [7] demonstrated that higher levels of self-efficacy are associated with rejecting violence, adopting more effective conflict resolution strategies, and maintaining healthier relationship dynamics. Conversely, low self-efficacy is linked to difficulties in coping with relational challenges, which may increase the likelihood of normalizing violence or being exposed to it [7, 21].

Building on these findings, recent research across diverse populations suggests that cognitive flexibility not only enhances individuals’ problem-solving abilities and emotional regulation but also plays a significant role in fostering self-efficacy [22–24]. These results enrich the conceptual framework of the present study by reinforcing the position of cognitive flexibility as a psychological resource that promotes more adaptive attitudes toward violence against women through enhanced self-efficacy. While the independent roles of cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy have been widely studied, to our knowledge, no research has systematically explored the mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between cognitive flexibility and university students’ attitudes toward violence against women. By addressing this gap, the present study provides novel insights into how cognitive and belief-based mechanisms interact to shape socially significant attitudes.

University students in healthcare fields constitute an important subgroup, as they are future health professionals with key responsibilities in preventing violence against women [25]. Enhancing their cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels may promote non-tolerant attitudes toward violence, which is essential for providing effective support to victims. Therefore, the aim of this study has to investigate whether self-efficacy mediates the relationship between cognitive flexibility and university students’ attitudes toward violence against women.

Aim

This study investigated the mediating effect of self-efficacy in the relationship between university students’ attitudes towards violence against women and their cognitive flexibility levels.

Method

Design

A cross-sectional and correlation study [26]. A cross-sectional design was chosen as it allows for the examination of the relationships between cognitive and attitudinal variables in a large student sample within a limited time frame. While this design precludes causal conclusions, it is appropriate for the exploratory nature of this study.

Population and sample

The population of the study consisted of 2841 university students enrolled in the Vocational School of Health Services (n = 2040) and Faculty of Health Sciences (n = 801) of a state university in Türkiye. The University’s Faculty of Health Sciences has Nursing, Nutrition and Dietetics, Physiotherapy and Rehabilitation Departments, while the Vocational School of Health Services has Home Care, Elderly Care, Physiotherapy, Oral and Dental Health, Medical Imaging, Medical Laboratory, Medical Documentation and Secretariat programs. Students from all these departments and programs were invited to participate in the study. The study sample was calculated as a minimum of 339 students with a 95% confidence interval and 5% margin of error according to the minimum sample size formula. The study was conducted between January 1–30, 2025, and 655 students agreed to participate in the study.

Data collection

An online questionnaire (Google Forms) was used to reach many students. The survey link was shared with the students, and they were invited to participate in the study. The students were informed about the study before they started to fill in the forms and those who volunteered to participate in the study were asked to fill in the scales. Students were also informed that they could leave the study at any time and that their data would be kept confidential. Since the scales were filled in online and it was compulsory to answer each question, there was no missing data. In the online forms, the settings were organized so that each student could answer the questions just once.

Data measurement

The Personal Information Form, ISKEBE Attitude Scale, Cognitive Flexibility Scale and General Self-efficacy Scale were used to collect the study data.

Information form

The form, which was created by the researchers in line with the literature [7, 21, 25], consists of a total of 10 questions including questions such as age, gender, family economic status, smoking and alcohol use.

ISKEBE attitude scale

The scale was developed by Kanbay et al. [27], to determine individuals’ attitudes towards violence against women. The scale consists of two sub-dimensions, “Attitudes towards the body (Sexual and physical violence)” and “Attitudes towards identity (Psychological and economic violence)” and a total of 30 questions. Questions 5 and 24 are reverse scored. The total score of the scale is obtained by summing the scores obtained from the two factors. The score that can be obtained from the first factor is between 16 and 80 and the score that can be obtained from the second factor is between 14 and 70. The score that can be obtained from the overall scale is in the range of 30–150. A high score indicates a negative attitude towards violence against women (the person is against violence against women), while a low score indicates a positive attitude towards violence against women (the person is not against violence against women). The scale has a cut-off point; individuals who score above 90 points are interpreted as being against violence against women, while those who score below 90 points are interpreted as not being against violence against women. Cronbach’s α value for the first factor was found to be 0.80, 0.83 for the second factor, and 0.86 for the overall scale [27]. In this study, Cronbach’s α value for the overall scale was found to be 0.95 (Table 2). In addition, the fit indices obtained from the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that the scale demonstrated an acceptable level of model fit (CFI = 0.94; GFI = 0.92; AGFI = 0.91; TLI = 0.93; RMSEA = 0.071). These results suggest that the hypothesized factor structure adequately fits the data [28].

Cognitive flexibility scale (CFS)

The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale developed by Martin and Rubin [29] was conducted by Çelikkaleli [30]. The scale, which consists of a total of 12 items and a single dimension, is a 6-point Likert-type scale (1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree). Items 2, 3, 6 and 10 are reverse scored. Scores that can be obtained from the scale vary between 12 and 72. High scores indicate that the level of cognitive flexibility is also high. Çelikkaleli [30] found the Cronbach’s α value of the scale to be 0.71. In this study, the Cronbach α value of the scale was determined as 0.76 (Table 2). In addition, the fit indices obtained from the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that the scale demonstrated an acceptable level of model fit (CFI = 0.93; GFI = 0.91; AGFI = 0.90; TLI = 0.92; RMSEA = 0.082). These results suggest that the hypothesized factor structure adequately fits the data [28].

General self-efficacy scale (GSS)

The scale was developed by Sherer et al. [31], to determine the self-efficacy of individuals. The Turkish validity and reliability study of the scale was conducted by Yıldırım and İlhan [32]. The scale is a 5-point Likert type and consists of 17 items. Items 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16 and 17 are reverse scored. The scale consists of three sub-dimensions: initiation, persistence, and maintenance effort-persistence. The total score that can be obtained from the scale varies between 17 and 85. An increase in the total score obtained from the scale indicates an increase in self-efficacy belief. While the Cronbach’s α value of the scale was found to be 0.80 in Yıldırım and İlhan’s study [32], the Cronbach’s α value was found to be 0.86 in this study (Table 2). In addition, the fit indices obtained from the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) indicated that the scale demonstrated an acceptable level of model fit (CFI = 0.90; GFI = 0.91; AGFI = 0.91; TLI = 0.90; RMSEA = 0.089). These results suggest that the hypothesized factor structure adequately fits the data [28].

Ethics

This study was conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Before the study was conducted, the ethics committee approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of a university (numbered 2024-SBB- 0967 and dated December 11, 2024) and the institutional permission was obtained from Vocational School of Health Services and Faculty of Health Sciences, where the study was to be conducted. After the students were informed verbally and in writing about the study, their written informed consent was obtained.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY). The Shapiro-wilk test was used for normal distribution of the data in the study. Descriptive data were analyzed using number, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. Mann-Whitney U test, Kruskal-Wallis test, Student t-test, and ANOVA were used for scale means comparisons. Pearson correlation analysis was used to determine the relationship between the scales. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was used to calculate the reliability coefficients of the scales. Mediation analysis was conducted to examine the effect of self-efficacy on the relationship between attitudes towards violence against women and cognitive flexibility level. In the model established for this purpose, it was assumed that cognitive flexibility affects self-efficacy, and self-efficacy affects the attitude toward violence against women. Mediation analyses were conducted with IBM SPSS Statistics Process macro v5.0. The regression method based on the bootstrap method; Model 4 was selected with a 5000 resampling option. p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, the directionality of the mediation model should be interpreted with caution, and causal inferences cannot be made.

In the present mediation analysis, the mediating role of self-efficacy in the relationship between cognitive flexibility and attitudes towards violence against women was examined. The overall model was statistically significant, F(2, 652) = 9.60, p < 0.001, explaining approximately 8.9% of the variance in attitudes towards violence against women (R² = 0.0887) (Fig. 1). The findings indicated that cognitive flexibility significantly predicted self-efficacy (a path), B = 0.736, SE = 0.043, t = 16.78, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.650, 0.822]. Moreover, self-efficacy significantly predicted attitudes towards violence against women (b path), B = 0.543, SE = 0.101, t = 1.41, p = 0.019, 95% CI [0.055, 0.341]. The direct effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes towards violence against women (c′ path) was also statistically significant, B = 0.365, SE = 0.135, t = 2.69, p = 0.043, 95% CI [0.099, 0.631]. In addition, the indirect effect of cognitive flexibility on attitudes towards violence against women through self-efficacy (a × b path) was also statistically significant, B = 0.105, Bootstrap SE = 0.074, 95% Bootstrap CI [0.035, 0.256]. Since the confidence interval does not include zero, this indicates the presence of a statistically significant indirect effect. These findings suggest that cognitive flexibility has a significant and positive direct effect on attitudes towards violence against women, and that self-efficacy plays a significant mediating role in this relationship. In other words, higher levels of cognitive flexibility are associated with increased self-efficacy, which in turn is associated with more positive attitudes towards violence against women (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, while university students were found to have negative attitudes (i.e., rejecting violence against women), their cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels were found to be moderate. Male gender, low economic level, associate degree education level, and smoking were found to be higher in terms of tolerant attitudes towards violence against women. As the cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels of the students increased, it was found that they exhibited negative attitudes (i.e., rejecting violence against women). It was determined that self-efficacy played a mediating role in the relationship between attitudes towards violence against women and cognitive flexibility level. However, given the small effect sizes and modest explained variance, these relationships should be interpreted with caution and may not imply strong practical significance.

In this study, it was determined that many university students showed negative attitudes towards violence against women. In other studies, conducted in line with the data of this study, it was found that university students exhibited negative attitudes towards violence against women [7, 33, 34]. In the study conducted by Seçgin et al. [35], the data from the studies conducted in the last five years in Türkiye were analyzed and it was found that 14.1–43.0% of women in Türkiye were exposed to one or more violence. It is also reported in the study that these figures increased especially after the Covid-19 pandemic [35]. Zark et al. [36] examined intercultural differences in students’ attitudes toward intimate partner violence and emphasized that the United States has a more negative attitude towards violence against women than Asian countries. It can be said that the cultural characteristics, patriarchal structure, gender inequality, and traditional gender roles of societies are effective in these findings. In addition, in this study, sociodemographic data were found to affect students’ attitudes toward violence against women. It was found that male students had more tolerant attitudes towards violence against women. Previous studies also revealed that male students were more likely to justify beating their wives and believed that beaten women benefited from beatings [37, 38]. In patriarchal societies, men see themselves as superior to women and are more likely to accept and normalize violence against women [37]. In addition, students with low socioeconomic status and smokers were found to have more tolerant attitudes towards violence against women. In the literature, it is also stated that low socioeconomic status and substance use are associated with violence against women [1, 39]. In particular, the situations experienced by people with substance abuse such as lack of social skills, inability to control anger, and attention deficits are reflected externally as violent behavior [14]. It was also found that associate degree students had more tolerant attitudes towards violence against women. It can be said that courses on sexual equality, women’s rights, and prevention of violence against women should be added to the course curriculum of associate degree students.

It can be said that the cognitive flexibility levels of the students participating in this study are at a moderate level. When the literature is examined, the cognitive flexibility levels of the participants in the studies conducted with working health professionals were found to be at medium and good levels [40, 41]. It is vital for health professionals, who play a critical role in human life, to be able to react and intervene quickly and effectively against emerging events. Therefore, it can be said that interventions to increase their cognitive flexibility are needed.

In this study, it can be said that the self-efficacy levels of university students are at a medium level. Again, in this direction, some studies found the self-efficacy levels of university students at a medium level [17, 42]. Since it has been reported that self-efficacy strengthens mental health by giving the individual the power to cope with problems [17–19], it can be emphasized that it should be at a high level, especially in health professionals.

In this study, it was determined that university students exhibited negative attitudes towards violence against women as their cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels increased. In addition, it was determined that self-efficacy had a mediating role in the relationship between violence against women attitude and cognitive flexibility level. Nevertheless, the mediation effect explained a limited proportion of the variance (8.9%), suggesting that other factors also contribute to attitudes toward violence against women. There is no study that evaluates cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels together, which are effective on attitudes towards violence against women. For this reason, when similar studies were examined, it was found that individuals exhibited more negative attitudes towards violence against women with an increase in self-efficacy and self-esteem levels [6, 7, 43]. It has been reported in studies that high cognitive flexibility reduces stressful events and increases positive coping styles [18, 19, 41, 44, 45]. In the study of Doğan Laçin and Yalçın [19], a positive relationship was found between cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy and positive coping strategies. Another study [23] examined female university students and determined that cognitive flexibility significantly mediates the impact of anxiety on self‑efficacy, highlighting flexibility’s role in bolstering self‑efficacy. Çakmak Tolan and Kara’s study [22] found cognitive flexibility mediates the effect of general self‑efficacy on social anxiety. Orakcı and Khalili [24] demonstrated a mediating role of self‑efficacy in flexibility‑critical thinking processes. Specifically, similar mechanisms have been identified, where cognitive flexibility predicts reflective thinking, and critical thinking disposition, with self-efficacy acting as a mediating factor [16, 24]. This study extends existing research by demonstrating that cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy jointly influence social attitudes, specifically attitudes toward violence against women. These findings suggest that enhancing cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy may play a critical role in shaping non-tolerant attitudes toward violence. From a practical standpoint, intervention programs designed to reduce tolerant attitudes toward violence against women should incorporate components aimed at developing flexible thinking and self-efficacy, particularly among university students in health-related fields. Strengthening these psychological resources can equip individuals with more effective problem-solving abilities and promote clearer, more adaptive attitudes toward violence, thereby reducing the likelihood of engaging in or tolerating violent behaviors.

Conclusion

It was determined that university students exhibited negative attitudes (i.e., rejecting violence against women) as their cognitive flexibility and self-efficacy levels increased; and it was determined that self-efficacy had a mediating role in the relationship between attitudes toward violence against women and cognitive flexibility levels. Male gender, low economic level, associate degree education level and smoking were found to be risk factors in terms of positive attitudes toward violence against women. It is extremely important to determine the attitudes of future health professionals, who have important roles in combating violence against women, on this issue. During the training process in which health professionals prepare for the profession, trainings, courses and activities on issues such as recognizing and recognizing violence against women, women’s rights and gender equality should be included in the training program. Increasing their knowledge and awareness about violence starting from their student years will be beneficial in terms of recognizing violence and implementing the right initiatives for individuals. A multidisciplinary struggle should be initiated to reduce violence against women. Therefore, cooperation should be established between health institutions and the necessary institutions, and the aim should be to contribute to public health by creating the necessary policies. Violence against women should not be perceived as a sign of male power and should not be accepted or normalized by society. There is an urgent need for studies that will break down society’s prejudices on this issue and increase social awareness. It can be suggested that psychosocial interventions that will increase self-efficacy and cognitive flexibility, which are understood to be effective on society’s attitudes toward violence against women, should be implemented and the results should be published. In addition, social improvements such as improving individuals’ living conditions, increasing their education levels, and reducing substance use can be effective in combating violence against women.
