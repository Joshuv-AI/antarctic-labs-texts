Single-cell RNA sequencing reveals lipid metabolism disorders in the retina in spontaneous high myopia
Authors: Bo Bao, Jinpeng Liu, Yunxiao Xie, Ruofan Xi, Yuecong Sun, Jingzhe Yan, Jizhao Xin, Zhaohui Yang, Xuewei Yin, Dadong Guo, Hongsheng Bi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMyopia is one of the most common eye diseases affecting children and adolescents, with its etiology often attributed to a combination of genetic and environmental factors. This study utilized single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) technology to investigate gene expression differences between guinea pigs with spontaneous high myopia (SHM) and those with normal vision.ResultsLens-induced myopia (LIM) and SHM guinea pigs exhibit significant retinal structural abnormalities and functional impairments, characterized by reduced retinal thickness and abnormal electroretinogram (ERG) responses, indicating progressive retinal dysfunction during myopia development. scRNA-seq and molecular experiments revealed that the ceramide synthase 5 (CERS5) / ceramide synthase 6 (CERS6) / alkaline ceramidase 3 (ACER3) / phospholipid phosphatase 1 (PLPP1) / prosaposin (PSAP) / UDP-glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase (UGCG) signaling axis is significantly activated in the retinas of myopic guinea pigs, suggesting dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism. These changes were associated with an increased expression of SPHK1 and a shift in receptor expression from S1PR1 to S1PR2, indicating a transition in lipid signaling from a physiological state to a stress-related pathological state. Additionally, the activation of the P62/NRF2/KEAP1 pathway confirmed enhanced oxidative stress in the myopic retina. This study demonstrates that the CERS5/CERS6/ACER3/PLPP1/PSAP/UGCG signaling axis mediates disruption of sphingolipid metabolism and oxidative stress in the myopic guinea pig retina.ConclusionsThe present study demonstrates that the CERS5/CERS6/ACER3/PLPP1/PSAP/UGCG signaling axis mediates sphingolipid metabolic dysregulation and oxidative stress in the retinas of myopic guinea pigs. Mechanistically, the abnormal activation of the SPHK1-S1P signaling pathway, the shift in receptors from S1PR1 to S1PR2, and the subsequent activation of the P62/KEAP1/NRF2 pathway collectively led to changes in retinal morphology (such as reduced thickness) and functional impairment. These findings establish a mechanistic link between lipid metabolic imbalance and retinal pathology in myopia offering potential therapeutic targets for preventing retinal damage associated with myopia.Keywords: Single-cell RNA sequencing, Myopia, Retina, Lipid Metabolism, Oxidative stress

Background

Myopia, a major category of refractive error, exhibits a steadily rising prevalence with a pronounced trend toward younger onset [1]. Research indicates that by mid-century, the proportion of myopic individuals will reach 50%, with high myopia accounting for as much as 10% of cases [1]. The primary cause of myopia is the excessive elongation of the eyeball, which leads to ongoing stretching of the retina and choroid. This condition results in reduced thickness and decreased vascular density, severely compromising visual quality [2].

Beyond elongated eyeball length, high myopia is associated with an increased risk of complications, including macular degeneration, retinal detachment due to peripheral retinal tears, and myopic macular holes [3–4]. The causes of high myopia are currently categorized into genetic and environmental factors. Research indicates that high myopia exhibits significant familial clustering and strong hereditary tendencies. Beyond this, common contributing factors include visual habits, working distance, and time spent outdoors [5–6].

The retina, located in the innermost layer of the eyeball, is a neural tissue that converts light signals from the external environment into neural electrical signals during visual transmission and performs preliminary processing. Under physiological conditions, the retina’s layers have distinct functions with clearly defined boundaries, including pigment epithelial cells, photoreceptor cells, bipolar cells, ganglion cells, and Müller glial cells. Metabolic abnormalities and functional impairments in these cells are closely associated with pathological changes observed on the retina [7].

A growing body of research indicates that lipid metabolism disorders play a significant role in the onset and progression of myopia and pathological myopia (PM) and are closely associated with oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and structural and functional retinal damage. In animal models of myopia, metabolomics studies have revealed that myopia induces widespread lipid metabolic abnormalities, including elevated levels of pro-inflammatory lipid molecules and decreased levels of neuroprotective metabolites. Red light intervention, however, can reverse these changes and improve choroidal thinning and refractive errors, suggesting that lipid metabolic remodeling contributes to myopia-related retinal damage [8]. In addition, a high-fat diet can significantly increase levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein (LDL), and is associated with reduced retinal thickness (RT) and choroidal thickness (ChT), as well as reduced choroidal blood flow. It also induces elevated levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, and NF-κB, suggesting that systemic lipid metabolism abnormalities can increase susceptibility to myopia [9].

Multi-omics studies have further confirmed that lipid metabolism, immune-inflammatory, and oxidative stress pathways are significantly enriched in PM and, together with extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, biomechanical abnormalities of the sclera, and alterations in neurotransmitter signaling, contribute to the progression of myopia [10]. Studies based on metabolomics and machine learning have also revealed significant lipid metabolic reprogramming in patients with PM, accompanied by oxidative stress and abnormalities in energy metabolism [11]. Among these, lipid-related metabolites such as azelaic acid exert antioxidant protective effects on ARPE-19 cells, suggesting that lipid metabolic abnormalities may be directly involved in pathological regulation [11].

Evidence suggests that abnormalities in lipid metabolism can further damage the retina by inducing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [12, 13]. A high-fat diet can lead to lipid metabolic disorders in the retina and RPE, activate inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways, and ultimately cause structural and functional damage [12]. In a dark-induced zebrafish model, levels of ROS, MDA, and 4-HNE were significantly elevated, accompanied by lipofuscin deposition in the RPE and outer segment structural abnormalities, suggesting that lipid peroxidation may be involved in myopia-related retinal degeneration [13]. Taken together, lipid metabolism disorders are not only a key metabolic feature of myopia but may also collectively drive the progression of myopia-related retinal pathology through inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and tissue remodeling.

Lipids participate in biological processes primarily through three pathways: contributing to cell membrane structure, engaging in cellular energy metabolism, and acting as signaling molecules in signal transduction [14]. Multiple studies confirm that lipid metabolism abnormalities, such as those involving phospholipids and polyunsaturated fatty acids, contribute to the pathogenesis of various ocular diseases, including diabetic retinopathy, retinoblastoma, and ocular abnormalities associated with schizophrenia [15–17]. In this study, we aim to investigate whether myopia is associated with abnormalities in lipid metabolism. The Lipid Metabolites and Pathways Strategy (LIPID MAPS) system classifies lipids into eight major categories: fatty acyls (FA), glycerolipids (GL), glycerophospholipids (GP), sphingolipids (SP), sterol lipids (ST), prenol lipids (PR), saccharolipids (SL), and polyketides (PK). This study focuses on sphingomyelin metabolism.

Sphingolipid metabolism plays a crucial structural and signaling regulatory role in the retina, and abnormalities in this process are closely associated with various retinal degenerative diseases. In age-related macular degeneration (AMD) and polypoidal choroidal vasculopathy (PCV), sphingolipid molecules such as sphingosylphosphatidylserine are significantly associated with macular structure and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), suggesting that abnormalities in sphingolipid metabolism can directly affect retinal structure and visual function [18]. In retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), inhibition of neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase) disrupts the normal breakdown of sphingomyelin into ceramides, leading to mitochondrial oxidative stress, RGC loss, and impaired axonal transport, indicating that sphingomyelin homeostasis is critical for retinal neuronal survival [19]. Further studies have shown that acid sphingomyelinase (ASMase) and neutral sphingomyelinase (NSMase) are widely distributed in the retina and optic nerve head. Under conditions of ischemia and elevated intraocular pressure, ASMase activity significantly increases, accompanied by ceramide accumulation, suggesting that sphingolipid metabolism is a key mediating pathway in retinal stress-induced damage [20]. Studies on diabetic retinopathy have also found that ASM-dependent mitochondrial ceramide accumulation can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammatory activation in RPE cells, while ASM inhibition can improve metabolic function [21]; under endoplasmic reticulum stress conditions, long-chain ceramides (CERs) and C1P significantly increase, accompanied by NF-κB activation and enhanced apoptosis [22].

Multiple studies have demonstrated that oxidative stress contributes to the induction of sphingomyelin metabolic disorders. In this process, sphingomyelin hydrolysis increases, and ceramides accumulate continuously, ultimately leading to various ophthalmic diseases. Elevated levels of Sphingomyelin Synthase 2 (SMS2) have been detected in the corneas and conjunctiva of benzalkonium chloride (BAC)-induced dry eye disease mice. Conversely, SMS2-KO mice exhibited reduced lipid peroxidation and a trend toward diminished corneal damage. SMS2 promotes the progression of dry eye disease by activating oxidative stress-induced lipid dysregulation and inflammatory responses [23]. In age-related macular degeneration studies, retinal pigment epithelial cells in systemic iron-overload mice accumulated substantial lipid peroxidation adducts and lysosomal dysfunction, leading to sphingolipid accumulation, including ceramide, and cell death. These findings suggest that oxidative stress processes and their products can alter sphingolipid metabolism, contributing to retinal degenerative damage [24].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), as a mature high-throughput sequencing technology, enables analysis of cellular genomes and transcriptomes at the single-cell level and is widely applied across various disease types. In myopia research, this technique has identified a novel rod cell subpopulation and revealed abnormal neuronal signaling pathways [25]. Additionally, a study on myopic mice uncovered extensive immunological alterations characterized by significantly increased macrophage numbers across multiple tissues and enhanced macrophage-mediated cell communication [26]. Beyond conventional lens-induced myopia, this study focuses on spontaneous high myopic guinea pigs to detect retinal metabolic differences and explore how metabolic abnormalities resulting from genetic and environmental factors affect visual quality.

Methods

Animals

Ethical approval

This study has been approved by the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Shandong University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (AWE-2022-055) and follows close to the line of the Animal Research for Vision and Ophthalmology (ARVO) Statement of Principles.

Animal breeding and experimental handling

All experimental animals in this study were tricolor guinea pigs (Cavia porcellus, British Shorthair), with an initial body weight of 140 ± 10 g.

All experimental animals were housed in standard animal cages throughout the study period. The animal facility maintained a 12-hour light/dark cycle daily, with temperature and humidity controlled at 24 ± 2 °C and 50 ± 5% RH, respectively. Prior to the experiment, all guinea pigs underwent a comprehensive physical examination to exclude those with systemic or ocular conditions that could affect the study, such as albinism, corneal lesions, or glaucoma.

Based on previous lens-induced findings, guinea pigs with baseline myopic refractive error exceeding − 6.0 diopter (D) and axial length greater than 8.2 mm were selected as study subjects [27]. Animals were divided into three groups: the normal control (NC) group, the lens-induced myopia (LIM) group, and the spontaneous high myopia (SHM) group. After the experiment commenced, guinea pigs in the NC and SHM groups received no intervention. Guinea pigs in the LIM group wore − 6.0D lenses in their right eyes until the experiment concluded. In preliminary experiments, we observed that some guinea pigs enrolled in a state of high myopia met the study requirements. However, during subsequent rearing, these animals reverted to emmetropia. These guinea pigs did not participate in the subsequent experimental validation in this study. The experimental animals were maintained for six weeks, with ocular parameters measured at the end of weeks 4 and 6. The experimental animals used in this study were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital at a concentration of 50 mg/mL and a dose of 200 mg/kg.

Ocular parameter measurement

Before the experiment began and at four and six weeks after myopia induction, all experimental animals underwent measurements of refractive power, axial length, and retinal thickness. To minimize experimental error, all measurements in this study were performed by the same researcher.

Refraction measurements were performed in dim lighting conditions. Prior to measurement, 0.5% compound tropicamide eye drops were instilled into the guinea pig’s conjunctival sac, 1 drop per application, with a 5-10-minute interval between applications. Measurements commenced 15–20 min after the final instillation. Bilateral refraction was measured using a Photorefractor (Steritech GmbH, Germany). Ten consecutive measurements were taken per eye, with the mean value serving as the final result.

Axial length was measured using an SK-3000 A ultrasonic device. Prior to measurement, 0.4% procaine hydrochloride eye drops were instilled into the guinea pig’s conjunctival sac, one drop per administration, for a total of one instillation. After instillation, excess solution was removed with a cotton swab. The probe was randomly placed perpendicular to the visual axis and gently contacted the anterior corneal surface. Ten consecutive measurements were taken for each eye, with the average serving as the final result.

Retinal thickness in guinea pigs was measured using SD-OCT (Heidelberg, Germany). Measurements centered on the optic nerve, capturing retinal thickness at the superior, inferior, nasal, and temporal sides of the optic disc. Following measurement, as illustrated in Fig. 3, we defined the measurement range as the distance from the retinal nerve fiber layer to the retinal pigment epithelium layer—corresponding to the distance between the blue and yellow lines in Fig. 3C —based on the research by Ferguson, L. R. et al. [28]. The average retinal thickness across the four directions served as the final thickness measurement for each eye.

Finally, a ground wire was attached to the right forelimb, and the examination table was placed in the machine for testing.

ScRNA-seq sample preparation and experimental workflow

Tissue processing and single-cell suspension preparation

At the fourth week of the experiment, guinea pigs were euthanized, and the right eyeball was removed to isolate retinal tissue. To minimize the impact of inter-individual variability on the experimental results, the retinal data for each group of guinea pigs in this study were derived from mixed samples from different individuals. Following the Epidermis Dissociation Kit protocol (Miltenyi Bioscience Incorporation Limited, 51429 Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) to dissociate the tissue into a single-cell suspension. Following quality control assessment (Countstar Rigel S2), samples that passed quality control were processed using the 10x Genomic Single Cell system for analysis.

On-machine detection

After loading the single-cell suspension, the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller’s microfluidic cross-linking system mixes single cells with 10x Barcode gel beads. These are then separated by oil phase to form GEMs (Gel Bead-in-Emulsions). Within the GEMs, the gel beads dissolve, enabling intracellular cell lysis and reverse transcription reactions. In valid GEMs, the 10x Barcode is ligated to the cDNA product. Next, the oil droplets are disrupted, and PCR amplification is performed using the cDNA as a template. After cDNA amplification is complete, quality control is performed on the amplified products. Following the reaction, a purification process removes short fragments and reaction reagents, ultimately yielding full-length cDNA.

Construction of cDNA sequencing library and sequencing

Full-length cDNA is fragmented into 200–300 bp fragments. PCR is used to introduce P5, P7, and R2 (Read2 sequencing primers) at both ends of specific cDNA fragments. Following amplification, the products are purified to yield a standardized cDNA sequencing library with a defined sequence structure, ready for subsequent sequencing.

The sample cDNA library was processed according to Illumina’s established workflow. The labeled library mixture was loaded onto flow cells and placed in the sequencer to complete cluster formation. Dual-end sequencing was performed during the sequence-by-synthesis (SBS) process. Sequencing quality control is performed using Illumina’s data collection software. Upon completion, the instrument software identifies nucleotides (a process called base calling) and estimates the accuracy of the base calls, generating raw data (. fastq files). The sequencing instrument employed is the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, utilizing paired-end (PE) sequencing with a read length of 150 bases.

Data preprocessing

Raw sequencing data were quality-controlled using FastQC (v0.11.2). After passing quality control, samples were analyzed with CellRanger 6.0, the foundational analysis software from the 10x Genomics platform. After sequencing completion for all samples, individual sample data analysis was performed first. Subsequently, multiple samples from the same project were merged for integrated multi-sample analysis.

Cell clustering and annotation

Cell clustering was performed using the R package Seurat. Visualization of gene distribution plots and UMI distribution plots assessed all cells and expressed genes across samples. R version 4.5.1, and the “Seurat” package version 5.1.0. For the data quality control phase, we set the parameters as follows: nCount is between 50 and 60,000, and nFeature is between 50 and 8,000. Based on quality control results, high-quality cell selection criteria were set as follows: cells with ≥ 200 genes, ranked by UMI count at the 99th percentile, and mitochondrial gene expression ≤ 25%. After filtering out low-quality cells, the remaining high-quality cells were normalized for gene expression. The mean and standard deviation for each gene were calculated to identify 2,000 highly variable genes that represent the overall cellular state.

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the single-cell data to map the high-dimensional expression data into a low-dimensional space. After selecting principal components, cells were clustered.

Cell type annotation was performed using the scRNA-seq annotation software Single R and cell-specific marker genes reported in the literature.

Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

For the differentially expressed genes calculated for each cell type, enrichment analysis was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO, http://geneontology.org/), KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes), Disease Ontology (DO, http://disease-ontology.org/), and Reactome (http://www.reactome.org/) databases. Fisher’s exact test was used to calculate pathway significance levels, identifying gene enrichment results relevant to the cell type.

We first identified all statistically enriched terms (GO/KEGG terms, canonical pathways, hallmark gene sets, etc., based on the default choices under Express Analysis or your choice during Custom Analysis). Cumulative hypergeometric p-values and enrichment factors were then calculated and used for filtering. The remaining significant terms were then hierarchically clustered into a tree based on Kappa-statistical similarities in their gene memberships (similar to what is used on the NCI DAVID site). Then a 0.3 kappa score was used as the threshold to split the tree into term clusters. The terms within each cluster are exported to the Excel spreadsheet named “Enrichment Analysis”. The significance level is P < 0.01.

Sample preparation and experimental protocol for lipidomics analysis

Tissue processing and pre-analysis preparation

Retinal samples were dissected from the guinea pig eyes of each group under low-temperature, sterile conditions. After dissection, the samples were placed in liquid nitrogen and flash-frozen for 30 min in preparation for subsequent experiments. To minimize the impact of inter-individual variability, all samples tested in this study were obtained from different individuals.

After 20 mg of each sample was weighed, 400 µL of methanol-water (1:1, v/v) was added, and the mixture was ground using a mill. Subsequently, 400 µL of chloroform was added, followed by vortexing for 30 s and sonication for 10 min. The mixture was then incubated overnight at − 40 °C. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min, 300 µL of the lower chloroform phase was transferred to an LC–MS sample vial and air-dried. The lipid residue was resuspended in 300 µL of isopropanol–methanol, vortexed for 30 s, and sonicated in an ice-water bath for 3 min. The resulting solution was transferred to a 1.5 mL centrifuge tube and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. Finally, 150 µL of the supernatant was transferred to an LC–MS sample vial with an insert for LC–MS analysis.

Mass spectrometry instrumental conditions

Chromatographic conditions were as follows: A Waters ACQUITY UPLC I-Class plus/Thermo QE system was used with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C8 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters). The injection volume was 3 µL, and the column temperature was 55 °C. Mobile phase A: acetonitrile: water = 6:4 (v/v, containing 10 mM ammonium acetate); Mobile phase B: isopropanol: acetonitrile = 9:1 (v/v, containing 10 mM ammonium acetate).

Gradient elution conditions were as follows: 0–1.5 min, 32% B; 1.5–15.5 min, 85% B; 15.5–15.6 min, 97% B; 15.6–18 min, 97% B; 18–18.1 min, 32% B; 18.1–20 min, 32% B. Flow rate: 0.26 mL/min. MS conditions: Full MS/dd-MS2 (TOP 10) scanning. Heated Electrospray Ionization (HESI). In negative ion mode, MS voltage: -3000 V; aux gas heater temperature: 350 °C.

Pathological section preparation and scanning

At the sixth week of the experiment, three guinea pigs per group were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital. The right eyeball was removed, rinsed with 0.9% saline to remove adherent blood, and excess connective tissue was excised. The eyeball was then placed in FAS eyeball fixative (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) for at least 24 h. The specimens were then embedded in paraffin for subsequent analysis.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E)

Sections were prepared from eye wax blocks, mounted on slides, and subjected to decolorization followed by dehydration to water. Sections were stained with H&E separately, then dehydrated and mounted for subsequent scanning.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Sections were dewaxed in water, subjected to antigen retrieval, and then treated with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sample locations were marked using a Liquid Blocker PAP Pen, then blocked with BSA. Diluted primary antibody was applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibody incubation proceeded at room temperature the following day. Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin solution. Sections were then dehydrated, cleared, and mounted for subsequent scanning.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

After dewaxing sections in water and performing antigen retrieval, mark sample locations with Liquid Blocker PAP Pen, then block with BSA. Place sections in a humid chamber, add primary antibody, and incubate overnight at 4 °C. Incubate the secondary antibody at room temperature the following day. After gently tapping off excess solution, add the DAPI staining solution and incubate at room temperature in the dark. Finally, add an autofluorescence quencher, let it stand for ten minutes, rinse with running water, and mount for subsequent scanning.

After preparing the above sections, all slides were scanned using the KFBIO Digital Pathology Scanner (Konfoong Bioinformation Tech Co., Ltd) with KFSlideOS as the analysis software. Three independent views were randomly selected from each group for analysis at a fixed magnification.

Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM)

After completing the experimental procedures for each group of animals, the eyes were removed and the retinas were dissected. Electron microscopy fixative (2.5% glutaraldehyde, pH 7.0–7.5) was added to a culture dish. The retinal samples were trimmed to an appropriate size in the fixative and placed in a sufficient volume of fixative for fixation. After rinsing and dehydration, the samples were embedded in Epon 812 resin using standard TEM preparation methods. After preparing semi-thin sections, ultrathin sections ranging from 70 nm to 100 nm were obtained using an LKB-V ultramicrotome. The sections were electron-stained with lead citrate and uranyl acetate using a JEOL-1200E transmission electron microscope, and images were recorded using the MORADA-G2 system.

Molecular experiments

At weeks 4 and 6 of myopia induction, guinea pigs were euthanized with sodium pentobarbital. The right eyeball was removed, excess fascial tissue excised, and blood thoroughly cleaned before separating the retinal tissue for subsequent experiments.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA extraction was strictly performed according to the instructions of the SPARKeasy Tissue/Cell RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (SparkJade Science Co., Ltd., Jinan, China). After the experiment, RNA concentrations in each group were measured using a UV spectrophotometer (K5600; Beijing Kaio Technology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). cDNA was then synthesized using HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR (gDNA wiper) (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) for qPCR experiments.

Experiments were conducted in 96-well plates (NEST Biotechnology, Wuxi, China) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd.) as the fluorescent dye. Primer sequences are presented in Table 1. qPCR amplification conditions were 94 °C for 5 s (1 cycle), followed by 45 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s, 54 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 10 s. Expression levels of all target genes were normalized to GAPDH, and results were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method [29].

Western blotting

Western blotting is used to detect the protein expression levels of various factors in the experiment. Fresh retinal tissue was mixed with Radio Immunoprecipitation Assay (RIPA) buffer and Phenylmethylsulfonyl Fluoride (PMSF) at a 100:1 ratio. The mixture was homogenized by sonication at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 2569 g for 1 min. The sample was then sonicated for 10 min and centrifuged at 2874 g for 5 min. The supernatant was collected. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Concentration Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The supernatant was diluted with SDS-PAGE Sample Loading Buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), heated at 100 °C for 5–10 min, and then used for experiments.

Prior to the experiment, prepare a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) gel to separate target proteins of different molecular weights. Load proteins from each group onto the gel sequentially. Transfer the separated proteins to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. Block the membrane with 5% BSA for 1.5 h. followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the primary antibody against the target protein (see Table 2 for antibody details). After incubation, the protein bands were washed, and the secondary antibody was incubated for 1 h at 4 °C in the dark. Finally, the bands were exposed using eBlot (Yibote Life Science (Shanghai) Co., Ltd.) and analyzed.

Measurement of reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels

We assessed oxidative stress levels by measuring ROS concentrations across groups. Fresh retinal tissue was isolated from eyeballs and digested in trypsin (Solarbio, Beijing, China). During digestion, the tissue and enzyme mixture was incubated at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 1 h, with manual inversion every 10 min. Digestion was terminated by adding complete medium. CM-H2DCFDA (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was diluted 1:1000 with probe dilution buffer. Collected cells were resuspended in the diluted CM-H2DCFDA solution and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, with gentle inversion every 5 min. After staining, wash cells three times with PBS and proceed to instrument detection.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

S1P detection in this study was performed using an ELISA kit (Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Quanzhou, China). Sample preparation steps were identical to those in the Western blotting experiment (Sect. 2.3.2). Prior to the experiment, equilibrate the kit at room temperature for two hours. During this period, prepare the washing buffer according to the instructions. After equilibration, remove the microplate. Add the corresponding calibrator or target protein to the wells. Add 100 µL of Assay Diluent to all wells except the blank wells. Mix well and incubate at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 60 min. After incubation, wash the plate three times. Add 100 µL of horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labeled detection antibody to each well except the blank wells. Incubate at 37 °C for 30 min, then wash the plate three times. Add 100 µL TMB to each well, mix thoroughly, and incubate at 37 °C in a cell culture incubator for 15 min. Add 100 µL stop solution to each well. Finally, measure the OD at 450 nm using an EnVision microplate reader (PerkinElmer).

Seahorse assay

After detaching the retina from the eyeball, we added digestion solution and prepared a single-cell suspension using the Single-Cell Suspension Preparation System (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China). The experimental procedure was strictly followed according to the Agilent Seahorse XF Pro Experiment Manual (Agilent Technologies, Inc., California, United States). Add medium to the cell culture plates. After cell digestion, count the cells and seed them into the plates. Incubate the plates at 37 °C overnight in a cell culture incubator after 1 h of static incubation, and await the next day’s experiment.

After removing the probe plate, invert it on the work surface. Add XF calibration solution to the tool plate. Assemble the probe plate and tool plate. Place the assembled plate in a 37 °C incubator without CO₂ overnight. The next day, remove the probe plate, add the drugs sequentially, and place it in the XF Pro analyzer for calibration.

After cell culture is complete, remove the assay solution, change the cell medium, and wash the cells. Following calibration, remove the cell culture plate for instrument analysis, initiating the cellular energy metabolism measurement phase.

Determination of GSH-PX, MDA, SOD, CAT, and LDH

The assay kits for glutathione peroxidase (GSH-PX), malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) were all sourced from the same company (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). For the measurement of tissue lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), we selected the Lactate Dehydrogenase (LDH) Activity Assay Kit (Solarbio Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Statistical analysis

All experimental data analysis in this study was performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). One-way ANOVA was used for intergroup comparisons, followed by LSD post-hoc tests. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Lipids serve as a vital energy source for human life activities. Abnormal lipid metabolism can lead to various ocular diseases, such as Refsum disease, retinal degenerative diseases, and cataracts [30–31]. Exploring the pathological changes involved in their pathogenesis is crucial for developing disease intervention strategies. The sphingomyelin metabolism examined in this study belongs to the SP pathway. Sphingomyelin, with its sphingosine backbone, serves as the primary storage site for ceramides. As key bioactive lipids, ceramides play vital roles in apoptosis and oxidative stress [32–34]. The main findings of this study are presented in Fig. 8.

Research mechanism diagram. Schematic diagram illustrating the role of lipid metabolism abnormalities and oxidative stress in promoting myopia progression

Given the highly stratified and heterogeneous nature of retinal cell types, we employed scRNA-seq to classify retinal cells into cones, rods, Müller glial cells, microglia, ON-Bipolars, OFF-Bipolar cells, retinal ganglion cells, and retinal pigment epithelial cells. At the single-cell level, we characterized cell types and pathological states across these retinal groups to investigate the role of sphingomyelin metabolism in pathological changes induced by high myopia.

Multiple studies indicate that abnormal sphingomyelin metabolism and oxidative stress jointly contribute to the pathogenesis of various retinal diseases. Ceramide, the core bioactive lipid in sphingomyelin metabolism, can induce inflammation and cell death in neurons and retinal pigment epithelial cells, and is closely associated with the progression of diseases such as AMD, uveitis, and glaucoma [35]. During our initial scRNA-seq, we identified the sphingomyelin metabolism pathway, which includes genes CERS5, CERS6, ACER3, PLPP1, PSAP, and UGCG detected across all eight cell types. Further analysis showed that these factors also influence changes in cellular oxidative stress through distinct biological processes. This study spanned six weeks. Male guinea pigs used in the experiment required two to three weeks from birth to reach the required weight for enrollment. Therefore, myopia induction in this experiment continued until the guinea pigs reached sexual maturity, simulating the process by which environmental or genetic factors trigger myopia in clinical patients from childhood to late adolescence or early adulthood [36]. Once established, true myopia is often irreversible. Beyond the known excessive elongation of the eyeball, this study explored the metabolic factors underlying the continued progression of myopia.

CERS5 and CERS6 primarily catalyze the synthesis of C14 and C16 chain-length ceramides [37]. CERS5 influences retinal ganglion cell viability by regulating specific chain-length ceramides; its knockout protects retinal function and reduces ganglion cell death in models of elevated intraocular pressure [38–39]. As sphingolipid metabolites, ceramides participate in retinal degenerative apoptosis. L-Cyclothreonine alleviates oxidative stress by inhibiting ceramide production, offering protective effects against retinal degenerative diseases. CERS6 deficiency improves glucose metabolism and hepatic steatosis in an alcoholic liver disease model. It attenuates pro-apoptotic effects in neuronal injury, reduces mitochondrial autophagy and dysfunction, thereby decreasing neuronal apoptosis [40].

ACER3, a key enzyme regulating the hydrolysis of long-chain and unsaturated ceramides, exerts protective effects in a non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) mouse model through inhibiting oxidative stress in hepatocytes within NASH livers. ACER3 deficiency increases C18:1-ceramide content, reduces oxidative stress levels in mouse and human hepatocytes induced by palmitate treatment, and alleviates hepatocyte damage caused by saturated fatty acid overload [41]. Studies on hepatic ischemia-reperfusion indicate that ACER3 influences cer(d18:1/18:1) transport from the endoplasmic reticulum to mitochondria by modulating its levels in mitochondria-associated membranes and the endoplasmic reticulum [42]. These findings suggest ACER3 serves as a critical junction linking sphingomyelin metabolism and oxidative stress.

Lysophosphatidic acid (LPA) is a hydrolytic product of autotaxin (ATX) that transmits signals through multiple G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs). Degradation by three types of lipid phosphatases (LPPs) terminates LPA signaling pathways, thereby interfering with cell proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, and inflammatory responses [43]. Multiple studies indicate that LPA signaling is closely linked to oxidative stress, promoting the production of ROS and exacerbating tissue damage [44–45]. Various heterocyclic analgesics, including γ-butyrolactone, have been found to regulate the levels of free radicals. Factors such as LPP1 may exert antioxidant functions by preventing the formation of peroxynitrite anion (ONOO-) or catalyzing its degradation, thereby modulating oxidative stress processes [46].

PSAP is a key regulator of lysosomal sphingomyelin hydrolysis, participating in glycosphingolipid and sphingomyelin metabolism and lysosomal homeostasis. Studies indicate that dopaminergic PSAP-deficient mice accumulate highly unsaturated and shortened lipids in the brain with reduced sphingolipid content, suggesting PSAP maintains dopaminergic lipid homeostasis to sustain normal neurological function [47]. In neuronal studies, PSAP protects retinal cells from oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation by maintaining lysosomal sphingomyelin homeostasis, thereby delaying cellular senescence and ferroptosis [48].

UGCG is a key enzyme in glycosphingolipid biosynthesis and is involved in metabolic disorders across multiple diseases. It also serves as a primary regulatory factor in maintaining balanced levels of various sphingolipids within the brain [49]. Research on neurodegenerative mechanisms confirms that altered β-GlcCer metabolism is closely associated with lysosomal function in neurons, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress [50]. As a GlcCer synthase, abnormal UGCG expression levels contribute to tumor progression through multiple classicals signaling pathways, including PI3K/Akt and MAPK signaling pathways. The close relationship between GlcCer metabolism and oxidative stress positions it as a novel target for research into metabolic disorders and tumors [51].

Unlike acute injury, the interplay between lipid metabolism imbalance and oxidative stress keeps ocular tissues in a persistent state of pathological stimulation, leading to functional decline. Our research confirms that environmental and genetic factors induce stress in guinea pig ocular tissues, activating aSMase/nSMase and causing excessive ceramide accumulation. In this process, CERS5 and CERS6 determine the type of ceramide synthesis. Excess ceramide acts on mitochondria, impairing the respiratory chain and increasing ROS levels. In this study, we selected the classic oxidative stress signaling pathway p62-NRF2-KEAP1 to assess hypoxia levels across groups. CAT efficiently decomposes hydrogen peroxide, maintaining cellular redox homeostasis and preventing oxidative damage to lipids, proteins, and DNA. It serves as a critical protective factor that links peroxisomal metabolism to lipid metabolism abnormalities. Beyond the p62 pathway, we investigated changes in oxidative stress across groups by measuring levels of CAT, LDH, MDA, and SOD—key oxidative stress intermediates.

The accumulation of ROS counteracts ceramides, amplifying their effects. Ceramides of specific chain lengths accumulate in response to abnormal CERS5 and CERS6 expression, leading to elevated ACER3 levels and increased tissue concentrations of sphingosine and S1P. This stimulates compensatory enhancement of S1PR2 signaling. Concurrently, PLPP1’s capacity to limit S1P is impaired, leading to reduced expression levels and mitochondrial dysfunction. Excessive ceramide accumulation triggers a compensatory increase in S1P levels. Initially, S1P functions protectively via S1PR1; however, with prolonged stress, the S1P receptor profile shifts, with upregulation of S1PR2. This shift intensifies ROS signaling and accelerates metabolic imbalance. Activation of SPHK1 also causes abnormal S1P accumulation, triggering oxidative stress and metabolic disorder. Throughout these processes, UGCG’s capacity to maintain sphingolipid homeostasis and to resist oxidative stress diminishes, ultimately leading to oxidative damage. The combined effects of dysregulated upstream factors and impaired downstream regulatory mechanisms result in abnormalities in retinal lipid metabolism and oxidative stress. These two mechanisms mutually reinforce each other, driving myopia progression and exacerbating retinal dysfunction.

In this study, an imbalance in sphingolipid homeostasis was found not only to be a consequence of retinal hypoxia but also to exacerbate oxidative stress. During myopia development, choroidal cell apoptosis reduces retinal blood oxygen supply, while oxidative stress triggers a reprogramming of sphingolipid metabolism. Concurrently, excessive accumulation of ceramides, imbalance in sphingolipid homeostasis, and abnormal S1P signaling—along with abnormal expression of its receptors (S1PR1 and S1PR2)—result in vascular abnormalities and mitochondrial damage, leading to decreased blood perfusion efficiency and increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. These changes stimulate the expression of ACER3 and UGCG to mitigate ceramide toxicity and buffer the tissue damage caused by its excessive accumulation. Given the retina’s extremely high oxygen demand, these pathological changes create a vicious cycle in which oxidative stress and sphingolipid metabolic remodeling are interlinked, leading to abnormal expression of lipid-related enzymes.

As a central component of lipid metabolism, excessive ceramide accumulation impairs mitochondrial physiological functions, leading to decreased mitochondrial membrane potential and disrupted homeostasis [52]. This results in impaired ATP production, reduced antioxidant capacity, and imbalances in mitochondrial quality and quantity. Molecular experiments confirm that myopia-induced or genetically altered expression levels stimulate excessive ceramide accumulation in the retina. This accumulation inhibits the respiratory chain, increases ROS, and simultaneously reduces S1P levels and repair capacity. Ultimately, this leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative damage. In this study, we examined both genetic and environmental factors and found that sustained myopia causes mitochondrial dysfunction in guinea pig retinas, elevated ROS levels, and altered lipase activity. Abnormal lipid metabolism increases intermediate metabolite levels (e.g., ceramides), disrupting mitochondrial function and exacerbating ROS production. Persistent stimulation leads to mitochondrial physiological imbalance and retinal dysfunction.

A growing body of research indicates that lipid metabolism disorders and oxidative stress are involved in the onset and progression of myopia; however, existing studies have primarily focused on scleral remodeling, choroidal hypoxia, and retinal energy metabolism abnormalities. As another potential cause of myopia, aside from environmental factors, there is limited research on lipid metabolism abnormalities and on genetic factors that alter retinal oxidative stress. This study focused on the retinas of guinea pigs with spontaneous high myopia and lens-induced myopia. Using single-cell transcriptomics, lipidomics, and molecular biological validation, we demonstrated that both types of myopia are associated with significant lipid metabolic disorders in the retina, accompanied by elevated oxidative stress. In particular, the abnormal activation of ceramides (Cer) and their related metabolic pathways, occurring concurrently with a decline in antioxidant defense capacity, suggests that lipid metabolic imbalance may not only be a concomitant phenomenon in the progression of high myopia but may also contribute to retinal pathological changes by exacerbating oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. This study further enriches metabolic research on spontaneous high myopia retinopathy and provides experimental evidence to aid understanding of the pathological changes associated with this condition.

Future research should further elucidate the molecular regulatory networks linking lipid metabolism abnormalities and oxidative stress. For example, it is worth investigating whether the Cer-S1P metabolic axis mediates retinal damage by regulating SIRT1/NRF2, p53, or signaling pathways related to mitochondrial function, and whether there is heterogeneity in the responses of different retinal cell types to lipid metabolism imbalances. Furthermore, oxidative stress and lipid metabolism disorders should be regarded as mechanisms associated with and potentially contributing to the progression of myopia, rather than definitive causative factors. It is also necessary to conduct intervention studies targeting sphingolipid metabolism or oxidative stress signaling pathways to determine their causal roles. Finally, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to determine whether the aforementioned changes represent primary pathogenic mechanisms or secondary consequences of axial length elongation.

This study identifies potential targets for myopia prevention and control from a lipid-metabolism perspective. However, limitations exist, including the lack of separate interventions targeting lipid metabolism and its pathways. Future research will further explore these areas.

Conclusion

In this study, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) was conducted on guinea pigs with spontaneous myopia and those from a normal control group. The research aimed to model how genetic and environmental factors contribute to the progression of myopia by using spontaneous myopia and lens-induced myopia, respectively. The findings revealed that the CERS5/CERS6/ACER3/PLPP1/PSAP/UGCG pathway is linked to the retinal changes observed during the progression of myopia. These results provide new insights into the pathogenesis of myopia.
