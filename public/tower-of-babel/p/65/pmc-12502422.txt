Genomic landscape of cancer driver genes in Omani breast cancers: a pilot study
Authors: Muna Al Dlali, Buthaina Al Amri, Noura Al Zeheimi, Raghad Al Busaidi, M. Mazharul Islam, Omar AL Omari, Marwa Al Riyami, Radiya Al Ajmi, Adil Aljarrah Alajmi, Yahya Tamimi, Cheryl Crozier, John M. S. Bartlett, Jane Bayani, Sirin A. Adham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global rise in breast cancer (BC) incidence is mirrored in the Sultanate of Oman, where the median age of diagnosis is strikingly young at 47 years. This pilot study represents the first in the region to examine the genetic alterations of Omani BC biopsies and resected tumors and their correlations with patient characteristics using a targeted pan-cancer panel.MethodsUtilizing the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus, we profiled 40 BC biopsies alongside 22 matched post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy samples, identifying single-nucleotide variations (SNVs) and copy number variations (CNVs). Chi-Square and Fisher’s Exact tests were used for categorical variables. In contrast, the Independent T-test and Levene test were used for continuous variables. Comparative analyses were conducted using the METABRIC and TCGA datasets to place these findings in a global context.ResultsThe data revealed molecular patterns between early-onset (under 50 years) and late-onset (50 years and older) cases. Notably, SNVs were predominant in late-onset tumors, while CNVs were more frequent in early-onset cases. PIK3CA SNVs emerged as a hallmark of late-onset BC, which persists across pre- and post-treatment stages. NTRK1 mutations were linked to late-onset cases at pre-treatment, while OR6F1 CNV was exclusive to early-onset tumors. The five genes with the highest CNV prevalence in the cohort were CDK12, ERBB2, BRIP1, and BLM, and they closely mirrored those found in global datasets. Correlations with clinical features identified CHEK2 alterations associated with high-grade tumors and luminal and HER2 type, while TP53 mutations were predominantly found in TNBC cases. Mutations in CBFB and GATA3 were predominantly enriched in luminal subtypes, and TSC1 mutations corresponded with smaller tumors, whereas FGFR4 SNVs were linked to nodal (N) status. Additionally, we identified 19 new SNV variants distributed across seven genes that were not recorded in the public databases.ConclusionThis study sheds light on the genetic landscape of BC driver genes in Oman and the shared molecular traits with Western populations while uncovering unique regional alterations in NTRK1, OR6F1, and FGFR4. These findings highlight the need for region-specific insights to inform targeted therapies and personalized care, advancing the global understanding of BC biology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13058-025-02133-3.

Background

Breast cancer [1] is considered the most diagnosed invasive cancer among women globally [2]. Over 2.3 million new cases and 685,000 deaths from BC occurred in 2020 [3]. According to the American Cancer Society’s 2024 data, US women had 310,720 invasive breast cancer cases and 42,250 expected deaths [4]. The incidence of BC has a considerable geographic variation across countries and world regions, with incidence rates ranging from < 40 per 100,000 females in some Asian and African countries to over 80 per 100,000 in Australia/New Zealand, North America, and parts of Europe [5]. BC incidence in Arab women has also been increasing over time. It is more likely to present with advanced stages, translating into a lower 5-year survival rate than that observed in patients from Western countries [6, 7]. In the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, BC incidence was higher among women aged < 50 years in comparison with high-income countries [8]. Moreover, in the Sultanate of Oman, BC contributes 32% of the total cancer incidence among women in the country, and 25% of patients are under the age of 39 years [9, 10]. On the genomic level, Arab breast cancer patients show distinct molecular profiles, with regional studies reporting varying prevalence of BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutations. In Lebanon, several high-risk hereditary cases have been identified as carrying unique BRCA variants, although specific founder mutations have yet to be confirmed. Across the Gulf and the broader Middle East, differences in mutation frequencies, rather than uniform mutation patterns, have been observed [11, 12]. At the molecular level, the distribution of intrinsic breast cancer subtypes among Arab women differs from that typically reported in European and North American cohorts. An extensive Saudi study demonstrated predominance of luminal A (41.7%) and luminal B (23.4%) tumors, yet also noted relatively high frequencies of triple-negative breast cancer (19.5%) and HER2-enriched disease (15.4%), both of which are associated with poorer survival outcomes [13]. Somatic mutation analyses have further identified TP53 (23.1%) and PIK3CA (15.4%) as the most common alterations, alongside less frequent mutations in PTEN, APC, and KIT, suggesting possible regional variation in mutation spectra compared with Western datasets [14]. Polygenic risk scores (PRS) in Arab BC patients have been shown to significantly stratify risk in patients with family history, particularly in those lacking identifiable pathogenic variants, and were associated with earlier disease onset [15]. Transcriptomic analyses incorporating Arab patients also suggest ancestry-related biological features, including enrichment for Basal Myo-like signatures and differences in immune and metabolic pathways, such as AMPK signaling, with potential implications for treatment response and prognosis [16]. Beyond biology, qualitative evidence emphasizes that social, cultural, and health system contexts profoundly shape the lived experiences of Arab women with breast cancer. Themes of coping, family roles, religious faith, and stigma consistently influence care-seeking behavior and quality of life [17]. Collectively, these findings show the need for region-specific epidemiological monitoring, molecular profiling, and culturally sensitive clinical approaches. Moreover, integration of Arab populations into multi-omic datasets is essential to refine precision oncology strategies, as highlighted by comparative genomic studies demonstrating ancestry-linked differences in mutation frequencies, epigenetic patterns, and tumor microenvironment biology [18].

BC is a heterogeneous disease with variability in tumor molecular and clinical characteristics, which results in variable therapy response, metastasis, and clinical outcome [19]. Breast tumor stratification based on histological grade, tumor size, lymph node status, and hormone receptor status currently provide prognostic predictors and guide clinical decision-making. In addition to these clinical variables, integrating molecular-based classification using gene panels and whole genome or transcriptome sequencing technology can reveal previously unseen biological properties of the tumor, impacting clinical management [20, 21]. Based on both molecular and histological evidence, BC can be categorized into three groups: hormone receptor-positive cancers (estrogen receptor (ER+)) and/or progesterone receptor (PR+)); human epidermal receptor 2 (HER2+)-positive cancers; and triple-negative BCs (TNBC) (ER−, PR−, HER2−) [22]. Several studies focused on the molecular profiles of breast tumors have demonstrated that intrinsic molecular characteristics of the tumor correlate with survival outcomes or treatment responses [20].

The standard treatment is based on clinical diagnosis, including hormone receptor and HER2 expression via immunohistochemistry (IHC). HER2-positive cancers are treated with a combination of anti-HER2 monoclonal antibodies and chemotherapy, such as trastuzumab and pertuzumab [23], and are used in the neoadjuvant setting in combination with docetaxel for 12 weeks, contributing to a significant increase in the pathological complete response rate, from 29.0 to 45.8% [24, 25]. Furthermore, ER and PR status are crucial determinants of anti-endocrine therapy benefits, targeting hormone-driven pathways. These treatments include selective ER modulators (tamoxifen), aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole, exemestane), and ER down regulators (fulvestrant) [26].

BCs are also genomically diverse [2, 27], revealing significant insights into the molecular mechanisms underpinning them and enabling personalized medicine approaches [28]. The lifetime risk of hereditary breast and ovarian cancer and early-onset BC are impacted by the status of the BRCA1/2 genes [29]. In addition to BRCA1 and BRCA2, high penetrance genes include TP53, PTEN, STK11, and CDH1, whereas moderate-penetrance genes include CHEK2, ATM, BRIP1, PALB2, RAD51C, RAD50, and NBN6 [30]. Approximately 5–10% of BC are familial, with the remaining cancers being sporadic. The most frequently reported somatic alterations in primary BC tumors include variants in TP53, PIK3CA, GATA3, MAP3K1, AKT1, and CBFB; amplification of ERBB2/HER2, MYC, FGFR1, and FGF3/4; deletion of PTEN, RB1 and CDKN2A/B [31], with the prevalence of these variants and copy number changes associated with the different clinical or molecular subtypes [32].

Ethnicity is increasingly being recognized as playing a role in disease prevalence, prognosis, and response to therapy [33]. Few studies describe BC clinicopathologic features and genomic profiling in the GCC [8, 34]. This study explores gene mutations and alterations in tumor tissues from 40 Omani BC patients using a next-generation sequencing (NGS) pan-cancer panel to determine similarities and differences of cancer driver genes from datasets predominantly populated from Western countries.

Methods

Breast cancer patient cohort

The study cohort comprised 40 BC patients who agreed and consented to enroll in the study. The patients were diagnosed and histologically confirmed at Sultan Qaboos University Hospital (SQUH) between January 2012 and December 2018. The inclusion criteria were primarily dependent on high-quality samples with complete clinical data, which limited the number of included cases in this pilot study. Additionally, the number of samples used was restricted to patients who signed a consent form to participate in the research. Standard diagnostic histopathological assessment was performed, including immunohistochemistry (IHC) testing of ER, PR, HER2, and Ki-67. Hormone receptor status was determined using the Allred scoring system [35], and HER2 status was scored per ASCO/CAP Guidelines (PMID: 37,284,804) [36]. Fluorescent in situ hybridization [37] was performed for cases that were HER2 equivocal. Ki-67 IHC was also performed to assess the tumor proliferation, and the subtyping was performed based on the St. Gallen International Expert Consensus on the Primary Therapy of Early Breast Cancer 2013 [38]. Pretreatment biopsies (n = 40) were collected prior to neoadjuvant therapy, while post-treatment biopsies (n = 22) were collected at the time of surgery following neoadjuvant chemotherapy. The Medical Research Ethics Committee approved, MREC Approval#2027, the project at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences, Sultan Qaboos University (#SQU.EC239/19). Molecular profiling was performed in Diagnostic Development at the Ontario Institute for Cancer Research (OICR) under the approved ethical review at the University of Toronto (protocol #40,090).

Oncomine comprehensive assay plus and next-generation sequencing (NGS)

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections were micro-dissected and processed for dual DNA and RNA extraction using methods previously described [39]. NGS-based profiling was performed for this study using the Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus (OCA Plus) (Thermo Ficher Scientific, USA). OCA Plus is a targeted assay of over 500 genes that provides comprehensive genomic profiling [40] without the need for germline DNA. Libraries were manually prepared from 50 ng of genomic DNA using the Oncomine™ Comprehensive Assay Plus kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON. CA). Briefly, two PCR reactions were prepared for each sample, one for each of the two primer pools in the panel (25 ng/pool). Following amplification, the two PCR reactions were combined, ligated with unique IonCode™ barcode oligonucleotides, purified using AmPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, Mississauga, ON. CA) and quantified using the Ion Library Taqman™ Quantification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON. CA). Libraries were pooled at 50 pM in batches of 4 libraries/pool. Libraries were loaded onto Ion 550™ chips, templated using the Ion Chef™ (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON. CA), and sequenced on the Ion S5™ XL System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Mississauga, ON. CA). The Ion Reporter™ software was used to annotate relevant and non-relevant SNPs, indels, and CNVs, leveraging information from the Oncomine™ Knowledge Base. Due to the variable quality of the DNA, a stringent threshold for the calling of ≥ 5% Variant Allele Fraction (VAF) was used with at least 30 reads containing the variant allele, as described previously [41], along with a minimum total read depth of 300X and p-value < 0.0001. Adjacent normal tissue was not available; to minimize germline variants, mutations with VAF > 50% were excluded. LOH events and putative founding mutations were assessed but removed from downstream analysis when not confidently distinguishable from sequencing artifacts or subclonal variants. Variants were further compared to METABRIC [42], TCGA [43, 44], ClinVar [45], dbSNP () [46], and COSMIC [47] databases. Additional manual filtering was performed to remove germline polymorphisms. Copy Number Variation (CNV) was also called by the Ion Reporter™ along with their 90% confidence intervals.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v21.0 (IBM Corp.). Chi-Square was used to determine the significance between two categorical variables of SNV, and Fisher's Exact test was used to determine the P-value, and the cut-off of significance was set to p ≤ 0.05. SNVs and/or CNVs were excluded from further analysis if the occurrence was in less than three patients. The Independent T-test was used to compare the means of two independent groups of CNV within the study cohort, and the Levene test was used to determine the P-value. The The results were compared with the globally available data in cBioPortal using the METABRIC and TCGA Firehose Legacy studies [32, 48].

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 40 pre-treatment biopsies and 22 matching post-treatment tumor resections were analyzed, and the clinicopathological criteria were described in Table 1. The median age at diagnosis was 45.5 years (range 19–78 years). The patients presented with locally advanced BC, and most cases had grade 3 (55%) and T4 (40%) cancers. Additionally, 45% of the cases were identified with 1–3 positive axillary lymph nodes (N1). The cohort had the expected distribution of the molecular subtypes typically seen in the clinic: Luminal A (7/40, 17.5%), Luminal B (7/40, 17.5%), Luminal B-like (9/40, 22.5%), HER2 type (6/40,15%) and TNBC (11/40, 27.5%) [49, 50].

Landscape of genomic change in the Omani cohort

NGS profiling using the OCA Plus assay was successful in 40 patient samples with 22 matched pre-treated and post-treated pairs (Fig. 1A, B). After quality control and filtering, 32 patient samples of the 40 had robust copy number variation (CNV) results (Table 2), and all 40 samples generated SNV calls. Several SNV classes were detected, including missense, nonsense, frame-shift deletion, frame-shift insertion, and variants of unknown significance (VUS). However, a total number of significant pathogenic mutations was 2, found in three genes in the 62 samples analyzed (pre- and post-treatment): (14 pathogenic variants of TP53 with a prevalence of 40%, five pathogenic variants of PIK3CA with a prevalence of 25.8%, and one pathogenic variant of KMT2C with a prevalence of 14.5%. Besides these three genes, FGFR4 and NTRK1 SNV variants were registered in public databases with conflicting pathogenicity (i.e., dPSNP and ClinVar). Variant details, including the pathogenic and non-pathogenic variants, are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. SNVs detected significantly among BC cohort cases pre- and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy were segregated based on their significance in relation to the different clinicopathological features of the studied cases. The total number of significant genes with SNV in both pre- and post-treatment was 11 (7 genes appeared in the pre-treatment (TP53, PIK3CA, FGFR4, GATA3, NTRK1, CBFB, TSC1), Fig. 1A top panel, and four genes appeared in the post-treatment (KMT2C, ASXL1, ZFHX3, and RET), as shown in Fig. 1B. The total number of genes with CNV was 25, and they were only detected in the pre-treatment samples (Fig. 1A, bottom panel).

Genomic changes associated with age

To determine whether there is a role for age at diagnosis, sequencing results were compared between those younger than 50 (n = 22) versus those 50 years and older (n = 18). In pre-treatment samples (n = 40), the analysis revealed two genes with significant SNV missense mutations between the two age groups: NTRK1 (p.Phe329Leu) and PIK3CA (p.His1047Arg, p.Glu545Lys, p.Asn345Lys, p.Pro449Ser) (p ≤ 0.05). Both genes exhibited a higher mutation prevalence in late-onset cases, with no NTRK1 variants detected in the younger patient group. Using the METABRIC dataset [32], NTRK1 SNVs were also absent from the early onset group, but it was reported in a small percentage in the TCGA datasets [48]. PIK3CA mutations had a similar prevalence to our data in TCGA and METABRIC datasets and were predominantly found in older patients (Table 3A).

When the mutational landscape in the available matched samples was compared, only SNVs in PIK3CA were found in both pre-and post-treatment tumors (p ≤ 0.05) (Fig. 1B and Table 3B).

Our data showed a missense mutation in RET (rs17158558) with conflicting pathogenicity classifications in ClinVar. This mutation was observed in post-treatment cases with SNVs and demonstrated a significant association with age (p ≤ 0.05), as shown in Table 3B. RET variants were also identified in late-onset cases. In contrast, the TCGA dataset reported a lower prevalence of these variants in both early-onset (0.7%) and late-onset cases (0.9%). CNVs varied between early and late-onset cases. In the 32 pretreated samples with detectable CNVs, there were significant associations with age (p ≤ 0.05), predominantly in the early-onset group, which was identical to the reported data in METABRIC and TCGA including gains of CDK12 17q12 (n = 8) and ERBB2 17q22 (n = 7) on 17q; gains of FANCL (n = 6) on 2q; gains of CSMD3 (n = 5) on 8q; gains of CDC73 (n = 5) on 1q; and gains of OR6F1 (n = 4) on 1q. Notably, the CDC73 CNV was not detected in the METABRIC and TCGA in the early onset but had a low percentage in the TCGA late-onset (0.9%). Similarly, OR6F1 was absent in the late-onset cases within the current study cohort but was detected in the late-onset group in TCGA (0.3%) but not early-onset. The METABRIC dataset shows no results for OR6F1 (Table 4).

Genomic changes associated with tumor grade

No significant genes associated with tumor grade were detected in pre-treatment samples with SNVs. However, three genes with SNVs showed significance with tumor grade in post-treated tumors: ZFHX3 (pAla772GlyfsTer17, pAsp500Val), KMT2C, and ASXL1 (p ≤ 0.05) with truncating and a loss of function mutation likely a consequence of the chemotherapy response impacting tumor biology. ZFHX3 was mutated in low-grade tumors only (G1), whereas KMT2C SNVs were detected predominantly in high-grade cases, and ASXL1 had an equal prevalence of SNVs (Fig. 1C). In pretreated samples, CNVs included gains located on chromosome 17q including BRIP1 (n = 14) and gains of SOX9 (n = 6); 7q including RPS6KB1 (n = 7); CHEK2 (n = 4) on 22q; and NOTCH1 (n = 4 cases) on 9q, that were significantly associated with grade (p ≤ 0.05), specifically high-grade tumors (Fig. 1D).

Genomic changes are associated with tumor size

Missense mutations in TSC1 (p.[Leu1125Phe: Gly1126Cys]), (p.Pro1082Ala) (p.Arg1027Gln) were associated with smaller tumor size (p ≤ 0.05) in the pre-treatment samples. Although not reported in METABRIC, it is reported to have a missense mutation in the TCGA dataset. In pre-treatment samples, CNVs were detected in two genes: NBN (8q21.3) and RECQL4 (8q24.3), each showing gains in 7 cases. These alterations were significantly associated with tumor size (p ≤ 0.05), with a higher frequency observed in cases with smaller tumors (Fig. 1E). In the TCGA datasets, NBN and RECQL4 were also altered more frequently in smaller tumors (57.8% and 62.6%).

Genomic changes associated with nodal status

FGFR4 was the only gene found to be significantly mutated in pre-treatment samples and associated with nodal status (p = 0.051), with a mutation found mainly in cases with greater number of positive nodes. This dataset detected the following mutations resulting in a dominant amino acid change p.Gly388Arg, rs351855, and one sample with a p.Pro694Leu with ambiguous pathogenicity status in ClinVar. In TCGA, FGFR4 mutations are truncating and mainly associated with lower node status (N0). There were no results regarding FGFR4 mutation in the METABRIC cohort.

In the pre-treatment samples, eight genes with CNVs were significantly associated with nodal status (p ≤ 0.05). These include copy number gains on Xq including ATRX (n = 4) and PHF6 (n = 4); Xp including ZRSR2 (n = 5); 1q including OR6F1 (n = 4) and PARP1 (n = 4); 15q including BLM (n = 9); 16q including TSC2 (n = 4) and CD79B (n = 6) on 17q. These alterations were collectively observed only in cases with N0 and N1 status. These findings were consistent with the findings in both METABRIC and TCGA datasets. In more detail, the percentage of N0 and N1 in the observed genes was as follows: OR6F1 and PARP1 (66.2%), BLM (69.1%), TSC2 (58.1%), and CD79B (56.8%) (Percentages were taken from the CBioPortal analysis).

Genomic changes associated with molecular and clinical subtypes

Using the IHC markers, ER, PR, and HER2 as well as Ki-67, we examined the genomic associations stratified by the molecular subtypes Luminal A, Luminal B, Luminal B-like, HER2, and Basal-like and clinical subtypes (HR+/HER2-, HR any/HER2+, TNBC respectively). Among the pretreated samples, mutations in TP53, GATA3, and CBFB (p ≤ 0.05) were most significantly associated with subtypes. TP53 mutations were found mainly in TNBC cases (53%), followed by Luminal B Like (23%), HER2+ (12%), and Luminal A (12%), consistent with findings in TCGA and METABRIC. GATA3 mutations were found mainly in Luminal A (60%) and B (40%) tumors, and variants in CBFB were detected in the Luminal A subtype (Fig. 1F). Finally, PIK3CA was mutated in pre- and post-treated samples (p ≤ 0.05) in cases that were HER2-negative. METABRIC and TCGA findings for GATA3, CBFB, and PIK3CA were consistent with the findings here. Amongst other HER2-negative cancers, there were gains in 17q including BRIP1 (n = 14), BLM (n = 9), RPS6KB1 (n = 7), and PHF6 (n = 4), while other gains across 17q including SPOP and RNF43 in 6 cases each were expectedly altered predominantly in HER2-positive tumors (Fig. 1G). In the present cohort, ERBB2 was amplified in (7/10 HER2 +ve, 70.0%) and (3/10 HER2−ve, 30%). Six CNVs (p ≤ 0.05) were significant across the pre-treatment samples when comparing the cases that were TNBC vs. the hormone receptor-positive or HER2-positive cases, including the 17q locus of genes including ERBB2 (n = 10) and SPOP (n = 6), CHEK2 on 22q (n = 6) and the 11q locus harboring FGF3 (n = 6), FGF19 (n = 5), and FGF4 (n = 5) (Fig. 1H). Similar results were observed in METABRIC and TCGA datasets.

Discussion

Using a targeted NGS pan-cancer panel, this study investigated an Omani cohort of 40 female BC patients across different clinical subtypes. There are few studies on the genomic profiles of Omani BC patients and the landscape of somatic changes compared to the annotated database comprised mainly of Western patients [51]. Furthermore, it has been observed that women in this region experience diagnosis at an earlier age [51, 52]. The goal of this study was to map the genomic landscape of Omani BC samples against a panel of pan-cancer driver genes used for diagnostic testing globally to determine whether there are any unique changes across these pan-cancer genes compared to predominantly Western-populated public databases like METABRIC and TCGA [23, 32]. Secondly, we explored correlations between clinical variables, including age, which could provide insight into the earlier onset of cancer in the Omani population. However, we recognize that this study's limitation is the small sample size and mixed subtypes.

In treatment, naïve samples, genes most commonly subject to mutation were TP53 (40%), PIK3CA (25.8%), and FGFR4 (14.5%), consistent with other studies [53–55]. Variants in different genes, including GATA3, NTRK1, CBFB, and TSC1, were also detected at frequencies comparable to other findings [32, 56]. Recently, Messaoudi et al. also reported the frequent alteration of AKT1, PIK3CA, PTEN, and TP53 in Saudi BC patients, in which the PIK3CA (15/32, 46.9%) and TP53 (26/32 86.7%) frequencies were similar to our findings [57]. Consistent with other findings [58], TP53 mutations were strongly associated with TNBC in our cohort (81.8%). Alongside variant detection, frequent copy number changes in 25 genes (Fig. 1) were identified across treatment-naïve samples exhibiting the characteristic landscape of chromosomal abnormalities in BC.

Due to the early onset of BC in Omani women, we investigated whether there were specific mutations or copy number aberrations in younger vs older women. In the pretreated samples, our analysis showed that NTRK1 and PIK3CA were significant with age (p ≤ 0.05), and the mutations were 100% observed in late-onset cases (Table 3A) and consistent with findings from both METABRIC and TCGA. PIK3CA is one of the most common genes subject to mutation in BC at a rate of approximately 35.7% [54], with mutations often leading to constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, promoting oncogenesis and tumor progression [59]. NTRK1 mutations are rare in BC, and the variants detected in this cohort are a mix of known pathogenic variants (rs6339 and rs6336) and those of unknown significance. We recognize that variants of unknown significance may be related to differences in ethnicity, and a further limitation of our study was the absence of matched germline DNA for deeper analysis of such variants. Amplifications on 17q, including CDK12 and ERBB2, in addition to FANCL at 2p16.1 and CSMD3 at 8q23.3, were also significantly associated with early-onset cases, similar to findings in the METABRIC and TCGA cohorts. Chromosome 17q is frequently subject to copy number gains in BC and associated largely with HER2 positivity and genomic instability. Specifically, in our cohort, we observed amplification of CDK12, which regulates the alternative splicing of the genes involved in the DNA damage response pathway and the pathogenesis of BC, as well as ERBB2 amplification, which drives receptor tyrosine kinase involved in cell growth, survival, and differentiation [60]. CDK12 was frequently amplified in the early-onset cases; in the 9 cases with CDK12 amplification, 4 cases had the highest CNV values (i.e., 19–46 copies) were also HER2 positive, confirming the co-amplification of both genes [61]. In comparison, the remaining 5 cases were HER2 negative (1 Luminal A and 4 TNBC), and only one patient out of the nine passed away at the age of 51. FANCL, which is the only Fanconi Anaemia (FA) protein known to possess a ubiquitin ligase activity [62], maps to this region and participates with FANCD2 to initiate the FANC/BRCA repair pathway, similar to our cohort finding, a previous report showed a gain of FANCL in early-onset cases [63]. Additionally, in a recent study, a total of 13 women aged < 35 years at diagnosis with early BC were analysed, and amplifications of MDM2, CDK12, ERBB2, SPOP, BRIP1, and CD79B genes were identified in treatment naïve samples [64]. However, Koçak et al. [65] reported a cohort of 34 cancers from young women (range 31–59) using a targeted sequencing and FISH approach to determine whether there were mutational and copy number changes in key driver genes. They demonstrated no significant differences from those from an age-unbiased cohort. Their observations seen with age were largely recapitulated with tumor size, grade, and nodal status with regions of copy number gains reflective of the genomic instability characterizing BCs. Though limited in number, our Omani cohort is similar to those in METABRIC and TCGA with higher grade tumors before treatment characterized by copy number gains in 7q, 17q, 9q, and 22q, consistent with the landscape of chromosomal changes and increasing genomic instability and heterogeneity [58]. Many genes within these chromosomal locations are subject to copy number gains with increased expression. In a study by Gupta et al. on 80 breast tumor samples from Omani patients, there was significant overexpression of BRIP1 (17q) in malignant breast tumors compared to benign samples [66], and this data would suggest that one of the mechanisms for such overexpression may be increases in gene dosage. In addition to the regions mentioned above, chromosomal gains in X, 1q, 15q, 16p and variants detected in FGFR4 were also associated with greater lymph node positivity consistent with the notion that alterations in gene dosage (gains and loss) contribute to increasing aggressiveness and metastatic potential [67]. A polymorphism in FGFR4, resulting in a glycine-to-arginine substitution (Gly388Arg, rs351855 G > A), was found significant with nodal status (p ≤ 0.05) and consistent with a study by Jiang et al., demonstrating an association between rs351855 and lymph node-positive BC [32]. Additionally, a meta-analysis of 27 studies (8682 cases) indicated that rs351855 G > A is linked to increased cancer risk, including BC [61]. In contrast, a European cohort found no significant association with FGFR4 rs351855; instead, FGFR2 rs2981582 showed greater relevance [62], suggesting that there may be differences between Eastern and Western cancers. Concerning tumor size, our cohort showed SNVs in the tumor suppressor TSC1 were associated with smaller tumors, while variants in ARID1B were detected in cases with larger tumors. The prominent role of these cancer drivers in PI3K/AKT [51] and chromatin remodeling [52] pathways contributes to the overall tumor biology, including size. However, more samples are required for validation. Findings from TGCA and METABRIC similarly confirmed our observations of copy number gains on 8q in the region of NBN and RECQL4 as predominantly associated with smaller tumors.

Our study also included the pre-treatment and post-treatment samples from 22 patients who underwent standard-of-care neoadjuvant therapy based on their clinical presentations. NGS results from the post-treatment cases showed common and unique changes from the treatment naïve samples, including detecting increased SNVs amongst DNA repair and chromatin remodeling genes. In most cases, variants in PIK3CA, TP53, and GATA3 persisted after neoadjuvant treatment and across different subtypes, indicating the continued presence of dominant clones carrying these variants. The value of profiling tissues pre- and post-treatment supports ongoing biomarker discovery for markers of therapeutic response, and even in this small cohort of samples, it demonstrates the impact of therapy on clonal evolution.

Conclusion

There is limited data on BC genomics in Oman, accounting for 32% of female cancers, with 25% of cases under 39 years [9, 10]. BC incidence is rising in Arab women, often presenting at advanced stages, leading to lower 5-year survival than in Western countries [6, 7]. In GCC countries, BC is more common in women < 50 years than in high-income nations [8]. Factors, including genetics and environment, contribute to increased BC incidence. A study of 1336 Omani BC patients (611 < 45 years, 45.7%) found no significant link between BRCA1/2 mutations and early onset BC (p = 0.229); most had no family history but had larger, higher-grade, HER2-enriched, and TNBC tumors with poorer outcomes [68]. Despite BRCA1/2's known role in early BC, this was not seen in Oman, suggesting other factors. Germline testing was incomplete in this patient cohort, making the impact of BRCA1/2 unclear. However, TP53 and PIK3CA frequencies matched Western data, and copy number profiles were similar; this targeted panel aided therapy but missed the role of ethnicity.
