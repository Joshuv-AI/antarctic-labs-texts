Identification of microbial groups and functional genes associated with greenhouse gas emissions in prawn farms
Authors: Abul Bashar, Amaru Miranda Djurhuus, Patrick Denis Browne, Raju Podduturi, Md. Zahid Hasan, Abdullah Al Rakib, Chantelle Hooper, Morena Santi, H. M. Rakibul Islam, Neaz A Hasan, Muhammad Tofazzal Hossain, M. M. R. Jahangir, David Bass, Md Ismail Hossain, Niels O. G. Jørgensen, Lars Hestbjerg Hansen, Mohammad Mahfujul Haque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Understanding microbial associations with greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions is essential for promoting climate-friendly prawn production, yet it remains largely unexplored. To examine microbial-GHG relationships, this study analysed CO2, CH4, and N2O emissions, characterised microbial communities, and measured the abundances of key functional genes (mcrA, pmoA, amoA, and nosZ) in water and sediment from Bangladeshi prawn farms. Results showed that microbial richness was positively correlated with CO2 and N2O emissions but negatively with CH4 emissions. Methanogenic archaea, most abundant in high-salinity farms, were positively associated with CH4 flux; however, their negative relationship with CO2 emission appeared insignificant. Conversely, the negative correlation between the relative abundance of anaerobic methanotrophs (ANME) and CH4 emission suggests that CH4 oxidation facilitated by ANME could be a promising strategy for reducing CH4 emissions. N2O emission was negatively linked to most bacterial and archaeal classes; however, N2O-consuming Chloroflexi exhibited a positive correlation with N2O emission. Additionally, a strong link between the mcrA gene in water and CH4 production may relate to previous findings of methane production in oxygenated water. This multidisciplinary study enhances our understanding and opens avenues for further research into reducing emissions by modulating microbial communities within prawn farming systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22768-8.

Introduction

Giant Freshwater Prawn (Macrobrachium rossenbergii; hereafter Prawn) farming in South Asian countries has long played a vital role in promoting health benefits, boosting socio-economic development, and increasing export earnings1–3. The rising global demand has driven the expansion of prawn farming in these nations, leading to a shift from traditional low-stocking polyculture (multi-species) systems to high-stocking intensive or semi-intensive culture patterns3,4. This intensification has adversely affected aquatic environments, causing increased, yet likely underestimated, greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, raising concerns about the sustainability and overall benefits of the industry5,6. Prawn farming enriches ponds with organic nutrients, stimulating microbial growth and activity, thereby serving as a hotspot for carbon cycling and emission processes7,8. An indirect estimate indicates that shrimp and prawn farming worldwide accounts for 21.09% of aquaculture-related emissions, although contributing only 10.49% to global fish and shellfish production9. Additionally, there is a potential hidden carbon footprint associated with establishing prawn farms due to mangrove deforestation, ranging from 1067 to 3003 MgCO2 per hectare10.

Microorganisms play a central role in the production, consumption, and emission of GHGs due to their widespread abundance and involvement in biogeochemical cycles11–13. However, the rates of these processes depend on environmental conditions and the types of microbes present14. For instance, microorganisms mediate the interconversion between CH4 and CO2: CO2 is transformed into CH4 by methanogens in anoxic conditions15,16, while methanotrophs oxidize CH4 back to CO2 in both oxic and anoxic environments before it is released into the atmosphere17. Furthermore, microorganisms contribute to CO2 flux through heterotrophic decomposition and respiration, while chemolithoautotrophs can reduce CO2 by fixing it from water18,19. Despite long-standing interests, the relationships between GHG emissions and microbial community composition in pond ecosystems are poorly studied. Given the vast diversity and functional complexity of microorganisms in aquatic ecosystems, studying the function and potential of each microbe individually is challenging. Therefore, statistical approaches that identify the key taxa can help focus on the most influential bacterial or archaeal groups, enabling scientists to better understand and harness their roles.

Studies investigating microbial associations have typically applied 16 S rRNA gene-based sequencing to examine microbial community structure, often alongside phylogenetic profiling to reveal community patterns and metabolic capabilities20,21. This phylogenetic marker gene enables the identification of co-occurring taxa, the creation of taxa-taxa networks, and the calculation of distance matrices22. However, accurately assigning functional capabilities and the specific role of taxa in GHG emissions remains challenging unless a statistical interface or machine learning algorithm is applied23. To address this, quantitative PCR (qPCR) has been used to detect and quantify functional genes related to GHG fluxes24, providing a gene-based estimate as evidence of metabolic pathways25,26. For instance, the abundance of the mcrA (methyl coenzyme M reductase) gene, which catalyses the final step of methanogenesis, is often measured to evaluate the presence and activity of methanogens in natural ecosystems26–28. Similarly, the pmoA (particulate methane monooxygenase) gene appears vital for initiating methane oxidation metabolism and has served as a molecular marker to identify groups of functional aerobic methanotrophs, with the exception of some species such as Methylocella sp. and Methyloferula sp29–31. Moreover, the amoA gene, common across ammonia-oxidising bacteria (AoB) and ammonia-oxidising archaea (AoA)32,33, along with the nitrous oxide reductase gene (nosZ), are key genetic markers linked to global N2O emissions34.

While the major microbial processes driving GHG emissions in terrestrial environments, including their functional roles and correlations, are relatively well-understood35–37, aquaculture systems remain largely underexplored in this context. GHG emission fluxes have been studied in shrimp38–40, finfish8,38,41, and mollusk38,42 farms, but none of these studies explicitly addressed the microbiome structure as a driving factor of GHG emissions. An exception is the study by Deng et al., who demonstrated that CH4 and N2O emissions from different aquaculture ponds (not including prawn farms) were not stochastic processes but were determined by microbial communities and specific functional genes14. Therefore, to provide novel empirical evidence from prawn farms in Bangladesh, we monitored CO2, CH4, and N2O fluxes and analysed microbial community compositions using high-resolution Illumina sequencing. Further, we determined the abundance of functional genes (mcrA, pmoA, amoA, and nosZ) related to GHG emissions using qPCR and investigated how these genes influence the emissions in relation to water quality parameters in prawn farms.

Methods

Study site and sample collections

The Southwest coastal region of Bangladesh resembles a typical setting for prawn farming in South Asian countries. A total of 35 prawn farms were selected in this region to collect GHGs, along with sediment and water samples and water quality data, ensuring coverage of different salinity gradients, farming systems, and practices (see Table 1, Table ST1, and Figure S1 for more information).

Gas samples were collected using the floating chamber method described by Yuan et al.43. Gas samples from the air-water interface within chambers were collected (Figure S2) at 30-minute intervals (0 min, 30 min, and 60 min) on sunny days between 09:00 and 12:00. These samples were placed into pre-evacuated 12 mL sealed glass vials (Labco Exetainer®, UK). In-situ environmental parameters, including water temperature, salinity, dissolved oxygen, pH, and total dissolved solids (TDS), were measured using a multi-parameter analyzer (Multi-Checker U-50, Horiba, Japan). Water samples for total ammonia nitrogen (TAN) analysis were collected at a depth of 15–20 cm and measured with a spectrophotometer (Hach, Germany).

For molecular analyses, water samples from a depth of 15–20 cm were filtered through glass fibre filters in triplicate and preserved in 98% ethanol. Sediment samples were collected using a sterile stainless-steel auger (50 mm diameter), with three cores (0–3 cm) obtained from each farm. Each core was further subdivided into two fragments: aerobic sediment (AeS: <1 cm) and anaerobic sediment (AnS: 1–3 cm). Although oxygen penetration in high-organic aquaculture systems is typically limited to a few millimetres44, we recognised that the bioturbation activity of prawns might facilitate oxygenation up to 1 cm. However, we acknowledge that these classifications are not definitive and do not specify precise redox boundaries. All triplicates were pooled and blended to minimise spatial variation within the farms, then stored at -80 °C for further processing.

GHG concentration measurement and emission flux calculation

Detector temperatures were set at 255 °C and 360 °C, respectively, and helium gas flowing at 30 mL/min was used as the carrier gas. DNA extraction, PCR library preparation, and 16 S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 0.3 g preserved sediment samples and filter papers using DNeasy PowerSoil and DNeasy PowerWater kits (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), respectively, following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA libraries were prepared using a two-step PCR method as described by Chen et al.45. Considering the importance of the archaeal community in GHG emission dynamics, the V4-V5 region of 16 S rRNA was amplified using the 519 F and 915R primer pair46, under the cycling conditions shown in Table S2. This primer pair amplifies both bacterial and archaeal domains and is well-suited for archaeal phylogenomics47. Illumina overhang adapters were added to the primer pair and sample-specific 8-bp oligonucleotide indexes were incorporated during the 2nd PCR step, following the Nextera XT library preparation protocol. PCR products were cleaned up after each PCR step using the DNA Clean and Concentrator kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA). More information on the two-step PCR method is provided in Supplementary file 1. In an equimolar concentration (15 ng/uL), amplicons were then pooled together in a single Eppendorf tube and submitted to Novogene Sequencing Services, UK, for pair-end (2×250 bp) Illumina Nova-Seq sequencing.

Bioinformatics

Raw pair-end FASTQ files were processed using the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME2: version 2023.9) pipeline, following the standard procedure48. All scripts used for QIIME2 analyses are publicly available in the GitHub repository (https://github.com/bashar43791/GHG-Microbes). Briefly, primers and adapter sequences were removed using the Cutadapt plugin. Reads were then demultiplexed and quality-filtered, discarding those with a Phred quality score below 25. The DADA2 program was used to denoise and merge reads, remove chimeras, and cluster into high-resolution amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). Alpha diversity and species richness for bacterial and archaeal communities were calculated using the Shannon and Chao1 indices, respectively, with the Phyloseq package in R. Beta-diversity was assessed with Bray-Curtis dissimilarity index and community variations across the salinity gradients were visualized using Nonmetric Multidimensional Scaling (NMDS) ordination plots generated in R with vegan and ampvis2 packages. Taxonomic assignment was performed with a trained Naïve Bayes Classifier via q2-feature-classifier plugin, referencing the SILVA 16 S rRNA SSU 138.1 gene database. Representative sequences were aligned with the qiime2 MFFT plugin to display phylogenetic trees using the Interactive Tree of Life (version: 6.9.1) annotation editor49.

Gene cloning and quantification of functional genes using qPCR

Using the primer pairs listed in Supplementary Table 1, the mcrA, pmoA, amoA, and nosZ genes were amplified from DNA samples and their abundances were calculated using the AriaMX qPCR system (Agilent Technologies, California, USA). To amplify target genes, PCR reactions were prepared in a 50 µL final volume containing 0.5 µL of VWR polymerase (5U), 5 µL of VWR key Buffer (10x), 5 µL of dNTPs (10mM), 1 µL of each primer (10µM), 3 µL of template DNA, and nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions were obtained from Bashar et al.50. PCR products were ligated into the pGEM-T Easy vector (Promega, Wisconsin, USA) and transformed into JM104: E. coli competent cells. After blue-white screening, successful colonies were cultured overnight at 37 °C in LB media containing ampicillin. Plasmid DNA was extracted using the QIAprep Spin Miniprep Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and successful inserts were confirmed through restriction digestion, followed by gel run and Sanger sequencing (Figure S3). Plasmid copy numbers were calculated and 10-fold serial dilutions were prepared to generate standard curves51. qPCR assays were carried out in a 20 µL final volume containing 10 µL SYBR green master mix, 7.2 µL nuclease-free water, 0.5 µL BSA (20 mg/mL), 0.4 µL of each primer (10 µM), and 1.5 µL of DNA template. Amplifications were performed using the following PCR thermal profile: initial hot start at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s and annealing at 55 °C for 60 s (mcrA), 60 °C for 30 s (amoA and nosZ), or 60 °C for 60 s (pmoA)50. Melt curve analysis ranged from 55 °C to 95 °C, with fluorescence signals captured at gene-specific temperatures: 89 °C for mcrA, 88 °C for pmoA, 83 °C for amoA, and 87.5 °C for nosZ. All reactions, including standards, were run in triplicate using 96-well PCR plates and average Ct values were obtained from AriaMx Software (version: 2.1) to calculate gene abundances.

Statistical analysis

The Kruskal-Wallis (KW) test, followed by Dunn’s test for post hoc comparison, was conducted to evaluate the statistical significance of differences in GHG emissions across salinity gradients, culture patterns, and systems. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using the cor function in R, and a correlation heatmap was generated with ggplot2 to visualise the relationship between emissions and water quality. To reveal microbial associations with GHG emissions, co-occurrence networks between GHG emissions and observed ASVs were constructed in Gephi (version 0.10.0). Plotting GHG fluxes against observed frequencies of bacterial and archaeal classes, Pearson correlation was computed to assess the statistical significance of taxa involved in emissions. A correlation threshold of r ≥ ± 0.3 was utilised to visualise the correlation network, with an FDR-adjusted p-value of < 0.05 to ensure the statistical robustness of the networks. To examine the effects of water quality on functional gene abundances and the relationships between GHG emissions and gene abundance, we applied the isoMDS function from the MASS package for non-metric MDS and used the corrplot function to visualise correlation matrices.

Scripts and codes related to these analyses are publicly available at Zenodo, deposited by Bashar et al.52

Results

Microbial diversity and community composition of Prawn farms

Salinity significantly affected microbial richness in prawn farming systems (Fig. 1A). The highest richness, as indicated by the observed ASVs index, was observed in medium-salinity farms, showing significant differences compared to other systems (p < 0.05; KW test). Similarly, anaerobic samples exhibited significantly higher observed ASVs than aerobic samples (p < 0.01; KW test) (Fig. 1B). The Shannon index was also highest in medium-salinity and anaerobic samples, although differences across salinity and redox conditions were not statistically significant (Fig. 1C-D). NMDS ordination plots revealed distinct clustering of communities across the salinity spectrum (Fig. 1E). A clear separation was observed in community composition between farms in high- and low-salinity zones, while samples from medium- and high-salinity ponds showed closer similarity. However, the beta-diversity index indicated no significant difference between the community structures of aerobic and anaerobic samples (Fig. 1F). Pairwise PERMANOVA confirmed a significant effect of salinity on microbial community composition (Pseudo F = 2.22, p = 0.001), while the sampling site had a non-significant impact (Pseudo F = 1.07, p = 0.298) (Figure S4 and Table S2).

After quality optimization, a total of 29,519 ASVs with an average length of 379.28 bp were obtained. A total of 62 phyla were detected, of which 12 phyla exhibited salinity-specific occurrence: nine unique to low-salinity, two to medium-salinity, and only one to high-salinity (Table ST2 in Supplementary file 2). These indicator taxa likely contributed to the observed variation in microbial communities across salinity gradients. Bacterial and archaeal phyla with a domain-specific relative abundance of ≥ 1% are shown in Fig. 2. The predominant phyla were Crenarchaeota (30.12%), Euryarchaeota (17.76%), Chloroflexi (13.15%), Parvarchaeota (11.06%), Proteobacteria (8.41%), Bacteroidetes (7.20%), and Firmicutes (3.42%) (Fig. 2A-D). Among them, Chloroflexi, Crenarchaeota, and Euryarchaeota, in decreasing order, shared the highest number of ASVs across the three salinity zones. Most of the archaeal phyla were relatively more abundant in high-salinity farms, except Crenarchaeota, which was relatively more abundant in low-salinity farms (Fig. 2A and Table ST3 in Supplementary file 2). Distinct differences in phyla composition were also observed between aerobic and anaerobic samples, with several indicator phyla present exclusively under one condition, reflecting habitat-specific microbial assemblages (Table ST4 in Supplementary file 2).

GHG emission fluxes and their relationship with microbial richness, diversity, and evenness

GHG emissions varied considerably with salinity and farming type, showing different trends. Average CO2 fluxes (1233.6 ± 484.7 g/ha/day) were significantly higher in low-salinity farms and decreased as salinity increased (Fig. 3A). Both culture patterns and systems significantly affected CO2 emissions, with polyculture (1068.3 ± 430.3 g/ha/day) and extensive farms (1233.5 ± 461.7 g/ha/day) exhibiting the highest average emissions (Fig. 3B, C). Conversely, average CH4 emissions were lowest in low-salinity farms (4.46 ± 3.5 g/ha/day), with increases observed in medium-salinity (8.75 ± 3.77 g/ha/day) and high-salinity (9.42 ± 4.46 g/ha/day) farms (Fig. 3A). N2O emissions were relatively higher in low-salinity (0.80 ± 0.39 g/ha/day), co-culture (0.65 ± 0.41 g/ha/day), and cluster (0.59 ± 0.28 g/ha/day) farms, though none of these differences were statistically significant (Fig. 3A-C). Correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive relationship between CO2 emissions and salinity. Meanwhile, CH4 fluxes were negatively correlated with dissolved oxygen and positively correlated with the age of standing water (Fig. 3D).

Although farms with different salinity levels exhibited the highest average emissions and microbial richness, CO2 and N2O emissions were strongly positively correlated with the Chao1 index (Fig. 4A, I) and richness (sum of ASVs: Fig. 4B, J). In contrast, CH4 emissions were negatively correlated with microbial richness (Fig. 4E, F). Alpha diversity in terms of the Shannon index (Fig. 4C, G,K) and Pielou’s evenness (Fig. 4D, H,L) showed no strong correlation with any of the GHG emissions, suggesting that species richness, rather than diversity and evenness, may be a key driver of GHG emissions in prawn farming systems.

Phylogeny and community composition of core bacterial and archaeal groups

Given their key role in regulating GHG emission and consumption, we focused on the community composition of core microbial groups, including methanogens, methanotrophs, archaeal and bacterial ammonium oxidizers (AoA/B), and nitrous oxide-reducing bacteria (NRB). To confirm their presence and explore diversity, we filtered ASVs using the qiime taxa filter-table feature to include only core taxa and visualize their relative abundance interactively with the phylogenetic trees (Fig. 5). A total of 1877 ASVs were assigned to these core taxa, representing 35.7% methanogens, 6.2% methanotrophs, 27.3% AoA/B, and 30.8% NRB (also see sample-specific relative abundance of core taxa in Table ST5 in Supplementary file 2).

Because many ASVs could not be classified down to the family level, we analysed methanogen diversity at the order level. Methanobacteriales was the most diverse order with 252 ASVs, followed by Methanosarcinales (221 ASVs) and Methanomicrobiales (117 ASVs) (Fig. 5A and Table ST6 in Supplementary file 2). Methanogen composition varied significantly with salinity, showing the highest relative abundance in high-salinity samples. Among the identified genera, Methanospirillum was dominant, followed by Methanosaeta and Methanosphaerula. With the exception of Methanobacterium beijingense, Methanothermobacter tenebrarum, and Methanosalsum zhilinae, methanogen species showed notably higher relative abundance in anaerobic samples. In contrast, methanotrophs, including archaeal (ANME-2a-2b, ANME-2D) and bacterial (Beijerinckiaceae and Methylobacteriaceae) families, were relatively more abundant in aerobic samples. Among these, ANME-2D (currently known as Methanoperedenaceae) exhibited the highest diversity with 58 ASVs. Methylocapsa aurea emerged as the most dominant methanotroph species across sites. Interestingly, the relative abundance of methanotrophs was lower in high-salinity farms (Fig. 5B and Table ST7 in Supplementary file 2).

Within AoA/B clades, archaeal families (Nitrososphaeraceae and Cenarchaeaceae) exhibited significantly lower relative abundances in medium salinity, whereas bacterial families, such as Chromatiaceae and Nitrospiraceae, were predominant at low salinities (Fig. 5D and Table ST8 in Supplementary file 2). Candidatus Nitrososphaera SCA1170 was the most dominant AoA species but was absent in aerobic samples. In the NRB clade, Bacillaceae was the most dominant family with a significantly higher relative abundance in low-salinity farms, while Caldilineaceae was the most diverse, accounting for 59.86% NRB ASVs (Fig. 5C and Table ST9 in Supplementary file 2). Only 15 NRB ASVs were shared across all salinity zones and no ASVs belonging to the Myxococcaceae family were detected in anaerobic samples. NRB taxa were relatively more abundant in medium-salinity farms and anaerobic samples, with Litorilinea aerophila emerging as the most dominant species.

Correlations among GHG emissions and the abundance of core taxa

To better understand the microbial link to GHG emissions, we examined correlations between GHG emissions and microbial taxa, focusing on class-level associations due to limited resolution at the genus or family level (Fig. 6). Pearson correlation coefficient values were used to build networks between emissions and microbial classes. CO2 emissions were significantly and positively correlated with several classes, including Deltaproteobacteria (r = 0.823, p < 0.05), Alphaproteobacteria (r = 0.76, p < 0.05), Gammaproteobacteria (r = 0.73, p < 0.05), MHVG (r = 0.72, p < 0.01), and OP11-3 (r = 0.71, p < 0.01) (Fig. 6A). These taxa also showed significant negative correlations with CH4 emissions (Fig. 6B). Interestingly, although the methanotroph class ANME-1 demonstrated a significant negative correlation with CH4 emissions (r=-0.913, p < 0.05), its relationship with CO2 fluxes was not significant (r = 0.43, p > 0.05). Among archaeal classes, Thermoplasmata (r = 0.92, p < 0.05), Methanobacteria (r = 0.85, p < 0.05), Methanomicrobia (r = 0.84, p < 0.05), and Thaumarchaeota (r = 0.70, p < 0.05) were positively associated with CH4 emission. However, only Methanomicrobia showed a significant negative correlation with CO2 emission (r= -0.92, p < 0.05). Conversely, Methanococci did not have a significant correlation with CH4 flux (r=-0.31, p > 0.05) but exhibited a strong negative correlation with CO2 emission (r=-0.90, p < 0.05). Other taxa negatively associated with CO2 emission included carbon-fixing microbes such as Chloroflexi (r=-0.84, p < 0.05) and Chloroplast (r=-0.73, p < 0.05). For N2O emission, Chloroflexi showed a positive association (r = 0.50, p < 0.05), which contradicts existing evidence53. Network modularity analysis highlighted that N2O emission was negatively linked with the largest number of bacterial and archaeal taxa, although none of these linkages were strongly correlated.

The abundance of functional genes and their correlation with GHG emissions

To substantiate the observed links between microbial taxa and GHG emissions, we examined the relationships between GHG emissions and the abundance of functional genes involved in GHG cycling, including mcrA, pmoA, amoA, and nosz. The mcrA gene, which indicates methanogenesis, was most abundant in anaerobic sediments (Table 2), highlighting its sensitivity to oxygen. This is further supported by the observed negative correlation between dissolved oxygen and mcrA genes (Fig. 7A). Interestingly, it was mcrA abundance in water, rather than in sediment, that showed a strong positive correlation with CH4 emission and a negative correlation with CO2 emission (Fig. 7B). Conversely, the pmoA gene was significantly more abundant in aerobic sediment (Table 2) and positively correlated with dissolved oxygen (Fig. 7A). Its abundance under aerobic conditions was positively associated with CO2 emission and negatively correlated with CH4 flux (Fig. 7B), indicating its role in CH4 oxidation.

The amoA gene showed the highest abundance in anaerobic samples and the lowest in water (Table 2). In sediment, its abundance was positively linked to dissolved oxygen, while in water, it was positively related to TAN (Fig. 7A). Notably, amoA abundance in sediment had a stronger negative correlation with CH4 emission than pmoa. In water, the gene had a significant positive association with N2O emissions. Finally, the nosZ gene was most abundant in anaerobic sediment and negatively correlated with TAN and pH (Fig. 7A). Although nosZ was found in all sample types, only its abundance in anaerobic sediment was significantly linked to N2O emission.

Discussion

Our average estimated emission of GHG for prawns was 589.2 ± 319.24 g CO2eq kg− 1, which is significantly lower than the estimate reported by Poore and Nemecek54, who projected an indirect emission of 9.0 kg CO2eq per 100 g of protein, equivalent to 18–22.5 kg CO2eq per kg of prawn. This discrepancy may arise from their combined (and possibly inflated) estimate, based on database values of both prawn and shrimp. Conversely, our estimate could also be conservative, as it did not include emissions related to value chains, on-farm energy use, and transportation, as in Poore and Nemecek54. The expected substantial impact of these non-biological factors highlights the need for a comprehensive life-cycle assessment to improve carbon footprint estimates. However, instead of focusing on the absolute carbon footprint, our study mainly aimed to demonstrate comparative emission dynamics in relation to water quality parameters and their statistical connection with microbial communities, thus establishing a basis for future research.

We observed that although salinity and redox potential significantly affected species richness, the differences in alpha diversity were not statistically significant. This suggests that salinity and likely oxygen availability influenced the presence/absence of taxa more than their relative abundances. Ordination plots further confirmed that salinity had a notable impact on microbial community composition, indicating that microbial sensitivity to salinity plays a vital role in shaping community structures (Fig. 1E, F). Although microbial richness correlated strongly with emissions and was highest at medium salinity, the highest CO2 and N2O emissions were recorded in low-saline farms. This apparent inconsistency may be due to the fact that not all microbial taxa contributing to richness are directly involved in GHG emissions dynamics. Therefore, we focused on specific functional groups, including methanogens, methanotrophs, AOA/B, and NRB, as core microbial groups most directly associated with either emission or consumption.

The substantial presence of methanogens across all salinity zones demonstrates their broad environmental tolerance and consistent ability for CH4 production regardless of conditions. Among them, acetoclastic methanogens, such as Methanosarcina and Methanosaeta, are known to significantly contribute to aquatic methane production55. In prawn farms, these taxa were relatively more abundant in high-salinity environments, which may explain the elevated CH4 fluxes observed in such farms. Methanogenesis in Methanosaeta follows the Wood–Ljungdahl pathway, acquiring methyl groups from acetate that are subsequently reduced to methane56. Yet, members of this genus can also utilise CO2 as a substrate57. This may account for the strong negative correlation observed between Methanomicrobia and CO2 emissions. Although most clades within the Thermoplasmata class are reported to have lost their methanogenic capacity58, we observed a strong association of Thermoplasmata with methane emissions. This could be due to certain lineages, such as Methanomassiliicoccales, believed to have either retained or acquired mcr genes, enabling methylotrophic pathways58,59. While the Methanobacteria class showed a strong correlation with methane emissions, their association with CO2 consumption was minor, indicating a hydrogenotrophic form of methanogenesis that relies more heavily on substrates like alcohols, formate, and hydrogen60. However, since the study did not use metagenomics, transcriptomics, or isotope probing, these functional interpretations should be considered with caution. Interestingly, higher relative abundances of some obligate anaerobes, including Methanobacterium beijingense and Methanothermobacter tenebrarum, were observed in the aerobic samples. This unexpected finding may point to physiological adaptation or local-scale evolution, enabling these species to survive under oxygenated conditions61,62.

The co-occurrence networks linking Alphaproteobacteria and CO2/CH4 emissions suggest a potential role for methanotrophs in both CO2 emission and methane oxidation. Since most CH4 is produced in anoxic sites by methanogens, where CH4-oxidising bacteria are typically absent, the anaerobic oxidation of CH4 by ANME appears to be an important biological sink for CH463. As illustrated in Fig. 6B, the negative association between ANME-1 and CH4 suggests a significant role of ANME lineages in anaerobic methane oxidation. However, because the ANME process is oxygen-sensitive and limited to anaerobic sediment, the CO2 produced during this process may either remain sequestered in the sediment or be underrepresented by the large amount of CO2 produced in oxic zones. This could partly explain the weak link between ANME and CO2 emissions observed in our study. Nonetheless, the first scenario highlights ANME-facilitated CH4 oxidation as a promising microbial approach to remove CH4 simultaneously and promote carbon sinks in aquatic ecosystems. Unexpectedly, no AOA/B class but Chloroflexi showed a significant correlation with N2O emissions. One explanation is that N2O emissions in prawn farms were mainly caused by incomplete oxidation of NH2OH, rather than autotrophic denitrification driven by AOA/B64. Alternatively, the observed clades may be involved in the initial nitrification process (oxidation of NH3 to NO2−), while later steps influencing N2O production may depend more on abiotic factors.

Most surprisingly, although present in smaller quantities, mcrA genes in the water correlated with CH4 production and CO2 consumption, suggesting the possible existence of some methanogen lineages adapted to aerobic physiology65,66. However, since correlation does not necessarily imply causation, the association in oxic waters could also result from micro-niches, sediment resuspension, or extracellular DNA, rather than indicating active methanogenesis. The association of pmoA genes in aerobic sediment with CH₄ oxidation and CO₂ emission likely reflects that aerobic sediment provides access to oxygen as an electron acceptor, while methane produced in underlying anaerobic zones serves as the substrate. Although the amoA gene encodes an enzyme for anaerobic ammonium oxidation, its abundance in anaerobic sediments, even when high, was not strongly correlated with N2O emissions. One possible explanation might be the presence of non-AOA taxa, such as certain Thaumarchaeota, which carry the amoA gene but are functionally inactive in ammonia oxidation67. As noted by Ge et al.68, some AOB genera, such as Nitrosomonas spp. and Nitrosococcus spp., may possess a broader metabolic capacity, including oxidising hydrocarbons like CH4 in addition to ammonium. This supports our observation of a strong negative association between the amoA gene and CH4 emission, suggesting a potential co-functional role for AOA/B. Although Marchant et al. reported that oxygen availability did not suppress nosZ expression in coastal sediment69, we did not observe any notable negative correlation between nosZ gene abundances and N2O emissions, unless the gene was present in the anoxic sediment.

Conclusion

As expected, this study revealed that CH4 emission was positively associated with most methanogen classes, except for Methanomicrobia. Our results also indicate the role of anaerobic methanotrophs in anaerobic methane oxidation, serving as a potential sink for CH4 and CO2 in pond sediments. This function could be a promising target for intervention, which could boost methane-reducing microbes in the aquaculture environment. Another important finding is that mcrA genes were widely distributed regardless of oxygen availability, although their actual activity and expression likely depend on environmental conditions and organismal adaptation. Although amoA gene abundance was higher in anaerobic sediments, it did not show any correlation with N2O emission. However, its strong association with methane oxidation suggests a possible co-function of AOA/B in CH4 oxidation. Based on these findings, the study generated several outlooks and concrete hypotheses for future exploration, including the aerobic physiology of the mcrA gene, anaerobic methanotrophs that facilitate methane oxidation, and methane co-oxidation by amoA-containing microbes.

The study has several limitations. First, gas sampling was conducted only once, which reduces the accuracy of carbon footprint estimates for prawn aquaculture. Second, although we measured gene abundances and microbial community composition, gene expression and enzyme activity were not analysed. Since gene abundance does not necessarily indicate function, this could influence the interpretations linking genes to GHG emissions. Third, while anaerobic methanotrophs were found to be associated with methane oxidation, specific genes responsible for anaerobic methane oxidation were not identified, nor was it possible to distinguish between mcrA genes involved in methanogenesis and those related to anaerobic methane oxidation. This restricts the functional conclusions regarding anaerobic methanotrophic archaea.
