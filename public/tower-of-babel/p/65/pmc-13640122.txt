Comparative outcomes of flexible ureterorenoscopy with high-power holmium: YAG laser and flexible, navigable suction access sheath vs mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy in 2–3 cm kidney stones treatment: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Muzaffer Tansel Kılınç, Yunus Emre Göger, Ömer Faruk Çavdar, Hakan Hakkı Taşkapu, Mehmet Serkan Özkent
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn this prospective controlled cohort study, we aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of Retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) combined with high-power holmium:YAG laser and vacuum-assisted, flexible, navigable access sheaths (FANS) vs. mini-percutaneous nephrolithotomy (miniPCNL) in 2–3 cm kidney stones.MethodsBetween June 2022 and June 2024, 60 patients with 2–3 cm in diameter kidney stones, aged 18 years or older, were prospectively included. Patients were classified into two groups based on their decision: RIRS (Group 1) and miniPCNL (Group 2). Patient demographic data, preoperative creatinine, and hemoglobin levels were recorded. Stone-related factors were evaluated using preoperative tomography. Creatinine and hemoglobin values were measured on the first postoperative day. Stone-free status (SFRs) was evaluated on the first postoperative day and at the first month. Hospital stays and complications were assessed. This study was retrospectively registered to ClinicalTrials.gov (ID = NCT07349992) on January 16, 2026.ResultsPatient demographic data, preoperative creatinine levels, hemoglobin levels, and stone-related factors were comparable between the groups. The prestenting rate was higher in Group 1 (16.6%, 95% CI: 3.3–30) than in Group 2 (0%, 95% CI: 0–0, p = 0.02). The operative time and complication rates were comparable between the groups. The fluoroscopy time was lower in Group 1 (p < 0.001). The mean hemoglobin decrease was greater in Group 2 (p < 0.001). Stone-free status was comparable on the first day and first month. Hospital stay was shorter in Group 1 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsRetrograde intrarenal surgery combined with a high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS shows comparable outcomes to miniPCNL for 2–3 cm kidney stones, yielding a similar SFRs with lower hemoglobin decrease, shorter hospitalization time, and reduced fluoroscopy exposure. However, increased prestenting rates should be considered.Trial registrationThis study was retrospectively registered to ClinicalTrials.gov (ID = NCT07349992) on January 16, 2026.

Introduction

Nephrolithiasis is increasingly prevalent worldwide and may affect 15% of the population in endemic areas [1]. Understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms specific to stone formation and medical preventive measures is the primary means of preventing recurrence. However, stones that have already formed usually require surgical intervention [2].

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS)— the most prominent endourological stone treatment modalities—are subject to growing interest among urologists today. Current guidelines recommend PCNL as the first-line modality for stones larger than 2 cm [3, 4]. The most significant concern about this surgical method is that, despite its effectiveness and stone-free rates (SFRs) of over 90% after the first session, associated complication rates can reach 30% because of its invasive nature [4, 5]. Therefore, in parallel with technological advancements, the miniaturization of nephroscopes has increased. Although there is no clear definition of miniaturized PCNL techniques in the literature, the accepted terminology for nephroscopes using < 24 French (Fr) sheaths is "miniPCNL" [6]. This method offers SFRs comparable to those of standard PCNLwhile providing advantages such as less bleeding, reduced invasiveness, lower pain, and shorter hospitalization time [7].

In endourology, the use of RIRS for stones measuring 2–3 cm is also becoming increasingly common, driven by innovations in flexible ureterorenoscopes and laser technology [8]. Smaller-caliber, flexible ureterorenoscopes with higher resolution and maneuverability facilitate ureteral passage and access to stones in the kidney [9]. In addition, surgical outcomes may improve and complication rates may decrease as experience increases and surgical skills improve over time [10].

The holmium:YAG laser, which is the gold standard for lithotripsy, has also been developed, enabling the use of "high-power" lasers that can produce power exceeding 35 Watts with higher pulse frequencies and energy [11]. Although recommendations and cost-effectiveness regarding routine clinical use remain topical and contradictory, SFRs comparable to those of low-power lasers, shorter operative time, and similar complication rates are promising [11, 12]. In addition, newer lasers, such as holmium:YAG lasers with lower peak-power pulse modulation and thulium fiber lasers, stand out for their similar intraoperative results and better dusting performance [13].

During RIRS, where drainage is slower than during PCNL, residual fragments, renal damage, and infectious complications due to increased intrarenal pressure remain significant issues, especially with stones > 2 cm [14]. With ureteral access sheaths (UASs) and vacuum-applied UASs, it has become possible to reduce these clinical problems and improve SFRs [15]. The latest innovations on the market are vacuum-assisted, flexible, and navigable access sheaths (FANS), which can be passively steered with a flexible tip and have a lower likelihood of mucosal trauma. FANS has been reported to be more advantageous in reducing intrarenal pressure and achieving higher SFRs than traditional UASs [16].

The combined use of endourological equipment in RIRS may show comparable outcomes to miniPCNL by increasing SFRs for 2–3 cm stones [17]. In this way, attempts to obtain similar SFRs with lower complication rates have been the topic of research. Current findings indicate that RIRS with FANS result in a lower bleeding risk, shorter hospital stay, less pain, and similar SFRs compared with miniPCNL for 2–3 cm kidney stones [17, 18]. However, the data are limited. To the best of our knowledge, although low-power lasers have been studied, no data are available on high-power lasers. This prospective controlled cohort study aimed to compare the safety and efficacy of RIRS combined with high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS with that of miniPCNL in patients with 2–3 cm kidney stones. The study was designed as a comparative analysis to assess differences in clinical outcomes between two endourological procedures rather than as a non-inferiority or equivalence study. The use of a high-power laser underscores the incremental value of this study compared with recent randomized controlled trials. The effects of FANS on intrarenal pressure are not directly measured in this study.

Materials and methods

Study design and data collection

Between June 2022 and June 2024, 60 patients aged 18 years or older with kidney stones 2–3 cm in diameter who were treated at a university hospital and agreed to participate were prospectively included in this study. Before the study, patients were informed about RIRS and miniPCNL surgeries. The study protocol was explained, and informed consent was obtained from all patients. Pregnant women and patients with kidney transplantation, congenital renal anomalies, and uncorrectable coagulopathy were excluded from the study. Patients were classified into two groups based on their decision to undergo RIRS (Group 1) or miniPCNL (Group 2).

Patient demographic data (age, gender, body mass index [BMI], comorbidities, anticoagulant use), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, preoperative creatinine and hemoglobin levels, and urine culture were recorded. All surgical procedures were performed after a negative urine culture. Surgery was planned for patients with urinary tract infections after obtaining negative cultures following antibiotic treatment.

Renal anatomic assessment and stone-related factors (side, size, number, location, and Hounsfield unit) were evaluated using preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) by a radiologist independent of the study. Stone size for single stones was measured as the longest diameter. For multiple stones, the sum of the longest diameters of each stone was calculated. Perioperative data, including prestenting, operative time, and fluoroscopy time, were recorded simultaneously for both groups.

Creatinine and hemoglobin values were measured on the first postoperative day. Stone-free status was assessed by kidney–ureter–bladder radiography for opaque stones on the first postoperative day and by urinary ultrasound for non-opaque stones. Stone-free status was defined as the presence of fragments with a maximum size of < 3 mm. Hospital stays and complications (Clavien grade) were assessed in the postoperative period. Patients were evaluated for early complications 2 weeks after discharge. Patients were reevaluated for SFR using low-dose CT in the first month. Because there are no exact SFR limits, based on the literature, < 3 mm was accepted as clinically insignificant [19]. Stone-free status in the first month was selected as the primary endpoint. If SFR was achieved, the double J (DJ) stent was removed in the first month. If this status was not achieved, auxiliary procedures were performed after the first month. Patients were also evaluated for long-term complications at the second postoperative month.

Surgical methods

Retrograde intrarenal surgery

All procedures were performed by two experienced (> 100 cases) surgeons in the lithotomy position. General anesthesia was preferred. The ureter was entered under direct vision with a 6/7.5 Fr semirigid ureterorenoscope (Richard Wolf, Germany), guided by a 0.035-inch guidewire placed in the collecting system. A 10 Fr 46 cm FANS (ClearPetra, China) was inserted under fluoroscopy, through which a 7.5 Fr flexible ureterorenoscope (HugeMed, China) was advanced to reach the renal pelvis. When the semirigid ureterorenoscope or FANS could not be advanced due to the ureter’s small caliber, hydrodilatation was performed. If access to the renal pelvis was not achieved after hydrodilatation, surgery was planned after passive dilatation with a 4.8 Fr DJ stent. Surgery was performed 2 weeks after prestenting.

After the stone to be fragmented was reached with a flexible ureterorenoscope, the FANS was directed toward the stone, and the suction device was activated with the pressure vent open. Laser fragmentation was performed using a high-power Ho:YAG laser (Jena Surgical, Germany) at energy settings of 0.3–0.5 J, 50–80 Hz/long pulse. After the stones were fragmented to a size that could pass through the FANS, the pressure vent was closed, and stone suction was performed. Irrigation was applied with a gravity-driven system at a flow rate of 80 mL/min, and suction was applied at a negative pressure of 80–90 cmH2O.

Once laser fragmentation was completed, the guidewire was placed into the renal pelvis, and the FANS was retreated under direct vision with the flexible ureterorenoscope. A 4.8 Fr DJ stent was then placed in the kidney. Operative time was measured from insertion of the semirigid ureterorenoscope through the urethra to insertion of the Foley catheter.

Minipercutaneous nephrolithotomy

All procedures were performed by the same two surgeons in the lithotomy position. General anesthesia was preferred. To eliminate ureteral pathologies, the ureter was entered with a 6/7.5 Fr semirigid ureterorenoscope (Richard Wolf, Germany) under 0.035-inch guidewire guidance, and the guidewire was placed in the renal pelvis. After a 5 Fr catheter was placed under fluoroscopy, the patient was turned to the prone position. If the catheter could not be placed, hydrodilatation was planned; if this procedure also failed, passive dilatation with a DJ stent was planned.

Puncture was performed with a needle (18-gauge) under fluoroscopy from the lower pole posterior calyx. The tract was dilated with Amplatz renal dilators, and a 22 Fr sheath was placed. Stones were accessed using a 20.8 Fr nephroscope (Richard Wolf, Germany), and fragmentation was performed using a pneumatic lithotripter. The fragmented stones were removed from the kidney using a grasper and retrograde irrigation. Suction was not used during miniPCNL.

The ureteral catheter was removed, and a 4.8 Fr DJ stent was placed antegrade. A 14 Fr nephrostomy catheter was placed at the end of the procedure and removed the next day. Operative time was measured from passing the semirigid ureterorenoscope through the urethra to inserting the nephrostomy tube.

Statistical analysis

GPower v3.1 was used for power analysis. Cohen’s ideal effect size of 0.50 or greater was accepted. For a statistical power of 0.80, a sample size of 52 or more was sufficient for the variables in our study. SPSS statistical software was used for the analysis. Numerical data are presented as mean, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum values. Categorical variables were assessed in terms of sample size and percentage. The normality of the distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The t-test or Mann–Whitney U test was applied depending on whether the continuous data followed a normal distribution. All categorical data were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

When perioperative data were examined, the prestenting rate was higher in Group 1 (16.6%, 95% CI: 3.3–30) than in Group 2 (0%, 95% CI: 0–0, p = 0.02). The operative time was 74.4 ± 20.7 (50–145), 95% CI: 66.6–82.1 min in Group 1 and 78.2 ± 16 (50–120), 95% CI: 72.2–84.1 min in Group 2, and was comparable (p = 0.07). The fluoroscopy time was statistically lower in Group 1 than in Group 2 (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Postoperative creatinine levels were similar between groups (p = 0.70); however, mean hemoglobin decrease was significantly greater in Group 2 (0.43, 95% CI: 0.28–0.57 vs. 1.27, 95% CI: 0.91–1.62 g/dL, p < 0.001). Stone-free status on the first day (80%, 95% CI: 64.3–93.1 vs. 80%, 95% CI: 65.2–93.1) and at the first month (90%, 95% CI: 78.6–100 vs. 86.7%, 95% CI: 74.2–97.1) was comparable between the groups (p = 1.00 and 0.68, respectively). Hospital stay was shorter in Group 1 than in Group 2 (p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Complication rates were comparable between groups (p = 0.15). In Group 1, grade 1 complications were observed in four patients (fever in two, pain in two), grade 2 complications were observed in two patients (infection in one, stent discomfort in one), and grade 3B complications were observed in one patient (ureteral stenosis). Balloon dilatation was performed on the patient with ureteral stenosis detected in the second postoperative month, and the patient is being followed up. In Group 2, grade 1 complications were observed in four patients (fever in one, pain in three), and grade 2 complications were observed in two patients (stent discomfort in one and urge incontinence in one). No significant difference was found between groups in the chemical contents of the stones (p = 0.41) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrated that RIRS combined with a high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS had SFRs and complication rates similar to those of miniPCNL for kidney stones 2–3 cm in diameter. Shorter fluoroscopy time, shorter hospital stay, and a smaller hemoglobin decrease stood out as advantages of RIRS. However, higher prestenting rates in the RIRS group indicated a greater need for anesthesia sessions and secondary surgery.

In parallel with advancements in endourology, endoscopic interventions have become the cornerstone of stone surgery today. Percutaneous nephrolithotomy is the first-line management option for kidney stones larger than 2 cm [3, 4]. This method can provide large-volume stone clearance, and SFRs reaching 90% can be achieved in the first session [20]. However, the invasive nature of PCNL is the main reason for increasing complication rates. In a systematic review evaluating approximately 12,000 patients, the main complications reported were fever (10.8%), transfusion (7%), and thoracic complications (1.5%) [21].

Miniaturization of tract size and equipment is a prominent strategy in PCNL for reducing complication rates while maintaining success rates. Instead of standard PCNL, which uses a tract size of 24–30 Fr, miniPCNL using a < 24 Fr sheath provides similar SFRs while allowing lower transfusion rates, less pain, and shorter hospitalization [22]. In our study, we achieved an SFR of 80% on the first day and 86.7% in the first month in the miniPCNL group. While the complication rate was 20%, we observed no high-grade complications. However, the fact that a longer operative time and auxiliary procedure may be required compared to standard PCNL indicates that the method has limitations [23].

Technological innovations, improvements in the deflection capacity, image quality, and caliber of flexible ureterorenoscopes, increased surgical experience over time, and efforts to achieve minimally invasive surgery are expanding the application of RIRS for 2–3 cm kidney stones [24]. With developments in the holmium:YAG laser, the use of high-power lasers that can achieve energy levels above 35 W across a broader frequency range may increase the preference for RIRS in large stone volumes [25]. High-power lasers can contribute to improved surgical outcomes by providing similar SFRs and complication rates as well as shorter operative time, hospitalization, and fluoroscopy time compared with low-power lasers [12]. In our study using high-power lasers, the first-day SFR was 80%, the first-month SFR was 90%, and the complication rate was 23% in the RIRS group. These results are consistent with the literature. In vivo studies comparing high-power and low-power laser settings have not consistently demonstrated superior ablation efficiency with high-power settings. However, high-power laser settings may increase intrarenal temperature and thermal risk [26, 27]. Although FANS and irrigation flow rate can reduce intrarenal temperature, care should be taken to avoid thermal damage.

In our study, similar SFRs and complication rates of miniPCNL and RIRS for 2–3 cm stones indicate that a high-power laser can be applied for large-volume stones. In comparing the combined use of RIRS with FANS with miniPCNL for 2–3 cm stones, Zeng et al. found that a longer operative time was required for the RIRS group [17]. The similar operative times between the groups in our study may be due to the advantages of the high-power laser. The lower hemoglobin decrease and shorter hospital stay highlighted in our study also reflect the less invasive nature of RIRS. This suggests that the need for transfusion and the possibility of renal parenchymal injury may be higher with miniPCNL. Accordingly, RIRS may be preferred in patients with a high risk of bleeding.

Increased renal pelvic pressure during RIRS, especially for large-volume stones, and residual stone fragments are important factors that may negatively affect surgical outcomes [14, 28]. Access sheaths may provide advantages in this respect by reducing intrapelvic pressure and facilitating spontaneous drainage of small fragments [29]. Newly developed FANS can minimize mucosal damage by facilitating passage through the ureteropelvic junction [16, 18]. In addition, active fragmented stone aspiration may have contributed to shorter of the operative times, lower complication rates, and higher SFRs. In our study, similar SFRs, complication rates, and operative times were observed in patients undergoing RIRS compared with miniPCNL; this may be attributable to the contribution of FANS. However, patients should be carefully monitored for late complications, such as ureteral stenosis, as seen in one patient in our study. Despite the advantages of access sheaths, it should be kept in mind that currently used sheaths are not suitable for every ureteral caliber and that smaller equipment is needed [30]. For this reason, in cases in which ureteral placement is not possible, re-evaluation after passive dilatation with a DJ stent can prevent ureteral injury [31].

Considering that DJ stent placement is performed under anesthesia, the clinical implications of higher prestenting rates in patients undergoing RIRS combined with a high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS for 2–3 cm kidney stones become significant. Although no difference was observed in our study, it is worth considering that the risk of cumulative anesthesia complications may increase and that hospital stays may be prolonged in these patients due to repeated sessions. This may also increase healthcare costs, prolong the overall treatment process, and negatively affect patients’ quality of life. Furthermore, repeated urological surgeries have been reported to increase anxiety levels [32]. This may lead to poor treatment compliance and increased medicolegal issues. Patients undergoing RIRS for 2–3 cm stones should be properly informed about the possibility of prestenting and secondary surgery before surgical planning.

In the present prospective cohort study, the RIRS group had significantly shorter fluoroscopy times than the miniPCNL group for 2–3 cm renal stones. Although the relatively small sample size limits definitive conclusions, this finding may suggest a potential advantage of this approach in reducing radiation exposure during endourological stone surgery. Considering the cumulative risks associated with ionizing radiation for both patients and operating room staff, even modest reductions in fluoroscopy use may be clinically relevant.

Studies comparing the results of RIRS and PCNL using combined endoscopic surgical instruments for stones measuring 2–3 cm are emerging and report similar efficacy [17, 18, 33]. Both methods are considered effective, with high efficacy and acceptable complication rates. In our study, consistent with the literature, we observed similar SFRs and complication rates to miniPCNL with RIRS performed with FANS and high-power Ho:YAG lasers for 2–3 cm stones. The lower bleeding and shorter hospital stay associated with RIRS indicate that it is less invasive. High prestenting rates highlight to the need for repeated sessions requiring anesthesia. The most reliable approach is to evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of surgical procedures and select to appropriate method on a case-by-case basis. Furthermore, increasing data suggest that indications for kidney stones measuring 2–3 cm may become more flexible.

Our study has some limitations. One of the major limitations is the non-randomized, patient preference–based treatment allocation, which may have introduced substantial selection bias and weakened the strength of the comparative analysis between the two techniques. Patients selecting a specific procedure may have differed in baseline clinical characteristics, expectations, symptom severity, or psychosocial factors that were not fully controlled for and may have influenced postoperative outcomes independently of the surgical technique itself. Therefore, the comparative findings of this study should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis generating rather than definitive. Future randomized controlled studies with balanced baseline characteristics are required to validate these results. The sample size may limit the statistical power of the results. Although the basic sample size requirement was met in our study, the current sample size limited our capacity to detect small effects. Therefore, the limitations of statistical power should be considered when interpreting the results. Exclusion of congenital renal anomalies may limit the generalizability of the study findings to the general population. Retrospective clinical trial registration can be considered a limitation. Differences in the energy sources used during surgeries may have influenced operative outcomes. Using maximum diameter instead of stone volume is a relevant limitation. Significant differences in stone volume can exist even among stones with the same maximum diameter. In addition, the use of the prone position in miniPCNL may have affected the operative time. The supine position may make a difference by shortening the positional preparation period.

Despite these limitations, our prospective study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that RIRS combined with a high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS has surgical outcomes comparable to those of miniPCNL for 2–3 cm stones. The use of a high-power laser also provides preliminary data to the literature and strengthens our study. Prospective randomized controlled trials with larger patient numbers should support our data.

Conclusions

In this prospective cohort, RIRS with a high-power Ho:YAG laser and FANS demonstrated short-term outcomes comparable to those of miniPCNL in patients with 2–3 cm renal stones. The prestenting rate was higher in the RIRS group. However, this higher rate can increase healthcare costs, prolong the overall treatment process, and negatively affect patients’ quality of life. Nevertheless, a lower hemoglobin decrease and shorter hospital stay may increase the desirability of RIRS in patients at high risk of bleeding. The study’s non-randomized design, small sample size, differences in energy sources, and inability to calculate stone volume are significant limitations. Despite these limitations, this prospective study contributes to the literature by demonstrating that RIRS combined with a high-power holmium:YAG laser and FANS has surgical outcomes comparable to those of miniPCNL for 2–3 cm stones, with similar SFRs and complication rates. Larger randomized studies are required to confirm these findings.
