Fifty Years From the First Framingham Risk Profile: Are We Getting Closer to Identifying and PREVENTing Subclinical Disease Progression?
Authors: Catherine G Derington, Larry A Allen
Published: 2026

Cardiovascular risk prediction has a long history: The first cardiovascular risk profiling scheme out of the Framingham Heart Study was published 50 years ago, in 1976. 1 Preventive cardiology fundamentally relies on risk scores to estimate who is likely to develop future disease. With the development of imaging modalities that better identify and characterize the spectrum of cardiovascular disease combined with a proliferation of biomarkers and more sophisticated prediction algorithms, cardiovascular risk scores have continued to evolve. The next era of cardiovascular prevention is being pushed to ask how well risk scores predict subclinical disease and what do we do with that information.

The Predicting Risk of Cardiovascular Disease EVENTs (PREVENT) equations represent an important evolution in this space, expanding beyond the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCEs) to include heart failure (HF), markers of cardio–kidney–metabolic risk, inclusion of younger individuals, and 30‐year event horizons. The calculators also replace race as a predictor with a sociodemographic zip code variable to “personalize” estimates using the Area Deprivation Index. All these improvements position PREVENT as a method of thinking more proactively about cardiovascular risk along the spectrum from primordial disease to death (Figure). With an expanding arsenal of lipid and metabolic therapies that can be deployed earlier in the life course to improve cardiovascular health, a better understanding of the relationship between PREVENT scoring and assessments of subclinical disease should help clinicians better understand when to follow up with additional testing versus committing patients to treatment.

In this issue of Journal of the American Heart Association (JAHA), Fahed et al 2 seek to refine our understanding of the relationship of modern risk scoring to subclinical disease using data from 1138 real‐world participants of the Project Baseline Health Study. Subclinical atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) was defined as a coronary artery calcium (CAC) score >0 on noncontrast chest computed tomography. Subclinical HF was defined on echocardiography by left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunctionor an ejection fraction <50%. The majority of participants had a 10‐year PREVENT score of <5% (ie, “low risk”); the percentage of patients at low risk for ASCVD was 73%, for HF was 74%, and for either (ie, total cardiovascular disease) was 59%. Subclinical ASCVD and HF were detected in 41% and 16% of participants, respectively. Using the disease‐specific calculators, at a 7.5% threshold (“borderline” risk), roughly a third had ASCVD and/or HF. The PREVENT scores had good discrimination for detecting subclinical ASCVD and HF, with area‐under‐the‐curve metrics ranging from 0.66 to 0.87 depending on the specific outcome. Adding ECG parameters and biomarkers (eg, C‐reactive protein) did not incrementally increase the likelihood of detecting subclinical ASCVD or HF. These findings on modern prediction scores serve as a practical guide for identifying individuals who have present but silent disease.

These findings further inform the already remarkably successful implementation of CVD risk scoring into the fabric of modern medicine. Although concerns over accuracy of risk estimation and applicability to diverse populations emerged soon after the PCEs were recommended in the 2013 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines, 3 use of the PCEs has gradually become standard practice, with health systems such as ours incorporating informatics tools such as clinical decision support to calculate risk using the PCEs at the point of care. Clinicians describe the utility of the PCE to ground conversations about lifestyle modifications and medication prescribing with patients. 4 The relative success of PCE implementation should facilitate replacement by PREVENT in clinical practice and further application to care recommendations. However, clinicians must still be convinced that new models meaningfully improve decision making relative to established tools and do not add significant burden or confusion. Risk calculators that are difficult to implement or that produce uncertain clinical implications often fail to gain traction, even when statistically superior. For this reason, evidence by Fahed et al 2 connecting risk scores with observable subclinical disease helps bridge the gap between statistical prediction and clinical intuition.

These observations extend a growing literature of studies evaluating disease markers across PREVENT risk groups (Table). 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Beyond earlier work establishing that the PREVENT equations predict future events, PREVENT also correlates with biomarkers such as lipoprotein(a) and structural manifestations of disease that precede clinical outcomes. In other words, PREVENT appears to reliably capture biological processes already underway, although the relationship is imperfect: Not all individuals with higher predicted risk have detectable disease, and some classified as lower risk harbor subclinical disease. This discordance highlights a central tension of preventive cardiology: Risk prediction is imperfect, such that there tends to be ongoing uncertainty about whether to order more tests versus committing to treatment (or not) on the basis of existing data.

Therefore, if the PREVENT risk score is highly correlated with these subclinical disease markers, can we avoid testing? And if the additional testing results diverge from the calculated risk, how do we reconcile the two? When these approaches align, such as when a high‐risk individual has a high CAC score, they reinforce each other. However, when they diverge, clinicians face uncertainty. In the current study, a substantial proportion of participants classified as higher risk had little or no coronary calcification, highlighting the heterogeneity of risk. Conversely, some individuals with relatively low predicted risk exhibited detectable calcification or diastolic abnormalities. This discordance illustrates that risk prediction and imaging answer different questions. Risk scores estimate who is likely to develop overt disease (a future state), while imaging ideally reveals who already has early disease (current state). Determining which approach should guide management depends on context, resources, and the clinical decision at hand. For some, particularly those clearly at high risk, imaging may add little information because guideline‐directed preventive therapies are already indicated even in the absence of current subclinical disease. For others in borderline or intermediate risk categories, imaging may help refine decisions about pharmacologic therapy or lifestyle interventions.

This approach is consistent with the 2026 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association multisociety “Guideline on the Management of Dyslipidemia,” 11 which directs clinicians to use the PREVENT–ASCVD for primary prevention risk assessment while reserving CAC for treatment decisions that remain uncertain. In adults with borderline (PREVENT–ASCVD 3 to <5%) or intermediate (PREVENT–ASCVD 5 to <10%) risk, CAC testing is recommended to further risk‐stratify patients and guide initiation and intensification of lipid‐lowering therapy. When any CAC is present (ie, CAC >0, the same definition used in the study by Fahed et al2), the low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol should be lowered to <100 mg/dL with lipid‐lowering therapy, with more aggressive low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol targets as CAC increases to 100 to 299 AU, then 300 to 999 AU, then ≥1000 AU. Compared with the 2018 cholesterol guidelines, which primarily positioned CAC as a tiebreaker when statin decisions were uncertain, the 2026 guideline more explicitly incorporates CAC into risk refinement and treatment targeting, anchored within a PREVENT‐based framework. A similar concept has been proposed for HF, but data and guideline incorporation are behind that of ASCVD. 12

The challenge for clinicians is deciding when additional testing meaningfully changes management and when it simply confirms what is already known. While the subclinical markers used in the current study certainly carry some prognostic significance, the real‐world interpretation of these findings often complicates, rather than simplifies, management. CAC in particular provides a pragmatic marker of atherosclerotic burden, but it remains a relatively crude measure compared with coronary computed tomographic angiography, which can directly visualize coronary plaque, characterize its composition, and assess luminal stenosis. CAC is also limited in its ability to assess atherosclerosis throughout the coronary tree, as it captures only calcified plaque within the coronary arteries, and provides little insight into noncalcified or obstructive disease. Similarly, echocardiographic markers such as diastolic dysfunction, although associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, are relatively nonspecific and often reflect the cumulative effects of aging, hypertension, and metabolic disease rather than discrete, modifiable pathology. Furthermore, CAC was intentionally excluded from the PREVENT equations because the model was designed to rely solely on universally available clinical variables. In practice, access to advanced imaging modalities may not be equitable across patient populations, and although insurance coverage for CAC is improving, it is not universally covered; however, the test may represent a financially attractive service for health systems when reimbursed.

The findings reported by Fahed et al 2 raise several important questions for future research. First, the Project Baseline Health Study provides deep phenotyping, but generalizability in larger and more diverse populations is needed. Second, longitudinal analyses may evaluate whether PREVENT risk scores combined with imaging improve prediction and impact management beyond either approach alone. These findings should also be replicated using more precise measures of subclinical cardiovascular disease, including coronary computed tomographic angiography for coronary atherosclerosis and more detailed imaging or biomarker assessments for early HF. Finally, implementation research will be vital to understand whether risk prediction influences clinical behavior and outcomes.

The broader question raised by the article may be less about the performance of any single model and more about the evolving relationship among risk prediction, disease detection, and clinical action. As predictive tools improve and imaging technologies proliferate, clinicians must continually ask when additional testing meaningfully changes care and when the information we already have is enough. The next generation of primordial and primary prevention patients already looks different from the last, particularly as technology will continue to advance, access to whole‐body imaging will increase, and social media will persistently influence patient behavior. Yet, despite these advances, the central challenge of prevention remains remarkably consistent: translating risk awareness into sustained action. Whether prompted by a risk score, an imaging finding, or a clinician's counsel, the practical task is the same: to help individuals prioritize behaviors and treatments that meaningfully reduce risk. Fifty years after the first Framingham Risk Profile, the long‐term success of cardiovascular prevention still depends not only on better prediction or better imaging but on our ability to cultivate environments, cultures, and health systems that support healthy behaviors and risk reduction across the life span.
