Similar Outcomes for Fixation of Undisplaced Intracapsular Hip Fractures with Either Cannulated Screws or Dynamic Hip Screws in 255 Patients: A Comparison of Propensity-Matched Groups
Authors: Benjamin Shardlow, Weronika Nocun, Abdul Khater, Jessica Nightingale, Catherine Gordon, Alan Norrish
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeInternal fixation of undisplaced intracapsular hip fractures is typically achieved using either cannulated screws (CS) or a 2-hole dynamic hip screw (DHS). However, there is a lack of consensus on which of these is more effective clinically. Whilst several biomechanical analyses of cadaveric hips show a higher construct stability of DHS fixation, there is a paucity of large clinical studies investigating patient outcomes.Materials and MethodsData from 2,705 patients at a single institution, including 322 internal fixations, were analysed retrospectively. Propensity scores were calculated to mitigate for the impact of covariates such as age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index and Nottingham Hip Fracture Score, producing an eligible group of 255 patients.ResultsThe CS group included 204 patients (mean age, 82.5±7.5 years; female, 90.7%), the DHS group included 51 patients (mean age, 82.4±8.0 years; female, 90.2%). There were no differences between groups after propensity matching. There were no significant differences in outcomes between CS and DHS groups for reoperation rate (CS 5.9% vs. DHS 5.9%, P>0.999), death <30 days (CS 5.9% vs. DHS 5.9%, P>0.999), length of stay (CS 11.5 days vs. DHS 14.0 days, P=0.294) and hours to surgery (CS 31:03 hours vs. DHS 29:23 hours, P=0.618). However, operation time was significantly shorter for CS (CS 39.0 minutes vs. DHS 44.0 minutes, P=0.013), an 11% reduction.ConclusionThere is no difference in clinical outcomes between CS and DHS fixation of intracapsular hip fractures. However, in this cohort the operating time was approximately 5 minutes shorter in CS fixation.Keywords: Hip, Surgery, Femoral neck fractures

INTRODUCTION

Hip fracture is the most common reason for an older person to be admitted to hospital for emergency surgery in England and Wales1). In the UK, the annual incidence of hip fractures is 65,000 with an approximate cumulative cost of £1 billion. Given the ageing population, this figure is expected to rise2). Most hip fractures occur in older patients with multiple comorbidities—a group that is highly vulnerable to complications, such as infection, delirium, and iatrogenic disease3). Hence, swift surgical management is paramount to improving patient outcomes2). Any developments in surgical protocols, even relatively small, could have a real impact on a significant number of patients.

The Garden classification is the most common method to categorize intracapsular hip fractures4). Fractures are categorized into four groups according to the extent of displacement: types I and II remain undisplaced, whereas types III and IV exhibit displacement4). The NICE (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence) recommends that hemi- or total hip arthroplasty should be offered to those with displaced fractures. However, no specific guidance is offered for the management of undisplaced hip fractures. Therefore, surgical management of undisplaced hip fractures is at the surgeon’s discretion2). One method commonly used is internal fixation. This is associated with the benefits of reduced duration of surgery, decreased blood loss, and a low rate of perioperative complications when compared to other surgical treatments of hip fractures, such as hemiarthroplasty and total hip replacement5). The surgical method of internal fixation is associated with valuable benefits for a vulnerable population5).

Multiple cannulated screws (CS) or a 2-hole dynamic hip screw (DHS) are among the most commonly used methods for internal fixation. Biomechanical analyses of cadaveric hips have indicated that the DHS provides a greater construct stability compared to that offered by CS fixation6). However, the clinical significance of this increased stability remains uncertain since a large randomized controlled trial (RCT) found no difference in the rates of reoperative surgery between DHS and CS fixation7). In contrast, CS fixation is considered a less invasive option associated with reduced surgical blood loss, short hospital stays8-11), and low incidence of avascular necrosis (AVN)12).

The aim of this retrospective study is to compare matched groups of patients with undisplaced intracapsular fractures treated with either CS or DHS fixation in a large cohort at a tertiary orthopedic center. The primary outcomes analyzed were the rate of reoperative surgery and the rate of infection. Secondary outcomes included assessing 30-day mortality, the length of hospital stay, the hours to surgery, and the duration of surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study Design and Setting

Our study was a retrospective cohort analysis of data gathered from patients who presented themselves at Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust, a tertiary orthopedic major trauma center, over a 6-year period between 2017 and 2023.

2. Participants

1) Inclusion criteria

This study included patients aged 65 years and older, diagnosed with an undisplaced intracapsular hip fracture treated with either CS or DHS internal fixation, and assessed with a propensity score less than 1.5 standard deviations (SD) from the mean, with a minimum follow-up of 284 days.

2) Exclusion criteria

We excluded patients with other concomitant injuries, high energy trauma (any fall higher than a fall from standing height), and displaced fractures requiring elimination from the study.

3) Treatment pathways

All the participants were treated according to a standardized care pathway for hip fractures on the basis of achieving the best practice tariff (BPT) of England and with the aim to receive surgery within 36 hours of admission. There were no differences in the rehabilitation program between the two groups. They were fully mobilized for weight-bearing according to a standardized rehabilitation program.

All the subjects received either general or regional anaesthesia, followed by a 28-day course of enoxaparin, a low molecular-weight heparin. In the absence of wound complications, the medication was resumed three days following surgery for those already taking a direct oral anticoagulant.

3. Data Collection

1) Data sources

The data used had been collected from hospital medical records and recorded in an institutional database.

4. Data Analysis

1) Propensity matching

A propensity score was calculated using a multivariate logistic regression analysis (including age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index [CCI], and Nottingham Hip Fracture Score). Mean and SD were then calculated for the CS and the DHS groups. Propensity score restriction was used to ensure populations were closely matched. Subjects, from both the DHS and the CS groups, with a score greater than 1.5 SD from the mean propensity score of the DHS group were removed to eliminate the confounding effect of potential covariates. The matched groups were then tested for multiple variables to determine any potentially confounding differences between the groups.

2) Comparative analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (ver. 29.0; IBM Corp.). For each variable, the normal distribution was evaluated using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test since the sample size exceeded 50. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to analyze non-parametric scale variables, and the chi-square test was used to analyze categorical data. Fisher’s exact test was employed specifically, when the expected number of observations in each category was less than 5 and the assumptions for chi-square test were not met. Furthermore, for contingency tables larger than 2×2, we employed a Monte Carlo simulation. The significance level was set at P<0.05.

5. Ethical Considerations

This retrospective study examined patient outcomes from a standard treatment protocol alone. Ethical approval was not required since there was no randomization or alteration to the clinical care in the study. The Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust’s audit approval number is 23-655C.

RESULTS

1. Data

We collected data from 2,705 patients with intracapsular hip fractures, who were treated at Nottingham University Hospitals NHS Trust with a minimum follow-up of 284 days. Internal fixation was performed in 322 surgeries. Hemiarthroplasty, total hip replacement, or non-surgical treatment accounted for the remaining subjects. Following the application of propensity score restriction, an eligible cohort of 255 patients remained.

The resulting population of 255 patients included 204 patients who underwent CS fixation and 51 patients who underwent fixation with a 2-hole DHS.

2. Demographics and Clinical Comparison

The CS and the DHS population groups were well matched following propensity score restriction because a demographic comparison showed no significant difference for any of the variables tested (Table 2). The median follow-up for the CS group was 1,386 (interquartile range [IQR] 873-1,899) days and the median follow-up for the DHS group was 1,268 (IQR 615-1,922) days. The median follow-up for all the patients in the study was 1,347 (IQR 816-1,878) days.

7. Secondary Outcomes

There were no significant differences in the 30-day mortality rate (CS 5.9% vs. DHS 5.9%, P>0.999), the median length of stay (CS 11.5 [IQR 6.6-16.4] days vs. DHS 14.0 [IQR 9.0-19.0] days, P=0.294), and the median hours to surgery (CS 31:03 [IQR 18:27-43:39] hours vs. DHS 29:23 [IQR 16:57-41:30] hours, P=0.618). However, the median duration of surgery was significantly shorter for CS (CS 39.0 [IQR 31.4-46.7] minutes vs. DHS 44.0 [IQR 30.9-57.2] minutes, P=0.013), with a reduction of 5 minutes (Table 6).

DISCUSSION

Our study investigated the clinical outcomes of internal fixation of intracapsular hip fractures by CS and DHS. There are limited studies that have compared the outcomes between these methods with inconclusive results7,10,12,17). In the UK there are no defined guidelines to select the appropriate method to treat undisplaced hip fractures. Therefore, it is left to the surgeon to make the informed decision. In our study, 80% of the included patients underwent fixation with CS. This indicates that CS fixation is currently a popular choice in the large tertiary orthopedic center that was sampled. However, further research is required to provide evidence-based guidance on which method is most likely to produce favorable clinical outcomes.

In our study, propensity score restriction was calculated to match the population of individuals in the CS group with that of the DHS group and to mitigate the impact of multiple potentially important confounding variables. This was confirmed by demographic analysis of the two groups where there was no significant difference between any of the included categories. To the best of our knowledge, no other retrospective studies comparing CS and DHS fixation have used propensity matching in any form. Hence, it seems reasonable to assume that the results of this comparative study are highly representative of the outcomes that were tested.

1. Primary Outcomes

This study obtained equivalent reoperative surgery rates of 5.9% in both the CS and the DHS groups. Although it is difficult to make meaningful comparisons between the groups due to the low incidence of reoperative surgery rates associated with the sample sizes (see limitations below), the findings are consistent with published research. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported no significant difference in the reoperative surgery rates between CS and DHS fixation12). However, the reoperative surgery rates in one systematic review were noticeably higher at 15.15% for DHS and 15.25% for CS12). This discrepancy is probably due to the inclusion of both displaced and undisplaced hip fractures since displaced intracapsular hip fractures are associated with a high incidence of AVN18). The results of our study also align with those of a large RCT that did not observe any significant difference in the reoperative surgery rates between patients treated with DHS and those treated with CS7). In the RCT by Nauth et al.7), the reoperative surgery rates within 24 months were reported as 20% for DHS and 22% for CS—again, probably due to the inclusion of both displaced and undisplaced fractures. Our study’s results indicate that a low reoperative surgery rate could be specifically attributed to undisplaced fractures. However, further studies are required to validate this level of reoperative surgery rate in a large population of patients with undisplaced fractures.

In our study, only 2.4% of patients had a Pauwel’s III fracture compared to 15% in the RCT by Nauth et al.7). An increased Pauwel’s angle has been associated with a high risk of non-union19,20). The low proportion of biomechanically unstable fractures in this study may explain the reduced reoperative surgery rates. These findings suggest that the fixation of undisplaced fractures, particularly those with low Pauwel’s angles, may be associated with better outcomes. Large cohort studies are needed to confirm this observation.

2. Secondary Outcomes

The lack of significant difference in the 30-day mortality rate—5.9%—in both the CS and the DHS fixation groups suggests that the choice of fixation method does not impact early postsurgical survival. These findings align with those of previous studies that also report no difference in the mortality between CS and DHS fixation of intracapsular hip fractures7,21). The cohorts in these studies were similar to those of the studies mentioned in the section on primary outcomes because they include a mixture of displaced and undisplaced fractures. The comparable mortality rates between CS and DHS fixation suggest that both the techniques are equally viable options in terms of short-term survival.

The median length of hospital stay was 11.5 (IQR 6.6-16.4) days and 14.0 (IQR 9.0-19.0) days for CS and DHS, respectively. This is consistent with Widhalm et al.22) who found that the mean length of hospital stay was 13.2 days with CS treatment and 14.2 days with the DHS fixation procedure. Widhalm et al.22) went on to speculate that the 1-day reduction in hospital admission would result in savings of €856 per patient—a significant finding when scaled up to an entire population. The 2.5-day reduction in hospital stay favoring CS fixation could provide substantial cost benefits. However, we were unable to draw any specific conclusions since the result was not statistically significant. Factors, such as length of hospital stay and associated costs should be considered in guiding future treatment recommendations since both CS and DHS fixation options yield similar clinical outcomes.

The median time to surgery was 31:03 (IQR 18:27-43:39) hours in the CS group and 29:23 (IQR 16:57-41:30) hours in the DHS group, which indicated that both the groups met the criteria for the BPT of less than 36 hours23). A significant improvement in survival at one year is associated with the achievement of BPT24) and provides an incentive of £1,335 for each case that achieves the tariff.

It was observed that the time for CS fixation was faster (39.0 [IQR 31.4-46.7] minutes) than that for DHS fixation (44.0 [IQR 30.9-57.2] minutes), a statistically significant difference and a key positive finding related to median surgery time. This is consistent with Widhalm et al.22) who found that the mean duration of surgery was 40.77 for CS and 53.35 minutes for DHS. Studies have shown that a long duration of surgery is associated with increased intraoperative blood loss25) along with increased rates of complications and readmission26). Therefore, the 5-minute reduction in the duration of surgery seen with CS fixation could represent a clinically important difference in the vulnerable group of patients most commonly presenting with hip fractures.

Finally, there has not been a formal cost-benefit analysis of CS compared to that of DHS fixation with respect to the average NHS operating theatre costs of approximately £1,200 an hour, including staff27). The 5-minute reduction seen in this study equates to £100 savings in favour of CS fixation. Over the course of a 5- to 7-operation theatre list, a reduction of 5 minutes could result in £700 savings or potentially allow for another short-duration surgery case to be performed in the theatre.

3. Limitations

1) Retrospective nature

The retrospective nature of the study could result in an information bias due to the study relying on existing records. This increases the risk of misclassification of exposure or outcomes. Although there was an increased risk of confounding variables, the risk was mitigated by propensity matching.

2) Generalizability

As this study was performed on retrospective data from a single center it probably cannot be generalized to other populations or settings.

3) Size of the DHS group

Another limitation of this study was that there were fewer subjects treated with DHS fixations than with CS fixations, which may affect the generalizability and the statistical power of our findings. This is especially true with respect to reoperative surgery rates. The small sample size in the DHS group limits the ability to draw definite conclusions about specific types of reoperative surgeries within this cohort. However, this risk is partially mitigated by the fact that both the groups were relatively large compared to the cohorts in other studies, and both the groups were well matched because of propensity matching.

4) Preoperative bone mineral density

A potential limitation of our study is the lack of a preoperative assessment of bone mineral density (BMD) as baseline bone strength could influence the risk of fixation failure. However, subjects who sustain a hip fracture following a low-energy event, such as a fall from standing height, are generally presumed to have compromised BMD. Propensity score matching was employed to balance the groups for numerous relevant covariates and helped to mitigate the limitations of our study.

CONCLUSION

This study aligns with the observations of the existing literature by demonstrating no significant difference in clinical outcomes between the CS- and the DHS-fixation of undisplaced intracapsular hip fractures.

It would be useful to perform a comprehensive cost-benefit analysis of CS and DHS fixation to provide clear insights into the financial implications of each method. Additionally, a long-term follow-up of patients will offer a deeper understanding of other subjective measures, such as patient satisfaction and functional outcomes, which will help to inform future clinical decisions.
