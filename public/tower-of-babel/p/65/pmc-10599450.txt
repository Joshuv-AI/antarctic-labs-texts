Extra-axial cavernous malformations of the foramen magnum: illustrative cases
Authors: Bo-Han Yao, Da Li, Liang Wang, Zhen Wu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT

Cavernous malformations (CMs), also known as cavernous hemangiomas, affect 0.4% to 0.9% of the population, constituting 10% to 20% of all vascular malformations in the central nervous system.1 CMs are benign vascular malformations that can be divided into intra-axial and extra-axial types. Extra-axial CMs are relatively rare and are mostly located in the sellar, parasellar, and pineal regions,2,3 especially in the cavernous sinus.4 Other locations, such as the foramen magnum, are very unusual, with fewer than 10 cases reported previously. Thus, there are challenges in diagnosis and treatment. The purpose of this report is to clarify 2 cases of CMs in the foramen magnum and to review the clinical features and surgical strategy for these rare diseases based on the literature.

Illustrative Cases

Case 1

A 59-year-old woman presented with a 4-month history of intermittent headaches. Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a mass on the left side of the foramen magnum. The lesion was hypointense on T1-weighted imaging (T1WI) and homogeneously hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging (T2WI). After the administration of the contrast medium, the mass enhanced homogeneously (Fig. 1). The left vertebral artery was encased by the lesion (white arrow). A left far lateral craniotomy was performed. During the operation, the lesion was purple-red in appearance and extremely rich in blood supply. It closely adhered to the left vertebral artery. After carefully separating the surrounding tissue, the tumor was completely removed, and the vertebral artery was spared. Postoperative MRI revealed no residual lesion. Pathological examination revealed CMs. The patient recovered without neurological deficits, and the headaches were relieved. Follow-up MRI performed 12 months later showed no evidence of recurrence.

Case 2

A 43-year-old male presented with intermittent neck pain for the past 10 years and dysdipsia for the past year. MRI examination showed a lobular mass attached to the right intervertebral foramen area of the atlas at the craniocervical junction (Fig. 2A–D), and the right vertebral artery was encased by the lesion (white arrow). Surgical removal of the lesion was performed via a far lateral approach. The lesion was intraoperatively identified as having a tough consistency and rich blood supply. The ipsilateral vertebral artery and C1 nerve root were completely involved in the lesion. Because it was so difficult to dissect the lesion from the surrounding neurovascular structures, the mass was partially resected. The postoperative clinical course was uneventful. Histopathological examination revealed CMs and no deposition of hemosiderin in peripheral brain tissues. The patient underwent conventional radiosurgery 6 months after the surgery. The neck pain and dysdipsia were alleviated, and the patient was in good condition without any neuropathy at the 1-year follow-up.

Literature Review

We performed a literature review of pathologically confirmed cases of extra-axial CMs of the foramen magnum and the craniocervical junction region based on the PubMed database, searching articles from January 1960 to December 2022 using a combination of the following terms: “cavernoma OR cavernous angioma OR cavernous hemangioma OR cavernous malformations” AND “foramen magnum OR craniocervical junction region.” Only studies in the English language were considered. A total of 4 cases in the foramen magnum were found. Of the 6 cases (including ours), 4 (66.7%) were male and 2 (33.3%) were female (male:female ratio of 2:1). All patients had symptoms of headache, and in 1 patient was accompanied by bilateral abducens nerve palsy. Three of 6 (50%) patients showed hyperdensities on computed tomography (CT) scan suggestive of subacute subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), and our patients had no such imaging characteristics. Oishi et al.5 reported a case of calcified extra-axial CM, which is rare in the foramen magnum. Most of the cases presented a hypointense signal surrounding the rim on T2WI or T2 gradient imaging. The signal was variable but predominantly hyperintense on T1WI. All lesions had contrast heterogeneous enhancement. All lesions were approached through a suboccipital craniectomy, and gross-total resection was attained in 5 patients (83.3%). Macroscopically, lesions appeared as red-purple-gray-bluish and were adherent to the brainstem. After the operation, except for 1 patient with bilateral abducens nerve palsy and mild right-sided paresis as reported by Oishi et al.5, the other 5 patients had no obvious cranial nerve dysfunction. Six patients favorably recovered postoperatively with an improvement in their neurological symptoms/signs; 5 patients were stable without any neurological deficits; 1 patient showed gradual improvement in his state of consciousness as well as his bilateral abducens nerve palsy and tetraparesis; 1 patient underwent postoperative adjuvant conventional radiosurgery, and the residual lesion was smaller than before. No recurrence was found during a 1-year follow-up.

Discussion

Observations

The natural history of cerebral CMs is relatively benign, and 10% to 44% of patients are asymptomatic.6 The occurrence of extra-axial CMs is much lower than that of intra-axial malformations.7 After a thorough search of the literature on PubMed, only 4 cases of operated intradural extra-axial foramen magnum CMs were found.5,8–10 Most of the patients presented with a sudden headache as an initial symptom, which was caused by the SAH. The lesions were completely removed, and their outcomes were favorable.

The pathogenesis of CMs is still unclear. Some studies have shown that hormones may play a certain role in the occurrence and development of CMs, and the incidence in middle-aged women is significantly higher than in other age groups. Neurological symptoms related to the lesion can worsen during pregnancy or after hormone treatment.11 Additionally, the clinical, radiographic, and pathological characteristics of the disease seem to differ between the extra-axial and intra-axial lesions. Electron microscopic and immunohistochemical analyses have documented disruption of the endothelial barrier by disassembling interbrain endothelial junctions in cerebral CMs.12,13 This loss of cell–cell junctions could trigger the recurring microhemorrhages that cause hemosiderin deposition and the propensity for seizures.1 Zhou et al.14 found that the vascular wall of the intra-axial lesion is thinner and the extracellular matrix is less. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the staining intensity of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) in the intra-axial CMs was higher than that in the extra-axial group,14 which may be related to the clinical characteristics of intra-axial lesions with frequent hemorrhage and less bleeding during operation, whereas the extra-axial lesions tend to be accompanied by uncontrollable massive bleeding during surgery.

Neuroimaging plays a crucial role in the diagnosis and monitoring of CMs. On CT, the typical imaging characteristics of cerebral CMs usually appear as focal areas of increased density in the brain, often without mass effect.15 MRI of extra-axial CMs generally shows hypointense signal on T1WI and hyperintense signal on T2WI with homogeneous enhancement. Most notably, extra-axial CMs can be misdiagnosed as meningiomas, especially when meningiomas have no obvious dural tail sign. The lesions can be preoperatively misdiagnosed as neoplasms or meningiomas and have been previously reported.16,17 This failure of radiological and clinical distinction can lead to unexpected findings in the operating room and can alter treatment plans. As in our cases, the preoperative diagnosis is meningioma, and CMs are finally diagnosed by intraoperative and postoperative pathology. In our opinion, the occurrence of CMs should always be suspected in the presence of extra-axial lesions in the foramen magnum, especially when a neuroradiological scan suggests a meningioma, and a pathological examination is necessary for final diagnosis of the lesion.

Current treatment options for CMs include observation, microsurgical resection, and radiosurgery. Asymptomatic lesions, regardless of their location, should be treated conservatively and with MRI follow-up. Resection is the standard treatment for symptomatic lesions at nonfunctional sites.18 Intraoperative hemorrhage is the main problem in the cavernous sinus area and other extra-axial sites, which is similar to our experience. For patients with gross-total resection, no postoperative adjuvant treatment is required. In cases following subtotal resection, radiotherapy can be used to reduce recurrence rates.19 Radiosurgery for extra-axial CMs is indicated for residual tumor after an operation or biopsy because radical surgery is highly risky due to intraoperative bleeding. We recommend radiosurgery for small CMs after biopsy or neuroimaging diagnosis. Larger tumors can be treated by conventional radiation with minimal risk or staged radiosurgery. Brain damage due to local irradiation can be avoided with stereotactic radiosurgery.20

Lessons

This article summarizes our first experience of extra-axial CMs in the foramen magnum with favorable surgical outcomes. Furthermore, extra-axial CMs should be considered in the differential diagnosis if encountering radiologically similar lesions in the foramen magnum.
