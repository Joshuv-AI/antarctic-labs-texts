Evaluating community pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward nutritional counselling and healthy lifestyles: evidence from a lower-middle-income country
Authors: Lana Hanaysheh, Sondos Rajab, Tala Masri, Dania Abuhalima, Samah W Al-Jabi, Muna H Shakhshir, Sa’ed H Zyoud
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCommunity pharmacists are among the most accessible healthcare professionals and are primarily responsible for medication management and patient counselling. Their role increasingly includes providing basic education on nutritional and healthy lifestyle practices and referring patients to dietitians when specialized nutritional care is needed. This study aimed to evaluate community pharmacists’ knowledge and practices related to nutritional counselling and healthy lifestyles and to identify the barriers that restrict pharmacists’ ability to provide nutritional counselling. A cross-sectional study was conducted that included 337 community pharmacists via a structured, self-administered questionnaire distributed through direct visits to community pharmacies and social media platforms. The data were recorded and analysed via SPSS version 21, with significance set at p < 0.05. Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarize and explore the data. The Mann–Whitney U test and Kruskal–Wallis test were used to assess the associations between variables. Of the 337 pharmacists who participated, the majority were female (80.1%) and were in the younger age group (18–29 years). Pharmacists demonstrated relatively strong knowledge in several medication- and nutrition-related areas, with 92.0% recognizing the importance of balanced diets and 95.0% identifying the need to administer certain medications with food. However, only 7.1% correctly acknowledged that dietary supplements are not always safe. Attitudes toward counselling were generally positive, with 69.8% agreeing that counselling improves patient outcomes. Despite this, practices were less consistent; only 24.6% routinely addressed physical activity, and 10.4% provided written educational materials. A lack of time (84.6%) and patients’ misconceptions about supplemental safety (68.2%) were the most reported barriers. Knowledge scores were significantly associated with recent graduation (p = 0.023) and supervising trainees (p = 0.037), whereas practice scores differed by employment status, with responsible pharmacists showing higher levels of engagement (p = 0.003). Community pharmacists in Palestine appeared to have relatively good knowledge and generally favourable attitudes toward nutritional counselling; however, reported practices were less optimal. A major knowledge gap was identified regarding dietary supplement safety, as only 7.1% of the pharmacists recognized that dietary supplements are not always safe. Structural and perceived barriers, particularly time constraints and patient misconceptions, may have influenced the provision of counselling services. Introduction

A healthy lifestyle involves a pattern of behaviors that improve people’s health, including healthy nutrition, weight control, physical activity, stress management and the avoidance of harmful substances such as smoking and alcohol1–3. These factors play a vital role in the management and prevention of noncommunicable diseases (NCDs), such as cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, diabetes and cancer. With the increasing prevalence of these diseases, which represent major causes of death worldwide according to the World Health Organization (WHO)4, access to reliable nutritional information and healthy lifestyle guidance at the community level is important.

Community pharmacists have a broad geographic distribution and are usually the initial points for contact with patients who need medical assistance without an appointment5,6. This has increased pharmacists’ involvement in providing basic health education related to nutrition and lifestyle, in addition to their primary responsibilities in medication management7–9. However, the ability of pharmacists to provide information depends mainly on their degree of knowledge, expertise and practical skills in nutritional and healthy lifestyle counselling10–12.

Dietary supplements and multivitamins have become very common worldwide in recent years. People usually take supplements to compensate for their deficiency, improve their general health and increase their immunity13–15. However, misuse or overuse of supplements and vitamins may cause potential health problems16,17. Pharmacists can support safe supplement use by educating patients about the appropriate selection, dosage and use of supplements and vitamins18,19.

Community pharmacists may support chronic disease prevention efforts through medication-related counselling and patient education20–24. Conventional narrative review findings revealed that pharmacist interventions are associated with improved immune uptake, enhanced chronic disease outcomes, including diabetes and hypertension, and reduced inappropriate antibiotic use25.

Research conducted in different countries has demonstrated variations in pharmacists’ knowledge, confidence, and practices in regard to providing nutritional advice11,26,27. Previous studies have highlighted the barriers that may affect the ability of pharmacists to provide nutritional guidance, including time constraints, insufficient training, limited confidence and a lack of resources or materials27,28. One study recommended enhancing pharmacists’ counselling by modifying pharmacy education, ongoing professional training and participation in national preventative healthcare programs26.

In Palestine, research on this topic remains limited, highlighting the need for an in-depth study to assess pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding nutritional and lifestyle counselling. This assessment helps identify existing gaps and provides a foundation for developing future training programs, updating curricula, and promoting the integration of medication-related counselling into community pharmacy practices in Palestinian society.

The importance of this study lies in offering baseline information about the knowledge and practices of pharmacists regarding nutritional counselling and healthy lifestyle promotion. This information can help in determining the gaps in pharmacists’ education, encouraging the development of professional training programs, and supporting the contribution of pharmacists to public health awareness and safe medication-related practices. This study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding nutritional counselling among community pharmacists in Palestine.

Methods

Study design and population

This cross-sectional survey study was conducted in Palestine and targeted community pharmacists working in community pharmacies. The study included licenced pharmacists currently working in community pharmacies across different regions of Palestine. All participants were licenced pharmacists employed in community pharmacies at the time of data collection. In the Palestinian context, some pharmacists may hold dual or multiple practice roles (e.g., part-time work in hospital pharmacies or outpatient clinics). Participants were instructed to report their primary place of employment, which was considered the main practice setting for this study. Any additional reported settings reflect secondary roles and do not contradict the inclusion criteria. The survey was distributed between January 2026 and March 2026 through professional pharmacist social media groups and through direct visits to community pharmacies. To minimize duplicate responses, the data were screened for potential duplicates, and the survey was designed to allow only one submission per participant using platform controls where applicable.

Sample size and calculation

The minimum sample size was calculated using Raosoft software29 on the basis of an estimated population of 4,000 pharmacists30. With a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, and a 50% response distribution, the required sample size was approximately 320. To increase the robustness of the analysis and account for incomplete responses, the final sample included 337 pharmacists.

Measures and data collection

The questionnaire used in this study was adapted from previous studies assessing the knowledge, attitudes and practices of community pharmacists toward nutrition and lifestyle counselling5,28,31–33. It was created to support clarity, ease of completion, and relevance to community pharmacy practice, and it was intended for self-administration. The survey was distributed between January 2026 and March 2026. Owing to the open distribution method, the total number of pharmacists who received or viewed the invitation could not be determined; therefore, a response rate could not be calculated. The relatively large sample size provides useful baseline evidence; however, regional representation could not be assessed because geographic data were not collected.

The questionnaire consists of five sections. The first section included questions related to the participants’ sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, sex, degree of education, year of graduation, practice experience, and employment status. In this study, a “responsible pharmacist” refers to a licenced pharmacist who is legally and professionally accountable for the operation of the community pharmacy, including medication dispensing, regulatory compliance, and supervision of the pharmacy staff. The second section included statements assessing pharmacists’ knowledge of nutrition and lifestyle counselling; responses were assigned values of 0 (false, do not know) and 1 (true). The knowledge scores ranged from 0 to 19, with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. The third section contains statements concerning the attitudes of pharmacists toward nutrition and lifestyle counselling; responses were scored from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). Attitude scores ranged from 12 to 60, with higher scores indicating more positive attitudes toward nutritional counselling. A positive attitude was defined as the combined proportion of “Agree” and “Strongly agree” responses on a 5-point Likert scale. The fourth section evaluated the practices and behaviors of the pharmacists; the responses were assigned values ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Practice scores ranged from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating greater engagement in counselling-related practices. The last section concerns barriers that may affect the ability of pharmacists to provide counselling and guidance. For the barriers section, participants were allowed to select multiple responses. The questionnaires were screened for completeness before analysis. Responses with substantial missing data in key sections (knowledge, attitudes, or practices) were excluded, while partially completed questionnaires with minimal missing items were retained for analysis.

Reliability and validity

To ensure validity, the questionnaire items were adapted primarily from previous studies5,28,32 with minor wording modifications to fit the Palestinian context. No reverse-coded items were included. The questionnaire was reviewed by a panel of experts, consisting of three faculty members with expertise in pharmacy practice, clinical nutrition, and public health, who evaluated the questionnaire for clarity, relevance, comprehensiveness, and cultural appropriateness. A pilot test was conducted among 20 pharmacists not included in the final sample, leading to minor revisions for clarity and cultural appropriateness. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (knowledge α = 0.74, attitude α = 0.81, practice α = 0.79).

Data and statistical analysis

The data were entered and analysed via the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) version 21. Incomplete responses were assessed during data cleaning, and cases with missing data for primary outcome variables were excluded from the analysis. No imputation was performed; therefore, analyses were conducted using a complete-case (listwise deletion) approach.

Descriptive analysis was conducted to summarize and explore the main features of the data. Frequencies represent categorical variables, whereas continuous variables are summarized using means ± standard deviations or medians [interquartile ranges], as appropriate, according to the data distribution. Associations between sociodemographic characteristics and knowledge, attitudes and practices scores were determined via the Mann–Whitney U test for variables with two independent groups and the Kruskal–Wallis test for variables with more than two independent groups. Variables potentially associated with knowledge, attitudes, and practices scores were further evaluated using multivariable linear regression analyses to identify independent predictors while controlling for potential confounding variables. The regression coefficients (B), standard errors, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values are reported. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University [IRB Reference #: Pharm. Nov. 2025/53]. The research adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki34. An information sheet was provided at the beginning of the online survey to explain the study objectives, voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and the right to withdraw at any time without penalty. Participants were required to indicate their informed consent electronically before they completed the questionnaire. Only those who agreed to participate were able to access the survey questions. No personally identifiable information was collected. All the responses were anonymous and stored in password-protected files accessible only to the research team. Data were used solely for research purposes.

Results

Participant sociodemographic data

A total of 337 community pharmacists were included in this study. The sex distribution revealed a predominance of females (80.1%) compared with males (19.9%). The mean ± SD age of the participants was 28.86 ± 8.12 years, and the median age was 26 years (IQR: 24–30). Nearly half of the participants held a bachelor’s degree (57.6%). Most participants had graduated less than two years ago (32.0%) or 2–5 years ago (32.6%). The majority of participants were responsible for pharmacists (86.1%), and most worked in independent pharmacies (75.7%). Additional sociodemographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. Attitudes toward nutritional and lifestyle counselling

Overall, the pharmacists demonstrated positive attitudes toward nutrition and lifestyle counselling, with attitude scores ranging from 12 to 60, with a median of 45 (IQR: 41–49). Participants’ practices and behavior toward nutritional and lifestyle counselling

The pharmacists’ practices and behavior responses varied; the practice score ranged from 21 to 75, with a median of 46 (IQR: 41–51). The most common behavior was reporting suspected side effects related to dietary supplement use or documenting them (58.5%), followed by recommending dietary supplements (57.3%) and then providing counselling to pregnant or lactating patients (56.6%). However, only 24.6% of the pharmacists provided advice on physical activity as part of a healthy lifestyle, and only 10.4% of the pharmacists provided patients with printed or written materials on nutrition and lifestyle. Barriers affecting the ability of pharmacists to provide nutritional and lifestyle counselling

There are many barriers that may affect the ability of pharmacists to provide nutritional guidance. The greatest barrier that affected the pharmacists in this study was lack of time (n = 285, 84.6%), followed by patients’ belief that dietary supplements are always safe (n = 230, 68.2%), whereas only 70 pharmacists considered counselling not a priority in pharmacy practice. Associations between sociodemographic factors and practices toward nutrition and lifestyle counselling

As shown in Table 8, sex, age, education degree, graduation year, attendance of training courses, years of practice, and supervision of pharmacy students or trainees were not significantly associated with practice scores (p > 0.05). However, practice scores differed significantly according to employment status, with responsible pharmacists reporting higher levels of engagement in nutritional and lifestyle counselling practices than pharmacy owners or partners did (p = 0.003).

Discussion

This study assessed community pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices related to basic nutritional counselling and healthy lifestyles in Palestine. In addition to their primary responsibilities in medication management and patient counselling, pharmacists may provide basic health and lifestyle education and refer patients when specialized care is needed. Therefore, assessing their knowledge, attitudes, and practices is important for identifying gaps and improving supportive health services within community pharmacies.

Although pharmacists demonstrated relatively strong knowledge in several medication-related areas, including drug use, adverse effects, and dietary supplements and their sources, uses and interactions with medications, important gaps remained regarding the safety of dietary supplements. Notably, only 7.1% of the pharmacists recognized that dietary supplements are not always safe and may have side effects. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions. These results are similar to the findings of a study conducted in Jordan, which revealed that pharmacists were less informed about the precise dosages, drug interactions, and side effects of supplements35.

Pharmacists’ attitudes were generally positive. Most pharmacists agreed that nutritional counselling may contribute to promoting healthy lifestyle behaviors and supporting patient care. Additionally, they acknowledged that providing basic information on nutrition, malnutrition risks and potential supplement–drug interactions can be a relevant part of pharmacy practice. They also expressed the need to improve their nutritional knowledge, update their competencies regularly, and receive additional nutritional training. These results are comparable with those of U.S. and Canadian studies that revealed strong agreement among pharmacists regarding the need for more training, more regulation, and high-quality information33. Despite these positive attitudes, actual practices and behaviors were more selective and less consistent. This discrepancy between positive attitudes and less consistent practices may reflect structural and organizational constraints within community pharmacies, including heavy workloads, limited counselling time, a lack of private counselling areas, and insufficient integration of counselling activities into routine workflows. Many pharmacists reported recommending dietary supplements, documenting suspected supplement-related side effects, and providing basic nutritional advice, particularly to pregnant or lactating women. However, fewer engaged in more comprehensive or sustained practices such as dietary assessment, physical activity counselling, the use of structured guidelines, participation in awareness campaigns, or the provision of educational materials. These activities may further support patient education and health awareness. This gap may be related to several barriers. The most common barriers they encountered were lack of time, patients’ belief that dietary supplements are always safe, low patient interest, limited formal training in nutrition or drug- supplement interactions, and low patient disclosure of supplement use. These findings are consistent with those of Semple et al. (2006), who reported similar barriers to pharmacist counselling practices, particularly workload and time constraints36. The weak recognition of supplement-related risks may reflect the widespread public perception that dietary supplements are inherently safe because they are marketed as ‘natural’ products. Limited emphasis on nutritional and supplemental safety during pharmacy training may also contribute to these findings. The results of the present study also revealed a significant association between graduation year and the supervision of students with knowledge scores, where pharmacists who had graduated within the previous two years and those involved in supervising trainees demonstrated higher levels of knowledge. This finding may be explained by the fact that recently graduated pharmacists are more likely to have received updated education and training during their academic programs, which may include more updated nutrition-related content. Previous studies have reported that formal education and professional training play important roles in improving healthcare professionals’ knowledge and maintaining competency over time28,33,37.

The study also revealed that pharmacists’ practices differed significantly according to employment status, with responsible pharmacists reporting greater engagement in counselling activities than assistants or trainees did. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies, where differences in professional roles and responsibilities were associated with variations in counselling practices among community pharmacists5,7. Responsible pharmacists often have greater authority in clinical decision-making and patient communication, which may facilitate more active involvement in counselling services.

Clinical and pharmacy practice implications and suggestions for future research

Community pharmacists in Palestine demonstrate fairly good knowledge of nutritional and lifestyle counselling, as well as positive attitudes toward encouraging healthy behavior. However, knowledge translation into practice is not consistently observed, despite frequent patient interactions and the high accessibility of community pharmacies. This might be attributed to structural and professional challenges. Expanding the role of pharmacists might contribute to safer medication use and improved patient awareness of nutritional and lifestyle-related issues.

Practically, the results indicate that pharmacists in Palestine might benefit from improved continuing professional development in nutrition, food‒drug interactions, and lifestyle counselling. More emphasis may be placed on developing hands-on counselling skills. Other pharmacy-level interventions include the availability of private counselling rooms, access to reliable patient education resources, and improved collaboration between pharmacists, physicians and dietitians.

Future studies might investigate the effectiveness of training programs, electronic decision support, or improved practice processes in improving counselling practices. Qualitative studies, along with the incorporation of patient perspectives and objective outcome measures, would help further elucidate the results.

Strengths and limitations

The present study aims to fill an important but understudied area in the Palestinian context: the role of community pharmacists and their level of knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding nutritional counselling and the promotion of healthy lifestyles. The use of a structured questionnaire based on previously published literature helped the study achieve some level of standardization in the collection of the required data and allowed the study to investigate some specific aspects of the pharmacists’ level of knowledge. In addition, the relatively large sample size provides useful baseline evidence on pharmacists’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices in the Palestinian community pharmacy context. However, regional representation could not be assessed because geographic data were not collected. This research has several limitations. The questionnaires used in this study were self-administered, which may introduce response bias, particularly when reporting practices and attitudes that may be impacted by social desirability. The cross-sectional nature of this study may limit our capacity to infer causation. Additionally, the use of convenience sampling and recruitment through social media platforms may introduce selection bias, as pharmacists who are more active online or more interested in nutritional counselling may have been more likely to participate. Therefore, the sample may not fully represent all Palestinian community pharmacists, particularly older pharmacists or those less engaged with online professional networks. The sample also included a high proportion of female participants (80.1%) and responsible pharmacists (86.1%), which may not fully reflect the actual national distribution of community pharmacists in Palestine. However, owing to the lack of publicly available national demographic data, representativeness could not be formally assessed. Additionally, regional representation could not be assessed because of the absence of geographic data collection. Therefore, caution is needed when generalizing the findings to all community pharmacists in Palestine.

Conclusions

This study assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding nutritional counselling and healthy lifestyles among community pharmacists in Palestine. Pharmacists have demonstrated strengths in several medication- and nutrition-related areas; however, important gaps have been identified, particularly regarding dietary supplement safety. The provision of nutritional counselling is constrained by structural and professional barriers, including a lack of time, insufficient training, a heavy workload, and limited privacy. These findings highlight the need for targeted educational interventions and system-level improvements to enhance pharmacists’ role in patient education and safe supplement use within their scope of practice.
