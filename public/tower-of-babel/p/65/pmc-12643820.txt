Construction of Quality Evaluation Index System for Surgical Positioning: A Delphi Method
Authors: Yue Guo, Xi Qu, Tiyu Zhao, Ying Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo establish a set of scientific and practical surgical positioning quality evaluation indicators for the operating room in China.DesignA mixed‐methods study with a quantitative component.MethodsLiterature review and expert group discussions were conducted to identify the potential surgical positioning quality evaluation indicators for the operating room. Then, Delphi surveys were used to collect opinions from experts in the field of surgical positioning. The consistency of the consultation results formed the basis on which we conducted the next rounds of consultation. Based on the importance given to each indicator by the consulted experts in the previous round, we determined the weight coefficient of each indicator with the analytic hierarchy process.ResultsThe questionnaire recovery rates of the two rounds of correspondence were 82.61% and 73.91%, and the average authoritative coefficients were 0.94 and 0.92. The Kendall W values ranged from 0.155 to 0.270 (p < 0.001). The weight coefficients for the first‐level, second‐level and third‐level indicators were 0.157–0.576, 0.046–0.292 and 0.014–0.210, respectively. Consensus was reached on 3 primary indicators, 7 secondary indicators and 19 tertiary indicators.No Patient or Public ContributionThis study did not involve patients or the public; data were derived from expert consultations and literature analysis.Keywords: analytic hierarchy process, China, Delphi, quality indicator, surgical positioning

Introduction

The operating room is considered a high‐risk area worldwide, and unsafe surgical practices—particularly those related to patient positioning—have been documented in North America, Europe, Asia and Oceania (Bates et al. 2023; World Health Organization 2021). This global burden prompted the World Health Organization to include ‘correct patient positioning’ as a core element of the 2021 Global Patient Safety Action Plan. Evidence shows that good quality surgical positioning is crucial in surgical procedures (Bentsen et al. 2024). It determines how the patient is placed on the operating table, ensuring proper access to the surgical site and minimising the risk of harm. Surgical positioning varies significantly based on patient anatomy and the type of surgery and each institution tailors its protocols accordingly. As surgical complexity increases, developing valid quality indicators for positioning has become a global priority for patient‐safety initiatives.

Surgical positioning is a complex process that requires careful consideration of various factors, including patient anatomy, the type of surgery being performed, and the potential risks associated with different positions. Supine, prone and lateral positioning are established procedures for ensuring safe patient positioning in the operating room setting, adopted by surgical teams worldwide. Improper positioning can lead to complications such as intraoperative peripheral nerve injury, intraoperative pressure injury and eye injury (Gefen et al. 2020; Ripa et al. 2022; Schwarzman et al. 2020), all of which can significantly affect patient outcomes. Many surgeries require specific, and sometimes extreme, positioning of patients after they have been anaesthetised. It's reported that long‐term steep Trendelenburg position without intraoperative monitoring can increase postoperative vision loss (POVL) (Emery et al. 2015; Goel et al. 2020), which can lead to partial or complete loss of vision, affecting visual acuity and potentially leading to permanent blindness. In a quality improvement project, 22 falls of patients in the OR reported by the Veterans Health Administration from January 2010 to February 2016 were described (Soncrant et al. 2018). Given these significant risks and complications associated with surgical positioning, there is a critical need for a systematic approach to assess and ensure the quality of positioning practices.

High‐quality nursing care in the operating room can significantly reduce the risks associated with surgical positioning and markedly improve outcomes for surgical patients. Over the years, nursing‐sensitive indicators have been developed to quantify the level of care delivered (Kleinpell and Kapu 2017). Current research on surgical positioning primarily focuses on the effects of equipment, refining procedural techniques and sharing nursing insights (Bentsen et al. 2024). To minimise risks associated with positioning, perioperative staff are strongly encouraged to proactively identify and address hazards related to positioning activities, such as the use of improper or malfunctioning positioning devices (Sørensen et al. 2016). Advances in technology, such as adjustable beds and secure harnessing systems, have improved the ability to achieve and maintain stable patient positions, thereby reducing the risk of complications related to instability (Soncrant et al. 2018; Suozzi et al. 2013). Tools such as the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist and AORN positioning guidelines address positioning only tangentially. None offers a comprehensive indicator system that is specific to nursing performance during positioning (World Health Organization 2021; Speth 2023). Consequently, perioperative teams must still rely on institutional protocols that are rarely evaluated for reliability or validity. This gap complicates benchmarking, education and continuous quality improvement.

This study aims to establish a quality evaluation index system specifically for nursing care during surgical positioning through the application of the Delphi method. By engaging a panel of expert practitioners, this research seeks to construct a comprehensive set of scientifically valid and contextually relevant nursing quality indicators. This system will provide standardised guidance for nursing practice, thereby enhancing the quality of care in surgical settings.

The Study

Aims and Objectives

Purposive sampling was employed to maximise geographic and professional diversity. Experts who were eligible for participation in the study fulfilled the following criteria: (1) educational background: holding a bachelor's degree or higher; (2) title requirement: possessing a senior professional title or an intermediate professional title with nursing experience in the operating room; (3) professional background and duration of employment: having more than 5 years of experience in operating room nursing; (4) volunteering to participate in the expert consultation and to complete the consultation questionnaire with timely feedback.

Methods

Literature Review

According to the 6S model of evidence‐based resources, a search was conducted for decision support systems, evidence thematic summaries, guidelines, systematic evaluations and original research related to surgical positioning quality indicators, progressing from top to bottom. Databases searched included PubMed, Embase, UpToDate, Cochrane Library, British Medical Journal (BMJ) Best Practice, Guidelines International Network, National Institute for Health and Care Excellence, National Guideline Clearinghouse, Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network and Chinese databases (CNKI, Wanfang) for eligible studies published from inception to December 2023. Keywords or medical terms ‘sensitive indicator,’ ‘quality indicator,’ ‘nursing quality,’ ‘Operating Room Positioning,’ ‘surgical positioning,’ and ‘surgical patient position’ were utilised. All retrieved records were screened by two independent reviewers. After duplicate removal, 148 full texts were assessed; 42 met inclusion criteria describing positioning‐related nursing indicators.

Focus Group Discussions

Findings from the literature were coded inductively into 112 candidate indicators. A multidisciplinary focus group (one operating room manager, four senior nurses, one anaesthesiologist and one surgeon) met twice. Using thematic content analysis, the list was consolidated by merging duplicates and excluding low‐frequency items, yielding four primary dimensions, ten secondary indicators and twenty‐nine tertiary indicators that formed the initial Delphi questionnaire.

Delphi Method

Two rounds of expert consultation were conducted from January 2024 to May 2024. The consultation questionnaires were sent out via email or WeChat, with a request for responses within 2 weeks. Following each round, the researcher entered and organised the results of the expert consultation questionnaire. The research team then deliberated and refined the entries of the evaluation index system, integrating the findings into the subsequent round of consultation.

The questionnaire is divided into four sections: (1) Instructions for completing the questionnaire, including the study's significance, purpose and guidance on filling it out; (2) Basic information: age, highest education level, position title and years of experience of the experts; (3) Body of the questionnaire with three‐level evaluation indicators for the quality of surgical positioning placement, and sections for adding indicators and providing comments for revision. Experts assessed using a Likert 5‐point scale (ranging from completely unimportant = 1 point to very important = 5 points); (4) Experts' familiarity with the content and evaluation criteria. The levels of familiarity are divided into five categories: very familiar, familiar, neutral, unfamiliar and completely unfamiliar. Evaluation bases included clinical experience, theoretical analysis, domestic and foreign references and intuitive feeling. Experts' reliance on each evaluation base was categorised as strong, medium or weak. Through expert consultations, we seek to gather valuable insights and recommendations from professionals in the field. This feedback will allow us to further refine the indicator system, ensuring its practicality and effectiveness for successful implementation.

Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP)

After the second round, the indicators were finalised. Analytic hierarchy process (AHP) was then implemented to determine the weight of each indicator. The AHP method was chosen for this study because it helps identify the relative importance of the three dimensions of surgical positioning quality and their second‐level indicators based on expert opinions (Byun et al. 2021; Shen et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2021). Each indicator was compared to others at the same level, with experts rating the importance of each indicator using the Saaty 1–9 scale, where 1 indicates equal importance, 3 signifies moderate importance, 5 indicates strong importance, 7 represents very strong importance and 9 denotes absolute importance. Values of 2, 4, 6 or 8 are used for intermediate judgements. For example, if the importance of safety over effectiveness was rated as moderate, the entry in the comparison matrix for (safety, effectiveness) would be 3, while the entry for (effectiveness, safety) would be 1/3. The comparison matrix was completed according to these ratings of relative importance (Jiang et al. 2019). Consistency was assessed with the consistency ratio (CR). If CR < 0.1, the judgement matrix is considered to have satisfactory consistency.

Data Analysis

All data were managed and analysed using SPSS 26.0 software. The authority coefficient (Cr), determined by the mean of the degree of familiarity and the basis of judgement, was employed to assess the experts' authority. Descriptive statistics such as mean, standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (CV) were utilised to summarise the data. The consistency of experts' responses was assessed using the Kendall coefficient of concordance (W). Criteria for retention included indicators with a mean importance rating > 4.0 and a coefficient of variation ≤ 0.25. These thresholds were pre‐specified in the study protocol before any data collection. For the opinions proposed by experts, the research team discussed and decided whether to adopt them or seek further consultation in the next round.

Mesh AHP software V1.82 was employed for the AHP. The weight of each indicator was calculated through AHP, with a higher weight indicating greater importance. Additionally, the consistency ratio (CR) was calculated, where higher CRs indicate better consistency.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of REDACTED (Approval NO. TJ‐IRB20240525). In our study, written informed consent was obtained electronically: before accessing the Delphi survey, experts ticked a consent box in the online questionnaire. Participants were clearly informed of their right to withdraw at any time without penalty. This process ensured compliance with ethical standards while accommodating the electronic format of expert consultation.

Results

Characteristics and Authoritative Coefficient of Experts

Two rounds of electronic consultations were conducted using the e‐Delphi method involving 23 experts, with an average age of 50.87 years (±6.76) and an average tenure of 30.70 years (±8.09) in their respective fields. In terms of educational background, 13 held bachelor's degrees and 10 held master's degrees. The expert panel included 2 charge nurses, 7 deputy chief nurses and 14 chief nurses. All experts were engaged in operating room nursing or operating room nursing management (Table 1). The positive coefficient of Delphi experts reflects the effective response rate of the questionnaire, which was 82.61% in the first round and 73.91% in the second round. The authority of the experts, as denoted by the authority coefficient (Cr), is derived from the arithmetic mean of the judgement coefficient (Ca) and familiarity coefficient (Cs) (Cr = (Cs + Ca)/2). Notably, the authority coefficient for this study exceeds 0.9 (Table 2).

Indicator Modifications

In the first round, a total of 49 expert suggestions were put forward. For the first‐level indicators, no changes were recommended. For the second‐level indicator ‘degree of vital‐sign fluctuations,’ multiple experts argued that the construct overlapped with routine intra‐operative monitoring standards and its isolated measurement added little incremental value to the safety domain; therefore, it was deleted. At the third level, seven indicators were removed because they were judged either redundant with broader safety metrics or difficult to measure reliably across institutions. Lastly, after the first round of expert consultation, based on statistical analysis, the mean values for the importance and feasibility of one tertiary indicator, ‘surgical patient comfort’ were both < 4.5 with a variability > 0.25; thus, this indicator was excluded. Ultimately, the questionnaire for the second round of expert correspondence included 3 first‐level indicators, 8 second‐level indicators and 19 third‐level indicators.

In the second round of expert consultation, a total of eight suggestions were proposed. There were no recommendations to modify the first‐level indicators. Experts proposed consolidating the second‐level indicator ‘rate of surgical position‐related complications’ and its corresponding third‐level indicators into ‘rate of adverse events during positioning and restoration for surgery’. For the third‐level indicators, experts suggested revising four indicators. After evaluating the importance scores in the second round of expert consultation, which ranged from 4.29 to 5.00 with coefficient of variation (CV) values ranging from 0.00 to 0.22 and the feasibility scores ranging from 4.29 to 5.00 with CV values from 0.00 to 0.20, it was determined that no indicators needed deletion based on statistical analysis. The final quality‐evaluation index therefore comprises three primary, seven secondary and nineteen tertiary indicators, detailed in Tables 4 and 5. The operational definitions were finalised by the consensus of the Delphi panel and subsequently cross‐checked against current literature to confirm content validity.

Relative Importance of Quality Indicators

The relative importance weights of each indicator are presented in Table 6. The CR values for each level indicator consistency test are all < 0.1. The primary indicators were weighted in descending order as follows: safety of surgical positioning (0.5757), effectiveness of surgical positioning (0.2671) and comfort of body positioning (0.1571). Within the secondary indicators, stability of surgical positioning support (0.2924), rate of adverse events during surgical positioning (0.1621) and degree of surgical field exposure (0.1896) ranked highest. Among the tertiary indicators, the frequency of detachment of surgical positioning devices (0.2102), intraoperative visual clarity (0.1119) and frequency of displacement of surgical positioning devices (0.0822) emerged as the top three indicators. To test the stability of these rankings, a leave‐one‐out sensitivity analysis was performed. Each expert's pairwise comparison matrix was sequentially removed and AHP weights recalculated. The relative importance rankings proved robust across all expert inputs.

Discussion

In this study, we addressed the urgent need to develop a standardised set of surgical positioning quality indicators to enhance the evaluation of surgical nursing quality. Our goal was to achieve more precise and efficient assessments. An initial set of surgical positioning quality indicators was formulated through an evidence‐based methodology. Subsequently, the Delphi technique was utilised to score the importance of each indicator, ensuring the scientific robustness of the findings. The literature suggests that a response rate of over 70% is indicative of high expert participation (Gao et al. 2018). In this study, the response rate for both rounds of consultation far exceeded this threshold, demonstrating substantial enthusiasm among the participating experts. Additionally, research indicates that an expert authority > 0.70 is deemed acceptable (Chen et al. 2023). The authority scores achieved in both rounds of expert consultation far surpassed this criterion, highlighting the strong credibility of the involved experts. Moreover, the Kendall W statistic provided further evidence of a high degree of consensus among the experts, thereby reinforcing the reliability of the consultation process. In conclusion, the quality evaluation system developed in this study has been validated through rigorous methodological and consultation processes, demonstrating high scientific rigour and reliability. This research encourages nursing managers to adopt these quality indicators as effective tools for evaluating surgical nursing standards and enhancing the safety of surgical patients.

Content Analysis of Surgical Positioning Quality Indicators

Quality indicators identified in this study suggested that experts in China rated good quality surgical positioning with the characteristics of safety, effectiveness and comfort. More specifically, the safety of surgical positioning included the stability of surgical positioning support, the rate of adverse events during positioning and restoration for surgery and patient limb protection. In terms of relative importance, safety showed the highest weight. This indicated that experts considered safety to be the most important aspect of good quality surgical positioning. Research by Deepanjana et al. highlighted that stable positioning systems significantly mitigate risks associated with patient movement or instability during surgery (Das et al. 2019). Our findings support this conclusion; the emphasis on stability is paramount, as unstable positioning supports can lead to intraoperative complications, adversely impacting surgical outcomes. While advancements in positioning devices have enhanced stability, discrepancies in their application persist (Farag et al. 2018). This inconsistency highlights the necessity for comprehensive training and standardisation in the use of these technologies across surgical teams. Improper positioning can lead to severe complications, including nerve damage and pressure ulcers (Emery et al. 2015; Goel et al. 2020; Nascimento and Rodrigues 2020). To address this issue, the ‘Rate of adverse events during positioning and restoration for surgery’ has been introduced as a quality evaluation indicator. This indicator is designed to systematically monitor and assess the various adverse events that may arise from improper positioning during surgical procedures. Establishing this indicator provides empirical support for future efforts toward standardisation and training in surgical positioning techniques. Studies by Lopes et al. and Speth et al. demonstrate that effective limb protection strategies are associated with a reduction in postoperative complications (Lopes and Galvão 2010; Speth 2023). By prioritising limb protection, healthcare professionals can significantly decrease the incidence of positioning‐related adverse events, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and satisfaction (Lopes and Galvão 2010). Aligning with these findings, the results underscore the necessity of implementing structured protocols for limb protection to minimise risks associated with surgical positioning. The indicator is essential for promoting patient safety and improving overall surgical results.

Effectiveness of surgical positioning had the second weight, indicating a recognition that effectiveness is necessary to ensure the quality of surgical positioning. The high relevance of this indicator reflects its influence on both operational efficiency and patient outcomes. Inadequate exposure during surgical procedures can complicate operations and hinder a surgeon's ability to navigate complex anatomical structure (Takmaz et al. 2018). Studies have demonstrated that optimising positioning significantly enhances visibility and access during surgery, thereby reducing operative time and the likelihood of errors (Ghomi et al. 2012). This suggests that careful attention to positioning not only supports the surgical team's operational effectiveness but also positively correlates with patient outcomes. In terms of surgeon satisfaction, our findings are consistent with previous studies that highlight the connection between effective surgical positioning and surgeon satisfaction (Joseph et al. 2018). Several authors have noted that when surgeons are well‐positioned, they tend to report greater satisfaction. This increased satisfaction can improve teamwork and lead to better surgical outcomes. Moreover, satisfied surgeons are generally more engaged and focused during operations, which can enhance their decision‐making and lower stress levels in the operating room. The importance of ‘effective surgical positioning’ emphasised in this study underscores how both exposure and surgeon satisfaction contribute to better surgical performance. The quality evaluation index system we developed represents a crucial step toward promoting better surgical practices.

Maintaining the comfort of both patients and surgical teams throughout the entire surgical process is crucial for minimising discomfort and potential complications arising from improper positioning. Though it ranked the least in importance, comfort should be emphasised as an essential factor of good quality surgical positioning. In this study, the comfort dimension included surgical patient comfort and surgical physician fatigue level, suggesting these indicators should be targets for quality management for surgical positioning. Research has demonstrated that enhancing comfort during procedures can lead to shorter recovery times and reduced postoperative pain (Johansson and Von Vogelsang 2019). To enhance patient comfort, practical interventions such as the use of padded cushions or foam pads under the patient's key pressure points—specifically the sacrum, elbows, heels and occiput—have proven effective (Salazar Maya and Osorio Galeano 2023). These measures can greatly enhance patient comfort during surgery, supporting our main focus on ‘surgical patient comfort’ and offering practical strategies for surgical teams to improve the patient experience in the operating room. ‘Surgeon fatigue level’ is included as an associated parameter. It has been established that inadequate positioning can lead to increased fatigue among surgeons, particularly during lengthy procedures (Kumar et al. 2024). It's reported that the occurrence of musculoskeletal pain due to non‐surgical procedures ranges from 1.5% to 89% (Gefen et al. 2020). Poor positioning can indirectly prolong surgery and increase surgeon fatigue. Recognising this relationship allows nursing teams to appreciate downstream effects on workflow and patient safety without claiming surgeon fatigue as a traditional nursing outcome. A focus on physician comfort is therefore essential, as it not only promotes the well‐being of the surgeon but also contributes to the quality of care delivered to patients. Integrating both patient and physician comfort into a single evaluation framework highlights the importance of a comprehensive approach to surgical positioning.

The importance of high‐quality surgical positioning for patient outcomes is often overlooked. It is crucial to develop indicators that can effectively measure the quality of surgical positioning through a national consensus. This study contributes to the research on creating quality indicators for surgical positioning from the perspective of both patients and surgeons. While the study was conducted within a Chinese healthcare context, the findings can be adapted to other healthcare settings as well. Identifying quality indicators for surgical positioning will help define the criteria for what constitutes effective positioning and guide surgical teams on how to enhance their practices. Additionally, these indicators can be used to assess and monitor the quality of surgical positioning, ensuring that patient care needs are better met and health outcomes are improved.

This study also had several limitations. First, considering the primary objective of establishing a set of quality evaluation indicators for surgical positioning in China, the Delphi study included only experts from China. Such context specificity matters; staffing models in China, nurse autonomy in positioning decisions is often shared with anaesthesiologists and resource availability (e.g., powered positioning beds, pressure‐relieving devices) varies widely between tertiary hospitals and secondary centres. Consequently, indicators that assume high nurse‐to‐patient ratios or advanced equipment—such as ‘real‐time pressure‐mapping monitoring’—may be less transferable to countries with different staffing norms or resource profiles. Second, this was a preliminary study focused on indicator development; their real‐world feasibility, reliability and sensitivity to change require prospective validation in diverse clinical settings before international adoption is recommended.

Conclusion

The constructed list of surgical positioning quality indicators can be used to evaluate and continuously improve nursing quality in the operating room. Validation of the quality indicators' impact in clinical practice is essential. In the future, several concrete steps should be taken. Firstly, clinical validation studies are necessary to confirm the effectiveness and reliability of these indicators in real‐world surgical settings. This will involve conducting large‐scale, multi‐centre trials to assess how well the indicators can accurately reflect the quality of surgical positioning. Secondly, efforts should be made to achieve real‐time data collection and dissemination. This can be accomplished by integrating the data collection process with existing hospital information systems. By doing so, nursing managers can obtain up‐to‐date information on surgical positioning quality, enabling timely interventions and quality improvement. Thirdly, the integration of these indicators into quality dashboards is essential. Quality dashboards can provide a visual and intuitive way for nursing managers to monitor the dynamic change process of surgical positioning quality. This will allow for quick identification of trends, problem areas and areas of success. However, in specific clinical applications, nursing managers can modify the data collection methods according to specific situations to more objectively and accurately evaluate the nursing quality for surgical positioning. Additionally, future research is also needed to ensure the authenticity of indicator data, which is fundamental for making reliable quality‐related decisions.
