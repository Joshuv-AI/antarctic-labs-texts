Early-life exposures and risk of multiple gynecological diseases: evidence from a large community-based study of 272,706 women
Authors: Juan Jiang, Yulian Fu, Yan Chen, Hao Liu, Zhaochen Sun, Yiheng Yin, Yuping Liu, Dongyu Li, Zhengwei Wan, Ping Shuai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly-life exposures may influence long-term reproductive health, but comprehensive population-based evidence remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the associations between six early-life factors (long-term/recurrent antibiotic use [LRAU], birth weight, multiple birth, breastfeeding, age at menarche and maternal smoking around birth) and the risk of six major non-neoplastic gynecological diseases in adulthood.MethodsThis large observational association study used data from 272,706 women derived from the UK Biobank, a population-based cohort resource. Six gynecological disorders—uterine fibroids (UF), polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), endometriosis, genital prolapse, female infertility, and premenstrual syndrome (PMS)—were identified from hospital inpatient records, first occurrence records, and self-reports. Multivariable logistic regression was used as the primary analysis to estimate adjusted odds ratios (aORs), with Cox models as supplementary analyses. The primary adjusted model included age at recruitment, ethnicity, educational attainment, Townsend deprivation index, and smoking status. A Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of P < 0.0014 was applied.ResultsAmong 272,706 women, the prevalence of the six non-neoplastic gynecological diseases ranged from 0.07% for PMS to 8.51% for UF. After Bonferroni correction, LRAU during early life was associated with higher odds of UF, PCOS, endometriosis, genital prolapse, and PMS. Earlier menarche was associated with higher odds of UF, PCOS, endometriosis, and genital prolapse. Maternal smoking around birth was associated with endometriosis, genital prolapse, and PMS. Not being breastfed as a baby was associated with endometriosis and PMS. Birth weight was associated with genital prolapse. Findings were generally consistent across Cox models and sensitivity analyses, although PMS estimates should be interpreted cautiously because of limited cases.ConclusionsThese findings suggest associations between selected early-life factors and adult non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. Some exposures are potentially modifiable, whereas others are non-modifiable. Together, these factors may help identify individuals at higher risk and inform future studies on risk stratification, but their potential preventive implications require further causal validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04646-1.

Introduction

Non-neoplastic gynecological diseases, including polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) [1], uterine fibroids (UF) [2], endometriosis [3], female infertility [4], genital prolapse, and premenstrual syndrome (PMS), are highly prevalent and contribute substantially to women’s reproductive morbidity, reduced quality of life [5–7], and increased healthcare costs [8]. Conditions such as UF, PCOS, and endometriosis often manifest with chronic pain, abnormal bleeding, and infertility, posing both clinical and societal challenges. Although their prevalence is rising globally [9, 10], effective prevention remains elusive due to incomplete understanding of their etiology and the absence of harmonized diagnostic criteria [10–12].

Emerging research suggests that exposures in early life—ranging from perinatal characteristics to adolescent health factors—may influence the risk of gynecological diseases in adulthood. For example, long-term/recurrent antibiotic use (LRAU) during childhood has been associated with increased risks of metabolic, gastrointestinal, and neoplastic conditions later in life [13]; early menarche has been linked to PCOS [14] and endometriosis [15]; and perinatal exposures such as low birth weight, lack of breastfeeding, and maternal smoking have been implicated in adverse reproductive outcomes [16, 17]. However, existing studies typically focus on single exposures or single diseases, often with heterogeneous designs and limited capacity to compare effect sizes across outcomes.

To address these gaps, we applied a life-course epidemiological framework using the UK Biobank (UKB) to simultaneously evaluate six early-life exposures—LRAU, birth weight, multiple birth, breastfeeding, age at menarche, and maternal smoking—and their associations with six major non-neoplastic gynecological diseases.

Methods

The study population was derived from the UK Biobank (UKB), a large population-based cohort resource [18]. Because gynecological outcomes could occur either before or after UK Biobank baseline, the present analysis was interpreted as an observational association analysis rather than a purely prospective incidence analysis; the temporal structure is shown in Supplementary Fig. S1. All participants provided written informed consent at baseline, and UKB was approved by the National Health Service National Research Ethics Service (11/NW/0382). To minimize the loss of study population, we separately removed individuals according to the absence of each early-life factors to analyze, namely eligible people divided into six independent datasets.

Early-life factors and covariates

Based on existing studies about association of early-life factors with gynecological disorders [13, 19] and available data from UKB, we chose six variables from touchscreen, online follow-up, and verbal interview to explore more comprehensively. LRAU (Field ID: 21,067) was explicitly defined as receiving "3 or more courses per year" during childhood or as a teenager. While the questionnaire did not capture specific pharmacological classes of antibiotics, it provided common clinical indications (e.g., "for tonsillitis or acne") to facilitate participant recall. Birth weight was self-reported and categorized as < 2,500 g, 2,500–3,999 g, and ≥ 4,000 g. Participants were also asked whether they were part of a multiple birth (twin, triplet, or other multiple birth), whether they had been breastfed as baby, and whether their mother smoked regularly around the time of their birth. Age at menarche was self-reported and categorized as ≤ 12 years or > 12 years. Participants with responses of prefer not to answer or do not know were treated as missing data. The detailed definition of exposures is available at Supplementary Table S1.

In this study, covariate selection was informed by prior literature and a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Supplementary Fig. S2). The DAG was constructed using DAGitty (www.dagitty.net, v3.0) to identify a minimal sufficient adjustment set for estimating the associations between early-life exposures and non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. Based on the DAG, age at recruitment, ethnicity, educational attainment, Townsend deprivation index, and smoking status were selected as adjustment variables. Age at recruitment was categorized as 40–49, 50–59, and 60–70 years; ethnicity was categorized as White or Other; educational attainment was categorized as low, intermediate, or high; smoking status was categorized as Never, Previous, or Current; and the Townsend deprivation index was categorized into quartiles (Q1, least deprived; Q4, most deprived). Other adult anthropometric, reproductive, and health-related variables, including BMI, number of live births, oral contraceptive use, menopausal status, history of hysterectomy, hypertension, and Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus, were not included in the DAG-informed adjustment set because they may be downstream of early-life exposures or related to disease development.

Definition and ascertainment of non-neoplastic gynecological diseases

We defined six non-neoplastic gynecological diseases from hospital inpatient records (including the first and the secondary diagnosis), primary care data (date information which one is the first occurrence in participants existing records), and self-reported diseases (condition first diagnosed by a doctor). Hospital records were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)−9 and ICD-10 codes, which were mapped according to the Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study cause list [20]. Detailed definitions of all gynecological outcomes, encompassing the specific ICD codes, primary care codes, and UKB self-report codes utilized, are comprehensively listed in Supplementary Table S2. Both hospital and primary care records were linked to hospitalization or prescription record of England, Scotland, and Wales. For self-reported conditions, the month and year of diagnosis were available from UKB records. To obtain a complete diagnosis date, we given the midpoint of the month (15th day) as an interpolated value. Although these dates contain some uncertainty and recall bias, we included this source to reduce data loss due to the large number of cases in the self-reported section. Multiple records of the same disease were available across different data sources, the earliest recorded diagnosis date was used as the incident date. Outcome ascertainment varied by disease according to data availability. A detailed breakdown of case numbers by data source is provided in Supplementary Table S3 and Supplementary Fig. S3.

Statistical analysis

Baseline distributions of six non-neoplastic gynecological diseases and controls across six early-life factors were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test. In the primary analysis, controls were defined as participants without records of any of the six non-neoplastic gynecological diseases, providing a disease-free gynecological reference group.

Age at menarche was evaluated using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to assess potential non-linear relationships with gynecological outcomes. Based on biological inflection points observed in the RCS curves—specifically distinct non-linear threshold effects around 12 to 13 years for diseases like PCOS and endometriosis—age at menarche was dichotomized (≤ 12 vs. > 12 years). As a sensitivity analysis, age at menarche was further categorized into five groups (< 12, 12, 13, 14, and > 14 years), with 13 years as the reference category, to assess whether the binary classification obscured more granular or non-linear associations.

Participants with missing data for early-life exposures were excluded on an exposure-specific basis rather than utilizing a unified analytic sample. This approach was deliberately chosen to avoid the severe compounded loss of sample size caused by simultaneous missingness across multiple variables. This strategy maximized the available case counts and preserved adequate statistical power, which is particularly important for outcomes with limited baseline prevalence, such as PCOS and PMS. The extent of missingness for each early-life exposure and covariate was summarized as the number and percentage of missing observations and is presented in Supplementary Table S4.

To address missing data for covariates, we used multiple imputation by chained equations with the R package mice. Five imputed datasets were generated (m = 5) with five iterations (maxit = 5) using a random seed of 123. Missing values were imputed only for covariates, whereas early-life exposures and gynecological outcomes were not imputed. The imputation model included covariates used in the regression models when missing, including the DAG-informed covariates in the primary model, namely ethnicity, educational attainment, Townsend deprivation index, and smoking status, as well as additional adult anthropometric, reproductive, and health-related variables used in the extended adjustment model, including BMI, number of live births, oral contraceptive use, menopausal status, history of hysterectomy, hypertension, and T1DM.

Due to the mixed nature of the outcomes, which included both prevalent and incident cases, and the lack of strict temporal clarity for some self-reported cases, multivariable logistic regression was used as the primary analytical approach to estimate the overall odds of lifetime disease occurrence. Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated across three models. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was the primary DAG-informed model and adjusted for age at recruitment, ethnicity, educational attainment, Townsend deprivation index, and smoking status. Model 3 was used as an extended adjustment model and further adjusted for BMI, number of live births, oral contraceptive use, menopausal status, history of hysterectomy, hypertension, and T1DM. Because these additional adult variables may be downstream of early-life exposures or related to disease development, Model 2 was considered the primary model, whereas Model 3 was used to evaluate the robustness of the findings under additional adjustment. Cox proportional hazards models were conducted as secondary supplementary analyses using the same adjustment strategy. For these models, follow-up time was calculated from age 18 years to the date of diagnosis, loss to follow-up, death, or October 31, 2022, yielding hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs.

To aid interpretation of the absolute magnitude of associations, we additionally calculated crude prevalence and prevalence differences across early-life exposure categories. For each outcome, prevalence was calculated using the outcome-specific denominator within each exposure category. Prevalence differences were calculated relative to the reference category of each early-life exposure and are presented in percentage points. Because outcomes included both prevalent and incident cases, these estimates were described as prevalence rather than absolute risk.

To assess the robustness of our findings and evaluate the potential impact of outcome misclassification, several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we applied propensity score-based inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) according to exposure status for each early-life exposure. Propensity scores were estimated using the DAG-informed covariates, including age at recruitment, ethnicity, educational attainment, smoking status, and Townsend deprivation index. Covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance. Second, we repeated the analyses using outcome-specific control groups, in which controls were defined as participants without the specific disease under analysis, regardless of whether they had other non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. Third, we evaluated the robustness of the adjustment strategy using the extended adjustment model, Model 3. Fourth, to assess potential misclassification bias, models were re-calculated by modifying the data sources used for outcome ascertainment. Separate analyses were conducted by excluding self-reported cases and by restricting outcome ascertainment to hospital inpatient records, primary care records, or self-reported diagnoses. Fifth, to reduce the potential impact of overall health status on the findings, we repeated the main analyses after excluding participants with poor health at baseline. Sixth, we additionally used birth date as the start time in Cox models to calculate HRs. All tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. To account for multiple testing across the 36 primary hypotheses (6 early-life exposures × 6 gynecological outcomes), a Bonferroni-adjusted significance threshold of P < 0.0014 (0.05/36) was applied. Analyses were conducted in R (version 4.2.3).

Results

Study participants

Of 502,355 participants, we excluded 229,062 men, 450 individuals without records of any early-life factors, and 137 women diagnosed with non-neoplastic gynecological diseases before the age of 18. Finally, 272,706 women were included in the analysis (Fig. 1). Association between early-life factors and non-neoplastic gynecological diseases

The risk of UF was elevated in participants exposed to LRAU during early life compared to those without exposure (aOR, 1.15 [95% CI,1.09–1.22], P < 0.0001; IPTW OR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.08–1.22], P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). The association remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (significance threshold: P < 0.0014). Women experiencing menarche at ≤ 12 years had a higher UF risk compared to those with later onset (aOR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.20–1.27], P < 0.0001; IPTW OR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.20–1.27], P < 0.0001) (Fig. 3). Restricted cubic spline analyses revealed a significant non-linear association between age at menarche and UF (P for overall < 0.001; P for nonlinear = 0.001) (Fig. 4). The association remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (significance threshold: P < 0.0014). Moreover, individuals with a lower birth weight (< 2500 g) had a higher risk of UF compared to those with a birth weight between 2500-3999 g (aOR, 1.06 [95% CI, 1.00–1.11], P = 0.038; IPTW OR, 1.07 [95% CI, 1.01–1.13], P = 0.017) (Supplemental Fig. S4). However, this association did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (significance threshold: P < 0.0014). Participants with a birth weight ≥ 4000 g had a higher risk of female infertility compared with those with a birth weight of 2500–3999 g (aOR, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.00–1.26], P = 0.044; IPTW OR, 1.14 [95% CI, 1.01–1.28], P = 0.028) (Supplemental Fig. S4). However, these associations did not remain statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (significance threshold: P < 0.0014). No discernible associations were observed between other early-life exposures and female infertility (Fig. 6 and Supplemental Fig. S5).

Discussion

Main findings

This study indicated that selected early-life exposures may be associated with higher odds of non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. The primary findings are summarized as follows. First, women with a history of LRAU showed higher odds of several non-neoplastic gynecological diseases, except female infertility. Second, earlier age at menarche was associated with higher odds of UF, endometriosis, and genital prolapse. Third, maternal smoking around birth was associated with higher odds of endometriosis, genital prolapse, and PMS. Finally, lack of breastfeeding during infancy was associated with higher odds of endometriosis and PMS. The prevalence difference analysis further contextualized these associations on an absolute scale. Although several associations reached statistical significance, some, particularly those for genital prolapse, corresponded to modest absolute differences in prevalence and should therefore be interpreted by considering both relative and absolute measures.

Interpretation

Many benign gynecological conditions can cause discomfort and pain such as endometriosis, PCOS, and infertility. Currently, people care about risk factors not only in childbearing age but also in early period of women's life. For example, endocrine disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are a series of exogenous agents such as bisphenol A, dioxins, and fungicides potentially interfering with the endocrine system, which can impact reproduction and developmental processes [21, 22]. Existing evidence has established that early-life environmental exposures to EDCs can increase the risk of UF [23, 24]. Expect EDCs, Kemi et al. reflected that genital talc use in early adolescence, alone and in combination with douching, is associated with prevalence of fibroids diagnosed before age 35 among Black/African American women and before ages 35 and 50 among non-Hispanic White women [19]. These results hint that early-life risk factors of gynecological diseases can be adverse exposures with potential harm appearing in adolescence even infancy.

Previous research suggests that inherent epigenomic plasticity in developing tissues can induce reprogramming, increasing the risk of UF following exposure to EDCs [23]. Moreover, effects of EDCs are extended to include induce inflammation and epigenetic alteration, which impacts cell proliferation and apoptosis signaling and lead to UF growth [25]. Additionally, a recent hypothesis proposes that endometriosis and PCOS are interconnected and represent a pair of diametric diseases [26]. This hypothesis builds on the key role of kisspeptin in the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal axis and the peripheral function of kisspeptin within female reproductive organs is associated with many physiological and pathological processes [27]. Therefore, endocrine regulation is also extremely possible to play a crucial role in the development of gynecological diseases for women with early-life exposures.

LRAU occurred during childhood or adolescence, but it still had significant implications for adult women. Growing evidence suggests gut dysbiosis influences the development of gynecological diseases like UF, PCOS, and endometriosis [28–30]. Factors such as diet, prebiotics, probiotics, and EDCs can alter gut microbiome diversity and disrupt flora balance [31]. Antibiotic use, while necessary for treating diseases, can also lead to gut dysbiosis by destroying beneficial microorganisms [32]. Studies have shown that certain antibiotics, like clindamycin [33], clarithromycin [34], and ciprofloxacin [35] can affect microbiota structure long-term. Prolonged antibiotic use in early life may increase the risk of UF, PCOS, and endometriosis by disrupting flora balance. However, without direct microbiome data in our study, this biological interpretation remains speculative. Furthermore, given the limited number of PCOS cases, the association between LRAU and PCOS warrants further validation in larger cohorts.

The maintenance of female pelvic organs depends on supporting structures such as uterine ligaments, fascia, muscles, connective tissues, and pelvic support. Weakness or damage to these structures can cause pelvic organ prolapse (POP) [36], with female genital prolapse—descent of vaginal or uterine tissues—being its primary manifestation [37]. Vaginal microbiota dysbiosis has been hypothesized to promote inflammation-related conditions (e.g., vaginitis, pelvic inflammatory disease), where inflammatory factors impair ligament repair, increasing POP risk [36, 38]. A longitudinal study found that women treated with antibiotics for inflammatory vaginal disease developed altered vaginal microbiota profiles [39], which might potentially contribute to the risk of developing genital prolapse. PMS involves physical, emotional, and behavioral symptoms during the luteal phase in reproductive-age women [40, 41], with PMDD as its severe form. Early-life dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis may increase PMDD susceptibility [42, 43]. The microbiota–gut–brain axis has also been speculated to mediate the effect of early-life LRAU on PMS [44]. Importantly, without direct microbiome evidence, these biological explanations remain purely speculative. Furthermore, given the limited sample size for PMS, the observed associations with LRAU and their potential mechanisms should be interpreted with caution.

Menstruation affects approximately 10% of the global population at any given time [45] and the onset of menstruation, typically occurs around the ages of 12 to 13 years [46]. There are notable differences in UF morbidity among racial groups, with women of color facing a higher risk [47, 48]. Edwards et al. observed an early age at menarche, defined as ≤ 11 years old, is associated with UF, with consistent findings across racial groups [49]. Early menarche has been linked to higher levels of estradiol and estrone, along with lower levels of sex hormone-binding globulin [50, 51], which may increase the risk of fibroid development. Moreover, early menarche may be associated with higher odds of endometriosis, and this association may partly reflect hormonal dysregulation or longer lifetime exposure to estrogen [16, 52]. Conversely, a Mendelian randomization study suggests a negative causal relationship between early menarche and genital prolapse [53]. The lifestyle choices made during pregnancy have long-term implications for future generations, leading to an increased focus on pregnancy management. Research by Peng et al. revealed an association between maternal smoking during pregnancy and childhood obesity [54]. Furthermore, a meta-analysis highlighted that maternal smoking during pregnancy has a more pronounced direct intrauterine effect on childhood obesity compared to paternal smoking [55]. Meanwhile, being overweight in early childhood can elevate the risk of obesity during school age and puberty [56]. Persistent obesity into adulthood can also heighten the risk of UF and genital prolapse in women of childbearing age [57, 58]. Smoking during pregnancy not only poses risks to women themselves but also increases the likelihood of mental health issues such as depression and schizophrenia in offspring [59, 60]. Longitudinal studies and genetic analyses have shown a significant association between endometriosis and depression [61, 62]. In addition, smoking during pregnancy can potentially affect many crucial organs of fetus particularly the brain [63]. In the mechanism of PMS, abnormalities of neurotransmitters (such as serotonin and gamma-aminobutyric acid) and in neurohormonal systems (such as the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system) can lead to premenstrual symptoms [64]. Therefore, early maternal smoking exposure may increase the risk of PMS by affecting foetal brain development. Similarly, this association need further research owing to the limited cases and relevant studies. Maternal smoking, as a prenatal exposure factor, also impacts female offspring's susceptibility to non-neoplastic gynecological diseases, underscoring the importance of early prevention strategies for these conditions.

LRAU, age at menarche, lack of breastfeeding during infancy, and maternal smoking around birth showed associations with selected non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. Bassetti et al. highlighted the widespread inappropriate prescribing of antibiotics, with up to 50% of antimicrobials prescribed in humans possibly being unnecessary [65]. Antibiotic abuse is a global issue, exacerbating antimicrobial resistance. Our findings suggest that long-term/recurrent antibiotic use during early life may help identify women with a higher likelihood of certain gynecological diseases, but these associations require further validation in prospective and mechanistic studies. Moreover, some studies showed a global trend towards earlier menarche onset [66, 67], and we observed an association between earlier menarche and higher odds of UF. In addition, maternal smoking around birth was also associated with higher odds of endometriosis and PMS, highlighting the potential relevance of smoking cessation efforts during pregnancy.

The observed associations between early-life exposures and subsequent gynecological morbidities may be partly explained by several plausible biological mechanisms. However, because our study did not include direct mechanistic measurements, these pathways remain speculative and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. First, in the context of long-term antibiotic use, recent mechanistic and preclinical studies have suggested a potential role of the gut microbiome, particularly the "estrobolome"—a distinct subset of enteric microbiota responsible for estrogen metabolism [68]. Consistently, murine models have suggested that antibiotic-induced gut dysbiosis may alter the trajectory of endometriotic lesion growth and may be involved in PCOS-related pathways [69].

Second, exposure to maternal smoking around birth may introduce endocrine-disrupting chemicals and toxins (e.g., nicotine, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons) in utero. Previous human placental studies have reported that maternal smoking induces specific epigenetic modifications (e.g., CYP1A1 promoter hypomethylation) that upregulate xenobiotic metabolism, which may increase the accumulation of reactive metabolic intermediates delivered to the fetus [70]. Human studies have also suggested that such exposures may increase fetal estrogen levels and dysregulate key developmental signaling pathways in the fetal ovary, including altered primordial follicle formation and disrupted FSH receptor expression [71]. Animal studies investigating transgenerational disease models have further suggested that in utero nicotine exposure may induce epigenetic changes within the developing fetal gonads, including significant global DNA methylation alterations in the fetal ovary [72]. These early gonadal changes may theoretically be related to accelerated follicular depletion in adulthood and altered ovarian steroidogenesis, which could contribute to later reproductive dysfunction [73, 74].

Third, abnormal birth weight, a well-established proxy for the intrauterine environment, aligns with the Developmental Origins of Health and Disease (DOHaD) hypothesis, which proposes that adaptive responses to adverse or excessive in utero conditions may lead to long-term changes in tissue structure and function [75]. Both low and high birth weights have been linked to early-onset insulin resistance, a well-established mechanistic driver for hyperandrogenism in PCOS [76]. Finally, early age at menarche may represent a prolonged lifetime window of estrogen exposure, which could stimulate the proliferation of estrogen-dependent pathologies, most notably UF and endometriosis [77]. These interpretations remain hypothesis-generating and require further confirmation in studies with direct biological measurements.

Strengths and limitations

The major strengths of this study include the large sample size and rich linked health data from the UK Biobank, the comprehensive evaluation of multiple early-life exposures and gynecological outcomes, and the use of multiple sensitivity analyses.

Our study is subject to several important limitations. First, several early-life exposures were self-reported many years after the exposure period, including long-term antibiotic use during childhood, breastfeeding history, maternal smoking around birth, multiple birth, and birth weight. This may introduce recall bias and non-differential misclassification, potentially attenuating the observed associations or reducing the precision of effect estimates. However, differential misclassification is also possible, because some participants may already have developed gynecological diseases before baseline and may have recalled or reported early-life exposures differently from those without disease. Therefore, associations based on retrospectively recalled exposures should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, the simplified categorical nature of these variables limited dose–response analyses. Rather than reflecting a direct causal mechanism of antibiotic exposure itself, self-reported LRAU may primarily serve as a proxy for underlying childhood infection burden, immune dysregulation, socioeconomic context, variations in healthcare-seeking behavior, or recall tendency, all of which could introduce residual confounding. Age at menarche was also analyzed using a binary categorization, which may reduce exposure granularity despite being supported by the RCS patterns and statistical power considerations.

Second, outcome ascertainment involved chronological and diagnostic complexities. Because the outcomes included both prevalent and incident cases, and some self-reported onset dates were imprecise, logistic regression was used as the primary model to estimate overall disease odds, whereas Cox models were treated as supplementary analyses. Combining hospital records, primary care data, and self-reports may also introduce outcome misclassification, although source-specific sensitivity analyses showed generally consistent results. In addition, the primary analysis used a common disease-free control group, which may have introduced selection bias if early-life exposures were associated with other gynecological outcomes. However, sensitivity analyses using outcome-specific controls showed generally consistent results, suggesting that the main findings were not driven solely by this control definition.

Third, there are limitations related to statistical power and rare outcomes. PMS was identified only from objective clinical coding because UKB lacks a self-report code for PMS, which improved diagnostic specificity but resulted in a limited sample size. In particular, PMS had only 200 cases overall, and the number of cases was smaller in exposure-specific analytic datasets. Therefore, PMS-related findings and other rare-outcome estimates should be interpreted with caution. The limited number of cases for some outcomes, particularly PMS and PCOS, also led to wide confidence intervals and statistical imprecision.

Fourth, generalizability may be limited because the UKB cohort consists predominantly of individuals of White British descent and is affected by healthy volunteer selection bias. These factors may influence healthcare access, diagnostic ascertainment, and reproductive history, thereby limiting the external validity of our findings.

Finally, the retrospective recall of some early-life exposures and the observational nature of the analyses preclude definitive causal inference. Future prospective studies with more detailed exposure assessment, objective biomarkers, and diverse populations are needed to validate these associations and clarify their potential biological and preventive implications. For example, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) may provide useful information on reproductive potential and ovarian reserve in future studies [78].

Conclusion

In conclusion, this large observational association study suggests that selected early-life factors, including LRAU, age at menarche, maternal smoking around birth, and breastfeeding history, may be associated with adult non-neoplastic gynecological diseases. However, these findings should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or direct prevention. Future prospective studies incorporating more granular exposure data, objective exposure assessment, and stronger causal designs are required to validate these associations and clarify whether they have preventive implications.
