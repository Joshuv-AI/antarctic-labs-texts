Hi-C informed kernel association test for integrating 3D genome structure into variant-set analysis
Authors: Yueyang Huang, Riddhik Basu, Yuhuan Cheng, Wenbin Lu, Shannon T Holloway, Chang Chen, Yun Li, Jung-Ying Tzeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVariant-set association analysis is a powerful strategy for genetic studies of whole-genome sequence (WGS) data, especially for rare variants. By aggregating variant signals, variant-set analysis can improve statistical power, result interpretability, and study replicability. Motivated by the evidence that 3D genome architecture plays a critical role in regulating gene transcription, several works have incorporated 3D genome architecture into gene-based association tests and demonstrated great promise. In this work, we extend the idea of 3D-genome guided test from gene-centric to gene-agnostic, whole-genome testing by introducing an Hi-C informed kernel association test (i.e. HiC-KAT). We present a principled procedure that converts Hi-C contact confidence into borrowing weights and integrates these weights into genetic similarity kernels so that higher-confidence interacting loci contribute more to the association test of the target variant set. We use a controlling parameter to adaptively determine the appropriate degree of information borrowing from its interacting loci during association testing. We assess the performance of HiC-KAT using simulations and illustrate its advantage in detecting rare-variant sets using WGS data from the ARIC study in the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine program.

Introduction

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) enables comprehensive analysis of all types of genetic variants across the entire genome, with particular strength in detecting rare variants. In human genome, rare variants constitute the majority of genetic variants [1] and are known to contribute to the etiology of many complex diseases [2–4]. For rare variant association studies, variant-set analysis provides an attractive alternative to single-variant analysis due to its ability to jointly evaluate the effects of multiple variants within a set (e.g. gene) and increase power to detect association signals in aggregate. Rare-variant tests can be broadly classified into two classes: burden tests, which assume variants influence the trait in the same direction and with similar strength [5–7], and variance component (kernel-based) tests, which model variant effects as random and can accommodate heterogeneous directions and magnitudes of effects across variants [8–13]. Recent studies have shown that incorporating biological and functional information of variants can further enhance the performance of variant-set analyses [e.g. 14, 15].

Besides functional annotation, integrating 3D genome architecture has also been shown to improve the power and interpretability of variant-set analyses [16–18]. The 3D genome architecture plays a critical role in gene regulation [19, 20]. In the eukaryotic nucleus, chromosomal DNA is folded into highly condensed and organized 3D structures, allowing transcriptional and regulatory processes to function efficiently within distinct domains [21, 22]. The 3D folding brings regions that are distant on the linear genome into close spatial proximity, enabling direct physical contact between regions. Consequently, noncoding regulatory elements (e.g. enhancers and silencers) can modulate gene transcription by acting on gene promoters located far away in linear distance. A well-known example is the obesity-associated locus within an intron of FTO, which physically interacts with and regulates IRX3 through enhancer activity [23–26]. Variants within IRX3 also associate with obesity [27–29], supporting the notion that risk variants in close 3D spatial proximity may jointly influence complex traits. Finally, disruption of 3D genome organization has also been linked to various diseases [30, 31], which further highlights the functional importance of 3D genome structure in human complex traits.

A number of chromosome conformation capture assays have been developed to investigate the 3D genome architecture [19, 20, 32, 33]. In particular, high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) adopts a genome-wide approach and captures all chromatin contacts between DNA regions in the nucleus simultaneously. Hi-C begins with formaldehyde crosslinking, which preserves interactions by chemically bonding two DNA molecules in close spatial proximity. The cross-linked DNA is then digested with a restriction enzyme, and the resulting DNA fragments are ligated to form a chimeric DNA that represents physical contacts. These chimeric DNA fragments are subsequently sheared and ligated to sequencing adapters to create an Hi-C library. The library is then sequenced using high-throughput sequencing technologies to identify interacting DNA regions.

Focusing on gene-based association test, H-MAGMA [18] uses Hi-C chromatin contact data to link distal regulatory elements to gene promoters, and aggregates association signals from all SNPs assigned to the gene, including SNPs located within the gene body, promoter, and linked distal regulatory regions. GeneScan3DKnock [17] develops a powerful multi-component gene-based testing framework that incorporates long-range chromatin interaction, functional annotations, and flexible definitions of genic and regulatory region sizes. In this framework, the gene-based variant sets are defined by incorporating putative regulatory elements identified from ChIP-seq data and the activity-by-contact model. The resulting variant sets used for gene-level association tests include SNPs located in the gene body as well as proximal and distal SNPs residing in putative regulatory elements. Both H-MAGMA and GeneScan3DKnock demonstrate the utilities of integrating 3D chromatin architecture to improve gene-level association tests.

In this work, we extend these ideas by introducing an Hi-C informed kernel association test (HiC-KAT) that adaptively integrates 3D genomic information into variant-set association analysis. We develop HiC-KAT under the kernel machine (KM) regression framework, because it includes both burden test and SKAT test [12] as special cases and can be naturally extended to the omnibus version, SKAT-O [34]. Our method differs from previous approaches in two key aspects. First, instead of restricting analyses to gene regions and focusing on the corresponding long-range interactions among gene promoters and regulatory elements, our approach considers gene-agnostic, whole-genome variant-set analysis across the entire genome, where we partition the genome into variant sets aligned with Hi-C data and incorporate SNP information from loci that significantly interact with the target variant set. Second, when evaluating the association of the target variant set, information from its interacting loci is incorporated according to the statistical confidence (e.g. q-value) of the corresponding Hi-C contacts. The contribution from interacting loci is adaptively weighted by the trait of interest, ensuring that only 3D interactions relevant to the trait under study are included. We evaluate the performance of HiC-KAT through extensive simulation studies using real WGS data from the Trans-Omics for Precision Medicine (TOPMed) Program of the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute. Finally, we apply HiC-KAT to WGS data from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study and identify promising genomic regions associated with platelet counts.

Materials and methods

Hi-C informed kernel association test

As Hi-C contact data are typically available at 10-kb resolution, we define each variant set as the variants located within a 10-kb genomic region. Following the Hi-C literature [35], we also refer to each 10-kb genomic region as a “locus.” As illustrated in Fig. 1, our workflow consists of four main steps: (1) Obtain locus–locus contact information in the form of q-values from Hi-C experiments; (2) convert contact q-values to locus–locus weight values, which determine the degree of information borrowing from the neighboring loci that significantly interact with the target locus; (3) construct the Hi-C informed kernel function by incorporating all variants in the target locus and its neighboring loci, along with the corresponding locus–locus weights, into the kernel calculation, so that variants in loci with stronger interaction evidence contribute more to genetic kernel similarity; and (4) conduct the HiC-KAT, which evaluates the association of the target locus with the trait while adaptively determining the optimal borrowing degree from its interacting loci. Step 1. Obtain the Hi-C locus–locus contact information (q-values)

We begin by retrieving Hi-C data downloaded from HUGin [36]. In this study, we use Hi-C data derived from lymphoblastoid cell lines (LCL) [37], which serve as a proxy for hematopoietic cell types given our application focuses on platelet-related traits. The proposed framework is flexible and allows the use of Hi-C data from other cell types depending on the trait under study.

Instead of using raw contact counts between locus pairs, we construct the Hi-C contact matrix using the corresponding q-values, which quantify the statistical significance of spatial proximity between locus pairs. The q-values are computed by Fit-Hi-C [38], a method that effectively distinguishes biologically meaningful chromatin contacts from those resulting from random polymer looping. Fit-Hi-C assigns q-values to chromatin contacts based on Hi-C sequencing results, adjusting for genomic distance and technical biases via spline regression. These q-values reflect the statistical confidence that two genomic loci are in close 3D spatial proximity, and hence provide a more functionally relevant measure of chromatin interaction than raw contact counts.

Step 3. Construct the Hi-C informed kernel for kernel machine regression

Simulation design

We conduct simulation studies with two main objectives: (i) to evaluate the performance of HiC-KAT in detecting association signal of the target locus, and (ii) to determine an appropriate specification of the grid \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal{C}$\end{document} and its maximum value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document}. We use the WGS data of the ARIC study, which was sequenced under the TOPMed program.

We partition each chromosome into 10 kb regions (hereafter referred to as “loci”) to align with the Hi-C data. For each locus, we collect the Hi-C information for its 200 neighboring loci within the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}1 Mb window, including \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sqrt{Z_{\ell \ell ^{^{\prime}}}}$\end{document} and the number of interacting loci, i.e. the number of loci with which the locus has significant 3D interactions (i.e. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $q_{\ell \ell ^{^{\prime}}}<0.05$\end{document}).

For simulation studies, we focus on data from chromosome 5 (chr5), which was chosen because its distributions of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sqrt{Z_{\ell \ell ^{^{\prime}}}}$\end{document} values and the number of interacting loci are similar to those observed in the whole-genome data (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Based on the information, we randomly select three loci as “target loci,” which have 30, 60, and 100 interacting loci, corresponding to the first, second, and third quantiles of the empirical distribution of the number of interacting loci per locus (Table S1 in the Supplementary Material). Given a target locus, we randomly select 15 of its interacting loci, and then randomly designate 10% of the variants within the target locus and each of these 15 loci as causal.

Robustness analysis

We conduct additional simulations to assess the robustness of HiC-KAT under various settings, based on 1000 simulation replicates. First, to evaluate robustness to locus resolution, we use simulated data with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n=6000$\end{document} from chr5 under the medium background effect and 60 interacting loci, and repeat the analyses using a finer locus definition (i.e. 5-kb loci) in addition to the primary 10-kb locus definition aligned with the Hi-C data resolution.

Second, to further evaluate generalizability beyond chr5, we conduct simulations using a target region on chr19 that contains 76 interacting loci within the LCL Hi-C structure [37]. In this setting, we compare HiC-KAT with the baseline SKAT as well as existing 3D genome-informed methods, i.e. H-MAGMA and GeneScan3D, under both well-matched and mismatched Hi-C structures. Specifically, we generate trait values based on the LCL Hi-C structure for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n=6000$\end{document} under Setting II of the Type I error simulation and under the power simulation, following the same procedure as in the chr5 analyses. As in the chr5 setting, we randomly selected 15 out of the 76 interacting loci (based on LCL) as background causal loci and considered a medium background effect (e.g. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $R^{2}\approx 0.14\%$\end{document}). We then evaluate the performance of analyses using LCL Hi-C data (i.e. well-matched) and using left ventricle (LV) Hi-C data [51] (i.e. mismatched). Under the mismatched (LV-derived) Hi-C structure, the target locus has 19 interacting loci (14 overlapping with those in LCL), among which only 4 are background causal loci.

To ensure fair comparisons across methods, we apply the same long-range chromatin interaction structure for all methods. In addition, we implement GeneScan3D (i.e. GeneScan3DKnock without the knockoff step) using the “GeneScan3D” function from the GeneScan3Dknock R package, consistent with [17] in using GeneScan3D instead of GeneScan3DKnock. Finally, instead of using the default burden test in H-MAGMA for rare variants, we adopt SKAT with the same weighted linear kernel as in HiC-KAT and the baseline SKAT, while incorporating Hi-C information following the H-MAGMA framework. These designs allow the comparisons to focus on differences in how long-range interactions are incorporated, rather than differences in the underlying association tests.

Power simulations

We observe a general trend that power decreases as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document} becomes smaller. The trend holds across different numbers of interacting loci and different magnitudes of background effects from neighboring loci, suggesting the power gain from incorporating information from interacting loci. However, larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document} does not necessarily yield higher power. For example, both \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/3$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/4$\end{document} often have similarly high power regardless of the sample size, but the Type I error rates for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/3$\end{document} are higher than those for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/4$\end{document}. These observations suggest that incorporating excessive neighboring information may dilute the association signals of the target locus, resulting in little or no additional power gain. At the same time, it can also increase the risk of false positives. Therefore, careful selection of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document} is essential to balance power gain with Type I error control. On the other hand, some \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} grid values may be too small to effectively leverage neighboring information. For example, the power of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/8$\end{document} is nearly identical to SKAT, both yielding the lowest power. This suggests that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $1/8$\end{document} could be excluded from the grid \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal{C}$\end{document}.

5-kb versus 10-kb locus resolution

Figure S2C in the Supplementary Material shows that HiC-KAT based on 5-kb locus resolution has slightly higher Type I error rates and slightly lower power compared with the 10-kb analysis (Fig. S2A in the Supplementary Material), resulting in a slightly lower f-measure. The reduction in f-measure becomes more pronounced after multiple testing adjustment (i.e. two tests per target locus under the 5-kb resolution; Fig. S2D in the Supplementary Material), reflecting the power loss due to increased multiple testing burden at finer resolution. These results suggest that while finer resolutions are feasible, aligning the locus definition with the Hi-C resolution may be a more practical choice.

Comparisons with H-MAGMA and GeneScan3D under well-matched versus mismatched Hi-C structures

Figure 5A shows that under well-matched Hi-C structures (LCL), HiC-KAT across all \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document} values achieves higher power and f-measure than H-MAGMA and GeneScan3D while maintaining reasonably controlled Type I error rates. In contrast, H-MAGMA shows substantially lower power and inflated Type I error rates; this is not unexpected, as the presence of interacting causal loci (15 out of the 76 interacting loci) could inflate Type I error rates, while noncausal interacting loci may dilute the association signals and reduce power. GeneScan3D maintains well-controlled Type I error rates but is less powerful than HiC-KAT and SKAT, likely because it aggregates \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document}-values across many candidate 3D windows, including those not associated with the trait, and hence weakens the association signal.

Under the mismatched (LV) structures (Fig. 5B), all methods except SKAT are affected as expected; nevertheless, HiC-KAT still shows higher power and f-measure while maintaining controlled Type I error rates. A few observations are worth noting. First, when the Hi-C structure is mismatched, not borrowing information from interacting loci (i.e. SKAT) yields the highest power. Second, the power of HiC-KAT is slightly lower than that of SKAT, even though SKAT is included as one candidate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} value in HiC-KAT; this reflects the cost of combining multiple \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} values via ACAT. A similar pattern is observed for GeneScan3D, although the impact is less pronounced under LV than under LCL, likely because fewer 3D windows are considered in LV. Despite the power loss, both HiC-KAT and GeneScan3D remain relatively robust under mismatched Hi-C structure (e.g. exhibiting modest power loss compared with LCL and well-controlled Type I error rates) due to their ability to aggregate information across multiple borrowing schemes or candidate 3D windows. Third, H-MAGMA shows reduced Type I error inflation and higher power under LV compared with LCL, likely because fewer interacting loci are included under LV (19 in total). Consequently, fewer background causal loci (i.e. 4) contributed to Type I error inflation, and fewer non-causal loci dilute the association signals at the target locus.

Application to the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities study

The ARIC study is a prospective cohort study designed to investigate the etiology of atherosclerotic diseases. DNA samples were sequenced at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim 30\times $\end{document} coverage, with genotype calls based on the TOPMed Freeze 8 release. Discussion

In this work, we present the HiC-KAT framework to incorporate 3D genome architecture into variant-set association tests. We illustrate strategies to translate data from high-throughput chromosome conformation capture (Hi-C) assays into locus-specific weights. These weights reflect the confidence of 3D interactions between the target locus and its neighboring loci within a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}1 Mb region, and specify how much information each interacting locus contributes to the association test of the target locus. In this framework, only loci with significant Hi-C interactions are considered, and the final weights are modulated by a tuning parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} that is determined by the data. Information from interacting loci is borrowed only when supported by the data. Through numerical studies using both simulations and real data application, we show potential gains in power and improvements in overall performance accounting for false positives and false negatives, as quantified by the f-measure. We provide the HiC-KAT code and instructions at https://github.com/TzengGroup/HiC-KAT.

There is an inherent challenge in methods that leverage 3D genomic information: while borrowing information from interacting loci can improve power, it may also make it more difficult to distinguish between “direct” association at the target locus and “indirect” associations arising from interacting loci. To address this challenge, HiC-KAT incorporates locus-specific weighting that assigns relatively smaller weights to variants in interacting loci compared with those in the target locus by limiting the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}$\end{document} value; consequently, association signals from interacting loci contribute to, but do not dominate, the association test at the target locus. In addition, HiC-KAT controls borrowing by adaptively selecting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document}. Our simulation results suggest that \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c_{\mathrm{max}}=1/5$\end{document} provides a good balance between leveraging 3D genomic information and maintaining locus-specific inference. Our real data application shows that interacting loci are not necessarily significant even when their corresponding target locus is significant. These findings suggest that the proposed weighting scheme allows information sharing while preserving locus-specific inference.

Our work provides a proof of concept for a whole-genome testing strategy that extends beyond gene regions to leverage 3D chromatin interaction information. Our implementation focuses on continuous traits, applies the SKAT framework with a linear kernel, and adopts a Gamma CDF based weighting approach to incorporate Hi-C data. While these specifications demonstrate the feasibility and utility of HiC-KAT, future work is needed to extend to other trait types, different testing approaches, and alternative strategies for integrating 3D genome information. In addition, the gene-agnostic nature of HiC-KAT also implies that significant loci may not overlap with known genes. In such cases, interpretation can be guided by annotations near the target locus (e.g. proximal genes and regulatory features), as well as genes and regulatory elements within its high-weight Hi-C interacting loci (as determined by the optimal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} value), which may help identify candidate long-range regulatory relationships. Collectively, the target locus and its high-weight interacting loci may be viewed as a set of potentially related regions and be considered as candidates for follow-up investigation and additional functional validation.

Hi-C data are often cell-type specific. When possible, 3D genome-informed methods should adopt a trait-relevant cell-type matching strategy, i.e. selecting Hi-C datasets according to their biological relevance to the trait under study. For example, H-MAGMA and GeneScan3D use brain-derived Hi-C data for neuropsychiatric traits and neurodegenerative disorders. In our application, we focus on platelet counts and, therefore, use Hi-C data derived from LCL, which are commonly used as a proxy for hematopoietic cell types. We note that HiC-KAT accommodates this by allowing users to specify Hi-C datasets so that analyses can be performed based on the most relevant biological context. When a clearly relevant cell type is not available, one possible strategy is to consider multiple Hi-C datasets from different cell types, perform analyses separately for each dataset (with the optimal tuning parameter \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} selected within each dataset), and combine the resulting \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $P$\end{document}-values across cell types using ACAT. This approach allows the data to inform which Hi-C dataset is most relevant, including the option of not incorporating Hi-C data at all.

A key advantage of the KMR framework lies in its flexibility to model diverse genetic effect mechanisms by choosing different kernel functions. Besides the linear kernel, e.g. burden kernels are well suited for scenarios where variant effects have the same direction and similar magnitude; polynomial kernels can capture more complex, potentially nonlinear relationships among variants. Hi-C information can be naturally incorporated into these kernels within the HiC-KAT framework. Specifically, the original burden kernel is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k(G_{T,i}, G_{T,j}) = \big (\sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus} T}u_{t} {G_{T,it}} \big ) \big (\sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus} T}u_{t} {G_{T,jt}} \big ), $\end{document} which is the product of weighted genotype sums for subjects \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $i$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $j$\end{document}. The corresponding Hi-C informed burden kernel extends this formulation by incorporating variants from interacting loci, with weight \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\omega _{\ell T}$\end{document} determining their contribution to the overall genetic similarity: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ k(G_{T,i},G_{T,j}) = \big ( \sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus} T} u_{t}\,G_{T,it} + \sum _{\ell \in \mathscr{N}_{T}}\sum _{s\in \mathrm{locus} \ell } \omega _{\ell T}\,u_{s}\,G_{\ell ,is} \big ) \times \big ( \sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus} T} u_{t}\,G_{T,jt} + \sum _{\ell \in \mathscr{N}_{T}}\sum _{s\in \mathrm{locus} \ell } \omega _{\ell T}\,u_{s}\,G_{\ell ,js} \big ). $\end{document}

Similarly, a polynomial kernel of degree \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $d$\end{document} is given by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $ k(G_{T,i}, G_{T,j}) = \big (1+ \sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus}\, T}u_{t} \cdot \ {G_{T,it}} \cdot{G_{T,jt}} \big )^{d},$\end{document} where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $d$\end{document} controls the degree of nonlinearity. The Hi-C informed polynomial kernel can be obtained by replacing the linear components with its Hi-C-augmented counterpart as did in the Hi-C informed linear kernel:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $k(G_{T,i}, G_{T,j}) = \big (1+ \sum _{t\in \mathrm{locus} T} u_{t} \cdot G_{T,it}\cdot G_{T,jt} \, + \sum _{\ell \in \mathscr{N}_{T}} \sum _{s\in \mathrm{locus} \ell }\, \omega _{\ell T}\cdot u_{s} \cdot G_{\ell ,is}\cdot G_{\ell ,js} \big )^{d}.$\end{document} Beyond these examples, HiC-KAT can be extended to other kernel choices in a similar manner to accommodate more complex biological scenarios, e.g. interaction effects via the interaction kernel [59], and structured effects at the gene-set or network level via pathway-informed kernels [60, 61].

We evaluate the computational cost of HiC-KAT using the chr19 LCL simulation setting with \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $n=6000$\end{document}. HiC-KAT with grid \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathcal{C}=\{1/5,1/6,1/7,1/8,1/100\}$\end{document} has an average runtime of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}284 s with a peak memory usage of 5 GB. When computations for each \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $c$\end{document} value are performed in parallel, the runtime is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}55 s. For comparison, H-MAGMA has an average runtime of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}49 s with 4 GB of peak memory usage, and GeneScan3D requires \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\sim $\end{document}18 s with 5 GB of memory. Although HiC-KAT incurs additional computational cost due to evaluating multiple c values, these computations are independent and can be efficiently parallelized. In addition, HiC-KAT is implemented in a locus-wise manner, allowing straightforward parallelization across genomic regions. Together, these features enable scalable application of HiC-KAT to large-scale genome-wide analyses.

In the current study, locus resolution of the association test is restricted to 10 kb segments. As Hi-C technology advances and higher resolution data become available through deeper sequencing, the test resolution can be further improved. The proposed framework remains applicable regardless of the resolution. At the same time, recent advances in deep learning and single-cell multi-omics approaches (e.g. [62–66]) highlight increasing efforts to characterize chromatin interactions and regulatory activity using richer and more complex data modalities. These developments may enable more refined representations of 3D genome structure, and incorporating such information into association testing may further improve the ability to detect and interpret genetic associations.

Finally, long-range and inter-chromosomal interactions can play important roles in gene regulation. However, such interactions are generally less frequent and could be more challenging to estimate reliably in Hi-C data. Because the performance of HiC-KAT can be affected by the accuracy and reliability of Hi-C data, in this study, we focus on intra-chromosomal interactions within a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}1 Mb region to balance biological relevance and the reliability of interaction signals. To further mitigate potential noise, we also restrict information borrowing to significant locus–locus interactions (interaction q-value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $<0.05$\end{document}) and adaptively control the extent of borrowing based on the data, including whether to borrow and how much to borrow. While these steps may not fully overcome limitations arising from Hi-C data quality, they provide a principled way to reduce its influence on the association test under the current \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\pm $\end{document}1 Mb interaction framework. An important direction for future work is to extend the framework to incorporate broader interactions.

Key points

Gene-based association tests leveraging 3D genome architecture have shown promise. Here, we generalize this idea to gene-agnostic, whole-genome testing by introducing an Hi-C informed kernel association test, which allows a target variant set to borrow information from its Hi-C interacting loci when assessing its association with the trait.

Gene-based association tests leveraging 3D genome architecture have shown promise. Here, we generalize this idea to gene-agnostic, whole-genome testing by introducing an Hi-C informed kernel association test, which allows a target variant set to borrow information from its Hi-C interacting loci when assessing its association with the trait.

We present a principled procedure that converts Hi-C contact confidence into borrowing weights and embed these weights into genetic similarity kernels, so that higher-confidence interacting loci contribute more to the association test of the target variant set.

We introduce a tuning parameter to govern how much the target locus borrows information from its interacting loci during association testing, so to ensure that only 3D interactions relevant to the trait under study are included.

Using numerical analyses with real whole-genome sequencing data, we show that integrating 3D genome architecture can improve association power and identify biologically meaningful variant associations that standard kernel association tests without Hi-C information may miss.
