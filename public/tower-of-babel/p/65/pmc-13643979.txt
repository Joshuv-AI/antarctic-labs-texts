The current discordance on Serratia spp. taxonomical diagnosis using proteomics or genomic tools
Authors: Blanca Pérez-Viso, Marta Hernández-García, Miguel D Fernández-de-Bobadilla, Fernando Baquero, Sonia Aracil-Gisbert, Teresa M Coque, Rosa del Campo, Rafael Cantón
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Serratia marcescens complex comprises several closely related species with potentially distinct epidemiological characteristics. Recent descriptions of new species further complicate accurate identification using routine clinical microbiology methods. We aimed to compare the current species-level identification by MALDI-TOF MS with Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS)-based approaches and to assess concordance among different diagnostic genomic tools.MethodsOverall, 107 Serratia bloodstream isolates were analysed. Initial identification was performed by MALDI-TOF MS. WGS data were analysed using: Kraken, PATO (MASH distance-based), ribosomal MLST (rMLST), and GTDB-Tk (ani-rep and classify_wf functions).ResultsMALDI-TOF MS identified S. marcescens as the predominant species (71%). Kraken assigned all isolates to S. marcescens. Phylogeny-based approaches revealed a markedly different distribution. rMLST and GTDB-Tk consistently identified Serratia sarumanii as the most frequent species (36.4% vs. 34.6%), followed by Serratia ureilytica (26.2% vs. 29%) and Serratia nevei (22.4% vs. 21.5%), while S. marcescens represented a minority (5.6% vs. 3.7%). GTDB-Tk showed near-complete concordance with rMLST (95.3%). Also, 38/107 isolates assigned to S. nevei by PATO were reclassified as S. sarumanii by both rMLST and GTDB-Tk. Complete concordance across all methods was observed in only 3.7% of isolates.ConclusionsAt the moment, species-level classification of Serratia bloodstream isolates is highly method-dependent. Routine diagnostic tools tend to underestimate diversity of the S. marcescens complex, whereas phylogeny-based genomic approaches reveal a more diverse population structure dominated by S. sarumanii, S. ureilytica and S. nevei. These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation and standardized genomic frameworks with updated databases.Keywords: Taxonomy, Genus Serratia, MALDI-TOF MS, Whole Genome Sequencing, Serratia bloodstream infection

Introduction

The genus Serratia comprises a broad and diverse group of Gram-negative bacteria comprising more than 20 validly published species with representation in a wide range of ecological niches, including environmental, plant and insect-associated, and human-related habitats [1]. This ecological versatility reflects a high degree of genomic plasticity and adaptive potential [2] of the Serratia marcescens complex, resulting in a multiplicity of specific lineages. Consequently, their taxonomy remains a difficult task, where closely related species are difficult to distinguish using conventional laboratory methods [3]. Therefore, species boundaries have historically been poorly defined, leading to inconsistencies in taxonomic assignments [4].

The introduction of Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) has substantially reshaped our understanding of Serratia species diversity. Several studies have demonstrated that S. marcescens, previously considered a single species with broad phenotypic variability, actually represents a complex (S. marcescens complex) of multiple phylogenetic lineages closely related to species such as Serratia ureilytica, Serratia nematodiphila, and others [3]. These findings highlight that many isolates identified as S. marcescens correspond to distinct genomic clades with clear taxonomic significance.

Recent large-scale genomic studies have provided a more comprehensive view of the diversity and epidemiology of the S. marcescens complex. In particular, phylogenetic analyses combining clinical and environmental isolates have demonstrated a substantial proportion of strains historically classified as S. marcescens actually belong to different species, with Serratia nevei emerging as a dominant lineage [5]. In September 2024, Klages et al. proposed the novel species Serratia sarumanii [6], and in 2026 identified this newly described species as the dominant lineage among clinical isolates of the S. marcescens complex, encompassing many strains previously misidentified as S. marcescens [7].

Recent studies highlight the practical challenges posed by the taxonomic complexity of the S. marcescens complex in clinical microbiology laboratories, where discrepancies between conventional identification methods and genome-based approaches remain frequent [8]. Overall, this suggests that species misclassification is not only a taxonomic issue but also a clinically relevant one with potential implications for surveillance and infection control.

Despite these advances, routine identification in clinical microbiology laboratories largely relies on MALDI-TOF MS. Although it provides rapid and cost-effective identification, its performance depends on the completeness of reference spectral databases, which often lack adequate representation of recently described or less common Serratia species [9]. This limitation may lead to systematic misidentification and underestimation of the true diversity and clinical relevance of non-S. marcescens species.

Nevertheless, there is still limited evidence on how these discrepancies translate into routine clinical practice in well-defined clinical cohorts, such as bloodstream infections, where accurate species identification may be particularly relevant. In this work, we compared current MALDI-TOF MS identification results, obtained through routine laboratory workflow, with several WGS-based taxonomic approaches, aiming to assess concordance and discrepancies among methods and to evaluate their impact on the accurate identification of Serratia species.

Materials and methods

Study design and bacterial isolates selection

A total of 107 Serratia spp. isolates causing bloodstream infection (BSI), affecting 106 patients were recovered at Ramón y Cajal University Hospital, (Madrid, Spain), between January 2005 and July 2020; patients were predominantly adults (age range 22–93 years), with only two paediatric cases (a two month-old infant and a 13 year-old child), consistent with the absence of a neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) in our institution. These isolates were previously characterized in an earlier study, but with few taxonomic tools [10].

At the time of isolation, bacterial identification was performed according to routine laboratory practices in place at each studied period, including biochemical methods and, in later years, MALDI-TOF MS. For the present study all isolates were re-identified by MALDI-TOF MS under standardized conditions, using the IVD 4194 and RUO 4274 libraries (Bruker Daltonics, Germany). Clinical and epidemiological data were retrospectively reviewed from patient’s medical records.

Whole genome sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Total DNA extraction was performed from 2 ml of bacterial exponential growth cultures using the Chemagic DNA Bacterial External Lysis Kit (PerkinElmer, USA), and WGS was performed with the Illumina-Novaseq 6000 platform (OGC, Oxford, UK) with 2 × 150 bp paired-end reads. Quality control and sequence filtering employing FastQC v.0.11.0 (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/) tools were performed. Short reads were assembled with SPAdes v3.13.1 [11], and genome assembly evaluation was performed by QUAST v5.1 [12].

Bacterial identification was confirmed using multiple approaches: [1] the Taxonomic Sequence Classification System Kraken v.1.1.1 [13], [2] PATO package in R, calculating the MASH distance (maximum distance = 0.06) based on all representative genomes from NCBI Refseq database [14], [3] ribosomal MLST (rMLST) from PubMLST (https://pubmlst.org/bigsdb?db=pubmlst_rmlst_seqdef_kiosk) which indexes variation of the 53 genes encoding the bacterial ribosome protein subunits and [4] Genome Taxonomy Database Toolkit (GTDB-Tk) (https://gtdb.ecogenomic.org/). Genome-based taxonomic assignment using GTDB-Tk was performed applying both the classify_wf workflow and the ani_rep function. The classify-wf approach assigns taxonomy based on phylogenetic placement within a reference tree constructed from conserved marker genes, whereas ani_rep performs assignment based on Average Nucleotide Identity (ANI) comparisons against representative genomes within the GTDB framework, where species assignments follow the internal classification criteria of the GTDB-Tk. No external ANI thresholds were applied manually in this study. The use of both approaches allowed cross-validation of taxonomic assignments derived from phylogenetic and similarity-based criteria. These tools were used according to their default implementation parameters to enable comparative assessment among methods.

The Mash v.2.3 and iTOL applications were used to generate and visualize a similarity tree based on a neighbour-joining algorithm [15], including all study isolates (n = 107) and all reference Serratia genomes (n = 25) available in the NCBI RefSeq database (accessed on 25 March 2026) to provide genomic context and enable comparative visualization of isolate clustering.

Sequence data were subsequently deposited in public databases as described in the Data Availability section.

Genome-based approaches revealed a markedly different distribution. Kraken, assigned all isolates (100%) to S. marcescens, without resolving alternative species within the dataset. In contrast, using PATO, S. nevei was the most frequently identified species (63/107; 58.9%), followed by S. ureilytica (38/107; 35.5%) and S. marcescens (6/107; 5.6%) (Table 1).

rMLST and GTDB-Tk analyses yielded a highly concordant taxonomic structure. rMLST identified S. sarumanii (39/107; 36.4%) as the most frequent species, followed by S. ureilytica (28/107; 26.2%) and S. nevei (24/107; 22.4%), with additional identification of S. bockelmannii (10/107; 9.3%) and S. marcescens (6/107; 5.6%). GTDB-Tk produced identical species-level assignments using both the ani_rep and classify_wf approaches, confirming the robustness of these classifications. Specifically, GTDB-Tk identified S. sarumanii (37/107; 34.6%) as the predominant species, followed by S. ureilytica (31/107; 29%), S. nevei (23/107; 21.5%), S. bockelmannii (10/107; 9.3%), S. marcescens (4/107; 3.7%) and S. nematodiphila (2/107; 1.9%). This last one was not identified by rMLST (Table 1).

Overall, species distributions differed substantially across identification methods. While MALDI-TOF MS and Kraken predominantly assigned isolates to S. marcescens, genome-based approaches consistently resolved a more heterogeneous population structure dominated by S. sarumanii, S. ureilytica and S. nevei.

Concordance between identification methods

Variability in species distribution across methods was reflected in the concordance analysis. Complete agreement among all identification approaches was observed in only 4/107 isolates (3.7%), indicating that most isolates showed discordant or partially concordant classifications depending on the applied method. Pairwise concordance varied substantially depending on the methods compared. The highest level of agreement was observed between rMLST and GTDB-Tk (102/107; 95.3%), demonstrating a near-complete consistency between these two genome-based approaches. Only five isolates showed discrepant assignments between them, mainly involving differences in the classification of closely related taxa within the S. marcescens complex.

In contrast, both MALDI-TOF MS and Kraken showed poor concordance with the other genome-based methods, with agreement rates consistently below 7%. Despite a higher apparent concordance between MALDI-TOF MS and Kraken (76/107; 71%), this was largely driven by the systematic assignment of isolates to S. marcescens by both methods, rather than true species-level agreement, further underscoring the limited resolving capacity of these approaches.

Moderate concordance was observed between PATO and both rMLST (58/107; 54.2%) and GTDB-Tk (55/107; 51.4%) indicating partial agreement but also substantial differences in species assignment as they remained substantially lower than rates observed between rMLST and GTDB-Tk.

Overall, these findings indicate that methods relying on proteomic profiles or k-mer classification tend to collapse the diversity of the S. marcescens complex into a single species, whereas phylogeny-based genomic approaches provide a substantially higher resolution and a more accurate representation of the underlying population structure of Serratia. As previously mentioned, discrepancies may stem from the fact that some methods used in our comparative approach were developed before the recognition of novel species within the Serratia marcescens complex.

Patterns of taxonomic reassignment

A consistent pattern of taxonomic reassignment was observed across methods. Isolates identified as S. marcescens by MALDI-TOF MS were frequently reassigned by genome-based approaches to alternative species within the S. marcescens complex, primarily S. nevei, S. ureilytica and S. sarumanii.

Notably, species such as S. sarumanii and S. nevei, which together accounted for a substantial proportion of isolates according to most of the genome-based methods, are not detected by MALDI-TOF MS and were not recognized by Kraken. In particular, a large subset of isolates initially classified as S. nevei by PATO (38/63), were consistently reassigned as S. sarumanii by both rMLST and GTDB-Tk approaches, highlighting the improved discriminatory capacity of phylogeny-based methods.

Similarly, S. bockelmannii, representing 9.3% of isolates according to rMLST and GTDB-Tk, was not identified by MALDI-TOF MS or Kraken. Conversely, S. nematodiphila, frequently reported by MALDI-TOF MS (24.3%), was largely unsupported by genome-based approaches.

Taken together, these reassignment patterns were highly directional rather than random, suggesting systematic methodological differences in species recognition.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates marked current method-dependent variability in species-level identification among Serratia bloodstream isolates. Although the collection was initially considered to predominantly represent S. marcescens, genome-based approaches revealed a substantially more heterogeneous taxonomic population. Overall concordance across the five evaluated methods was low (3.7%), underscoring both the complexity of species delineation within the genus and the strong influence of the identification strategy applied.

MALDI-TOF MS identified S. marcescens as the predominant species, accounting for 71% of isolates, whereas phylogeny-based approaches resolved the population into multiple species within the S. marcescens complex. High-resolution genomic methods consistently reassigned most isolates to alternative species, while retaining only a small fraction as S. marcescens. These findings strongly suggest that routine proteomic identification may overestimate the prevalence of S. marcescens in clinical settings. This limitation is consistent with previous reports describing reduced discriminatory power of MALDI-TOF MS for closely related taxa within species complexes, particularly when reference databases incompletely represent recently described species or genomically redefined lineages [9, 16]. Spectral similarity among members of the S. marcescens complex likely contributes to systematic consolidation under a single species label.

Kraken exhibited an even more striking pattern, assigning all isolates to S. marcescens. While k-mer-based classifiers have demonstrated high performance in metagenomics contexts [13], their taxonomic output is inherently dependent on reference database structure and curation. Underrepresentation or recent taxonomic updates may therefore bias classification toward historically dominant species names. In our dataset, this resulted in a complete loss of species-level resolution, with no alternative taxa being detected despite clear genomic evidence of diversity. Similar database-driven effects have been described in other genera undergoing taxonomic revision [4], highlighting the importance of reference genome completeness in automated classification workflows.

A key finding of this study is the very high concordance observed between rMLST and GTDB-Tk (95.3%), including complete agreement between the two GTDB-Tk approaches (ani_rep and classify_wf). This nearly identical performance supports the robustness and reproducibility of phylogeny-based frameworks for species delineation within Serratia. In contrast, methods with lower discriminatory power, i.e. those based on MASH distance (PATO), showed markedly reduced agreement with these approaches, reflecting fundamental differences in taxonomic resolution. Discrepancies between rMLST and GTDB-Tk were limited to a small number of isolates and primarily involved closely related taxa within the S. marcescens complex, further supporting the overall consistency of genome-based classification [17, 18]. Overall, genome-based taxonomic approaches (rMLST and GTDB-Tk), supported by ANI-based species assignments within the GTDB-Tk framework, consistently assigned the dataset into multiple species within the S. marcescens complex, in contrast to MALDI-TOF MS and Kraken, which largely collapsed the population into S. marcescens.

These findings are in line with recent genomic studies redefining the taxonomy of the S. marcescens complex. WGS has led to the description and reclassification of several species within the former S. marcescens complex, including S. nevei, S ureilytica and S. bockelmannii [19, 20], and more recently S. sarumanii has been proposed as a dominant lineage among clinical isolates [7]. Our results strongly support this observation, as a substantial proportion of isolates were assigned to S. sarumanii by phylogeny-based approaches, including many previously classified as S. nevei by PATO similarity-based method.

Although S. sarumanii was only recently described as a distinct species within the S. marcescens complex [6], accumulating genomic evidence suggests that it may represent a substantial proportion of isolates historically classified as S. marcescens [7]. A recent genomic reassessment of clinical isolates highlighted the epidemiological potential impact of S. sarumanii and the need for updated taxonomic frameworks. Our findings are consistent with these observations. At present, however, the biological characteristics, virulence potential, antimicrobial resistance patterns and clinical significance of S. sarumanii remain unexplored. Further studies integrating genomic, microbiological and clinical data will be required to determine whether this species differs meaningfully from other members of the complex beyond its taxonomic designation.

Similar discrepancies between routine identification and genomic taxonomy have been reported in other clinically relevant genera, such as Enterobacter, Klebsiella and Acinetobacter, where WGS have reshaped species boundaries previously defined by phenotypic or limited molecular methods [21–23]. More recent genomic studies have highlighted the extensive taxonomic restructuring that has occurred within the S. marcescens complex and the challenges associated with interpreting historical species designations considering updated genomic frameworks [24, 25]. Our results extend these observations specifically to bloodstream infections caused by Serratia, suggesting that taxonomic heterogeneity may be under recognized in invasive disease. Importantly, the systematic reassignment of isolates observed in this study indicates that misclassification is not random but follows consistent patterns linked to methodological limitations. However, the differences observed may also reflect a more accurate resolution of the underlying diversity rather than true temporal changes in species distribution. Regardless, the clinical relevance of this genus, particularly in immunocompromised patients, supports the need for continued surveillance using accurate and high-resolution identification approaches.

From a clinical perspective, the implications of this heterogeneity remain to be fully clarified. While empirical management of Serratia BSI is unlikely to depend on species-level identification, as treatment decisions are primarily guided by antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, accurate taxonomic resolution may still be relevant for epidemiological surveillance, outbreak investigation, and the interpretation of virulence or resistance determinants. Recent genomic epidemiology studies have demonstrated that hospital environment, particularly intensive care units, contributes to the persistence of S. marcescens complex, highlighting the clinical importance of accurately resolving species within this group [5]. If species within the S. marcescens complex differ in ecological niches, transmissibility, or resistance acquisition dynamics, routine over-assignment to S. marcescens could obscure clinically meaningful patterns.

This study has different limitations. It represents a single-centre retrospective collection, and the observed species distribution may not be generalizable to other geographic regions or clinical settings. In particular, the absence of a NICU at our institution may have influenced the epidemiological profile of Serratia infections, as neonatal settings are known to represent a key niche for S. marcescens outbreaks. Additionally, bacterial taxonomy within Serratia remains dynamic, and future revisions of genomic databases may further refine species boundaries. Importantly, this study was not designed to propose a formal taxonomic reclassification of the S. marcescens complex, but rather to evaluate the concordance and limitations of currently used identification methods in a clinical microbiology context.

Future studies addressing the taxonomy of the complex would benefit from core-genome phylogenetic reconstruction including type strains and representative genomes of all currently recognized species, could further refine species boundaries within this complex; however, these analyses were beyond the scope of the present study. Moreover, this is a retrospective study, including isolates collected over a prolonged period (2005–2020), preserved under standardized conditions and processed using uniform protocols. Importantly, all identification methods, including MALDI-TOF MS and genomic analyses, were applied contemporaneously, minimizing methodological variability. Therefore, the observed discrepancies are more likely attributable to intrinsic differences between identification approaches rather than to temporal or pre-analytical biases.

Conclusions

Current species-level classification of Serratia BSI isolates is highly dependent on the identification method employed. Routine diagnostic approaches, such as MALDI-TOF MS, or genomic approaches such as k-mer based classifiers, tend to collapse the diversity of the S. marcescens complex into a single species, potentially masking its true taxonomic structure. In contrast, phylogeny-based genomic methods consistently reveal a more diverse population with a predominance of lineages assigned as S. sarumanii, S. ureilytica and S. nevei, and only a minor proportion of isolates corresponding to S. marcescens. The high concordance observed between rMLST and GTDB-Tk supports the robustness of these approaches for species delineation within this complex.

These findings highlight the need for cautious interpretation of routine species identification in clinical microbiology and underscore the importance of integrating high-resolution genomic frameworks and updated reference databases to achieve accurate taxonomic assignment in closely related bacterial groups.
