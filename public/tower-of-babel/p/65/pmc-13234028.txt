Spatiotemporal evolution and driving factors of urban–rural integration in the Jinan metropolitan area
Authors: Dawei Mei, Ningning Liu, Haijiao Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Research on the spatiotemporal evolution of urban–rural integration areas within metropolitan regions has received increasing attention with the growing availability of remote sensing data and spatial analytical methods. However, existing studies often rely on single data sources or static frameworks, limiting their ability to capture the dynamic evolution and spatial heterogeneity of urban–rural integration. Based on National Polar-Orbiting Partnership (NPP)/Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) nighttime light data from 2017 to 2023, this study develops an integrated framework combining nighttime light threshold segmentation, an improved gravity model, and spatiotemporally weighted geographic regression (GTWR) to identify urban–rural integration areas and examine their spatiotemporal evolution and driving mechanisms in the Jinan Metropolitan Area. The main findings are as follows: (1) Urban–rural integration areas expanded significantly from 2017 to 2023, with fragmented patches evolving into a more continuous spatial pattern, particularly toward the northeast and east. (2) The spatial association pattern showed dynamic shifts in dominant centers between Licheng–Lixia and Taishan–Daiyue, while remaining relatively stable overall and exhibiting increased association intensity. (3) Spatial interaction intensity was concentrated in a few core districts, whereas the socioeconomic locational index showed pronounced spatial differentiation, with declining trends in central areas and increases in peripheral regions. (4) The effects of economic development level, industrial upgrading, fiscal health, the consumer price index, urban land expansion efficiency, and air pollution displayed significant spatial heterogeneity, varying in both magnitude and direction across regions. Compared with existing studies, this research integrates multi-source data and dynamic spatial econometric modeling to capture the identification, evolution, and driving mechanisms of urban–rural integration areas. Overall, urban–rural integration in metropolitan areas is characterized by spatial expansion and strengthening of spatial linkages, providing a replicable framework for targeted policymaking and coordinated regional development.

Introduction

Rapid urbanization has profoundly reshaped metropolitan regions worldwide, generating economic dynamism while simultaneously intensifying spatial inequalities within urban systems1. In many metropolitan areas, urban cores increasingly concentrate capital2, infrastructure3, and high-value industries4, whereas peripheral rural areas experience population outflow5, uneven public service provision, industrial fragmentation, and ecological pressure. These structural imbalances have made urban–rural integration a critical pathway for achieving coordinated metropolitan development. Rather than treating urban and rural spaces as separate entities, urban–rural integration emphasizes factor mobility, functional complementarity, and spatial restructuring across administrative and functional boundaries6.

In China, rapid urbanization since the reform and opening-up period has further amplified disparities within metropolitan systems7. Although urban growth has strengthened national economic capacity, persistent gaps in infrastructure provision, industrial development, income levels, and demographic composition continue to constrain coordinated regional transformation8. Within this context, urban–rural integration areas are typically characterized by mixed land use, moderate population density, and rapid socioeconomic transition, and they function as key spatial interfaces between urban cores and rural hinterlands9. These transitional zones facilitate industrial spillover, in situ urbanization, and ecological buffering, making them crucial arenas for policy intervention and structural adjustment10. Examining the spatiotemporal evolution and spatial interaction patterns of urban–rural integration areas is therefore essential for understanding how metropolitan regions transition toward more balanced and resilient development structures.

Early studies often delineated urban–rural integration areas using simplified approaches such as administrative boundaries11 or distance-based buffers from urban centers. However, these approaches may not adequately capture the complex and transitional characteristics of urban–rural interface spaces. With the increasing availability of high-resolution remote sensing data, nighttime light imagery has been widely used to identify spatial structures associated with urbanization processes12. In this study, NPP/VIIRS nighttime light data are used to delineate urban–rural integration areas, providing the spatial basis for analyzing their spatial interaction patterns and driving mechanisms.

Urban–rural spatial relationships have long been examined from the perspective of functional linkages and spatial interaction. Existing studies can be broadly grouped into three analytical traditions. The first emphasizes conceptual and relational frameworks, highlighting the importance of coordinating urban and rural development through strengthened spatial linkages and network integration13,14. The second adopts composite indicator approaches to evaluate urban–rural connectivity by integrating demographic, socioeconomic, and infrastructure dimensions15. The third applies spatial interaction models, most notably gravity-based models and social network analysis, to quantify flows of people, goods, and economic activities across space16,17. While these approaches have advanced the quantitative assessment of urban–rural connections, two limitations remain. First, most studies focus on linkages either between administrative cities or between aggregated urban and rural units, rather than examining internal spatial association patterns within functionally defined urban–rural integration areas. Second, interaction intensity is often treated as spatially static, with limited attention to how connection structures evolve over time. As urban–rural integration is inherently dynamic and structurally uneven, further investigation is needed to measure spatial association patterns at finer functional scales and to capture their temporal evolution.

The process of urban–rural integration is shaped by interacting economic, institutional, and spatial forces. From an endogenous perspective, economic development, industrial restructuring18, fiscal capacity, and factor mobility19 influence the allocation efficiency of land, labor, and capital between urban and rural systems20–22. Higher levels of economic development generally enhance market integration and promote the circulation of production factors across space, thereby facilitating the diffusion of urban functions toward surrounding rural areas. Industrial restructuring may strengthen production linkages and stimulate the spatial spillover of employment and services from urban cores to peripheral regions. Meanwhile, stronger fiscal capacity enables local governments to invest in infrastructure and public services, improving institutional conditions for coordinated urban–rural development. These mechanisms may generate either agglomeration effects that reinforce core concentration or diffusion effects that enhance peripheral linkages. From an exogenous perspective, infrastructure expansion23, technological change24,25, and policy coordination26 can alter accessibility conditions and institutional environments, thereby reshaping spatial interaction patterns. Improvements in transportation infrastructure reduce spatial frictions and strengthen interregional connectivity, while technological advancement facilitates information exchange and industrial collaboration across space. Policy coordination and institutional reforms may further reduce administrative barriers and promote the integrated governance of urban and rural development. Meanwhile, geographical constraints such as terrain conditions may moderate the intensity and direction of integration processes27. Importantly, these driving forces do not operate uniformly across space or over time. At different stages of metropolitan development, the relative influence of economic growth, industrial upgrading, infrastructure expansion, and environmental constraints28 may vary. Moreover, the same factor may produce divergent effects in central and peripheral areas due to differences in development levels and resource endowments. This suggests that the mechanisms underlying urban–rural integration are inherently spatiotemporally heterogeneous. However, many existing empirical studies rely on global regression frameworks29 or focus solely on spatial cross-sectional heterogeneity30, implicitly assuming temporal stability in parameter effects. Such assumptions may obscure dynamic structural shifts in the integration process. Therefore, analytical approaches capable of capturing both spatial and temporal nonstationarity are necessary to more accurately reveal the evolving drivers of urban–rural integration.

In the context of accelerating metropolitan development and promoting coordinated urban–rural integration, this study takes the Jinan Metropolitan Area as a case study to examine the spatial structure and driving mechanisms of urban–rural integration areas. Using nighttime light data and socioeconomic indicators, functionally defined urban–rural integration areas are delineated. A modified gravity model is employed to measure spatial association intensity and interaction potential, while the GTWR model is applied to reveal the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of key driving factors, thereby providing empirical evidence for optimizing metropolitan spatial organization. Compared with existing studies, the main contributions of this research are threefold. First, it focuses on urban–rural integration areas within metropolitan regions, providing a finer-scale perspective on areas where urban–rural interactions are most active. Second, it integrates nighttime light data with socioeconomic indicators to develop a functional identification approach for delineating urban–rural integration areas. Third, it combines a modified gravity model with the GTWR framework to jointly analyze spatial interaction patterns and the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of driving factors. This study contributes to a better understanding of the spatial evolution and interaction dynamics of urban–rural integration areas and provides empirical support for promoting coordinated urban–rural development in metropolitan regions.

Materials and methods

The main technical workflow of this study is illustrated (Fig. 1). First, during the data preparation stage, the spatial extent of the study area was defined using administrative boundary data, and urban–rural integration areas were preliminarily identified based on land-use data, high-resolution Google imagery, and nighttime light data. Second, in the area identification stage, the spatial boundaries of urban–rural integration areas were delineated by analyzing the intensity and variability of nighttime lights. Third, in the empirical analysis stage, a modified gravity model was applied to examine spatial connection patterns, interaction potential, and the socioeconomic location index of urban–rural integration areas. Finally, GTWR model was employed to analyze the driving mechanisms underlying the development of these areas.

Study area

The Jinan Metropolitan Area is located in the central-western part of Shandong Province, within the lower reaches of the Yellow River (Fig. 2). Centered on Jinan, the provincial capital, the metropolitan area covers approximately 22,300 km² and has a permanent population of about 18.1 million. Urbanization levels within the metropolitan area exhibit marked intra-regional disparities, reflecting uneven development among its constituent cities.

The development of the Jinan Metropolitan Area is constrained by a pronounced urban–rural dual structure and weak integration mechanisms. These constraints are reflected in strong central-city concentration effects, limited spillover capacity, and inefficient factor mobility between urban and rural areas. As a metropolitan region characterized by a dominant core city surrounded by extensive rural hinterlands, it provides a representative case for examining pathways toward coordinated urban–rural integration and balanced regional development.

The period from 2017 to 2023 represents a critical phase in the evolution of urban–rural relationships, characterized by accelerating urbanization, increasing regional connectivity, and intensified flows of population, capital, and information between urban and rural areas. Examining urban–rural integration during this period therefore provides valuable insights into recent changes in spatial linkages and development dynamics. In addition, the availability of consistent multi-year remote sensing and socioeconomic data enables a systematic analysis of the spatiotemporal evolution of urban–rural integration.

Data sources and preprocessing

The spatial assessment of urban–rural integration within the Jinan Metropolitan Area was conducted using monthly composite nighttime light remote sensing imagery (Table 1). The imagery was first clipped to the administrative boundary of the metropolitan area and projected into the Gauss–Krüger coordinate system to ensure spatial consistency. To match the 30 m resolution land cover dataset, the nighttime light data were resampled from the original 463 m resolution to 30 m using bilinear interpolation32. This resampling enabled pixel-level overlay analysis while preserving the spatial variation in light intensity.

To ensure temporal representativeness while maintaining analytical simplicity, a three-year interval was adopted, and 2017, 2020, and 2023 were selected as benchmark years. These time points correspond to different phases in the metropolitan development process and reflect variations in spatial structure and integration intensity. Specifically, 2017 represents a baseline period prior to large-scale metropolitan restructuring and intensified regional coordination. The year 2020 captures a transitional stage characterized by accelerated spatial integration under strengthened provincial capital development strategies. The year 2023 reflects a consolidation stage marked by improved infrastructure connectivity and enhanced spatial coordination. Before selecting these benchmark years, the complete annual nighttime light time series was systematically examined to assess interannual stability and detect potential anomalies. No significant outliers or abrupt fluctuations were identified, supporting the temporal comparability and representativeness of the selected observations.

It should be noted that this study aims to compare structural differences across key developmental stages rather than to model continuous annual dynamics. Although the GTWR model generally benefits from a larger number of temporal observations to improve parameter stability, the present design emphasizes stage-based spatial differentiation. Therefore, the three benchmark years provide sufficient temporal reference points for identifying structural evolution patterns of urban–rural integration at the metropolitan scale.

Nighttime light data were derived from the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Suomi NPP Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS) Day/Night Band (DNB) monthly composite product (NOAA/VIIRS/DNB/MONTHLY_V1/VCMCFG). This dataset provides monthly averaged top-of-atmosphere radiance values with a spatial resolution of approximately 463 m and radiometric units expressed in nanoWatts per steradian per square centimeter (nanoWatts/sr/cm²). The monthly composites are generated through quality control procedures that remove cloud-contaminated observations and reduce the influence of lunar illumination, stray light, and other transient noise sources.

In this study, the imagery was clipped to the administrative boundary of the Jinan Metropolitan Area and projected into the Gauss–Krüger coordinate system to ensure spatial consistency. Radiance values were converted to integer format to improve computational stability, and nighttime light fluctuation indices were calculated using the maximum and minimum radiance values within each study year. In addition, the complete annual time series was examined to verify interannual stability and to exclude abnormal spikes or extreme outliers, thereby ensuring temporal comparability across the selected benchmark years.

Annual land cover data were obtained from the 30 m Annual Land Cover Dataset and Its Dynamics in China from 1990 to 2024, developed by research teams at Wuhan University based on long-term Landsat observations and implemented on the Google Earth Engine platform. The dataset provides annual land cover maps with a spatial resolution of 30 m and was generated using a random forest classification framework that integrates multi-temporal spectral features and terrain variables. Training samples were constructed by combining stable reference samples from China’s national land use and land cover datasets with visually interpreted samples derived from high-resolution satellite imagery.

To enhance temporal consistency, spatial–temporal filtering and logical post-processing procedures were applied to the initial classification results. The resulting annual land cover maps follow a standardized classification system that includes major categories such as cultivated land, forest, grassland, water bodies, built-up land, and unused land. For spatial analysis, the land cover data were reprojected and spatially aligned with the nighttime light imagery to ensure consistency in the coordinate system and spatial reference prior to overlay analysis.

It should be noted that the road network dataset used in this study corresponds to the year 2020, whereas the nighttime light data cover multiple benchmark years (2017, 2020, and 2023). The overall configuration of the primary road framework in the Jinan Metropolitan Area remained relatively stable during this period. Although incremental expansions and local adjustments occurred in secondary and tertiary roads, the hierarchical structure and spatial backbone formed by expressways and major arterial roads changed only marginally. Therefore, the 2020 road network can reasonably represent the fundamental spatial connectivity pattern influencing urban–rural integration during the study period. However, newly constructed transport infrastructure after 2020 may have slightly enhanced accessibility and spatial interaction in some areas. As a result, the estimated transport-related effects in this study may be relatively conservative.

Extraction of urban–rural integration areas

NPP/VIIRS nighttime light remote sensing data offer higher spatial resolution and effectively reduce light spillover compared with earlier DMSP-OLS products. Because the data are derived from a single VIIRS sensor onboard the NPP satellite, they ensure temporal consistency and avoid calibration inconsistencies across sensors. These characteristics make VIIRS data well suited for identifying transitional spatial structures within metropolitan regions.

To reduce the impact of extreme illumination values and sensor-induced noise, a percentile-based winsorization procedure was applied before spatial analysis. Specifically, DN values below the 1st percentile were replaced with the 1st percentile threshold, and values above the 99th percentile were truncated to the 99th percentile. This preprocessing reduces the impact of extreme outliers while preserving spatial continuity. The threshold selection was guided by three theoretical assumptions: (1) higher nighttime light intensity increases the probability that an area functions as an urban center; (2) in nighttime light imagery, urban patches gradually contract as the intensity threshold increases, while fragmentation emerges when the threshold decreases to a certain level33; and (3) urban–rural integration areas exhibit transitional and heterogeneous characteristics that differ from the relative homogeneity of both urban centers and rural areas, resulting in light fluctuation patterns distinct from either type34.

Based on these assumptions, this study adopted a theory-guided and data-driven calibration process. Through repeated comparison and validation using the administrative boundaries of the Jinan Metropolitan Area and high-resolution Google imagery, the thresholds for light intensity and light fluctuation were optimized (Fig. 3). When the light intensity threshold was set to 25 and the light fluctuation threshold to 5, the extracted urban–rural integration areas showed the highest spatial consistency with urban planning data and high-resolution Google imagery. Accordingly, the spatial identification criteria were defined as follows: areas with light intensity values below 25 and light fluctuation values above 5 were classified as urban–rural integration areas. This identification standard has also been applied in related studies35.

For each parameter combination, urban–rural integration areas were re-extracted and compared with the baseline result defined by DN < 25 and undulation > 5. Measurement of spatial association in urban-rural integration areas

Spatial association feature measurement

This study employed a modified gravity model to measure the intensity of spatial correlations within the urban–rural integration areas of the Jinan Metropolitan Area. Traditional gravity models typically use population size or GDP aggregated at administrative units to represent regional mass and interaction potential. However, such indicators may not adequately capture the socioeconomic intensity within functionally defined urban–rural integration areas, which often transcend administrative boundaries. To better reflect the functional characteristics of these areas, nighttime light data were incorporated into the gravity model. Nighttime light intensity provides a comprehensive reflection of urban economic activity and population concentration and is therefore widely used as a proxy for urbanization levels36. Accordingly, the total nighttime light intensity within each identified urban–rural integration area was adopted to represent its regional mass, reflecting the overall economic scale of the area. Specifically, the regional quality of each integration area was calculated by aggregating the nighttime light intensity values of all pixels contained within the spatial boundary of the identified urban–rural integration area, thereby representing the overall level of socioeconomic activity within that area. Meanwhile, the economic attractiveness coefficient was represented by the nighttime light intensity of each integration area, which reflects the relative level of economic activity and the attraction capacity of different integration nodes.

It is acknowledged that the use of aggregated nighttime light values may introduce potential scale effects, as larger spatial units could exhibit higher total light intensity partly due to greater areal extent rather than stronger intrinsic development intensity. However, in this study, nighttime light aggregation was conducted strictly within the boundaries of functionally delineated urban–rural integration areas, which were identified through a consistent framework integrating threshold segmentation and spatial network analysis. These units represent coherent interaction zones rather than arbitrary administrative divisions. In this context, spatial extent constitutes an inherent characteristic of the integration area and reflects the scope of urban–rural interaction. Therefore, the total nighttime light intensity captures not only development intensity but also the functional economic scale of each integration unit.

Although geographical distance is often used to represent the potential for interregional interaction, reliance on straight-line (Euclidean) distance alone is insufficient because it cannot adequately represent the distance decay effect37 and overlooks the influence of transportation accessibility and the economic attractiveness of a node38. Given that highways constitute the primary mode of intercity transport within the study area39, this study used the density and design speed of national highways and expressways in the Jinan Metropolitan Area as key indicators of transportation accessibility. Analysis of influencing factors

Selecting drivers

Based on relevant theoretical frameworks and the specific conditions of the study area, socioeconomic and environmental factors were selected to examine the drivers of spatial association intensity among urban–rural integration areas. The selected indicators represent key dimensions of economic scale, structural transformation, fiscal capacity, consumption dynamics, urban expansion, and environmental quality.

Specifically, per capita GDP (x₁) was used to represent the overall level of economic development and productivity, as higher economic levels generally enhance market capacity and interregional flows42,43. The ratio of tertiary to secondary industry value added (x₂) reflects industrial upgrading, with a larger service-sector share typically associated with stronger functional connectivity and regional integration44. The fiscal revenue-to-expenditure ratio (x₃) indicates local fiscal sustainability and governance capacity, which influence infrastructure provision and coordinated development45. Per capita disposable income of urban residents (x₄) reflects consumption capacity and living standards, both of which may stimulate cross-regional mobility and economic interaction46. The ratio of built-up area to total administrative area (x₅) represents the intensity of urban expansion, which is associated with infrastructure development, population agglomeration, and spatial spillover effects47. Environmental quality was also included as a potential influencing factor. PM2.5 concentration (x₆) was selected as an indicator of air pollution due to its effects on public health, labor productivity, and population mobility48. Higher pollution levels may reduce regional attractiveness and weaken spatial linkages.

It should be noted that all socioeconomic indicators were obtained at the district and county levels due to data availability constraints. As detailed socioeconomic statistics are not available at the scale of the identified urban–rural integration areas, these indicators were used as proxy variables to represent the broader socioeconomic context in which the integration areas are embedded.

Spatiotemporal geographically weighted regression model

The spatiotemporally weighted geographically weighted regression model was employed to assess the level of urban–rural integration across the metropolitan area. By incorporating both geographic coordinates and temporal dimensions into locally varying parameters, this model effectively captures the spatiotemporal heterogeneity in the effects of explanatory variables.

In this study, spatial coordinates were defined based on the geometric centroids of each district and county unit, while the temporal dimension was represented by the corresponding observation year. This configuration allows the model to account for both spatial heterogeneity and temporal dynamics in the relationships between urban–rural integration and its driving factors.

Results

Evolution of urban-rural integration areas in the Jinan metropolitan area

The evolution of urban–rural integration areas in the Jinan Metropolitan Area from 2017 to 2023 exhibits a clear outward expansion trend, with the total area increasing by 48.34% compared with 2017, representing a net expansion of 759.96 km² over the study period (Fig. 4).

From a spatial perspective, initially scattered urban–rural integration patches gradually merged and expanded along the periphery of the central urban area, forming a more continuous spatial pattern. The expansion occurred in multiple directions, with the most pronounced growth observed toward the northwest, while the eastern sector also experienced noticeable expansion. In contrast, some integration areas within the central districts, particularly in Lixia District, showed a gradual reduction as urbanization intensified and portions of the integration zone were absorbed into the core urban area. Overall, the spatial pattern evolved from fragmented patches toward a more contiguous metropolitan integration belt surrounding the central city.

Measurement of spatial association in urban-rural integration areas

The spatial correlation intensity of urban–rural integration areas in the Jinan Metropolitan Area exhibited observable variations during the study period (Fig. 5). In terms of association centers, the locations of strong spatial association centers changed across the three observation years. In 2017, they were concentrated in Licheng District and Lixia District. In 2020, the centers shifted to Taishan District and Daiyue District, before returning to Licheng District and Lixia District in 2023. Secondary association centers were primarily distributed in Jinan and Tai ‘an, while no other regions formed association hubs of comparable intensity. From a spatial configuration perspective, the pattern of spatial association consistently exhibited a core–periphery structure across the three observation years. Although the relative prominence of certain areas changed, the overall pattern remained concentrated in a limited number of districts. The extent of spatial association varied across the observation years, and the overall intensity of spatial correlation showed an increasing trend.

Dynamics of the drivers

The spatial distribution of GTWR regression coefficients for the driving factors of urban–rural integration in the Jinan Metropolitan Area exhibits significant spatiotemporal variation from 2017 to 2023 (Fig. 7). Over this period, the coefficients of economic development level (EDL) were predominantly negative, with their absolute magnitude increasing over time. In 2017, strong negative coefficients, generally below − 4.9, were mainly concentrated in the southwestern part of the metropolitan area, whereas the central and eastern regions exhibited relatively weak or negligible effects, particularly in Laiwu, Gangcheng, and Zichuan Districts. By 2020, high-magnitude negative coefficients expanded toward the central–western region, while areas with positive or weak effects further contracted. In 2023, pronounced clusters of high-magnitude negative coefficients were observed in both the northeastern and southwestern regions, especially in Yucheng City, Qihe County, and Linyi County. The spatial extent of medium-to-high negative coefficients continued to expand, whereas positive coefficients generally declined toward near-zero levels.

The coefficients of industrial structure upgrading (ISU) remained predominantly positive throughout the study period, although their spatial distribution became increasingly concentrated. In 2017, high positive coefficients, exceeding 4.8, were widely distributed in the southeastern region, while central areas exhibited moderate effects. By 2020, strong positive clusters persisted in the southeast but with reduced spatial coverage, whereas low-value or transitional zones expanded across the central and northwestern regions. In 2023, high positive coefficients were primarily concentrated in Laiwu and Gangcheng Districts and in parts of the southwestern region, while most central areas shifted toward medium-to-low levels. Overall, the positive effects of ISU became increasingly localized over time.

Fiscal health (FH) exhibited notable spatiotemporal variation in both coefficient sign and magnitude. In 2017, coefficients were predominantly positive, with high-value clusters above 2.35 concentrated in the northern and southwestern regions, whereas the central–southern areas displayed comparatively lower values. By 2020, positive clusters persisted but became more spatially dispersed, and medium-level coefficients expanded across the central region. In 2023, a polarized pattern emerged, with strong positive coefficients shifting toward the eastern part of the study area, particularly around Zibo, while high-magnitude negative coefficients, below − 1.7, appeared in the northwestern region.

The coefficients of the consumer price index (CPI) exhibited a clear sign transition over time. In 2017 and 2020, strong negative coefficients dominated the southern regions, with values below − 9.0 in 2017, whereas northern regions exhibited positive effects. Negative areas progressively contracted between 2017 and 2020. By 2023, all coefficients became positive, with high-value clusters exceeding 6.6 in the northeastern and central regions, while previously negative southern areas shifted to moderate positive levels.

The coefficients of urban land expansion efficiency (ULEE) exhibited a gradual weakening in both magnitude and spatial coverage over time. In 2017, strong negative coefficients, approximately − 5.8, were widely distributed in the northern region, whereas high positive clusters occurred in the south. By 2020, negative clusters persisted but contracted, and medium-value zones expanded across central areas. In 2023, the magnitude of negative coefficients moderated to around − 1.7, while strong positive areas became confined to limited southern and eastern peripheral regions.

The coefficients of the air pollution index (API) also exhibited a weakening trend in both magnitude and spatial coverage. In 2017, strong negative coefficients, around − 4.6, were widely distributed across the central and southern regions, whereas high positive clusters were concentrated in the northeast. By 2020, negative clusters persisted and expanded spatially, with medium-value zones extending toward peripheral counties. In 2023, negative coefficients remained relatively stable, while positive clusters were confined to the northeastern fringe and gradually extended toward the southeastern region.

Discussion

Urban–rural integration has become an important component of China’s national development agenda, and its level of advancement is widely regarded as an indicator of improvements in the quality and efficiency of urban and rural development. Examining integration processes within metropolitan regions therefore requires analytical approaches that extend beyond conventional composite index measurements.

Unlike previous studies that primarily evaluated integration levels49, this study identifies urban–rural integration areas using nighttime light data to assess the integration status of the Jinan Metropolitan Area. This approach emphasizes functional connectivity and spatial interaction, thereby extending traditional regional correlation analysis within an explicit urban–rural integration framework. By integrating an improved gravity model with spatiotemporally weighted geographically weighted regression, the analysis captures both spatial dependence and temporal dynamics in the evolution of integration. This methodological framework helps address limitations in existing research, particularly the insufficient consideration of spatiotemporal heterogeneity and spatial effects among influencing factors, and provides empirical evidence supporting differentiated urban–rural coordination strategies within metropolitan systems.

Comparative advantages of nighttime light data over conventional spatial datasets

Compared with conventional administrative statistical data, nighttime light imagery provides a spatially continuous and temporally consistent representation of human activity intensity, avoiding the boundary rigidity and reporting delays inherent in official datasets50. Administrative statistics are typically aggregated at fixed jurisdictional levels and may obscure intra-district heterogeneity, whereas NPP/VIIRS data capture fine-scale luminosity gradients reflecting variations in population concentration, infrastructure density, and economic activity51. This continuous surface characteristic makes nighttime light data particularly suitable for identifying transitional zones such as urban–rural integration areas, where administrative boundaries often fail to represent functional spatial patterns.

Relative to land-use remote sensing data, nighttime light imagery emphasizes functional intensity rather than physical surface classification. While land-use maps effectively distinguish built-up from non-built-up areas52, they may not adequately capture differences in operational efficiency or socioeconomic vitality within built-up zones. Nighttime light intensity, by contrast, reflects the level of human and economic activity embedded in physical space, enabling a more dynamic assessment of integration processes.

Compared with emerging big-data sources—such as mobile signaling data53,54, social media check-ins55, or POI datasets56—nighttime light data offer broader spatial coverage and stronger temporal comparability. Although platform-based datasets can capture detailed flow dynamics, they are often constrained by privacy restrictions, platform bias, limited accessibility, or short time series. NPP/VIIRS data provide globally consistent long-term observations, enhancing comparability across years and regions, which is particularly advantageous for metropolitan-scale structural analysis.

Therefore, the use of nighttime light data in this study is not merely a data substitution strategy but a methodological choice aligned with the objective of identifying functional spatial integration patterns at the metropolitan scale.

Evolution of urban-rural integration areas

Spatial analysis reveals a dual dynamic of “large-scale expansion combined with embedded integration,” characterized by dominant northwestward growth and pronounced regional disparities. During the study period, the urban–rural integration area expanded substantially, while nighttime light intensity and spatial coverage in the core urban area continued to increase, indicating a strong primacy-driven agglomeration effect associated with Jinan’s role as a provincial capital. This expansion appears to be related to macroeconomic and structural factors57, including industrial structure, fiscal capacity, and household consumption, and may also be associated with policy initiatives such as pilot zones for the conversion of old and new growth drivers, rail transit expansion, and spillover effects from leading industries.

This trajectory is consistent with spatial evolution patterns observed in other Chinese urban agglomerations, particularly the “core spillover with secondary center absorption” model identified in the Beijing–Tianjin–Hebei region58. Meanwhile, the contraction of nighttime light boundaries in Lixia District reflects spatial restructuring associated with urban renewal and improved land-use efficiency, corresponding to the sustainable development paradigm of “greenfield expansion combined with brownfield redevelopment”59. Similar integration mechanisms have been documented in global metropolitan regions, such as North American cities, where spatial rebalancing occurs through a combination of infill development and peripheral expansion60.

Overall, this spatial restructuring is associated with improvements in land-use efficiency and broader sustainability objectives, including more compact urban forms, reduced ecological pressure, and the revitalization of underutilized urban spaces. Integrating green infrastructure and low-carbon technologies into redevelopment processes may further support long-term environmental sustainability and urban resilience.

Measurement of spatial association in urban-rural integration areas

The gravity-based spatial interaction pattern is characterized by pronounced core–periphery gradients, with Licheng District and Lixia District maintaining stronger association intensity than other units. This concentration of interaction may be related to their positions within regional industrial chains, transportation corridors, and administrative resource networks. Within the context of the Jinan–Tai’an integration strategy, Tai’an’s urban area functions as a secondary high-intensity node, indicating the emergence of multiple interaction centers in terms of gravitational linkage. Studies on metropolitan restructuring suggest that such secondary nodes often arise through policy coordination, infrastructure expansion, and functional specialization, contributing to partial decentralization while preserving core dominance. Nevertheless, interaction intensity remains largely concentrated around leading hubs, whereas horizontal connections among peripheral areas appear relatively limited. This configuration therefore resembles a radial interaction pattern rather than a fully developed polycentric network structure.

Comparable gradient-based interaction systems have been documented in developing European metropolitan regions, where spatial networks initially evolve through strong core concentration before gradually strengthening inter-nodal linkages61. In contrast, Chinese urban agglomerations such as the Pearl River Delta exhibit62 more mature polycentric coordination with denser horizontal connectivity. These differences may be associated with variations in development stage, institutional coordination mechanisms, and the level of transportation integration.

At the same time, several uncertainties should be acknowledged. First, the gravity model captures interaction magnitude rather than relational topology, which limits its capacity to formally identify network structural types. Second, the estimation results are sensitive to parameter specification and distance-decay assumptions. Third, data heterogeneity across districts and time periods may affect the stability of interaction coefficients. These considerations suggest that the identified spatial patterns should be interpreted as indicative interaction characteristics rather than definitive structural classifications.

Mechanism interpretation of driving factors

The GTWR analysis indicates that fiscal health, industrial upgrading, and consumption capacity are positively associated with the level of urban–rural integration. Similar findings have been reported in studies of metropolitan restructuring in both European and East Asian contexts63, where fiscal capacity and tertiary-sector expansion enhance factor mobility and public service provision, thereby facilitating regional coordination. In this regard, the observed positive association between fiscal sustainability and integration is consistent with the view that stable local public finance improves the efficiency of infrastructure investment and reduces institutional fragmentation.

The GTWR results indicate that the evolution of urban–rural integration in the Jinan Metropolitan Area is associated with the combined influence of economic development level (EDL), industrial structure upgrading (ISU), fiscal health (FH), consumer price index (CPI), urban land expansion efficiency (ULEE), and air pollution index (API). The spatial heterogeneity and temporal variation of these coefficients suggest that urban–rural integration is likely shaped by multiple interacting processes rather than a single dominant driver, a pattern also noted in previous studies8.

The consistently negative coefficients of EDL, with increasing absolute magnitudes over time, suggest that rapid economic growth in some districts may coincide with intensified factor concentration within urban cores. Such spatial concentration may correspond to structural disparities between core and peripheral areas, indicating that economic expansion alone does not necessarily align with balanced urban–rural coordination. In contrast, the predominantly positive and spatially clustered coefficients of ISU imply that transformation toward service-oriented and higher value-added industries is more closely associated with integration dynamics. Previous studies have suggested that industrial upgrading can strengthen functional linkages between urban markets and rural production systems through diversified employment and sectoral interdependence64.

FH exhibited increasing spatial polarization, shifting from broadly positive associations to the coexistence of positive and negative clusters. This pattern suggests that fiscal capacity may operate differently across districts. Where fiscal resources are effectively mobilized and allocated, stronger associations with integration tend to appear, whereas limited redistribution capacity may correspond to weaker integration performance45. Thus, fiscal conditions may mediate how economic activity is translated into spatially balanced development.

The temporal evolution of CPI coefficients—from predominantly negative to largely positive—may reflect changing consumption dynamics within the metropolitan system. Earlier negative coefficients could be associated with structural imbalances, whereas later positive values may indicate strengthened consumption capacity and improved market connectivity. Increased consumption demand has been linked in prior research to intensified urban–rural economic circulation, particularly through the expansion of rural services and agricultural value-added activities65.

ULEE showed a gradual weakening in both magnitude and spatial coverage, suggesting that the influence of spatial expansion on integration has moderated over time. In earlier stages, land development may have played a stronger role in shaping infrastructure extension and factor redistribution65. As development progressed, these effects appear to have become more localized, implying a gradual shift from expansion-driven growth toward more structurally embedded coordination mechanisms.

Environmental conditions, represented by API, exhibited persistent negative associations in several regions, with expanding spatial coverage of medium-to-high negative coefficients. This pattern suggests that environmental quality may influence integration processes by affecting residential attractiveness, labor mobility, and industrial distribution66. The coexistence of positive clusters in some areas further indicates that environmental impacts are spatially differentiated and may depend on local structural conditions.

Nevertheless, several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, the gravity model and the GTWR framework capture interaction intensity and statistical associations rather than direct causal mechanisms, which limits structural inference. Second, potential multicollinearity among socioeconomic variables may affect coefficient stability despite model optimization. Third, data heterogeneity—particularly differences in statistical standards across districts and temporal inconsistencies in certain infrastructure datasets—may introduce measurement bias. These considerations suggest that the estimated relationships should be interpreted as associative patterns within a specific modeling framework rather than deterministic causal effects (Fig. 8).

Conclusions and limitations

This study examines the urban–rural integration area within the Jinan metropolitan area. Using NPP/VIIRS nighttime light data, it characterizes patterns of resource allocation, operational efficiency, and development performance in the region. By refining the gravity model, the study quantifies the spatial interaction intensity between urban and rural areas and reveals spatiotemporal differentiation in spatial connectivity potential. The research also analyzes the socioeconomic location index of the urban–rural integration area and applies a GTWR model to investigate the driving mechanisms of urban–rural integrated development in the Jinan metropolitan area. This study makes three main contributions. First, methodologically, it integrates nighttime light remote sensing, a gravity-based spatial interaction model, and GTWR to develop an integrated analytical approach for examining urban–rural integration at the metropolitan scale. Second, the study provides empirical insights into the spatial interaction patterns and driving factors underlying metropolitan urban–rural integration. Third, by examining the Jinan Metropolitan Area, a rapidly transforming provincial metropolitan region in China, the study contributes case-based evidence to the comparative literature on urban–rural integration in transitional economies.

Although nighttime light data provide an effective proxy for capturing spatial variations in population concentration, economic activity, and infrastructure intensity67, several limitations should be noted. Thereby offering a consistent empirical basis for identifying urban–rural integration areas. Previous studies have demonstrated strong correlations between nighttime light intensity and conventional socioeconomic indicators, supporting its reliability as a macro-scale spatial indicator. In addition, other datasets such as land use information and remote sensing indices (e.g., NDVI) can provide valuable complementary insights into land surface characteristics and ecological conditions. Integrating these datasets may help to enrich the multidimensional understanding of urban–rural transition processes. In this study, nighttime light data were selected as the primary indicator because they provide consistent temporal coverage and directly reflect the intensity of human activities and economic vitality across the metropolitan area, making them particularly suitable for identifying functional gradients between urban and rural systems.

The quantitative relationship between nighttime light intensity and specific dimensions of urban–rural integration may vary across regions and development stages. However, such variability is unlikely to substantially alter the overall spatial differentiation patterns identified in this study, as the analysis focuses primarily on relative gradients rather than absolute thresholds. Although spatial thresholds were determined using a fixed-threshold approach combined with visual calibration, sensitivity checks indicate that moderate threshold adjustments do not substantially change the spatial configuration of integration areas. Future research may apply adaptive thresholding methods or machine learning algorithms to further enhance procedural objectivity.

With respect to spatial connectivity measurement, the gravity model remains a widely used and theoretically grounded approach for approximating interregional interaction intensity. Although it simplifies complex flows into mass–distance functions, this abstraction is suitable for capturing aggregate structural tendencies at the metropolitan scale. The primary objective of this study is to examine comparative interaction strength and spatial gradients rather than to simulate specific flow types, such as human mobility or capital circulation. Although distance-decay parameters were selected based on established theoretical conventions, robustness tests across alternative parameter settings indicate that the core–periphery interaction pattern remains stable. Future studies incorporating mobile signaling data or multi-source flow datasets could further refine travel-cost estimation and improve micro-level explanatory precision.

In addition, limitations remain in the selection of socioeconomic indicators. Specifically, the use of per capita disposable income of urban residents primarily reflects the income level of the urban population and does not directly capture the income conditions of rural residents, which may constrain the representation of income disparities within the urban–rural system. This indicator was adopted due to its consistent availability and comparability across districts in statistical yearbooks, ensuring data reliability and temporal continuity. Moreover, urban income levels can partially reflect regional economic vitality and consumption capacity, which are closely associated with urban–rural economic interactions at the metropolitan scale. Nevertheless, future research could incorporate more comprehensive income-related measures, such as rural per capita disposable income or the urban–rural income ratio, to better capture the income structure and enhance the robustness of the analysis.

Finally, policy documents, institutional arrangements, and digital infrastructure were not explicitly quantified in the regression framework. The inclusion of such variables requires standardized and comparable measurement indicators, which are currently limited at the district scale. Importantly, many policy-related influences may be indirectly reflected in fiscal capacity, industrial structure, and infrastructure investment variables already incorporated in the model. Therefore, although the omission of explicit policy variables may limit detailed institutional interpretation, it does not invalidate the macro-level association patterns identified. Subsequent studies could adopt mixed-method approaches or text-mining techniques to incorporate institutional dimensions more explicitly.

Overall, these considerations define the methodological scope of the study rather than undermining its analytical validity. The identified spatial patterns and driving associations remain robust within the selected modeling framework and data resolution.
