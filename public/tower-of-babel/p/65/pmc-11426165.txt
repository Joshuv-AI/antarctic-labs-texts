Impact of laparoscopic training course for surgical trainees based on an evidence-based pedagogical framework: randomized trial
Authors: Ruijun Pan, Xueliang Zhou, Chao Wu, Luyang Zhang, Jiayu Wang, Minhua Zheng, Ting Shi, Wei Cai, Jing Sun
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ‘Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, Maintain’ (LSPPDM) pedagogical framework is an evidence-based framework developed through a careful review and synthesis of the literature. The purpose of the study was to explore the effectiveness and applicability of the LSPPDM pedagogical framework in the laparoscopic training course for surgical residents.MethodsProspective study of surgical residents who underwent standardized surgical residency training in a single institution from December 2020 to December 2022. Trainees were randomized to either the pedagogical group (6-step LSPPDM pedagogical framework) or traditional group (2-step traditional approach with twice-weekly lectures and a weekly laparoscopic operating skills session). The Global Operative Assessment of Laparoscopic Skills scale was used for technical skills, and the Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons assessment form was used for non-technical skills.ResultsSixty trainees were randomized. The pedagogical group scored higher on subjective perception (P < 0.050) and the theoretical assessment (mean(s.d.) 41.83(6.66)) than those in the traditional group (37.83(5.77)) (P = 0.016). Trainees in the pedagogical group took less time to complete the assessment of models, had fewer failures and higher scores, took less time to complete laparoscopic sutures and knots, showed better proficiency with laparoscopic instruments, and completed at higher quality (P < 0.050). Trainees performing laparoscopic cholecystectomy in animal models demonstrated higher technical and non-technical skill scores in the pedagogical group (P < 0.050). ‘Tissue handling’ and ‘Decision making’ were common areas for improvement for both groups of trainees.ConclusionsThe LSPPDM pedagogical framework is feasible and demonstrated improvements in technical and non-technical skills in surgical trainees compared to a traditional training programme.

Introduction

Minimally invasive approaches are now the mainstream surgical modality in general surgery, urology, obstetrics and gynaecology, and thoracic surgery1,2. However, laparoscopic surgery is technically challenging, with less tactile feedback, long learning curves and the requirement for continuous operational training3. In the United States, training and assessment in laparoscopic surgery have become a mandatory part of the training of laparoscopic surgeons. In 2004, the American Association of Gastrointestinal and Endoscopic Surgeons conducted the Fundamentals of Laparoscopic Surgery certification programme, which was a milestone in the training of basic laparoscopic skills4. With the implementation of the standardized residency training system in China and the integrated system of residency and specialist training, the National Health and Wellness Commission of the People’s Republic of China explicitly included laparoscopic techniques in the surgical residency and specialist training programmes and assessment indicators5 (Supplementary material). In the late 1990s, Ruijin Hospital took the lead in developing laparoscopic training in China. A series of training systems and courses were designed, becoming the first clinical and research training centre specialized in minimally invasive surgery in China, accredited as one of the Asia–Pacific Regional Minimally Invasive Surgery Training Centres by Endoscopic Laparoscopic Surgeons Society of Asia.

Laparoscopic technique is a procedural skill, and the ability to acquire procedural skills is a fundamental goal of medical education, requiring specialized education, training and assessment. The ‘Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, Maintain’ (LSPPDM) model is an evidence-based pedagogical framework that has been developed by careful review and synthesis of the literature, which is a mainstream position in the modern medical education system6. This pedagogical framework serves as a guide in the teaching and training of procedural skills. The LSPPDM pedagogical framework is divided into cognitive and psychomotor phases and is a step-by-step process consisting of six steps (Fig. S1). The first step of ‘learn’ and the second step of ‘see’ belong to the cognitive stage, where ‘learn’ refers to the theoretical way in which the trainee acquires knowledge of the procedure through books, lectures and videos. The step of ‘see’ focuses on the visualization of the learning process, mainly in the form of standardized operations and demonstrations by the trainer. The steps of ‘practice’, ‘prove’, ‘do’ and ‘maintain’ belong to the psychomotor phase of the framework. In the ‘practice’ step, the trainee is formally trained in the simulator, and in the ‘prove’ step, the trainee performs a summative evaluation and feedback of the skills on the simulator. In the ‘do’ step, the trainee passes a simulator-based assessment and then performs the skills on a patient under direct supervision in a real clinical situation. The final step, ‘maintain’, is carried out throughout the trainee’s career, emphasizing the maintenance of the trainee’s skills in clinical practice, assisted by simulators where necessary, and is pivotal in the acquisition of procedural skills.

The aim of this study was to assess the effectiveness of laparoscopic training for surgical residents based on an evidence-based pedagogical framework.

Methods

Ethical approval

Laparoscopic training is part of the Chinese surgical residency programme and participation in this study was voluntary. This study was approved by the local ethics committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. 2020-384). All participants were provided with detailed explanations and signed informed consent.

The use of laboratory animals in this study followed the Guidelines for the Ethics and Welfare of Laboratory Animals. This study was approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Ruijin Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (No. AUP-20240628-01).

Study population

First- and second-year surgical residents undergoing standardized surgical residency training at a large, internationally accredited laparoscopic training centre and high-volume teaching hospital in China from December 2020 to December 2022 volunteered to participate in this study.

Inclusion criteria: (1) enrolled in first or second year of standardized residency training; (2) major in surgery with knowledge of laparoscopic techniques; (3) aged between 25 and 35 years; (4) no physical or mental health diseases; (5) understanding the nature of the study and able to consent.

Exclusion criteria: (1) disability in the hand, not right-handed; (2) corrected visual acuity not 1.0 and above; (3) previous attendance on courses related to laparoscopic training.

Study design

An overview of the prospective study design is presented in Fig. 1. Sixty trainees were randomly divided into two groups (a traditional group and a pedagogical group) according to the balanced randomization key generated by the www.random.org website, with 30 participants in each group. Each participant received a unique identification number after randomization (pedagogical group: 1–30, traditional group: 31–60). Because three trainees in each group were required to interact with each other as the lead surgeon, the first assistant and the assistant handling the laparoscope in laparoscopic cholecystectomy in live animals, random pairings were made within the two groups based on identification numbers. Immediately after randomization into groups and random pairing within groups, each participant was registered with the study leader and the system and was not allowed to change their identification number, group or pairing partner until the study was completed.

Design of the prospective study

LSPPDM, Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, Maintain.

The envelope method was used to hide the allocation of trainees, which was implemented as follows. The resulting random allocation sequence was placed in sequentially coded, sealed, opaque envelopes, which were opened sequentially and assigned to the appropriate comparison group only after the researcher had determined the eligibility of the subjects. The researcher wrote the names and details of the eligible subjects on the appropriate envelope surface with pressure-sensitive copy paper on the inside or with copy paper inside the envelope before opening the envelopes sequentially. Cardboard or tin foil was placed inside the envelopes so that the envelopes were not see-through in bright light. Trainees in both groups were not allowed to discuss course details with each other until the study was completed. The professionals who assessed and evaluated the skills of the trainees were unaware of the allocation status of the trainees and the sequence of scenarios in the pre- and post-assessment. Subsequently, the two groups of trainees underwent separate laparoscopic training according to the course design without interaction with each other.

Laparoscopic training course based on a traditional pedagogical framework (traditional group)

The traditional group learned through the two-step traditional approach, which consisted of twice-weekly lectures on laparoscopy, and the second step was a weekly laparoscopic operating skills session under the supervision of a training instructor. The lectures were based on the history and contemporary advancements in laparoscopy, covering the basic principles and functions of laparoscopic surgical equipment and instruments, the medical requirements, indications and contraindications for laparoscopic surgery, anatomical basics of laparoscopic surgery and operative techniques, with each lecture spanning one hour. The skills operation course was mainly divided into two parts: basic skills operation and advanced skills operation. In the basic skills operation course, trainees practiced individually to master basic laparoscopic skills using surgical instruments such as separating forceps, grasping forceps and other basic laparoscopic surgical skills such as grasping, transferring and eye–hand coordination. The training equipment were models, including One Track Transfer, One Tunnel Pass and High and Low Pillars. The advanced skills operation was based on the clinical practice of laparoscopic surgical skills simulation training in a group of three trainees. Ex-vivo animal tissue models (cholecystectomy) were used as the training object to cultivate the spatial and tactile sense of the trainees in the laparoscopic operation and to develop the laparoscopic precise positioning, handling and suture tying and other clinical surgical operations. The trainees were all guided and supervised by the training instructor to perform the skill operation training, and technical errors were corrected immediately.

Laparoscopic training course based on LSPPDM pedagogical framework (pedagogical group)

The pedagogy group was instructed following the six steps of the LSPPDM pedagogy, which consisted of 2 lectures/week + video + 2 skills sessions/week. In the first step (Learn), the trainees were given pedagogical lectures, which were the same in both the traditional and pedagogical groups, and given by the same lecturers. In the second step (See), trainees watched a 30-min instructional video on laparoscopic operations. After the trainees watched the instructional video in class, it was shared via email so that the trainees could watch it at any time. In the third step (Practice), the trainees performed the same basic and advanced laparoscopic skill operations as the traditional group on a simple simulator under the guidance and supervision of the instructor, and technical errors were corrected immediately. In the fourth step (Prove), trainees enhanced their learning and memory by proving their operational skills on the simulator with reference to the Operational Assessment Sheet. In the fifth step (Doing), trainees observed and learned the basic processes and procedures of laparoscopic surgery through clinical practice and operating theatre rotations 1 day per week. Finally, step 6 (Maintain) was established through an additional hour per week dedicated to self-directed learning in an operational skills course. Through self-directed learning, trainees further identified their needs and deficiencies.

A post-training questionnaire for subjective perception scores and feedback

A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect baseline information from all study participants and obtain the subjective perception scores and feedback from the two groups of trainees after the training, which consisted of six sections (Supplementary material). All study participants volunteered to participate in the questionnaires and answered them by themselves without any interference. The questionnaires were completed, quality-controlled by relevant professionals and collected on the spot.

Assessment of basic laparoscopic operating skills

The purpose of the basic laparoscopic operation skills assessment was to evaluate the proficiency of the trainees in utilizing basic laparoscopic surgical instruments, such as separating forceps and grasping forceps, as well as basic laparoscopic operation skills, such as grasping, transferring and eye–hand coordination. The assessment projects were laparoscopic theoretical knowledge assessment and operation assessment of models including One Track Transfer, One Tunnel Pass, High and Low Pillars and Laparoscopic Suturing and Knotting (Fig. 2). The theoretical knowledge assessment was conducted in a closed-book format under the supervision of the invigilator and was submitted through the Internet. Operation assessment methods required each trainee to complete One Track Transfer (a rubber ring over a track), One Tunnel Pass (a rope crossing a horizontal row of track), High and Low Pillars (place the balls on all the pillars) and Laparoscopic Suturing and Knotting (suture and knot three surgical knots). The time of completion, the number of failed attempts during the assessment, and the scores of the assessment were recorded. The original assessment videos for checking were retained. The results of the operational assessment of each trainee were independently scored by each of the three trained full-time assessors, and the mean score was taken as the final score.

Models and training scenes for basic laparoscopic skills training

Technical and non-technical scores of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in live pigs

Laparoscopic cholecystectomy in live pigs was performed in a simulated operating theatre at our medical centre and approved by the local institutional laboratory animal ethics committee. A surgical group of three trainees randomly paired prior to the training took turns to be the lead surgeon, first assistant and assistant holding the laparoscope respectively, depending on the stage of the surgery and the coordination within the group. However, each trainee had to go through all three of these roles before the surgery was completed. The live pigs were operated on in strict accordance with the standard procedures of aseptic operation and clinical surgery. Each group was supervised by an experienced laparoscopic surgeon during the surgical procedure, who provided appropriate guidance and feedback on complex anatomical structures and surgical steps to ensure that the operation of the trainees did not endanger the safety of the live pigs and needed to balance the role of the trainees during the surgical process. The assessment was divided into two parts, technical and non-technical scores, which were blindly and independently scored by five professionally trained assessors. The technique score was based on the Global Operative Assessment of Laparoscopic Skills (GOALS) scale, which consisted of five sections: depth perception, bimanual dexterity, efficiency, tissue handling and autonomy7. The non-technical score was based on the Non-Technical Skills for Surgeons (NOTSS) evaluation form, which consisted of four sections: Situation awareness, Decision-making, Communication and teamwork, and Leadership8. The entire surgical procedure was video-recorded and retained in its entirety.

Statistical analysis

No formal power calculation was undertaken; a pragmatic number of 60 trainees were planned for recruitment to study the impact of the pedagogical framework.

Data were analysed with the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) 25. Descriptive statistics were used to present the characteristics of the sample. Count data were compared between the two groups with the χ2 test. Measurements were expressed as mean(s.d.). To compare the differences between the two groups, an independent samples t-test was used. P < 0.050 was considered significant.

Technical skills

Trainees in the pedagogical group scored higher in ‘Depth perception’ (t = 2.26, P = 0.028), ‘Bimanual dexterity’ (t = 3.23, P = 0.002), ‘Efficiency’ (t = 2.59, P = 0.012), ‘Tissue handling’ (t = 4.91, P = 0.000), ‘Autonomy’ (t = 2.78, P = 0.008) and ‘Mean GOALS score’ (t = 3.81, P = 0.000).

Non-technical skills

Trainees in the pedagogical group performed better in ‘Decision making’ (t = 2.44, P = 0.018), ‘Communication and teamwork’ (t = 3.29, P = 0.002), ‘Leadership’ (t = 2.98, P = 0.004) and ‘Mean NOTSS score’ (t = 3.49, P = 0.001). There were no differences in situational awareness between the two groups.

Both groups had lower assessment scores for ‘Tissue handling’ (Pedagogical group: 3.70(0.48), Traditional group: 2.98(0.64)) in the technical skills score and ‘Decision making’ (Pedagogical group: 3.68(0.41), Traditional group: 3.41(0.43)) in non-technical skills scores (Fig. S2).

Discussion

Trainees undergoing the laparoscopic training course based on the LSPPDM pedagogical framework had a better subjective and training experience overall. The subjective perception scores of this study indicated that the trainees in the pedagogical group had higher subjective perception scores for ‘Teaching methods and time schedule’, ‘Adequate practice opportunities’, ‘The course contributes to clinical work’ and ‘Post-course evaluation meets pre-course expectations’ as compared to the trainees in the traditional group. In many laparoscopic training courses, virtual reality technology was incorporated to simulate the surgical procedure to increase the perception of surgery for young surgeons while keeping the training interesting. These innovative training systems and models have greatly enhanced the interest of the trainees in learning, enabling them to gain a sense of realism in actual clinical practice during simulation training and increasing their competence in the workplace when they enter clinical practice9.

The laparoscopic theoretical knowledge learning and basic operation skills training courses based on the LSPPDM pedagogical framework significantly improved the training efficiency and quality of the trainees. The use of simple models for hands-on training helped trainees understand laparoscopic surgery and reinforced theoretical knowledge. It also allowed the trainees to adapt more quickly to the spatial orientation in the laparoscopic environment, which was essential to shorten the learning curve10. In this study, trainees in the pedagogical group achieved higher scores in both the theoretical knowledge assessment and the basic laparoscopic operating skills assessment. This indicated that the LSPPDM pedagogical framework had greater advantages and applicability in laparoscopic theoretical knowledge learning and basic skill operation training.

The LSPPDM pedagogical framework was feasible and had significant advantages in advanced laparoscopic skills training courses and the improvement of non-technical skills of trainees. The assessment scores of laparoscopic cholecystectomy in live pigs in this study demonstrated that the trainees in the pedagogical group scored significantly better on both the technical and non-technical skills aspects than those in the traditional group. The initial intention of the advanced laparoscopic training course was to promote the mastery of basic laparoscopic skills through homogeneous and systematic training. This would be a surrogate for clinical quality, and the goal of the training was to reduce errors and improve real-life clinical practice11.

Neither group performed well in ‘Tissue handling’ in technical skills or ‘Decision making’ in non-technical skills. The benefits of simulation training of basic and advanced laparoscopic skills should be balanced with the relative inexperience of the young residents. Trainees were outside a ‘real-life’ clinical environment, and it was observed that there were still differences in the real response to clinical surgical processes. Although simulation is valuable it does not replace the need for training in real-life clinical environments.

Sawyer et al., for the first time, proposed a six-step evidence-based pedagogical framework (LSPPDM) for procedural skills training in medicine: Learn, See, Practice, Prove, Do, and Maintain6. The framework was developed following a review and critical synthesis of the literature, building on adult learning theory. The evidence behind each of the key components of the framework was rooted in empirical investigation. In 2020, Goldman et al. demonstrated that an advanced paediatric emergency airway management training course based on the LSPPDM pedagogical framework significantly improved the self-reported comfort of trainees in handling the emergency paediatric airway12. In 2021, Liaqat et al. successfully applied the LSPPDM pedagogical framework to neonatal resuscitation skills training and demonstrated the effectiveness and strengths of the pedagogical framework13. During the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) epidemic in 2022, Liow et al. revealed that skills training based on the LSPPDM pedagogical framework could effectively train many national healthcare workers on personal protective equipment (PPE), resulting in a high level of adherence to the appropriate use of PPE and the prevention of healthcare-associated COVID-19 infections14. However, no relevant studies have reported the application of the LSPPDM pedagogical framework in laparoscopic training.

Laparoscopic surgery is one of the main approaches for many surgical disciplines. Surgical specialists and educators are focusing on the reform and quality improvement of medical education including laparoscopic training15. There are three important components: training system, training team and training experience. However, most of the current research focuses only on the innovation of training experience based on high-tech training products, ignoring the importance of improving the training system and expanding the training team. It is undeniable that the introduction of high-tech training products for the trainees to provide good training experience and simulation of real clinical surgery scenes greatly improves the efficiency and quality of training16. Therefore, based on the innovation of the training experience through the introduction of the highly simulated and applicable Darwin laparoscopic simulation training system (Fig. S3) into the training course, the research team focused on the improvement and innovation of the training system. This focused on the pedagogical framework of the training course, to maximize the efficiency and quality of the training for the trainees, to shorten the learning curve of laparoscopy and to maintain the proficiency of skills for a long period of time.

This study has some limitations. First, the present study was a prospective single-centre study, and the small number of prospective enrolments (n = 60) may limit the applicability of the LSPPDM pedagogical framework to laparoscopic training courses in different medical centres. Second, simulation training aims to achieve a smooth transition to clinical practice, and medical education research should seek to measure training outcomes in real operating theatres. However, this is practically unfeasible in most studies because the heterogeneity of patients, and in particular concerns about patient safety based on ethical perspectives, makes it difficult to assess the true clinical skills of trainees after training. Third, the technical and non-technical skills in this study were not assessed through objective data collection and scoring tools or platforms, but were scored by professional full-time assessors, which was inevitably influenced by the subjective aspects of the assessors, even though the blind procedures had been implemented. Finally, the technical and non-technical skills of the trainees were assessed shortly after the end of the training, so the long-term maintenance effect is beyond the scope of our study and requires further long-term follow-up.
