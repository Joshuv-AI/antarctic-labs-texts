Association between inflammatory cytokines and carotid plaque in young and middle-aged adults
Authors: Zhiyin Dai, Rui Chen, Junying Duan, Ye Zhou, Wei Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory cytokines are crucial in the development of atherosclerosis. However, the association between inflammatory cytokines and carotid artery plaques in young and middle-aged individuals is not well-defined. This retrospective study analyzed hospitalized adults aged ≤65 years who underwent carotid ultrasounds (October 2022–November 2024). Participants were grouped by carotid plaque presence, and inflammatory cytokine levels and plaque incidence were compared between groups.This study compared 528 subjects without carotid plaques and 635 subjects with carotid plaques. Baseline characteristics analysis showed that the prevalence of smoking and hypertension was significantly higher in those with carotid plaques. Among the cytokines assessed, only Log-transformed interleukin-10 (Log IL-10) levels showed a significant difference between the two groups. Univariate analysis revealed that age, diabetes mellitus, body mass index, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, estimated glomerular filtration rate, Log IL-10, and hypertension were significantly associated with the presence of carotid plaques. Multivariate regression analysis indicated that fasting blood glucose, Log IL-10, and hypertension remained independently associated with carotid plaques, with Log-transformed IL-10 exhibiting a protective effect. In young and middle-aged women, different levels of Log IL-10 were negatively correlated with the presence of carotid plaques. However, no significant association was observed in men after adjusting for confounding factors. In conclusion, circulating IL-10 levels play a protective role in the formation of carotid artery plaques in young and middle-aged individuals, and this effect demonstrates sexual dimorphism.Keywords: Inflammatory cytokines, Carotid artery plaques, Young and middle-aged

Introduction

Inflammatory cytokines play a dual role in the progression of atherosclerosis: they both initiate plaque formation and drive its progression. Regardless of etiology, they can drive plaque progression from a stable to an unstable state [1,2]. For example, circulating interleukin (IL)-6 not only predicts the severity and vulnerability of carotid artery plaques but also correlates closely with disease progression, providing a basis for clinical risk stratification [3]. Moreover, pro-inflammatory mediators, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [4], are involved in endothelial dysfunction [5], lipid accumulation [6], inflammatory cell infiltration [7], plaque formation, and plaque vulnerability. Carotid atherosclerosis is linked to cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases and predicts the occurrence of these disorders [8]. Carotid ultrasonography is a common diagnostic tool, but it has limitations in detecting early or subclinical plaque lesions [9]. Circulating inflammatory cytokines, as non-invasive potential biomarkers, can be easily and quickly detected. Analyzing the correlation between these cytokines and carotid plaques can improve early diagnosis, assess plaque stability, and predict cardiovascular events, thus informing clinical decisions.

Compared with the elderly, unhealthy lifestyle factors-such as a high-salt and high-fat diet, prolonged staying up late, lack of physical exercise, smoking, and alcohol consumption are more prevalent among young and middle-aged adults [10,11]. These behaviors are more likely to accumulate potential pathological foundations in the body, thereby promoting the development of carotid atherosclerosis. Specifically, risk factors like physical inactivity [12] and smoking [13] have been confirmed to directly accelerate the progression of atherosclerosis by affecting the levels of circulating inflammatory factors [14]. However, the relationship between circulating inflammatory cytokines and carotid atherosclerosis in young and middle-aged group is not fully understood. This study aims to analyze the differences in circulating inflammatory cytokine levels, including pro-inflammatory factors (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12p70, IL-17A, TNF-α, interferon (IFN)-α2, and IFN-γ), anti-inflammatory factors (IL-4, IL-10), and immunomodulatory factors (IL-5)-between young and middle-aged individuals with and without carotid artery plaques, and to explore the association between these cytokines and plaque formation.

Methods

This retrospective study included 1581 consecutive inpatients, ages ranging from 18 to 65 years, inclusive, from the Cardiology Department of the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University who had undergone cytokine testing between October 2022 and November 2024. Following the application of specific inclusion and exclusion criteria, 1163 patients were ultimately selected for the final analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria were strictly limited to: age 18–65 years, focusing on the core research population to exclude the interference of elderly degenerative vascular diseases on carotid artery plaques, ensuring that the research results are more in line with the characteristics of the association between inflammation and plaques in young and middle-aged populations, completion of carotid artery ultrasound examination (independently performed by two experienced ultrasound physicians with consistent results, referring to the “Chinese Guidelines for Carotid Artery Ultrasound Examination in Healthy Physical Examination Population [15]” to ensure the objectivity and reproducibility of carotid artery plaque diagnosis and provide a reliable basis for grouping) and detection of circulating cytokines. The exclusion criteria are as follows: First, patients with acute cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, including acute myocardial infarction with onset less than 28 days (according to the “2019 Chinese Society of Cardiology (CSC) guidelines for the diagnosis and management of patients with ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction” [16]), moderate to severe valvular heart disease, cardiomyopathy, severe arrhythmias such as sustained ventricular tachycardia, myopericarditis with onset less than 1 month, aortic dissection, and acute pulmonary embolism (all confirmed by imaging or laboratory tests). Such diseases can activate systemic acute inflammatory responses, leading to abnormal increases in factors such as IL-6 and TNF-α, which may mask the underlying inflammatory state related to carotid artery plaques and introduce confounding bias; second, patients with severe hepatic and renal insufficiency (liver function Child-Pugh C class or alaninetransaminase/aspartate transaminase >3 times the upper limit of normal [17], renal function eGFR<30 mL・min−1・1.73 m−2, according to the “Guidelines for Screening, Diagnosis, and Prevention of Chronic Kidney Disease” [18]). Because the liver is the main organ for the metabolism of cytokines, and kidney disease is often accompanied by chronic uremia-related inflammation, both may lead to distorted levels of circulating cytokines and interfere with “inflammatory-plaque” association analysis; third, active inflammatory/infectious diseases (such as pneumonia, urinary tract infection) and active autoimmune diseases (such as rheumatoid arthritis and systemic lupus erythematosus, according to the “2020 Chinese Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of Systemic Lupus Erythematosus” [19]). The former directly stimulates immune cells to release proinflammatory cytokines, and the latter causes long-term immune disorders and imbalance of proinflammatory/anti-inflammatory cytokines. Both are unrelated to the chronic inflammatory mechanism of carotid artery plaques and are strong confounding factors; fourth, patients diagnosed with new malignant tumors within the past 5 years or currently receiving anti-tumor treatment, because tumor-related inflammation and anti-tumor treatment may lead to abnormal secretion of inflammatory cytokines, and 5 years is a critical period for tumor recurrence. Excluding such patients can avoid the interference of tumor-related factors on the research results; fifth, patients with incomplete clinical data, including missing basic information such as age, gender, blood pressure, or incomplete carotid ultrasound report, or missing inflammatory cytokine detection data. The lack of basic information will lead to the inability to adjust plaque-related risk factors, and the lack of core indicators will affect grouping and statistical analysis, reducing the reliability of research data.

Clinical data were extracted from all medical records by trained clinicians blinded to the study's objectives. Data collected included age, gender, history of diabetes or hypertension, smoking status, height and weight. Peripheral venous blood samples were collected from patients in the early morning after an overnight fast and analyzed immediately. Serum cytokine concentrations were measured using a liquid suspension chip detector (Luminex-MAGPIX). Cytokine detection was performed via multiplex microsphere flow immunofluorescence luminescence and analyzed using a 12-in-1 human cytokine assay kit (Biopredia Biological Technology, Jiangsu, China).

Body mass index (BMI) was computed by dividing an individual's weight in kilograms by the square of their height in meters. Hypertension was diagnosed based on the following criteria: a confirmed diagnosis by a healthcare professional, current use of antihypertensive medication, or a systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mmHg or a diastolic blood pressure of ≥90 mmHg [20]. Diabetes was defined according to the "Guideline for the Prevention and Treatment of Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus in China (2020 Edition)" as meeting one of the following criteria: a fasting plasma glucose level of ≥7.0 mmol/L, a random plasma glucose level of ≥11.1 mmol/L, a 2-h plasma glucose level of ≥11.1 mmol/L following a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test, or a glycated hemoglobin level of ≥6.5 % [21]. According to the World Health Organization definition, a smoker was defined as an individual who had smoked continuously or cumulatively for six months or more throughout their lifetime [22].

Carotid artery atherosclerosis was assessed using a Dutch PHILIPS ie 33 color ultrasound diagnostic instrument with a high-frequency probe (intermediate frequency 8 MHz). Examination protocol: (1) Measurement of Intima-Media Thickness (IMT): Patients were positioned supine, and a longitudinal scan was performed along the carotid artery from its origin. Bilateral common carotid arteries, bilateral carotid artery bifurcations, extracranial segments of bilateral internal carotid arteries, and external carotid arteries were sequentially examined. IMT was measured as the distance from the intimal surface of the vessel wall through the media to the interface between the media and the adventitia. (2) Assessment of atherosclerotic plaques: Two-dimensional and color flow imaging were used to identify plaques. Plaques were defined as: local IMT values ≥ 1.30 mm as measured by two-dimensional ultrasound, or a thickness ≥0.50 mm greater than adjacent sites, or 1.5 times the IMT value of adjacent sites, or color images demonstrating a color flow filling defect within the vascular lumen, with a defect area ≥10 mm. The average of the IMT values from both sides was used as the IMT value.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.0. Normality of continuous variables was assessed. Non-normally distributed data were presented as medians (interquartile ranges), and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for group comparisons. Circulating inflammatory cytokines underwent log transformation. Independent samples t-tests were used to compare cytokine levels between groups. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages, and the chi-square test was used for between-group comparisons. A logistic regression model was used to analyze factors associated with carotid artery plaques. Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic binary regression model. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Confounding factors in the adjusted model included those with significant differences in the univariate logistic regression. The variance inflation factor was used to assess multicollinearity among confounding factors. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 presents a comparison of baseline characteristics between two cohorts: individuals without plaques (n = 528) and those with plaques (n = 635). In this study, the no - plaque group had a median age of 56 years (interquartile range (IQR): 51–60 years) and the plaque group had a median age of 57 years (IQR: 52–61 years), with no significant difference (P = 0.078). Males accounted for 57.5 % in the no - plaque group and 61.2 % in the plaque group, showing no intergroup difference (P = 0.202). The smoking rate was 33.9 % in the no - plaque group and 39.3 % in the plaque group, with a significant difference (P = 0.044). Hypertension was present in 57.5 % of the no - plaque group and 63.7 % of the plaque group, also showing a significant difference (P = 0.031). No significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of diabetes, BMI, fasting blood glucose, blood lipids, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR).

Table 2 displays the results of both univariate and multivariate analyses examining the relationship between each variable and the presence of carotid plaques. In the univariate analysis, age, diabetes, BMI, fasting blood glucose, triglycerides, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, eGFR, Log IL-10, and hypertension were all significantly associated with the presence of carotid plaques (all P < 0.05). The multivariate analysis revealed that fasting blood glucose, Log IL-10, and hypertension remained statistically significant. The β value for fasting blood glucose was 0.017 (SE = 0.182), with an OR of 1.015 (95 % CI: 1.001–1.032; P = 0.012). For Log IL-10, the OR was 0.482 (95 % CI: 0.263–0.883; P = 0.018), indicating a protective effect. Hypertension had an OR of 1.152 (95 % CI: 1.087–1.427; P = 0.005), highlighting its role as a significant risk factor for carotid plaque formation.

As illustrated in Fig. 3A, in the cohort of young and middle-aged women, the unadjusted, age-adjusted, and fully adjusted effect estimates were all below 1, with CIs that did not encompass 1.0. This observation suggests a negative association between Log IL-10 and carotid plaques under various adjustment conditions, implying a potential risk reduction for carotid plaque formation. Conversely, Fig. 3B demonstrates that in young and middle-aged men, the unadjusted effect estimate was slightly above 1, with a CI including 1.0. Following age adjustment and full adjustment for covariates, the effect estimate approached 1, and the CI continued to include 1.0. These findings indicate that the association between Log IL-10 and carotid plaques in men was not statistically significant after accounting for relevant confounders.

Discussion

This investigation examined a cohort of young and middle-aged subjects, demonstrating a significant association between the presence of carotid plaques and multiple risk factors. The prevalence of smoking and a history of hypertension was notably elevated in individuals with carotid plaques compared to those without, suggesting a potential link between these factors and carotid plaque development. Devesa et al. previously reported that tobacco use is a major risk factor for premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) in adults aged 20 to 39 [23]. Tobacco-derived substances can induce vascular damage, thereby increasing ASCVD risk [24]. Similarly, hypertension is a critical risk factor, as chronic elevated blood pressure can injure the vascular endothelium and promote plaque formation [25]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing modifiable risk factors, such as smoking and hypertension, to mitigate the risk of carotid plaque development and subsequent cardiovascular events in younger populations.

Furthermore, the study revealed significant differences in circulating IL-10 levels between subjects with and without carotid plaques, with higher IL-10 levels observed in those without plaques. Multivariate logistic regression analysis confirmed that fasting blood glucose and a history of hypertension were independent positive predictors of carotid plaque formation, whereas circulating IL-10 levels were identified as an independent negative predictor. In young individuals, chronic hyperglycemia may activate pathways such as the polyol and protein kinase C pathways [26], leading to endothelial cell damage, monocyte adhesion, accelerated lipid deposition, and the progression of carotid atherosclerosis [27]. Additionally, elevated blood glucose can increase blood viscosity and alter hemodynamics, further promoting atherosclerotic plaque formation [28]. In older adults, the presence of multiple comorbidities can synergize with hyperglycemia, accelerating carotid atherosclerosis [29]. For example, the combination of hyperglycemia and hyperlipidemia significantly increases the likelihood of lipid plaque formation [30]. Early detection and active management of elevated fasting blood glucose are therefore critical in young individuals. Effective glycemic control can mitigate the risk of carotid atherosclerosis. While younger patients typically exhibit superior vascular elasticity and compliance [31], potentially buffering the effects of blood pressure fluctuations, suboptimal adherence to antihypertensive therapies may occur, as the absence of overt symptoms can lead to the neglect of blood pressure control [32]. This can result in sustained hypertension, thereby accelerating the progression of carotid atherosclerosis. Early intervention and improved management of these risk factors are essential to prevent long-term cardiovascular sequelae.

IL-10, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of carotid atherosclerosis [33]. Over two decades ago, Mallat et al. demonstrated the protective effects of IL-10 against atherosclerosis in animal models [34]. IL-10 may inhibit carotid plaque formation by suppressing inflammatory responses and modulating immune cell function. For example, IL-10 can reduce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by immune cells such as macrophages, thereby minimizing inflammation-mediated vascular damage and reducing plaque formation [35]. This study found that IL-10's protective effect against carotid atherosclerosis exhibits significant sex-specificity: elevated circulating IL-10 levels in women are associated with a reduced risk of the disease (consistent with previous studies showing an inverse correlation between elevated IL-10 levels and increased carotid IMT in premenopausal women [36]). However, elevated IL-10 levels in postmenopausal women with coronary atherosclerosis are associated with an increased risk of future cardiovascular events [37], suggesting that its effects vary dynamically with disease progression. Mechanistically, this sex difference may be related to the female hormonal environment: estrogen can inhibit disease by upregulating IL-10 expression and enhancing its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties [38]. While androgens in men can promote IL-10 expression in some immune cells [39], the intensity of this regulation is far less than that of estrogen, leading to a less pronounced protective effect of IL-10 in men. Furthermore, studies have shown that the IL-10 signaling pathway may be associated with reduced plaque stability and an increased risk of recurrent cardiovascular events [40]. Plaque vulnerability has many typical characteristics, such as a larger lipid pool, a thinner fibrous cap, a higher density of macrophages in the fibrous cap, and the presence of neovascularization in the plaque, all of which are associated with the responsible lesion [41]. A study by Gasbarrino et al. further revealed that there are significant gender differences in plaque characteristics: compared with women, male patients have a higher proportion of unstable plaques, which is characterized by more intraplaque hemorrhage, a larger lipid core, and a more pronounced local inflammatory response [42]. However, it remains unclear whether IL-10 acts as a key regulator in this process, thereby influencing the gender differences in plaque stability.

Interestingly, no elevation in peripheral blood pro-inflammatory cytokine levels (such as IL-1β, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, TNF-α and IL-17A) was observed in either the group with carotid artery plaques or the plaque-free group. In contrast, Manukyan et al. found an association between proinflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) and carotid atherosclerosis in patients with resistant hypertension [43]. This phenomenon may be attributed to the following reasons: (1) The core function of IL-10 is not only to suppress an erupted cytokine storm but, more importantly, to continuously inhibit pro-inflammatory signaling pathways at a low baseline level, thereby preventing excessive activation of the immune system. (2) The peripheral blood cytokines measured in the study reflect the systemic inflammatory status. Although pro-inflammatory cytokines may be elevated locally within plaques, their changes might not be detectable in peripheral blood due to dilution effects and the presence of local barriers. (3) The included study subjects are different. This study included participants aged ≤65 years, whose inflammatory state is mostly in a stable “ow-grade smolderin” plateau phase. IL-10 levels may reflect the body's ability to maintain this homeostasis; in individuals with plaques but preserved IL-10 function, systemic inflammation might thus be kept at a relatively low level. It should be noted that even if the levels of proinflammatory cytokines in peripheral blood are not elevated, they may still work synergistically with risk factors such as hyperlipidemia, hypertension, and obesity to accelerate the progression of carotid atherosclerosis [44]. Therefore, young and middle-aged people need to regulate their inflammatory levels through a healthy lifestyle such as a balanced diet, increased physical exercise, smoking cessation, and alcohol restriction, to provide a basic guarantee for the prevention and management of carotid atherosclerosis.

Limitations

This study has certain limitations, which are mainly reflected in the following aspects: First, the evaluation dimension of the core outcome indicators is relatively simple, and only “the presence or absence of carotid artery plaque” is used as the judgment standard. The pathological stability characteristics of the plaque are not further analyzed in depth - for example, the volume of the lipid core and the thickness of the fibrous cap in the plaque are not detected, and it is not clear whether there are key pathological manifestations such as calcification or ulcers that affect the risk of plaque rupture. This may lead to an incomplete assessment of the clinical significance of the plaque. Second, the study has not fully controlled the interference of potential confounding factors, such as whether the subjects have received antithrombotic treatment, statin treatment or anti-inflammatory treatment; In addition, other studies have demonstrated that the monocyte chemoattractant protein 1 (MCP-1) gene is highly expressed in plaque samples obtained after endarterectomy [45], and its expression level is closely correlated with histopathological markers of plaque vulnerability [46]. However, this study did not include chemokines such as MCP-1 in the analysis, and therefore could not explore their association with variables such as carotid atherosclerosis in this study population, which is also a limitation of the study. Finally, although this study focused on young and middle-aged individuals, the sample size was insufficient, particularly limiting our ability to explore potential differences between young adults (e.g., <45 years old) and middle-aged individuals (e.g., 45–65 years old).

Conclusions

This study, the first to focus on a young and middle-aged cohort, clarified the importance of traditional risk factors such as smoking, hypertension, and fasting blood glucose, and revealed a specific protective effect of IL-10 in women. Our results suggest that in young people, measuring peripheral blood proinflammatory cytokines alone may not be sufficient to assess atherosclerosis risk, and that IL-10, particularly in women, may serve as a more sensitive indicator of immune regulatory function.
