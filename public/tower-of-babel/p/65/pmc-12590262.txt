Correlation between gingival fibroblast cell viability and degree of conversion of resin composites at different light-curing tip distances
Authors: Atieh Hashemian, Mahshid Hodjat, Marjan Behroozibakhsh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the effect of light-curing tip distance on the degree of conversion (DC), as well as on the cell viability. The study also aimed to assess the correlation between DC% and cell viability at different light-curing tip distances.Materials and methodsNanocomposite discs were cured using an LED light-curing unit at distances of 0 mm (G0), 2 mm (G2), 4 mm (G4), 6 mm (G6), and 8 mm (G8) for 20 s. The DC was measured using ATR-FTIR analysis. Cell viability was assessed through MTT assay on human gingival fibroblasts. The results were analyzed using one-way ANOVA and Pearson correlation analysis.ResultsThe DC at the surface was significantly higher than the bottom of the samples in all groups (p < 0.001). The DC on both surfaces decreased as the distance between the light-curing unit and the sample surface increased. Moreover, with an increase in distance, the cell viability decreased. This difference was statistically significant in all groups, except for the G0 and G2 groups (p < 0.001). The results of Pearson correlation analysis showed a positive and statistically significant correlation between cell viability and DC% at both surfaces (p < 0.05). Furthermore, a negative and statistically significant correlation was observed between tip distance and DC% at both top and bottom surfaces as well as cell viability(p < 0.05).ConclusionIncreasing the distance from the light-curing tip adversely affects composite polymerization and biocompatibility, likely due to insufficient curing and subsequent monomer and nanoparticle release. This study emphasizes the importance of optimal curing conditions.

Introduction

Dental composites are widely used in both direct and indirect restorations. These materials have demonstrated high long-term success rates. However, several factors can contribute to treatment failure, including the caries-prone oral environment, the type of composite used, restoration fractures, and aesthetic concerns related to composite discoloration.1

The degree of conversion of a composite (DC) is a key factor affecting treatment success. DC refers to the percentage of the carbon double bonds converting to single carbon-carbon bonds.2 During the polymerization process, most monomers are converted into polymer, though some monomers remain unreacted within the resin matrix.3,4 High DC% is associated with improved physical and mechanical properties, color stability, and enhanced biocompatibility.5 Several factors, such as the composite's shade, curing time, cavity depth and location, temperature, filler type, and the distance of the light-curing tip from the composite surface, affect the DC%.6, 7, 8

Previous studies have reported a reduction in light intensity reaching the composite as the distance between the composite and the light-curing tip increases.9,10 One reason for this distance is the height and angle of cusps and the depth of prepared cavities.11 Studies have reported this distance, in case, in deep Class 2 proximal cavities, reaching up to 9 mm.12 In the study by Price et al., a 6 mm distance from the light-curing tip led to a 50–77 % reduction in light intensity, depending on the type of light-curing device used.13 This reduction has been shown in multiple studies to correlate with a decrease in surface hardness and a lower DC of the composite.14, 15, 16, 17 A lower DC% can lead to an increase in the release of unreacted monomers from the composite, eventually decreasing its biocompatibility.18,19 This reduction becomes especially important when considering the proximity of the deeper composite layers to the pulp and gingiva in the depth of proximal cavities. The aim of this study is to investigate the impact of the distance between the light source and the composite surface on the DC% at both the top and bottom surfaces of the composite, as well as on cellular viability. The study also aimed to assess the correlation between DC% and cell viability at different light-curing tip distances.

Materials and Methods

Sample preparation

In this study, a universal composite (3M Espe Filtek Z350 XT Composite, Dentin shade A2) was used. Disk shaped samples (2 × 6 mm) were cured at distances of 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mm from the light-curing tip, with 4 samples in each group. The samples were cured using an LED light-curing unit (O-Star: Cordless curing light – WOODPECKER) for 20 s at an intensity of 1200 mW/cm2 and in a polycarbonate mold. Based on the light curing tip distance the experimental groups were assigned to five groups including G0, G2, G4, G6 and G8. The distance from the light tip was adjusted using metal pieces with 2 mm high, as shown in Fig. 1. To ensure consistent light intensity during curing, the light intensity was measured using a radiometer at each curing distance. The samples then were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The degree of conversion of the composite samples was measured. The biocompatibility of the dental composites was assessed using the MTT cell viability assay. Degree of conversion

The degree of conversion of the composite samples was measured using Attenuated Total Reflectance-Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR, Nicolet iS50, USA). Briefly, the discs were cleaned with a cotton swab soaked in alcohol and placed on the ATR crystal. Absorption peaks were obtained separately from the top and bottom surfaces of each sample. Additionally, 1 g of uncured composite was separately placed on the ATR crystal, and absorption peaks for uncured resin were recorded under similar conditions. To calculate the degree of conversion, the peak heights for the aliphatic carbon-carbon bond (1638 cm−1) and the carbonyl bonds (used as a reference peak) (1720 cm−1) were measured and recorded using the Omnic software. The degree of conversion was then calculated using the following formula.20:DC%=100−peakheightofcuredaliphaticC=CpeakheightofcuredcarbonyleC=OpeakheightofuncuredaliphaticC=CpeakheightofuncuredcarbonyleC=O×100

Biocompatibility

The biocompatibility of the dental composites was assessed according to ISO 10993 standards. For this purpose, the MTT cell viability assay was used for evaluating cell viability. Gingival fibroblast cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % antibiotics. The composite samples were incubated in the culture medium (3 cm2/ml) for 24 h, and the released materials were extracted. The cells were then cultured in 96-well plates, and after 24 h, the sample extracts were added to the cells. After 24 h of treatment, the MTT solution was added to the wells, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 for 4 h. After incubation, the formazan crystals were dissolved using DMSO, and the optical absorbance was measured using an ELISA reader at a wavelength of 570 nm. The cell viability was calculated and recorded based on the optical absorbance percentage of the treated samples compared to the control group.

Data analysis

The normality of the obtained results was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Then, the results of each test were compared using one-way ANOVA. Furthermore, the correlation between the DC on the top and bottom surfaces of the composite discs and the cell viability percentage and distance and DC at both sides was separately analyzed using Pearson correlation, and the correlation coefficient was reported.

Discussion

Many studies have reported a decrease in light intensity and degree of conversion as the distance from the light-curing unit increases. This is particularly significant in deep proximal cavities and adjacent the gingiva, where maintaining optimal curing conditions is challenging. Furthermore, previous research has shown that lower DC% lead to higher levels of residual monomer release in composites, which can negatively affect cell viability.21 These released monomers have been associated with various adverse biological effects, such as cytotoxicity, inflammation, and allergic reactions.

In this study, the Filtek Z350 composite (3M) was cured at distances ranging from 0 to 8 mm for 20 s and the DC% was measured at the surface and bottom of the samples using ATR-FFTIR. The results showed that, at all distances, the DC% at the composite surface was significantly higher than at the bottom of the composite. Moreover, as the distance from the light-curing unit increased, the DC decreased; however, this decrease was not statistically significant. The relationship between the distance of the light-curing unit tip and composite surface curing has been widely studied. It has been established that, for light-curing units with spherical light emission, the light intensity decreases with the square of the distance.22,23 In the study by Lehman et al.,24 the intensity of light from the Woodpecker C cure light-curing unit decreased from 1018 mW/cm2 at a distance of 0 mm–721 mW/cm2 at 6 mm, and further to 602 mW/cm2 at 8 mm. It has also been mentioned, the statistical analysis showed a significant difference in light intensity at 0 mm and 2 mm compared to 6 mm and 8 mm.24, 25, 26 However, the effect of distance on light intensity varies among different light-curing units, with some units maintaining better light distribution than others.23 In this study, the light-curing unit used has a light emission angle of 15.8°, and it was observed that at a distance of 8 mm, the light intensity decreased to about 313 mW/cm2.27 Previous studies have indicated that a minimum light intensity of 300 mW/cm2 is required for proper composite curing.28,29

Although in our study no statistically significant difference in the DC was found between different distances, the DC decreased from 44.57 % at 0 mm to 32.99 % at 8 mm. Previous investigations have demonstrated that up to 10 % of residual monomers in dental composites may leach into the oral environment.30 This release can have various effects in the oral cavity, such as inflammation, allergic reactions, and cytotoxicity.31,32 Furthermore, these remaining monomers can act as plasticizers, decreasing the clinical longevity of the composite and accelerating material degradation, finally leading to the release of other harmful substances from the composite structure.21,33

In a recent study, the extraction of composite monomers was performed within 24 h after curing. According to Ferracane,34 around 50 % of the remaining monomers in the composite are released within 3–24 h post-curing. According to the manufacturer's data, the Filtek Z350 resin system contains monomers such as BIS-GMA, BIS-EMA, UDMA, PEGDMA, and TEGDMA, all of which can be released in organic solvents post-curing.35 Previous studies have reported the release of UDMA and Ethyl-4-dimethylamino benzoate from composites in artificial saliva within the first 24 h after curing.36 It has also been reported that, the release of TEGDMA has been correlated with the degree of conversion.37

In our study, cell viability decreased as the distance from the light-curing unit increased. This decrease was statistically significant at all distances except for G0 and G2 groups. It seems that the reduction in the DC due to increased distance between the light source and the composite led to higher monomer release, which consequently resulted in a decrease in cell viability. These findings support the importance of achieving a highDC to minimize the release of residual monomers and improve the biocompatibility of dental composites.

TEGDMA and UDMA can have cytotoxic and genotoxic effects on dental pulp cells and gingival tissues. Studies have shown that these monomers can induce apoptosis, reduce cell viability, and cause inflammation in adjacent tissues. Schweikl and colleagues reported that TEGDMA can lead to cell death by interfering with mitochondrial function and inducing oxidative stress.38 Furthermore, UDMA can activate inflammatory pathways and increase the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α.

The monomers released from nanocomposites can enter cells through endocytosis or phagocytosis, disrupting cellular function by interfering with the cell membrane and organelles. Additionally, some nanoparticles can induce inflammatory responses and toxicity associated with autophagy by disrupting lysosomal function. Oxidative stress caused by ROS may also lead to DNA strand breaks and genetic mutations.39 Some studies have shown that nanoparticles may interact directly with the cell nucleus and alter the expression of target genes. Besides, released monomers and nanoparticles can trigger chronic inflammation by activating pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6.39 In the present study, increasing the distance to 4 mm significantly reduced cell viability compared to distances of 0 and 2 mm. However, this viability remained above 70 %, which is the biocompatibility threshold according to the ISO standard.

However, with increasing the distance to 6 and 8 mm, cell viability decreased to 65.5 and 58, respectively, which falls below the biocompatibility threshold according to the ISO standard for the composite. This reduction in biocompatibility may be partially attributed to the incomplete curing of the material, which not only leads to the release of residual monomers but may also facilitate the release of nanoparticles such as silica and zirconia from dental nanocomposites like Filtek Z350 XT. Although these materials benefit from nanofiller structures and optimal polymerization, insufficient curing can induce oxidative stress and cellular inflammation.40

Based on the findings of this study and similar investigations regarding the factors contributing to the reduction in the degree of conversion—and consequently the decreased biocompatibility—of resin composites, further studies on other types of composites and different light-curing units appear to be of significant importance. Moreover, it is recommended to employ more specialized biocompatibility assays that better simulate clinical conditions, such as the dentin barrier test and agar diffusion test.

Conclusion

Based on the results, increasing the distance between the light-curing tip and the composite resin adversely affects composite polymerization and biocompatibility, likely due to insufficient curing and consequent release of monomer and nanoparticles. Additionally, cell viability and DC% showed a positive and statistically significant correlation on both top and bottom surfaces of dental composite. This study emphasizes the importance of optimal curing conditions for dental resin composites.
