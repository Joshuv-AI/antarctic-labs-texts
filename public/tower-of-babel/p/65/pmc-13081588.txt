Iron concentration in the ventral pallidum of chronic migraine with medication-overuse headache
Authors: Xiaopei Xu, Jiahui He, Mengting Zhou, Xiao Wu, Fangling Zhao, Jingxin Fu, Xiao Luo, Kaicheng Li, Qingze Zeng, Chao Wang, Xiaojun Guan, Peiyu Huang, Minming Zhang, Kaiming Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChronic migraine (CM) imposes a significant disease burden and frequently co-occurs with medication-overuse headache (MOH). MOH is a secondary headache disorder occurring especially in individuals with primary migraine and associated with excessive analgesic use. Impaired iron homeostasis may be related to the medication overuse behavior, however, its’ pathophysiology is complex and incompletely understood. This study examines regional iron deposition in the subcortical nuclei in two comparisons: (1) controls versus chronic migraine patients; and (2) CM patients with medication-overuse headache (wMOH) versus those without MOH (woMOH). Furthermore, it investigates the association between iron deposition and disease outcomes.MethodsWe recruited 38 controls and 72 CM patients (40 wMOH, 32 woMOH). All participants underwent MRI and assessments of migraine duration, analgesic use, and health metrics. CM patients with MOH were further evaluated for one-year relapse rates and Severity of Dependence Scale scores. Quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) was used to measure iron content in subcortical regions, and correlations with clinical variables. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the QSM’s predictive value for MOH relapse.ResultsCM patients with MOH had lower iron content in the ventral pallidum (VeP, 0.178 ± 0.112 ppm vs. 0.183 ± 0.082 ppm, p < 0.001) compared to those without MOH. Notably, VeP iron content negatively correlated with analgesic excessive use (r = -0.557, p < 0.001) and relapse rates (r = -0.360, p < 0.05). ROC analyses demonstrated significant discriminative capacity for relapse prediction (VeP: AUC = 0.900, 95% CI [0.773–0.999], p < 0.001) in wMOH.ConclusionsThe reduced iron in subcortical nuclei, especially the VeP, relates to analgesic excessive use and relapse in MOH. This insight enhances our understanding of MOH’s neurobiological basis and supports the potential development of QSM-based biomarkers for relapse risk prediction.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-026-02321-2.

Background

Chronic migraine (CM) affects 1.4–2.2% of the global population, representing a substantial public health challenge due to its severe disease burden [1]. These patients suffer from markedly reduced health-related quality of life and higher levels of disability relative to their counterparts with episodic migraine (EM) [2]. Moreover, excessive analgesic use can exacerbate migraine frequency and induce the transition from episodic to chronic headache or medication-overuse headache (MOH). According to the International Classification of Headache Disorders 3rd edition (ICHD-3) [3], MOH is defined as a secondary headache disorder resulting from the prolonged use of multiple or potent analgesics, and nearly three-quarters of patients with CM experience medication overuse [4]. The Global Burden of Disease study estimates that MOH affects approximately 59 million people worldwide, ranking it among the top 20 causes of global disability [5]. Despite its profound impact on functional capacity and quality of life, MOH remains underdiagnosed and inadequately managed in clinical settings. MOH is a multifaceted disorder shaped by genetic, environmental, and biopsychosocial factors. A pre-existing primary headache disorder is a prerequisite for MOH development, with migraine being a prominent risk factor [6]. Our prior research revealed significantly greater iron accumulation in subcortical regions among migraine patients compared to controls (CNs) [7]. Furthermore, patients with CM exhibited a greater volume of iron deposits in subcortical regions than those with EM [7].

A significant proportion of MOH cases originate from migraine, with a notably higher prevalence among CM patients [8]. Recent advances in migraine research have elucidated the iron deposition-related pathophysiology of MOH. Welch et al. reported elevated iron accumulation in the periaqueductal gray (PAG) in CM patients, suggesting a selective impairment of iron homeostasis in migraine patients, potentially driven by recurrent migraine attacks [9]. Dysregulated function of subcortical structures, including the PAG, thalamus, hypothalamus, amygdala, and insula, is increasingly implicated in migraine pathophysiology. During the induction of a migraine attack, altered subcortical networks reflect multisite dysfunctional plasticity across sensory, autonomic, and affective circuits [10]. Chronic exposure to anti-migraine medications, such as triptans, disrupts the balance between 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) and dopamine (DA) systems [11]. Furthermore, inflammatory vasoactive peptides trigger meningeal vessel dilation during migraine attacks [12] enhancing iron permeability, and disturbing iron homeostasis [13]. This excessive iron accumulation promotes the persistent production of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species [14], potentially impairing dopamine synthesis [15] and causing broader harm to the nervous system.

However, the neurobiological mechanisms underlying MOH remain elusive. MOH is hypothesized to be closely associated with reversible morphological and functional brain changes related to the reward circuitry [16]. The incentive-sensitization theory suggests that dopamine (DA) drives the desire (“wanting”) for a drug, distinct from the pleasure (“liking”) it provides [17]. This framework posits that patients overusing analgesics may continue seeking these medications despite diminished pain relief, propelled by psychological dependence and pharmacological tolerance, which reduces the drug’s rewarding effects. Behavioral sensitization, mediated by cortical, striatal, and other subcortical structures, is under investigation as a potential mechanism. This process is defined by a progressive and persistent increase in behavioral responses (e.g., locomotion, stereotypy) following repeated, intermittent exposure to a stimulus, most commonly psychostimulant drugs or stress [18]. Neuroimaging studies have revealed aberrant intra-network connectivity in the prefrontal-subcortical limbic system in MOH [19], alongside reduced neuronal activity in the operculum, insula, and spinal trigeminal nucleus following drug withdrawal [20]. Other findings include shorter T2 values in the putamen (Pu), globus pallidus (GP), and red nucleus (RN) [21], as well as increased iron accumulation in the bilateral substantia nigra (SN) in MOH patients [22]. Exploring subcortical iron homeostasis in MOH could deepen our understanding of these mechanisms and support the development of targeted therapies.

This study employs quantitative susceptibility mapping (QSM) to thoroughly examine regional iron deposition in subcortical nuclei of CM patients with MOH (wMOH) compared to those without MOH (woMOH) and its association with disease outcomes, which provides evidence for the hypothesis that iron deposition is associated with excessive drug use.

Methods

Participants

This study was approved by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (IRB2021001248), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. From September 2021 to February 2024, individuals diagnosed with CM, CM with MOH (wMOH) or CM without MOH (woMOH) according to ICHD-3 criteria were selected for inclusion.

Patients were recruited based on the following inclusion criteria: (1) Age: 18–70 years; (2) Confirmed diagnosis is CM with MOH or without MOH; (3) History of migraine greater than 1 year and CM more than 3 months. Subjects were excluded if they had: (1) high blood pressure; (2) coronary disease; (3) diabetes mellitus; (4) hypercholesterolemia; (5) infectious diseases; (6) chronic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, systemic lupus, and other autoimmune conditions; (7) severe systemic diseases; (8) pregnancy or lactation; (9) obesity (body mass index > 30 kg/m2); (10) smoking habit (within the previous 12 months); and (11) recent consumption of antiplatelet drugs or vasoactive drugs (> 4 times the medium half-life of the active substance); (12) Current or prior presence of addiction to any other drug (including psychotropic drugs, anesthetic drugs) or food (including caffeine, alcohol); (13) other primary headaches (including tension headache and cluster headache). Eventually, a total number of 72 patients who met the criteria participated in current study. Age and sex matched controls were recruited from the community if they were free of any headache or psychiatric disorder. Eventually, a total number of 38 CNs who met the first included criteria and all excluded criterias participated in current study.

Clinical assessment

All subjects underwent a medical interview including demographic data (age, sex) and personal family histories. For migraine patients, disease duration (measured in years from first symptoms) were registered. The 6-item Headache Impact Test (HIT-6), Migraine Disability Assessment (MIDAS), Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7 (GAD-7) and The Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) were performed to measure the degree of migraine-related functional disability, Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was performed to assess sleep quality of migraine patients over the past month, and Severity of Dependence Scale (SDS) was also performed to assess the degree of drug excessive use of migraine patients. In addition, each MOH patient was prospectively followed up for one year. Investigators determined a patient’s relapse by outpatient appointment or telephone follow-up, according to the MOH criteria of the ICHD-3. Relapse is defined as meeting the diagnostic criteria for MOH again within one year after discontinuing the overused medication. All MOH patients were divided into a relapse group and a no-relapse group. In the present study, the relapse group was defined as patients who were re-diagnosed with MOH after achieving freedom from its post-treatment.

Image acquisition

All the MR images were acquired using a United Imaging MR790 3.0T scanner (Shanghai, China). T1 weighted images were acquired with a 3D fast spoiled gradient-echo sequence; the parameters were: TR = 6.9 ms, TE = 2.9 ms, flip angle = 9°, inversion time = 1000 ms, field of view = 256*240 mm, voxel size = 1*1*1 mm3, 208 sagittal slices. T2 weighted images were acquired with a MATRIX (modulated flip angle technique in refocused imaging with extended echo train, equivalent to CUBE for GE, SPACE for Siemens, and VISTA for Philips) sequence; the parameters were: TR = 3000 ms, TE = 405.46 ms, echo train length = 180, field of view = 256*240 mm, voxel size = 0.8*0.8*0.8 mm3, 208 sagittal slices. The susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) sequence was acquired using a multi-echo 3D gradient echo sequence (GRE) with following parameters: TR = 36.5 ms, first TE = 3.1 ms, last TE = 30.4 ms, number of echo = 8, bandwidth = 350 kHz, flip angle = 15°, field of view (FOV) = 240*240 mm, voxel size = 0.75*0.75*2 mm3. For quality control, a two-step procedure was implemented: first, all scans were visually inspected for motion artifacts during acquisition, and scans were repeated if quality was not satisfactory. Second, a post-acquisition quality check was performed, and data with significant motion artifacts, such as ghosting or blurring, were excluded from the final analysis.

Image processing

The QSM images were reconstructed from GRE data using the SEPIA (SuscEptibility mapping PIpeline tool for phAse images) toolbox [23] in MATLAB program (The Mathworks Inc., Natick MA). Brain extraction was performed on whole-brain magnitude data based on the BET tool in Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging of the Brain (FMRIB) Software Library v6.0 (FSL; Oxford University, UK; https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/FSL) package from the MEDI toolbox. The phase images were unwrapped with SEGUE [24]. After unwrapping, the background field was removed with the regularisation-enabled SHARP (RESHARP) [25] filtering method. Lastly, magnetic susceptibility was quantitatively calculated using MEDI [26] and QSM images were generated.

The magnitude image, in the same space as the QSM image, was used for registration and was obtained by summing the squares of magnitude images among different TEs. 3D T1, T2 and magnitude images were skull-stripped using BET in FSL. By using citatlaskit (https://github.com/jmtyszka/citatlaskit) tool based on Advanced Normalization Tools v2.1 (ANTs; http://stnava.github.io/ANTs/), SyN multimodal warping was performed using joint T1 and T2 cost function to transform the high-resolution probabilistic subcortical brain nuclei atlas to individual space. The subcortical nuclei atlas in native space was manually refined by a neuroradiologist (X. Xu) with 4 years’ experience to ensure the segmentation precision. Eventually, the mean QSM value of regions of interest (ROI) in native subcortical nuclei based on the human subcortical brain nuclei atlas was extracted to quantify the tissue susceptibility, indicating iron deposition in certain subcortical nucleus (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

Sex was recorded as a binary variable. Age, disease duration, HIT-6, MIDAS, PHQ-9, GAD-7 and PSQI were recorded as continuous variables, and one-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to check the normality of all continuous variables. Demographics, clinical variables, and regional iron-related metrics were compared between CN and CM subjects, as well as between wMOH and woMOH, using independent samples t-test for continuous variables and the chi-squared test for proportions. The variables were tested for normal distribution and continuous or ordinal scale, and partial correlation analysis was conducted to detect the potential relationship between regional iron-related metric and clinical variables. Bonferroni correction for the problem of multiple comparisons in multiple-region level, and to further control for the type I error was performed. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied a global Bonferroni correction across all primary analyses. Given that we performed two group comparisons (CN vs. CM, and wMOH vs. woMOH) across 26 predefined cerebral regions, a total of 52 statistical tests were conducted. Therefore, the family-wise error rate was controlled by setting the significance threshold at P < 0.05 / 52 = 0.00096. Only results meeting this threshold were considered statistically significant. Sex and age were included as covariates in the ROC analysis, and the results containing these variables were corrected by bootstrap. SPSS 22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL) was used for all the statistical analyses mentioned above. Continuous variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median (interquartile range) otherwise.

Results

Demographics

A total of 38 CNs and 72 patients with CM were recruited, with 40 diagnosed with MOH, and 32 without MOH (Fig. 2). Two MOH patients were lost to follow-up during one-year period. Patient demographics and statistical significance of group comparisons are summarized in Table 1. There was no significant difference in age or sex between wMOH patients and woMOH. Among the patients with woMOH, 19 were taking analgesics, with an average use of 4.094 ± 1.322 days per month. Of these, 18 were using NSAIDs (1 used single-agent NSAIDs, 17 used combination NSAIDs including caffeine). Additionally, 8 patients were on preventive therapy (2 on antiepileptics, 2 on antidepressants, 2 on beta-blockers, and 3 on other medications). A significant proportion of woMOH patients declined to pursue guideline-concordant analgesic therapy. Instead, they opted for modalities such as traditional Chinese medicine (e.g., acupuncture and tuina) or other non-pharmacological therapies (e.g., non-invasive neuromodulation devices) for headache relief. Most of the wMOH patients were regular medication users, with an average use of 22.525 ± 8.079 days per month and a history of medication overuse lasting 7.9 ± 5.3 years. The specific overused analgesics included: NSAIDs in 40 patients (3 used NSAIDs alone, 2 used NSAIDs containing phenobarbital, and 35 used NSAIDs combined with caffeine), triptans in 3 patients and opioids in 2 patients. Concurrently, 20 patients were receiving preventive therapy (10 on antiepileptics, 4 on antidepressants, 2 on beta-blockers, 1 on lisinopril, and 6 on other agents). With the exception of NSAIDs containing phenobarbital, which were tapered off over one week, all other overused medications were discontinued abruptly. Our study findings demonstrated that prolonged analgesic use was prevalent in migraine patients. Analysis revealed non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) as the predominant therapeutic choice, comprising 56.3% of medication regimens in woMOH patients and achieving universal utilization (100%) among wMOH cases.

Regional comparisons of iron-related metric between groups

Figure 3A compares the QSM values between the CM patients and CNs. QSM revealed significant regional iron dysregulation in CM patients compared to CNs. Elevated iron deposition was observed in the putamen (Pu), caudate (Ca), nucleus accumbens (NAC), and substantia nigra subdivisions (SNc). Conversely, reduced iron levels were detected in red nucleus (RN), ventral pallidum (VeP:), habenular nuclei (HN), hypothalamus (Hp), subthalamic nucleus (STH), and multiple thalamic subregions—ventral anterior (VA), ventral lateral anterior/posterior (VLA, VLP), ventral posterior lateral (VPL), pulvinar (Plv), and lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) (see Supplementary Table 1 for specific data).

CM patients with MOH demonstrated significantly lower regional iron deposition in the VeP (0.178 ± 0.112 ppm (95%CI 0.125–0.210 ppm) vs. 0.183 ± 0.082 ppm (95%CI 0.146–0.213 ppm), p < 0.00096, Supplementary Table 2) compared to those without MOH (Fig. 3B). No significant differences in regional iron deposition were observed in the other subcortical nuclei when comparing CM patients with and without MOH.

Relationship between iron-related metric and clinical variables

In CM with MOH, higher QSM values of GPi were significantly associated with higher scores on PHQ-9 and GAD-7. The QSM values of AV were positively associated with the MIDAS (Fig. 4A). Higher QSM values of VeP and MGN were associated with lower scores on the SDS in CM with MOH. Additionally, Higher QSM values in Pu, Ca, RN, VeP, VLA, VLP, VPL, MGN and Plv were significantly associated with a lower relapse rate (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

QSM revealed significantly altered iron deposition in some subcortical nuclei in CM patients compared to CNs (see Fig. 3A for specific data). CM patients with MOH exhibit significantly lower QSM values in the VeP than CM patients without MOH (Fig. 3B), indicative of reduced iron deposition in the subcortical nuclei. Elevated iron deposition within the periaqueductal gray has been documented in migraine patients and is correlated with the disease severity, progression to CM, and reduced efficacy of anti-migraine drugs [27]. The observed increase in iron concentration within subcortical nuclei in migraine suggests a potential role for ferroptosis and ferritinophagy in its pathophysiology [28]. Critically, lower iron levels in the VeP were strongly associated with higher relapse rates and greater severity of analgesic excessive use (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that iron dyshomeostasis in reward-related subcortical structures is associated with addiction-like behaviors and relapse, implicating its role in sustaining the vicious cycle of MOH pathogenesis.

Previous research, including our own, has shown elevated iron accumulation in subcortical regions—such as the Pu, Ca, and NAC—in migraine patients compared to controls, with further increases in CM relative to EM [29]. This iron accumulation likely reflects heightened metabolic activity and increased vulnerability to oxidative stress [30]. Excessive iron deposition may impair synaptic function, modulate protein synthesis, and alter neurotransmitter levels, contributing to basal ganglia dysfunction.

In contrast, the current study reveals a paradoxical reversal in CM patients with MOH, characterized by reduced iron levels in the VeP. To explain this finding, we propose two complementary hypotheses:

Neuroadaptive Iron Redistribution: Chronic analgesic overuse may deplete iron in reward-related hubs through dopaminergic dysregulation [31]. Iron is a cofactor for tyrosine hydroxylase, the rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine synthesis [32]. Reduced iron levels could impair dopamine production, leading to compensatory upregulation of D2 receptor-mediated signaling, which is implicated in craving behaviors in addiction models [33]. The incentive-sensitization theory suggests that chronic drug exposure amplifies motivational drive (‘wanting’) while diminishing hedonic reward (‘liking’) [34]. As key nodes in the mesocorticolimbic circuit, the VeP regulates motivational salience and habit formation. Iron depletion in these regions may disrupt dopaminergic signaling, amplifying ‘wanting’ circuitry and perpetuating analgesic overuse. Notably, Chen et al. reported increased Pu volumes in migraine patients, possibly due to sustained reward system activation [35], which could involve neuronal or glial proliferation [36]. VeP iron content demonstrates stronger correlations with relapse rates and SDS scores than with MIDAS (Fig. 4 MIDAS: R = 0.087 P = 0.607, SDS: R=-0. 557 P < 0.05), providing partial support for the hypothesis that reduced VeP iron reflects impaired dopamine synthesis and compensatory changes that drive compulsive use. Furthermore, correlation analyses revealed that combination NSAIDs containing caffeine show stronger associations with VeP iron content than single-agent NSAIDs or triptans. These findings indicate a significant association between iron depletion in the VeP and both medication excessive use patterns and specific medication classes, particularly caffeine-containing combination NSAIDs, offering partial validation of the addiction-circuit remodeling hypothesis wherein VeP iron depletion selectively modulates “wanting” versus “liking” pathways.

Iron homeostasis is tightly regulated through sophisticated pathways governing import, chaperoning, sequestration, and export [47]. Disruptions lead to iron deficiency or overload, both implicated in migraine pathophysiology [48]. Given that MOH often evolves from migraine, alterations in subcortical iron deposition, exacerbated by chronic analgesic overuse, are highly relevant. We propose that iron depletion in the VeP creates a neurobiological ‘trap’: by dysregulating dopaminergic signaling and habit circuits, it amplifies analgesic ‘wanting,’ reduces behavioral control, and elevates relapse risk. Quantifying VeP iron content via QSM could enable stratification of relapse risk in MOH patients, while targeted modulation of iron-dependent dopaminergic pathways, such as D2 receptor stabilization, offers a promising therapeutic avenue to disrupt the cycle of overuse and relapse.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, patients with MOH secondary to EM or other primary headaches were not included from the study, and the sample size of MOH patients (n = 40) is relatively modest, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Larger cohorts are needed to validate these results and enhance the precision of prognostic factor identification. Second, the cross-sectional design of our study precludes establishing causality between iron depletion and MOH. Longitudinal QSM studies are essential to determine whether iron dyshomeostasis is a cause or consequence of MOH. Third, while our findings highlight the role of dopaminergic pathways, we did not explore potential interactions between iron metabolism and other neurotransmitter systems, such as glutamatergic signaling, which may also contribute to MOH pathophysiology. Future research should investigate these interactions to provide a more comprehensive understanding of MOH mechanisms. Forth, due to strict regulations on opioid medications in China and the fact that ditans are not marketed in the country, our study included very few patients who used opioids or ditans. Fifth, although we propose targeted modulation of iron-dependent dopaminergic pathways (e.g., D2 receptor stabilization) as a potential therapeutic strategy, its feasibility and efficacy in interrupting the cycle of analgesic overuse and relapse require rigorous clinical investigation. What’s more, the lack of routine blood test data (e.g., for hemoglobin, ferritin, and transferrin saturation) from our migraine patient cohort is a study limitation, as it precludes the analysis of potential correlations between brain iron deposition and systemic iron status. Applying strict exclusion criteria—and including only relatively healthy patients with MOH—would significantly limit the generalizability of our findings. It would reduce the clinical relevance and applicability of our results to the broader patient population seen in real-world practice. The ROI refinement was performed by a single experienced neuroradiologist to maintain consistency across all scans. While this avoids inter-rater variability, it also means a formal inter-rater reliability analysis was not performed. A further limitation is that our processing pipeline did not include formal computational algorithms for partial volume correction (PVC) or head motion correction. To mitigate PVE, we relied on the high spatial resolution of our data, as increased resolution is known to reduce partial volume averaging and improve the specificity of QSM in small, deep nuclei [49]. For head motion, we employed a stringent qualitative quality control protocol involving real-time inspection, rescans, and post-hoc exclusion of poor-quality data. However, we acknowledge that future studies would benefit from incorporating advanced automated pipelines. For example, tools such as Ironsmith [50] offer more sophisticated processing by creating gray matter masks that are eroded by one voxel to minimize PVE with adjacent tissue. Such pipelines also include quantitative, MAD-based outlier detection procedures to identify and exclude voxels affected by motion artifacts, extreme susceptibility, or phase-unwrapping failures, thereby providing a more robust and reproducible method for data cleaning. Some CM patients in this study received certain therapeutic approaches with Chinese characteristics, such as acupuncture and tuina. This specific pattern of management may limit the generalizability of symptomatic-use data to external cohorts managed according to standard guideline-based care. We confirm that the data correction does not materially affect the main conclusions. Finally, it is important to acknowledge that QSM measures magnetic susceptibility, which is influenced not only by iron but also by other tissue components like myelin and calcium. Although the deep gray matter nuclei are known to be iron-rich, the observed QSM changes cannot be exclusively attributed to iron concentration. Our interpretation of the findings should be considered in light of this.

Conclusions

Our findings suggest that iron homeostasis in the VeP is selectively and progressively disrupted in CM patients with MOH compared to those without MOH. We hypothesize that this iron depletion reflects maladaptive remodeling of reward and habit circuits, linking chronic analgesic overuse to addiction-like mechanisms. Reduced iron levels in the VeP, particularly, is associated with heightened analgesic excessive use and relapse risk, highlighting its role as a potential neurobiological marker. Quantifying iron deposition via QSM offers a promising approach to stratify relapse risk and guide personalized interventions, paving the way for targeted therapies to disrupt the cycle of MOH and improve patient outcomes.
