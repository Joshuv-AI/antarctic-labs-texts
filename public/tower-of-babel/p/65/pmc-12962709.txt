Association between circadian rhythm-related behaviors and breast cancer: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yan-Yu Hou, Hua-Tao Wu, Wen-Jia Chen, Yang-Zheng Lan, Xin-Ning Yu, Jing Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality in women, with its development linked to circadian rhythm disruption. This study employs two-sample bidirectional Mendelian randomization (MR) to explore their causal relationship.Materials and MethodsGenome-wide association study (GWAS) from the MRC Integrated Epidemiology Unit (MRC IEU) on circadian rhythm-related behaviors and BC subtypes was used. Causal effects were assessed using multiple MR methods, with IVW as the primary approach. Sensitivity analyses were conducted to ensure result robustness.ResultsIncreased sleep duration was associated with a higher BC risk (IVW model: OR = 1.33, P < 0.001), while being a morning person (IVW model: OR = 0.76, P = 0.003) and getting up in the morning (IVW model: OR = 0.71, P < 0.001) were protective factors. These findings underscore the role of genetic factors influencing circadian biology in BC etiology and warrant further investigation into the underlying mechanisms.Keywords: Breast cancer, circadian rhythm disruption, Mendelian randomization, prognosis, susceptibility

Introduction

Breast cancer (BC) is recognized as the most prevalent cancer among women globally and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in this population. In 2022, global statistics estimated around 2.3 million new BC cases, accounting for approximately 23.8% of all new cancer diagnoses in women [1]. Additionally, BC was attributed to around 666,000 deaths, representing 15.4% of all cancer-related fatalities among women [1]. This substantial burden has elevated BC to a major global public health concern, underscoring its profound impact on women’s health worldwide. The development of BC is influenced by a myriad of factors, encompassing both genetic predispositions and external elements like lifestyle and environmental exposures. While previous research has highlighted a strong link between specific genetic mutations and the onset as well as the progression of BC, evidence suggests that non-genetic factors may play an even more prominent role in BC pathogenesis [2]. Numerous lifestyle-related elements, such as body mass index (BMI), obesity, alcohol intake, and smoking habits, exhibit a strong correlation with BC risk and progression [3]. Given this, the role of individual lifestyle choices in shaping BC susceptibility and advancement has attracted substantial interest from the research community, with growing efforts to elucidate these complex relationships.

Circadian rhythms are fundamental expressions of the internal biological clock, orchestrating rhythmic physiological processes that help maintain an organism’s homeostasis. These rhythms are primarily regulated by the daily light/dark cycle, which serves as the main external cue [4]. However, circadian rhythms are also susceptible to modulation by various environmental factors, such as temperature fluctuations and variations in light intensity, as well as social and behavioral influences, including exposure to artificial lighting, shift work, frequent air travel, irregular sleep patterns, and eating schedules [5]. Disruptions in these natural rhythms have been closely connected to the occurrence and progression of BC in humans [6]. A wealth of epidemiological studies has shown that women who engage in night shifts or rotating shift work face a higher risk of developing BC compared to those who adhere to regular circadian patterns, suggesting that alignment with a normal circadian rhythm may offer some protection against BC [7–9]. Additionally, exposure to artificial light at night has been associated with an elevated risk of BC, likely due to light exposure during typical sleep hours, which can disturb sleep rhythms and lead to melatonin dysregulation factors thought to contribute to cancer development [10,11]. Lifestyle choices, such as diet and physical activity, also appear to play a role in circadian health and may influence BC risk. For instance, people who consume their last meal before 9 PM have been observed to have a lower risk of BC compared to those who eat later, after 10 PM [12]. Similarly, women who engage in morning physical activity, particularly between 8 and 10 AM, have shown a reduced risk of BC [13]. Disruptions in sleep duration further contribute to BC risk, as studies suggest that individuals who sleep fewer than 6 hours or more than 8 h have an increased likelihood of developing BC compared to those maintaining a sleep duration within the normal range of 6–8 h [14]. This body of evidence highlights the complex interaction between circadian rhythm integrity, lifestyle factors, and BC risk in observational studies.

While numerous observational studies indicate that poor lifestyle habits may contribute to BC onset and progression through circadian rhythm disruption[15], the precise causal relationship between circadian dysregulation and BC remains unclear. To address this ambiguity, Mendelian randomization (MR) is recognized as an influential epidemiological approach for examining causality between exposures and outcomes. MR utilizes single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) to approximate exposures like lifestyle or biological factors, leveraging genetic variants to establish a causal inference framework [16]. Because alleles for these genetic variants are randomly distributed during meiosis, the use of SNPs as IVs minimizes the potential for confounding factors typically present in observational studies [17]. Furthermore, as genetic variants exist before disease onset, MR reduces the chance of reverse causation by ensuring that the exposure precedes the outcome [17]. Thus, by combining genetic and epidemiological insights, MR allows for a more robust examination of causal links.

In this context, we conducted a two-sample bidirectional MR analysis as an etiological investigation to examine the potential causal relationship between circadian rhythm-related behaviors and BC risk. This approach aims to elucidate the biological basis of this association and inform hypotheses for future mechanistic and intervention studies.

Materials and methods

Research design and data origins

This study aimed to utilize a two-sample bidirectional MR analysis to explore prospective causal links between circadian rhythm-related behaviors and the risk of BC, as illustrated in Figure 1. To achieve this, genome-wide association study (GWAS) data for both circadian rhythm-related behaviors and BC were obtained from the MRC Integrated Epidemiology Unit (MRC IEU) [18]. The circadian rhythm-related behaviors analyzed in this study encompassed a range of sleep characteristics and shift work exposure. Specific sleep-related traits included sleeplessness, sleep duration, morning person (chronotype), getting up in the morning, and snoring. In this study, the “morning person (chronotype)” phenotype used was the individual’s self-reported biological clock type, reflecting the degree to which they tend to go to bed early and wake up early. The “getting up in the morning” phenotype was defined as the self-reported time of waking up in the morning. Although the two are conceptually related, in this study, we used independently clustered SNPs as IVs, which effectively avoided the problem of multicollinearity among the IVs. The pooled BC GWAS data set comprised 122,977 cases, including 69,501 cases of estrogen receptor-positive (ER+) BC and 21,468 cases of estrogen receptor-negative (ER−) BC, along with 105,974 controls. SNPs were selected as IVs for subsequent analysis. It’s important to note that all circadian rhythm-related behavior phenotypes used in this study were based on self-reported measures from the original GWAS sources. Ethical approval and informed consent were not required for this research, because it used publicly available, de-identified genetic summary data.

Instrumental variables selection

In the two-sample MR framework, the IVs must satisfy three essential assumptions to ensure valid causal inferences [19]. Firstly, the IVs should demonstrate a substantial association with the exposure variables of focus. Secondly, the IVs must be independent of any confounding variables that could simultaneously influence both exposure and the outcome. This independence minimizes the risk of confounding bias, a critical aspect of reliable causal estimation. Thirdly, the IVs should affect the outcome exclusively through the exposure variables, without acting through alternative pathways, thereby fulfilling the exclusion restriction criteria. To meet these requirements, after obtaining the GWAS data on circadian rhythm-related behaviors and BC, SNPs substantially associated with the exposure variables (P < 5 × 10−8) were initially identified as potential IVs. Next, SNPs with an F-statistic lower than 10 were excluded to reduce the likelihood of weak IV bias, which could compromise the validity of causal conclusions. To further ensure independence among IVs, the PLINK clumping method (with parameters r2 <0.001 and kb = 10,000) was employed, referencing the European 1000 Genomes data. This clumping process minimizes bias from strong linkage disequilibrium (LD), ensuring that the selected SNPs are largely independent of one another. To maintain alignment in effect direction between the exposure and outcome datasets, we used the harmonize_data function from the “TwoSampleMR” R package to harmonize SNPs effects. This procedure automatically identifies and removes palindromic SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation, specifically those with intermediate allele frequencies (minor allele frequency, MAF, between approximately 0.42 and 0.58, which is the package’s default threshold). Outlier SNPs with disproportionate effects that might bias MR results due to horizontal pleiotropy were identified and excluded using the Radial Mendelian randomization (Radial MR) approach [20]. As an additional measure, the MR Steiger filtering method was applied to verify the direction of causality [21]. This method helps exclude IVs whose correlation with the outcome variable is stronger than with the exposure, thereby retaining only those SNPs that are substantially associated with the exposure and are less likely to represent reverse causation.

Mendelian randomization analysis

The bidirectional causal relationships between circadian rhythm-related behaviors and BC were assessed through a combination of statistical methods, including the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method, MR-Egger regression, weighted median, IVW radial, and maximum likelihood approaches. Each of these methods offers unique strengths for deriving causal estimates and detecting potential biases in MR analysis. The IVW method, which was the principal methodological approach in this study, presumes that each selected SNPs qualify as valid IVs and free from horizontal pleiotropy [22]. Individual SNPs are analyzed using the ratio method to estimate each SNP’s causal effect on the outcome. These individual effects are then pooled into a weighted regression to calculate an overall causal effect between the exposure and outcome. In addition to IVW, complementary approaches were employed to further validate findings and assess pleiotropic effects. The MR-Egger regression method, for instance, enables testing and adjustment for directional pleiotropy, a potential source of bias when SNPs affect the outcome via pathways distinct from the exposure [23]. MR-Egger assumes the InSIDE condition, where the size of each SNP’s effect on the exposure is uncorrelated with its direct effect on the outcome, thus allowing for relaxed exclusivity assumptions compared to IVW. The weighted median method assumes that at least 50% of the included SNPs are valid IVs; by ranking causal effect estimates by weight, the weighted median provides a consistent effect estimate that is less sensitive to pleiotropic SNPs [24]. The IVW radial approach, a robust extension of the IVW method, was also applied to address potential bias and improve the detection of outlier SNPs, thereby enhancing the reliability of causal inference [20]. Lastly, the maximum likelihood method calculates causal estimates by optimizing the likelihood function, allowing for a robust analysis even in complex datasets with mixed data structures.

Given the study’s two-sample bidirectional MR design, we performed multiple independent hypothesis tests. In total, 36 independent MR tests were conducted, including 18 tests in the forward direction (6 circadian rhythm behaviors × 3 BC outcomes) and 18 tests in the reverse direction (3 BC outcomes × 6 circadian rhythm behaviors). To control the family-wise error rate, a Bonferroni correction was applied, yielding a stringent significance threshold of P<0.05/36≈0.00139. Associations with P-values between 0.00139 and 0.05 were considered suggestive of causality but not conclusive after multiple testing correction.

Sensitivity analysis

Heterogeneity among the IVs was evaluated using Cochrane’s Q test, which assesses the extent to which the individual SNP estimates are consistent with each other. This test provides insight into whether variation exists across IVs that may affect the reliability of causal estimates [25]. Additionally, to examine the potential symmetry of the IV effects, funnel plots were visually inspected. Symmetrical funnel plots indicate consistent estimates across SNPs, while asymmetry may suggest the presence of directional biases, such as pleiotropy. To detect horizontal pleiotropy, where SNPs influence the outcome through pathways other than the exposure, an MR-Egger intercept test was employed. This test estimates the intercept of the MR-Egger regression line, with a non-zero intercept indicating potential pleiotropic effects that could bias causal inferences [23]. The significance level for both Cochrane’s Q test and the MR-Egger intercept test was set at P < 0.05, providing a threshold to determine when heterogeneity or pleiotropy may be influencing the results. A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was also performed to assess the influence of individual SNPs on the overall causal estimates. In this approach, each SNP was systematically removed from the analysis, one at a time, to observe any shifts in the causal effect estimate [26]. If removing a particular SNP markedly changes the result, it suggests that this SNP may be an outlier or exert a disproportionate effect, thereby highlighting its impact on the overall findings.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was carried out via R software, including both MR and sensitivity analyses with the “TwoSampleMR” package [27].

Results

There is a causal relationship between sleep duration, morning person and getting up in the morning and BC

The forest plot revealed that sleep duration was associated with an increased risk of BC, with an odds ratio (OR) of 1.33 (95% CI: 1.13–1.56, P < 0.001). This indicates that for every unit increase in genetically predicted sleep duration, there is a 33% higher likelihood of developing BC. In contrast, we did not find significant genetic evidence supporting a causal role for sleeplessness, snoring, or shift work on BC risk in our MR analysis, as illustrated in Figure 2A. Conversely, the analysis highlighted that morning person and getting up in the morning were linked to a reduced risk of BC. Specifically, each one standard deviation (SD) increase in genetically predicted morning person behavior was associated with a 23% decrease in BC risk (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.64–0.91, P = 0.003), while a similar increase in the timing of getting up in the morning corresponded to a 29% reduction in risk (OR = 0.71, 95% CI: 0.59–0.85, P < 0.001), as depicted in Figure 2A. Additionally, further analyses employing the weighted median, IVW radial, and maximum likelihood estimation methods consistently supported the existence of a genetic causal relationship between sleep duration, morning person behavior, and getting up in the morning with BC risk. Scatter plots further illustrate these findings, indicating that increased sleep duration is associated with a higher occurrence of BC, whereas identifying as a morning person and waking up early confer protective effects against the disease (Figure 2B and Table 2).

The IVW analysis demonstrated that longer sleep duration is connected to an increased likelihood of ER+ BC (OR = 1.56, 95% CI: 1.28–1.91, P < 0.001). This finding suggests that individuals with longer sleep duration have a 56% higher likelihood of developing ER+ BC. In contrast, our analysis did not detect significant causal associations for sleeplessness, snoring, or shift work with the risk of ER+ BC, as illustrated in Figure 3A. Conversely, the analysis found that identifying as the morning person and getting up in the morning were linked to a decreased likelihood of developing ER+ BC. These findings underscore the potential protective effects of the morning person and getting up in the morning on the development of ER+ BC. However, for ER- BC, no significant causal relationship was detected between circadian rhythm-related behaviors and the risk of morbidity. Reverse causation between sleeplessness and BC

The forest plot derived from the inverse MR analysis identified BC occurrence as a potential risk factor for developing sleeplessness, as illustrated in Figure 4A. This finding suggests a direct relationship where an increase in BC risk may contribute to an increased likelihood of experiencing sleeplessness. Additionally, analyses using the weighted median, IVW radial, and maximum likelihood estimation methods further corroborate the presence of a genetic causal link between BC risk and the incidence of sleeplessness. Discussion

The extant literature indicates that disruption to circadian rhythms increases susceptibility to BC in women[15]. The present study employed a two-sample bidirectional MR framework to investigate the genetic etiological roles of circadian rhythm-related behaviors in BC risk. Our primary findings indicate that a genetic predisposition to longer sleep duration is associated with an increased risk of overall BC and the ER+ subtype, while a genetic predisposition toward morning chronotype and earlier waking time is associated with a decreased risk. Additionally, reverse MR analysis suggested that genetic liability to BC is causally linked to a higher risk of developing insomnia.

Individuals with a genetic predisposition toward morning chronotype, as inferred from their chronotype, are often associated with behavioral patterns that include earlier bedtimes and wake times, as well as more regular sleep schedules. These patterns are hypothesized to promote circadian stability and sleep quality. Observational studies further link the morning chronotype to a cluster of health-promoting behaviors, such as maintaining a balanced diet, engaging in regular physical activity, and avoiding harmful habits like smoking and excessive alcohol consumption [28–30]. Collectively, these behavioral correlates offer plausible, non-mutually exclusive pathways through which the genetic variants underlying morning chronotype may contribute to a reduced risk of chronic diseases and cancer, potentially via favorable modulation of metabolic and immune function as influenced by these genetic variants. Conversely, a genetic inclination toward evening chronotype has been associated with tendencies to skip meals or eat later at night [31,32]. This nocturnal preference often coincides with greater exposure to artificial light at night, which can suppress melatonin secretion and disrupt core circadian timing [33]. Furthermore, evening-type individuals frequently experience a misalignment between their endogenous circadian phase and societal schedules, a phenomenon termed “social jet lag,” which can lead to chronic sleep curtailment and compound circadian rhythm disruption [32].

Melatonin is synthesized at night in the pineal gland under the action of noradrenergic energy and plays a pivotal role in maintaining the circadian rhythm of the organism. In physiological conditions that are not pathological, melatonin plays a role in regulating a few important functions, including the stabilization of the sleep-wake cycle, the regulation of gonadal activity, the maintenance of cellular redox homeostasis, the enhancement of immune function and the inhibition of cancer cell growth and other anticancer effects [34]. Disturbances to the biological clock result in a disruption to the melatonin secretion rhythm, which in turn leads to a reduction in melatonin levels. A reduction in melatonin levels has been linked to disturbances in sleep patterns and is believed to be a contributing factor in the increased risk of various types of cancer, including BC [35,36]. Several studies have revealed that melatonin levels are lower in individuals diagnosed with breast cancer than in those who are healthy [37,38]. Conversely, plasma levels of estrogen in women are strongly associated with the risk of BC. Melatonin has been demonstrated to reduce estrogen synthesis and interfere with the proliferation of BC cells by inhibiting estrogen receptor activity [39]. In ER+ BC, estrogen is a significant driver of cancer growth. Melatonin can indirectly inhibit BC cell growth by reducing estrogen production through the reduction of aromatase activity, which serves as a crucial enzyme involved in the process of converting androgens to estrogens [40]. Melatonin has been demonstrated to reduce Delta-like ligand 4 (DLL4) expression in ER+ BC cells and induce apoptosis in BC cells. Furthermore, melatonin has been shown to inhibit BPA-induced BC cell proliferation by blocking the expression of Bisphenol A (BPA)-triggered steroid receptor coactivators (e.g., SRC-1 and SRC-3) and inhibiting estrogen-responsive element activity. Melatonin also exerts antioxidant effects, protecting cellular DNA from oxidative damage, maintaining mitochondrial function, and preventing mitochondrial damage, thereby reducing the risk of cancer. It is hypothesized that gut microbiota may act as a modulator between melatonin and BC. Imbalances in the gut microbiota have been observed to stimulate the kynurenine pathway, which diverts tryptophan away from the melanodermic pathway [41]. This results in a decrease in circulating melatonin levels, which may subsequently increase the risk of BC.

It is a widely held view that adequate sleep is vital for maintaining good health and may help diminish cancer risk. However, contrary to this conventional wisdom, our MR analysis suggested a potential causal link between genetically predicted longer genetically predicted sleep duration and an increased risk of BC, particularly the ER+ subtype. Crucially, while our sensitivity analyses did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy, we acknowledge that residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. The genetic variants used as instruments for sleep duration might influence BC risk through pathways other than sleep, for instance, by proxying for underlying health conditions (e.g., depressive symptoms, chronic inflammation, or a sedentary lifestyle) that themselves are risk factors for cancer.

With this important methodological caveat in mind, several non-mutually exclusive hypotheses could be considered if the observed genetic association reflects a true biological relationship. First, longer sleep duration may not be a direct cause but rather a marker of underlying poor health or an altered physiological state. For example, it could be strongly associated with poor lifestyle factors such as physical inactivity and chronic psychological stress, which negatively affect metabolism and have been genetically linked to BC risk [42,43]. Second, excessive sleep duration may be indicative of a sleep disorder or other underlying health issues, such as depression or chronic illness [44]. These conditions not only impair quality of life but may also disrupt immune and endocrine function, thereby increasing cancer susceptibility. Specifically, depression can induce a state of chronic inflammation, alter immune responses, and promote the secretion of pro-cancer cytokines [45], while also affecting hormonal balance relevant to ER+ BC [46]. Therefore, in some instances, prolonged sleep might be a consequence or indicator of a preexisting disease process that indirectly elevates breast cancer risk, rather than purely protective behavior.

This study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal link between BC liability and a higher risk of insomnia, adding a novel perspective to the established clinical association. Insomnia in this context likely stems from a complex interplay of factors inherent to the cancer experience. The physiological burden of the disease, the side effects of treatments, such as chemotherapy, which may impact the nervous system, and the significant psychological distress commonly reported by BC patients, including anxiety and depression, are all recognized contributors to sleep disruption [47]. Chemotherapy agents in particular have been associated with exacerbating insomnia [48]. Furthermore, insomnia may not merely be a consequence but could also interact with disease progression. Evidence suggests that sleep deprivation can impair immune function and diminish the body’s antitumor capacity, potentially worsening prognosis [49,50]. Additionally, by disrupting circadian rhythms and melatonin secretion, insomnia may perturb hormonal homeostasis, including estrogen-related pathways, which could in turn influence BC pathophysiology [39].

An interesting finding from our study was the lack of a significant causal association between genetic liability to shift work and BC risk, which contrasts with some observational epidemiological evidence [8,9]. This discrepancy may be attributed to several factors. Firstly, the GWAS for shift work had a considerably smaller sample size compared to the other circadian traits, which likely resulted in reduced statistical power to detect a causal effect of smaller magnitude. Secondly, the self-reported, binary definition of “job involves shift work” in the GWAS may not fully capture the intensity, duration, or night-specific aspects of shift work that are most disruptive to circadian rhythms and have been linked to cancer risk in observational studies. Thirdly, it is possible that the effect of shift work on breast cancer is mediated more strongly through non-genetic pathways (e.g., direct light-at-night exposure, and social jet lag) that are not fully proxied by genetic instruments [51]. Therefore, our MR result does not definitively rule out a causal effect but highlights the need for larger GWAS on more precisely defined shift work phenotypes and further investigation into the specific mechanisms linking occupational circadian disruption to cancer.

In this study, the application of two-sample bidirectional MR methods effectively addressed the limitations of observational studies, thereby enhancing the accuracy of causal inferences. The application of multiple sensitivity analyses served to further enhance the robustness of the results. Furthermore, the GWAS data applied in this investigation were derived from two independent samples, thereby optimizing statistical efficacy. Nevertheless, the present study is not without limitations. Firstly, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the exclusive use of GWAS data from individuals of European ancestry. Therefore, caution is warranted when extrapolating these results to other populations. This is particularly important given known differences in the genetic architecture and prevalence of circadian rhythm phenotypes (e.g., chronotype distribution), as well as in BC risk factor profiles across diverse ancestral groups [52,53]. The applicability of our findings to non-European populations requires direct validation in future multi-ancestry studies. Secondly, as noted in Materials and Methods, the circadian rhythm behavior data were self-reported, which may introduce recall or reporting bias compared to objective measurements. Further validation using objectively measured sleep phenotypes is warranted. Thirdly, although the present study reveals potential genetic associations between circadian rhythm-related traits and the likelihood of BC and its subtypes, the specific biological mechanisms involved need to be further explored. Fourthly, our findings underscore the complex relationship between sleep patterns and BC. Future research using more refined genetic instruments and objective sleep measures is needed to disentangle these intricate relationships and to clarify the extent to which our findings might be influenced by residual pleiotropy. Fifthly, and fundamentally for interpreting MR results, the causal estimates here reflect the lifelong influence of genetic liability to circadian-related traits. They provide strong evidence for the etiological role of these traits in BC pathogenesis. However, the effect size of modifying these behaviors through environmental or clinical intervention may differ, as such interventions might not replicate the specific biological pathways influenced by the genetic variants studied.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this bidirectional MR study provides genetic evidence supporting a causal relationship between genetic predisposition to certain circadian rhythm-related traits (specifically, morning chronotype, getting up in the morning, and sleep duration) and BC risk. The findings suggest that genetic variation influencing circadian biology contributes to the etiology of BC, with notable associations for the ER+ subtype. Conversely, genetic liability to BC was causally linked to a higher risk of insomnia. These results reinforce the hypothesis that genetically influenced circadian regulation plays a role in BC pathogenesis and identify circadian biology as an important area for future etiological and mechanistic investigation.
