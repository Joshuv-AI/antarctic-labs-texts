The primary care brain health navigator: A perspective on implementation
Authors: Deanna R. Willis, Diana Summanwar, Nicole R. Fowler, Jared R. Brosch, Dustin B. Hammers
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGaps in primary care (PC) early detection are caused by systems‐based issues that require a systems‐based solution. A PC brain health navigator (BHN) can mitigate the time limitations of PC providers, standardize high‐quality PC‐based evaluation of cognitive impairment, and improve the appropriateness of specialty care referrals. The design of the BHN role and function should be part of the patient‐centered PC medical home team and integrated wholly within the PC space to achieve its potential. Sustainability of a PC BHN relies upon advanced payment models and requires further research and policy evaluation. The role and function of the PC BHN is the linchpin necessary to enable feasibility of early detection of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias in PC by creating capacity and capability of the primary care team. To be effective, the PC BHN must be intentionally and contextually developed, implemented, and supported within PC and with PC leaders.

INTRODUCTION

The prevalence of dementia in the United States is growing, and there continue to be gaps in early detection and accurate diagnosis. More than 90% of Medicare beneficiaries with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), a preceding stage of dementia, go undiagnosed. 1 Few patients see specialty brain care providers or do so late in the disease course. However, most people in the United States receive care from a primary care provider (PCP), 2 giving primary care (PC) the potential to play a critical role in early detection and timely diagnosis of dementia.

Implementing early detection in PC is not without challenges. Lack of validated screening tools embedded into electronic health record workflows, inadequate reimbursement for cognitive assessments, limited time, insufficient training and confidence in diagnosing cognitive impairments, and limited awareness of the benefit of early interventions are all significant factors increasing the challenge for PC‐based early detection. 3 Technological advancements, including digital cognitive assessment devices, have offered new opportunities to expand early detection and case finding. Recently demonstrated real‐world implementation of these tools in PC, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 coupled with national and international efforts to increase PCP awareness, knowledge, and confidence in early detection, 8 will enhance the identification of older adults with MCI and early dementia in PC practices. However, as PC‐based identification improves, it must be accompanied by coordinated diagnostic and care strategies to manage downstream impacts—particularly the growing strain on specialty care resources such as neurology and geriatrics. 9

After identifying a cognitive concern, PCPs must guide patients through a complex array of diagnostic and treatment options. These often include referrals to geriatrics, neurology, or neuropsychology, as well as consideration of pharmacologic and non‐pharmacologic treatments and support for caregivers. The resulting care pathways are frequently fragmented and nonlinear, which can be confusing for patients, caregivers, and PCPs alike. Patient‐level factors such as multimorbidity and polypharmacy, combined with system‐level barriers such as limited access to or understanding of community resources, 10 further challenge PCPs who attempt to facilitate early detection for their patients. As a result, patients with cognitive complaints or symptoms are frequently referred by their PCP to specialty care without a working diagnosis, 11 further compounding challenges with long specialty care wait times and failing to match patients with the most appropriate follow‐up care and services. Addressing these dynamic barriers will require transformative changes in the culture, structure, and function of health systems, particularly through the development of robust, primary care–based pathways that support early detection and diagnosis.

To date, efforts to improve detection and diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) in PC have focused on approaches to improve the care provided by PCPs as individual care providers. However, high‐quality PC has been shown to be most effective when delivered by PC teams, with 10 principles, referred to as the “building blocks,” being recognized as the cornerstones of the patient‐centered primary care medical homes (PCMH) in the United States. 12

Patient navigation in PC has emerged as a care model to address care fragmentation and support patients with complex needs. In PCMH settings, nurse navigators have improved chronic disease management, increased screening rates, increased patient adherence to care plans, and decreased patient anxiety. 13 , 14 PC navigators also provide important connections to community resources for patients and their caregivers by coordinating care across the health system continuum, resulting in high patient and provider satisfaction 15 and improved outcomes in patients with chronic conditions (CCs). 16

The evaluation of a patient with cognitive impairment in PC has many elements of complexity that are ideal for care navigation, including the need for review of comorbidities and medications, screening for addressable causes, and facilitation of additional testing. Improving early detection in PC has the potential to increase specialty care wait times. Improving the reliability and effectiveness of PC‐based diagnostic evaluation through nurse navigation has the potential to mitigate this effect by improving the quality of the PC evaluation and identifying the best care pathway for each patient, especially those that are most appropriate for ongoing PC management. For PC‐based early detection to be effective at a systems level, this mitigation of specialty care backlog must occur in parallel to increased detection. A care pathway connecting cognitive assessment in primary care to a navigation model has potential for both.

We first introduced the Brain Health Navigator (BHN) as part of the Indiana University (IU)/IU Health site of the Davos Alzheimer's Collaborative Healthcare System Preparedness Early Detection Flagship Program in 2022. The role was created due to overwhelming feedback from PCPs that additional support was needed to facilitate the care process for patients who had abnormal cognitive screenings. 4 , 5 , 17 The responsibilities and functions of the BHN were designed to facilitate the PC‐based clinical evaluation of patients with cognitive impairment, connect patients and caregivers to resources, and facilitate the patient's diagnostic and treatment pathway (Table 1). Our successful implementation and sustainment has led to improvement in completion rates of diagnostic tests for addressable causes of cognitive impairment and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the head, as well as a higher likelihood of receiving a diagnosis of MCI than those patients who did not see the PC BHN. 17 We argue that implementing a PC BHN using our proposed framework will be advantageous in closing known gaps in PC‐based early detection.

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR AND THE PRIMARY CARE MEDICAL HOME

Team‐based care, patient–team partnership, care coordination, comprehensiveness, and prompt access to care represent 5 of the 10 PCMH building blocks, 12 each of which can be built into a PC‐based early detection model.

Over the past two decades, PC in the United States has undergone significant transformation toward team‐based care through the PCMH model. This shift, supported by national collaboratives and evolving payment models, has made team‐based care a key feature of many PC practices. PCMH PC teams typically consist of a “teamlet,” pairing a clinician with a medical assistant, supported by an interprofessional team that may include registered nurses, pharmacists, and behavioral health specialists. A 2019 national study found that 70% of Family Physicians reported practicing in settings that use team‐based care structures, indicating a strong foundation for integrating new roles such as the BHN. 18

Patient–team partnership allows patients to be engaged with a team that they come to know personally. Co‐visits are a team‐based partnership model in PCMH practices where the Registered Nurse participates in the visit with the provider, and after the provider leaves the examination room, they implement the care plan. Although co‐visits are traditionally synchronous for the PCMH team and PCP, asynchronous adaptations of the co‐visit model have been successful. 19 An asynchronous PC‐based BHN can be a pivotal team‐based solution because of the challenges of incorporating cognitive and diagnostic assessments, including time and space limitations, into PC practices. 4

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR FACILITATION OF DIAGNOSTIC EVALUATION

Care coordination (the responsibility of PC teams to arrange services they cannot directly provide) and comprehensiveness (the capacity of a practice to address most patient needs) are essential components of a PC‐based diagnostic evaluation for individuals with subjective cognitive concerns or abnormal screening cognitive assessments. 12 Although 64% of people in the United States receive their dementia diagnosis from their PCP, 20 gaps in care coordination and comprehensiveness lead to significant variability in the diagnostic process of those patients. For example, despite recommendations for structural MRI, 21 when imaging is ordered, PCPs are more likely to order computed tomography (CT) scans over MRI; and only 14% of PCPs report high confidence in interpreting brain imaging results. 22 As a result, those persons with MCI and early dementia remain under‐detected, under‐diagnosed, and under‐treated in PC. An array of complex system factors contributes to these outcomes, primarily manifesting as a symptom of “time constraints.” These barriers will likely be further complicated by the integration of blood biomarkers (BBMs) into PC practices. We found previously that PCPs responded most effectively to order proposals for missing diagnostic testing compared to clinical messages recommending orders to be placed. 17 We initially designed the PC BHN role for a Licensed Clinical Social Worker but later converted the role to a Registered Nurse, so clinical order proposals could be sent to the patient's PCP. A clinical algorithm can be used by a nurse navigator to support the PCP with laboratory and imaging result interpretation and management. In contrast to some specialty care settings, advanced practice providers, such as nurse practitioners or physician assistants, are often the patient's primary care provider, particularly in rural settings. Using an advanced practice provider as a primary care brain health navigator would, therefore, reduce the impact of a targeted facilitation of specific gaps in care by the primary care team. Thus, the PC BHN offers a system‐based solution that offers potential to enhance diagnostic clarity in the face of these challenges.

Time‐requirements

Time constraints remain one of the most cited barriers to cognitive assessment in PC. Although tools such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) or the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), are designed to take less than 10 min to complete, they often take longer to administer in real‐world practice, especially among patients with limited health literacy, which comprise up to 30% of adults is the United States. 23 Most cognitive screening tests suitable for PC practice were developed, validated, and timed in homogenous research populations with higher health literacy and educational attainment than many PC patients. 23 Although some tests incorporate demographic adjustments, such as educational attainment, a single cut‐point is often used to determine normal versus abnormal, leading to bias in the interpretation of the results. These limitations can contribute to educational bias, which raises the likelihood of making the screening task harder for those with low health literacy, lower educational attainment, or limited English proficiency. This can result in over‐diagnosis of MCI, if the PCP does not accurately take these moderating variables into account, which complicates the diagnostic process for PCPs.

Although up to 40% of dementia cases are attributable to life‐course modifiable risk factors, 24 the use of validated and standardized PC‐based screening scales improves the identification and detection of these addressable conditions. However, the collective time required to complete a full assessment of cognitive impairment using high‐quality validated structured tools to assess for potential addressable conditions can be substantial(Table 2). Time constraints have been identified consistently as a significant barrier to PC‐based early detection, but few systems‐based approaches have been offered as potential solutions. Although a PCP may not have time in a busy clinic to complete the needed assessments, patient education, and Shared Decision‐Making (SDM), a PC BHN can provide the needed capacity for a comprehensive evaluation and care plan development. Through a coordinated nurse‐led diagnostic evaluation, the PC BHN offers an opportunity to enhance comprehensiveness of brain health evaluation in PC, by improving the quality of the PC‐based diagnostic evaluation and mitigating time constraints on PCPs.

Enhanced diagnostic clarity

Up to 60% of older adults with cognitive impairment remain without a diagnosis of dementia or MCI in PC, 25 with the majority of patients with cognitive impairment not ever being referred to a dementia specialist. 26 Although differentiating the underlying cause of dementia often requires advanced testing, PCPs can reliably diagnose MCI and mild dementia syndrome. Nurse‐led diagnostic algorithms have been shown to be effective in facilitating diagnoses of both common and complex conditions. 27 By guiding patients through structured, PC‐based diagnostic evaluation of cognitive concerns, a BHN can support PCPs in diagnosing MCI and mild dementia syndrome earlier and more effectively.

Recommendations for clinical implementation of BBMs are evolving to include PC pathways that integrate BBMs in the evaluation of patients with cognitive concerns. Shared decision tools and workflows that further support integration into PC practices with diverse patient populations and high rates of multi‐morbidity are being developed collaboratively. As this body of work develops and evolves, the role of the BHN in facilitating patients' education and understanding of BBMs to enhance diagnostic clarity will need to be explored further, but the PC BHN BBM‐related workflows offer tremendous opportunity.

The BHN model has already shown promising outcomes. In prior work, we observed increases in MCI diagnosis rates, laboratory testing for addressable causes, and MRI utilization following BHN implementation in PC practices offering cognitive screening. 17 By improving both care coordination and comprehensiveness, the BHN can enhance diagnostic consistency, improve patient outcomes, and ensure more appropriate referrals to specialty care.

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR AND SHARED DECISION‐MAKING

SDM is a collaborative process that respects patients’ rights to make health care choices based on their personal values and preferences. High‐quality SDM includes six key components: (1) explanation of the health care problem, (2) presentation of available options, (3) discussion of risks and benefits for all options, (4) clarification of patient values and preferences, (5) assessment of patient understanding, and (6) allowing the patient to make the decision. Higher patient‐reported health outcomes, improved quality indicators, and lower health care utilization have all been demonstrated outcomes of SDM. 28

Health care providers widely accept the importance of these aspects of care, yet the majority of providers do not consistently use a form of structured SDM in their practice. 29 Although lack of adequate time and concerns about increased duration of the encounter are the most cited concerns, SDM does not significantly extend visit duration. 30 Due to these perceptions and the complexity of demands on PCPs, SDM based on physician‐driven processes is unreliable. The use of a clinical PC team member for SDM processes in PC is an evidence‐based solution to address time constraints, engage patients in diagnostic testing decisions and care goals, and improve outcomes in PC patients with other CCs. 28 The role of nurse‐based SDM models related to the diagnostic, treatment, and care planning process for people with cognitive impairment and dementia has been documented as early as 1995. 28 The use of structured tools for SDM has been found to be helpful in the context of early detection and diagnosis, and a variety of SDM tools are widely available, including tools to assist with discussions about BBMs and disease‐modifying treatments. With staff‐driven SDM processes having higher reliability, 28 engaging clinical team members other than physicians is even more critical to the success of SDM implementation of PC‐based early detection pathways.

Older adults with cognitive impairment can reliably communicate their values and choices, even when cognition declines. SDM can therefore be a powerful tool to reduce fear of loss of autonomy and increase patient engagement. A PC nurse‐based SDM approach using a PC BHN can improve the reliability and effectiveness of patient‐engaged care decisions throughout the ADRD diagnostic journey, particularly as BBM testing becomes more common in PC.

Brain health navigator connection to specialty care

Even with robust PC diagnostic evaluation in PC, the connection to specialty care is a critical part of the cognitive care pathway. Yet, patients often face challenges completing these referrals. Fragmentation, related to lack of coordination and misalignment of organizational structures and systems, can lead to inefficiencies and potentially harm patient outcomes. Lack of clarity about care coordination responsibilities, long wait times for specialty care providers, geographic barriers, and concerns for cost of care are contributors to gaps of care as patients transition from PC to specialty care providers. These gaps leave as few as 40% of patients who are referred from PC to actually have a specialty care appointment. 31 These barriers disproportionately impact patients with low health literacy and/or limited language proficiency—who are also at higher risk of over‐diagnosis of MCI—along with those patients experiencing transportation barriers and multi‐morbidity. 32

Communication gaps, both to and from PCPs and specialists, further complicate referral pathways. For example, many PC referrals do not include a transfer of critical information to the specialist, and reciprocally, PCPs are often unnotified whether the patient was seen by the specialist. 33 Follow‐through with specialist‐based recommendations can also be compromised by communication gaps, as the PCP may not receive follow‐up treatment plans from the specialist, necessitating reliance entirely upon the report from the patient about the specialist's recommendations. Lack of clarity about whether the referral represents a single consultation or ongoing co‐management 33 further convolutes the relationships. These challenges in PC–specialty care referrals can contribute to missed or delayed diagnoses, delays in treatment, and other patient safety concerns.

Boundary spanners are individuals who facilitate the flow of information, ideas, and resources between different groups or disciplines by functioning at the “boundary” between those groups. 34 Effective partnerships between PC and other health care system disciplines require both sides to span the boundaries that separate them, which can be both conceptual and practical. In PC, boundary spanners often function by helping facilitate patient connections to other parts of the health care system to facilitate collaborative care. 35 Conceptualizing the BHN role as a boundary spanner between PC and specialty care through navigation/care management holds promise to improve the over‐ and under‐utilization of dementia specialty care providers. When paired with collaboratively‐developed guidelines that identify the most appropriate care pathway for patients who have completed PC characterization and evaluation of their cognitive impairment, the BHN can play an important role in facilitating the right patients to the most effective and efficient care pathway for their individual care, thus pulling specialty care services across the boundary towards PC and making specialty care more accessible to the patients who need it most. Improved utilization of specialty care services, combined with improved quality of diagnostic evaluations in PC, has the potential to expedite access for patients who need timely referrals and in geographic areas where specialist access is particularly limited. In the era of disease‐modifying therapies, these optimized care pathways are essential to creating access to treatment.

This boundary spanning function of the BHN role requires intentional implementation through partnership of PC and specialty care. Inter‐clinician relationships have been shown to have a direct impact on resource utilization and timeliness of care. 36 Patients often identify non‐physicians, such as nurses, as key facilitators in navigating across the health care system. 36 Building close relationships between a PC‐based BHN and the clinical care team in the memory care specialty services department is essential to successful transitions of patients who need specialty care services.

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR CONNECTION TO RESEARCH OPPORTUNITIES

Recruitment of participants through PC has tremendous potential to enhance the real‐world validity and representativeness of ADRD clinical trials. The PC BHN can play a key role by exploring patients’ interest in research and introducing research opportunities to patients with cognitive impairment. This potential arises from a combination of factors including demographics of PC patient panels, higher rates of multi‐morbidity in PC, and success of PC practices in practice‐based recruitment for research studies.

In the United States, PC patient panels closely resemble the population demographics of the communities they serve, with PC patients being slightly older and sicker than the general population. 37 By 2050, the prevalences of hypertension, obesity, and diabetes mellitus are expected to increase beyond 60% of the U.S. adult population. 38 The majority of patients with CCs receive care from their PCP, and PCPs deliver a disproportionate amount of the ambulatory care to disadvantaged populations in the United States. 37

New ADRD diagnostics and treatments increase the urgency to include diverse, multi‐morbid populations in research. Significant disparities in ADRD incidence, progression, and mortality are evident across diverse racial, ethnic, gender, and geographic groups. Yet, these high‐risk groups remain markedly underrepresented in clinical trials, 39 thereby limiting generalizability. Despite comorbidities being common in patients with Alzheimer's disease (AD), clinical trials for AD commonly have exclusion criteria related to comorbidities. CCs have been found to be related to BBM levels, yet there is a paucity of guidelines for interpretation of BBM in multi‐morbidity. Common CCs, such as diabetes mellitus and cardiovascular disease, appear to have multi‐faceted associations with AD prognostic indicators, including risk of developing AD and rapidity of progression. 40 CCs are more common in people living with dementia (PLWD) than people who do not have dementia, 41 complicating gaps in further evaluation of abnormal cognitive assessments. Understanding how CCs and multi‐morbidity impact patient willingness to pursue further care after an abnormal or borderline cognitive assessment is an essential aspect to offering BBMs as part of early detection in PC.

Physician support for participation in research is a strong motivator for research participation. A 2020 study found that having a PCP is the strongest predictor of successful follow‐up among trial participants. 42 PC practices can be effective in recruiting participants for clinical trials and can enhance the diversity of clinical trial populations. Practice‐Based Research Networks (PBRNs) were established in the late 1990s to designate a group of ambulatory practices devoted principally to the PC of patients and working to identify and frame research questions to improve the practice of PC. A 2007 nationwide survey of 111 PC PBRNs in the United States found that 27% provided services entirely to recruit and implement studies from investigators outside of PC and 59% of networks implemented research ideas from both PC leaders in their network and outside investigators. 43 Over‐representation of minority and underinsured patients in PBRN practices offers promise for PC PBRN recruitment as a key strategy to enhance gaps of those populations often noted in clinical trial recruitment.

Despite the complexity of recruiting subjects with cognitive impairment to clinical research, more than 40% of patients with cognitive impairment reported willingness to participate in dementia research clinical drug trials. 44 In our implementation work, 43% of patients by a PC BHN expressed interest in research referral. 17 By exploring patient interest in research opportunities, the PC BHN has potential to offer diverse real‐world PC patients with cognitive impairment or subjective cognitive concerns participation in ADRD research. Such a function could be tremendously valuable in creating access to research opportunities to patients who might not otherwise encounter those opportunities, and offering value to the ADRD research community by improving the representativeness of potential research subjects compared to the general population.

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR PATIENT AND CAREGIVER SUPPORT

PLWD face significant challenges accessing support for their complex care needs. Disconnected system infrastructure, lack of comprehensive services to meet diverse needs, and inconsistent understanding of dementia are key gaps. 45 PLWD without identified caregivers experience poorer quality of life and often require institutional care earlier in the course of their disease compared to those with caregiver support. 46 Caregiving carries its own substantial burden: less than one third of caregivers report confidence in managing dementia‐related problems, less than one in five know how to access community services, and only 28% feel supported by their provider in addressing dementia‐related concerns. 47 In the United States, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience (GUIDE) model launched in 2024 and is designed to reduce strain on unpaid caregivers and enable PLWD to remain in their homes and communities through an alternative Medicare payment model. Despite the large and diverse group of organizations participating in the model, many PLWD, such as those who have Medicare Advantage, and their caregivers, will be unable to access the program.

The PC BHN is well positioned to provide patient and caregiver support, including connection to community resources to patients at the earliest stage of impairment and to those who do not have access to GUIDE or a similar program. Nursing interventions, across a variety of complex CCs, have demonstrated effectiveness in patient and caregiver support. 48 PC‐based navigation support for access to community resources has been found to be satisfying to patients and resulted in more than half of patients accessing one or more community services. 49

Despite available resources, patients and caregivers often under‐utilize support services due to lack of awareness or difficulty navigating access. 50 , 51 As a key team member in the cognitive assessment process, the PC BHN can identify support needs early and connect patients and caregivers with appropriate services. This proactive engagement has the potential to improve caregiver confidence, increase community service utilization, and strengthen long‐term support for PLWD.

BRAIN HEALTH NAVIGATOR SUSTAINABILITY

Sustaining the BHN role in primary care will require alignment with evolving payment models and recognition of its value in improving patient outcomes. Although the economic impact of a PC BHN is unknown, the economic impact of patient navigator programs has been evaluated in other settings, primarily in cancer screening, and have found net financial benefit to provider practices. 52 With patient navigation being a key aspect of PCMHs, a variety of payment models are being investigated. A 2021 report by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine on PC called for a hybrid payment approach, including a mix of fee‐for‐service and population‐based payments, to support PC practice transformation. A 2024 Medicare Physician Fee Schedule final rule provided payment for principal illness navigation services to help patients and caregivers in care treatment for serious illnesses. This rule was viewed as providing access to care navigators and access to PC support for holistic care of patients and their families. 53 A variety of advanced payment innovation models are being tested in the United States, which support and promote PC‐based strategies aligned with a PC BHN. Developing sustainable models of supporting PC‐based BHN services is a critical element of success for future early detection and accurate diagnosis clinical pathways in the United States.

CONCLUSION

The PC BHN is a central and essential part of the early detection and diagnosis care team that can mitigate the time limitations of PCPs, standardize high‐quality PC‐based evaluation of cognitive impairment, and improve the appropriateness of specialty care referrals. The design of the BHN role and function should be part of the PCMH team and integrated wholly within the PC space to achieve its potential. Essential functions of a PC BHN include SDM, facilitation of brain health diagnostic evaluation, connection to specialty care, connection to research opportunities, proactive patient and caregiver education and support through the diagnostic journey, and connection to community resources. Sustainability of a PC BHN relies upon advanced payment models, including care navigation mechanisms, and requires further research and policy evaluation.

The BHN is the linchpin necessary to enable feasibility of early detection of ADRD in PC by creating capacity and capability of the primary care team. To be effective, the PC BHN must be intentionally and contextually developed, implemented, and supported within PC and with PC leaders.
