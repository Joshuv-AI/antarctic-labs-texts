Management of gynecomastia in adolescence and adults: the clinical practice guidelines from the Italian Society of Andrology and Sexual Medicine (SIAMS)
Authors: Carlotta Pozza, Riccardo Selice, Arcangelo Barbonetti, Valeria Hasenmajer, Francesco Lotti, Davide Menafra, Daniela Pasquali, Andrea Sansone, Andrea M Isidori, Vincenzo Rochira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGynecomastia, defined as the benign proliferation of male breast glandular tissue, is a common condition in both adolescence and adulthood, often associated with physical discomfort and psychological distress. Despite its high prevalence, clinical management is frequently inconsistent and lacks standardized, evidence-based recommendations.MethodsThe Italian Society of Andrology and Sexual Medicine (SIAMS) convened a multidisciplinary expert panel to develop updated clinical practice guidelines for the evaluation and management of gynecomastia. Recommendations were formulated based on a systematic review of the available literature and developed through expert consensus according to the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation) system. The document was critically reviewed and approved by the SIAMS Executive Committee.ResultsThe guidelines address physiological, pharmacological, pathological, congenital, and genetic forms of gynecomastia, providing a structured diagnostic and therapeutic approach. Clinical assessment, including detailed history and examination, is the cornerstone of diagnosis, and should be complemented by targeted hormonal and imaging investigations when appropriate. Observation is recommended for physiological or recent-onset cases. Pharmacological therapy may be considered in selected idiopathic or painful forms, whereas testosterone replacement should be restricted to men with proven hypogonadism. Surgery is indicated for long-standing fibrotic gynecomastia or significant psychological distress. Psychological support is also advised, especially for adolescents.ConclusionsThese SIAMS Guidelines provide an evidence-based, standardized framework for the diagnosis and management of gynecomastia, promoting individualized, multidisciplinary care and identifying priorities for future research.Keywords: Gynecomastia, Male breast disorders, Testosterone, Estradiol, Prolactin, Adolescence, Adult, Hypogonadism

Methods

The Italian Society of Andrology and Sexual Medicine (SIAMS) established an expert task force to develop updated guidelines on gynecomastia aimed at providing practical guidance for the clinical evaluation and management of gynecomastia in adolescents and adults. A multidisciplinary panel of experts in andrology, endocrinology, pediatric endocrinology, and related fields was convened by SIAMS and identified the main clinical domains relevant to gynecomastia management, including pathophysiology, causes, diagnostic evaluation, laboratory assessment, imaging, and therapeutic approaches. Key clinical questions were formulated using the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, Study design) when applicable. A structured literature search was conducted primarily using the PubMed/MEDLINE database to identify relevant publications addressing the epidemiology, pathophysiology, diagnosis, and treatment of gynecomastia. The search included studies published up to June 2025, using combinations of keywords such as “gynecomastia”, “male breast enlargement”, “etiology”, “diagnosis”, “hormonal evaluation”, “imaging”, and “treatment”. Additional studies were identified through manual review of reference lists. Both observational studies and interventional studies were considered, with particular attention to diagnostic strategies, endocrine causes, drug-induced gynecomastia, and medical or surgical treatment options. When high-quality comparative evidence was not available, recommendations were based on best available evidence together with expert consensus and clinical judgement. The strength of recommendations and the certainty of the supporting evidence were classified according to the principles of the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework [1], in which a strength rating 1 corresponds to a strong recommendation (“we recommend”) and a rating of 2 to a weaker recommendation (“we suggest”). The quality of evidence is categorized into four levels, visually represented as follows: ØOOO for “very low-quality evidence,” ØØOO for “low quality,” ØØØO for “moderate quality,” and ØØØØ for “high quality.” Given the limited availability of high-quality comparative studies in several areas of gynecomastia management, some statements reflect expert consensus supported by the available literature and should not be interpreted as evidence-based recommendations.

Draft recommendations were developed by the writing group, coordinated by the senior author and two members of the SIAMS Guideline Committee, and subsequently discussed by the entire panel until consensus was reached. The final document was reviewed and approved by the SIAMS panel and Executive Committee and was made available to SIAMS members for two weeks on the society’s website, siams.info, to collect additional feedback before submission to the Journal of Endocrinological Investigation for international peer review.

Introduction

Gynecomastia is defined as a generalized benign growth of glandular tissue in the male breast leading to palpable and/or visible swelling of breast tissue in boys or men. Gynecomastia is the most common breast disorder in men and is generally considered significant in adult men when there is palpable breast tissue [2, 3]. The etymology comes from Greek origin combining the words gyneaikaco (female woman) and mastòs (breast), probably via the French term gynécomastie or the German terms Gynäkomastie or gynecomasti. The first known use of gynecomastia was in 1881 (https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/gynecomastia). Gynecomastia is a common observation in the general population [4], and can be unilateral or bilateral, most commonly the latter [5]. Gynecomastia must be distinguished from pseudogynecomastia (lipomastia), which is characterized by excessive fat deposition without glandular proliferation, by comparing the results of palpation of the subareolar fat with the adjacent subcutaneous fat in the anterior axillary fold or other areas of the chest wall [6, 7].

Physiological gynecomastia occurs in neonates, adolescents, and older men, reflecting transient hormonal changes, while pathological gynecomastia can be secondary to endocrine disorders, systemic diseases, drug-induced effects, or idiopathic causes. The clinical approach to gynecomastia is often inconsistent, with some cases receiving only a superficial assessment, while others undergo unnecessary investigations. The diagnostic process should be systematic and comprehensive, integrating a thorough medical history, a careful physical examination, and, when appropriate, biochemical and imaging studies. Beyond its medical implications, gynecomastia can significantly impact psychological well-being, affecting body image, self-esteem, and social interactions, particularly in adolescents and young adults. Several guidelines and expert opinions exist regarding the diagnostic and therapeutic approach to gynecomastia, yet no universal consensus has been established [3, 4, 6–8]. The aim of this document is to provide a structured and evidence-based approach to the evaluation and management of gynecomastia, offering recommendations that align with current clinical practice while addressing existing gaps in knowledge and patient care (Fig. 1).

Diagnostic and management algorithm for gynecomastia. Initial clinical assessment (history and physical examination, with ultrasound when needed) distinguishes true gynecomastia from pseudogynecomastia. Physiological causes are first considered; otherwise, a targeted etiological work-up is performed. Imaging and specialist evaluation are reserved for suspicious or inconclusive cases. Management is tailored to the underlying cause and clinical presentation

Epidemiology

The prevalence of gynecomastia can vary widely (4%–69%) and is influenced by factors such as age, underlying medical conditions, medications, and lifestyle factors [4, 9–11]. Male patients complaining of gynecomastia have increased significantly in recent years [12]. According to later epidemiological data, the rate of men reporting breast soreness would seem to have risen from 0.8% to 2.4% in 20 years [13]. According to different studies, 30 to 65% of adult males have palpable breast tissue [4, 8, 11, 14, 15]. Gynecomastia can occur at any age but is most common during three distinct life stages in males: infancy, puberty, and older age.

Newborn and infancy

Some estimates suggest that temporary breast enlargement occurs in 60–90% of infants. In infants, gynecomastia is mainly due to the influence of maternal hormones (i.e. high fetal blood levels of estradiol and progesterone) that stimulate breast tissue in the newborn. Another mechanism is the increased conversion of steroid hormone precursors to sex steroids and increased aromatization of androgen because of neonatal surge of luteinizing hormone (LH). Neonatal gynecomastia may persist for several weeks after birth and may be associated with a milky breast discharge called “witch’s milk” [3, 6]. Boys also have a “mini-puberty” occurring during the first months after birth [16], with a temporary increase in adult serum gonadotropin concentrations resulting in an imbalance of estradiol to testosterone ratio with serum estradiol concentrations exceeding that of serum testosterone [4, 17], a condition that can induce gynecomastia.

Puberty

Pubertal gynecomastia is quite common and is estimated to affect up to 70% of adolescent boys [10]. It is typically a benign and self-limiting condition that usually resolves within a few years as hormone levels stabilize [9].

Aging

Gynecomastia prevalence increases with age, and it is more common in older men. In adult males, the prevalence is estimated to be around 30% to 50%, with variations based on different population studies. 57% of healthy older men [15], and more than 70% of hospitalized elderly men [8] present gynecomastia. Drug-induced gynecomastia accounts for 20% to 25% of cases [18], reaching the 80% in some studies [19]. Even with detailed evaluation, there is no identifiable cause in about 25% of cases.

Pathophysiology

Gynecomastia mainly occurs as a consequence of a change in the endocrine balance involving androgens and estrogens at the level of the breast tissue [6, 20]. Outside the context of some genetic causes with self-explanatory pathogenetic mechanism (e.g. aromatase excess syndrome [21]), the exact pathophysiological mechanisms resulting in the development of gynecomastia have not been completely elucidated [4]. Several factors can contribute to this imbalance, from physiological or para-physiological conditions, such as puberty and aging, to more severe conditions, including hormone-secreting tumors.

From an endocrine point of view, several hormones are involved in the pathogenesis of gynecomastia [22], among which the most important are estrogens and androgens. Activation of the estrogen receptor acts as a proliferative signal for breast tissue, whereas activation of the androgen receptor results in inhibitory effects on tissue growth. The delicate balance between these two antagonist systems influences the growth of breast tissues during development, possibly providing a rationale to transient, benign forms of gynecomastia occurring during puberty and with aging. Both conditions of decreased androgen activity and estrogen excess can thus be involved in the pathogenesis of gynecomastia. Gonadotropins also play an important role in the hormonal regulation underlying gynecomastia. Luteinizing hormone (LH) stimulates testicular Leydig cells to produce testosterone, while follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) is involved in testicular function and spermatogenesis. Alterations in gonadotropin secretion may therefore contribute to conditions of androgen deficiency or imbalance between androgen and estrogen action. In primary hypogonadism, increased LH and FSH levels reflect impaired testicular testosterone production, which may favor the development of gynecomastia through reduced androgenic inhibition of breast tissue growth. Conversely, in secondary hypogonadism, reduced gonadotropin secretion results in decreased testosterone production with similar downstream effects. In addition, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) can stimulate Leydig cells and increase testicular estrogen production. Pathological elevations of hCG, such as those occurring in certain germ cell tumors or other hCG-secreting neoplasms, may therefore contribute to the development of gynecomastia through increased estrogenic stimulation. It is important to note, however, that gynecomastia might occur in conditions in which the ratio between estrogen and androgen activity is affected, despite no overt endocrine derangement [4, 23]. Several forms of male hypogonadism feature gynecomastia in association with a decrease in testosterone levels [24], with or without reaching overtly pathological levels, such as in the case of Klinefelter Syndrome [25–27]. Likewise, forms of impaired receptor activity, such as in partial androgen insensitivity syndromes [28, 29] or in individuals with higher number of CAG repeats in the gene encoding the androgen receptor [30], can result in the development of gynecomastia. Other environmental factors, such as obesity, alcohol use, use of appearance and performance enhancing drugs, recreational drug use, several medications, and malnutrition are also affecting the balance between androgens and estrogens [7]. In the newborn, transplacental maternal estrogens and progesterone transfer can stimulate breast tissue development, jointly with increased conversion of steroid hormone precursors to sex steroids and increased aromatization of androgen because of neonatal surge of luteinizing hormone (LH). Indeed, neonatal gynecomastia may persist for several weeks after birth [3, 22, 31–33], beyond maternal hormones withdrawal [4] and moreover gestational age at birth seems to affect the size of neonatal breast tissue [34]. Additionally, other hormones might be involved in the pathogenesis of gynecomastia, such as prolactin, IGF-1 and progesterone, whose receptors are present and coexpressed in breast tissue [35–37]. Hyperprolactinemia can exert suppressive effects on the hypothalamus-pituitary-gonadal axis and a direct effect on epithelial cell proliferation has also been postulated: however, the suppressive effects on the axis might decrease estrogen action, thus limiting the potential risk of developing gynecomastia [38].

Causes of gynecomastia

Physiological

Recommendations

R1. We suggest avoiding starting an extensive diagnostic work-up before ruling out physiological causes of gynecomastia, as unnecessary investigations may lead to overmedicalization and overdiagnosis (Expert opinion).

Evidence

Breast development in males can occur normally during three phases of life, neonatal, puberty and aging. Physiologic gynecomastia is usually transient, bilateral, smooth, firm and symmetrically distributed under the areola. Most of the enlargement is due to proliferation of stroma and not of breast ducts.

Newborn and infants: Palpable breast tissue can be observed overtly or palpably in newborn as a consequence of transplacental exposure to maternal estrogens [31] and gestational age at birth seems to affect the size of neonatal breast tissue [34]. Some studies have reported breast tissue to be more frequent and pronounced in girls than in boys [39], independently from body weight, while others did not observe gender difference in the occurrence or size of breast tissue [34]. Neonatal breast enlargement is usually transient and commonly regresses spontaneously within the first weeks after birth as maternal hormone levels decline. However, palpable breast tissue may persist for several months in otherwise healthy infants [17] and, in isolation, does not necessarily indicate an underlying pathological condition. Further evaluation should therefore be reserved for cases characterized by progressive enlargement, asymmetry, signs of inflammation, or the presence of additional clinical findings suggesting an endocrine or systemic disorder.

Puberty

One-half of adolescent males will experience gynecomastia, with a peak incidence between 13 and 14 years of age or pubertal stages G3 and G4. Pubertal estrogen production begins prior to testosterone production due to early maturation of aromatase, and the increase of estrogen to testosterone ratio could promote glandular proliferation. In addition, other mediators, as GH and IGF-1, can sustain breast development [40]. During puberty, the interplay between sex steroids and the somatotropic axis is critical. GH and IGF-1, through their effects on GnRH neurons and gonadotropin secretion, promote pubertal development and indirectly influence breast tissue proliferation. Locally, IGF-1 enhances steroidogenesis and may amplify estrogen activity, while estradiol modulates GH secretion and suppresses IGF-1 production. This hormonal dynamic fosters a balance essential for normal pubertal progression and contributes to the transient nature of pubertal gynecomastia [41]. Recent studies have shown a significant positive association between breast enlargement and IGF-1 levels during puberty, namely boys who developed gynecomastia reached an age at peak height velocity earlier compared to boys who did not develop gynecomastia [42, 43]. Regression spontaneously occurs in 75–90% adolescents within 6 months to 1–2 years in most cases [44], when the ratio is restored [22, 45]. This means that in case of primary pubertal gynecomastia the correct conduct is observation and reassurance without specific treatment [44]. However, in a subset of adolescents, gynecomastia may persist beyond the typical period of spontaneous resolution, causing significant physical or psychological discomfort [46]. Persistent gynecomastia is often associated with sustained hormonal imbalances, obesity, or other underlying conditions [7]. In these cases, where gynecomastia remains stable or progresses, therapeutic intervention may be warranted to alleviate symptoms and improve quality of life.

Aging

Up to 65% of men may develop gynecomastia during aging, largely as a consequence of changes in the balance between androgenic and estrogenic activity [22]. With advancing age, circulating testosterone levels tend to decline, while several factors, including increased adiposity and enhanced peripheral aromatase activity may contribute to relatively greater estrogenic influence at the breast tissue level. In many cases, this imbalance reflects not necessarily an absolute increase in circulating estradiol, but rather a relative shift in the estrogen-to-androgen activity ratio.

Age-related hormonal changes may further modify this balance. Serum sex hormone binding globulin (SHBG) concentrations increase with age which reduces the fraction of bioavailable testosterone to a greater extent than that of estradiol due to the higher binding affinity of SHBG for androgens. As a consequence, the bioavailable estradiol-to-testosterone ratio may increase, favoring estrogenic effects at the breast tissue level [45, 47]. However, while gynecomastia in adults is frequently attributed to physiological aging or medications, these apparent causes should not preclude a thorough investigation to exclude underlying pathological condition.

Remarks

Gynecomastia during neonatal, pubertal, and aging phases is typically physiological and self-limiting condition in most cases, requiring only observation and reassurance. Understanding the transient nature of these changes helps avoid unnecessary interventions. Persistent gynecomastia beyond the expected timeframe warrants careful evaluation. Psychological distress associated with gynecomastia can significantly impact quality of life, self-esteem, and social interactions and underscores the need for a holistic approach to management, addressing both physical and emotional aspects. Differentiating physiological gynecomastia from pathological causes is essential across all life stages. Clinical history, hormonal evaluation, and consideration of patient age provide critical context for determining when intervention is necessary.

Recommendations

Evidence

Drug-related gynecomastia is one of the most common forms of adult-onset gynecomastia, since gynecomastia could be caused by drugs in 10–25% of cases [3, 6, 15, 48]. Drugs having direct hormonal activity have been widely associated with the occurrence of gynecomastia, since they could contribute to the imbalance of the estrogen to androgen ratio, therefore inducing breast gland enlargement [3, 22, 48].

Compounds having stimulating activity on estrogen receptors have been demonstrated to cause gynecomastia and include: exogenous estrogens used for the treatment of prostate cancer, estrogenic creams used to treat baldness, foods derived by animals treated with estrogenic hormonal stimulation or supplementary foods containing phytoestrogens, manufacture-derived exposure to estrogen-containing drugs, abuse of substance like marijuana which plant has been suggested to contain estrogens, and cardiac agents such as digitalis glycosides having structural similarities with phytoestrogens. Furthermore, for other drugs which have been related to the appearance of gynecomastia with an unclear pathophysiological mechanism, a possible estrogen-related activity has been hypothesized: for example, for antitumoral chemotherapeutic drugs such as alkylating agents, it has been assumed that gynecomastia could be induced by a relative estrogenic excess deriving from increased levels of LH which could on one hand compensate for testosterone deficiency creating a picture of subclinical hypogonadism, on the other hand stimulate increased estrogen production, while for proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) such as omeprazole and esomeprazole currently used for the medical treatment of peptic ulcer, it has been hypothesized that gynecomastia could be caused by an estrogenic excess derived from the inhibition of estradiol catabolism by cytochrome P450 (CYP) 3A4 [48–50]. Drugs acting with anti-androgenic effects have also been frequently associated with the occurrence of gynecomastia: in particular, they include androgen receptor antagonists and GnRH agonists used for the treatment of prostate cancer, 5-alfa reductase inhibitors acting through the reduction of DHT levels such as finasteride and dutasteride used for the symptomatic treatment of prostate hyperplasia, and ketoconazole (used as antifungal and adrenostatic therapy) acting through steroidogenesis and inhibition of testosterone synthesis.

Furthermore, spironolactone, an aldosterone receptor antagonist currently used for the treatment of heart failure and arterial hypertension, has been frequently associated with the occurrence of gynecomastia through multiple antiandrogenic mechanisms. In particular, spironolactone may inhibit testosterone synthesis, antagonize the androgen receptor, and interfere with androgen metabolism thereby reducing androgenic activity and promoting a relative imbalance between androgenic and estrogenic effects at the breast tissue level [6, 48, 51].

In addition, hormonal therapies used both for the treatment of endocrinological disorders and to increase sport performance have been frequently associated with gynecomastia and are considered the most common cause of gynecomastia when used as doping substances, including androgens and anabolic agents that could be aromatized into estrogens, human chorionic gonadotropin and growth hormone [22].

Lastly, other drugs have been associated to gynecomastia with uncertain pathophysiological mechanisms; for some of these, including cardiac agents such as calcium-channel blockers, anti-ulcer drugs like anti-histamine receptor 2 cimetidine and neuroleptics with dopamine-receptor antagonist activity it has been hypothesized that gynecomastia could be caused by secondary hypogonadism induced by iatrogenic hyperprolactinemia, whereas for antiretroviral therapies used for HIV treatment a direct mammotrope effect and for statins used for treatment of hypercholesterolemia a reduction of testosterone secondary to decreased cholesterol synthesis have been supposed [6, 15, 22, 48].

Remarks

Drug-induced gynecomastia represents one of the most common causes of adult-onset gynecomastia. Several classes of drugs have been implicated, primarily through mechanisms that disrupt the estrogen-to-androgen ratio. Medications with direct hormonal activity, such as exogenous estrogens, anabolic steroids, and certain anti-androgenic therapies, can promote glandular breast enlargement. Drugs with estrogenic effects, including digitalis glycosides, phytoestrogen-containing supplements, and some chemotherapeutic agents, may induce gynecomastia via direct receptor stimulation or secondary hormonal imbalances. Anti-androgenic drugs, such as androgen receptor antagonists, GnRH agonists, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, and spironolactone, are commonly associated with gynecomastia due to their interference with androgen synthesis and action. Additionally, some medications with unclear pathophysiological mechanisms, including PPIs, calcium-channel blockers, H2-receptor antagonists, neuroleptics, antiretroviral therapies, and statins, have been linked to gynecomastia, possibly through effects on hormone metabolism or secondary hypogonadism. Given the wide range of implicated drugs, a careful medication history is essential when evaluating patients with gynecomastia to identify potential iatrogenic causes and guide appropriate management.

Endocrine (hormonal) causes of gynecomastia

Evidence

Hormones are the main regulators of breast enlargement in both males and females, and endocrine conditions are among the most common causes for pathologic gynecomastia. Any endocrine disorder altering the estradiol/testosterone ratio activity in the breast can induce gynecomastia in otherwise healthy adult males, even though other hormones seem to play a role in pathologic conditions. Aside from iatrogenic causes of increased estradiol or decreased testosterone such as treatment with anabolic steroids or unintentional exposure to estrogens, endocrine causes of gynecomastia can be therefore divided into (I) conditions causing absolute or relative reduction in testosterone levels, (II) conditions characterized by increased estrogen synthesis. The latter can be associated to low or high levels of testosterone.

Testosterone and estradiol

Primary and secondary hypogonadism are both characterized by low testosterone levels, resulting in a decrease of the inhibitory effect of androgens on the growth and differentiation of the breast tissue, and an increased estrogen to testosterone ratio. Causes of primary and secondary hypogonadism are too numerous to count in this manuscript but are available within guidelines on male hypogonadism [24]. Estrogen activity is also influenced by aromatase-mediated conversion of androgen precursors [15]. In hypogonadal men, adrenal production of steroid precursors may remain relatively preserved despite reduced testicular testosterone secretion. This condition may contribute to a relative imbalance between estrogenic and androgenic activity rather than reflecting a consistent absolute increase in circulating estradiol levels.

In primary hypogonadism testicular failure causes an increase in LH secretion, which also enhances the activity of aromatase [52].

Klinefelter syndrome (KS) is the most common cause of primary hypogonadism, characterized by early testicular failure, increased LH levels and testicular degeneration [53, 54]. Patients with KS often experience gynecomastia both due to low circulating testosterone and increased aromatase activity [25].

In secondary hypogonadism, low testosterone is associated with low or inappropriately normal levels of gonadotropins, that can be either due to hypothalamic/pituitary organic failure or to functional conditions such as aging, concomitant illnesses and obesity [55–58]. In obese patients, increased aromatase activity has also been described [59].

In some cases, gynecomastia may serve as an early indicator of hormone-secreting tumors, which can include Leydig cell tumors (LCTs), Sertoli cell tumors, granulosa cell tumors, gonadal and extra-gonadal germ cell tumors [60]. Such neoplasms can disrupt the balance between androgens and estrogens, leading to clinical manifestations that range from gynecomastia to infertility. Leydig cell tumors can synthesize androgens, estrogens or both, depending on their functional status, however while gynecomastia is often cited as a frequent clinical presentation of LCTs [61], some authors found it to be less prevalent, suggesting that gynecomastia might not always be a prominent feature in all populations with LCTs [62]. Germ-cell tumors may produce beta-human chorionic gonadotropin, which can stimulate estrogen synthesis, thus suppressing gonadotropins and exacerbating the estrogen-androgen imbalance with consequent clinically manifest gynecomastia [63]. It is reported that 5–7% of GCT present with gynecomastia with an equal incidence between seminomas and non-seminomatous germ cell tumors [64]. The early recognition of gynecomastia as a paraneoplastic phenomenon can significantly impact clinical outcomes .

Other endocrine conditions characterized by the development of gynecomastia are thyroid disorders, GH/IGF-1 excess or defect and hyperprolactinemia.

Thyroid hormones

Thyroid dysfunction leads to gynecomastia mostly disrupting the hypothalamic- pituitary- gonadal axis [65] and breast enlargement can be present as a concomitant – and even first [66] – symptom in both hyperthyroid and hypothyroid patients. In longstanding severe hypothyroidism, prolactin levels are significantly increased due to high TRH levels, thus leading to hypogonadism. On the other hand, men with hyperthyroidism show increased levels of SHBG and consequent relative hypogonadism, which in turn increase LH secretion and aromatization of androgens to estrogens, possibly promoting the development of gynecomastia.

GH and IGF-1

GH and IGF-1 are key mediators of estrogen and progesterone activity, and estrogens induce expression of IGF-1 receptors on breast tissue. The stimulatory effect of IGF-1 on breast formation may be synergized by estradiol [42].

Gynecomastia has been described in acromegaly patients [67], and as side effect of GH treatment. Studies on correlation with IGF-1 values in patients with gynecomastia have yielded conflicting results [42, 68]. As for prolactinomas, GH-secreting pituitary adenomas can also cause gynecomastia by inducing hypogonadism for compression or pituitary stalk damage.

Prolactin

The mechanisms of hyperprolactinemia-induced gynecomastia are still under debate.

Hyperprolactinemia does not appear to directly stimulate breast gland proliferation in males. Rather, elevated prolactin levels may suppress the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis, leading to secondary hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and reduced testosterone secretion. The resulting decrease in androgenic inhibition of breast tissue may favor the development of gynecomastia.

Prolactin and progesterone receptors have been identified in male breast tissue [22, 35], suggesting that prolactin might have a modulatory role in breast physiology [69]. However, current evidence indicates that hyperprolactinemia contributes to gynecomastia primarily through its effects on gonadal function rather than through a direct mammotropic effect [70].

Remarks

The recommendation to exclude pathological entities associated with gynecomastia in adults, even in the presence of an apparent modifiable cause, places a high value on detecting life-threatening organic causes. In clinical practice, the diagnostic evaluation should follow a pragmatic prioritization based on prevalence and clinical relevance. Physiological conditions (puberty, aging and obesity) and drug- or substance-related causes should be considered first, followed by hypogonadism or endocrine disorders, chronic liver or kidney disease, and finally rare causes such as hormone-secreting tumors. The diagnosis of an organic cause of gynecomastia imposes to reevaluate gynecomastia after the removal of or the effective treatment of the primary disorders, leaving medical or surgical treatment of gynecomastia only in case of persistent breast enlargement.

Other causes

Evidence

Aside from endocrine disorders, several other conditions have been associated with the development of gynecomastia. For most, underlying causes are ultimately an imbalance in testosterone and estrogen levels, including reduced testosterone bioavailability.

Kidney disease

In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), gynecomastia is often reported as an adverse drug-related event [71]. However, both hypogonadism and hyperprolactinemia have been reported in men with chronic kidney disease (CKD) as well [72], further contributing to the development of breast tissue.

Liver disease

Gynecomastia has been described in patients with liver conditions, including HCV infection and cirrhosis [73]. Male patients with liver insufficiency often develop hypogonadism [74]. Reduced free testosterone availability and increased estrogen levels due to altered liver metabolism and SHBG synthesis are characteristic of cirrhosis and other liver conditions [75]. Actually, increased SHBG conditions are often characterized by altered T/E ratio, since SHBG binds testosterone more avidly than estrogen. Among the clinical consequences, increase in breast tissue has been described, and a recent epidemiological study has identified a fivefold risk for death to hepatic failure in patients with gynecomastia [76]. It is worth mentioning that several cirrhosis patients have an associated history of alcohol abuse, which is an independent factor contributing to the development of gynecomastia.

HIV infection and treatment

In men living with HIV, earlier reports identified a significantly increased prevalence of gynecomastia, both before starting highly active antiretroviral therapy (HAART) and, more frequently, afterwards [77, 78], with an estimated prevalence around 2.8–2.9% [79, 80]. The occurrence of unilateral or bilateral gynecomastia in men living with HIV was associated to many pathological mechanisms. First, the concomitant presence of HCV-induced cirrhosis, alcohol and recreational drug use, along with treatment for other conditions such as neurological complications are non-HIV-related predisposing mechanisms [79]. Second, there is an increased prevalence of hypogonadism in men living with HIV that could alter the estrogen/testosterone ratio leading to breast tissue enlargement [81]. Among HAART-related proposed mechanisms is included the occurrence of lipodystrophy (peripheral fat tissue wasting), immune reconstitution and mitochondrial toxicity, and estrogen receptor modulation [82]. The relative importance of each one in the development and natural history of breast enlargement is still unclear. An association with protease inhibitors and efavirenz has been highlighted by cohort studies [77, 79, 83] but similar symptoms have been reported under other regimens as well [84]. Malignant breast cancer was described anecdotally in men with HIV developing gynecomastia, suggesting a cautious and thorough evaluation at diagnosis [85, 86]. Proposed treatments for benign enlargement included local DHT injections [87] and tamoxifene [88]. However, many cases also showed a spontaneous resolution.

Alcohol

An association between alcohol abuse and gynecomastia has been confirmed by recent population studies [89], reporting alcohol abuse as the second more prevalent risk factor, after medication, for gynecomastia in a Danish nationwide register cohort. Aside from alcoholic cirrhosis and liver disease, an independent effect of alcohol on SHBG [90] and Leydig cells [91] has been proposed.

Recreational drugs

Use of cannabis has been associated with gynecomastia. A few mechanisms, such as increased prolactin or decreased available testosterone, have been proposed [92]. Even if a recent study suggests an association [93], the exact physiological mechanism is still under debate [94].

Remarks

Disease- or behavioral-associated causes for gynecomastia should be investigated to identify modifiable factors only after potential life-threatening conditions, such as hormone-secreting neoplasms, have been excluded. Modern regimens of HAART in people living with HIV are less associated with lipodystrophy and gynecomastia, but a careful and thorough evaluation and potential discussion with the infectious disease specialist is recommended after exclusion of HIV-associated hormonal imbalances.

Congenital and genetic causes of gynecomastia

R1. We recommend ruling out genetic causes in prepubertal gynecomastia (1 ØØØO).

R2. We suggest ruling out genetic causes in severe peripubertal gynecomastia (2 ØØØO).

Evidence

Pre -and peri-pubertal onset of gynecomastia may be due to genetic causes [7]. Estrogen excess due to aromatase excess syndrome is a monogenic, rare disease caused by gain-of-function mutations of the aromatase (CYP19A1) gene [21]. At present, around 30 cases have been described worldwide. Gynecomastia may occur during infancy around 5 to 7 years of age in these patients [95]. The hormonal analysis shows elevated serum estrogens (both estradiol and estrone), normal serum androstenedione, and prepubertal levels of serum total testosterone. Estrogen production derives from adrenal androgens (i.e. androstenedione) after aromatase conversion into estrogens. Beyond gynecomastia, these patients have accelerated growth and early onset of growth spurt possibly affecting final adult height. When gynecomastia occurs in the peripubertal period usually it is of mild degree [96].

Other genetic causes of prepubertal or peri-pubertal gynecomastia are (i) partial androgen resistance [97], including also androgen insensitivity linked to CAG repeat length such as Kennedy’s disease (spinal and bulbar muscular atrophy) [98], where the increased circulating testosterone favors its conversion to estradiol, (ii) Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome [99] and (iii) Carney complex [100] which are both due to increased aromatase activity and expression. Peutz-Jeghers Syndrome is characterized by gastrointestinal hamartomatous polyps and dark spots of the skin and mucous membranes (particularly around the mouth) [101] while Carney complex by myxomas, various endocrine cancers, and blue nevi [102]. Also, 11β-Hydroxylase and non-classical 21-hydroxylase deficiency may cause pre- or peri-pubertal gynecomastia in boys due to high serum levels of 17-OH progesterone and an imbalance between androgen and estrogens [103–105]. In this case, biochemical analysis is characterized by increased adrenal androgens that may be coupled with cortisol insufficiency [103–105].

Other genetic diseases leading to congenital hypogonadism may present with gynecomastia [7]. Among them congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism [106] and defects in steroid biosynthesis, such as P450 oxidoreductase deficiency, 17-hydroxylase/17,20-lyase and 3βHSD deficiency [7].

Ovotestis is a very rare congenital disorder of sexual development that may present with gynecomastia in the peripubertal period but is generally coupled with other anomalies of sexual development [107].

Remarks

The genetic diagnosis of aromatase excess syndrome should be guided by clinical and biochemical examinations and is based on CYP19A1 gene sequencing [21, 96, 108].

When congenital adrenal hyperplasia and defects of steroid biosynthesis are suspected, the genetic analysis should be oriented by the hormonal pattern found at biochemical analysis in order to select the candidate gene to be sequenced. Similarly, the suspicion of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and of defects of steroid biosynthesis requires confirming genetic analysis.

The clinical suspicion of ovotestis requires a more complex approach based on a multidisciplinary evaluation [109].

Evaluation

Clinical history and physical examination

Recommendations

R1. We recommend taking an accurate and thorough medical history of any patient complaining of gynecomastia, including comorbid conditions, concomitant medications, dietary habits, and changes in sexual functioning (1 ØØØØ).

R2. We recommend performing a structured clinical examination followed by palpation of breast region to discriminate a diagnosis of gynecomastia or pseudogynecomastia including genital examination (1 ØØØØ).

R3. We recommend distinguishing through clinical examination between unilateral and bilateral gynecomastia and to exclude eventual clinical signs of suspicious malignancy in unilateral forms (1 ØØØØ).

Evidence

The diagnostic work-up of gynecomastia begins with a thorough clinical evaluation, which includes an accurate medical history and a complete physical examination.

Medical history might provide some important clues on its pathogenesis. The onset of gynecomastia, including its suddenness and the exact timing (e.g. in association with puberty onset), is the first item to investigate when collecting medical history. Likewise, several other factors should be investigated, such as the use of medications, recreational drugs, and doping agents [15]. Nutritional status should also be investigated by enquiring on dietary regimens, also paying attention to products containing high dosages of phytoestrogens [110, 111]. Sexual history should also be collected, as a sudden loss of sexual desire, the onset of erectile dysfunction and the reduced frequency of morning erections might be suggestive of hypogonadism [24, 112]. Likewise, clinicians should look for telltale signs of other metabolic derangements associated with gynecomastia, such as edema suggestive of chronic kidney disease or liver failure, or goiter and bulging eyes suggestive of hyperthyroidism [7]. A psychological assessment is also recommended, as gynecomastia may have relevant psychological and psychosocial consequences [113]. A guideline-driven clinical examination should be performed, starting with the palpation of the breast region to distinguish true gynecomastia from pseudogynecomastia, the latter being characterized by increased subareolar fat without glandular enlargement. In this context, a crucial diagnostic role should be attributed to breast physical examination to assess the symmetry, firmness and tenderness of the subareolar tissue, which often allows clinicians to distinguish true gynecomastia from pseudogynecomastia. [4, 6, 51]. Evidence suggests that the reliability of physical examination also depends on how it is performed: in particular, the patient should lie flat on his back with his hands clasped beneath his head in order to facilitate the examination of the axillary region and the examiner should slowly squeeze the breast between the thumb and forefinger in order to assess the gland outer limits and size. In patients with true gynecomastia, a soft, elastic or tender or firm mass of tissue that is concentric with the nipple–areolar complex is palpable, whereas in patients with pseudogynecomastia, no such disk of tissue is demonstrable [6].

Gynecomastia generally features bilateral, painless and relatively firm glandular tissue located under the nipple. Gently squeezing the subareolar tissue to assess the presence of galactorrhea (discharge of milky liquid from the nipple) may also be performed during breast examination. Since gynecomastia can present as either unilateral or bilateral [4], with bilateral forms being slightly more common, a careful and symmetrical breast examination is necessary to differentiate the two. In unilateral cases, special attention must be given to signs of malignancy, including a hard, tender mass located beyond the areolar limits, as well as skin changes such as ulceration, nipple retraction, or bleeding [3, 6]. On top of breast evaluation, physical examination should be performed to assess signs of virilization; genital assessment and tumor marker evaluation should be performed to exclude testicular cancer, particularly in patients presenting with unexplained gynecomastia [112, 114, 115].

Remarks

A structured diagnostic approach to gynecomastia is essential to differentiate physiological from pathological cases. A detailed medical history should be collected to investigate the onset, progression, and potential contributing factors, including medications, recreational drugs, anabolic steroids, dietary phytoestrogens, and metabolic disorders. Particular attention should be given to sexual dysfunction, which may indicate underlying hypogonadism, and systemic signs suggestive of renal, hepatic, or thyroid dysfunction. A guideline-based physical examination remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, beginning with breast palpation to distinguish true gynecomastia from pseudogynecomastia, and including genital assessment. Evaluation for galactorrhea may also be performed, as it can occasionally suggest hyperprolactinemia or pituitary disease. However, the clinical evaluation of suspected hypogonadism should follow established diagnostic guidelines, as the manifestations of hypogonadism extend beyond those associated with hyperprolactinemia alone. The distinction between unilateral and bilateral forms is crucial, as unilateral gynecomastia requires careful assessment for malignancy, with attention to hard, irregular masses, nipple retraction, ulceration, or skin changes. Psychological assessment is recommended, as gynecomastia can significantly affect self-esteem and social well-being.

Laboratory

Recommendations

R1 We suggest evaluating LH, total testosterone, SHBG, albumin, PRL levels and β-HCG as the first diagnostic step. Measurement of estradiol may also be considered as part of the endocrine assessment, although its interpretation should be cautious due to analytical limitations and the lack of validated diagnostic thresholds. (Expert Opinion).

R2 We suggest including thyroid hormones, IGF-1, liver and kidney function evaluation along with any additional examination guided by patient history and clinical presentation (Expert Opinion).

Evidence

Since an endocrine dysregulation or imbalance is supposed to be the main determining factor for gynecomastia, it is unsurprising that laboratory assessment might be a necessity. However, some Authors suggest that accurate medical history might spare the need for laboratory assessment [7] if patients have no remarkable findings, such as occurring in pubertal or late-onset gynecomastia.

If the clinical scenario deserves further investigation, a first-line approach would inevitably require an assessment of endocrine status (Table 2). Testosterone measurement should be done in the morning, after an overnight fast. As gynecomastia is a common symptom associated with hypogonadism, it is also recommended to measure SHBG and albumin levels to calculate free and bioavailable testosterone using the Vermeulen formula [116] (Table 2). Estradiol measurement may provide additional information in selected cases, particularly when clinical suspicion of estrogen excess or hormone-secreting tumors exists. However, clinicians should interpret estradiol levels with caution, as commonly used immunoassays have limited accuracy at the low concentrations typically observed in men [117–121]. When available, more reliable analytical techniques such as liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC/MS) should be preferred [119] (Table 2). As already stated, clinicians should also consider that while T and E2 levels might be in the reference ranges, their ratio might be imbalanced [95].

On top of T and E2, diagnostic assessment should include measurement of LH and FSH, prolactin, TSH, GH and IGF-1 and, based on clinical suspicion, biomarkers of testicular cancer (including α-fetoprotein and hCG) (Table 2). In case of suspicion of hypogonadism clinical examinations should be performed according to guidelines for the management of male hypogonadism [24]. Specifically, gonadotropin levels can guide the clinician towards other conditions affecting the endocrine balance between androgens and estrogens: increased LH levels, being a marker of impaired testicular response, suggest a diagnosis of subclinical hypogonadism [112, 122], whereas high T levels associated with suppressed FSH and LH could be suggestive of use of androgen anabolic steroids [22] or of endogenous testosterone secretion stimulated by betaHCG, as occurring in testicular tumors [63, 123–125]. Thyroid function is usually assessed by measurement of TSH levels, although it might be useful to also consider dosing thyroid hormones (fT3, fT4) in selected cases. GH and IGF-1 can sustain breast development [40]; this is of paramount importance in puberty, when the interplay between sex steroids and the somatotropic axis is critical. GH and IGF-1, through their effects on GnRH neurons and gonadotropin secretion, promote pubertal development and indirectly influence breast tissue proliferation. Locally, IGF-1 enhances steroidogenesis and may amplify estrogen activity, while estradiol modulates GH secretion and suppresses IGF-1 production. These hormonal dynamics foster a balance essential for normal pubertal progression and contribute to the transient nature of pubertal gynecomastia [41]. Other laboratory analyses might be required according to clinical suspicion (Table 2).

Remarks

When laboratory assessment is needed, it should follow a structured approach. Morning testosterone measurement is recommended, alongside SHBG and albumin to calculate free and bioavailable testosterone. Measurement of estradiol may be considered in selected clinical scenarios suggestive of estrogen excess or hormone-secreting conditions. However, its clinical interpretation is limited by the lack of validated thresholds and by methodological issues related to commonly used assays, favoring more reliable techniques such as LC/MS. Further hormonal investigations should include LH, prolactin, as well as tumor markers (hCG and α-fetoprotein) when testicular malignancy is suspected. In selected cases, additional tests may be warranted based on clinical suspicion, including TSH, IGF-1, renal and liver function along with infectious disease serologies (HIV, HCV, HBV), adrenal precursors such as 17-hydroxyprogesterone and androstenedione, especially when androgen excess or adrenal enzyme defects are suspected.

Imaging and core needle biopsy

Recommendations

R1 We recommend breast imaging to assess gynecomastia when physical examination is equivocal or suspicious for breast cancer (1 ØØØØ).

R2 We suggest ultrasound as the initial imaging study of male breast, especially in men younger than age 35, and eventually in men younger than age 45 (2 ØØØO).

R3 We suggest using ultrasound beside physical examination and gynecomastia-related symptoms and duration assessment to better plan medical therapy and monitor its efficacy over time (2 ØØOO).

R4 We recommend mammography if physical examination is strongly suspicious and/or ultrasound doubtful for breast cancer, prior to core needle biopsy (1 ØØØØ).

R5 We recommend core needle biopsy when physical examination and mammography outcomes are strongly suspicious for malignancy, and ultrasound can assist in lesion characterization and guide core needle biopsy (1 ØØØØ).

R6 We recommend clinical and ultrasound evaluation of the testes to exclude a palpable or non-palpable testicular tumor when gynecomastia is confirmed (1 ØØØO).

Evidence

The most recent “American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria for the evaluation of the symptomatic male breast” [126] report that: (i) in men with clinical findings consistent with gynecomastia or pseudogynecomastia, no imaging is routinely recommended; (ii) if an indeterminate breast mass is identified, the initial recommended imaging study is ultrasound (US) in men younger than age 25, and mammography or digital breast tomosynthesis in men aged 25 and older; (iii) if physical examination is suspicious for a male breast cancer, breast imaging is recommended prior to core needle biopsy, mammography is recommended as the initial imaging modality regardless of age, and ultrasound can assist in lesion characterization and guide core needle biopsy.

So far, it has been reported that most men with gynecomastia and related symptoms can be diagnosed by physical examination without imaging [30, 67, 74]. Physical examination represents the cornerstone of the diagnostic evaluation of male breast enlargement. In most patients, careful clinical assessment allows the clinician to distinguish gynecomastia from pseudogynecomastia and to identify features suggestive of malignancy. In particular, physical examination shows high sensitivity and negative predictive value for excluding breast cancer when typical benign features are present. Gynecomastia is the most common cause of a palpable mass, breast enlargement, or pain [126–129] and is bilateral in approximately half of patients [126]. Clinically, it often presents as a soft, rubbery, or firm mobile mass under the nipple [6, 127]. It has been suggested to quantitatively diagnose it by physical examination as a subareolar breast tissue ≥ 2 cm in transversal diameter [3, 6].

The most common differential diagnosis of gynecomastia at physical examination is pseudogynecomastia, relatively frequent in overweight and obese men and difficult to distinguish from true gynecomastia [4, 130]. Hence, when physical examination is equivocal in distinguishing the two conditions, ultrasound is suggested.

On the other hand, the most important life-threatening differential diagnosis of gynecomastia is breast cancer., which predominantly affects older men [129]. It is usually unilateral and typically presents with a painless, hard mass, which may be subareolar or, unlike gynecomastia, eccentric to the nipple [126, 129]. With breast cancer, there may be secondary signs of malignancy, such as nipple or skin retraction, nipple discharge, or axillary lymphadenopathy [126, 129, 131]. Breast cancers in men often present at a later age (105) and a more advanced stage than in women, with up to 47% of men having axillary nodal involvement at the time of diagnosis [126, 131]. In addition, nipple discharge is suspicious for breast cancer in men, with two studies showing carcinoma in 23% to 57% of men presenting with this symptom [132, 133]. Conversely, only 6% of male breast cancers occur in men < 45 years of age and < 1% in men < 35 years of age [134].

It has been reported that breast physical examination in men lacks specificity in the detection of malignancy [135]. One study on 628 male patients reported a positive predictive value (PPV) for cancer detection of 19.2% [127]. Other studies reported a PPV of 8%–55% [135, 136]. Therefore, physical examination should be considered the first diagnostic step, and imaging should be reserved for cases with equivocal findings or features suspicious for malignancy [135].

However, only a few studies determined and compared the diagnostic performance of physical examination, US and mammography in evaluating the male breast and differentiating gynecomastia from cancer. In particular, one study [127] found that the diagnostic performance of physical examination was lower than that of US and mammography (p < 0.05 for specificity). In addition, US was the most specific (95.3%) and mammography the most sensitive (94.7%) for detection of malignancy (p > 0.05). US and mammography, with a negative predictive value (NPV) close to 100%, make it possible to avoid many unnecessary surgical procedures in men [127]. Hence, when a breast mass is detected at physical examination, imaging is very useful to increase the diagnostic accuracy and for differential diagnosis.

For men with a physical examination strongly suspicious for malignancy, mammography is recommended as the best imaging tool because it has high sensitivity, specificity, and negative predictive value (NPV) for breast cancer diagnosis [126, 128, 129]. If mammography is indeterminate or suspicious, US can assist in lesion characterization and guide biopsy [126].

Although some Authors [127] suggest using US as the initial imaging modality in the “young” male < 25 years old according to ACR [126] or < 35 years old according to Giordano et al. [134]) with an “indeterminate” physical examination, since he is unlikely to have breast cancer, mammography shows a higher accuracy for breast cancer diagnosis, and should be performed before any biopsy [126]. Hence, while US should represent the initial imaging modality in “young” men to assess gynecomastia when physical examination is equivocal, when physical examination is strongly suspicious for malignancy mammography represents the imaging modality of choice prior to core needle biopsy at any age.

Remarks

The ACR recommendations [126] are mainly focused on ruling out breast cancer diagnosis than assessing gynecomastia. However, gynecomastia has a significant higher prevalence than breast cancer [134] and deserves specific recommendations on its assessment.

The most common differential diagnosis of gynecomastia is pseudogynecomastia, while the most relevant for general health is male breast cancer [130]. While pseudogynecomastia is relatively frequent and benign, malignancy is very rare but life-threatening. In particular, male breast cancer is virtually absent below the age of 35 [134]. Its prevalence is 5.5% in men aged 35–44 years, increasing especially in men older than 45 years old [134], with a median age at diagnosis of 63 years [129].

To provide differential diagnosis, US and mammography have been previously considered. US is a simple, rapid and harmless diagnostic tool, not using radiations as mammography, and, among imaging techniques, is the least expensive [137]. In addition, an US console is often available to the clinicians (including endocrinologists and andrologists) in outpatient clinics, making its use simple during a medical visit and easier than to resort to other imaging techniques in charge of radiologists.

According to the aforementioned statements, if an indeterminate breast mass is identified at physical examination, the initial suggested imaging study should be US, especially in men younger than age 35 (and eventually in men younger than age 45) instead of mammography, that could be associated to US when the latter is doubtful.

Special attention should be given both at physical examination and imaging to white and, secondly, black men, at higher risk of breast cancer than other subjects [134, 138]. In addition, patients with Klinefelter Syndrome (KS) deserve high attention for male breast cancer risk [139, 140]. A meta-analysis of case-control studies reported a prevalence of breast cancer in KS of about 3% [141], while its prevalence is rare in non-KS men (0.6% of all breast carcinomas and < 1% of all malignancies in men) [140]. KS subjects showed a 30- to 50-times higher risk than the general male population [139, 142]. A recent meta-analysis [143] confirmed KS as significantly associated with breast cancer (OR = 24.7; 95% CI = 8.94 to 68.4). Hence, annual breast clinical examination, and imaging when physical examination is equivocal, is recommended in KS men.

The US diagnosis of gynecomastia describes three main patterns (with definitions derivable from mammography), including “nodular”, “dendritic” and “diffuse”, and US allows differential diagnosis especially with pseudogynecomastia [130, 135, 144] (Fig. 2). Nodular gynecomastia occurs in the early (florid) phase of gynecomastia and appears at US as a subareolar fan- or disk-shaped area of hypoechoic tissue. It typically correlates with symptomatic duration of < 1 year [135]. Dendritic gynecomastia occurs with long-standing gynecomastia (quiescent phase) that has been present for 1 year or longer. It typically presents as a serpiginous hypoechoic area with finger-like projections in the subareolar region [135]. Diffuse gynecomastia is usually seen in the setting of high-dose estrogen therapy or exogenous hormone use. On US, there is increased volume and echogenicity of breast tissue, identical to female breasts [135]. Finally, pseudogynecomastia is characterized by increased subareolar fat without enlargement of the breast glandular component [135].

In order to guide the physician for wait and see, medical or surgical treatment, US can be useful to indicate the “pattern” of gynecomastia, especially when symptomatic duration of gynecomastia is not clear. In fact, the nodular pattern, occurring in the florid phase of gynecomastia, can show spontaneous regression in adolescents and/or is more sensitive to medical therapy, while the dendritic pattern, related to the quiescent phase, is less responsive [6, 135]. In addition, US is more accurate than physical examination to evaluate changes of breast tissue overtime or under medical treatment and could be considered along with physical and symptoms changes to guide the duration of the wait and see phase or of the medical treatment. Furthermore, US can be useful to differentiate true gynecomastia and pseudogynecomastia when physical examination is doubtful, especially in overweight and obese men [130, 144].

However, so far, the US diagnosis of gynecomastia is mainly qualitative, and only one study [145] reported a quantitative, not evidence-based, definition, suggested as increased breast tissue defined as “anteroposterior depth > 1 cm from the skin surface under the nipple to the anterior surface of the pectoralis muscle”. In addition, no threshold for a diagnostic transversal diameter at US is available, while a threshold of this diameter (≥ 2 cm) is used in clinical practice for a physical examination-based diagnosis [3]. Finally, the definition of “normal” breast tissue is only suggested, but not evidence-based [130, 144]. Hence, a quantitative standardization of gynecomastia at US is advocated. Finally, when gynecomastia is confirmed, several evidence [6, 62, 144, 146] and available guidelines [4] indicate to perform clinical and US evaluation of the testes to exclude a palpable or non-palpable testicular tumor. Testicular US is advocated as the detection of a testicular tumor by palpation has low sensitivity [4]. On the other hand, when physical examination is strongly suspicious for a male breast cancer, breast imaging and core needle biopsy are recommended (see Recommendations). The importance of preoperative histological diagnosis in the assessment of breast lesions in women is widely established, but in men with breast lesions histological diagnosis is obtained in a limited number of cases [147]. The main reason is probably related to the low incidence of breast cancer in men: male breast cancer accounts for only 1% of breast cancer diagnoses in the USA and less than 0.1% of cancer-related deaths in men [148]. Despite its rarity, incidence of male breast cancer has increased over the past three decades, and for this reason it is increasingly important to achieve a preoperative pathological diagnosis using core needle biopsy or fine-needle aspiration cytology [149]. While fine-needle aspiration yields quite a high rate (13–15%) of inadequate specimens [150, 151], core needle biopsy allows sufficient tissue for diagnosis and represents an effective method in distinguishing benign and neoplastic lesions in the male breast [147, 149, 150]. In fact, core needle biopsy allows histological examination, which is mandatory if malignancy is suspected [3, 147, 149, 150].

Clinical management

Watchful waiting

Recommendations

R1 We recommend watchful waiting for at least 6 months after resolution of the underlying secondary causes of gynecomastia prior to considering any medical or surgical treatment (1 ØØOO).

R2 We recommend watchful waiting for new-onset pubertal gynecomastia with duration of follow-up not exceeding 2 years and still to be determined on a case-by-case basis depending on psychological burden and gynecomastia severity (1 ØØØO).

Evidence

The culprit medications should be discontinued, when possible, based on careful evaluation of cost-benefit ratio. If gynecomastia is suspected to be caused by abuse of exogenous substances, then withdrawal of the inciting agent(s) should be encouraged. Improvement should be evident within one month after discontinuation [6]. If gynecomastia secondary to comorbidities, medical therapies, and use/abuse of exogenous agents has lasted for more than 6 months, regression is unlikely due to the presence of less reversible fibrotic tissue [7]. In cases of pubertal gynecomastia, as well as newly onset adulthood idiopathic gynecomastia, a wait-and-see approach may be recommended because most cases may resolve spontaneously [152]. However, there remains uncertainty about the duration of the wait-and-see attitude. In the few available longitudinal studies, physiological pubertal gynecomastia exhibited a duration of 1 to 2 years [42, 153, 154], while one study reported that in 60% of boys, gynecomastia lasted less than 6 months [155]. Approximately only 20% of boys persist with residual breast enlargement at the age of 20 years [7].

Remarks

In physiologic gynecomastia, follow-up should include clinical assessments every 3–6 months [152], setting decision-making strategies on whether and when to intervene on an individualized basis, taking into account the psychological burden and clinical severity of gynecomastia [156]. In severe and/or long-lasting forms with no tendency for remission within 2 years, the increase in stromal hyalinization and inactive fibrotic tissue [157] makes the efficacy of any medical therapy unrealistic, placing an indication for surgical treatment [152].

Medical treatment

Recommendations

R1 We recommend prescribing testosterone therapy in men with gynecomastia only in the presence of proven androgen deficiency (1 ØØØØ).

R2 We suggest a short course (3–6 months) of treatment with Tamoxifen (20 mg/day) in selected cases of idiopathic gynecomastia of recent onset (< 6 months) before resorting to the surgical option in case of failure (Expert Opinion).

R3 We suggest a short course (1–3 months) of transdermal dihydrotestosterone (DHT) 2.5% in selected cases of idiopathic gynecomastia of recent onset (< 6 months) before resorting to the surgical option in case of failure (Expert Opinion).

R4 We do not recommend prescribing aromatase inhibitors (AIs), danazol or nonaromatizable androgens in the treatment of gynecomastia (1 ØØOO).

R5 We suggest the use of tamoxifen (10–20 mg daily) to prevent bicalutamide-induced gynecomastia and/or breast pain in patients with prostate cancer (2 ØØOO).

R6 We suggest the use of radiotherapy (10 to 15 Gy) to prevent bicalutamide-induced gynecomastia but not mastodynia in patients with prostate cancer (2 ØOOO).

R7 We recommend not prescribing AIs for preventing bicalutamide-induced breast events in patients with prostate cancer (1 ØØOO).

Evidence

In men with proven androgen deficiency, testosterone replacement therapy can improve gynecomastia as well as the other clinical features of hypogonadism [158]. On the contrary, in eugonadal men, aromatization of excess exogenous testosterone to estradiol may exacerbate or even induce breast enlargement [20]. No RCTs are available on the effect of nonaromatizable androgens, although percutaneous treatment with dihydrotestosterone has shown some efficacy in small series of patients [87, 159, 160].

Many other drugs have been tested as possible medical treatments for gynecomastia in uncontrolled trials or series of cases. These included selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) [161–167], antiestrogens with weak androgenic activities, such as danazol [161, 168, 169] and AIs such as testolactone [170] or anastrozole [167, 171, 172]. Despite often encouraging results, unfortunately, the uncontrolled design makes it impossible to draw firm conclusions, as gynecomastia can also resolve spontaneously. Randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind trials that have evaluated the effects of SERMs, namely tamoxifen (10 mg twice daily), in patients with idiopathic gynecomastia are limited to two older, small crossover studies [173, 174].

In the study by Parker et al. [173], 7 out of 10 patients had a reduction in gynecomastia size with tamoxifen (p < 0.005), and all 4 with pain reported relief. McDermott et al. [174] found that tamoxifen significantly reduced pain in 5 out of 6 patients, while no improvements were seen in the placebo group (p = 0.04). Breast size reduction was observed in 3 tamoxifen-treated patients, but the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.09).

The effect of other medical therapies in idiopathic gynecomastia has been evaluated in scanty RCTs. In a randomized double-blind study of 52 patients with idiopathic gynecomastia [175], treatment for 3 months with danazol (200 mg twice daily) significantly improved breast tenderness compared with placebo (P = 0.022) and was associated with significant reduction in the degree of gynecomastia and its measured size (P < 0.05). In the same study, danazol also reduced the likelihood of surgical treatment but significantly increased body weight.

A more recent randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial assessed the possible utility of anastrozole for the treatment of pubertal gynecomastia [167]. Eighty boys aged 11–18 years with pubertal gynecomastia received anastrozole (1 mg) or placebo once daily for 6 months. The percentage of patients with a response (at least 50% reduction in breast ultrasound volume) was 38.5% for the anastrozole arm and 31.4% for the placebo arm, a statistically nonsignificant difference (OR: 1.51; 95%CI: 0.49 to 4.84). At present the use of AIs is indicated only for aromatase excess syndrome, a genetic cause of gynecomastia (see above the paragraph on genetic causes of gynecomastia).

More evidence is available on the role of nonsurgical treatments in preventing gynecomastia and/or breast pain in patients with prostate cancer undergoing androgen ablation therapy (AAT) with bicalutamide. As shown in Table 3, to date, the efficacy of prophylactic treatment with tamoxifen for this indication, albeit not approved by the Food and Drug Administration, has been evaluated in six randomized trials [176–181], including three double-blind placebo-controlled trials [176, 178, 180]. In some of these studies, the prophylactic efficacy of anastrozole [176, 180] and radiotherapy [177, 179] was also evaluated. Radiotherapy was also used in additional 3 RCTs [182–184]. In the study by Boccardo et al. [176], patients with prostate cancer on bicalutamide were randomized to receive placebo, tamoxifen, or anastrozole. Gynecomastia occurred in 33% of the placebo group, 17% in the anastrozole group, and none in the tamoxifen group. Breast pain was also more frequent in the placebo group (39%). Similarly, Di Lorenzo et al. found that tamoxifen significantly reduced gynecomastia and breast pain compared to bicalutamide alone [177]. In a subsequent RCT [179], bicalutamide alone caused gynecomastia in 35 of 51 patients, while tamoxifen reduced this to 4 of 50. Fradet et al.‘s trial [178] showed a dose-dependent effect of tamoxifen, with 81.7% of placebo patients developing gynecomastia compared to 14.7% in the 20 mg tamoxifen group. Saltzstein et al. [180] found that tamoxifen, but not anastrozole, reduced gynecomastia and pain. Serretta et al. [181] observed a significant reduction in gynecomastia and pain with tamoxifen. Additionally, radiotherapy has been shown to help prevent gynecomastia [182], though it was ineffective in preventing breast pain in some studies [183, 184].

Remarks

The available evidence on the efficacy of medical therapies in idiopathic gynecomastia and associated chest pain is insufficient to draw any firm conclusion. Although not approved for this indication, of some usefulness may be tamoxifen at a dose of 20 mg daily [185], or DHT 2.5% daily application [3, 87, 159, 186, 187] in cases of painful gynecomastia of recent onset to offer pain relief and, occasionally, partial regression of the breast enlargement. For these pharmacological treatments an informed consent is imperative as they are off label. The grading of the evidence is possible for nonsurgical therapies, namely tamoxifen, anastrozole, and radiotherapy, when used as prophylaxis of breast events in men with prostate cancer undergoing AAT with bicalutamide [188, 189].

Although all RCTs on tamoxifen have produced results in the same direction indicating its efficacy in preventing both AAT-induced gynecomastia and breast pain, a number of limitations reduce the quality of the evidence supporting the recommendation. First, the evidence is downgraded by the low number of RCTs, involving few patients and documenting few outcomes with quite wide confidence intervals. The low precision prevents the achievement of adequate statistical power of the overall effect estimates. Second, a methodological limitation arises from a possible attrition bias because not in all available RCTs the dropout rate was appropriately reported; however, when specified, it was below 20% [178, 180, 181]. Third, an obvious heterogeneity between the studies reflected differences in tamoxifen doses (10 mg or 20 mg per day) and duration of treatment (varying from 3 to 12 months). Finally, it is difficult to evaluate the efficacy of a drug since gynecomastia may resolve spontaneously. Treatment regimens remain largely empirical, and in the absence of comparative studies, no recommendation can be made for one dosage over another. In an open multicenter randomized phase 3 trial [190], daily treatment with tamoxifen 20 mg was superior to the scheme with tamoxifen 20 mg weekly (after 8 weeks of daily therapy) in preventing bicalutamide-induced gynecomastia and breast pain. As for anastrozole, the 2 available RCTs [176, 180] agreed in recognizing its ineffectiveness in preventing bicalutamide-induced breast events in patients with prostate cancer. On the contrary, in these patients, some prophylactic efficacy on the occurrence of gynecomastia, but not mastodynia, seems attributable to radiotherapy [177, 179, 182–184]. Nevertheless, the availability of only one sham-controlled RCT [183], along with differences in doses (10 to 15 Gy) and treatment modalities, downgraded the overall quality of the evidence.

Medical treatment of genetic and congenital causes of gynecomastia

R8 We suggest treating children with documented aromatase excess syndrome with aromatase inhibitors and those with congenital adrenal hyperplasia with hydrocortisone, in order to correct the underlying endocrine disorder and improve gynecomastia (2 ØØØØ).

R9 We suggest treating the underlying clinical condition in case of congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism and of defects of steroid biosynthesis which may contribute to the improvement of gynecomastia (2 ØØØO).

Evidence

The aromatase inhibitor anastrozole at the dose of 1.0 mg daily is effective in normalizing serum estrogens [21], promoting growth in order to reach a final height close the genetic target and gynecomastia disappearance [96, 108].

Treatment with hydrocortisone substitution at the dose of 12.5 mg/m2/day in boys with non-classical 21-hydroxylase deficiency or 11β-Hydroxylase may completely regress gynecomastia in 5–8 months after the start of therapy [103–105].

Treatment of gynecomastia in patients with ovotestis depends on multidisciplinary consensus about the final goal concerning phenotypic sex. Thus, mammary tissue should be surgically removed in case of development towards an adult male phenotypic aspect [109].

Remarks

The treatment of gynecomastia in the context of rare genetic or congenital diseases is complex and should be based on the best practice approach available for the disease, considering also the main objectives of the therapy beyond gynecomastia.

Surgical treatment

Recommendations

R1 We recommend surgical treatment for patients with long-lasting fibrotic gynecomastia with no tendency to spontaneous regression and no response to appropriate medical therapy, especially in the presence of psychological distress (1 ØØOO).

R2 We recommend tailoring the surgical technique on a case-by-case basis, according to the severity degree, the amount of fatty tissue and the surgeon’s experience (1 ØØØO).

Evidence

Although most patients with gynecomastia will experience its spontaneous resolution after medical treatment or following elimination of the underlying cause, a small proportion of cases with persistent or refractory gynecomastia will require surgery. Need, time and type of surgery should be defined based on the severity of breast enlargement and psychological implications.

Surgery should be performed by an experienced surgeon because there is a risk of cosmetic complications due to residual scarring and asymmetry. Different techniques have been proposed with an individualized approach [191], including semicircular, inframammary, intra-areolar, or axillary incision [192, 193], glandular and adipose tissue excision combined with liposuction [152, 194, 195]. It is critical to retain some subareolar tissue and subcutaneous vascular plexus to ensure sufficient blood supply, avoiding nipple necrosis and retraction [192, 196]. Depending on the size of the gynecomastia, reduction surgery may be indicated [195].

Remarks

Although a reasonable observation period is always necessary before surgical treatment is offered, if gynecomastia has persisted for more than 6–12 months, it is unlikely to regress due to the presence of fibrosis. Nevertheless, in the case of pubertal gynecomastia, regression can occur up to 2 years [42, 153, 154]. Surgical treatment for pubertal gynecomastia should be postponed after the end of puberty to minimize the risk of recurrence after surgery [197] apart from cases characterized by severe psychological distress.

Psychological/cosmetic treatment

Recommendations

R1 We suggest screening for psychological distress in men with gynecomastia, especially adolescents and young adults (2 ØØOO).

R2 We suggest proposing psychological counseling in men with gynecomastia and provide psychological support in selected cases before and after eventual surgical or cosmetic treatments (2 ØOOO).

R3 We suggest aesthetic or reconstructive surgical treatments in men with gynecomastia-related severe psychological distress, especially adolescents and young adults (2 ØØOO).

Evidence

Gynecomastia may have relevant psychological and psychosocial consequences. A systematic review on psychosocial aspects of gynecomastia in adolescents and young men [113] identified a variety of psychosocial correlates of gynecomastia. These included several anecdotal negative “opinions” of varying severity, going from feeling embarrassed to suicidal despair [113]. Psychosocial correlates that were measured using psychometrically sound instruments included anxiety, depression, and health-related quality of life [113]. Another review [198] confirmed the association of gynecomastia with depression, anxiety, low self-esteem and body image concerns, issues that may lead patients to maladaptive coping mechanisms.

A few studies with low quality and strength of evidence [113], suggest routine screening for psychological distress [199] as well as psychotherapy [200]/mental health treatment [201] in the management of adolescents with gynecomastia. A few studies reported that when gynecomastia causes considerable cosmetic and psychological distress, surgical treatment is justified [113, 194, 202]. A recent systematic review [203] reported that for many patients the psychological impact of gynecomastia is substantial, and supported improvement in quality of life and several psychological domains after surgical treatment of gynecomastia.

Besides surgical treatment, cosmetic treatment of gynecomastia can be considered. So far, minimally invasive procedures to correct aesthetic problems of male breast are available. The major indications are gynecomastia, breast asymmetry, and body sculpturing [204]. Minor surgery, including current treatments combining subcutaneous fat and breast tissue excision, laser lipolysis, dermal filler and cell-assisted lipotransfer are among the most promising techniques available [204, 205].

Remarks

Most of the available literature on the topic refers to adolescents or young men, while other populations, including older men or iatrogenic gynecomastia are less represented [4, 201].

The evidence regarding the psychological and psychosocial consequences of gynecomastia is poor. Considering one aforementioned systematic review [113], overall, the articles reviewed included small samples. All studies were limited to single sites. Several studies included no or limited statistical analyses of the data. The population estimates for gynecomastia and its correlates varied widely within the reports.

Regarding psychosocial interventions, they included vague terms such as psychotherapy, mental health treatment, and early intervention to improve both physical and emotional symptoms [113]. In addition, no study reported the development or testing of psychosocial interventions specifically designed for young males with gynecomastia [113, 206]. Finally, although improvement in quality of life and several psychological domains after surgical treatment for gynecomastia has been reported by a systematic review, the level of evidence on this topic is very low.

Regarding cosmetic treatments (as well as surgery), the male breast is of increasing interest in esthetic surgery in general and for minimally invasive techniques in particular [205]. Gynecomastia is perceived by men as a stigmatizing disease [205]. Cosmetic treatments should be considered especially in adolescents and young adults with severe psychological distress. Adolescence is a very critical period where self-esteem is formed and where it is easier to be teased by peers, and physicians should consider surgical or cosmetic therapeutic options, eventually assisted by psychological counseling.

Conclusions and future directions

Gynecomastia represents a complex clinical condition arising from diverse etiologic pathways, ranging from physiological hormonal shifts to pathological endocrine imbalances and iatrogenic causes. It often resolves spontaneously in physiological cases but may require further investigation and management in pathological or drug-induced cases. Effective diagnosis and treatment involve understanding the underlying causes, considering patient age, possible psychological and physical burden on patients and evaluating risk factors. Our aim was to address this gap by proposing a standardized diagnostic and therapeutic approach grounded in current evidence and expert consensus. However, several unmet needs persist. First, high-quality prospective studies are still lacking in many areas, including the natural history of persistent pubertal gynecomastia, the comparative effectiveness of medical therapies, and long-term outcomes of surgical intervention. Furthermore, the psychosocial impact of gynecomastia, frequently underestimated, deserves more structured assessment tools and integrated care pathways involving psychological support when appropriate. In future research, the role of novel imaging techniques and minimally invasive diagnostic strategies should be further explored. Given the increasing exposure of the general population to endocrine-disrupting chemicals and off-label use of anabolic agents, epidemiological surveillance and public health initiatives will be crucial to mitigate emerging risks. Finally, patient-centered care should remain the guiding principle: clinical decisions should not only be evidence-based, but also tailored to the individual’s age, comorbidities, expectations, and quality of life concerns. The development of multidisciplinary networks, combining endocrinologists, andrologists, pediatricians, surgeons, and mental health professionals, will be essential to ensure comprehensive and compassionate care for patients with gynecomastia.
