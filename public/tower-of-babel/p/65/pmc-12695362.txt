Privacy preserving federated learning for 90-day mortality prediction in colorectal surgery: a multicenter retrospective development and comparison study
Authors: Max M Maurer, Bjarne Pfitzner, Robin P van de Water, Lara Faraj, Christoph Riepe, Daniela Zuluaga, Felix Krenzien, Nathanael Raschzok, Robert Siegel, Christian Schineis, Bert Arnrich, Katharina Beyer, Johann Pratschke, Igor M Sauer, Axel Winter
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Limited data availability impedes the advancements of artificial intelligence (AI) applications in surgery to date. Federated learning (FL), a novel privacy-focused machine learning technique, introduces a decentralized framework to facilitate multicenter modeling. This study constructs a FL network, including differential privacy (DP), to predict 90-day mortality following colorectal surgery.Methods:Patients undergoing elective colorectal surgery across three tertiary centers (C1–C3) between January 2015 and December 2021 were retrospectively enrolled. Neural networks (NN) for mortality prediction were trained and validated for all three centers individually and after data aggregation using centralized and distributed FL analysis. Local and central DP was then applied as additional data protection framework components. Areas under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) and precision-recallcurves (AUPRC) including 95%-confidence intervals were calculated.Results:A total of 2959 patients (mean [SD] age: 56.9 [16.8] years; n = 1677 [56.7%] male) were enrolled. The 90-day mortality rate was 3.1% (n = 92). Local NNs achieved AUROCs of 0.80 ([0.74–0.87]; C1), 0.81 ([0.75–0.87]; C2), and 0.84 ([0.75–0.92]; C3) and corresponding AUPRCs reaching 0.26 [0.11–0.41], 0.31 [0.21–0.41], and 0.21 [0.11–0.32], respectively. An aggregated centralized NN (NNCZ) achieved an AUROC of 0.81 [0.76–0.85] and AUPRC of 0.29 [0.22–0.36]. Distributed FL (NNFL) was comparable (AUROC: 0.78 [0.72–0.84], P = 0.67; AUPRC: 0.26 [0.17–0.35], P = 0.44) to the centralized model. Central DP reduced the performance of FL-based prediction by 5% in AUROC (0.74 [0.64–0.84]) and 35% in AUPRC (0.17 [0.10–0.23]). Local DP almost diminished the performance (AUROC: 0.52 [0.49–0.55]; AUPRC: 0.05 [0.03–0.06]). Feature importance analysis revealed age, blood status and the Charlson Comorbidity Index as highest weighted features for both NNCZ and NNFL.Conclusion:Federated learning demonstrates similar performance to centralized machine learning in preoperative mortality prediction, providing an encouraging framework to accelerate the development of future surgical AI applications. Improving data privacy through DP is associated with compromises in model performance.

Introduction

Colorectal procedures account for nearly 70% of all major abdominal surgeries in Western populations.[1–3] Despite considerable advancements in surgical techniques, one in four patients faces at least one severe perioperative complication after major colorectal surgery, with the overall mortality rate exceeding 5%[1,2]. Consecutively, numerous risk models have been developed to assist surgeons in preoperative risk stratification over the past decades.[4–7] Given the high-dimensional, nonlinear dependencies among multiple risk parameters, machine learning (ML) models have only recently been introduced as a new approach to improve risk prediction and have shown encouraging results, surpassing the accuracy of traditional risk scores.[8–10]

However, several challenges impede the effective development of artificial intelligence (AI) models in surgery to date. First, ML-driven pattern analysis essentially requires large sample sizes to develop solid prediction models, often exceeding the quantity of data available in single-center settings[11]. Moreover, models trained solely on data from individual departments bear a high risk of non-generalizability. This phenomenon, commonly referred to as “overfitting”, reflects solid performance results when tested on data utilized for model development but poor outcomes when applied to new sets of data[12]. Consequently, the joint aggregation of data from multiple centers is necessary to meet both the high-quality and high-quantity standards for the implementation of AI in clinical practice. However, multicenter data acquisition is resource intensive and can ultimately impede data governance for both patients and hospitals[12,13].

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a novel subfield of ML in which distributed analysis is used to increase data security and efficiently facilitate data integration from multiple owners, e.g., surgical departments[14,15]. In contrast to traditional ML, FL does not require patient data transfer to an external server for centralized analysis; instead, an evolving global AI model is sent to each participating hospital for on-site model improvement. The individual model refinements derived from local training are thereafter reintegrated into a final global algorithm. As a result, FL can increase data protection and governance while effectively facilitating data management across multiple network participants, as the amount of information is considerably reduced when algorithm parameters rather than full patient datasets are transferred.

Although FL has been successfully adopted in other medical fields[16–18], the feasibility and potential for distributed multicenter ML analyses in the domain of surgery remain unknown. Furthermore, a comprehensive FL framework considered for clinical implementation must address the additional risk of reconstructing patient data from ML parameters, which has been demonstrated in computational FL simulations[19]. To mitigate this risk, the concept of differential privacy (DP) provides an additional basis for creating a robust FL framework suitable for real-world medical applications. DP introduces noise into the training process, which obscures the impact of individual patient records and provides additional data protection[20].

In this study, the aim is to introduce privacy-preserving FL in the field of abdominal surgery by training, validating, and comparing neural networks (NNs) for the prediction of 90-day mortality after major colorectal surgery. An introducing step-by-step explanation is provided, and the performance of localized, centralized, and federated NNs is compared. Furthermore, the integration of local and centralized DP measures as auxiliary network elements to further strengthen FL data privacy is evaluated. The results highlight the potentially applicability of this novel technique, which might essentially contribute to the advancement and implementation of AI applications in the field of surgery.

Methods

This retrospective, multicenter study included patients who underwent major colorectal surgery at three different tertiary centers (C1–C3). Major elective oncological and nononcological procedures were identified on the basis of the German adaptation of the International Classification of Procedures in Medicine (eTable 1). Only cases including the conduction of an anastomosis were considered.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (EA4/238/21). The study furthermore adheres to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis Or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines and its recent update[22,23].

Preoperative data for ML training included 106 patient, disease, laboratory, and procedural features comprising categorical, bivariate, and numerical parameters as outlined in eTable 2. The input variables, as well as the procedures for data extraction, cleaning and prepocessing, were identical across all networks and centers. Comorbidities and the revised Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) were derived retrospectively, as described by Quan et al[24]. The primary study endpoint was defined as all-cause 90-day mortality.

All three cohorts were subjected to statistical analysis via one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal‒Wallis test via SciPy 1.0[25]. Statistical significance was defined as P <0.05. Data preprocessing for ML model development involved the deletion of features with >50% missing values, high collinearity (>0.95), and single unique values. Missing values among the remaining features were imputed via the k-nearest neighbors algorithm, numerical features were normalized, and categorical parameters were one-hot encoded via the scikit-learn framework[26]. Model assessment was performed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) curve and the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC), including 95% confidence intervals and two-sided t-tests for comparison, computed and visualized via the scikit-learn framework.

ChatGPT (OpenAI, San Francisco, CA, USA) was used for spelling, grammar and language corrections, with no intellectual contribution to content.

Localized and centralized neural network model evaluation

The considered ML model architecture was a NN with two hidden layers containing 128 and 64 neurons, a dropout of 0.5 and a ReLU activation function. A dropout rate of 0.2 was set for the input layer, and the Softmax function was applied to the two output neurons.

Local NNs were trained and evaluated for all three centers individually (NNL-C1, NNL-C2, and NNL-C3) with an 80:10:10 split for training, validation, and test partitions. The datasets of all three centers were subsequently merged to mimic a multicenter centralized analysis for model development. The emerging centralized NNCZ was trained, validated, and tested analogously.

Federated learning neural network model evaluation

FL involves decentralized ML model training across a network of distributed clients instead of consolidating all samples at one site for analysis (Fig. 1). The model framework consists of k network participants (data owners) who are responsible for supervising the training process at their respective locations during the model development process. The additional parameter server governs the facilitation and coordination of the training procedure. The basic model architecture, including hyperparameter optimization, is predefined and must be followed by all network clients. Figure 1.Federated learning (FL) network.The external server orchestrates the transmission of a global algorithm for training and model refinement to each participating local network client. The training and refinement process at each center follows an identical protocol, applying the same feature space, training configuration, and model parameters. Subsequent model updates from each individual client are retransmitted after local training and reintegrated by the external server, resulting in an updated global model version. This model evolution process is repeated for T rounds or until a stopping criterion is met. Additionally, differential privacy (DP) can be incorporated into the FL framework by adding noise either to each participant’s local model updates (local DP) or to the retransmission and updating process at the server level (central DP).

Federated learning (FL) network.

The parameter server then selects a subset of clients according to the sampling probability q to initiate the distributed optimization runs. The selected clients receive the global model for training with their local data over E epochs. The optimized model is subsequently compared with the global model initially received, and model weight differences are computed. Importantly, the training and refining processes at all centers adhere to the identical training framework, using the same feature space, training parameters and model configurations. Finally, all model adjustments are retransmitted to the orchestrating parameter server, where all updates are averaged and integrated into the revised global model, commonly using a stochastic gradient descent (SGD) optimizer with a learning rate of 1.0. This procedure is repeated for T rounds or until a stopping condition is met.

Our complete code is publicly available at https://github.com/BjaThrnePfitzner/differentially-private-fl-with-personalisation.

Differential privacy

DP constitutes a mathematical concept for quantifying the privacy risk associated with repeated queries of highly sensitive databases[20]. Fundamentally, DP involves injecting noise into database interactions, ensuring that any outcome does not disclose whether an individual sample is present or absent in the underlying data. In the context of FL, noise injection can be performed either in the individual center model update steps on-site (local DP) or during model update reintegration at the parameter server level (central DP).

Formally, (∈,δ) -DP is based on the following inequality: PrMd∈S≤eεPrMd′∈S+δ

Here, M corresponds to the privacy mechanism (or query) being applied to adjacent datasets d and d′. Adjacent datasets are characterized by the presence or absence of only a single sample. S is any subset of possible outputs of M.

Essentially, ε delineates the privacy level by constraining the likelihood of observing the same output of M given any two adjacent inputs. Conversely, δ denotes the probability that the privacy level of ε may not be upheld. To strengthen the reliability of DP assurances, δ is usually selected to be significantly smaller than the reciprocal of the database size 1/|D|. In accordance with the related literature, ε=10 and δ=10−4 were set to ensure that the requirements stated above are met[27].

Feature importance analysis

A feature importance analysis was performed for all models to quantify the contribution of each individual feature within the final model and thus identify the highest-weighted parameters. Using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP)[28], feature weights were averaged across 10 random seeds within each local non-DP NNL-C1, NNL-C2, and NNL-C3, as well as for the aggregated non-DP and DP models, NNCZ and NNFL. Finally, the parameter weights for each model were normalized for comparison.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

A total of 3861 patients underwent major elective colorectal surgery, with 2959 patients providing 90-day follow-up data (C1: n = 1089, C2: n = 1205, and C3: n = 665). A detailed description of the patient characteristics is presented in Table 1. The overall completeness of the dataset was 80.2%. The majority of patients were male (n = 1677, 56.7%), and the mean age was 56.9 (± 16.8), with no significant difference across the participating centers (P = 0.18). Most patients had malignant diseases (53.4%). The most common comorbidity according to the revised CCI was diabetes without chronic complications (n = 269, 9.1%). The CCI score was the only significant difference in patient characteristics among the cohorts, with C2 having the highest median score of 6 (P < 0.01). Several other features showed a trend as reflected by higher P values though not reaching statistical significance, which may be attributed to center-specific expertise, patient populations and particularly clinical subspecializations. A local NNL-C1 individually trained and validated with data from C1, the largest network participant, achieved an AUROC of 0.80 [0.75–0.87] and a corresponding model AUPRC of 0.26 [0.11–0.41]. Patient age emerged as the most relevant model feature given a normalized SHAP value of 0.015, followed in descending order by laboratory values for glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c; 0.011), red blood cell count (RBC; 0.009), and urea (0.009), as well as the CCI (0.008).

C2 presented the highest 90-day mortality rate among all centers and hence the greatest number of positive samples. The corresponding local NNL-C2 yielded an AUROC of 0.81 [0.75–0.87], thus outperforming the local NNL-C1 of C1. Furthermore, it showcased the highest AUPRC of 0.31 [0.21–0.41] among all three local models. Preoperative albumin (Alb) exhibited the highest feature impact (0.036). In accordance with NNL-C1, CCI (0.030), age (0.024), and RBC (0.015) ranked among the five predominant features for NNL-C2.

NNL-C3, which was developed for C3 data, yielded the overall highest AUROC of 0.84 [0.75–0.92] but the weakest AUPRC (0.21 [0.11–0.32]) among all three local NNs. In accordance with the results for NNL-C1 and NNL-C2, RBC (0.028), CCI (0.027), and age (0.020) were among the top-ranked features for NNL-C3.

Centralized and federated learning neural networks

A centralized NNCZ trained, validated, and tested on the merged dataset from all three centers achieved an AUROC of 0.81 [0.76–0.85], hence surpassing the local performance of NNL-C1, matching that of NNL-C2, but falling short of that of NNL-C3. In terms of AUPRC, NNCZ yielded an AUPRC of 0.29 [0.22–0.36], thereby outperforming NNL-C1 and NNL-C3 but displaying inferior to the local NNL-C2. Patient age was the feature with the greatest impact (0.012), followed by CCI (0.010), RBC (0.009), Alb (0.008), and Hb (0.007).

Finally, the distributed NNFL reached an AUROC of 0.78 [0.72–0.84] and an AUPRC of 0.26 [0.17–0.35], hence demonstrating similar performance in terms of both AUROC (P = 0.67) and AUPRC (P = 0.44) compared with the centralized NNCZ approach. Feature importance analysis demonstrated that RBC (0.015), Hb (0.018), CCI (0.011), and patient age (0.010) were among the highest-ranked contributing factors, although the order of the features differed from those of the centralized model.

Differential privacy

The incorporation of server-side central DP during distributed model training for FL resulted in only a minor decrease in the AUROC to 0.74 [0.64–0.84] but a considerable decrease in the AUPRC (0.17 [0.10–0.23]; Fig. 2C and D). When noise at the client level was injected, a substantial decrease to nearly the chance level was observed in terms of the AUROC for both the centralized NNCZ-LDP (0.57 [0.54–0.60]) and the federated NNFL-LDP (0.51 [0.49–0.55]). The same was true for the AUPRC results (NNCZ-LDP: 0.12 [0.08–0.15]; NNFL-LDP: 0.05 [0.03–0.06]).

Discussion

The development of intelligent decision support systems in surgery remains fundamentally constrained by limited data availability and heterogeneity to date. The aim of this study was to introduce FL as a novel technique to accelerate the development and implementation of multicenter AI applications in colorectal surgery. In addition, incorporating DP could further enhance data privacy, which is essential to strengthen trust and acceptance of innovative AI frameworks in everyday surgical practice.

The genuine potential of ML in colorectal surgery has recently been shown in the prediction of postoperative bleeding[29], surgical site infections[30,31], the occurrence of anastomotic leakage[32,33], ileus[34], and 30-day mortality[35]. However, most studies were conducted using single-center data only,[29–34] hence bearing a high risk of overfitting and non-generalizability. Moreover, no study has investigated the accuracy of ML prediction for 90-day mortality, which has emerged as a more favorable benchmark for assessing perioperative outcomes given the considerable increase in mortality compared with 30-day mortality alone[36]. Moreover, the concept of FL in health care has only lately gained significant interest to facilitate multicenter ML analysis. However, in contrast to big-data driven medical domains such as image-recognition in radiology or pathology, no study has evaluated its potential for preoperative risk stratification so far.

In our analysis, the distributed FL network configured across three tertiary surgical centers for 90-day mortality after major colorectal surgery demonstrated that the performance of a decentralized NN was comparable to that of a corresponding centralized model, in terms of both AUROC and AUPRC. Importantly, both the centralized and FL NNs achieved adequate prediction performances (AUROCs of 0.81 and 0.78, respectively). The performance was moreover only marginally lower than that of a large-scale multicenter study for the prediction of 30-day mortality after colorectal surgery recently reported applying conventional ML analysis[35]. We emphasize that the AUROC alone may be critically inconclusive for model assessment in analyses with high class imbalance as present in most studies investigating perioperative mortality. Therefore, the AUPRC is considered a more accurate metric in these scenarios. In this context, both the centralized as well as the distributed FL models outperformed the local NNs of two out of the three participating network centers in terms of AUPRC. This clearly highlights the benefits of aggregated data utilization as achieved through FL. Only the local NNL-C2 achieved a higher discriminative performance than the global FL model, which could be attributed to its higher proportion of positive samples compared to the mean and therefore providing a favorable class distribution for model training. This finding distinctly exemplifies the important challenge of achieving network client fairness in FL networks: clients with superior results at the local level may become unmotivated to participate in a joint network setting, fearing weaker performance of the final global model. Future FL frameworks may therefore need to also include fine-tuning as an additional processing step, which involves center-specific adjustments to the global model prior to application on-site. Nevertheless, the narrower confidence intervals of the aggregated centralized NNCZ and the distributed NNFL, compared to those of the individual local models underscore the enhanced robustness of the multicenter NNs.

Next, the introduction of DP as an additional privacy-preserving mechanism enables to further leverage data protection within ML frameworks, as models without DP remain vulnerable to leaking information about individual data points within datasets. In contrast, DP allows us to reduce this risk of data exposure to a mathematically quantifiable limit.

In this investigation the implementation of DP had ambivalent effects on FL model performance: introducing noise centrally (central DP) during the distributed learning process reduced model performance by only 5% in terms of the AUROC but led to a considerable decrease of 35% in terms of the AUPRC. In contrast, employing local DP via data noise injection into the on-site model training procedure substantially reduced the predictive performance of the aggregated NNCZ-LDP and NNFL-LDP, rendering them insufficient for clinical implementation. To our knowledge, this is the first evaluation of DP measures in surgical ML model analysis.

Previous studies noted lack of comprehensibility, and therefore credibility, as a key barrier for medical professionals in the use of risk stratification tools in clinical practice[37]. Given the black box aspect of high-dimensional ML models, this challenge may be even more pronounced in the field of AI applications. Therefore, providing future users with an in-depth perspective of the underlying model architecture is highly important for enhancing trust and increasing clinical adaptation. In our study, feature analysis revealed high congruence between NNCZ and NNFL, as blood status, patient age, and the CCI consistently emerged as the top-ranked model factors. Notably, these parameters are well-established risk factors known from previous studies using conventional statistics, hence reinforcing the models’ plausibility.[38–41] Interestingly, the presence of malignancy did not appear among the top-ranked patient parameters in any of the models. This finding supports the inclusion of both benign and malignant cases in general models, as it enhances the available sample size without substantially compromising model performance or interpretability. Overall, the component weights of the aggregated NN models differed considerably from those of the local models, with the latter demonstrating a stronger impact on preoperative laboratory values.

In summary, our findings establish the first proof-of-principle for the viability of distributed multicenter AI modelling in the domain of surgical risk stratification. Consequently, several key aspects need to be considered by surgeons and surgical departments for FL real-world application in the near future: First, elaborate training of medical professionals as the primary user is essential to ensure accurate understanding and interpretation of FL-based risk assessments. Next, departments need to establish and maintain high-quality surgical databases, representative of their local surgical cohorts. This is essential not only to successfully contribute to the global model but also to allow for local fine-tuning of the received FL model, thereby enhancing its predictive accuracy on-site. Furthermore, close interdisciplinary collaboration between medical practitioners, IT specialists and data protection officers is crucial to facilitate the technical infrastructure and to meet data privacy standards. The latter can particularly be increased by using DP framework components, however, only at the expense of model accuracy as shown in this analysis. Therefore, future studies are necessary to determine an optimal compromise between data protection and model performance that is mutually agreed upon by all participating network clients. Finally, the inclusion of additional patient characteristics, such as tumor size, neoadjuvant treatment or postoperative events, could further improve risk stratification and prediction adjustments throughout the perioperative course. Consequently, future studies are needed to assess an optimal feature space for continuous risk prediction.

On the other hand, several distinct challenges yet need to be overcome. Establishing a FL network requires a general agreement regarding model parameters among all participating clients; consequently, only standard parameters should be considered. Ensuring model fairness is an additional challenge as an emerging global model may achieve more favorable results for some network participants than others[42]. Finally, network participation requires sufficient computational resources from participating centers for the respective on-site model adjustments. Otherwise, smaller centers might be unable to participate, undermining the principle of data democratization inherent in FL.

This study has several strengths. Beyond its multicenter design that allows for the simulation of a real-world collaboration across multiple institutions, a large and heterogeneous patient cohort was enrolled to reflect clinical variability and ensure robust model training. Moreover, the broad feature space enabled a holistic representation of patients for high-dimensional preoperative risk modeling. The development of all models furthermore adhered to strict methodological standards, applying identical data cleaning, preprocessing and training steps to ensure consistent comparability. Finally, the multilevel comparisons between centralized and distributed models – as well as against each participating center individually – demonstrate not only the feasibility and similar performance of FL compared to the current standard of conventional ML, but also highlight specific challenges for single centers in terms of limited sample sizes and model fairness.

Despite its strengths, some limitations of the study should be acknowledged. The retrospective design and reliance on administrative data may increase susceptibility to documentation and data errors. Moreover, only patients whose follow-up data were available for at least 90 days were considered for the analysis. As patients with uncomplicated courses after discharge are more likely to not attend follow-up appointments, the true 90-day mortality rate may be lower than that apparent in our cohorts. This incongruity in the incidence of the final endpoint could affect the overall accuracy and generalizability of our results. Nonetheless, this assumption equally affected localized, centralized, and FL analyses, preserving the overall comparability of the different analysis approaches.

Conclusion

FL offers an effective framework for training multicenter ML models to predict mortality in patients undergoing colorectal surgery. DP measures may further improve data governance and security, however, only at the expense of model accuracy. Future studies must be conducted to evaluate the impact of local fine-tuning in FL frameworks and to ensure fairness among network clients.
