Factors that influence the adoption of heat-health protective actions during pregnancy: a cross-sectional study conducted in San Antonio, Texas, United States, in 2024
Authors: Lisa K. Zottarelli, Robyn Stassen, Yejin Heo, Madeline Navarrete, Shamshad Khan, Thankam Sunil, Andrea Shields
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionExposure to extreme heat during pregnancy is associated with adverse maternal and child health outcomes. Heat-health protective actions such as spending time in air conditioning and reducing skin exposure to the sun can reduce exposure to extreme heat, but little is known about the willingness and ability of women to adopt heat-health protective actions during pregnancy.MethodsIn summer and fall of 2024, a cross-sectional survey of 202 pregnant women 18 years and older living in Bexar County, Texas, was conducted. Participants were recruited from social service agencies, health clinics, and other facilities that provide pregnancy-related services. The self-administered survey was conducted online and in person in English and Spanish. The study examined the Health Belief Model’s dimensions of knowledge, risk perception, cue-to-action, self-efficacy, and socioeconomic factors on adoption of heat-health protective actions. Descriptive and bivariate statistics and logistic regressions were conducted.ResultsDrinking plenty of water to stay hydrated (n = 164, 81.2%) was the most frequently reported heat-health protective action adopted, followed by limiting time in the sun (n = 159, 80.7%), and spending time in air conditioning (n = 155, 77.9%). An 11-item heat-health protective actions scale had a mean of 6.84 (sd +/- 3.02). Statistically significant relationships were found between cue-to-action (Spearman’s rho=0.234, p<.001), severity of extreme heat during pregnancy (Spearman’s rho=0.119, p=.049), extreme heat knowledge (Spearman’s rho=0.174, p=.008), and self-efficacy in transit (Spearman’s rho=0.143, p=.024) with the heat-health protective actions scale. In a logistic regression model, knowledge of excessive heat health risks had 19.2 greater odds (p=.022) of adopting above the mean number of heat-health protective actions. Cue-to-action in the form of daily weather checks had 5.2 (p=.053) greater odds of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions. Difficulty in self-efficacy during transit had 2.8 greater odds (p=.010) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions.ConclusionsThe results indicate that the pregnant women who are knowledgeable about the effects of heat when not pregnant, actively seek weather information, and recognize their vulnerability to heat in locations such as work and in transit are more likely to engage in heat-health protective actions. Targeted messaging tailored to pregnancy is needed to encourage consistent and comprehensive use of heat-health protective actions.

Extreme heat events are expected to increase in frequency and intensity due to climate change. Heat-health risks refer to the likelihood that exposure to elevated ambient temperatures will result in adverse health outcomes. Vulnerable populations are disproportionately affected by extreme heat events [1, 2], and extreme heat poses significant risks to maternal and fetal health [3, 4]. Exposure to extreme heat during pregnancy is associated with maternal morbidity, preterm birth, low birth weight, cardiac birth defects, neural tube defects, stillbirth, and placental abruption [4–9]. Recent scoping reviews have included studies conducted in the United States, South Korea, Italy, Spain, The Gambia, Ghana, Australian, China, and Canada, and have found the negative impacts of extreme heat on maternal health [3–5]. Despite growing evidence of the potential health impacts of extreme heat, little is known about the women’s willingness or ability to adopt heat-health protective actions during pregnancy [10].

In the United States, federal, state, and local governments utilize a variety of approaches to coordinate actions across organizations and to communicate risk to the public to reduce adverse health effects from extreme heat. Heat Action Plans (HAPs) are written documents that contain strategies for addressing the threat posed by extreme heat within a geographic location aligned with a governmental jurisdiction [11, 12]. While some larger municipalities use fully or partially developed HAPs, many rely on the National Weather Service and other federal agencies for heat-specific warning systems and individual information, including heat-health protective actions, to be communicated to the public. Municipal government extreme heat websites are used to communicate information directly to the public to increase risk awareness and to provide actionable steps to reduce risk [13].

Heat-health protective actions that individuals can take include a range of activities that limit exposure to high temperatures such as spending time in air conditioning and limiting time in the sun. Other protective actions focus on modifying food and beverage consumption to stay hydrated and reduce digestion-related heat generation. Actions extend to clothing choices such as wearing wide-brimmed hats and loose and lightweight clothing to reduce skin exposure to the sun and to promote sweat evaporation. Buddy-systems are recommended so that people can monitor each other for signs of heat-related illness. These heat-heath protective actions are not exclusive to pregnancy and are communicated to the public with varying levels of consistency [10, 11].

The Health Belief Model (HBM) provides a framework for examining adoption of heat-health protective actions. The HBM suggests that health behaviors are shaped by knowledge, risk perception, cue-to-action, self-efficacy, and socioeconomic factors [14]. There is a need to understand the link between heat risk perception, in terms of severity and susceptibility, and adoption of heat-health protective actions [15]. Cue-to-action are signals that prompt individuals to take a specific action and are often understood as a key factor in shaping response to extreme heat [16, 17]. Self-efficacy refers to an individual’s belief in their capacity to act to protect themselves and is limited by barriers. In the case of heat, barriers may be external to the individual such as a lack of control to modify working conditions in a place of employment [16]. Socioeconomic factors such as low income and lack of education, which are often prevalent in minoritized populations, have a critical role in determining the interpretation or prioritization of risk messaging [18], as well as access to heat-health protective resources such as access to air conditioning through transportation to cooling centers and financial means to pay for air conditioning in the home [19, 20].

Building on evidence that extreme heat exposure contributes to adverse maternal and fetal health outcomes, this research seeks to address the gap in understanding how perception of heat-related risks and engagement in heat-health protective actions mitigates risk during pregnancy. Specifically, this study explores how knowledge, risk perception, cue-to-action, and self-efficacy influence the willingness to engage in heat-health protective actions.

Methods and data

A cross-sectional survey design was utilized to collect information from a convenience sample of pregnant women 18 years and older living in Bexar County, Texas, in summer and fall 2024. Participants were recruited at in-person pregnancy-related events such as childbirth classes, pregnancy information fairs, and through flyers posted at clinics and social service agencies. Surveys were self-administered in person on paper or online via Qualtrics. The instrument was offered in English and Spanish. Lead Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville (UTK IRB-24-07988-XM).

Study setting

San Antonio is the 7th most populous city in the United States, with a population in the city and surrounding county exceeding 2 million people [21]. It is ethnically diverse with a large Hispanic population, and it has a sustained and pronounced economic, education, and health disparities [21]. It serves as a regional hub for healthcare and public services. The economy is based in military/defense, education, tourism, and technology. San Antonio has a subtropical climate of hot summers with an average daily high temperature above 32.2 °C (90 °F) in June through September [22]. There is wide-spread heat-mitigating infrastructure including a high prevalence of air conditioning in private homes, schools, and businesses.

The City of San Antonio uses “excessive heat” [23], and many broadcast meteorologists use the term “extreme heat” to describe the weather conditions of high temperature with high humidity (i.e., heat index) weather conditions. While day and night temperatures and heat index thresholds exist for specific types of warnings and alerts, the health risks posted by hot weather conditions for at-risk populations can occur at temperatures below those thresholds. Additionally, information about the dangers posed by hot weather is often shared with the public as the heat index approaches 32.2 °C (90 °F) with terms such as “excessive heat” and “extreme heat”. Therefore, “excessive heat” and “extreme heat” were used in this survey to describe dangerous weather conditions.

The majority of the participants (54.5%) had an associate or technical degree and/or some college. In San Antonio, the median annual household income was USD 65,056 [24]. Among participants, 42.6% had a household income of USD 30,000 or lower, which is less than half the median household income, while 19.5% had a household income greater than USD 75,000. Measurement

To measure the adoption of heat-health protective actions, we asked about 11 specific actions that are frequently recommended on federal and local government heat-health websites [10]. These are to: 1) stay indoors as much as possible; 2) limit time in sun; 3) eat well-balanced, light and regular meals; 4) spend time in air conditioning; 5) wear loose, lightweight, light-colored clothing; 6) wear a wide-brimmed hat when outside; 7) drink plenty of water to stay hydrated; 8) avoid outdoor work or activities during the warmest part of the day; 9) use the buddy system when outside; 10) spend time in public building such as libraries, schools, movie theaters, shopping malls, and other community facilities that have air conditioning; and 11) wear sunscreen. Respondents were asked, “If there was excessive heat in the weather forecast would you [heat-health action]?” The response categories were “more than I normally do”, “same as I normally do”, less than I normally do” and “I would not do this.” “More than I normally do” was coded 1 and other responses were coded 0. We created a composite heat-health protective actions scale by summing responses to the 11 questions, resulting in a range from 0 to 11, with zero indicating no increase in any actions and 11 indicating an increase across all 11 heat-health protective actions. The Cronbach’s α = 0.82.

Knowledge of heat-health risks was measured using a single item that stated, “Excessive heat can affect my health when I am not pregnant.” The response categories were “no” (coded 0), “maybe” (coded 1), and “yes” (coded 2).

Risk perception was operationalized into the variables of susceptibility to the hazard and severity if exposed to the hazard. Susceptibility was measured with the prompt, “Being pregnant puts me at greater risk of heat exhaustion, heat stroke, and other heat-related illnesses.” Responses were “no” (coded 0), “maybe” (coded 1), and “yes” (coded 2). Severity was measured using a single item that stated, “Excessive heat may lead to preterm birth” with response categories of “no” (coded 0), “maybe” (coded 1), and “yes” (coded 2).

Cue-to-action was measured by asking participants, “In a normal week, how often do you check the weather information?” The original response options were “Every day or more often”, “Most days”, “Some days”, “Rarely” and “Never.” Responses were recoded into “every day” (coded 4), “most days” (coded 3), “some days” (coded 2), and rarely or never (coded 1).

Self-efficacy identified locations where the respondents could engage in heat-health protective actions. The question asked, “Where would it be difficult to protect yourself during excessive heat?” The four locations were “home”, “work”, “school”, and “going to or from home, work, or school”. Respondents could select all that applied. A dummy variable was created for each location with “no” = 0 and “yes” = 1.

Participant characteristics included in the analysis were race, ethnicity, level of education, household income, and trimester of pregnancy. Table 2 contains detailed information about each variable including the question/prompt used in the survey, corresponding response categories, and coding.

Analysis

The data were analyzed using International Business Machines Corporation Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM-SPSS) version 29.0.2.0 [25]. First, descriptive statistics were conducted on all variables included in the study. Second, given that the adoption of heat-health protective actions scale is not normally distributed, Spearman’s rho tests were conducted between the ordinal independent variables of knowledge, susceptibility, severity, cue-to-action, self-efficacy, education, household income, and trimester of pregnancy and the dependent variable of the heat-heath protective actions scale. Ethnicity and race are nominal variables, and the Mann-Whitney U and the Kruskal-Wallis H were used to test the relationships between ethnicity and race and the heat-health protective actions scale. Finally, the heat-health actions scale was recoded to create a dichotomous variable comparing the adoption of heat-health protective actions below the mean number of actions (coded 0) and above the mean number of actions (coded 1) to allow for logistic regression analyses. The alpha was set at p<.10.

The least frequently reported actions were wearing a wide brimmed hat when outside (n = 82, 41.2%), eating well-balanced, light, and regular meals (n = 80, 40.8%), and using a buddy system when outside (n = 73, 36.9%).

The descriptive statistics for the dependent and independent variables are shown in Table 4. The 11-item heat-health protective actions scale had a mean of 6.84 (sd +/- 3.02). Approximately 40% (n = 79) of respondents engaged in the cue-to-action of checking the weather every day. Almost 80% (n = 160) had the knowledge that they were susceptible to heat-related illness when not pregnant. 87% of participants perceived themselves as susceptible to adverse health effects of extreme heat. For severity, 41.4% (n = 81) of respondents believed that extreme heat could have serious health consequences during pregnancy. Finally, 46.5% (n = 94) expressed that they would have difficulty in engaging in heat-health protective actions while in transit, 26.7% (n = 54) would find it difficult at work, and 14.9% (n = 30) would find it difficult at home.

Next, the bivariate analyses were conducted. The correlations showed statistically significant relationships between five of the variables and the heat-health protective actions scale. The Spearman’s rank order correlation indicated a weak positive association between the cue-to-action of checking the weather and the heat-health protective actions scale (Spearman’s rho=0.234, p<.001). There were weak positive associations between perceived severity of extreme heat on their health during pregnancy and the heat-health protective actions scale (Spearman’s rho=0.119, p=.049), knowledge of the general health threat of extreme heat and heat-health protective actions (Spearman’s rho=0.174, p=.008), and between self-efficacy to when in transit and the heat-health protective actions scale (Spearman’s rho=0.143, p=.024).

Two logistic regression models are provided in Table 5. In Model 1, respondents who reported uncertainty about whether excessive heat could affect them when not pregnant had 11.4 greater odds (p=.045) of adopting above the mean number of heat-health protective actions compared to respondents who did not have that knowledge. Respondents who affirmed that excessive heat could affect their health when not pregnant had 8.0 greater odds (p=.071) of adopting above the mean number of health protective actions compared to people who reported that excessive heat could not affect their health. The odds of reporting above the mean number of heat-health protective actions adopted increased with the frequency of checking the weather. Specifically, respondents who checked the weather on some days had 4.2 greater odds (p=.071) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions compared to the reference category of rarely or never checked the weather. Respondents who checked the weather on most days had 5.8 greater odds (p=.026) of above the mean heat-health protective actions compared to the reference category. Respondents who checked the weather daily had 6.2 (p=.021) greater odds of above the mean heat-health protective actions. At work, respondent’s self-efficacy had 2.7 lower odds (p=.002) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions, while in transit, respondents had 1.9 greater odds (p=.063) of reporting adopting above the mean heat-health protective actions.

The second logistic regression model included the variables in Model 1 and controls for participant sociodemographic characteristics and pregnancy duration. Respondents who responded “maybe”, that is they were uncertain if excessive heat effected health when not pregnant had 29.6 greater odds (p=.013) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions compared to people who reported that excessive heat would not affect their health when they are not pregnant. Respondents who responded in the affirmative that excessive heat could affect health when not pregnant had 19.2 greater odds (p=.022) of adopting above the mean number of heat-health protective actions. Respondents who checked the weather most days had 6.0 greater odds (p=.039) of adopting above the mean heat-health protective actions compared to the reference category. Daily weather checks had 5.2 (p=.053) greater odds of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions. Low self-efficacy at work had 0.2 lower odds (p<.001) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions. Conversely, respondents who reported difficulty protecting themselves during transit had 2.8 greater odds (p=.010) of adopting seven or more heat-health protective actions. Of the control variables, only household income at the USD 30,001 to USD 45,000 level was statistically significant with respondents having 4.2 greater odds (p=.036) of above the mean heat-health protective actions compared to respondents with household incomes of less than USD 15,000 per year.

Discussion

This study examined factors that influence people’s heat-health protective actions during pregnancy. The relationships between heat-health protective actions and constructs from the Health Belief Model affirms the relevance of this theoretical framework for understanding maternal proactive protective actions in the context of extreme heat. The results indicate that pregnant women who are knowledgeable about the effects of heat when not pregnant, actively seek weather information, and recognize their vulnerability in locations such as at work and in transit, are more likely to engage in heat-health protective actions. These findings are consistent with prior research related to climate change adaptive behaviors among the general public, which have shown that knowledge, risk perception, and self-efficacy are the strongest predictors of adoption of health actions [26]. However, self-efficacy to protect oneself from heat exposure in transit and at work also points to the importance of addressing environmental and infrastructure barriers that limit heat-health protective opportunities outside of the home. These results highlight both the individual-level and structural dimensions of heat-health protective actions and point to the need for future interventions that integrate infrastructure supports, such as providing shaded areas at bus stops and along walking routes, with individual actions, such as spending time in air conditioning, to reduce heat-related risks during pregnancy.

While most respondents reported engaging in heat-health protective actions during extreme heat, some actions such as using a buddy system when outside and wearing a wide-brimmed hat were not adopted as readily. The patterns of protective actions suggest that participants were more likely to engage in relatively easily accessible and low-cost behaviors, such as drinking water to stay hydrated and limiting time in the sun. Yet, actions that require planning, resources, or coordination with others, such as using a buddy system when outside, were less common. These findings align with prior studies that show individuals often take immediate, practical, and easily adopted measures over more resource-intensive behaviors [27]. The lower uptake of actions such as light meals and protective clothing that could reduce the physiological strain of extreme heat exposure suggests potential areas for targeted messaging tailored to pregnancy, especially given that comfort around eating and clothing are often topics of discussion and information-seeking as pregnancy progresses [28].

Limitations

While this study is one of the first to examine factors that influence the adoption of heat-health actions during pregnancy, the cross-sectional design and self-reported data create limitations. The participants may have overstated their use of protective behaviors. Additionally, as a cross-sectional study, the reported use of protective behaviors does not capture the frequency or consistency of use. Almost all participants took the survey in English, despite many reporting a preference to receive weather information in Spanish and that Spanish or a combination of English and Spanish were spoken in the home. Finally, this cross-sectional study utilized a convenience sample conducted in a single city, bringing to question generalizability beyond this study. Convenience samples often must be used with this population as people transition into and out of pregnancy, and they are dispersed throughout the general population. The data collection sites allowed for recruitment of a diverse sample of people who were pregnant at various points in their pregnancies.

A majority of the participants were Hispanic and White, with more than half having at least some postsecondary education. A substantial portion of the population reported lower household incomes. While the sample reflects the population of San Antonio, Texas, in terms of race, ethnicity, and income, the context of San Antonio as a place with hot summers and wide-spread air conditioning availability limits the generalizability of the study. Future research needs to be conducted in locations with less heat adaptation infrastructure to understand factors that influence heat-health protective actions in those environments.

Despite these limitations, the research does shed light on a growing public health concern of extreme heat exposure during pregnancy. As average temperatures increase and extreme heat events become more frequent, identifying factors that influence the adoption of protective behaviors becomes increasingly important. Understanding heat risk perception among women is important because women are more vulnerable to the effects of a disaster and they are critical actors in managing risk for themselves and others, including children [29, 30].

Conclusion

This study contributes to a growing body of evidence on the perceptual, behavioral, and structural factors that influence the adoption of heat-health protective actions during pregnancy. While awareness of heat-related health risks during pregnancy was relatively high among participants, this awareness did not consistently result in the adoption of more heat-health protective actions. Although most participants believed that they were susceptible to heat-related illness, fewer recognized the potential severity of its impacts during pregnancy, such as preterm birth. The results underscore the need to develop interventions that increase risk perception, strengthen the use of cues-to-action such as monitoring the weather forecast, and addressing contextual barriers to engagement in protective actions among pregnant women.
