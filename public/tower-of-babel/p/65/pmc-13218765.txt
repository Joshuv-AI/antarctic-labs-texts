Integrating Intact Fish Skin Grafts With Muscle and Fasciocutaneous Flaps to Optimize Wound Coverage and Eliminate Secondary Donor Sites: A Single-surgeon Experience
Authors: Emily Baruch, Jill Anderson, Matthew Endara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:In complex soft tissue reconstruction, pedicled muscle and fasciocutaneous flaps continue to serve as fundamental techniques. Nevertheless, flap insufficiency, stemming from limited reach, tension, or insufficient bulk, frequently requires secondary flap or grafting procedures, resulting in additional donor site morbidity. This study evaluates the intraoperative application of intact fish skin grafts (IFSGs) as a biological scaffold to assist with wound closure and achieve complete wound coverage without the need for additional donor sites.Methods:A retrospective case series involving 11 patients who underwent soft tissue reconstruction using muscle (n = 3) or fasciocutaneous (n = 8) flaps integrated with IFSG was conducted by a single plastic surgeon. The application of IFSG was performed intraoperatively when the flap volume or reach proved inadequate. Clinical outcomes, healing timelines, and the necessity for reoperation were systematically evaluated.Results:Indications for reconstruction included exposed joints, hardware, bone, tendons without paratenon, and aesthetically critical facial defects. All flaps survived, and IFSGs demonstrated full integration. Ten of 11 wounds achieved complete closure without further surgery. The remaining case, complicated by severe lymphedema, achieved flap survival and coverage of the intended exposed bone but did not go on to fully heal. No secondary tissue harvests were required. The mean healing time was 66.6 days.Conclusions:Intraoperative IFSG integration demonstrates the potential to enhance the functional coverage and healing potential of pedicled muscle and fasciocutaneous flaps, eliminating the need for secondary grafting, and warrants further study.

Takeaways

Question: What can surgeons do when flap tissue proves insufficient intraoperatively to achieve tension-free wound closure without creating an additional donor site?

Findings: In this retrospective review of 11 patients undergoing muscle or fasciocutaneous flap reconstruction augmented with intact fish skin graft, all achieved durable wound coverage without secondary tissue transfer, additional donor site creation, or reoperation.

Meaning: An intact fish skin graft can be a valuable adjunct in flap reconstruction, extending effective coverage, reducing donor site morbidity, and optimizing wound healing when primary flap dimensions are inadequate.

INTRODUCTION

Complex wound reconstruction continues to be a fundamental aspect of plastic surgery, where the strategic use of pedicled muscle and fasciocutaneous flaps has historically been regarded as the gold standard.1 Even these dependable techniques may prove insufficient due to restricted reach, excessive tension on surrounding structures, or inadequate volume to obliterate dead space or safeguard vital structures.1 Such constraints would traditionally necessitate the addition of a skin graft (split- or full-thickness) or the transfer of a secondary flap. These options require an additional donor site harvest, thereby elevating the complexity and morbidity associated with these surgical procedures.1,2 Sir Harold Gillies3 described this in his seminal work The Principles and Art of Plastic Surgery as part of his fifth principle, eloquently stating: “It is impossible to be sure that a flap will fit or look well or even survive in its new place. It is well to have a reserve plan in the form of another flap or skin graft—a lifeboat to get you home.”

Recent advancements in biological matrices have introduced regenerative alternatives that can complement flap-based reconstruction.2,4 Intact fish skin graft (IFSG), which is abundant in omega-3 fatty acids and possesses a structure analogous to human dermis, exemplifies such innovation.5 As a decellularized, lyophilized xenograft harvested from wild-caught Atlantic cod (Gadus morhua), IFSG has demonstrated efficacy in treating both chronic and acute wounds.5–11 Nevertheless, its intraoperative application as a biological adjunct to optimize flap coverage in high-risk reconstructions remains insufficiently explored.6 This study delineates a single surgeon’s experience using IFSG in conjunction with muscle and fasciocutaneous flaps to optimize coverage while maximizing flap function, minimizing closure tension, and eliminating the need for secondary donor sites.

METHODS

Following institutional review board approval (no. 2024/02/55), a retrospective review was conducted on 11 consecutive cases in which intraoperative use of IFSG (Kerecis Omega3, Isafjordur, Iceland) was implemented to augment flap reach, volume, or surface coverage. The IFSG was applied commensurately with its Food and Drug Administration indications in each case. A single, board-certified plastic surgeon conducted all procedures.

The inclusion criteria encompassed patients undergoing flap-based reconstruction wherein an IFSG was applied concurrently to assist with flap reach, dead space fill, or to reduce tension on surrounding structures. No patients undergoing this reconstructive technique were excluded from our review. Flaps used included both muscle (n = 3) and fasciocutaneous (n = 8) designs. The matrix product was applied immediately after flap inset during the pedicled, axial, random pattern, or muscle flap transfer procedure. Complete coverage was defined as the point at which the surgical site was fully covered intraoperatively with the flap and IFSG, achieving a visibly intact surface with no residual open wound, and this was achieved in all patients. Complete healing was defined as full reepithelialization of the surgical site. Data extracted for this study included demographic information, achievement of complete healing, time to complete healing, and any secondary interventions performed. These were assessed during postoperative follow-up visits and documented by the treating surgeon. Descriptive statistics were performed and analyzed using Microsoft Excel 2023.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The decision to use the IFSG with a pedicled flap was made preoperatively and intraoperatively during the study period. Preoperatively, the decision was made when the proposed flap was likely going to be insufficient but would save the patient from a multistage surgery. An example of this was combining a dorsal nasal flap with the IFSG as opposed to using a paramedian forehead flap in a patient who refused the multistage reconstruction. Intraoperatively, the decision was made when the transposed flap did not fully cover the wound, could not be inset without undue tension, or lacked sufficient bulk to fill the dead space.

All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia in the hospital setting. All procedures began with debridement of the wound requiring coverage, followed by elevation and inset of the planned flap. Following the transfer of the flap, the need for IFSG was assessed and measured. Standard sizes of the graft product were used and cut to fit the size of the defect requiring coverage. The IFSG was hydrated with sterile normal saline and manually fenestrated by the surgeon. The graft was not only inset to cover the wound but also tucked into any areas of undermining and dead space. The graft was affixed to the surrounding skin using 5-0 chromic sutures. Bolster dressings were applied in all cases using either negative pressure therapy or mineral oil–soaked sterile sponge wrapped in Adaptic and secured 2-0 silk sutures. In instances involving muscle flap use, the IFSG was hydrated, manually fenestrated, trimmed to size, and secured directly over the muscle surface with 5-0 chromic sutures, functioning as the biologically active covering layer in place of a split-thickness skin graft. Dressings remained undisturbed for 4–5 days, and the primary surgeon performed the initial change. Patients were subsequently followed up weekly in the wound center. Subsequent dressing selection was made with each visit and based on wound needs. Patients who went on to complete healing were subsequently discharged from the wound center.

RESULTS

Among the 11 patients, 6 (54.5%) were women and 5 (45.5%) were men. Comorbidities were similar among the patients (Table 1). The indications for flap surgery included skin cancer in 5 cases (45.5%), limb salvage in 3 (27.3%), exposed hardware in 1 (9.1%), scar contracture in 1 (9.1%), and infected total knee arthroplasty in 1 (9.1%). The types of flaps used were rotation advancement in 4 cases (36.4%), filet of toe in 2 (18.2%), gastrocnemius muscle in 1 (9.1%), abductor digiti minimi in 2 (18.2%), dorsal nasal in 1 (9.1%), and bilateral back keystone flaps in 1 (9.1%). Graft sizes used ranged from 1 × 7 to 7 × 10 cm.

Case Details

Ten of 11 (91%) patients achieved full healing without further surgery. Healing time ranged from 24 to 170 days (mean: 66.6 d). In those patients who went on to complete healing, the portion of the wound covered with the IFSG had the clinical appearance of an area closed primarily. These areas did not demonstrate thin, widened, puckered, or contracted scars, as would have been expected if the wound were allowed to heal entirely by secondary intention.

One patient with severe lymphedema and a threatened limb achieved flap survival and stable coverage of exposed bone but did not reach complete closure as of 360 days postintervention. No further surgical intervention was attempted in this patient for closure due to inadequate control of the lymphedema. Multiple interventions, including lymphedema therapy, lymphedema wraps, pumps, and wound VAC therapy, were used without success, and as such, no secondary graft or flap was attempted, but limb salvage was indeed achieved.

REPRESENTATIVE CASES

Case 1: Filet-of-Toe Flap With IFSG Extension

A 61-year-old man with diabetes mellitus sustained a forklift injury resulting in a 5.2 × 3.1 × 2 cm defect of the dorsum of his right foot (Fig. 1). The injury resulted in an open third metatarsophalangeal joint as well as exposure of the second metatarsophalangeal joint and the extensor tendons of the third, fourth, and fifth digits. A filet-of-toe flap was performed using the third digit to cover the exposed bone and joint at the base of the second. IFSG was used to optimize distal coverage, as the flap had insufficient reach to completely close the wound or obliterate dead space. The patient was subsequently followed up in the wound center until healing was complete at 168 days postoperatively, without the need for further intervention.

Case 2: Abductor Digiti Minimi Muscle Flap

A 50-year-old man with diabetes mellitus presented with a necrotizing soft tissue infection of the right lower extremity involving multiple fascial compartments, with extension into the ankle joint and complete destruction of the joint capsule, necessitating local muscle coverage (Fig. 2). He underwent serial operative debridement and intravenous antibiotic therapy until infection control was achieved, and tissue cultures were negative. At final debridement, he was noted to have a septic, open ankle joint (7 × 3 × 2 cm) and a large area of exposed Achilles tendon (12 × 6 cm). IFSG was applied to promote granulation over the exposed tendon, followed by 2 applications of a collagen-based antimicrobial matrix (PuraPly) in the wound clinic. Forty-two days later, definitive reconstruction was performed with a pedicled abductor digiti minimi muscle flap for ankle coverage; IFSG was used intraoperatively to protect the vascular pedicle and fill dead space. The Achilles tendon defect was able to be resurfaced with a split-thickness skin graft. Durable wound closure was achieved at 67 days postoperatively.

Case 3: Bilateral Keystone Flaps for Back Wound

A 68-year-old morbidly obese woman with diabetes mellitus presented following spinal fusion for severe spondylolisthesis with a deep wound and threatened hardware. Initial attempts by her spinal surgeon to re-excise and close an early surgical dehiscence were unsuccessful and were followed by repeat dehiscence and a deep space abscess (Fig. 3). Repeat debridement with exploration of the deep hardware was performed in conjunction with the spinal surgeon. The 15 × 5 × 4 cm resulting wound appeared to be clean but did have light growth of Morganella in culture broth only. The decision was made in conjunction with the patient and her spinal surgeon to attempt closure given the appearance of the wound and the critical need to protect the critical hardware. Large bilateral keystone flaps were designed and successfully transferred centrally over her spinal defect. Due to the patient’s body habitus, the keystone flaps lacked sufficient reach to completely close the wound without significant tension. IFSG was used to bridge the residual gap, which also allowed for partial closure in the setting of lightly positive cultures. The keystone flaps demonstrated 100% viability, and the patient went on to complete durable closure at 84 days postoperatively.

DISCUSSION

Regenerative dermal matrices have arisen as a very useful tool for reconstructive surgeons, providing a biocompatible scaffold for neovascularization, fibroblast infiltration, extracellular matrix deposition, and formation of a durable neodermis. These characteristics help facilitate coverage of wounds that might otherwise necessitate flap reconstruction.2,4 In certain cases, especially when managing exposed vital structures, defects in cosmetically sensitive areas, high-weight-bearing surfaces, or when hardware coverage is required, autologous tissue transfer remains the optimal choice, as these flaps hold benefits that matrix products are still not able to replicate or improve upon.1,2 Even so, the utility and versatility of these products seemingly allow them to be combined with flaps to synergize the effectiveness of each.2,4 To our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically examined the combination of IFSG and local flaps in complex reconstructive situations—this approach seems truly novel and has the potential to simplify an otherwise very challenging situation.

This series demonstrates that IFSG had the ability to extend the utility of pedicled flaps in 11 challenging reconstructions. The matrix product was chosen for its ability to integrate rapidly and reepithelialize efficiently.5–9 Application of the matrix product in an immediate setting where flap transfer proved insufficient to completely close a wound allowed for complete closure while avoiding additional donor sites. Our findings support this technique and product as a potentially valuable adjunct when flap reach, bulk, or tension is suboptimal.

Though multiple regenerative dermal matrices are currently available, each holds unique factors that must be considered by the reconstructive surgeon when selecting the product to use. The natural dermal architecture of IFSG, combined with its high omega-3 content and antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties, likely contributes to its observed clinical benefits.4,5,12 Prior studies have established its efficacy in chronic wounds, diabetic ulcers, and burn donor sites.5–11 Our findings further extend this application into the intraoperative reconstructive domain. We do believe, however, that additional studies to further define the role of alternative products in these cases are warranted.

This study has limitations, including the small sample size, heterogenous patients, lack of a comparator group, and single-surgeon design, as well as the use of only 1 type of regenerative dermal matrix product. There was also a somewhat prolonged healing time of 66 days, which must still be contextualized by the high-risk nature of the wounds and patient comorbidities such as diabetes and lymphedema.13–18 Previous research has indeed found that with standard care, deep diabetic foot wounds often require 56–126 days to heal, and many do not completely close within approximately 90–150 days.13–15 The time frame to heal in our study is actually at the faster end of what would be expected for complex wounds in these populations, but direct comparison between the use of the matrix product versus healing by secondary intention was not performed.

In addition, although we believe that mitigating secondary donor sites clearly minimizes the human cost of this strategy, financial cost data are lacking. Previous cost–benefit analyses comparing acellular dermal templates such as Integra (Integra Life Sciences, NJ) to flap surgery demonstrated that dermal substitutes may provide a favorable economic alternative in certain settings.19 Future prospective studies to clarify these limitations are warranted.

CONCLUSIONS

IFSGs constitute a promising modality for enhancing flap-based reconstruction. Their application during surgery enables surgeons to circumvent secondary donor sites while accomplishing durable, tension-free closure. As biological matrices see increased integration into surgical practices, IFSG could potentially become a primary adjunct for intricate soft tissue reconstruction.
