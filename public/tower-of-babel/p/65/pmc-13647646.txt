Socioeconomic gradients in hypertension prevalence and management: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Eunji Kim, Hokyou Lee, YunJae Jung, Hyeon Chang Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite advances in hypertension treatment, achieving optimal blood pressure control remains a major public health challenge. Socioeconomic status (SES) may influence hypertension management; however, evidence on its associations with pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, and self-management education remains limited. In this study, the association between SES and hypertension-related outcomes in the Republic of Korea was investigated.MethodsIn this nationwide cross-sectional study, data from the 2023 Korea Community Health Survey were analyzed, comprising 231,584 and 228,608 adults for educational attainment and household income analyses, respectively. Hypertension-related outcomes included diagnosed hypertension, pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, and self-management education. Crude and age- and sex-adjusted rates were calculated, followed by multivariable logistic regression analyses, adjusting for potential confounders.ResultsOverall, the prevalence of diagnosed hypertension was 31.3%. Among participants with hypertension, the rates of pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, and self-management education were 95.9%, 23.8%, and 15.5%, respectively. The multivariable analyses showed a dose–response relationship between SES and outcomes: participants with primary school education or less had higher odds of diagnosed hypertension (odds ratio, 1.79; 95% confidence interval, 1.71–1.88) and pharmacotherapy use (2.04; 1.67–2.50) but lower odds of lifestyle modification engagement (0.47; 0.43–0.50) and receiving self-management education (0.34; 0.31–0.38) compared to those with college degree or higher. Notably, these socioeconomic gradients remained robustly consistent across all strata, while significantly steeper gradients were observed among females and younger adults (< 65 years). Analyses by household income showed similar patterns of associations, although with slightly weaker magnitudes.ConclusionsLower SES was associated with a higher prevalence of diagnosed hypertension and pharmacotherapy use but lower participation in lifestyle modifications and self-management education. Strengthening tailored self-management education and lifestyle modification support for socioeconomically disadvantaged groups may be an important strategy to reduce hypertension-related disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28502-x.

Background

Globally, an estimated 1.4 billion adults have hypertension, a leading modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature death [1–3]. Previous studies have shown that lowering blood pressure (BP) significantly reduces the risk of CVD and renal disease [4, 5]. Effective strategies for BP control have been established; however, hypertension management remains suboptimal globally [6], with only 54% of patients with hypertension being diagnosed, 42% receiving treatment, and 21% achieving BP control [7]. Moreover, these management rates vary by a country’s socioeconomic level, with lower rates in low- and middle-income countries, primarily due to limited access to medications and healthcare professionals [8]. Nevertheless, patient-related factors, such as medication adherence and lifestyle modifications, also influence effective management, even in high-income countries [9, 10].

Socioeconomic status (SES) at the individual level has also been established as an important factor influencing hypertension prevalence and management [11, 12]. Findings from a meta-analysis revealed that lower SES was associated with an increased risk of elevated BP and hypertension, with education level showing the strongest association among the various socioeconomic indicators [13]. However, findings on the association between SES and hypertension management remain inconclusive. Reportedly, lower education levels are associated with a lower likelihood of hypertension treatment and control [11, 14]. In a systematic review of the impact of social determinants on hypertension outcomes, significant disparities in hypertension management among adults in the United States were highlighted. Despite variations in the definition of social determinants, evidence indicated that higher education levels, insurance coverage, and income were associated with lower hypertension prevalence and better BP control [15]. However, the study highlighted that these findings may be specific only to the United States context; hence, caution is required when applying them to other populations [15]. Some studies have found no significant differences in hypertension awareness, treatment, or control based on individual SES [16, 17]. Lifestyle modification and self-management are crucial components of hypertension treatment, yet their relationship with SES remains unclear [18, 19]. For instance, a systematic review on the moderating effect of SES on self-management support interventions showed reduced engagement among individuals with low SES, but unclear findings regarding retention and post-intervention results [18].

The 2025 World Health Organization Global Report on Hypertension cites the Republic of Korea as a case study for effective hypertension control, noting that its national control rate has surpassed 50% [20]. This progress is largely attributed to the systemic integration of hypertension care within universal health coverage and the reduction of financial barriers to medication [20]. However, these macro-level indicators may mask internal disparities in hypertension management, particularly non-pharmacological interventions. This study, therefore, aims to examine socioeconomic gradients in diagnosed hypertension and management-related outcomes to identify persistent inequalities that may be masked by favorable national performance averages and universal health coverage.

Methods

Data source and study participants

In this cross-sectional study, data from the 2023 Korea Community Health Survey (KCHS) were analyzed. The KCHS is a nationwide, standardized health survey conducted by the Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency [21]. Data on sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviors, chronic diseases, accidents, medical utilization, and quality of life are collected through face-to-face interviews. A two-stage sampling method is employed in this nationwide survey: administrative divisions and housing types are stratified, followed by a random selection of individual households within sampled units. The target sample size is approximately 900 respondents per stratum, yielding 230,000 individuals in all administrative divisions (258 municipalities) with a margin of error of ± 3%. The details of the KCHS have been described elsewhere [22].

Between May 16 and July 31, 2023, 231,752 individuals aged ≥ 19 years participated in the survey. For the educational attainment analysis, 168 participants (0.07%) were excluded owing to incomplete data on SES, hypertension-related variables, or confounders, leaving 231,584 individuals in the analytic sample. Similarly, 3,144 participants (1.36%) were excluded from the household income analysis, resulting in an analytic sample of 228,608 individuals (Additional Figure S1).

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gachon University Gil Medical Center (GBIRB2025-026) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was waived because this study used deidentified, routinely collected survey data.

Socioeconomic status and variables

Socioeconomic disparities in diagnosed hypertension and management were investigated using two indicators: educational attainment and household monthly income. Educational attainment was categorized into four levels: primary school or lower, middle school, high school, or college or higher. Household monthly income was divided into quartiles based on the data distribution. The medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were as follows: low (Q1), 1 million Korean Won (KRW) (IQR: 0.6–1.2 million); lower-middle (Q2), 2.5 million KRW (2–3 million); upper-middle (Q3), 4.2 million KRW (4–5 million); and high (Q4), 7.5 million KRW (6–10 million).

Possible confounders were selected a priori based on previous literature that has reported associations with diagnosed hypertension or hypertension management [23, 24]. Sociodemographic factors, including age, sex, marital status (never married, married, or divorced/widowed), and area of residence (metropolitan, urban, or rural), were included. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated from self-reported height and weight and classified into four categories [25]: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–22.9 kg/m2), overweight (23.0–24.9 kg/m2), or obese (≥ 25.0 kg/m2). Smoking status (never, past smoker, or current smoker) and physical activity (none, moderate, or high) were also considered [26]. No physical activity was defined as not engaging in moderate- or vigorous-intensity exercise. Moderate activity comprised 1–149 min of moderate or 1–74 min of vigorous activity, or an equivalent combination per week. High activity was defined as ≥ 150 min of moderate exercise, ≥ 75 min of vigorous exercise, or an equivalent combination per week. Unmet healthcare needs were defined as having any experience in the past 12 months of needing healthcare services but not receiving them [27].

Hypertension-related outcomes

Hypertension was defined as self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension. For hypertension management, data on pharmacotherapy use, engagement in lifestyle modifications, and participation in hypertension management education were collected from participants with diagnosed hypertension. Pharmacotherapy use was defined as the current use of any antihypertensive medication. Lifestyle modification was assessed using a single self-reported question among participants with diagnosed hypertension. Participants were asked whether they had practiced healthier lifestyle behaviors, such as improving dietary habits or increasing physical activity, in response to their hypertension diagnosis. Receiving hypertension management education at healthcare facilities, including community health centers, clinics, and hospitals, distinct from standard medical consultations lasting < 10 min, was defined as self-management education.

Statistical analyses

Participants’ characteristics were summarized as medians with IQRs or frequencies with survey-weighted percentages, stratified by educational attainment and household income. For each socioeconomic group, crude and age- and sex-adjusted rates of diagnosed hypertension and management were calculated. The rate of diagnosed hypertension was defined as the proportion of participants with diagnosed hypertension among all study participants, while the rates of pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modifications, and self-management education were defined as the proportions of participants with hypertension receiving these respective interventions. Age- and sex-adjusted rates were estimated using the direct standardization method. The total study population was used as the standard population to account for differences in age and sex distribution across groups. Linear trends across socioeconomic categories were assessed by treating the ordinal values of each category as a continuous variable in linear regression and multivariable logistic regression models.

Associations between socioeconomic indicators and hypertension-related outcomes were examined using multivariable logistic regression models. To evaluate the robustness of the associations across different levels of covariate adjustment, we performed sequential adjustment analyses. Model 1 adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 further adjusted for marital status, area of residence, and unmet healthcare needs. Model 3 additionally adjusted for BMI, smoking status, and physical activity, corresponding to the fully adjusted model used in the main analyses. The main analyses were conducted in the overall population using educational attainment and household income as socioeconomic indicators. Subgroup analyses were stratified by sex, age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), area of residence (urban vs. rural), and unmet healthcare needs. To evaluate potential effect modification by these subgroup variables, multiplicative interaction terms were included in the multivariable models. The significance of these interactions was determined using the joint Wald test.

Survey weights were accounted for in all analyses, which were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Diagnosed hypertension and management rates by socioeconomic status

Overall, 31.3% of participants were diagnosed with hypertension. Among them, 95.9% were receiving pharmacotherapy, 23.8% reported practicing lifestyle modifications, and 15.5% had received self-management education. Figure 1 shows the crude and age- and sex-adjusted rates of diagnosed hypertension and management by educational attainment. After adjusting for age and sex, the rates of diagnosed hypertension significantly decreased as the educational levels increased: primary school education or less, 28.0%; middle school education, 25.4%; high school education, 23.1%; and college education or higher, 19.8%. Among individuals with diagnosed hypertension, pharmacotherapy use was high across all education groups. The age- and sex-adjusted pharmacotherapy rates, estimated using the direct standardization method, ranged from 72.6% among participants with primary school education or less to 78.6% among those with college education or higher. The lower adjusted rate in the lowest education group, despite its higher crude rate, reflects the higher proportion of older adults in this group after standardization to the age and sex distribution of the total study population. However, the rates of practicing lifestyle modifications were generally low, ranging from 20.6% in the lowest education group to 38.8% in the highest. Association between hypertension-related outcomes and socioeconomic status

Table 2 shows the associations between educational attainment and diagnosed hypertension and hypertension management outcomes across adjustment models. The socioeconomic gradients were generally consistent across models, with modest attenuation after additional adjustment for BMI, smoking status, and physical activity. Figure 3b and Additional Table S1 present the associations between household income and diagnosed hypertension and hypertension management outcomes. The household income analysis showed similar but weaker socioeconomic gradients compared with educational attainment. Subgroup analyses

In subgroup analyses stratified by sex, age, area of residence, and unmet healthcare needs, dose–response gradients between educational attainment and hypertension-related outcomes appeared largely consistent, although specific associations varied by demographic strata (Table 3). For educational attainment, inverse dose–response associations with diagnosed hypertension were consistently observed, and these gradients were significantly more pronounced among females, younger adults (< 65 years), and urban residents (P for interaction < 0.001). Regarding pharmacological management, the association between lower educational attainment and higher pharmacotherapy use was also notably stronger in the younger age group (P for interaction < 0.001). Lower educational attainment was consistently associated with lower odds of lifestyle modification and self-management education across most strata, with limited evidence of effect modification. Significant interactions were observed for self-management education, where the educational gradient was steeper among younger adults (P for interaction < 0.001). Stratification by unmet healthcare needs revealed no significant effect modification for any hypertension-related outcomes. Discussion

This nationwide study examined the association between SES and diagnosed hypertension and hypertension management, including pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, and self-management education. Overall, the prevalence of diagnosed hypertension was 31.3% and decreased with increasing SES. Pharmacotherapy rates were consistently high across all socioeconomic groups (overall 95.9%), whereas lifestyle modification engagement (23.8%) and exposure to self-management education (15.5%) were generally low. Across sequential adjustment models, educational attainment showed dose–response associations with diagnosed hypertension and hypertension management, with only modest attenuation after additional adjustment. Lower educational attainment was associated with higher odds of diagnosed hypertension and pharmacotherapy use. In contrast, lower educational attainment was associated with lower engagement in lifestyle modification and self-management education. These associations were less pronounced for household income; nevertheless, the socioeconomic gradients for lifestyle modification and self-management education were consistent. Subgroup analyses showed that socioeconomic gradients persisted across all strata, with more pronounced disparities among females and younger adults. Notably, hypertension management gaps did not significantly differ by area of residence or unmet healthcare needs, suggesting that improving physical infrastructure or healthcare access alone may be insufficient to reduce these entrenched socioeconomic disparities.

The association observed in our study between lower SES and a higher prevalence of diagnosed hypertension is consistent with previous investigations. Individuals with lower educational attainment, lower income, or no health insurance were more likely to have hypertension [13, 15, 28]. In a meta-analysis of 51 studies, low educational attainment (vs. high educational attainment; pooled OR, 2.02; 95% CI, 1.55–2.63) and low income (vs. high income; pooled OR, 1.19; 95% CI, 0.96–1.48) were associated with higher hypertension prevalence [13]. Although statistically significant results were not attained in some studies, the overall evidence consistently supports a positive association between low SES and hypertension [11, 29].

On the other hand, the association between SES and hypertension management remains underexplored and controversial [13]. Only a few studies have reported higher treatment rates among individuals with higher educational attainment [11, 30]. For instance, in a Korean study, in which data from the 2007–2008 Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey was used, 4,275 middle-aged adults (aged 40–64 years) were analyzed. The results showed that those with higher education were more likely to receive pharmacological treatment [11]. Notably, an opposite pattern was found in our study, which was conducted in the same country, possibly reflecting differences in study populations and periods, as adults of all ages were included in our study, and more recent data from 2023 were analyzed. The impact of the Chronic Disease Management Program (CDMP), introduced in 2012, could have influenced this discrepancy. The CDMP is a nationwide, multifaceted program that offers reduced out-of-pocket costs and provider incentives [31]. In a pre-post retrospective study, it was demonstrated that this program improved adherence and persistence with antihypertensive medication [32]. Such financial support may have improved healthcare access, particularly for individuals with lower SES, potentially contributing to their higher reported pharmacotherapy use. However, this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Given the high prevalence of pharmacotherapy use, ORs may overstate the relative magnitude of the association. In addition, higher pharmacotherapy use among lower SES groups may also reflect greater clinical need, longer disease duration, more severe hypertension, or other unmeasured clinical factors.

In France, treatment rates did not differ significantly by SES; however, among those receiving antihypertensive medication, individuals with lower SES were more likely to have uncontrolled BP [29]. Similarly, findings from a study in Singapore revealed that individuals with no formal education had higher odds of receiving treatment (OR, 6.6; 95% CI, 2.9–14.7) compared with those with more than 10 years of education, yet their odds of achieving controlled BP were lower (OR, 0.3; 95% CI, 0.2–0.5) [33]. This inverse relationship between treatment and BP control across SES groups, as observed in previous studies, may partly reflect that individuals with higher SES may be more likely to adopt nonpharmacologic approaches, whereas those with lower SES may rely more on pharmacologic therapy and have lower medication adherence [6]. In our study, lower SES was associated with lower engagement in lifestyle modification and self-management education, suggesting potential gaps in nonpharmacological management among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. Indeed, suboptimal lifestyle behaviors have been more prevalent among individuals with lower SES [34]. A review of 131 studies from European countries showed that total leisure-time physical activity was lower in lower SES groups than among their counterparts [35]. In a Korean study, where smoking trends were examined between 1989 and 2003, an overall decline was observed in smoking rates across all SES groups, but widening disparities were found in smoking prevalence by educational level among adults under 45 years of age [36]. Dietary intake and quality also appear to differ by SES; for instance, research has shown that individuals with lower SES consumed more bread, oils, and sugar, but their intake of protein and vitamins was lower than that of their higher SES counterparts [37]. Given the importance of self-management education in facilitating such lifestyle modifications [38], the low participation rates observed in our study, particularly among lower socioeconomic strata, highlight a critical gap in current preventive strategies. This pattern is notable even in the context of Korea’s established hypertension care infrastructure: while universal health coverage and CDMP may have reduced financial barriers to pharmacotherapy, engagement in nonpharmacological management appeared lower among socioeconomically disadvantaged groups.

Among the socioeconomic indicators examined, educational attainment showed a stronger association with hypertension-related outcomes than income. This is consistent with previous studies reporting that educational attainment is more strongly associated with health outcomes than other SES indicators [11, 13, 30]. A possible explanation is that income is often difficult to assess accurately and may fluctuate over time, whereas educational attainment is generally more stable across adulthood. Nevertheless, a study from India showed that income, rather than education, had a prominent association with hypertension, suggesting that the most appropriate SES proxy may vary by sociocultural context and health system structure [39].

In our subgroup analyses, socioeconomic disparities in diagnosed hypertension were more pronounced among females, consistent with prior literature [11, 13, 29]. Studies from France and Korea have shown that lower educational attainment was significantly associated with diagnosed hypertension in females but not in males [11, 29]. Although diagnosed hypertension is generally higher in men, socioeconomic disparities appear to be greater in women. This pattern is consistent with findings for obesity in Korea [40], where obesity prevalence is generally higher in men, but inverse socioeconomic gradients are markedly steeper in women. Similarly, educational disparities in diagnosed hypertension, pharmacotherapy, and self-management education were more pronounced among adults < 65 years than among those ≥ 65 years. This finding should be interpreted cautiously, as educational attainment may partly reflect cohort differences in Korea, where educational attainment differs substantially across generations. Among working-age adults with lower SES, greater reliance on medication at the expense of lifestyle modification or self-management education may reflect time poverty [41], as inflexible work conditions can make pharmacological treatment the more feasible option. Except for a steeper socioeconomic gradient in diagnosed hypertension among urban residents, disparities in hypertension management did not differ significantly across residential areas or unmet healthcare needs. This suggests that improving physical infrastructure or subjective clinical access alone may not be sufficient to narrow the socioeconomic gaps in hypertension management.

This study has several strengths. First, we analyzed a nationally representative sample collected via complex sampling design (stratification, clustering, and weighting) from a large, community-based survey. This sampling enhances the generalizability of our findings to the Korean adult population. Second, the KCHS includes important variables, such as participation in hypertension education, engagement in lifestyle modification, and unmet medical needs that are generally not available in other large-scale datasets. This enabled us to examine socioeconomic gradients across multiple domains of hypertension management, including pharmacotherapy, lifestyle modification, and self-management education. However, some limitations should also be considered. First, owing to its cross-sectional design, causal interpretations should be made with caution. Reverse causation is unlikely, given that the analysis involved past educational attainment; nevertheless, longitudinal studies are required to establish causal relationships based on our findings. Second, because the KCHS data were collected by trained enumerators through self-report, clinical confirmation of hypertension diagnosis and treatment status was not possible. In particular, hypertension was defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis rather than measured BP; therefore, our outcome should be interpreted as diagnosed hypertension rather than BP-defined hypertension prevalence. This definition may also introduce detection bias, because SES can influence healthcare access, screening opportunities, and disease awareness. Thus, the observed socioeconomic gradients in diagnosed hypertension may reflect differences in detection as well as differences in the underlying burden of hypertension. However, the prevalence of diagnosed hypertension in our sample was broadly comparable to the national estimate reported in the 2024 Korea Hypertension Fact Sheet (31.3% vs. 30.1%) [42]. The higher pharmacotherapy rate in our sample than the prescription-based estimate (95.9% vs. 74.1%) may reflect differences in treatment definitions and data collection methods, further supporting cautious interpretation of self-reported treatment status [42]. Third, information on measured BP, BP control, disease duration and severity, and medication adherence was unavailable. Therefore, pharmacotherapy use should be interpreted as treatment uptake rather than adequate hypertension management or successful BP control. Fourth, lifestyle modification was assessed using a broad self-reported measure of whether participants practiced healthier lifestyle behaviors in response to their hypertension diagnosis, rather than individual behavioral components such as dietary change, physical activity, smoking cessation, weight control, or sodium reduction. This measure may be vulnerable to recall and social desirability bias, and such bias may differ across educational groups. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as reported engagement in diagnosis-related lifestyle modification rather than objectively measured behavioral improvement. Last, as our findings were derived from a population within a universal health coverage system, caution is warranted when generalizing these results to countries with different healthcare systems.

Conclusion

This study identified significant socioeconomic gradients in the prevalence and management of diagnosed hypertension using nationally representative data from the Republic of Korea. Although pharmacotherapy rates were consistently high across all socioeconomic groups—and even slightly higher among individuals with lower SES—engagement in lifestyle modification and participation in self-management education were markedly lower in these groups. These findings underscore the need to promote hypertension-related education and support behavior change, particularly among socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, to achieve more equitable and effective hypertension care. Community health centers and CDMP may serve as practical platforms for delivering tailored self-management education and lifestyle modification support to these groups.
