Enhanced immunogenicity in mouse by recombinant BCG prime and protein boost based on latency antigen Rv1733c and/or reactivation antigen RpfE
Authors: Mitra Ashayeripanah, Bahram Kazemi, Narcis Saubi, Joan Joseph-Munne, Fereshteh Eftekhar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMycobacterium bovis bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) is the only licensed vaccine against tuberculosis (TB), but it fails to prevent the establishment and reactivation of latent TB. Overexpression of latency and reactivation antigens by BCG may enhance its efficacy against latent infection and subsequent reactivation. This study aimed to construct recombinant BCG strains (rBCGs) overexpressing the latency antigen Rv1733c and/or resuscitation promoting factor E (RpfE).MethodsEpisomal (pJH222) or integrative (pJH223) plasmids–either empty or encoding rv1733c or rpfE–were transformed into a lysine auxotroph strain of BCG for lysine complementation. The resulting rBCGs were genetically and phenotypically characterized. Female BALB/c mice underwent a six-month vaccination regimen consisting of a homologous prime-boost immunisation with the rBCGs, followed by a heterologous boost with Rv1733c and/or RpfE recombinant proteins. Splenocytes from immunised mice were stimulated ex vivo with purified protein derivative (PPD) or the corresponding recombinant protein(s) to measure secreted cytokines.ResultsThe rBCGs carrying episomal recombinant plasmids encoding rv1733c or rpfE elicited higher levels of antigen-specific interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) compared to empty-vector controls. rBCG strains with integrative constructs encoding either antigen induced higher levels of tumour necrosis factor-alpha relative to the sham, wild-type BCG, and empty-vector groups. All rBCGs expressing either or both antigens generated more favourable IFN-γ to interleukin (IL)-10 ratios upon stimulation with their respective antigen(s) than with PPD. While two rBCGs elicited detectable IL-4 responses, the rBCGs expressing both antigens did not induce IL-4.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates promising immunogenicity of the latency antigen Rv1733c and the resuscitation antigen RpfE, highlighting their potential to enhance BCG efficacy through a tailored prime-boost vaccination strategy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11534-w.

Background

Mathematical modelling estimates that a quarter of the world’s population in 2014 was latently infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) [1]. Reactivation of this latent infection leads to tuberculosis (TB) [2], which contributed to an estimated 10.8 million new cases of active TB disease, and 1.25 million deaths worldwide in 2023 alone [3]. The only licensed vaccine against TB, Mycobacterium bovis bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) [3, 4], protects children against pulmonary and extra-pulmonary TB for up to 10 years, however, its efficacy declines with each subsequent year [5, 6]. Consequently, BCG provides limited and variable protective efficacy (0–78%) against pulmonary TB in adults [5, 6]. Indeed, BCG is ineffective against post-exposure active TB, latent TB infection, and subsequent reactivation later in life [4].

Tailored adolescent and adult vaccines could therefore help prevent progression to TB disease following primary infection, as well as reinfection and reactivation in patients with latent infection [7]. Consistent with this, BCG revaccination in South African adolescents has shown significant protection against sustained, de novo Mtb infection [8]. Thus, genetically modified BCG strains that induce superior immune responses to antigens associated with latency and reactivation phases of TB [9, 10], represent promising vaccine candidates for adolescents and adults.

Antigens associated with the latency and resuscitation/reactivation phases of Mtb infection include DosR regulon-encoded proteins [10] and resuscitation-promoting factors (Rpfs) [11], respectively. Although BCG harbours genes encoding DosR antigens [12] and Rpfs [13], due to its inability to establish a dormant state in immunocompetent vaccinees, these late-stage proteins are unlikely to be efficiently expressed [9]. Therefore, immune responses to these antigens could be harnessed to enhance the efficacy of BCG by overexpressing selected DosR antigens and/or Rpfs from Mtb.

Rv1733c is a latency-associated protein encoded by the DosR regulon. It is a conserved transmembrane, non-allergenic antigen (Table 1) [13]. One of the five Rpfs of Mtb, Rv2450c (RpfE) [11], is a relatively small, probably-secreted, non-allergenic antigen (Table 2) [13]. Both Rv1733c and RpfE contain multiple T cell and B cell epitopes (Tables 1 and 2), supporting their potential as vaccine candidates for overexpression in BCG [14–18]. This study aimed to construct recombinant BCGs (rBCGs) overexpressing Rv1733c or RpfE. Subsequently, we assessed the immunogenic potential of these rBCGs by evaluating cytokine responses by mouse splenocytes following a six-month vaccination regimen. The vaccination included rBCGs–administered alone or in combination–as prime and homologous boosters, along with recombinant Rv1733c and/or RpfE proteins as heterologous boosters.

Methods

Construction of Escherichia coli-mycobacterial shuttle vectors expressing Rv1733c or RpfE

The coding sequences of rv1733c and rpfE from Mycobacterium tuberculosis H37Rv were obtained from the accession numbers JLDD01000018.1 and NP_216966.1, respectively. These sequences were chemically synthesized and provided as PCR products (Shingene, China). To distinguish the native proteins from those overexpressed by the recombinant plasmids, coding sequences of HA and V5 tags (codon-optimized for expression in mycobacteria) were added to the 5’ end of rv1733c and rpfE, respectively, using PCR and the primers listed in Table 3. These primers include a string of three termination codons, shown in bold. Also, the coding sequence of HA tag added to rv1733c and the V5 tag sequence added to rpfE are underlined. pJH222 is a low-copy-number, episomal vector containing a mycobacterial origin of replication (oriM). pJH223 is an integrative vector carrying an attachment site (attP) and the integrase gene (int) from mycobacteriophage L5, allowing single-copy integration into the mycobacterial chromosome [20]. Both pJH222 and pJH223 also contain the Mtb α-antigen promoter for gene expression in mycobacteria, a kanamycin resistance gene (aph) as a selectable marker, an E. coli origin of replication (oriE), and a wild-type lysine A-complementing gene that allows plasmid maintenance in lysine auxotrophic BCG [21–24]. Kanamycin resistance and lysine complementation were used to select and maintain plasmids in E. coli and BCG, respectively.

Bacterial strains and cultures

Luria–Bertani (LB) broth or LB agar plates (Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO, USA) supplemented with 50 μg mL−1 kanamycin (prepared in-house) were used to culture competent E. coli DH5-α (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) at 37 °C. Empty pJH222 and pJH223 plasmids, and the recombinant plasmids (pJH222-rv1733c, pJH223-rv1733c, pJH222-rpfE, and pJH223-rpfE), were transformed into electrocompetent lysine auxotrophic M. bovis BCG strain Pasteur [25] using a previously described protocol [24]. Mycobacterial cultures were maintained in Middlebrook 7H9 broth or on Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with 10% albumin–dextrose–catalase (ADC; Becton Dickinson, Sparks, MD, USA), 0.05% Tween 80, and 0.2% glycerol (Sigma-Aldrich), hereafter referred to as complete Middlebrook. When required, kanamycin was added at a concentration of 25 µg mL−1. L-lysine monohydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the medium at a concentration of 40 μg mL−1 prior to transformation of the auxotrophic BCG.

Selection of recombinant clones

Primary selection of recombinant colonies was done by growing the BCG transformants on Middlebrook 7H10 agar supplemented with kanamycin but without lysine. Colony PCR was carried out on a subset of colonies applying the same primers used for cloning. Selected colonies were subject to PCR across four successive subculturing passages using complete Middlebrook lacking both kanamycin and lysin to confirm the in vitro stability of the plasmid. Given that the generation time for BCG is approximately 24 h, these four subcultures correspond to around 30 bacterial generations [22, 23]. To confirm successful integration of pJH223, four primer sets P1/P3.1, P1/P3.2, P2/P4.1, and P2/P4.2 (Table 3) were used to amplify the phage-bacterium junctions, attR and attL sequences [20]. For colonies with episomal pJH222, plasmid DNA extraction was carried out as previously described [24], and restriction enzyme (NEB, Beverly, MA, USA) analyses were performed for screening and on subsequent cultures of selected colonies.

Dot-blot and western blot analyses

Mid-logarithmic-phase BCG transformants were lysed in 50 mM Tris–HCl (pH 7.5), 5 mM EDTA, 0.6% sodium dodecyl sulfate, and 5 µl mL−1 protease inhibitor cocktail set IV (Calbiochem, CA, USA) using a homogenizer and glass beads (Minilyser, Bertin technologies, France). Cell lysates from various samples were adjusted to a total protein concentration of 1000 µg mL−1. For dot-blot analysis, lysates were blotted onto pretreated polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Bedford, USA). For western blotting, each lysate was mixed with an equal volume of 2X Laemmli Sample Buffer (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) supplemented with 5% (v/v) β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich), and then either heated for 10 min at 100 °C or left unheated. Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) was performed using the Amersham ECL Gel box and Amersham ECL Gel 8–16% (GE Healthcare, Freiburg, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein bands were transferred onto PVDF membranes using a Semi-Dry Transfer Cell (Bio‒Rad Laboratories Inc., CA, USA). HA-tagged Rv1733c and V5-tagged RpfE proteins were detected on dot-blots and western blots using mouse IgG1 monoclonal anti-HA antibody (clone 2–2.2.14; Thermo Scientific, Rockford, USA) and mouse anti-Pk (V5) monoclonal antibody (clone SV5-Pk1; AbD Serotec, Oxford, UK), respectively. The secondary antibody was a goat anti-mouse IgG horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibody (Santa Cruz, CA, USA). Multi-Tag-Marker (Roche, Mannheim, Germany) was used as a positive control for western blot analysis. Signal detection was performed using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Pierce, Rockford, IL), and the dots and the bands were visualised using the Typhoon 8600 gel imaging system (GE Healthcare, Piscataway, NJ, USA). Quantification of the signal was carried out using ImageQuant TL (GE Healthcare).

Immunization of mice

Female BALB/c mice (7 to 8 weeks old) were purchased from the animal facilities of the Pasteur Institute in Karaj, Iran. All animal husbandry and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with established Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-approved protocols at Shahid Beheshti University and were approved by the Research and Ethics Committee of Shahid Beheshti University, Tehran, Iran (Approval number D/920/2289). The experimental groups included sham, wildtype BCG Pasteur strain (BCGwt), rBCG::pJH222, rBCG::pJH223, rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c, rBCG::pJH223-rv1733c, rBCG:pJH222-rpfE and rBCG::pJH223-rpfE. Two additional groups received a 1:1 mixture of rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c and rBCG:pJH222-rpfE (referred to as rBCG::pJH222-Multi), and a 1:1 mixture of rBCG::pJH223-rv1733c and rBCG:pJH223-rpfE (referred to as rBCG::pJH223-Multi). Mice in each group were immunized subcutaneously at the scruff of the neck with a total of 106 colony-forming unit (CFU) of viable BCGwt or each of rBCGs or rBCG mixtures in 100 µl of PBS containing 0.05% Tween 80 (PBST). The sham group received 100 µl PBST alone. Four weeks later, mice were boosted via the same route with the same dose of BCGwt, rBCGs, or PBST (i.e,. homologous boost). Twenty weeks after the second immunization, all mice received a subcutaneous heterologous boost with 10 µg/mouse of either recombinant Rv1733c [26] or recombinant RpfE (produced using a similar methodology; Supplementary Fig. 1), each emulsified with an equal volume of incomplete Ferund’s adjuvant (IFA) (Razi Serum Institute, Iran). The rBCG::pJH222-Multi and rBCG::pJH223-Multi groups received a total of 10 µg/mouse of a 1:1 mixture of both recombinant proteins. The six months vaccination regimen was employed to assess the long-term immunogenicity of the rBCG constructs. Mice were euthanized immediately upon completion of the six-month regimen, with no delay prior to euthanasia. No animals died prior to reaching the experimental endpoint. A total of 50 mice were used and euthanized in this study (Table 4). All injections, animal monitoring and euthanasia were carried out by fully trained and qualified personnel. Mice were monitored 5 min after each injection, then monitored daily for one week post-injection, and weekly thereafter. No analgesics, anaesthetics, or special housing conditions were required or employed during the study.

Mouse splenocyte cultures

To prepare single-cell splenocyte suspensions, spleens were gently perfused with RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum and 10 U mL−1 penicillin–streptomycin (complete RPMI) (Biosera, Ringmer, UK). Red blood cells were lysed using a buffer containing 155 mM NH4Cl, 12 mM NaHCO3, and 0.1 mM EDTA (pH 7.4). Viable cells were counted using the trypan blue exclusion method (Sigma-Aldrich). Splenocyte cultures were established in 96-well tissue culture plates (Nunc, Roskilde, Denmark) at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well in 200 µl complete RPMI. Cells were stimulated with 10 µg mL−1 of purified protein derivative (PPD; Razi Serum Institute, Iran), recombinant Rv1733c, recombinant RpfE, or a 1:1 mixture of both recombinant proteins, or were left unstimulated. All cultures were prepared in duplicate. After 72 h of incubation at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, culture supernatants were harvested and stored at –20°C until further analysis.

Cytokine measurement (IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-4 ELISA assays)

The concentrations of T helper 1 (Th1) cytokines interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and tumour necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin (IL)−10, and the T helper 2 (Th2) cytokine IL-4 in the supernatants of splenocyte cultures were measured using sandwich ELISA with Ready-SET-Go ELISA kits (eBioscience, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's instructions. The detection limits for IFN-γ, TNF-α, IL-10, and IL-4 were 15, 8, 32 and 4 pg mL−1, respectively. Cytokine responses to PPD or specific antigens were considered positive when values were more than twice those of unstimulated cells. The average of two technical replicates per sample was reported.

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as group means ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and were plotted using GraphPad Prism. Independent-samples t-test with Welch’s correction (no assumption of equal variances), followed by Dunnett's T3 multiple comparisons test, was used for statistical analyses. Detailed statistical information, including the number of mice per group and P values, is provided in the figure legends. A P value of 0.05 or lower was considered statistically significant.

To construct the recombinant BCG vaccines, the rv1733c and rpfE genes were synthesized, and the sequence of respectively HA and V5 tags with codons optimized for the GC-rich mycobacterial genome, were added using PCR. The rv1733c-HA and rpfE-V5 fragments were cloned into E. coli-mycobacterium shuttle plasmids pJH222 and pJH223. E. coli transformants carrying the recombinant plasmids were selected based on kanamycin resistance, and the plasmids were sequenced to confirm in-frame insertion of the genes. A lysine auxotrophic strain of BCG was transformed with the empty pJH222 and pJH223 plasmids, as well as with the recombinant plasmids pJH222-rv1733c, pJH223-rv1733c, pJH222-rpfE, and pJH223-rpfE. Discussion

Given that BCG is still widely administered at birth in developing countries where TB is endemic [4], efforts to develop recombinant BCGs with improved efficacy remain valuable [28]. Aiming to enhance BCG’s effectiveness against latent TB and its reactivation, this study describes the construction of four rBCG strains overexpressing either the latency-associated antigen Rv1733c or the resuscitation promoting factor RpfE. It also evaluates the cytokine responses of splenocytes from BALB/c mice vaccinated with each of these rBCGs individually or in combination.

Healthy latently M. tuberculosis-infected individuals (H-LTBI), including healthy household contacts of TB patients, are considered to harbor protective immunity against Mtb infection. Studies across various populations have identified Rv1733c and RpfE as strong inducers of IFN-γ responses in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from H-LTBI. Notably, these responses to Rv1733c and RpfE were significantly higher in H-LTBI compared to those in TB patients [14, 15, 17, 29], suggesting a possible role for these immune responses in the containment of latent infection and the prevention of reactivation.

Despite the promising data on ex vivo antigenicity and the potential correlation with protection, findings on the immunogenicity and protective efficacy of Rv1733c and RpfE in animal models have been inconsistent. Vaccination of BALB/c and C57BL/6 mice with plasmid DNA encoding Rv1733c, followed by ex vivo stimulation with Rv1733c protein, resulted in very low IFN-γ and IL-2 responses. Furthermore, when splenocytes from these vaccinated mice were stimulated with synthetic, overlapping 20-mer peptides spanning the entire protein sequence of Rv1733c, no immunodominant peptide was identified [30]. Conversely, Lin and colleagues vaccinated BALB/c mice with plasmid DNA encoding Rv1733 and reported high level of IFN-γ production upon ex vivo stimulation with Rv1733c protein [10]. Regarding RpfE, the protein formulated with CpG elicited minimal reduction in lung CFU in vaccinated C57BL/6 mice following low-dose aerosol challenge with Mtb H37Rv strain [31]. In contrast, vaccination of C57BL/6 mice with RpfE protein in IFA resulted in significant protection against high-dose intravenous challenge with Mtb H37Rv, as evidenced by reduced mycobacterial loads in the lung and spleen, and extended post-infection survival [32].

These discrepancies highlight the need for further immunogenic characterization of Rv1733c and RpfE using appropriate constructs, study designs, and timelines. In this study, we expressed these late-stage antigens in recombinant BGCs, based on the rationale that BCG itself is unlikely to express them following vaccination. Although the DosR regulon of BCG shares over 97% amino acid sequence homology (85.4% identity) with that of Mtb [33], and BCG can activate DosR under aerobic, hypoxia and nitric oxide exposure [10, 12], BCG vaccination has consistently failed to induce strong immune responses to latency antigens. For instance, neonatal BCG vaccination was shown to elicit IFN-γ responses to several non-DosR antigens but not to DosR antigen HspX [34]. Similarly, BCG did not enhance responses to latency antigens in adolescents or mice, while DNA vaccines encoding these proteins did [10]. Higher IFN-γ responses to HspX in Mtb-exposed individuals compared to BCG vaccinees, and the absence of HspX-derived epitopes recognition in BCG-vaccinated mice [35], further underscore this gap. This poor response is likely due to BCG’s inability to express late-stage antigens, as it does not persist long enough post-vaccination to induce their expression [9]. Consequently, BCG fails to elicit immunity against Mtb latency antigens and provides limited protection against latent infection and its reactivation. We therefore reasoned that incorporating these antigens into BCG using recombinant approaches could broaden its antigenic repertoire, thereby enhancing immunogenicity and potentially improving vaccine efficacy.

We constructed rBCGs overexpressing Rv1733c or RpfE with high in vivo stability by utilizing lysine complementation, which promote retention of recombinant plasmids in the absence of antibiotic selection in vivo [21–24]. The BALB/c mouse strain was used for immunization, as it has been reported to recognize a broader array of peptides from several Mtb latency antigens–and with higher magnitude–compared to C57BL/6 mice [30]. We employed both homologous and heterologous prime-boost regimens [36]. Although BCG can persist in C57BL/6 mouse organs for five months post-vaccination [37], we used a four-week interval for the homologous rBCG prime-boost regimen. This approach is supported by a study in red deer, which found that shortening the interval between primary and secondary immunizations from eight to four weeks did not decrease the protective effect of BCG vaccination against M. bovis challenge [38].

We employed a six-month vaccination schedule. In a previous study involving recombinant BCG overexpressing the latency protein HspX, extending the evaluation period to 24 weeks–as opposed to six weeks–was shown to enhance the immune responses induced by the rBCG [39]. Based on this, we adopted a 24-week interval between our first rBCG vaccination and the recombinant protein boost, evaluating cytokine responses by splenocytes from vaccinated mice six months after the initiation of the regimen.

Despite the absence of reliable correlates of protection against Mtb infection [40], the Th1 cytokines IFN-γ and TNF-α are considered integral components of the immune response to TB [41]. FN-γ stimulates infected macrophages to produce reactive nitrogen intermediates, thereby restricting the growth of the intracellular Mtb [42, 43]. Although each of our rBCG strains elicited a distinct cytokine response profile, overexpression of Rv1733c or RpfE by rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c or rBCG::pJH222-rpfE led to enhanced antigen-specific IFN-γ responses compared to control groups. In contrast, expression of these antigens via the integrative plasmid pJH223 did not yield a marked improvement in either PPD- or antigen-specific IFN-γ responses over controls. Unexpectedly, the rBCG-Multi vaccines induced slightly lower antigen-specific IFN-γ responses than the single-antigen rBCGs. Given that the lysA complementation strategy confers in vivo plasmid stability for both episomal and integrative constructs [21–24], our findings suggest that single-antigen rBCGs carrying multicopy episomal plasmids serve as more potent inducers of IFN-γ responses and warrant further investigation in future studies.

While expression of Rv1733c or RpfE by rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c or rBCG::pJH222-rpfE did not enhance PPD- or antigen-specific TNF-α responses, vaccination with rBCG::pJH223-rv1733c or rBCG::pJH223-rpfE significantly improved TNF-α responses. We speculate that the moderate expression levels of these recombinant proteins, mediated by the integrative plasmid pJH223, may have favoured the induction of TNF-α. Interestingly, combining both antigens in rBCG::pJH223-Multi attenuated the enhanced TNF-α response, whereas rBCG::pJH222-Multi outperformed the single-antigen rBCGs, eliciting the highest mean PPD- and antigen-specific TNF-α responses across all vaccination groups. In contrast, BCGwt was a relatively poor inducer of TNF-α following stimulation with either of the recombinant proteins or PPD. In a non-human primate TB model, sterile granulomas showed higher proportion of TNF-producing T cells, correlating with lower bacterial load [41]. Furthermore, an increased risk of latent TB reactivation has been observed following the administration of a TNF-α neutralizing agent [44]. These highlight a critical role for TNF-α in controlling latent infection and preventing reactivation. Therefore, the elevated TNF-α responses induced by our rBCGs overexpressing RpfE and Rv1733c may translate into improved protection compared to BCG against latent TB and its reactivation.

A properly balanced immune response against TB includes not only Th1 cytokines associated with protection, but also anti-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-10, which are necessary to dampen the pathological effects of chronic inflammation [41]. Indeed, complete abrogation of IL-10 has been shown to ultimately promote TB progression [45]. In this context, rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c, rBCG::pJH223-rv1733c, and rBCG::pJH223-rpfE induced higher antigen-specific IL-10 responses compared to BCGwt and rBCGs carrying empty plasmids. Conversely, PPD-specific IL-10 responses were significantly higher in BCGwt than in our rBCGs. Interestingly, the ratios of mean IFN-γ to mean IL-10 responses were 2.98 and 3.68 for rBCG::pJH222-rv1733c, and 2.58 and 4.59 for rBCG::pJH223-rv1733c in response to Rv1733c and PPD, respectively. The corresponding ratios for rBCG::pJH222-rpfE were 3.99 and 6.0, and for rBCG::pJH223-rpfE were 2.69 and 3.54 in response to RpfE and PPD, respectively. In comparison, BCGwt showed a lower IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio of 2.37 in response to PPD. We did not extend this comparison to the antigen-specific responses of BCGwt, as it likely does not express Rv1733c or RpfE post-vaccination, leading to low IL-10 responses that could skew the ratios. Notably, high IFN-γ/IL-10 ratios have been correlated with successful resolution of TB infection, as healthy household contacts of TB patients exhibited elevated IFN-γ/IL-10 ratios compared to the household contacts who developed active disease [46]. Of particular interest, rBCG::pJH223-Multi induced the highest IFN-γ/IL-10 ratios–13.15 for PPD-specific response and 9.84 for the combined antigens Rv1733c and RpfE. Given these high ratios and the integrative plasmid-based construction of this multi-stage vaccine, rBCG::pJH223-Multi represents a promising candidate for further studies.

The role of Th2 cytokines such as IL-4 in protection against TB remains a subject of debate. A Th2 response mediated by the IL-13/IL-4 receptor-α has been implicated in TB-associated tissue pathology and reactivation [47]. Conversely, a study using IL-4 knockout mice demonstrated that IL-4 is required for protection against mycobacterial infection [48]. In line with this, two of our rBCG constructs elicited PPD- and antigen-specific IL-4 responses. Interestingly, these same rBCGs, when combined with another rBCG to form rBCG-Multi vaccines, did not induce any IL-4 responses. Whether IL-4 induced by these rBCGs contributes to protection warrants further investigation in future challenge studies.

BCGwt induced IL-4 in response to PPD, but not to either of the studied late-stage antigens. The high IL-10 and IL-4 responses to PPD, but low to absent responses to Rv1733c and RpfE, suggest inefficient expression of these antigens by BCGwt post-vaccination in mice. This highlights the significance of this study in overexpressing these antigens via rBCGs, aiming to expand the antigenic repertoire of the parental BCG vaccine.

Nonetheless, the study has some limitations. Secreted cytokine profiling was conducted in this study without identifying the cellular sources of these responses. Additional immunological analyses using flow cytometry are needed to determine whether these cytokines are produced by Th1 CD4+ T cells, which are key correlates of protection against TB [49], or by other subsets such as Th2 or Th17 cells, or, in case of IL-10, potentially by both T cells and monocytes as previously reported [50]. Furthermore, while the rBCG strains elicited enhanced immunogenicity, it remains to be determined whether this will confer improved protection in mice, particularly in models of persistent Mtb infection [30].

Conclusions

To conclude, we leveraged BCG as a platform to elicit immune responses not only against early infection antigens but also against late-stage mycobacterial antigens through overexpression of a latency antigen or a resuscitation promoting factor. The resulting single-antigen rBCGs demonstrated enhanced immunogenicity, as evidenced by elevated IFN-γ, TNF-α, and IL-10 responses to the respective latency and resuscitation antigens. Additionally, two of the rBCG constructs induced IL-4 responses. When combined to form multi-stage rBCG vaccines, the construct based on integrative plasmids exhibited strong immunogenicity characterized by exceptionally high IFN-γ/IL-10 ratio. This suggests the potential of this multi-stage rBCG to offer protection against latent TB and its reactivation, warranting further investigation.

Future directions include evaluating the protective efficacy of these rBCGs in mouse models challenged with Mtb. Moreover, it would be valuable to assess the role of elevated TNF-α and IL-4 induced by some of our rBCGs by studying protection outcomes under conditions where these cytokines are neutralized. Collectively, this study renews interest in, and paves the way for, the design of rBCGs as multi-stage TB vaccines.
