A qualitative study on pre-exposure prophylaxis uptake and retention among men who have sex with men and transgender women in Myanmar
Authors: Ni Ni Tun, Soe Yu Naing, Nyan Lynn Tun, Myo Min Min Hteik, Myat Ko Ko, Htet Htet Aung, Nyan Phone Myint, Frank Smithuis, Tom Decroo, Tinne Gils, Thijs Reyniers
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) is efficacious in reducing human immunodeficiency virus transmission among people at substantial risk. In Myanmar’s PrEP demonstration project among men who have sex with men (MSM) and transgender women (TGW), 39% (n=216) of eligible MSM/TGW initiated daily oral PrEP and 43% (n=93) of them remained on PrEP at 1 y. Here we aimed to understand the barriers and facilitators of PrEP uptake and retention among Myanmar’s MSM/TGW and healthcare providers. We conducted three focus group discussions among 29 MSM/TGW (PrEP users and non-users) and in-depth interviews among 18 MSM/TGW and 8 healthcare providers (non-PrEP and PrEP prescribers) from Yangon. Interviews were audio recorded and transcribed in Burmese. Transcripts were analysed using thematic analysis. Among 47 participating MSM/TGW, the median age was 27 y, half identified as TGW (51% [n=24]) and 49% (n=23) were MSM and 53% (n=25) were taking PrEP during the study. Among healthcare providers, the median age was 31 y, seven were male, one was female and half were PrEP providers. In our study, perceived stigma linked with homosexuality, the daily pill burden and community misconceptions of PrEP as antiretroviral therapy were barriers. Providing telemedicine services and incentives and having a supportive network and peers facilitated PrEP uptake and retention.Keywords: men who have sex with men, Myanmar, pre-exposure prophylaxis, qualitative study, transgender women

Introduction

Although new human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infections among all populations have declined globally by 40%, 80% of new HIV infections happened among key affected populations (KAPs) and their partners in 2022.1 Despite progress in treatment and prevention, HIV transmission among KAPs remains a significant public health concern. In Myanmar, there were an estimated 11 000 (range 9500–12 000) new HIV infections in 2024. HIV prevalence was concentrated at 22.3% among people who inject drugs (PWID), 8.8% among men who have sex with men (MSM), 23.5% among transgender people and 4.9% among sex workers (FSW).2,3 In Yangon, the largest city, HIV prevalence is estimated to be 26.6% among MSM and transgender women (TGW), which might be attributed to a higher rate of partner change, selling or buying sex and lower knowledge of HIV transmission and prevention.4

Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) has proven to be highly effective in reducing HIV transmission among individuals at substantial risk.5 It has been recommended by the World Health Organization (WHO) since 2015 as part of a combination prevention approach.6 PrEP has been increasingly implemented worldwide and it is estimated that about 6.7 million PrEP initiations had been carried out by the first quarter of 2024.7 However, its reach and utilization vary across countries and populations.8 The first daily oral PrEP demonstration program among MSM/TGW persons in Myanmar was introduced in 2020. Of the individuals who were offered PrEP, only 38.8% of MSM/TGW at higher risk of HIV infection were initiated on PrEP, and 43% of them remained on PrEP after a 1-y follow-up.9 A similar uptake of PrEP was seen among MSM from Rwanda at 35.4%, but the retention was higher than in Myanmar at 88.2%.10 In contrast, MSM/TGW from Thailand yielded a higher PrEP uptake of 62.8%, and a higher 1-y persistence rate of 85.9%.11 These findings indicate that PrEP uptake and retention were still low among MSM and TGW in Myanmar. However, in South Africa and Kenya, where uptake and reported retention are higher, only 11–18% of MSM/TGW who reported being PrEP adherent had protective tenofovir-diphosphate levels in dried blood spots.12,13

PrEP uptake and retention can be explained at three levels: micro (individual), meso (clinic service) and macro (society). Previous studies have shown that PrEP use can be hindered by individual factors, such as the lack of awareness of PrEP, doubts about its effectiveness, worries about side effects, low risk perception, service-related factors including pill burden, cost, patient burdens such as frequent HIV testing for prescription refills and societal factors such as stigma against PrEP in the Southeast Asia region.9,14,15 Evaluation of PrEP programs is important to enhance PrEP scale-up.16 Qualitative research is well-suited to provide MSM/TGW’s and healthcare providers’ (HPs) experiences and needs to effectively deliver PrEP throughout the country. Thus we conducted a qualitative study to explore the barriers and facilitators encountered in daily oral PrEP use by MSM/TGW and HPs to identify ways to improve PrEP programs in Myanmar, potentially influencing practices across Southeast Asia.

Methods

Study overview, designs and settings

This qualitative study included focus group discussions (FGDs) and in-depth interviews (IDIs) among MSM, TGW and HPs from Yangon, Myanmar, conducted between January and June 2022. We used FGDs to explore a wide range of views and opinions through discussion of the acceptability of PrEP among MSM/TGW and IDIs to understand in-depth individual views and perceptions towards PrEP. This study was conducted in parallel with a PrEP demonstration program among MSM/TGW and HPs in Yangon with the Medical Action Myanmar (MAM) organization. MAM, a non-governmental medical organization, has provided HIV prevention and treatment services in Myanmar since 2009. MAM started the PrEP program for the MSM/TGW population in Hlaingthaya township, one of Yangon’s largest suburban slum areas, in 2020.9

Sampling

The MAM prevention team purposively selected the FGD participants based on their PrEP intake status (user/non-user). Information-rich events are particular behaviours or situations of interest that a participant may have experienced (e.g. not adhering well to daily PrEP). IDI participants were selected by trained MAM peer educators (PEs) based on information-rich events, including good adherence to PrEP and poor adherence to PrEP, discontinuation of daily PrEP use and changes in sexual risk behaviours, to capture both barriers and facilitators. At least two IDI participants were included for each criterion. In addition, for IDIs with HPs, we purposively included HPs who were either prescribing PrEP or not, to get a broad perspective on barriers and facilitators of prescribing PrEP. These HPs were recruited through MAM clinic networks.

Study procedure and data collection

TR and NNT trained all the researchers (HHA and NPM) who were involved in FGDs and IDIs. Topic guides for FGDs and IDIs were developed for this study by a qualitative research team and piloted and adapted before use. We included open-ended questions in the topic guides for FGDs to foster discussions on barriers and facilitators of PrEP uptake and retention. We used case vignettes (e.g. a case of a fictional character named ‘Mg Mg’, not taking PrEP despite being at risk of HIV acquisition, such as condomless sex with more than one partner), considering the sensitivity of the subject. Interview guides for MSM/TGW and HPs were semi-structured to allow for consistency between interviews and flexibility for a more natural flow of discussion. They were designed to explore the barriers and facilitators of taking or prescribing PrEP. The topic guides for FGDs and IDIs were tested with PE and MAM doctors and revised accordingly. The final topic guides for MSM/TG and HPs are in the supplementary section. Two researchers and two PE conducted three FGDs of 8–10 participants in each group in the meeting room of the MAM clinic; informed consent was received from each participant before FGDs. IDIs were conducted by two researchers in private rooms in MAM clinics, MAM offices and tea shops in Yangon and written consent was received. Each FGD lasted 60–90 min, while each IDI took 15–45 min. Participants received compensation for transportation and lunch expenses equivalent to US$5 each.

All FGDs and IDIs were conducted in Burmese and audio recorded. Transcripts were not translated to English. as coding and analysis was conducted by Burmese researchers (NT and SYN) and reviewed by other Burmese-speaking research team members (NLT, MMMH, MKK, HHA and NPM). Selected quotes were translated from Burmese into English by NT and SYN and subsequently reviewed by the entire research team.

Data analysis

The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim in Burmese and the transcripts were checked for consistency and accuracy at the MAM office by NT, NLT, HHA and NPM. The transcripts were pseudonymized. To be immersed in the collected data, NT, HHA, NPM and SYN first reread the transcripts and listened again to the audio recordings. The initial codes were generated using a deductive coding framework (Figure 1), which was later coded inductively in response to emerging findings. After generating the first code, the researchers (NT and SYN) kept reading the data until a decision was made on whether the initial code could still be applied or a new code was needed to capture that piece of data. This process was repeated throughout the entire dataset.17 All relevant codes were sorted into themes relating to PrEP use and factors that hinder or support its uptake and retention were identified. Subsequently we identified the patterns among individual factors, factors related to service provision and broader societal factors and adopted a framework to help explain the barriers and facilitators found regarding PrEP uptake and retention on three levels, i.e. individual, service and societal (Figure 1). The local and international research teams (NT, SYN, NLT, MMMH, MKK, HHA, NPM, FS, TD, TG and TR) verified the final analysis and results. All data were analysed using NVivo version 11 (Lumivero, Denver, CO, USA).

Results

Sample characteristics

We invited 32 MSM/TGW to participate in the FGDs. Three (9.4%) refused to join because they were traveling out of town. For IDIs, we invited 20 MSM/TGW. Two (10%) refused because they were too busy at work. A total of 47 MSM/TGW participated in both FGDs and IDIs. Among them, 49% (n=23) of participants were MSM and 51% (n=24) identified as TGW. The median age was 27 y (interquartile range [IQR] 20–30). Just over half of the participants (53% [n=25]) were taking PrEP, whereas the rest (47% [n=22]) did not take PrEP at the time of the study. The characteristics of the 47 MSM and TGW who participated in FGDs and IDIs are described in Table 1. All eight invited HPs participated in the study; four (50%) were PrEP providers (Table 2). Adequate knowledge of PrEP was reported by 81% (n=38) of MSM and TGW compared with 63% (n=5) of HPs.

The Framework

At the individual level

Barrier

Low perceived risk. Some TGW from the interviews perceived themselves as having a low risk of getting HIV, although they had a high risk of contracting HIV, and they refused to take PrEP. It was expressed by one participant as:

I don’t want to take PrEP because I don’t think I will get HIV, though I missed condoms sometimes.

— MSM/TGW IDIe-6

Facilitators

Increased confidence in having sex.

Many interviewees agreed that PrEP use could protect them from HIV and make them feel safe and secure, thus improving their confidence in having sex. This facilitates them for PrEP uptake and retention. They also understood that although PrEP prevents them from getting HIV, it does not prevent them from other sexually transmitted diseases. One participant mentioned:

After initiating PrEP, I feel satisfied, confident, and more pleasurable in having sex.

— MSM/TGW IDI-2

At the service provision level

Barriers

Daily pill burden.

Participants from FGDs and IDIs mentioned the burden of taking pills daily as a barrier to PrEP uptake or retention. Some participants from FGDs 1 and 2 mentioned that ‘to remember taking daily pills made me stressed and thus I don’t want to take it’. One interviewee explained about his experience of refraining from taking PrEP because of the constraints of daily intake:

I feel lazy to take oral medicine daily since my childhood…that’s why I do not take PrEP.

— MSM/TGW IDI-15

One HP mentioned that

I have some clients who stopped PrEP due to the pill burden since they could no longer stand the stress of taking daily pills.

— HP IDI-6

Limited PrEP options.

Some participants highlighted the importance of availability of alternative PrEP options to improve PrEP uptake and retention. Some study participants from FGDs 1 and 2 highlighted that

I want to take a pill that will protect us for a longer period of time. With this current daily pills regimen, I have to remember to take pill every day and it made me stressed.

— FGD-2, 5, 9, 10, 13, 15

One HP mentioned that

…the PrEP uptake and retention could be improved if there are more PrEP options such as long-acting injectable PrEP.

— HP IDI-6

Burden of follow-up visit and transport costs.

Some participants had difficulties coming back for three monthly visits for HIV retesting and pill refills for reasons such as moving out of town or not being able to pay transport fees and therefore it was difficult for them to continue on PrEP. However, other interviewed participants did not mention it. One HP reflected:

I encountered some clients who stopped taking PrEP for either moving out of town or could not afford transport costs.

— HP IDI-6

Facilitators

Telemedicine service and incentives for visits (e.g. transportation fees).

The HP interviewees explained that many PrEP clients were facing difficulties in coming back for PrEP follow-up visits. They shared their experiences of overcoming such barriers by providing remote telemedicine services with flexible follow-up visits and cash support for travel, thus improving the PrEP retention of their clients. For example, one HP said:

[For] some clients who moved out of Yangon and could not come back for 3-months physical visits, I organized to do HIV self-testing and confirmed the result with me via a video through the Viber-based platform and send PrEP pills with mail delivery to be able to continue PrEP.

— HP IDI-7

Change the packaging of PrEP bottles.

Participants highlighted the similarity of packaging between antiretroviral therapy (ART) and PrEP bottles and therefore people misunderstood and stigmatized them as being HIV positive and it was suggested to change the packaging of PrEP bottles to improve PrEP uptake and retention. This may be a potential intervention to facilitate PrEP uptake or retention in the future.

In the general community, PrEP medicine is considered as ART medicine. Because of this perception, I suggest separating packaging to differentiate between ART and PrEP medicine.

— FGD-10

At the society level

Barrier

The perceived association between PrEP use and sexual identity (MSM/TGW).

Participants mentioned that PrEP is well known as a pill for MSM/TGW in Myanmar. They considered PrEP to be associated with being identified as MSM or TGW. This was considered difficult for those who did not want to disclose their sexuality status to family or friends, representing an barrier to uptake or retention. For example, one FGD participant explained that:

…it is difficult to take PrEP in front of others, especially for those who do not disclose themselves as MSM/TGW in their family or community.

— FGD-2

Moreover, several participants raised concerns about privacy and social acceptability acting as barriers to PrEP uptake. One participant described this barrier as:

Those who have not come out yet hesitated to take PrEP since PrEP might reveal their sexual identity. This is very bad.

— FGD-9

Misunderstanding of PrEP as ART among the general population.

In general, almost all participants highlighted that they felt discriminated against by the general population, as PrEP was associated with ART. Thus the general population’s misunderstanding of PrEP or poor knowledge of PrEP is a significant barrier to its uptake and/or retention. One participant stated:

The majority thinks that people are taking (PrEP) because they have HIV. The reason I do not take PrEP currently is because of my home. As I said, the general community cannot get an education (about PrEP). Since they lack knowledge and understanding, if I am holding the (PrEP) bottle, they will think that I take ART because I have HIV.

— FGD-2

Facilitators

Strong peer and family support.

The interviewees who have friends taking PrEP (peer support) or have family/friends who are aware of HIV infection and understand PrEP explained that, according to them, taking PrEP is easier. It reflects the importance of having supportive friends or families who are aware of HIV and PrEP to improve PrEP uptake and/or retention. One interviewee mentioned:

I started to take PrEP since I was told about PrEP by one of the TGW living in my street. Moreover, both of my parents knew and accepted me as being LGBT (lesbian, gay, bisexual, and transgender) and taking PrEP, and therefore I have no problem taking PrEP at my home.

— MSM/TGW IDI-13

Discussion

We explored the barriers and facilitators of MSM/TGW and HPs towards daily oral PrEP in Myanmar. Being stigmatized or stereotyped as homosexual, having limited PrEP options and the public’s misconception of PrEP as ART were found to be important barriers according to participants. Providing incentives, telemedicine services and having supportive friends, families and peers facilitated PrEP uptake and retention.

Study participants considered having a low perceived risk of HIV as a barrier to PrEP uptake at an individual level, especially among TGW. A similar finding was also seen among TGW in Thailand and the eastern and southern USA.15,19 The current strategy of demand creation is based on risk perception. This message of ‘PrEP is for those who think themselves at high risk’ might not effectively reach the population needing PrEP due to their low perceived risk. Moreover, risk perceptions of HIV are complex, subjective and influenced by community beliefs and attitudes. Therefore, reframing the conceptualization of HIV risk perception is crucial for effective implementation of PrEP.20

On the other hand, many participants from our study agreed that PrEP improved sexual pleasure and confidence in having sex. A systematic review across eight countries, including the USA, Canada, Eswatini, France, Uganda, England, Australia and Belgium, revealed significant themes regarding PrEP as increasing emotional intimacy, closeness, connectedness and improved sexual pleasure and argued that positive sexual pleasure motivations as part of the demand creation message along with destigmatizing sexual pleasure and sexual activities of MSM.21

The study participants identified the burden of taking daily oral pills and the lack of alternative PrEP options as barriers to the uptake and retention of PrEP. The challenge of daily pill burden has also been reported in other studies.22–24 The efficacy of PrEP largely depends on daily adherence of taking pills.25 Long-acting injectable cabotegravir might be a new game-changing option for HIV prevention in people who are having difficulty with daily oral PrEP; however, more intensive interaction with systems of healthcare delivery, greater expense, complexity of implementation and a strong commitment from all stakeholders are needed to increase access.26,27

All PrEP-providing HP participants from our study shared their experience of improving PrEP retention by providing telemedicine services, including online consultations, HIV self-testing, PrEP home delivery and covering transport costs. Other studies reported regular clinic follow-up visits and transport costs as structural-level barriers to PrEP implementation.23,28 Several successful interventions have been documented and could be integrated into the health system in Myanmar. For instance, in Brazil, PrEP users accessing a telehealth application with three asynchronous consultations complemented with an annual in-person visit had a lower risk of discontinuing PrEP compared with those who opted out of telehealth support.29 Providing a differentiated and simplified PrEP delivery approach, including person-centred care and adapting services to the needs and preferences of individuals interested in PrEP, is important and was recommended by the WHO in 2022.6 Therefore, the future of successful PrEP will depend on differentiated delivery systems such as integration of PrEP within pharmacies and primary care clinics, family planning and needle-and-syringe programs and the availability of long-acting formulations.28 A scoping review concluded that the successful integration of PrEP services for MSM requires understanding both formal and informal routes of PrEP use, fostering a positive therapeutic alliance between users and providers and providing culturally and ethnically sensitive models tailored to different risk groups within MSM communities.30

The association of PrEP and sexual identity (MSM/TGW) emerged as a significant barrier to PrEP uptake or retention among our study participants. Similar findings were seen in other studies conducted among MSM in Africa and China.22,31,32 These findings suggest that this association of PrEP with disclosure of sexual identity needs to be removed to improve initiation and retention of PrEP in these populations. Social marketing needs to redress PrEP-related stigma through health promotion campaigns with a message reframing PrEP as a positive and responsible option to help people remain HIV negative.33 Moreover, PrEP should also be framed as a tool for anyone concerned about HIV rather than exclusively targeting MSM to reduce stigma and promote uptake.33

Our participants expressed concern about potential discrimination from the general public, who may confuse PrEP with ART. They suggested promoting knowledge and awareness targeting the general public and disseminating PrEP information more broadly. This concern of misconception of PrEP as ART was earlier reported as a barrier in a systematic review of the experiences of healthcare workers in low- and middle-income countries and experiences among TGW from Thailand.15,23 A multicountry study among MSM in West Africa suggested wider dissemination of information to the non-MSM population to improve PrEP uptake.31 Study participants highlighted the similarity of packaging between ART and PrEP bottles, expressing concerns about being mistaken for HIV-positive individuals. They suggested changing the packaging of PrEP bottles to improve uptake and retention. It was also mentioned by key stakeholders from Zimbabwe22 and by MSM in West Africa.31 Based on these findings, a simple change in packaging could significantly reduce the stigma and improve PrEP use. More importantly, health information about the differences between the use of PrEP as prevention and ART as treatment for people with HIV should be strengthened at the facility and community levels.

The supportive social relationships among family or friends who understand PrEP or are taking PrEP were pointed out as a strong facilitator for PrEP uptake and retention by some of our study participants. Similar findings have been reported in other studies.23,34 Community engagement strategies involving community leaders as role models, involving peer educators in PrEP recruitment and follow-up and organizing community-led dissemination events have been proven effective and are recommended for promoting PrEP.35 Moreover, since 2021, with COVID-19 and the coup, Myanmar’s healthcare services have been severely impacted by increasing conflicts, security issues and economic stress, and overall access to health services remains constrained and fragmented. This ongoing political instability also had an important impact on our PrEP programs. Therefore, going forward, more robust community engagement with local and civil society organizations is needed to support the communities’ scaling up of PrEP in Myanmar.

Our findings cannot be generalized to the whole Myanmar population, as all FGDs and interviews were mainly conducted within MAM offices and MAM HIV clinics and participants were recruited through MAM peer educators. This recruitment approach may have resulted in the exclusion of individuals who were afraid of being associated with homosexuality or HIV. Another limitation of this study is that the limited views expressed by non-prescribers may reflect their unfamiliarity with PrEP, which had been available in the country for just over 1 y at the time of the study, potentially influencing the breadth of perspectives captured.

Conclusions

Our study identified barriers to PrEP uptake and retention in Yangon, Myanmar, including perceived stigma linked with homosexuality, confusion of PrEP as ART among the general population and daily pill burden. Participants also highlighted the importance of supportive social networks, telemedicine services and incentives for travel costs in facilitating PrEP uptake and retention. Reframing public PrEP promotion messages, providing differentiated delivery systems and the availability of suitable long-acting formulations might be needed to improve PrEP uptake and retention in the region.
