ACT001 alleviates MASLD through gut microbiota–bile acid–FXR axis in mice
Authors: Bin Niu, Jing Liu, Hui Zhou, Weike Chu, Xue Wu, Yibing Zhou, Ze Chen, Yonggang Liu, Yuqiang Mi, Ping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectiveACT001 is a novel sesquiterpene lactone derivative with noteworthy antineoplastic and anti-inflammatory properties. This study aimed to investigate the efficacy of ACT001 against metabolic dysfunction associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) pathogenesis in two established murine models, and to elucidate its regulatory role in the gut microbiota–bile acid–FXR axis.Materials and methodsA high-fat diet (HFD) and a methionine–choline-deficient (MCD) diet induced two MASLD models in C57BL/6J mice. Histological analysis was performed, biochemical indices were measured, and the expression of tight junctions and farnesoid X receptor (FXR)-related pathways was detected. Gut microbiota was assessed by 16S rDNA sequencing, while bile acids (BAs) in fecal samples were analyzed using UPLC/MS–MS.ResultsACT001 decreased liver injury, alleviated hepatic lipid accumulation, and restored intestinal barrier integrity. Furthermore, 16S rDNA illustrated that ACT001 could partially rebalance intestinal dysbacteriosis and upregulate the relative abundance of various bacteria, especially Clostridium and Erysipelotrichaceae_Clostridium. Moreover, ACT001 facilitated the generation of uncombined BAs and accumulated specific BAs within the ileum, such as 23-Nor-deoxycholic acid (23-DCA) and lithocholic acid-3-sulfate (LCA-3S), which had a downregulation effect on the expression of the enteral FXR–fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) pathway.ConclusionACT001 exerted a multifactorial therapeutic effect on MASLD, potentially linked to the regulation of the gut microbiota–BAs–FXR axis. Thus, it has the potential to be an effective treatment for MASLD.Keywords: ACT001, bile acid, gut microbiota, farnesoid X receptor, MASLD

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), also known as nonalcoholic or metabolic-associated fatty liver disease (NAFLD or MAFLD), requires the presence of hepatic steatosis in ≥5% of hepatocytes, concurrent with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor—including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), or dyslipidemia [1,2]. MASLD has posed a huge burden on patients and the medical system. One-quarter of people suffer from MASLD globally, and the morbidity rate of NASH is estimated to grow by 56% during the next decade [3]. Currently, MASLD is the quickest increasing cause of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and the most dominant reason for liver transplantation in recent decades [4,5].

There is no duly authorized pharmacological choice for MASLD [6]. Many agents that enter phase 2 or phase 3 clinical trials currently have side effects that would bring about drug safety problems, and no clinical trial has more than 50% of patients reaching the main treatment endpoint to this day [1,7]. Lifestyle modifications focused on healthy dietary strategies and physical training remain the sole effective intervention for MASLD [8]. However, this recommendation has always been unsuccessful owing to its poor acceptability and sustainability. Consequently, it is imperative to discover safe and effective medications for lowering lipid levels and protecting the liver.

ACT001 (Figure 1) is a novel anti-inflammatory and anticancer agent that originates from chemical modification of parthenolide (PTL) [9]. ACT001 has been demonstrated to exert multiple therapeutic effects in oncological and inflammatory disorders [10]. It has been approved as an orphan drug for the treatment of human cerebral glioma by the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and several clinical trials have been conducted to evaluate its efficacy (ACTRN12616000228482, Australia New Zealand; Clinical Trial Registration: ChiCTR-OIC-17013604, China Clinical Trial Register) [11]. A phase II clinical study that explored the effect of ACT001 on progressive fibrosis interstitial lung disease (ACTRN12619001643167) has also been approved in Australia. PLT, the prototype drug of ACT001, is a sesquiterpene lactone compound mainly extracted from wild chamomile, which has a certain therapeutic effect on various cancers and inflammatory illnesses. However, its practical application is greatly limited owing to its poor stability and oral bioavailability [12–14]. Based on PLT, guaiacol sesquiterpene lactone (MCL) is transformed by chemical modification and is more stable both in vivo and in vitro [15,16]. ACT001 is a drug synthesized by MCL through a Michael addition reaction, namely the Dimethylamino Michael adduct of michelactone (DMAMCL), which can slowly and continuously release MCL in plasma [17]. Therefore, ACT001 has longer drug potency and more stable pharmacokinetic properties, which makes ACT001 have a greater potential in clinical applications. Polyenyl phosphatidylcholine (PPC), a harmless mixture rich in unsaturated fatty acid groups derived from soybeans, can be used as an adjuvant therapy for MASLD treatment [18]. Hence, we used PPC as a reference pharmaceutical agent for the evaluation of ACT001.

Molecular formula and synthesis pathway of ACT001 (School of pharmacy, Nankai University).

Based on the above, our lab conducted experiments to investigate the effect of ACT001 on MASLD, which revealed that ACT001 has the ability to improve hepatic steatosis and inflammation in C57BL/6J mice, and no obvious toxicity has been observed [18]. It was implied that researching how ACT001 treats MASLD has broad application prospects and practical significance; however, the underlying mechanisms have not been further explored.

At present, correlations between the development of MASLD and alterations in intestinal microbiota have been widely studied. Several approaches targeting the gut microbiota have been applied to improve MASLD, including probiotics, prebiotics, synbiotics, and even fecal microbiome transplantation (FMT) [19]. Numerous reasonable mechanisms have been proposed to explain how gut microbiota influence MASLD, such as alterations in bile acids (BAs), short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), ethanol metabolism, heightened intestinal permeability, and inflammatory responses [20,21]. BAs participate in the regulation of lipid metabolism by influencing cholesterol catabolism, which facilitates digestion and absorption of dietary lipids [22]. It can prevent or treat MASLD through activation or inhibition of the farnesoid X receptor (FXR). Emerging evidence suggests that inhibiting ileal FXR–fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF15) could have beneficial effects on obesity [23]. Conversely, activating hepatic FXR upregulates the expression level of small heterodimer partner (SHP), which improves MASLD by inhibiting cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1) [24]. Alterations in BAs metabolism homeostasis impair bidirectional effects between the intestinal microbiota and BAs, contributing to metabolic dysregulation, eventually leading to the physiopathology of MASLD [25].

Accordingly, we used two different MASLD mouse models induced by a high-fat diet (HFD) and a methionine–choline-deficient diet (MCD). To evaluate the treatment of MASLD mice with ACT001, we used 16S rDNA sequencing to investigate changes in the gut microbiota. Furthermore, the metabolic regulation mechanism of ACT001 on MASLD was analyzed using targeted metabolomic analysis to explore the mechanistic links of such effects in relation to the gut microbiota–BAs–FXR axis.

Materials and methods

Reagents materials and chemicals

ACT001 (Lot: C10668-11) was generously supplied and authenticated by Prof. Yue Chen from Shangde Pharmaceutical Technology Co., LTD (Tianjin, China). Polyenyl phosphatidylcholine PPC (lot: ABJD465B) was obtained from Sanofi Pharmaceutical Co., LTD (Beijing, China).

Normal diet (ND) (10% kcal from fat, 70% from carbohydrate, and 20% from protein), HFD (60% kcal from fat, 20% from carbohydrate, and 20% from protein), methionine–choline supplement diet (MCS), and MCD were purchased from Xiaoshu Youtai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China, No: SCXK (Jing) 2018-0006). The specific feed components are listed in Table S1.

Animal models and experiment design

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Nankai University (Approval No. 2021-SYDWLL-000003). The MCD modeling referred to our team’s prior publications [18].

Fifty-six specific-pathogen-free (SPF)-grade C57BL/6J female mice (8 weeks old, 20 ± 2 g) were purchased from Huafukang Biotechnology Co. Ltd (Beijing, China, No: SCXK (Jing) 2019-0008). They were housed in the Animal Center of Radiological Medicine, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences, where was maintained in a stable environment (22 ± 2 °C and a 12-hour light/dark cycle) with an unrestricted access to water and food.

Our experiment consisted of two models: the HFD and MCD models. Acclimatization feeding was implemented for 1 week prior to the start of the experiment (Figure 2). The drug was dissolved in saline solution for gavage and the dose was determined based on the body mass of the animal.

The workflow of experiments.

In the HFD model, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 7): the ND group was fed a basal diet and the other three groups were fed a high-fat diet for 20 weeks. The ND group was administered saline, while the other three groups were administered saline (HFD), 200 mg/kg/d ACT001 (HFD-ACT001), and 150 mg/kg/d PPC (HFD-PPC). At the end of the 21st week, administration was stopped in all the groups.

In the MCD model, the mice were randomly assigned into four groups (n = 7): the MCS group was fed a choline-sufficient l-amino acid diet and the other three groups were fed a choline-deficient l-amino acid diet for 6 weeks. The MCS group was administered saline, and the other three groups were administered saline (MCD), 200 mg/kg/day ACT001 (MCD-ACT001), and 150 mg/kg/day PPC (MCD-PPC). At the end of the 7th week, administration was stopped in all the groups.

Body weights were recorded weekly until the end of the experiment. Mice were executed quickly by cervical dislocation after 12 h of fasting on the last day. Blood samples were collected from the eye veins, centrifuged at 3000g for 15 min at 4 °C, and serum was retained. The liver, ileum, and cecum feces were collected, and some liver and ileum tissues were sectioned and fixed in freshly prepared 10% formalin for histological analysis. The rest of these specimens were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. In addition, liver weight was measured, and the hepatic index was calculated by dividing liver weight by body weight. (hepatic index = liver weight/body weight).

Biochemical analysis in serum and liver

Hepatic levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) were measured using commercially available biochemical kits.

Hepatic and ileal histological staining

Liver and ileum tissues were fixed in 10% formalin, dehydrated using a gradient of ethanol, vitrified with xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (5 μm) were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson staining. Under a microscope (LEICA DM2000, Germany), liver histologists blinded to the experimental assignment were analyzed and photographed.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

RNA Extraction Solution (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used to extract total RNA from liver and ileum tissues. A reverse transcription kit (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) was used to reverse transcribe the total RNA to cDNA. The reaction system was configured according to the manufacturer’s instructions and PCR amplification was performed. Relative expression was measured using the 2-ΔΔCt method with GAPDH as a control. The primer sequences of the genes itemize in Table S2.

Ileal immunohistochemistry

After deparaffinizing the ileum with xylene and gradient ethanol, antigen repair using 0.01 M sodium citrate–hydrochloric acid buffer solutions. Serum blocking with antibodies and biotin-labeled secondary antibodies was performed serially, and positive staining was detected by adding chromogenic agents. Hematoxylin was used to preserve all the sections. A fully automated digital scanning system (PRECICE 500, China) was used to scan the sections and brown staining was considered positive for antibodies.

16S rDNA sequencing and metabolomic analysis

Mouse feces were preserved at −80 °C and thawed in an ice bath to decrease specimen degradation. The analysis of intestinal microbiota was performed by Personalbio Bio-technology Co., Ltd.(Shanghai, China). The profile of BAs in feces was measured by MetWare (Wuhan, China) Co., Ltd., based on the AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC–MS/MS platform.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0, and GraphPad Prism 8.0, were used for all statistical analyses and graph processing. The measured data were first tested for normality. Normal distribution data are represented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and non-normal distribution data are represented as median ± interquartile range. Univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare multiple groups, and pairwise post hoc testing was conducted using Student’s t test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Therapeutic effect of ACT001 on MASLD mice

In the HFD-induced model, the weight of mice in the normal diet (ND) group slowly increased, whereas a dramatic increase was observed in the HFD, HFD-ACT001, and HFD-PPC groups (Figure 3A). At the end of the experiment, mean body weights in HFD-ACT001 and HFD-PPC groups were numerically lower than in the HFD group, though without statistical significance (p > 0.05) (Figure 3B). The liver weight of the mice was significantly higher in the HFD group than in the ND group (p < 0.05). HFD-ACT001 and HFD-PPC groups showed lower mean liver weights compared to HFD, with PPC achieving statistical significance (Figure 3C). No significant difference was detected between HFD and HFD-ACT001 groups (p > 0.05, Figure 3D).

Therapeutic effect of ACT001 on mice in HFD-induced MASLD model: (A, B) changes in body weight, (C) liver weight, (D) hepatic index, (E) liver photograph, (F) hepatic histological patterns, (G) hepatic level of ALT, (H) hepatic level of AST, (I) hepatic level of TC, (J) hepatic level of TG. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

Compared with the ND group, liver content in the HFD group appeared more steatotic, as evidenced macroscopically by a yellow color, with a pinguid surface and blunt edges. Notably, the livers from mice fed HFD supplemented with ACT001 or PPC were not significantly different in volume compared to the ND group, with a reddish-brown color (Figure 3E). In the HFD-induced model, lipid deposition and ballooning degeneration were observed, and the hepatocytes were obviously swollen and round. Inflammatory cell infiltration was also observed in the HFD group, suggesting that the MASLD model was successfully established. Compared with the HFD group, the HFD-ACT001 group mice showed a reduction in the area of liver damage under the microscope, lipid deposition, and liver cell swelling. The HFD-PPC group showed a reduction in visible fat globules in the liver under a microscope, but the improvement in liver damage was inferior to that in the HFD-ACT001 group (Figure 3F). The histological images of 40X of the HFD-induced MASLD mouse model can be found in Figure S1.

The levels of hepatic aspartate transaminase (AST), triglyceride (TG), and total cholesterol (TC) in HFD mice were higher than those in ND mice with statistically significant (p < 0.05). The differences in the AST and TG levels improved by ACT001 and PPC were statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 3G,H,I,J).

In the MCD-induced model, mouse weight in the methionine–choline supplement diet (MCS) group remained stable, while mouse weight in the MCD, MCD-ACT001, and MCD-PPC groups exhibited progressive weight loss (Figure 4A). At the end of the experiment, mouse weight in the MCD group observably decreased compared to the MCS group (p < 0.05). Mean weights in MCD-ACT001 and MCD-PPC groups were comparable to the MCD group (p > 0.05, Figure 4B). The liver weight of the mice was significantly higher in the MCD group than in the MCS group (p < 0.05), yet no significant alteration was observed after ACT001 or PPC treatment (p > 0.05) (Figure 4C). However, for the liver index, no statistically significant differences were observed between any groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 4D).

Therapeutic effect of ACT001 on mice in MCD-induced MASLD model: (A, B) changes in body weight, (C) liver weight, (D) hepatic index, (E) liver photograph, (F) hepatic histological patterns, (G) hepatic level of ALT, (H) hepatic level of AST, (I) hepatic level of TC, (J) hepatic level of TG. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

The mouse livers in the MCS group were dull red with glabrous surfaces and sharp edges, while mice in the MCD group had smaller livers, which had higher liver fat content and harder texture. After ACT001 or PCC administration, the color gradually approached crimson with a soft texture (Figure 4E). The pathological sections revealed that the liver tissues in the MCD mice displayed larger vesicular steatosis, ballooning degeneration, and inflammation than those in the MCS mice. Likewise, ACT001 and PPC improved hepatic cell status, reduced lipid droplet size, and alleviated inflammatory infiltration (Figure 4F). The histological images of 40X of the MCD-induced MASLD mouse model can be found in Figure S2.

The levels of hepatic ALT and AST in MCD mice were markedly higher than those in ND mice (p < 0.05), with non-significant increases in TC/TG (p > 0.05). Neither ACT001 nor PPC significantly altered any hepatic marker versus MCD controls (all p > 0.05) (Figure 4G–J).

These results indicate that ACT001’s efficacy was restricted to specific metabolic dysregulations in HFD models, with no detectable activity in MCD-induced injury.

Effect of ACT001on intestine and intestinal epithelial barrier on MASLD mice

In the HFD-induced model, the ileum mucosa of the ND group mice was intact, and no significant mucosal structural alterations were observed. The ileum mucosa of the HFD group was disordered, with some villi being broken and damaged. After drug therapy, the ileum of HFD-fed mice improved, with neatly arranged intestinal villi (Figure 5A). Accompanied by intestinal mucosal damage, intestinal IL-6 level was numerically higher in HFD mice than in ND mice without statistically significant (p > 0.05). Both ACT001 and PPC tended to reduced intestinal IL-6 levels after administration, although there was no statistically significant difference between the groups (p > 0.05). TNF-α levels were not significantly different between the ND group and HFD group (p > 0.05), but the HFD-ACT001 and HFD-PPC groups showed a further decrease with statistically significant (p < 0.05) (Figure 5B,C). A decrease in intestinal ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 expression was observed in mice fed a long-term HFD. The expression of intestinal tight junction proteins was restored after ACT001 therapy, but the improvement in intestinal permeability was not significant after PPC intervention (Figure 5D).

Effect of ACT001 on intestine of mice in HFD-induced MASLD model: (A) ileal histological patterns, (B) level of IL-6, (C) level of TNF-α, (D) intestinal barrier proteins. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

In the MCD-induced model, the mucosal structure of the MCD group mice was severely damaged, accompanied by epithelial necrosis, shedding, gap widening, a slight inflammatory infiltration, and part of the epithelial layer was separated from the lamina propria. These symptoms were repaired to a certain extent after ACT001 or PPC intervention (Figure 6A). The levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in the intestine of MCD mice were dramatically increased (p < 0.05), whereas the intervention of ACT001 or PPC contributed to a significant decrease in intestinal inflammatory factor levels (p < 0.05) (Figure 6B,C). The expression of ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1 in the ileum was significantly decreased compared to that in the MCS group, and the function of intestinal barrier-related proteins was partially restored in both the MCD-ACT001 and MCD-PPC groups (Figure 6D).

Effect of ACT001 on intestine of mice in MCD-induced MASLD model: (A) ileal histological patterns, (B) level of IL-6, (C) level of TNF-α, (D) intestinal barrier proteins. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

Pathological changes in tight junction proteins (ZO-1, Occludin, Claudin-1) were observed alongside numerical reductions in select inflammatory markers following ACT001 administration. However, no functional evidence confirms actual barrier restoration or anti-inflammatory efficacy.

Effect of ACT001 on the diversity and composition of intestinal microorganisms of MASLD mice

In the HFD-induced model, the α-diversity indices showed that no significant difference was presented in the Shannon index and Simpson index between the ND group and HFD group, or between the HFD group and the HFD-ACT001 group (p > 0.05) (Figure 7A). In accordance with the results of the α-diversity index, the dilution curve and rank abundance curve showed that the sample curves of each group eventually tended to flatten (Figure 7B,C).

Effect of ACT001 on the gut microbiome of mice in HFD-induced MASLD model: (A) α-diversity, (B) abundance curve of rank, (C) dilution curve, (D) PCoA plot, (E) NMDS plot, (F) clustering analysis, (G) taxonomic tree plot, (H-I) LEfSe: cladogram and LDA of dominant microorganisms, (J) relative abundances of differential bacteria phylum, (K) relative abundances of differential bacteria genus. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

Principal component analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) were implemented using Bray–Curtis distances, which showed that the sample points of every group could be completely separated from each other, and the sample points of the ACT001 intervention group were closer to those in the control group (Figure 7D,E). Meanwhile, clustering analysis revealed that the microbiota community of ACT001 groups resembled that of the PPC groups, and the distance from the control group to the intervention group was smaller than the distance from the normal group to the HFD group (Figure 7F).

The differential abundance of bacterial communities among groups is shown (Figure 7G), and we used KRONA software to display the species interpretation results visually, so as to display the species annotation results between groups online (https://genescloud.cn/data/conf/user/tjlplxg/MD202209162340E19R/results_origin/2_composition/2.5_krona/krona.html?dataset=0&node=0&collapse=true&color=false&depth=7&font=11&key=true).

ACT001 treatment modulated bacterial communities at multiple levels, including the phylum, class, order, family, and genus. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) and effective size (LEfSe) analysis were performed to investigate the modulation of the bacterial community, and the levels of Verrucomicrobia (LDA > 4) were significantly enriched by ACT001 treatment at the phylum level. Verrucomicrobiae, Verrucomicrobiales, Verrucomicrobiaceae and Akkermansia were the most influential microorganisms in the ACT001 group at the class, order, family, and genus levels (Figure 7H,I). As noted above, a large number of bacteria were differentially expressed between the HFD group and ND/ACT001 group. Specifically, ACT001 reversed the TM7 due to high-fat feeding at the phylum level (Figure 7J). In addition, the levels of Clostridium and Erysipelotrichaceae_Clostridium were markedly lower in HFD-fed mice than in ND mice, whereas the changes in Sutterella and Rikenella showed completely opposite trends. This proves that ACT001 can reverse the abnormal levels (Figure 7K).

In the MCD-induced model, the α-diversity of the intestinal microbiota in MCD mice was increased, while ACT001 did not further promote diversity. However, no difference in the Shannon and Simpson indices was observed between the MCD and ACT001 groups (p > 0.05) (Figure 8A). Similar to the HFD model, the dilution and rank abundance curves demonstrated flat trends (Figure 8B,C).

From the PCoA, the score plot could be classified into three fractions. The results also showed that the microbiota of the MCD group had a shift along the positive direction of the PCo2 compared with that of the MCS group, while the supplement of ACT001 reverted the variations along the negative direction of PCo2, which was similar to that of the PPC group (Figure 8D). Likewise, NMDS analysis also showed that the homologous results were consistent (Figure 8E). Clustering analysis indicated that the distance between the ACT001 or PPC and MCS groups was shorter than the distance between the MCD and MCS groups (Figure 8F).

The bacterial communities are also illustrated in the taxonomic tree in the circle packing chart (Figure 8G), and an online version is shown (https://genescloud.cn/data/conf/user/tjlplxg/MD202209162205SO9D/results_custom/2_composition/2.5_krona/krona-MCD-202301150841-3575487f710e/krona.html?dataset=0&node=0&collapse=true&color=false&depth=7&font=11&key=true).

Firmicutes had the highest abundance at the phylum level. LEfSe analysis was also performed to identify altered intestinal bacteria with biological significance. At the phylum level, ACT001 mainly induced the enrichment of Bacteroidetes, and Bacteroides significantly increased after ACT001 supplementation at the genus level (Figure 8H,I). The MCD diet increased the abundance of Tenericutes, which was reduced by ACT001 supplementation (Figure 8J). At the genus level, several relative abundance alterations at the genus level, ACT001 significantly elevated the relative abundances of Streptococcus、Enterococcus、Erysipelotrichaceae_Clostridium、Clostridium、Adlercreutzia、Subdoligranulum、cc_115 and Gemella, but decreased Bifidobacterium and Shigella compared with the MCD group (Figure 8K).

These findings reveal an association between ACT001 intervention and concurrent structural reorganization of gut microbiota across multiple taxonomic levels, alongside phenotypic improvement in MASLD. Notably, this occurs without major alterations in the Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes ratio.

Effect of ACT001 on fecal BAs composition of MASLD mice

The principal component analysis (PCA) indicated that the ND group could be commendably differentiated from the HFD group, but the extent of dispersion between the ACT001 or PPC group and the model group is small (Figure 9B). Furthermore, orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to visualize BAs alterations between the ND and HFD groups, as well as between the HFD group and the ACT001 group (Figure 9C,E). In addition, scoring maps were drawn for each model to further verify their accuracy and efficiency (Figure 9D and F).

According to the research, 30 types of BAs were differentially expressed between the ND and HFD groups, and the bar chart shows the most significant differential expression of 20 BAs (Figure 9G). Moreover, the levels of 16 types of detected BAs were significantly different between the HFD and ACT001 groups (Figure 9H). These results illustrate that ACT001 not only increased the fecal excretion of BAs but also modulated the profile of BAs in the feces of HFD and MCD-induced MASLD mice.

Effect of ACT001 on bile acid–related mRNA expression of MASLD mice

In the HFD-induced model, ACT001/PPC treatment showed a trend toward reducing ileal FXR mRNA expression in HFD mice, although the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05) (Figure 11A). Notably, ACT001 significantly downregulated the ileal FGF15 levels elevated by high-fat diet (p < 0.05) (Figure 11B). Similarly, the MCD group showed a trend toward increased ileal mRNA expression of both FXR and FGF15 (p > 0.05) (Figure 11G). Intervention with ACT001 significantly reduced the levels of FGF15 in MCD mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 11H).

Effect of ACT001 on the FXR-related axis: changes of (A) ileal FXR, (B) ileal FGF15, (C) CYP7A1, (D) hepatic FXR, (E) SHP, and (F) BSEP in mice in HFD-induced MASLD model; changes of (G) ileal FXR, (H) ileal FGF15, (I) CYP7A1, (J) hepatic FXR, (K) SHP, and (L) BSEP in mice in MCD-induced MASLD model. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001; #p < 0.05, ##p < 0.01, ###p < 0.001.

The results also revealed that the mRNA expression levels of CYP7A1 were significantly higher in the ACT001 group than those in the HFD and MCD groups (Figure 11C, I). In contrast, no significant differences in BSEP mRNA expression were observed among the groups in the high-fat diet model (Figure 11F). Compared with the model group, ACT001 intervention significantly increased the relative mRNA expression level of the bile salt export pump (BSEP) in MCD-fed mice (p < 0.05) (Figure 11L).

However, the hepatic expression of FXR and SHP was not significantly affected in the ACT001 groups in either model (p > 0.05) (Figure 11D,E,J,K).

These results suggest that ACT001 may regulate BAs homeostasis and cholesterol metabolism through the FXR–FGF15 signaling pathway in the intestine of MASLD mice rather than directly influencing the expression of BAs receptors in the liver.

Correlation analysis of targeted metabolomics and gut microbiota

Spearman’s correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between the gut microbiota and BAs content in feces in the model and ACT001 groups.

Discussion

With the rapid development of the social economy, unhealthy lifestyles, such as excessive energy intake and sedentary lifestyles, have led to a global prevalence of obesity and metabolic syndrome. Accordingly, the global prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease continues to increase synchronously, making it one of the most pressing public health problems worldwide [26]. Chronic overnutrition engenders the emergence of MASLD in adults and children by disturbing metabolic homeostasis, engendering low-grade inflammation and exerting a direct effect on hepatic fat accumulation through a variety of pathways [27]. Dietary interventions are helpful in the prevention and treatment of NAFLD in both adults and children, particularly low-fat diets and the Mediterranean diet [28,29]. In the present study, MCD and HFD diets were used to construct MASLD models. Consistent with our previous study, ACT001 had the ability of suppressed MASLD symptoms, such as weight gain, dyslipidemia, and liver damage. In addition, our study provides evidence that ACT001 could restore the intestinal barrier and correct intestinal dysbiosis, which is correlated with the modulation of BAs metabolism, further regulating the gut–liver axis.

The hallmark of MASLD is hepatocellular steatosis [30]. The present pathological examinations both indicated significant steatosis, lobular inflammation, and cellular damage in the HFD and MCD groups, which were reduced by ACT001 treatment. As expected, both HFD and MCD diets resulted in elevated hepatic cholesterol levels, once described as an inflammatory marker, which is a critical trigger for developing and progressing MASLD [31]. More importantly, we found that the contents of TC and TG declined after ACT001 administration, and a synchronous trend in liver enzyme indices of liver homogenate was observed. Consistent with our previous study, the results confirmed that ACT001 could ameliorate inflammation infiltration and fat deposition in the liver in the HFD model. MASLD is associated with systemic low-grade chronic inflammation, referred to as ‘meta-inflammation,’ which is characterized by recruiting immune inflammatory cells and increasing abnormal cytokines such as IL-6 or TNF-α [25]. HFD feeding has been linked to hepatic oxidative stress, hepatic steatosis, and mitochondrial dysfunction [32]. Oxidative stress, for example, leads to the activation of the redox-sensitive transcription factor (NF-κB), which in turn triggers the expression of TNF-α and IL-6 [33]. The gut is not only affected by inflammation but also plays an essential role in the pathogenesis of MASLD. Intestinal tight junction proteins, such as ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1, effectively regulate intestinal barrier function [34]. This research shows that both HFD and MCD feeding resulted in low-grade intestinal inflammation, reflected by the elevated levels of ileal IL-6 and TNF-α, which are thought to be involved in meta-inflammation. As expected, these cytokines were significantly alleviated by ACT001 supplementation, which also markedly safeguarded the intestinal barrier function by promoting expression-related proteins. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [23,35,36].

The gut microbiota community is a crucial foundation across the liver–gut axis. Intestinal dysbiosis is strongly associated with the initiation and progression of MASLD. Recent studies have shed light on the possibility that anti-MASLD drugs may come into effect by improving the gut microenvironment [36–38]. This study showed that the number of microbial species and α-diversity in the HFD-induced model decreased, whereas the MCD-induced model showed the opposite trend, which was consistent with previous relevant research [39,40]. Interestingly, ACT001 supplementation did not increase the microbial abundance in HFD or MCD mice. The PCoA plot, NMDS plot, and clustering tree showed that the β-diversity of the intestinal microbiota in the ACT001 group resembled that of the control group, suggesting that ACT001 could restore the diversity of the gut microbiota in MASLD mice.

The abundance of TM7 in the intestine increased significantly after ACT001 intervention in our two MASLD models. TM7, also known as Saccharibacteria, is one of the most enigmatic phyla that can repress TNF-α production in macrophages, indicating that it has the potential to inhibit inflammation [41,42]. Most studies have focused on TM7 as an oral microbiome for the regulation of body metabolism, and few studies have investigated its mechanism in gut microbiota. Regarding the relationship between TM7 and MASLD, the increased abundance of TM7 in the gut after ACT001 administration was consistent with the findings of previous studies [43,44]. Altogether, the existence of TM7 is inversely related to MASLD; however, its specific mechanism remains unclear. Verrucomicrosbia was the most abundant phylum in the ACT001 intervention group in the HFD-induced model, which increased significantly compared to that in the ND group. However, this phenomenon was not observed in MCD-induced model. Unlike TM7, Verrucomicrobia is a phylum of bacteria that has beneficial effects on metabolism [45]. Several studies have already reported that attenuation of hepatic steatosis is linked to an elevated proportion of Verrucomicrobia in HFD mice [37,46]. The phylum Bacteroidota is the most abundant bacteria in MCD-fed mice after ACT001 treatment, and it is also widely recognized as a well-known probiotic that has the ability to mitigate the occurrence of obesity-related diseases. In addition, some studies have shown that patients with MASLD exhibit a higher proportion of Bacteroides [47]. In fact, either an increased or decreased abundance of Bacteroides has been reported in mice or patients with metabolic disorders, so the exact role of Bacteroides in MASLD remains inconclusive [23,37,39,48]. Consistent with previous studies, ACT001 also reduced the abundance of Tenericutes in the MASH model, and compelling evidence supports the notion that Tenericutes is a salient feature of obesity, type 2 diabetes, MASLD, and other metabolic diseases [49,50].

Further analysis at the genus level indicated that the abundance of Clostridium and Erysipelotrichaceae Clostridium in the two groups of MASLD model mice decreased significantly, which could be reversed by ACT001. The low relative levels of Clostridium in MASLD patients have been shown to be beneficial in MASLD, mediating a key step in the process of converting primary bile acids into secondary bile acids in the gut [51–54]. It is also considered an important weapon in combating obesity and MASH, as it regulates lipid and cholesterol metabolism [55]. It has been shown that fecal SCFA production is significantly reduced in HFD-fed C57BL/6J mice [56]. Furthermore, research has shown that the alleviating effect of luteolin against MASLD is associated with an increase in the abundance of Erysipelotrichaceae_Clostridium, which is relevant to hepatic steatosis and is known as an SCFA-producing bacterium [40,57]. ACT001 effectively induced the expression of Dehalobacterium, cc_115 and Coprobacillus. The specific mechanism of Dehalobacterium in MASLD has not been clarified, but it has been confirmed to be associated with glycolysis and related pathways [58]. Sodium acetate/sodium butyrate has been shown to enhance the abundance of cc_115 in mice, which is related to epithelial barrier function and intestinal inflammation [59]. Coprobacillus is also considered an SCFA producer, stimulating energy consumption and accelerating fat oxidation, which play a crucial role in treating MASLD with psyllium husk [60].

In the HFD-induced model, the ACT001 group showed richness of Akkermansia compared to the HFD group. Akkermansia belongs to the Verrucomicrobia phylum, which is a mucin-degrading bacterium that resides in the mucus layer. Studies have shown that the presence of this bacterium is negatively correlated with body weight [61,62]. In this study, the Akkermansia abundance by ACT001 was consistent with that of Verrucomicrobia, suggesting that Akkermansia is the representative genus of the Verrucomicrobia phylum. Our study also showed a low relative abundance of Sutterella and Rikenella in HFD mice supplemented with ACT001, which is consistent with previous studies [63,64]. In the MCD-induced model, ACT001 increased the levels of Streptococcus, Adlercreutzia, Subdoligranulum and Gemella, which have been demonstrated to be inversely associated with dietary fructose or fat intake in previous studies [57,60,65,66]. Meanwhile, Enterococcus was elevated after ACT001 intervention, which could treat diarrhea and hepatic disease. It can adapt well to the intestinal microenvironment and promote the production of SCFA and the growth of other bacterial strains beneficial to the host, thus providing further health benefits for the host [67]. Herein, we observed that Shigella and Bifidobacterium were relatively low in mice after ACT001 administration, which is similar to previously published results [68]. Taken together, we propose that these changes in the microbiota contributed, at least partially, to the therapeutic effect of ACT001 in MASLD mice, although previous studies have slightly inconsistent with our findings.

Another basis of the liver–gut axis is the dynamic balance of the BAs community in the host body. BAs metabolism is regulated by the liver and gut microbiota, which influences the biotransformation, reabsorption, and excretion of BAs; however, BAs act as signaling molecules, and signaling cascades induced by BAs not only control the expression and activity of genes involved in lipid and energy metabolism, but also mutually regulate the composition of the gut microbiota [22,25]. BAs can be classified into primary and secondary types based on their origin. CDCA and CA are major primary BAs, which are produced from hepatic cholesterol and catalyzed by intestinal microbial activity to form secondary BAs. DCA and LCA are the dominant secondary BAs [69]. BAs can also be classified as conjugated and unconjugated BAs based on their molecular structures. In hepatocytes, primary BAs can combine with taurine or glycine to generate conjugated BAs, which have the key physiological function of emulsifying and solubilizing fat. In the intestine, conjugated BAs are hydrolyzed by specific bacteria, yielding unconjugated BAs [70].

Homeostatic disorder of BAs is involved in the pathogenesis of MASLD [52]. When liver function is impaired, excess bile acid cannot be completely removed, causing more bile acid to appear in the bloodstream. As a result, in most cases, MASLD patients tend to have higher levels of total bile acid [71,72]. In this study, the contents of total BAs, primary BAs, secondary BAs, conjugated BAs, and unconjugated BAs in feces were increased in the HFD diet-induced NAFL mice. However, the levels of BAs in mice fed MCD dramatically decreased, which was also observed in the MASLD mouse model induced by MCD [73,74]. The MCD model is designed to induce injury in a short time, so it was used as the first choice to induce MASH in mice, but its etiology was essentially different from that of MASLD in humans [75]. Mice fed with MCD often have decreased appetite, malnutrition, and other symptoms, which may explain why the levels of BAs always decreased in the MCD model. When BAs reach the end of the ileum, approximately 95% of BAs are actively reabsorbed into intestinal epithelial cells and then transported back to the liver, mainly as conjugated BAs, while the rest of the BAs enter the colon and are excreted in feces, which is called the enterohepatic cycle [74]. In the HFD-induced model, ACT001 alleviated excessive BAs accumulation by increasing the total BAs in the stool and then reducing blood reabsorption. However, ACT001 did not further increase fecal BAs in the MASH model, which has been previously reported in similar articles [24].

Bile salt hydrolases (BSH) are produced by the microbiota and deconjugate-conjugated BAs to form unconjugated BAs [73]. BSH activity is highly associated with metabolic disorders. The reabsorption efficiency of unconjugated BAs produced by BSH is low, and they can be excreted out of the body in large quantities with feces, thus increasing the synthesis of BAs from scratch, which lowers cholesterol levels in the serum [76]. This conclusion has been confirmed by many studies [73,77]. In this study, we found that ACT001 could elevate the content of Clostridium and Erysipelotrichaceae_Clostridium in MASLD models, which had the ability to increase the activity of BSH [78].

Cholesterol is a key ingredient in BA production, and CYP7A1 and CYP27A1 are rate-limiting enzymes for BA synthesis [79]. CYP7A1 is an enzyme that converts cholesterol to primary BAs such as CA and CDCA via classical pathways, in which CYP8B1 is essential [80]. CYP27A1 and CYP7B1 are required for cholesterol conversion to CDCA via alternative pathways [81]. Thus, CYP7A1 and CYP27A1 regulate BA production and play key roles in maintaining cholesterol and BA balance [82]. The majority of the BAs pool is synthesized in the liver principally through the classical synthesis pathway and then excreted into the bile through the elevation of BSEP [25].

FXR, as the major intracellular receptor of BAs, is highly expressed in the liver and intestine and regulates glucose and lipid metabolism through different mechanisms. Activated hepatic FXR induces a sequence of downstream effects, including upregulation of the target protein SHP, which subsequently inhibits triglyceride production and BA synthesis in the liver [83]. In the gut, BAs influence intestinal FXR and alter the secretion of FGF19 or FGF15. FGF19/15 is transported to the liver via the portal vein and binds to FGFR4 in liver cells, ultimately accelerating or inhibiting the synthesis of CYP7A1 [84]. Several studies have suggested that activating intestinal FXR is a potential therapeutic target for MASLD treatment [73,85]. Contrary to the above studies, an increasing number of studies have shown that the inhibition of intestinal FXR can improve metabolic diseases [86,87]. Hepatic cholesterol accumulation has been attributed to the activation of the ileum FXR–FGR15 pathway due to the heightened passive absorption of BAs into ileal enterocytes in rats fed a HFD, which inhibited hepatic CYP7A1 gene transcription to decrease metabolic elimination of cholesterol [88]. Our study showed that both HFD and MCD feeding increased the downstream effects of intestinal FXR and FGF15 regulation and subsequent administration of ACT001 reduced the mRNA level of FXR in the ileum, which led to a decrease in FGF15 expression. FGF15 negatively accelerates BA synthesis in the liver by activating CYP7A1 expression

However, there were no significant differences between the expression of hepatic FXR and SHP between the model and ACT001 groups. Thus, ACT001 may have no direct effect on the liver receptor, and the pharmacological effect of ACT001 may be a secondary effect after its regulation of the gut microbiota and subsequent BAs metabolism in the intestine. The previous study has demonstrated that some hepatocyte-protective agents, like Norursodeoxycholic acid (NorUDCA), do not activate hepatic FXR/TGR5, but may exert FXR antagonistic effects through gut–liver axis, enhancing BA synthesis and lowering plasma cholesterol, which was our results [89].

As anticipated, we found that ACT001 administration mainly increased the content of ILCA, 23-DCA, LCA-3S, and 6-ketoLCA but reduced the levels of conjugated BAs, including TLCA, THDCA, and TUDCA, in feces. LCA and its derivatives will be discharged directly into the stool due to its insolubility, and the increase in its content in the stool suggests potential effects toward enhancing fecal BA loss. ACT001 induced notable upregulation of specific BAs, including 23-DCA and LCA-3S, in MCD-fed mice, which was consistent with our observations in HFD-fed mice. DCA is the ingredient derived from 23-DCA, and the traditional view is that DCA exhibits strong hepatotoxicity due to its hydrophobicity [90]. Recently, compelling evidences have observed that DCA function as antagonists of FXR [91,92]. Consistent with our findings, Zhang et al. revealed that the deficiency of DCA in feces was also involved in the development of liver inflammation and displayed a positive correlation with accumulated LPS levels [34]. The discrepancy in the results might reveal that the diverse concentrations of DCA and different sites of action might play diverse roles at the MASLD stage. Because 23-DCA is a derivative of DCA, the results of this study were consistent with those of previous studies, indicating that the enhancement of 23-DCA might markedly increase the mRNA levels of hepatic CYP7A1, which was ascribed to the dynamic changes in the intestinal FXR and FGF15 levels.

The current study had several limitations. Firstly, the study only included female mice, meaning the impact of estrogen on MASLD was overlooked. Secondly, there was insufficient mouse serum for further study of serum metabolomics. Furthermore, experiments involving FMT in germ-free mice are lacking to validate the association mechanism between gut microbiota and BAs. Finally, while the HFD/MCD diet model closely mimics the progression of MASLD in humans, the results of this study cannot be applied directly to clinical studies. Further studies are needed in the future to address the above limitations. Last but not least, a limitation of this study is the qualitative assessment of histology. While representative images are presented, the findings were not quantified using a standardized scoring system (e.g. NAFLD Activity Score or fibrosis stage), which should be incorporated in future work to provide more objective data. It is also important to note that the current study did not evaluate potential off-target or systemic effects of ACT001. Future studies should therefore include comprehensive toxicology profiles to assess its safety in non-hepatic organs.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that ACT001 administration alleviated the development of MASLD in MASLD models, as shown in previous experiments, and this effect was partly associated with regulation of the gut microbiota–BAs–FXR pathway. However, further investigation is required to verify this conclusion and to explore the exact mechanism.
