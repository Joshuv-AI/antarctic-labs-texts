Triple‐Negative Breast Cancer Presenting as Recurrent Cystic Lesions During Lactation: A Case Report
Authors: Lixin Xue, Zhengzuo Sheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTLactation‐associated breast cancer poses diagnostic challenges due to physiological breast changes that may mask malignancies. Triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) during lactation is rare and aggressive, requiring vigilant evaluation and treatment. This report highlights the diagnostic dilemma of recurrent cystic breast lesions during lactation, which can mimic benign conditions like galactoceles but may conceal aggressive TNBC, leading to potential delays in diagnosis despite initial conservative approaches such as aspiration. A 26‐year‐old lactating woman presented with recurrent complex cystic lesions initially mistaken for benign lactational changes. Despite multiple aspirations, the lesions enlarged, leading to surgical excision. Pathology confirmed TNBC (ER−, PR−, HER2−, Ki‐67 ~40%). Core needle biopsy and extended resection with sentinel lymph node biopsy confirmed grade III invasive ductal carcinoma. The patient received 8 cycles of chemotherapy (4 cycles of pegylated liposomal doxorubicin and cyclophosphamide, followed by 4 cycles of taxane) without radiotherapy, achieving 6‐year recurrence‐free survival. Persistent complex cystic lesions during lactation require core needle biopsy to rule out malignancy. Multimodal TNBC treatment can yield favorable outcomes, including fertility preservation, as evidenced by the patient's subsequent pregnancy in 2024.

Introduction

Pregnancy‐associated breast cancer (PABC) generally includes breast cancer diagnosed during pregnancy or within 1 year postpartum [1]. Compared to sporadic breast cancer, PABC tends to occur in younger women, and certain tumors may progress more rapidly, posing greater challenges for prognosis [2, 3]. Clinical judgments may prematurely attribute suspicious symptoms to common lactational issues such as mastitis or cysts, potentially delaying diagnosis [4].

In recent years, as childbearing age rises and breast cancer incidence climbs, PABC has garnered growing clinical attention [2, 5]. Among such cases, lactation‐associated breast cancer (LABC) can be particularly difficult to identify at an early stage due to increased glandular hyperplasia, abundant blood supply, and inflammatory or cystic manifestations. Some studies indicate that postpartum involution alters the local immune microenvironment and collagen structures, potentially facilitating tumorigenesis and metastasis [6].

Additionally, triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) lacks traditional therapeutic targets, making it heavily reliant on chemotherapy and surgical intervention. If diagnosed at an advanced stage or if axillary lymph nodes are already involved, the prognosis is more challenging. Therefore, heightened vigilance for suspicious breast masses during lactation, combined with comprehensive imaging and pathological evaluation, is essential. This case of TNBC presenting as recurrent complex cystic lesions during lactation highlights diagnostic pitfalls and the need for timely intervention to optimize outcomes and preserve fertility.

General Information and Chief Complaint

A 26‐year‐old female was admitted on April 9, 2018, with bilateral breast lumps for 10 months and progressive enlargement of a left‐sided mass for 1 month. Initial symptoms began 10 days postpartum during lactation. She had a history of breast fibroadenoma.

Present Illness

Ten months prior, the patient visited a local hospital for suspected blocked milk ducts. Breast ultrasound revealed multiple cystic lesions consistent with lactation, the largest measuring 1.5 × 0.7 cm (left) and 1.5 × 0.8 cm (right), with well‐defined boundaries and regular shapes. No palpable masses were noted, and regular follow‐up was advised. Five months before admission, a “peanut‐sized” lump was incidentally found in the upper inner quadrant of the left breast, without redness, swelling, pain, or nipple discharge. Repeated ultrasounds showed persistent bilateral cystic lesions, and no intervention was initiated due to lactation. One month prior to admission, the left breast mass enlarged rapidly to approximately pigeon‐egg size, with mild pain and superficial nipple erosion. Aspiration at a local facility yielded ~30 mL of old bloody fluid, with temporary shrinkage, but the mass re‐enlarged. Cytological analysis of the aspirated fluid showed no tumor cells, and bacterial culture was negative. Nine days before admission, ultrasound revealed a 4.3 × 2.7 cm irregular cystic lesion at the 11 o'clock position of the left breast with internal septations. Three additional aspirations (each yielding ~30 mL of bloody fluid) failed to reduce the mass, and bacterial cultures remained negative. The patient declined further surgical drainage, core needle biopsy, and was referred to our hospital.

Diagnosis and Treatment Course.

Under local anesthesia with ultrasound guidance, a 5.5 × 6.5 cm lesion at 11 o'clock of the left breast was excised, with 35 mL of old bloody fluid drained. Vacuum‐assisted excision was also performed for nodules at the 1 o'clock (left) and 9 o'clock (right) positions.

An extended resection of remaining left breast tissue and pectoralis major fascia was performed, along with sentinel lymph node biopsy (frozen section: negative).

Postoperative Adjuvant Chemotherapy

Beginning June 2018, the patient received 8 cycles of chemotherapy: 4 cycles of pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (40 mg) and cyclophosphamide (950 mg), followed by 4 cycles of a taxane.

She tolerated treatment well. Radiotherapy was not administered due to negative surgical margins, negative sentinel lymph nodes, and the patient's lactation status, balancing the risk of radiation‐induced fibrosis against the low risk of local recurrence [7]. After thorough consultation, the patient declined radiotherapy, prioritizing breastfeeding continuation and fertility preservation.

Postoperative Follow‐Up and Prognosis

The patient underwent outpatient follow‐up every 3–6 months, with regular breast ultrasound, chest computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging, and abdominal ultrasound evaluations. As of 2024 (6 years postoperatively), she remains in good condition with no local recurrence or distant metastasis and successfully conceived again in 2024, highlighting the feasibility of fertility preservation in TNBC management (Figure 3).

Discussion

This patient presented with recurrent complex cystic lesions during lactation, which continued to enlarge despite multiple aspirations. Ultimately, an expanded surgical resection and in‐depth pathological assessment confirmed the diagnosis of TNBC. This case not only underscores the diagnostic complexity of LABC but also emphasizes the unique clinical considerations and therapeutic opportunities inherent in managing TNBC in lactating women.

The physiological changes of lactation, including augmented glandular tissue and increased vascularity, can obscure malignancies, presenting as cystic or inflammatory‐like lesions that reduce imaging accuracy [8]. Mammography is often limited by increased breast density, whereas ultrasound, though relatively sensitive, may not effectively distinguish between benign inflammatory changes and malignancy [9]. Persistent lesions larger than 2 cm, recurring after aspiration, or showing complex features (e.g., septations, wall nodularity) on ultrasound should prompt immediate core needle biopsy, as recommended by current breast imaging guidelines. Aspiration cytology may miss malignant cells, as seen in this case where repeated aspirations yielded bloody fluid negative for tumor cells.

The pathological workup illustrates the difficulties in identifying TNBC. Initial IHC showed weak ER/PR/HER2 positivity, discordant with the clinical presentation of rapid enlargement and recurrence post‐aspiration. This discrepancy, likely due to tumor heterogeneity or sampling error, highlights the need for larger tissue samples via core needle biopsy [10]. Tertiary center review and extended resection confirmed TNBC with a Ki‐67 of ~40%, reflecting high proliferative capacity. Differential diagnoses included lactational adenoma, galactocele, and phyllodes tumor, but rapid growth and bloody fluid favored malignancy.

Management of TNBC during lactation requires a careful and multidisciplinary approach, involving surgical oncologists, pathologists, medical oncologists, and reproductive specialists to optimize treatment, ensure accurate diagnosis, and address psychological and fertility concerns [11, 12, 13]. In the absence of ER, PR, and HER2 targets, treatment strategies for TNBC hinge primarily on surgery and chemotherapy [11, 12, 13]. TNBC is known for its aggressive biology and higher risk of early recurrence, making prompt and comprehensive treatment essential for achieving long‐term disease control [14]. In this patient, an 8‐cycle regimen of 4AC followed by 4T was administered postoperatively, resulting in favorable tolerance and no evidence of recurrence at the 6‐year follow‐up. This outcome reinforces the efficacy of multimodal therapy, even in the complex context of lactation. The decision to omit radiotherapy was reasonable given the negative surgical margins, negative sentinel lymph nodes, and the patient's lactation status, which prioritized breastfeeding continuation and fertility preservation. A multidisciplinary tumor board review, involving surgical oncologists, medical oncologists, pathologists, and reproductive specialists, was conducted to evaluate the case, and the decision was informed by evidence from Haque et al. [7], which demonstrated no significant survival benefit for postmastectomy radiotherapy in T1‐2N0 TNBC, supporting shared decision‐making with the patient to balance treatment efficacy with her reproductive goals. This approach ensured that the omission of radiotherapy was carefully weighed against the low risk of local recurrence and potential radiation‐induced complications, aligning with the patient's preferences and clinical context.

Moreover, a breast cancer diagnosis during lactation often necessitates ceasing breastfeeding to facilitate surgical and chemotherapeutic interventions. This case required heightened attention to postpartum maternal health and psychological support. The absence of HBOC, confirmed by negative BRCA1/2 testing and no family history of breast or ovarian cancer, reduced concerns about hereditary syndromes [15, 16]. While hereditary TNBC, often linked to BRCA1/2 mutations, tends to present in young women with higher‐grade tumors, larger size, and a prevalence of 10%–15% in TNBC cases, this non‐hereditary (sporadic) case in a young lactating patient demonstrates comparable aggressiveness but similar survival prospects post‐treatment compared to BRCA carriers, highlighting that genetic status does not necessarily alter prognosis in early intervention scenarios. At 6 years postoperatively, the patient has remained disease‐free and successfully conceived again in 2024, indicating that long‐term survival can coexist with fertility preservation in selected TNBC cases. Pre‐treatment fertility counseling and options such as oocyte cryopreservation, offered prior to chemotherapy, were critical in preserving reproductive potential, highlighting the importance of oncofertility in young TNBC patients [17]. Their safety and efficacy in lactating women require further study, but they hold promise for improving outcomes. Globally, LABC–TNBC cases are rare, with a 2023 review reporting < 5% of PABC cases as TNBC, emphasizing this case's rarity and educational value [18]. Notably, TNBC prevalence is higher in certain populations, such as Asian and African women, underscoring the need for region‐specific vigilance in diagnosing atypical presentations like cystic lesions [19, 20] (Table 2).

Recent advancements in oncofertility have provided updated guidelines for fertility preservation in cancer patients. The American Society of Clinical Oncology has released an updated guideline in 2025, emphasizing the importance of early counseling and offering various fertility preservation options, including oocyte and embryo cryopreservation, for young breast cancer patients [21]. This is particularly relevant for patients with TNBC, who may face aggressive treatment regimens that can impact fertility.

Furthermore, managing breast cancer during pregnancy and lactation presents unique challenges. A comprehensive review by Mumtaz et al. [22] discusses the diagnosis, management, and outcomes of PABC, highlighting the need for a multidisciplinary approach to balance maternal and fetal health. Given that TNBC is more prevalent in younger women and can occur during the reproductive years, understanding the specific considerations for PABC is crucial for optimizing care in this population.

Conclusion

This case underscores the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges associated with lactation‐associated TNBC. The unique physiological changes during lactation can obscure malignancy, necessitating a high degree of clinical vigilance for persistent or recurrent cystic lesions. Early and accurate diagnosis, facilitated by core needle biopsy and advanced imaging, is pivotal for timely intervention. Aspiration cytology may miss malignant cells, as demonstrated in this case, highlighting the superiority of core needle biopsy.

The successful management of this case illustrates the efficacy of a multimodal approach, combining surgery and chemotherapy, in treating TNBC even during lactation. The patient's 6‐year disease‐free survival and subsequent conception in 2024 demonstrate that aggressive treatment can still lead to favorable outcomes, including fertility preservation. Emerging therapies, such as immune checkpoint inhibitors and PARP inhibitors, offer promise for increasingly personalized TNBC management, though their use in lactating women requires further investigation.

Furthermore, given the patient's young age and TNBC diagnosis, this case highlights the importance of considering genetic assessment in similar scenarios. Even without a family history of breast or ovarian cancer, screening for hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome (HBOC) or other genetic predispositions can guide tailored treatment plans and inform long‐term surveillance strategies.

This case reinforces the critical role of individualized care and rigorous follow‐up in achieving optimal outcomes for TNBC, particularly amidst the unique challenges posed by lactation.
