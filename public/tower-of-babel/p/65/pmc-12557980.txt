Studying the heterogeneity of gestational diabetes mellitus: cardio-metabolic alteration and treatment response in a multi-ethnic population in Singapore (GDM-CARE): a study protocol
Authors: Qing Yi Chen, Johan Gunnar Eriksson, Mihir Gandhi, Daphne S L Gardner, Citra Nurfarah Zaini Mattar, Lin Lin Su, Adrian Kee Keong Teo, Shiao-Yng Chan, Ling-Jun Li

We hypothesize that identification of these heterogenous phenotypes offers better opportunities for more effective and precise treatments in preventing GDM-related adverse outcomes. Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as hyperglycemia first detected at any time during pregnancy, with glucose levels lower than those of overt diabetes, among pregnant women without pre-existing chronic diabetes conditions [1]. GDM increases the risk of adverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes, including preeclampsia, macrosomia, preterm birth, and offspring respiratory distress syndrome [2–4]. While GDM usually resolves following delivery, it can lead to long-lasting health consequences, including heightened risk for type 2 diabetes (T2D), cardiovascular diseases (CVDs), renal impairment, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in both mothers and offspring [5–10].

The prevalence of GDM varies from 5% to over 25% in different populations globally and is particularly high among pregnant women of Asian descent (11.5%) when compared with others like Europeans (10.9%) or non-Hispanic White (5.3%) [11–14]. Compelling evidence has shown Asians shared a widespread presence in spite of country or region due to lifestyle, diagnostic criteria, and ethnicity differences in body mass index (BMI). In Singapore, the overall GDM prevalence was reported to be 18.9% in the Singapore birth cohort (Growing Up in Singapore Towards Health Outcomes, GUSTO), and was noted to be higher in Chinese (21.0%) and Indian (22.3%) ethnic groups than Malay (12.1%) [15]. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF) report in 2019, Thailand and Singapore has shown the highest rate of GDM prevalence (24.7% and 23.5%) in southeast Asia, followed by Malaysia (22.5%) and Vietnam (21.3%) [16]. There is also considerable variability in the prevalence of GDM between races and ethnicities, even though the same diagnostic criteria were applied. Such phenomenon indicates that variations in the distributions of inherent characteristics of study populations may contribute to the observed variability.

Current guidelines by the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) recommend universal screening for GDM among pregnant women between 15 and 30 weeks of gestation. And in the current clinical practice, GDM is managed as a homogenous disease. However, emerging evidence has shown that GDM is a heterogenous condition similar to T2D that can be classified into different phenotypic subtypes, where these subtypes possibly comprise different metabolic entities and could require different treatment. For instance, Banhalima et al. suggested different pregnancy outcomes across GDM subtypes based on insulin resistance [17], and Chatzakis et al. mentioned GDM can be classified into three phenotypes based on the 75 g three time points oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results [18]. Furthermore, Powe et al. demonstrated heterogeneity in the contribution of insulin resistance and insulin deficiency to GDM [19].

More than a third of Caucasian women (38%) diagnosed with GDM had fasting glucose impairment alone; while findings from our unpublished data highlighted the majority of Singapore Chinese (84%), two thirds of Singapore Malay (65%) and more than half of Singapore Indian (57%) women diagnosed with GDM had 1-h and/or 2-h post-challenge glucose intolerance alone [20]. A recent study showed that Indian women developed GDM at lower BMI and had better insulin sensitivity than Scandinavian women [21]. This suggests a potential variation in both insulin sensitivity and fat deposition related β-cell activity across heterogeneous GDM phenotypes, independent of known risk factors of GDM (e.g., maternal BMI, family history of T2D, history of macrosomia and ethnicity).

In this study, we hypothesize that fasting glucose impairment may be the results of: 1) pre-existing peripheral insulin resistance exposed by adiposity; 2) deficiency in fat expandability (e.g., fail to store fat in adipose tissue leading to fat accumulation in ectopic organs such as liver and muscle) may lead to increased gluconeogenesis (e.g., increase fasting blood sugar) and decreased mitochondrial function (e.g., reducing uptake of blood sugar during fasting phase). On the contrary, the post-challenge glucose intolerance group may be due to: 1) -cells expansion deficiency leading to insufficient -cell increment in pregnancy; 2) pancreatic hyperplasia exposed by the release of placental lactogen and placental growth hormone that drive insulin resistance and maternal hyperlipidemia; and 3) reduced skeletal muscle disposal of peripheral glucose.

Since the early days of the 21st century, the continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) system has been used for constant evaluation of glucose levels by measuring interstitial glucose concentrations [22]. A large number of studies have examined its accuracy and safety and has been tested in pregnant women with GDM [23, 24]. A study in 2020 demonstrated a trimester-specific difference of glucose profiles, and such gestational glucose patterns-identified GDM phenotypes might allow treatment optimization for pregnant women [19]. Therefore, applying the CGM technique universally during early, mid, and late pregnancy might be important to provide more indicative information on glucose profiles before and after meals, identify diurnal and nocturnal fluctuations in glucose at different trimesters, and even help in providing guidance of GDM clinical screening and treatment [25]. Glycemic variability indexes, such as postprandial time to peak, interquartile range (IQR), mean of daily difference (MODD), and mean amplitude of glucose excursions (MAGE) were reported to be higher in GDM mothers, compared with their non-GDM counterparts during mid-late pregnancy [26]. It is worthwhile investigating whether such differences between GDM and non-GDM pregnant women remain throughout pregnancy; 1) if there would be subtle changes seen in glycemic variables such as coefficient of variation (%CV), standard deviation (SD), mean glucose or composite glycemic score go beyond traditional indexes like hemoglobin A1C (HbA1c), fasting glucose or post-challenge glucose in diagnosing or predicting GDM; and 2) if a higher %CV of glucose can predict -cell dysfunction and further guide an earlier use of insulin.

Unfortunately, current treatment targets a lower risk of complications by not addressing the underlying pathophysiological factors causing GDM. Notably, current clinical guidelines in Singapore regarding GDM treatment during pregnancy and follow-up postpartum lack consensus. Even though the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) guidelines are recommended in most countries, local practices vary among obstetric specialists in Singapore (e.g., local guidelines), in terms of frequency of glycemic monitoring and opting for dietary control and/or medication including metformin and insulin as the first-line regimen [27]. Thus, effective, easy-to-administer and less time-consuming treatment strategies targeted to individuals are sought. A thorough understanding of the pathophysiology and heterogeneity of GDM is required to develop such treatments and improve the medical healthcare delivery system.

We hypothesize that identification of these heterogeneous phenotypes offers better opportunities for more effective and precise treatments for the prevention of GDM complications. It is necessary and clinically significant to address the heterogeneity in GDM in terms of cardio-metabolic alteration and treatment responses, especially among a higher risk group of overweight and obese pregnant women from different ethnic background in Singapore. By studying these variations across different GDM phenotypes, we would improve our understanding of GDM pathophysiology and further tailor effective treatment strategies to individuals based on their GDM phenotype. Once such proof-of-concept is established, we may even further study the prediction model to GDM phenotype based on the signatures of metabolites and biomarkers in early pregnancy, in order to provide more timely prevention in women at risk of GDM.

In this pregnancy cohort, we aim to conduct a longitudinal study among 800 overweight (23–24.9 kg/m2) or obese ( 25 kg/m2) singleton pregnant women—without a history of diabetes and composed of all ethnicities (mainly Chinese, Malay and Indian)—in a Singaporean tertiary hospital, National University Hospital (NUH) throughout pregnancy. We intend to recruit all pregnant women no later than 14 weeks of gestation (including 13W6D) and screen them for GDM between 15 and 30 weeks of gestation. We will further follow up the GDM women (approximately 200 women) between 34 and 36 weeks gestational to delivery. Lastly, we will characterize heterogeneous GDM phenotypes using a comprehensive cardio-metabolic examination throughout pregnancy.

Methods

Eligibility, recruitment process, and consent

This is a prospective longitudinal study based on a tertiary hospital, NUH Obstetrics and Gynecology outpatient clinic in Singapore. A cohort of 800 pregnant women will be recruited at NUH over a period of 3 years. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are described in Table 1. We will recruit participants attending NUH antenatal clinic during their booking visit in the first trimester. Prior to baseline research visit, participants must be confirmed to have a viable, singleton pregnancy. Three research visits will be scheduled as baseline (5–13 weeks of gestation), visit 1 (15–30 weeks of gestation); and visit 2 (34–36 weeks of gestation). Visit 2 is only for participants diagnosed with GDM.

Study cohort

The study opened to enrollment on 3rd October 2022. Recruitment is projected to continue for 3 years until 30th September 2025. We expect the GDM-CARE cohort to represent the demographics of the pregnant population in Singapore, including race and ethnicity, body weight distribution, and the adult reproductive age spectrum.

Outcomes

Our primary outcome will be unique glycemic profiling recorded via a CGM device and distinctive cardio-metabolic biomarkers alteration from early to late pregnancy that determine different GDM phenotypes. Our secondary outcome will be specific treatment response according to different GDM phenotypes in late pregnancy. We hypothesize that GDM heterogeneity might not only be differentiated by patterns of glycemic profiles and signatures of cardio-metabolic biomarkers or metabolites throughout pregnancy, but also lead to phenotype-specific treatment response in late pregnancy. Furthermore, given the early discovery of unique glycemic profiles and cardio-metabolic biomarkers and metabolites among GDM subjects in the first trimester, we also plan to explore a prediction model using machine learning technique.

Specific Aim 1 (Primary Outcome): To characterize GDM phenotypes through trajectories of glycemic profiling and cardio-metabolic biomarkers or metabolites from early to late pregnancy.

Hypothesis: Different GDM phenotypes manifest specific patterns of glucose profiling (e.g., mean glucose level, TIR [% time in range between 3.5–7.8 mmol/L, glycemic variability indices]), altered levels of insulin sensitivity and secretion measures (e.g., homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistant [HOMA-IR], insulin secretion [HOMA-β], insulinogenic index/HOMA-IR, Matsuda index), fasting, 1-h and 2-h insulin, fasting c-peptide, HbA1c, fasting adipokines e.g. leptin and adiponectin), and different combinations of targeted lipidomic and metabolomic metabolites (e.g., acylcarnitines, fatty acids and amino acids).

Specific Aim 2 (Secondary Outcome): To study treatment response linked to different GDM phenotypes.

Hypothesis: GDM phenotypes such as fasting glucose impairment alone and both fasting and post-challenge glucose abnormality will require immediate attention of medication (metformin and/or insulin), while GDM phenotypes such as different pattern of post-challenge glucose intolerance alone will require more diet and lifestyle modification other than medication.

Exploratory Aim 1 (Machine learning prediction model): To use machine learning to develop a prediction model for GDM diagnosis in the first trimester.

Hypothesis: With comprehensive information on continuous glycemic profiling (e.g., greater glycemic variability), clinically established biomarkers (e.g., greater HOMA-IR) and targeted metabolites (e.g., greater acylcarnitines) in early pregnancy, a prediction model for GDM might be applicable.

Study procedures

The GDM-CARE study protocol is registered on ClinicalTrial.gov NCT06542718 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06542718).

The study procedures to be conducted throughout the whole pregnancy in the GDM-CARE study: baseline (5–13 weeks of gestation), visit 1 (15–30 weeks of gestation); and visit 2 (34–36 weeks of gestation). Visit 2 is only for participants diagnosed with GDM

At baseline visit, questionnaires will be completed with the assistance of a trained research coordinator. Random blood (serum and plasma) samples, fecal sample, and medical record (fetal scan and routine blood test results) will be collected, whereas anthropometric and blood pressure measurements will be performed by the clinic nurse. The CGM device will be worn for 7–14 days under the supervision of research coordinators.

At visit 1, OGTT fasting blood sample, fecal sample, and medical record (fetal growth, routine blood test results, and OGTT results) will be collected, whereas anthropometric and blood pressure measurements will be performed by the clinic nurse. The CGM device will be worn for 7–14 days under the supervision of research coordinators.

At visit 2, random blood sample, fecal sample, and medical record (fetal growth and routine blood test results) will be collected, whereas anthropometric and blood pressure measurements will be performed by the clinic nurse. The CGM device will be worn for only 7 days under the supervision of research coordinators.

At delivery, pregnancy complications, gestational age at delivery, delivery mode, neonatal anthropometric biometry and medical condition during the 24 h after delivery, treatment of GDM, Apgar score, and other pregnancy maternal and fetal outcomes will be collected.

If withdrawal from a participant occurs, the evaluation from her latest visit will sustain and be valid for analysis. Participants may withdraw voluntarily from participation in the study at any stage. Participants may also withdraw voluntarily from blood draws, CGM installation, and fecal sample collection. All participants will still be under standard antenatal care in NUH clinic, and no adverse event is expected after withdrawal.

CGM data, biospecimen, and questionnaire collection

Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM): CGM provides a detailed determination of dynamic glycemic patterns by measuring interstitial glucose every 5 min, providing up to 288 glucose readings per day. We chose the FreeStyle Libre Pro CGM device (Abbott Diabetes Care, Wiesbaden, Germany, https://www.freestyle.abbott/in-en/libre-pro.html) because of its ability to blind glucose values and the lack of need for fingerstick calibration. The CGM will be placed on the upper arm. CGM devices are used in “blinded” mode so that participants, providers, and the GDM-CARE research team do not observe participants’ CGM data. The participant will wear the device for 7–14 days and subsequently return it to the research coordinator. CGM data are downloaded for quality assessment. If there are less than 7 days of data, participants will be asked to reinstall the CGM for 7–14 days, provided this can be accomplished within the study visit window.

Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT): The 2-h 75 g OGTT is conducted after an overnight fast ( 8 h duration). After fasting sample is drawn, participants consume the oral glucose load within 10 min. The timing of hourly sample collection is based on the start time of OGTT beverage consumption. 0, 1, and 2-h blood samples are processed and analysed by the NUH clinic. GDM is diagnosed if any of the IADPSG 3-point diagnostic criteria are met (Table 2).

Additional biospecimen collection: Maternal blood and fecal will be obtained at each visit for additional laboratory measurements and future use. The serum blood tube will first be kept at room temperature to clot for 30–45 min, strictly less than 60 min, then placed in a thermal insulated bag containing ice packs or on ice. Whereas the plasma blood tube will be placed in the thermal insulated bag containing ice packs or on ice immediately. Both samples will be centrifuged and promptly frozen at -80 . All additional biospecimen collected will be analyzed by an external lab in Singapore.

Anthropometrics: Participants' height, weight, and right arm systolic and diastolic blood pressure will be measured at all visits using the Avamech B1000 BMI Machine (Avamech, Singapore) and the BPBIO 320 Blood Pressure Monitor (InBody, Seoul, South Korea) respectively.

Questionnaire: At baseline visit, research coordinators will interview participants regarding their demographics, occupation, housing and household composition, household income, personal medical history, obstetrical history, family history, medication, activity and exercise, diet, alcohol consumption, and smoking history. Questionnaires are only available in English.

Data collection and management

The patients’ participation in this study will involve the collection of “Personal Data”. “Personal Data” means data about the patients which makes them identifiable from such data or from that data and other information which an organization has or is likely to have access to. This includes medical conditions, medications, investigations and treatment history.

Information and “Personal Data” collected for this study will be kept confidential. The patients’ records, to the extent of the applicable laws and regulations, will not be made publicly available. To minimize the risk of loss of confidentiality, all information related to study subjects will be confidential and kept in secure cabinets or password-protected computer files. Study subjects will be identified on all study-related case report forms (CRFs) and databases only by the study ID number, which will not include any personal identifying information. However, the National University Health System (NUHS), regulatory agencies, the National Healthcare Group (NHG) Domain Specific Review Board (DSRB) and the Ministry of Health (MOH) will be granted direct access to their original medical records to check study procedures and data, without making any of their information public.

The study recognizes the NUH Personal Data Protection Notification (https://www.nuh.com.sg/Pages/Personal-Data-Protection-Act.aspx). This Statement recognizes that the rights and privacy of people who participate in the Study must be protected at all times including in the course of sharing data. The guidelines further recognize that the primary investigators are granted sufficient time in which to analyze and publish their primary research initiatives.

The principal investigator (PI) will be responsible for the inclusion and exclusion of data items that potentially become identifiable information such as in the case of a rare outcome in a remote geographic location or in the event that particular data items render little scientific information (e.g., no variation) in the final database. The PI will finalize the process for requesting analytic files.

Statistical methods and analysis

Sample size and power calculation

Based on our prior unpublished Asia–Pacific collaborative study on both Chinese and Indians overweight and obese pregnant subjects (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2), the effect size based on analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparison of glucose across five groups will be more than 0.60 (large effect size), targeting 800 subjects to be recruited. Considering a local setting in Singapore that might carry a lower effect size in glycemic difference between GDM phenotypes, we are considering a moderate effect size (0.3) for the sample size calculation. As we will be interested in comparing 10 outcomes, the sample size of 200 participants with GDM and overweight/obese will be sufficient to detect a difference of 0.3 effect size across five GDM phenotype with 80% power and 0.5% type-I error (applying Bonferroni correction for 10 multiple comparisons to control type-I error rate to 5%) and 10% drop-out rate. The sample size assumes prevalence of GDM phenotypes at 20% for fasting glucose impairment alone (n = 40), 60% for post-challenge glucose abnormality alone (n = 120), and 20% for both fasting and post-challenge glucose abnormality (n = 40).

Analysis plan

Student’s t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, and Fisher’s exact test will be applied based on the distribution or scale of the variables to assess observed differences in maternal characteristics, pregnancy outcomes, and CGM parameters at early pregnancy between GDM and non-GDM participants. Logistics regression will be implemented to assess the associations between each CGM parameter (per SD increase) and GDM diagnosis after adjusting for confounders in the model, including maternal age, ethnicity, BMI at study entry, and systolic blood pressure at study entry.

Next, the analyses will be performed on the changes of glycemic profiles, glycemic indexes and targeted metabolites from early to mid-pregnancy (baseline visit and visit 1 in this study) in relation to GDM phenotypes. Time-series analysis and linear regression will be applied for this analytical plan.

Analyses will be performed on the association between OGTT 0, 1-h and 2-h glycemic values (continuous variables) and GDM phenotypes (categorical variables) (as exposure) (visit 1 in this study) with treatment strategies (diet alone, diet and metformin, diet and metformin and/or insulin) received in late pregnancy (as outcomes) (visit 2 in this study). Ordinal logistic regression will be applied for this analytical plan.

In order to build machine learning prediction models (as per our exploratory outcome), both stepwise logistic regression and ensemble random forest methods will be applied for variable selection.

We will perform multiple imputations for all analyses, to address the issue of missingness in our cohort (< 10% missing data on all covariates and outcomes, no missing on exposure). We will generate 50 imputed datasets to account for sampling uncertainty and pool the results using Rubin’s rules to incorporate this uncertainty into the final.

The effects sizes will be reported as estimates () or relative risk with a 95% confidence interval (CI), using multiple linear regression or Modified Poisson Regression, after adjusting for key confounders such as maternal age and BMI at baseline. The two-sided p value will be set at significance if less than 0.05.

Possible risks and side effects to participants

The study is considered to have a minimal risk to participants, risks will be explained to participants during the informed consent process. These include risks associated with CGM installation or wear and collection of health information (e.g., loss of confidentiality). The wearing of CGM might cause discomfort and skin irritation in participants with allergic or sensitive skin. However, it has been proven to be very safe even among patients with such skin reactions. Rarely, a severe and possibly life-threatening allergic reaction will occur.

Adverse events or complaint handling

There is no potential risk for the participants. Nonetheless, any adverse events or complaints will be recorded by the study team members and reported to the PI. Participants can also contact the PI directly via her contact listed on the consent form. The PI will then investigate the issues before reporting them to the NHG DSRB if necessary.

Discussion

Benefits of identifying individuals at risk for GDM in the first trimester

Pregnancies complicated with early GDM (between 18 and 20 weeks of gestation) were proposed to be associated with maternal and neonatal metabolic dysregulation (e.g., higher glycated hemoglobin, C-peptide) and adverse pregnancy complications (e.g., large-for-gestational-age [LGA]) [28]. Studies have also demonstrated fetal hyperinsulinemia can occur as early as 14 weeks of gestation, well before GDM is typically diagnosed, suggesting that early hyperglycemia-related dysregulation could affect fetal development [29]. Given these points, the recent Treatment of Booking Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (ToBOGM) study showed immediate treatment of GDM before 20 weeks of gestation steered to a lower occurrence of adverse neonatal outcomes [30]. Thus, diagnosis of early-onset GDM may be critical in optimizing maternal and perinatal outcomes.

In 2011, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) and the IADPSG recommended that pregnant women at a heightened risk undergo GDM screening during their first trimester, or as early as the first prenatal visit. However, the effectiveness of screening for mild glucose abnormalities and the best approach remains limited. By analyzing the changes of glycemic profiles, glycemic indexes, and targeted metabolites from early to late-pregnancy, the GDM-CARE study will be able to identify a clinically impactful diagnostic alternative in predicting GDM, fetal and neonatal adverse outcomes (e.g., LGA), delivery mode, transgenerational development of NAFLD and postpartum CVDs (Fig. 2) [9, 10].

Use of CGM in identifying glycemic patterns that may predict GDM, LGA, and other pregnancy complications

The OGTT has been the primary tool for diagnosing GDM for decades, but its status as the gold standard is now being questioned for several reasons. It may have a low reproducibility and accuracy due to various factors in the pre-analytical, analytical, and post-analytical stages, such as the dynamic regulation of glycemia throughout gestation and post-sampling ex-vivo glycolysis; lack of a universally standardized OGTT procedure and diagnostic threshold; and may cause gastrointestinal discomfort [31]. Whereas self-monitoring of blood glucose (SMBG) being invasive and inaccurate. However, the interstitial glucose levels can be constantly evaluated using the CGM system [22]. While CGM evidently offers a comprehensive view of glycemic profiles, studies regarding CGM’s utilization in early prediction of GDM remains unclear, particularly in the Asian population.

The GDM-CARE study will fully explore the utility of CGM in screening, predicting, and managing GDM during the perinatal period, providing an in-depth round-the-clock perspective, encompassing diurnal and nocturnal glucose fluctuations. The effectiveness of CGM in detecting hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia episodes were reported, and further revealed a wider glycemic variability by CGM in contrast to SMBG use in GDM mothers than in those without GDM [26]. As of today, CGM-derived parameters displayed a high-performance predictive model with a 95% area under the curve (AUC) and a 100% negative predictive value among overweight and obese pregnant women [32]. The CGM parameters showed significantly higher values in the GDM mothers during the first trimester were indicative of a substantially elevated risk of developing GDM in the second trimester [32]. CGM significantly enhanced the predictive value for diagnosing GDM from poor to excellent compared to the traditional model considering only maternal age, ethnicity, BMI, and systolic blood pressure (AUC: 0.953 versus 0.722; R2: 0.574 versus 0.110, p 0.001) (Fig. 3) [32]. By recognizing the utility and advantages of CGM in GDM diagnosis, the GDM-CARE study discovered the upper centiles of GMI%, %CV, liability index, and J-Index (measure of quality of glycemic control based on the combination of information from the mean and SD) were positively associated with a two- to three-fold increase in the risk of cesarean delivery [32]. Furthermore, GMI% was correlated with a 4.47-fold increase in the risk (95% CI: 1.24–16.12) of delivering a LGA infant, risk that the IADPSG-defined GDM diagnosis did not identify (Fig. 4) [32].

Treatment response link to different GDM phenotypes

Diabetes begets diabetes. The vicious cycle needs to be interrupted if diabetes epidemic is to be combatted. Evidence in treatment response to lifestyle changes, blood sugar monitoring, and medication prescribed according to different GDM phenotypes is inadequate. Abnormal 1-h and 2-h plasma glucose level at OGTT were found to be significantly associated to the use of rapid and long acting analogues; while normal fasting glucose values at OGTT were found as independent predictors for the use of only rapid insulin analogues [33]. Therefore, the GDM-CARE study hypothesizes GDM phenotypes could potentially be used as precision markers in the treatment of GDM.

Next phase

Over the next 2–3 years, we anticipate that the GDM-CARE cohort will yield promising preliminary results that would enable us to: 1) discover GDM phenotypes that exhibit different degrees of insulin resistance and -cell dysfunction; 2) differentiate and even predict GDM phenotypes as early as the first trimester of pregnancy via glycemic profiling, cardio-metabolic profiling, and metabolites profiling; 3) devise GDM phenotype-based treatment strategies.

Upon completion of the study, we plan to validate our results across other populations with different genetic backgrounds. We will develop a one-step screening approach incorporating integrated glycemic variables and targeted metabolites as biomarkers among Asian women. This approach aims to predict GDM accurately and inform effective management decisions from the onset of pregnancy. On the other hand, based on the current clinical gaps in performing universal GDM screening in Singapore, a new one-step screening approach may greatly reduce the tedious three time point blood sampling process, lessen patients’ burden of venipuncture pain, and provide earlier intervention. By identifying their GDM phenotypes, clinicians might be able to provide: 1) more accurate treatment to individual GDM patient during pregnancy to improve treatment effect; 2) reduce personal and clinical burden of daily glucose testing and monthly medical review.

Moving forward, once the proof-of-concept of GDM phenotypes can be differentiated by a series of signatures in metabolites and glycemic profiles, we will further investigate the postnatal glucose intolerance development related to different GDM phenotypes. Equally important, studies have demonstrated that women with GDM often progress to impaired glucose tolerance (IGT) or T2D based on their antenatal OGTT profiles. There is a growing concern about the loss of follow-up among GDM mothers post-delivery. Therefore, it is necessary to develop more effective screening and intervention programs to prevent postpartum abnormal glucose metabolism in GDM mothers.

Next, we will increase the GDM samples and consequently study the prediction model of postnatal maternal glucose intolerance outcomes among all GDM phenotypes, especially during the first 3 months postpartum period. In the long run, we strive to develop a personalized and precision care model for pregnant women, which will be based on individual’s glucose and metabolites profiles obtained through GDM screening, and with treatment guided. We anticipate such qualitative examination will provide earlier prediction of GDM and pre-emptive management that may reduce incidence of GDM, and eventually be implemented in all tertiary hospitals and polyclinics for antenatal and postnatal screening, treatment, and intervention guidance.

Strengths and limitations of the GDM-CARE study

Our study shows several markable strengths. The prospective and longitudinal design allowed the research of timely relationships between CGM parameters in early pregnancy and GDM diagnosis and detrimental outcomes. The comprehensive range of CGM profile provides an inclusive understanding of glycemic variability. Moreover, the GDM-CARE study participants wear blinded CGM devices at least two time points over the course of pregnancy and generally complete OGTT at 15–30 weeks, leading to a unique and valuable dataset for investigating the longitudinal glycemic profile of GDM complicated pregnancies. As the sample size is small, a direct association between CGM glycemic profiles or metabolic profiles and GDM phenotypes may be challenging to analyze, constraining the statistical power to detect more associations. Larger studies are needed to confirm the findings. Not to mention the lack of information related to dietary patterns and physical activity during pregnancy would lead to biased information. Lastly, the generalizability of the GDM-CARE study findings may be limited since the cohort is composed of overweight and obese Asian women, with the ethnicity of only Chinese, Malay, and Indian within a subsidized medical setup outpatient clinic.

The maternal outcomes are transgenerational, the GDM-CARE study group studies the pathophysiological link between mother and offspring which facilitates the development of a more efficient and precise screening approach or targeted interventions to disrupt the vicious cycle.
