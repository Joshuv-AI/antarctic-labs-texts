Inflammatory exacerbation of hepatic alveolar echinococcosis during dupilumab therapy: A case report

Background

Alveolar echinococcosis (AE) is a potentially fatal zoonosis caused by the larval stage (metacestode) of Echinococcus multilocularis (E. multilocularis), a helminth endemic in the northern hemisphere, particularly in Europe, Asia, and North America. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1], AE is among the most lethal helminthic infections, classified as a neglected tropical disease due to its insidious onset, long asymptomatic incubation period, and frequently delayed diagnosis. AE primarily affects the liver and exhibits infiltrative, tumor-like growth with potential to invade adjacent structures or disseminate to distant organs in advanced stages. Clinical manifestations often emerge only after years, commonly as cholestasis, portal hypertension, or hepatic failure.

The pathogenesis of AE reflects a highly evolved host-parasite relationship characterized by sophisticated immune evasion. The parasite forms an acellular laminated layer that limits immune recognition and actively induces local and systemic immunoregulatory responses. Infected hosts often exhibit a shift from early type 1–biased immune responses, which may restrict lesion growth, toward type 2–dominant and immunoregulatory responses, characterized by cytokines such as interleukin-10 and TGF-β [2]. As highlighted in WHO guidelines, AE progression is strongly influenced by host immune status, with faster progression observed in immunocompromised individuals, including transplant recipients and those receiving immunosuppressive therapy [1].

Biologic therapies targeting type 2 immune responses—particularly those involving interleukin-4 and interleukin-13, such as dupilumab—are increasingly used in the treatment of atopic and allergic diseases. Although these agents are not broadly immunosuppressive, their targeted inhibition of type 2 immune pathways may compromise host defenses against helminths, where such immune responses are critical for containment.

Here, we report a rare case of hepatic AE with an atypical inflammatory course in a young adult undergoing dupilumab therapy, raising concerns about the potential interaction between targeted immunomodulation and latent parasitic infections.

Case

A 29-year-old man was referred to our hospital with worsening abdominal pain after one week of mild epigastric discomfort. On physical examination, his body temperature was 36.3 °C, blood pressure was 140/88 mmHg, pulse rate was 100 bpm, and SpO₂ was 97% on room air. Abdominal examination showed epigastric tenderness without rebound tenderness or rigidity. Initial laboratory findings revealed leukocytosis with neutrophilia (WBC 12,530/µL; neutrophils 85.7%) and markedly elevated CRP (11.96 mg/dL). Liver function tests were largely within normal ranges, except for mildly elevated ALP (139 U/L) and γ-GTP (85 U/L). Total bilirubin was 1.5 mg/dL, albumin was 4.8 g/dL, and prothrombin time was within the normal range (INR 1.09). Serologic tests for viral hepatitis were negative, and tumor markers were within normal limits. CT performed on admission revealed a 6-cm hypodense lesion with 3-cm calcification protruding from the left lateral segment of the liver (Fig. 1A). Additionally, small calcifications were observed in the liver segments S5 and S6 (Fig. 1B, C).

The patient had lived in Hokkaido, Japan, an endemic area for E. multilocularis, since childhood and was engaged in agricultural work. He reported no known direct contact with foxes or other wild animals, no history of consuming wild game, and no overseas travel history. He had a history of atopic dermatitis since childhood, with remission during adolescence. Approximately one year before presentation, his eczema worsened and was initially treated with topical therapy by a local dermatologist, with insufficient response. Five months before presentation, dupilumab therapy was initiated with subcutaneous injections administered every two weeks. Before the initiation of dupilumab therapy, the patient had no abdominal symptoms suggestive of hepatic disease. No abdominal imaging studies had been performed before dupilumab initiation, and therefore the presence and extent of hepatic lesions at that time could not be assessed.

On the day of hospitalization, his abdominal pain worsened and extended to the left flank. CT imaging on hospital day 6 showed fluid collection in the subdiaphragmatic and left pleural space around the liver lesion, raising suspicion of abscess formation and empyema (Fig. 1D). No pulmonary parenchymal nodules or mass lesions were identified. Broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy was initiated to control the inflammatory process. Serological testing by Western blot demonstrated positivity for antibodies against recombinant Em18, supporting the diagnosis of exacerbated hepatic alveolar echinococcosis [3]. Despite intensive antibiotic therapy, the patient remained febrile with elevated inflammatory markers. Given the insufficient response to medical treatment, surgical intervention was performed approximately one month after the initial presentation.

The surgical procedure consisted of partial hepatectomy involving the lateral segment and segments S5 and S6, together with partial resection of the diaphragm and adjacent pleura, as well as wedge resection of the left lower lobe, performed to treat hepatic cysts, a subdiaphragmatic abscess, and a pleural abscess. Histopathological examination of the hepatic lesion revealed eosinophilic laminated material without identifiable protoscoleces, consistent with the laminated layer of E. multilocularis, surrounded by thick fibrous and granulation tissue, thereby establishing the diagnosis of hepatic echinococcosis (Fig. 2A). In the pleural specimens, laminated layer formation accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration was observed (Fig. 2B–D). The laminated layer was confined to the pleura and diaphragm, while no parasitic structures were identified within the lung parenchyma, suggesting direct pleural extension rather than hematogenous dissemination (Fig. 2B, C). Postoperative oral albendazole therapy was initiated with serial serological monitoring of antibodies against recombinant Em18. Dupilumab was discontinued after the diagnosis of AE without apparent exacerbation of atopic dermatitis.

Discussion

Alveolar echinococcosis (AE) is a parasitic infection characterized by slow, infiltrative lesion growth and a clinical course that often mimics hepatic malignancy. Epidemiological studies indicate that AE is typically diagnosed in middle-aged to older adults, most commonly between 50 and 70 years of age in endemic regions [4], [5], [6]. The rarity of AE in younger individuals reflects the prolonged incubation period between initial infection and the onset of clinical symptoms. When complete surgical resection is feasible, long-term survival rates are favorable, with reported 10-year survival approaching 99% [7], underscoring the importance of early diagnosis and timely surgical intervention.

The clinical course of AE is strongly influenced by host immune status. In early stages, immune responses may partially restrict lesion growth; however, over time, parasite persistence is facilitated by immune modulation, allowing slow but progressive tissue invasion. This delicate host–parasite balance enables prolonged asymptomatic infection but may be vulnerable to disruption under altered immune conditions [2], [8].

In advanced AE, extensive necrosis, calcification, and inflammatory reactions often lead to the disappearance of protoscoleces despite continued lesion expansion and local tissue destruction. Accordingly, the absence of identifiable protoscoleces does not necessarily indicate inactive or resolved disease. Previous immunological studies have suggested that such infiltrative growth may occur even in the absence of overt parasite viability, reflecting altered host inflammatory responses in advanced disease [9]. In the present case, the histopathological findings are consistent with advanced-stage AE; however, the precise immunological mechanisms underlying these pathological changes cannot be determined from a single clinical observation.

Dupilumab selectively inhibits interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 signaling and is widely used in the management of atopic diseases [10]. Although dupilumab is not considered a broad immunosuppressive, caution has been advised regarding its use in patients with potential helminth infections [11], [12]. In the context of AE, suppression of type 2 immune pathways may alter the established host-parasite immune equilibrium, potentially leading to an inflammatory destabilization of previously indolent lesions. Such changes may manifest clinically as enhanced inflammation, tissue necrosis, and extension to adjacent structures, rather than accelerated parasite proliferation.

Among biologic agents targeting type 2 immunity, dupilumab appears distinctive in that several reports have suggested a possible association with atypical inflammatory manifestations of AE. Jacquel et al. described an elderly immunocompromised patient receiving dupilumab in whom AE presented with systemic involvement, including hepatic and pulmonary lesions, and was considered to have progressed under interleukin-4/interleukin-13 blockade [13]. Although the clinical background differs, both the previously reported case and the current observation raise concern that targeted inhibition of type 2 immunity may influence the clinical behavior of latent E. multilocularis infection. In contrast, long-term concomitant use of omalizumab has been reported in a patient with hepatic AE without radiological progression following initiation of antiparasitic therapy [14], suggesting that different biologic agents may have distinct effects on disease stability. At present, it is not possible to conclude that the observed disease behavior is specific to dupilumab, given the limited number of reported cases. The available evidence remains insufficient to establish a drug-specific effect on the clinical course of AE.

Given the prolonged incubation period of AE and the advanced-stage pathological features, progression of a pre-existing infection appears more likely; however, in the absence of pre-dupilumab diagnostic data, primary infection cannot be completely excluded. It should also be acknowledged that the role of type 2 immunity in helminth infections is complex and not exclusively protective. In chronic parasitic infections, type 2–associated immune responses may contribute to parasite persistence and immune regulation rather than effective clearance. Accordingly, suppression of type 2 immune pathways does not necessarily imply enhanced parasite activity or disease progression. In the present case, the observed inflammatory exacerbation may therefore reflect a host-driven inflammatory response rather than parasite-driven progression. Moreover, pleural involvement can occur as part of the natural course of AE; nevertheless, the marked inflammatory exacerbation with pleural extension that became apparent during dupilumab therapy represents a clinically notable temporal association that warrants attention.

Conclusion

Taken together, these findings describe a clinically significant inflammatory exacerbation of AE that became apparent during dupilumab therapy, suggesting a temporal association in the setting of potentially altered host–parasite immune interactions; however, causality cannot be inferred from a single case. In the present case, the clinical deterioration may reflect destabilization of a long-standing subclinical lesion under an altered immune environment, but pleural extension may also represent disease progression over time, and the contribution of dupilumab remains uncertain. Although a causal relationship cannot be definitively established and the timing of parasite acquisition relative to dupilumab initiation remains unknown, this case highlights the importance of considering latent helminth infections and underlying disease activity when initiating dupilumab therapy in patients residing in endemic areas. Careful clinical monitoring and appropriate imaging evaluation may be warranted when new abdominal or thoracic symptoms arise during treatment.
