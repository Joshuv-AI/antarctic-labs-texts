Contentopic mapping in ventral and dorsal association cortex: The topographical organization of manipulable object information
Authors: J Almeida, S Kristensen, Z Tal, A Fracasso
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•We used population receptive field analysis to identify the topography of object information.•Contentopic maps representing mid-level object-related dimensions (e.g., object grasp) were observed in dorsal and ventral occipital cortex.•Contentopic maps are continuous, consistent across individuals, and specific to each dimension tested.•Contentopic maps are not fully derived from the eccentricity signatures of the visual stimuli used.•Topographic organization is a fundamental neural strategy for encoding complex, as well as simple sensory information.Keywords: Contentopy, Manipulable objects, Topography, Neural mapping, Mid-level dimensions, Conceptual organization

Introduction

A significant part of the life of an animal is spent on recognizing and identifying what is present in the environment in order to make informed decisions on essential issues such as foraging, mating, fighting, or fleeing. Object recognition became even more central for humans because our environment evolved beyond the natural world, and a myriad of human-made objects invaded our daily routines. A starting point for understanding how we recognize objects is focusing on how object-specific information is represented and organized in the brain (Almeida et al., 2023; Grill-Spector and Weiner, 2014; Mahon and Caramazza, 2011; Op de Beeck et al., 2008).

The best-known organizational principles of information in the brain are those present at primary sensorimotor cortices. For instance, visual cortex is organized by cortical columns that locally and continuously code for orientation of the visual stimulus (Yacoub et al., 2008). Moreover, individual cortical columns refer to particular locations in the visual field, with proximity in neural space mirroring proximity in the visual field. This organization musters a continuous and topographical map of the visual field across the different cortical columns that gradually follows retinal/visual field location of the stimulus (i.e., a retinotopic map; Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008; Sereno et al., 1995) (for similar topographical maps in other sensorimotor cortices see Formisano et al., 2003; Graziano and Aflalo, 2007).

Importantly, extant data on mid- and high-level object processing may point to a similar pattern of organization of object information in the brain. Work on non-human primates seems suggestive of a columnar organization within association cortex that is responsive to mid-level object-specific features such as complex shapes or combination of shapes, or object textures (Tanaka, 1996). Moreover, adjacent columns code for ever so slightly different mid-level features (Tanaka, 1996), suggesting similar topographical properties to those present in the columns within early visual cortex. Interestingly, recent work on object-driven human neural responses within association cortex showed that these regions code for object-related dimensions (Almeida et al., 2023; Arcaro et al., 2019; Bao et al., 2020; Coggan and Tong, 2023; Gomez et al., 2018; Hebart et al., 2023; Konkle and Caramazza, 2013; Malach et al., 2002; Op de Beeck et al., 2008). Examples of the dimensions tested include, among others, real object size (Konkle and Caramazza, 2013), shape curvature and spikiness (Bao et al., 2020; Coggan and Tong, 2023; Op de Beeck, Torfs, et al., 2008), or visual field preferences and eye fixation patterns on the processing of different object domains (Arcaro et al., 2019; Gomez et al., 2018; Malach et al., 2002). These attempts have revealed very large organizational mosaics and extremely coarse topographies, whereby different regions dedicated to different dimensions or categories have been proposed (e.g., regions that code for large objects that are physically separated from regions that code for small objects (Konkle and Caramazza, 2013). It is not clear whether this level of explanation and degree of topography is sufficient to understand the organization of object knowledge in the brain, or whether we need to inspect finer-grained organizational levels and look for true topographical maps – i.e., look for finer-grain continuous mapping of cortical preferences within the cortical surface as a function of the (continuous) levels of an object-related dimension.

One aspect that may have deterred finding such topographic maps relates to the nature and precise content of object-related dimensions, which are considerably more elusive than those of the sensorimotor dimensions that rule the organization of sensorimotor cortices. The complexity of the world dictates that contrary to tone frequency of a sound, or the eccentricity of a visually presented stimulus in the visual field, an individual object-related dimension may be harder to isolate from other object-related dimensions. That is, in the real world, the position of a hammer within the periphery or the center of the visual field (i.e., its eccentricity) can easily be separated from its location in relation to the vertical meridian (i.e., polar angle) or its orientation. However, the typical grasp of a hammer (a power grasp) may be decodable also, to some extent, from the overall size of the object, the amount of force needed to use the target object (e.g., precision grips may not be suited for high force grasps), or the weight of the object, among other mid-level (and potentially low-level) object properties. Nevertheless, and despite the fact that these dimensions are intertwined (Almeida et al., 2023; Hebart et al., 2023), we can still disentangle them – e.g., the size of an object and its typical grasp are different types of information that can be independently impaired in individuals with brain damage (Goodale et al., 1994; Goodale et al., 1991). This leads to the question of whether the organizational principles of sensorimotor cortices are also applicable to object-related information within association cortex. That is, are there topographic maps that follow object-related dimensions – by analogy, contentopic maps – as there are topographic maps that follow sensory dimensions (e.g., the eccentricity of a visual presented stimulus)?

Materials and methods

To test whether object-related dimensions are represented topographically in the same fashion as low-level visual dimensions are, we presented participants with manipulable object stimuli in sequences that followed the rank-order of those objects in their scores on a particular object-related dimension (Almeida et al., 2023) while acquiring functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) data. Specifically, we selected object-related dimensions that we obtained and validated previously for a set of manipulable objects based on the subjective similarity between those manipulable objects on how they are manipulated in order to be used (see Methods; Almeida et al., 2023). We chose the first two dimensions out of a 6-dimensional multidimensional scaling solution whose estimated distances between the tested objects were a good fit with the real distances (see Methods; Almeida et al., 2023). That is, while still noisy and lacking a definitive understanding of their nature and content, these dimensions are a relatively faithful description of the representational space we hold about how to manipulate objects. According to the labels provided by naïve participants, one of the selected dimensions refers to the type of grasp used when interacting with an object: power vs. precision grip (henceforth MD1; Almeida et al., 2023); the other dimension refers to the amount of force that is necessary to exert when interacting with an object: dexterity vs. force (henceforth MD2; Almeida et al., 2023). Because we selected only the first two dimensions, perceived proximity between objects in this putative 2D space (MD1 and MD2) should be understood in the context of the full 6D space. Moreover, and importantly, the names describing these dimensions, are, themselves, best approximations and may not fully capture the true underlying dimension, as they suffer from the noise inherent to the final multidimensional solution described above, as well as the necessarily subjective experience of the participants that labelled them. Nevertheless, at the level of analysis we are focusing here, these dimensions are sufficiently detailed and have been shown to track, behaviorally and neurally, relevant object processing (Almeida et al., 2023). Importantly, the values of these dimensions were independently validated as they are able to predict, in a continuous way, the responses of naïve participants to the target objects, as well as the neural signatures elicited by these objects (Almeida et al., 2023).

Importantly, different objects have different scores for each dimension, and these scores refer to the “amount” each object has in relation to each dimension (Almeida et al., 2023). Thus, objects can be rank-ordered in terms of their scores per dimension. We took advantage of this, and in an experimental run (e.g., referring to dimension MD1 – standing for the first Manipulation dimension), we cycled 6 times through the object stimuli rank-ordered by the target dimension (e.g., from objects that are grasped with a clear power grasp to objects that are grasped with a clear precision grip; see Fig. 1A & 1B, Figure S1, and Methods for information about the distribution of stimuli through a cycle as a function of the dimension being tested). What we are cycling through, then, are the different levels of the object-related target dimension, via the presentation of different objects according to the rank-order of their dimensional scores. What we are modeling is how cycling through these different dimensional levels elicits ordered changes in preferred responses in contiguous parts of the cortical surface in a continuous way. Objects were presented continuously and at the center of the screen. Importantly, MD1 and MD2 are uncorrelated (Almeida et al., 2023) (i.e., their correlation is 0), and thus object sequences for MD1 are unrelated to those for MD2.

Forty stimuli were presented per cycle, sampling the different levels of the dimension evenly. To achieve this, the 80 objects were first grouped into 20 bins (4 objects each) along the MD1/MD2 dimension. The predicted time series for the selected voxel was calculated by passing the dimension-related stimulus presentation through the gaussian model. The resulting time course was then convolved with the hemodynamic response function. This created different fMRI predicted time courses for the different neural models tested, per voxel. Final model parameters – µ and σ – are obtained by minimizing the difference between the predicted and the observed fMRI data.

Given the two dimensions selected, potential contentopic maps should be obtained primarily within posterior parietal and dorsal occipital cortical regions, from the intraparietal sulcus to more posterior areas within or around V3d/V3A, as these areas code for shape and 3D representations of objects and grasp planning (Cavina-Pratesi, Monaco, et al., 2010; Culham et al., 2003; Fattori et al., 2017; Freud et al., 2016; Georgieva et al., 2009). Moreover, aspects of object grasping that relate to typical object use (i.e., functional grasps; Gallivan et al., 2013; Knights et al., 2021; Valyear and Culham, 2010) and to coding of grip force and object weight that allows for the derivation of the amount of force to apply to an object (Gallivan et al., 2014; Keisker et al., 2010) have been shown to be associated with responses within lateral and ventral occipitotemporal cortex starting posteriorly within lingual gyrus and fusiform gyrus.

Participants

8 individuals (5 women; average age: 24; age range: 19–36) from the community of the University of Coimbra participated in the experiment. All participants were right-handed, had normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and were naive as to the experimental manipulations. The experiment was approved by the ethics committee of the Faculty of Psychology and Educational Sciences of the University of Coimbra and followed all ethical guidelines. Moreover, participants provided written informed consent and were compensated for their time by receiving either course credit on a major psychology course or financial compensation.

Stimuli

We used pictures of a set of 80 common manipulable objects previously used to determine and explore object dimensionality and for which we have scores in object-related mid-level dimensions (Almeida et al., 2023) (see Figure S1 for one exemplar of each object used). These were selected to be representative of the different types of objects that humans use routinely. We selected 3 exemplars per object type in a total of 240 images. Images were 400-by-400 pixel squares and subtended approximately 10° of the visual angle.

Object-related dimensions

We selected two mid-level object-related dimensions from those that were obtained and tested in behavioral and neural experiments previously in our lab (Almeida et al., 2023). To extract these dimensions, we analyzed how different objects relate to each other in a large conceptual space, and extracted the main axes that organize this conceptual multidimensional space. Specifically, we presented 60 participants with words referring to the target 80 manipulable objects and asked them to think about how similar these objects were in the manner in which we manipulated them. We used an object sorting task to derive dissimilarities between our set of objects - each participant was asked to sort all 80 objects into different piles such that objects in a pile were similar to each other, but different from objects in other piles, on the target knowledge type. The number of piles was decided by participant but had to be more than one and <80. This piling solutions were then entered in a participant-specific similarity matrix that reflected pile membership, and an averaged dissimilarity matrix was then created from the matrices of all the participants (Almeida et al., 2023).

We then applied non-metric multidimensional scaling (MDS; Torgerson, 1965) to the dissimilarity matrices obtained in order to extract key object-related dimensions that structure our object representational space. For details on the selection of the dimensions, the similarity matrices, and the behavioral and neural testing please refer to Almeida and Colleagues (Almeida et al., 2023). The two dimensions selected were the first dimensions, out of a 6-dimensional solution, that structured similarity in how we manipulate objects, and are the dimensions that explain most variance in these similarity judgements. The Kruskal stress value of the 6-dimensional solution was relatively low (Stress = 0084) suggesting that the estimated distances between the tested objects in this multidimensional solution were a good fit with the real distances. These dimensions are uncorrelated (Almeida et al., 2023). Moreover, a given value along the dimension gradient represents a relative position within the continuous coordinate space defined by the MDS solution. Thus, a zero value does not represent a boundary between dichotomous categorical states, but rather an intermediate value along a continuum. Hence, the value of zero is arbitrary, and does not hold particular significance.

Finally, these dimensions were labeled by another set of naïve participants as referring to the types of grasps applicable to the objects (MD1), and to the amount of force that is necessary to interact with an object (MD2; for details on all these experimental procedures and analysis please see Almeida and Colleagues; Almeida et al., 2023).

fMRI experimental design

The fMRI experiment consisted of 8 different sessions on different days. On day 1 and 2, dimension MD1 was presented in a particular order. On day 3 and 4, dimension MD1 was presented in reversed order in relation to sessions 1 and 2. On day 5 and 6, MD2was presented in a particular order, and on day 7 and 8 MD2 was presented in reversed order in relation to sessions 5 and 6. Each run included 6 cycles of stimuli presentation of a given dimension. To optimize run duration while maintaining balanced sampling across the dimensions, the objects were grouped into 20 bins (4 objects per bin) such that each bin corresponded to objects located near one another in the MD1/MD2 dimension (see Figure S1). At the beginning of each run (and independently for each run), 2 objects were randomly drawn from each bin to create a 40-object subset for cycle 1; the complementary 40 objects (the remaining 2 from each bin) were used in cycle 2. This randomization was replicated for the remaining pairs of cycles. This binning and subdivision strategy ensured that each cycle contained only half of the stimulus set, keeping cycle duration feasible, while evenly sampling the MD1 and MD2 continuum across the run, and importantly decoupled exemplar and object type from location in the dimension.

Specifically, for instance in an experimental run referring to dimension MD1, we cycled through the object stimuli rank-ordered by this dimension 6 times. In a cycle, we first presented objects (one at a time) that are clearly used with a power grasp (e.g., a shovel, followed by a weight, followed by a tennis racket, etc.; see Fig. 1A and Figure S1). These objects were then followed by objects whose grasp still falls closer to a power grasp than a precision grip (e.g., a grater, followed by a wooden spoon, followed by a computer mouse), and these were followed by objects whose grasp is increasingly closer to a precision grip (e.g., a fork, followed by a spray bottle, followed by a stamp). This cycle finished with objects that are clearly used with a precision grip (e.g., clothespin, tweezers and nail clipper).

Object images were presented for the duration of 1 TR (2 s). All runs were preceded and followed by 16 s of fixation. We used 3 different exemplar images per object which were counterbalanced across runs (exemplar 1 for run 1 and 3, exemplar 2 for run 2 and 5, and exemplar 3 for run 3 and 6). All participants completed all sessions and runs. Participants performed a simple task: they were instructed to maintain fixation on a yellow dot in the center of the image and press a button with index or middle finger when the dot changed color to red or green. Fingers and colors were counterbalanced across runs and sessions. The dot changed color on 10 % of the trials.

MRI acquisition

Scanning was performed with a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma-fit 3T MRI Scanner (Siemens Healthineers) with a 64-channel head coil at the University of Coimbra (Portugal; BIN - National Brain Imaging Network). Functional images were acquired with the following parameters: T2* weighted (single-shot/GRAPPA) echo-planar imaging pulse sequence, repetition time (TR) = 2000 ms, echo time (TE) = 30 ms, flip angle = 75°, 37 axial slices, acquisition matrix = 70×64 with field of view of 210×192 mm, and voxel size of 3 mm3. Structural T1-weighted images were obtained using a magnetization prepared rapid gradient echo (MPRAGE) sequence with the following parameters: TR = 2530 ms, TE = 3.5 ms, total acquisition time = 136 s, FA = 7°, acquisition matrix = 256×256, with field of view of 256 mm, and voxel size of 1 mm3.

Preprocessing of MRI data

Processing of the anatomical T1-weighted MR images was performed in Freesurfer image analysis suite (version 6.0, http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) using the recon-all pipeline, that included white and grey matter segmentation, skull removal, and cortical reconstruction. Pre-processing of functional MRI data was performed in AFNI (Cox, 1996; Cox and Hyde, 1997) and included the following steps: slice acquisition time correction, head motion correction, detrending and scaling. Following the initial pre-processing steps, the time-series of each dimension (MD1 and MD2) and order of presentation (ascending and descending dimensional values) were averaged across repetitions for each participant. The averaged functional image was co-registered to the anatomical scans, using the function 3dAllineate, using local Pearson correlation as the cost function.

Finally, to control for specific presentation parameters we detrended stimulus-specific information prior to data analysis. Specifically, for each stimulus we extracted the visual coordinates of the center-of-mass of the object. These parameters were averaged across all object-stimuli within a bin, and were detrended prior to data modelling (please see Supplementary Text 1 for details of the detrending step). All data and plots presented herein refer to data over which we removed the contribution of these types of information. Note, however, that our results were virtually indistinguishable, with or without removing these contributions.

Population receptive field analysis (pRF) and topography

The pRF analysis is a forward modelling approach aimed at accounting for the observed BOLD time series in each individual voxel by estimating parameters at the neuronal population level (Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008; Fabius et al., 2022; Fracasso et al., 2016; Harvey et al., 2013; Kristensen et al., 2021). pRF parameters estimates were obtained from percent BOLD time series by fitting a linear model via an iterative procedure, similar to previous applications in the domains of vision, sensorimotor integration and quantity/numerosity perception. (1), p(t) represents the predicted BOLD signal, β is a scaling factor necessary to match the prediction to the observed data, defined in arbitrary units (A.U.), and e is measurement error, representing the departure from the estimated and the observed BOLD. The fitting was performed via the General Linear Model approach (GLM), using ordinary-least-square estimation (OLS).

We obtained the prediction p(t) starting from a parameterized model of the stimulus sequence. To parametrize the stimuli sequence, we used a Gaussian tuning function (see Eq. (2)) with the neuronal parameters (location, µ; width, σ) arranged in a two-dimensional grid. Gaussian location parameter (µ) was allowed to vary between −0.6 and 0.6 in 20 steps. Gaussian width (σ) was allowed to vary between 0.05 and 0.25 in 18 steps, for a total of 360 predictions.

For each voxel’s percent BOLD time series, the fit was performed with an iterative procedure. On each iteration the predictor was built based on the selected width and location parameters. The predictor was set as the independent variable of a general linear model with the percent BOLD time series as the dependent variable. For each iteration we computed model goodness-of-fit, measured with R2. The set of neuronal parameters (location, µ; width, σ) that maximized R2 were assigned to the individual voxel.

It is important to note that in this modelling approach, the estimated parameters (Gaussian location - µ; and width - σ) are connected to local features of the percent BOLD time series. This approach facilitates the following analyses (statistical and topographical), allowing to assess the distribution of specific features of the percent BOLD time series across different experimental conditions and ROIs.

Definition of ROIs

For each individual, we defined the regions of interest (ROIs) based on a leave-one-out procedure. First, we projected individual pRF parameter maps (R2, location and width) onto standard surface space using the AFNI function 3dVol2Surf.

For participant x, we averaged and visualized the remaining R2 maps across the n-1 participants. The resulting map was threshold above 10 % R2. We then ran a clustering algorithm at the surface level to remove small clusters of activation that survive the thresholding (minimum cluster size: 25mm2). The outcome of this operation yielded R2 maps with two main clusters that were then used to delineate two ROIs per hemisphere: a dorsal-occipital ROI (d-occ) and a ventral occipital ROI (v-occ). The d-occ and v-occ ROIs were then projected back on the left out individual x for subsequent analysis. We repeated this leave-one-out procedure for the 8 individual participants, thus defining independent ROIs. The individual ROIs are presented in Figures S4 and S5 along with the individual pRF maps. Please note that all the analyses presented in the current manuscript were performed on the native and topologically correct individual participant space.

Compared to standard volumetric approaches, the standard surface maps approach offers an advantage in terms of between participant co-registration at the cortical level (Argall et al., 2006). Surface-based mapping relies on matching between common anatomical features such as sulci and gyri, and it preserves the local topology of the individual cortical sheet at a single hemisphere level. Thus, this procedure is more accurate than what could be obtained by classical volumetric-based registrations between individuals.

For visualization purposes of group averaged pRF maps (i.e., the ROIs presented in Figs. 2B and 4A), we averaged the individual ROIs on the common space. Please see Tables S1 and S2, as well as Figure S3 for the location of these averaged ROIs.

Permutation testing

Within the ROIs, the best modelling parameters across participants differed between object-related dimensions MD1 and MD2 (Fig. 2C). Specifically, MD1 yielded more positive tuning location estimates and narrower tuning width compared to MD2. From an fMRI perspective, the only difference between MD1 and MD2 is the temporal order in which the visual stimuli are presented. It might be argued that the observed differences between MD1 and MD2 modelling parameters could be simply due to a response to specific objects within the sequence, presented at different times during each dimension-specific sequence.

To test for this possibility, we extracted 12 objects around the average location estimate of MD1 and MD2 (i.e., 3 bins), separately. We observed an overlap of 2 out of 12 objects between MD1 and MD2 (16.6 % of objects overlap). We asked whether this overlap would be sufficient to drive the observed difference between MD1 and MD2 average location estimates. To assess this, we run a permutation test, where we obtained the null distribution of the percentage of object overlap between 12 elements extracted separately from two shuffled sequences of 80 objects. If obtaining a 16.6 % overlap would be extremely rare, then it could be argued that the difference between MD1 and MD2 average location estimates might be driven by object overlap per se. If 16.6 % would fall within the expected range of overlap, then it would be less likely that the object overlap would account for the MD1 and MD2 location difference.

Permutation results indicate that the 95 % confidence interval of the null distribution ranges between 0 % and 33 %, with a median of 16.6 %. That is, in 95 % of the repetitions, the number of overlapping objects between the two sequences ranged between 0 % and 33 % (see Figure S6). In our data, we observe an overlap of 16.6 % between the objects around the average location parameter of MD1 and MD2 – which falls within the 95 % confidence interval of the null distribution. Thus, the number of objects overlap derived from our data is not different from what we would expect by chance. Hence, the difference in tuning parameters we observe is unlikely to be accounted for by the number of overlapping objects falling around the estimated mean tuning locations.

Analysis of the orientation of the contentopic maps

A topographic map is a gradual and ordered representation of an estimated variable over the human cortical or subcortical location (e.g., visual eccentricity; Wandell et al., 2007). To test for the presence of topographical maps at the individual participant level we proceeded as follows: for each participant’s ROIs and hemisphere we computed the node-to-node geodesic distance from each node in one ROI – the adjacency matrix, derived from the distance metric, dist – geodesic distance, between two mesh vertices xi and xj:

Then we computed the spectral decomposition of the adjacency matrix and selected the first eigenvector of the decomposition (vector 1 – v1) (Lombaert et al., 2011). This vector represents a map of cortical distance over the ROI along its longest axis, where each point in the map is the geodesic distance of a node along the selected axis with respect to 0 – the middle of the ROI along its longest axis. The second eigenvector of the decomposition (vector 2 – v2) represents a map of cortical distance over the ROI along its shorter axis.

Vector 1 and vector 2 effectively represent a 2D mapping over the cortical surface. These are used for example in surface-flattening approaches. In this application we iteratively rotated the 2D mapping with respect to its original axis in 24 steps using an 2D matrix rotation of the desired angle (in radians), multiplied by the original 2D mapping coordinates (Eq. (4)). Note that this operation does rotate the vectors in surface space but does not shift the center of the vectors.

For each iteration, this operation resulted in a rotated version of vector 1 and vector 2 (resulting in rotated vectors – v1r v2r). We used vector 1 as our reference for the subsequent analysis steps.

For each participant, ROI, dependent variable (pRF location and pRF width) and dimension tested (MD1 and MD2), each of the 24 rotations of vector 1 map were projected in the single participant anatomical space using the AFNI function 3dSurf2Vol. We compared the linear progression between the rotated vector 1 map and the estimated dependent variable. The comparison between the rotated vector 1 and the variable of interest was performed using simple linear regression. The obtained t-statistic was stored for subsequent analysis.

For each participant, ROI, dependent variable (pRF location and pRF width) and experimental condition (MD1 and MD2) the best orientation characterizing the map was defined as the one yielding the highest t-statistic among the 24 possible rotations (linear fit – topography, see main manuscript, Fig. 4B). Following this procedure, we obtained the best rotations capturing the gradual representation of the given estimated variable (pRF location and pRF width) over each participant human cortical surface and ROI. We compared the obtained highest t-statistic for each participant and ROI with those obtained from a control ROI of similar size derived from each individual dorso-lateral cortex (ctrl ROI, main manuscript, Figs. 2B and 4B). We focused on the dorso-lateral cortex because based on the current literature and a general understanding of the human visual system, this region is unlikely to be sensitive to visual contrast. Moreover, it does not prefer manipulable objects over other categories of objects, and it is not majorly involved in visuomotor processing. We first defined the crtl ROIs manually and bilaterally. This was performed in the common cortical surface and then transformed them into individual volumetric space. Care was taken to keep ROI volume similar between the crtl ROI and the main ROIs.

We opted for a linear model for theoretical (i) and computational (ii) reasons. (i) Theoretically, topographic maps are, for the most part, arranged linearly over the cortical surface, with a notable exception being tonotopic maps showing a non-linear progression along the Heschl’s gyrus (Da Costa et al., 2011). Somatotopic maps, although showing a discontinuity necessary to cover the two-dimensional body over a quasi-1D strip of cortex (pre-central and post-central gyrus), are largely arranged linearly when taking one effector at a time. For example, the hand shows an ordered progression of finger representation over the cortical surface (Schellekens et al., 2018). Thus, at the very least, linear models would be best approximations for a first level analysis of contentopy; (ii) From a computational perspective, adding non-linear components would increase the likelihood of overfitting, thus potentially capturing noise variability instead of ‘true’ underlying map features.

pRF analysis of retinotopy and eccentricity of the stimuli

Because we are testing a set of topographical maps within putative visual regions, we need to ensure the topographical dimensions we are characterizing cannot be fully accounted for by known retinotopy – the main topographic organization principle in those areas. This is especially true within mid- and lower-level visual cortical regions.

To directly assess whether the contentopic maps we observed could be accounted for by the known underlying retinotopic organization of the visual cortex, we fitted contrast-based population receptive fields (2D pRFs) based on the visual stimuli presented for MD1 and MD2. We implemented pRFs using 3 parameters: x spatial position, y spatial position and size.

We built predicted times series based on the interaction between the sparse presentation of visual stimuli in MD1 and MD2, and the target pRF, chosen iteratively among a bank of possible pRFs from a [10×10] grid covering visual space and 12 possible pRF sizes, for a total of 1200 possible pRFs.

To provide a fair comparison between the retinotopy model and the Gaussian modelling approach, we averaged the gray-scale images every two TRs (as done in the Gaussian modelling approach). We then computed the local contrast energy for every pixel of this averaged image and obtained the corresponding predicted time series for each image sequence. We then averaged predicted time series with the same pRF features (size and position), resulting in the set of predictions used to fit our MD1 and MD2 time series.

We iteratively fitted all predicted time series on our observed data and assigned the best prediction (based on least-square estimates) to each voxel. Finally, we averaged the pRF features obtained for the two dimensions (MD1 and MD2) and stimuli sequence (regular and reverse) to obtain one retinotopic map per participant.

Note that the extent of activation for the 2D pRF model (Eccentricity) appears to be less than that for the Gaussian pRF models for our dimensions (MD1, MD2; Fig. 5). This is due to the differences between the models. i) In the 2D pRF model, the fovea is always stimulated, with visual stimuli presented consistently in the center of the screen. As a result of constant stimulation in the fovea, no signal change is expected. Additionally, ii) due to cortical magnification, the fovea is overrepresented in the cortex. The combination of these factors (i) and (ii) leads to differences in the extent of activation between the 2D pRF model (Eccentricity) and the Gaussian pRF models (MD1, MD2).

We then tested two other models as alternative control models to our main gaussian model to assess how our model fares in relation to alternatives. Specifically, we used a (i) a linear tuning function that predicts monotonic increase or decrease in neural response as a function of dimensional values; and (ii) a location-only model, implemented as a Gaussian with a fixed narrow tuning width (σ = 0.05). This narrow width was chosen as the smallest value in our fitting procedure, reflecting a maximally selective response to a single location, with zero or near-zero response for all other locations. We use adjusted R² values to compare model performance.

High-definition eccentricity maps extracted from the 7T HCP dataset

To further assess whether the contentopic maps we observed could be accounted for by underlying retinotopic organization of the visual cortex, we used the Human Connectome Project 7 Tesla retinotopy dataset (Benson et al., 2018). This high-resolution dataset includes functional data and pRF solutions obtained from 181 participants (6 runs per participant, with spatial resolution of 1.6-mm isotropic and 1-s TR). For visualization and further analysis (cross-validation, see below), we used the results of the group average S184 (in which the pRF solutions were calculated by fitting the model to the averaged time series across all 181 participants), as this dataset provide high signal-to-noise ratio and reliable retinotopic maps, especially in early visual areas (with variance explained values above 75 %).

For detailed information about the stimuli, acquisition and analysis methods of this dataset, please refer to Benson and Colleagues (Benson et al., 2018).

Leave-one-out cross-validation to test for the robustness of contentopic maps

To test for the consistency of the contentopic maps within a given dimension (MD1 or MD2) and to further test their distinctiveness from the other dimension and the eccentricity organization known to dominated regions within our ROIs, we applied a leave-one-participant-out cross-validation analysis for each ROI. For each dimension, we conducted 4 different cross-validation procedures in surface space:

a)Within-dimension condition - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged location map of that same dimension (e.g., MD1) of N-1 participants.

Within-dimension condition - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged location map of that same dimension (e.g., MD1) of N-1 participants.

b)Between-dimension condition - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged location map of N-1 participants of the other dimension (e.g., MD2).

Between-dimension condition - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged location map of N-1 participants of the other dimension (e.g., MD2).

c)Stim-based eccentricity - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged eccentricity map of N-1 participants that was obtained from the same set of visual stimuli and fMRI data used to compute contentopic maps.

Stim-based eccentricity - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the averaged eccentricity map of N-1 participants that was obtained from the same set of visual stimuli and fMRI data used to compute contentopic maps.

d)HCP-based eccentricity - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the high-definition eccentricity maps extracted from the HCP dataset.

HCP-based eccentricity - for each iteration we compared the left-out participant location map on the target dimension (e.g., MD1) with the high-definition eccentricity maps extracted from the HCP dataset.

To ensure that the cross-validation procedure included a consistent shared number of nodes between the N-1 average and the left-out participant surface, we selected nodes based on the averaged map that was tested at each step (i.e. MD1 or MD2 N-1 average location maps, stim-based N-1 average eccentricity maps, and HCP-based eccentricity maps) and exceeded 50th percentile of variance explained. Prior to the fitting procedure, the values of the left-out participant and the comparing maps were binned into 0.5 % quantile-based intervals (200 bins, based on the selected comparing map) and averaged within each bin. Please note that because the location values (of the MD1 and MD2 maps) and eccentricity values (of the contrast-based and HCP retinotopy maps) have different dynamic ranges (i.e. MD1 and MD2 range between −0.4 and 0.4, whereas eccentricity ranges between 0 and 10), the range of the cross-validation slope values differed based on the specific used dataset. Thus, to allow for meaningful comparisons across datasets, regardless of their initial dynamic ranges, the averaged bin values of the comparing maps were standardized prior the cross-validation procedure.

We assessed the similarity between the averaged map of N-1 participants and the left-out participant map using a linear regression model, storing the model goodness-of-fit (R2 – explained variance) and the model slope (beta coefficient). We report the model goodness-of-fit and model slope, as the linear model could yield high explain variance (R2) estimates even if the comparison maps and the left-out participant map were going in the opposite direction. Reporting the model slope resolves this issue, as the sign of the slope indicates the direction of the relation between maps. A negative slope indicates estimates in opposite direction, a positive slope indicates estimates along the same direction.

We then computed 4 separate 4-by-2 ANOVAs one per each object-related dimension (MD1 and MD2) and fitting parameters (Variance explained – i.e., R2 – and slope values), with prediction-type (within-dimension, between-dimension, Stim-based eccentricity, and HCP-based eccentricity) and ROI (d-occ, and v-occ) as factors. We did so to test for differences in prediction ability of the different conditions (please see Supplementary Text 2 for details on the rationale of this analysis). We then performed simple paired t-tests (Bonferroni corrected) to explore the effects of the different ANOVAs. As we only obtained effects of prediction-type across all ANOVAs, we computed simple t-test collapsing across ROIs. For the R2 values, we compared the within-dimension prediction condition to the other three prediction conditions to test the hypothesis that the within-dimension condition explained individual contentopic maps over and above all other conditions. Note that for these t-tests, the critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value was p < 0.016 (i.e., 0.05/3). For the slope values we compared each condition against zero to test the direction the relationship between the different averaged maps and the individual contentopic maps. Note that for these t-tests, the critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value was p < 0.0125 (i.e., 0.05/4).

Results

Modeling of object-related dimensions

To capture the systematic variation of fMRI signal elicited by the sequence of objects presented, as a proxy to the varying dimensional scores, we adapted standard population receptive field analysis (pRF) typically used to unravel the organization of low-level visual information (e.g., eccentricity; Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008), and applied it to our manipulable object dimensions. Specifically, we created one-dimensional gaussian pRFs to model neural signal (Fig. 1C). The model had two parameters: one that coded for the preferred location in the dimension (i.e., which values of the dimension are preferred by the neural population being inspected); and one that coded the tuning width of the response (i.e., the specificity and range of dimensional values the neural population responds to). That is, we modeled preferred level of the target dimension (i.e., preferred location) across the cortical surface using a 1D gaussian with different tuning widths. Importantly, we show that this model reliably captures signal fluctuations as a function of the dimensional scores of the objects that were presented (goodness-of-fit: R2), accounting for the time course of different voxels by different preferred dimensional tunings and width values (Fig. 2A, voxels in insets 1 to 3).

We then tested how our model fares in relation to other potential alternative models. Specifically, we tested a purely linear model that predicts monotonical increases/decreases of neural response as a function of the dimensional scores (i.e., a linear model), as well as a simpler gaussian model that exclusively modeled preferred location in the dimension, using a fixed narrow tuning width (σ=0.05; effectively resulting in a location-only model). Our original 1D gaussian model with 2 parameters (i.e., preferred location in the dimension and tuning width) outperformed the other models in explaining the data (see Figure S2). As such, we focused exclusively on this model in all subsequent analysis exploring topographical representations of object knowledge.

Comparing the tuning of the neural responses to the target object-related dimensions

In order to test for topography, we selected participant-specific cortical regions where our model robustly accounted for neural responses (R2 > 10 %, approximately corresponding to p < 10−7, uncorrected). This led to the definition of two bilateral regions of interest (ROIs): a dorsal occipital area (d-occ) around the dorsal aspects of the middle occipital sulcus and gyrus, including voxels in the vicinity of V3A (Georgieva et al., 2009), and encompassing regions LO1, V3d (and parts of V3B), V2d and V1d and V1v, as defined in the cortical parcellation by Wang and collaborators (Wang et al., 2015) (see Figure S3 for the relationship between our ROIs and available atlases such as those proposed by Destrieux et al., 2010, Glasser et al., 2016, and Wang et al., 2015); and a ventral area around lingual gyrus, inferior occipital gyrus, and occipitotemporal cortex, encompassing V3v, V2v, hV4, and V1v as defined in the same cortical parcellation (v-occ; Fig. 2B, Tables S1 and S2 for areas included in the ROIs and MNI coordinates; Figure S3). Thus, the individual ROIs are generally within the regions expected to be tuned to these dimensions (Cavina-Pratesi, Monaco, et al., 2010; Culham et al., 2003; Fattori et al., 2017; Freud et al., 2016; Gallivan et al., 2014,; 2013; Georgieva et al., 2009; Goodale et al., 1994; Keisker et al., 2010; Knights et al., 2021; Valyear and Culham, 2010), but also largely extend into early visual cortex more posteriorly than predicted. However, other cortical locations were engaged by the continuous presentation of objects in the orders dictated by different dimensions. Specifically, variations in the scores of MD2 were also accounted for in bilateral regions in lateral temporal cortex (MNI coordinates for the left ROI −40, −70, −7, and for the right ROI 43, −69, −15). Finally, inspection of individual maps for both MD1 and MD2 demonstrates that most participants (6 out of 8; Figures S4 & S5) also showed responsive regions in posterior parietal cortex, but these cortical regions did not survive the strict inclusion criteria used.

We then focused on further understanding the topographical organization of the object-related dimensions of interest. MD1 and MD2 showed similar average goodness-of-fit of the pRF models across participants in d-occ and v-occ (Fig. 2C, top inset), and these average goodness-of-fit values were much higher than those observed in a control region (Fig. 2C, top inset; see Methods for the selection of the control region). Centrally, MD1 and MD2 differed in terms of average preferred location and width (i.e., the parameters of our pRF models) even though they engaged similar neural populations. This contrasts with the lack of a difference in location and width estimates for each of the dimensions in the control region (Fig. 2C, middle and bottom insets).

Importantly, differences in average preferred location for each dimension at the two ROIs could not be explained by an overlap in the objects whose scores are within these average preferred locations for each dimension – the amount of object overlap around the average location estimated for MD1 and MD2 does not differ from what would be obtained by chance (permutation testing; see Methods; see Figure S6). This means that this effect reflects a specific preference for the particular target dimension (MD1 or MD2) and the dimension-specific sequence. Moreover, we observed a reliable difference in average width estimates between MD1 and MD2 within our ROIs. Specifically, MD1 shows more specificity – i.e., its tuning is sharper – than MD2. These patterns of results speak in favor of the reliability and sensitivity of our estimates.

Object-related dimensions are arranged topographically

In the next step, we investigated the presence of contentopic maps – i.e., topographic maps that show a gradual and continuous progression of preferred dimensional scores over the cortical surface. We rendered preferred location maps for each dimension over the individual cortical surface (see Fig. 3A for an example of an individual contentopic map for MD1). To estimate the topographic organization of the maps, and to define their main axis, we conducted the following analysis: for each participant, each ROI, and each hemisphere, we computed a cortical distance map over the ROI along its longest axis, where 0 represents the middle of the ROI along this axis (Fig. 3B left; see Methods for details). Each cortical distance map was rotated in 24 steps, yielding 24 cortical orientation maps (Fig. 3B). We iteratively fitted each cortical orientation map with the preferred location map, for each dimension, to obtain the best linear relationship between the two. (Fig. 3C). We then selected the orientations with the best fits per participant, dimension and ROI (Fig. 3C, inset 2). Thus, these orientation maps capture the main axis of the contentopic maps.

Fig. 4A shows the average topographic progression and the average best orientation of the linear fits for dimensions MD1 and MD2. In both dorsal and ventral ROIs, we observed reliable topographical progression of preferred location within the target dimensions across the cortical surface – i.e., we obtained contentopic maps (see Figure S4 & S5 for individual best orientations of the linear fit). To quantify contentopic organization, we first showed that the best fits across participants are significantly stronger in d-occ and v-occ for both MD1 and MD2, when compared to those in the control region where overall poor goodness-of-fit was observed (Fig. 4B). This demonstrates that there is a linear progression along the cortical surface as a function of the score of the objects in each of the dimensions.

Robustness of the contentopic maps obtained

Finally, we tested the robustness of our fits for each dimension and each ROI via leave-one-participant-out cross-validation at the cortical surface level in terms of their relative independence from the other object-related dimension and known organizational principles of the ROIs involved. If contentopic maps are consistent across participants and relatively independent from other dimensions, then we should be able to predict the contentopic map of one of the participants from the contentopic maps of the remaining participants within the tested dimension, over and above our ability to predict the contentopic map of that participant from any other maps. Specifically, we used this cross-validation approach to test map consistency and specificity with respect to the other object-related dimension (MD1-to-MD2 and vice-versa), and the eccentricity map characterizing the underlying retinotopic organization in early visual cortex.

We extracted participant-specific contentopic maps for MD1 and for MD2, and computed averaged contentopic maps for MD1 and for MD2 over the remaining participants in all possible folds. Moreover, we obtained two sets of eccentricity maps – eccentricity maps based on the eccentricity signatures of the stimuli we used to elicit our contentopic maps, and eccentricity maps from an established retinotopic database (the Human Connectome Project; HCP; Benson et al., 2018; Uğurbil et al., 2013; Van Essen et al., 2013). The reason we focus on eccentricity is two-fold: 1) eccentricity has been shown to be a major contributor to the organization of information well beyond low-level visual cortex (e.g., category-selective areas; Arcaro et al., 2019; Gomez et al., 2018; Malach et al., 2002); and 2) perhaps related with the elongated nature of manipulable objects (Almeida et al., 2014; Fabbri et al., 2016), eccentricity of a stimulus may be an important proxy for manipulation-specific object-related properties potentially reflected in MD1 and MD2.

To obtain eccentricity maps from our own stimuli, we created typical 2D gaussian pRF models (Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008) to capture signal fluctuations as a function of the contrast-energy over time of the stimuli used in the experiment. That is, we followed pRF methods exactly as they are regularly employed in the literature to model retinotopy (Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008), coded stimulus as contrast energy over time, performed 2D pRF fitting, and obtained participant-specific retinotopic maps based on the contrast energy of our stimuli. Note that this 2D gaussian pRF model partially account for the observed responses at the individual voxel level (average R2 = 0.13). This shows that, as expected, the retinotopic signatures typical of these regions are present in our data. For the high-definition eccentricity maps, we used the established HCP 7T fMRI retinotopic database and extracted an eccentricity map, applied to the ROIs derived from our measurement (see Figs. 2 and 5A-D).

We then tested, for each object-related dimension separately (i.e., MD1 or MD2), whether and how the left-out participant, dimension-specific contentopic map (i.e., individual participant MD1 or MD2 maps) could be accounted for by 1) the average within-dimension contentopic maps of the remaining participants – i.e., Within-dimension condition (e.g., participant-specific contentopic maps for MD1 being predicted by the average contentopic MD1 maps of the remaining participants) testing whether contentopic maps for a dimension are consistent across participants; 2) the average between-dimension contentopic maps of the remaining participants - i.e., Between-dimension condition (e.g., participant-specific contentopic maps for MD1 being predicted by the average contentopic MD2 maps of the remaining participants) testing whether contentopic maps for each dimension are different from each other; 3) the average eccentricity maps computed over the stimuli used to elicit contentopic maps – i.e., Stim-based eccentricity condition (e.g., participant-specific contentopic maps for MD1 being predicted by the average eccentricity maps of the remaining participants computed over the exact stimuli used to elicit MD1 maps) testing whether contentopic maps are exhausted by the eccentricity signatures of the stimuli used; and 4) the high-definition eccentricity map obtained from an establish retinotopic database – i.e., HCP-based eccentricity condition (e.g., participant-specific contentopic maps for MD1 being predicted by the eccentricity map derived from HCP) testing whether the contentopic maps are exhausted by eccentricity information. We obtained linear fit values (R2 values) as well slope values of the linear fits, for each of these four conditions, per dimension (MD1 and MD2) per ROI.

We analyzed these data in 4 separate 4-by-2 ANOVAs, one per each object-related dimension (MD1 and MD2) and fitting parameters (variance explained – i.e., R2 – and slope values), with prediction-type (Within-dimension, Between-dimension, Stim-based eccentricity, and HCP-based eccentricity) and ROI (d-occ, and v-occ) as factors. We obtained a main effect of prediction-type in all ANOVAs (see Table S4 for individual statistics, including Cohen’s d effect-size measures). These results indicate that both R2 and slope values are significantly modulated by prediction-type, across both ROIs. We then performed simple t-tests (using Bonferroni correction; collapsing ROIs) to explore the main effect of prediction-type on R2 and slope values, for each dimension – for R2 values we compared the within-dimension prediction condition to the other three prediction conditions (between-dimension, Stim-based eccentricity, and HCP-based eccentricity) to test the hypothesis that the within-dimension condition explained individual contentopic maps over and above all other conditions; for the slope values we compared each condition against zero to test the direction of the relationship between the different averaged maps and the individual contentopic maps. Below we present these results.

Object-related dimensions distinctively engage similar cortical locations

For the R2 values, Fig. 5E and F (R2 graph) show that the topographic progression of MD1 and MD2 location maps were consistent across participants, such that we can reliably predict the map on the left-out individual based on the average of the remaining participants. Importantly, within-dimension prediction was significantly better than between-dimension prediction (for MD1, within- vs between-dimension: t (7) = 3.887, p = 0.006; for MD2, within- vs between-dimension: t (7) = 4.118, p = 0.005; critical Bonferroni corrected p-value: 0.0167). This shows, unequivocally, that these contentopic maps are dimension-specific – i.e., contentopic maps for MD1 and MD2 are distinct from one another.

We then analyzed the slope of the linear fits for the leave-one-participant-out cross-validation described above. For the maps to be consistent across participants, they have to match in orientation, and most importantly, in the direction of the linear progression. In fact, simply matching in orientation could mean that the maps are in exact opposite directions. Thus, positive slopes indicate that the maps tested follow a similar linear progression. Fig. 5E and F (slope graphs) show that the slope of the within-dimension cross-validation linear fit for MD1 and for MD2 is positive (for MD1: t (7) = 6.003, p = 0.0005; for MD2: t (7) = 5.977, p = 0.0006), whereas the slope of the between-dimension prediction were not significantly different from zero (for MD1: t (7) = 2.994, p = 0.02; MD2: t (7) = 2.015, p = 0.084; critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value: 0.0125 for the slope t-tests; same results were obtained with a Wilcoxon test, see table S4). This shows that, within each dimension (MD1 or MD2), individual maps can be predicted from the maps of the remaining individuals both in orientation and direction of its topographical progression (for an analysis of the cross-validation results using a multiple linear model with the different prediction-types, please see Figure S7). This demonstrates the reliability of the maps obtained for each dimension at the individual participant level, and, importantly, the independence of the contentopic maps for the two different dimensions.

Are object-related contentopic maps accounted for by retinotopic responses?

Furthermore, we tested whether contentopic maps relate to underlying retinotopic organization. That is, as our ROIs span retinotopically-organized cortex, we need to demonstrate that our MD1 and MD2 maps are relatively distinct from the underlying retinotopic maps. As described above, we tested whether and to what extent our MD1 and MD2 contentopic maps could be fully accounted for by retinotopic – and in particular eccentricity – responses (elicited by the stimuli we used in our experiments, or obtained from the HCP database; Benson et al., 2018; Uğurbil et al., 2013; Van Essen et al., 2013). That is, we wanted to test whether the underlying eccentricity map characterizing retinotopic responses to the stimuli used could be the driver of the maps we observed. Please note that this test is potentially agnostic as to the more interesting and far-reaching question of the computational relationship between mid-level contentopic maps and low-level retinotopic and eccentricity maps (for similar discussions see Arcaro et al., 2019).

Once again, as can be seen in Fig. 5E and F (R2 graphs), within-dimension average contentopic maps can predict participant-specific contentopic maps over and above eccentricity maps – irrespective of whether they are participant-specific eccentricity maps derived from the same exact data and stimuli used to elicit contentopic maps (for MD1, within-dimension condition vs. Stim-based eccentricity: t (7) = 3.144, p = 0.016; for MD2, within-dimension condition vs. Stim-based eccentricity: t (7) = 3.979, p = 0.005; critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value: 0.0167), or whether they are extracted from available retinotopic databases (for MD1, within-dimension condition vs. HCP-based eccentricity: t (7) = 5.727, p = 0.0007; for MD2, within-dimension condition vs. HCP-based eccentricity: t (7) = 5.486, p = 0.0009; critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value: 0.0167)

This demonstrates that our contentopic maps are related to neural responses (and are dependent on information) that are beyond those that can be attributed to eccentricity – that is, within dimension contentopic maps truly outperform other maps including eccentricity, and thus contentopy is better characterized by the object-related dimension itself rather than fully or largely driven by eccentricity information. Having said that, part of the variability of MD1 (but MD2) could be explained by eccentricity through a negative relationship between MD1 location values and eccentricity values (Fig. 5E, slope graphs; t (7) = −6.506, p = 0.001; critical Bonferroni-corrected p-value: 0.0125). Specifically, this could be suggestive of a potential relationship between eccentricity and the MD1 contentopic map, whereby increases in eccentricity (more peripheral stimulation) may correlate with objects that are more dependent on power-grasps. This potentially signals the importance of exploring both the handles of objects (perhaps more prominent in power grasp objects) and the functional tip of these objects.

While eccentricity per se cannot fully account for our results, we cannot exclude that the maps reported could be affected by the interaction between low-level visual features characterizing the objects presented. However, it is also important to consider that low-level visual features do not need to be necessarily treated as nuisance variables, as they can play a fundamental role in object recognition and provide information about the object function.

Discussion

Previous studies have consistently demonstrated topographical mapping within sensorimotor cortices over particular dimensions that mirror response properties of the corresponding sensory receptor (e.g., location in the retina; sound frequency in the cochlea). This topographical organization is a central feature of sensory processing and dictates how we perceive and interact with the world (Groen et al., 2022). Here we show that topographical organization is also present for information pertaining to objects. Particularly, we obtained contentopic maps over object-related action dimensions (e.g., grasp type) – i.e., neural responses within dorsal and ventral occipital cortex are tuned to the two mid-level object-related dimensions tested independently. These topographic maps are continuous, in that they progress through the cortical surface gradually as function of the level of the target dimension; are consistent across participants, in that maps of individual participants are predictable from the maps of the remaining individuals; are specific for each dimension, in that topographic maps for one of the dimensions cannot be predicted from the maps of the other dimension; and are relatively independent from underlying retinotopy, as participant-specific eccentricity maps and eccentricity maps from HCP data are significantly worse at accounting for contentopic maps than contentopy itself.

A remarkable aspect of our data is the independence of MD1 and MD2 maps, as showcased by the cross-validation analysis. To elicit contentopic mapping for MD1 and MD2, we used the same stimuli, with the only difference being that we presented these stimuli in different orders as dictated by the scores of each object in each dimension. Moreover, mapping was tested over the same neural population. Notwithstanding, contentopic pRF modeling of the neural responses (i.e., the modeling parameters of location and width) varied significantly by dimension. The computational separability of the neural tuning for these two dimensions suggests some level of topographic flexibility in how cortical regions process object-related information. Neural populations are capable of picking up different informational properties of an object and encode them independently in a superimposed way. Having considered this, we cannot exclude that the maps reported could be affected by the low-level visual features characterizing the objects presented. However, it is also important to consider that low-level visual features do not need to be necessarily treated as nuisance variables, as they can play a fundamental role in object recognition and provide information about the object function.

Action dimensions are topographically represented in dorsal and ventral occipital cortex

The ROIs that show contentopy for the two target dimensions are part of visual association cortex. Importantly, while the content of the dimensions used herein still needs to be fully fleshed out, and the labels used are but approximations based on subjective judgements of naïve participants, generally, these dimensions describe object manipulation space. In line with this, the ROIs obtained have also been shown to code for action-related information. For instance, voxels within the dorsal occipital ROI are involved in the processing of object-specific 3D shape (Freud et al., 2016; Georgieva et al., 2009), and participate in object grasping, and in hand shaping and orientation in preparation for grasping (Fattori et al., 2017; Goodale et al., 1994). Moreover, voxels within the ventral occipital ROI have been shown to be also involved in object-specific (and potentially function-specific) action related processing (Gallivan et al., 2013; Knights et al., 2021), as well as in the processing of object properties that impact action such as an object’s weight (Gallivan et al., 2014; Keisker et al., 2010). Importantly, early visual cortical areas, including V1, have been (even causally) implicated in high-level planning and execution of object-directed actions (Gallivan et al., 2019; Gutteling et al., 2015; Monaco et al., 2017; 2020; Singhal et al., 2013; Whitwell et al., 2011). V1 and other early visual cortical regions show reactivation under delayed object-directed action tasks (Monaco et al., 2017; Singhal et al., 2013), with stronger activation for grasping than reaching (Gallivan et al., 2019; Singhal et al., 2013). These activations happen also in the dark and are stronger for real action when compared to motor imagery (Gallivan et al., 2019; Gutteling et al., 2015; Monaco et al., 2017,; 2020; Singhal et al., 2013). Perhaps central to the contentopic maps presented here for MD1 and MD2, data from these early visual cortical regions allow for consistent decoding of action (grasp vs. reach) and even grasp content (whole hand large object grasps vs. small precision grips; Gallivan et al., 2019; Monaco et al., 2020).

Undoubtedly, one should expect parietal cortical regions more anterior to the ROIs herein to also show some degree of tuning to the object-related mid-level dimensions used, as these areas code for object grasping and manipulation (Almeida et al., 2013; Bergström et al., 2021; Culham et al., 2003; Fattori et al., 2017; Kalénine et al., 2010; Mahon et al., 2007). In fact, inspection of individual maps (Figure S3 & S4) shows that more posterior and superior parietal cortical regions do present neural responses tuned to both dimensions for most of the participants. Perhaps the weaker and less consistent responses in posterior and superior parietal cortex, as well as the relatively posterior activation in ventral and dorsal occipital cortex, may be related with the format of stimuli presentation (exclusively visual) and the task required (unrelated to the object). Moreover, and in a similar line, overtly manipulating mid- and high-level action related properties (e.g. orientation of the stimuli towards the effector as a way of promoting visuomotor processing of affordance-like properties) could have also enhanced responses within parietal and occipito-parietal regions for these manipulation dimensions (Shikata et al., 2008).

Representational nature of the information within the contentopic maps obtained?

A major question relates to the nature of the representations within these contentopic maps. Our contentopic mapping data cannot be fully explained by the underlying retinotopic organization known to be present in our ROIs. Specifically, while fluctuations in contrast-energy over time across the visual field and over the ordered stimuli used can partially explain neural signal fluctuations in the ROIs described here, our contentopic maps for MD1 and MD2 cannot be strongly and fully predicted from the underlying retinotopic organization – as measured from participant-specific typical eccentricity maps computed over the same stimuli used for our contentopic maps and from HCP data eccentricity maps. It is important to note, though, that testing eccentricity effects on contentopic mapping does not preclude the existence of confounding effects dependent on other low-level visual dimensions. This is certainly a limitation of our approach as there may be untested low-level properties that relate to the object-related dimensions tested herein. It is our contention though, that although we cannot control for all low-level confounds, the existence of two independent maps (MD1 and MD2), and the relative independence between MD1 and MD2 maps and the retinotopic profiles of the stimuli used, suggests that our MD1 and MD2 dimension values are, at least, partially independent from the low-level properties of the stimuli used – and, importantly, that our maps, although potentially correlated with low-level visual properties, are not exhausted by them and are, in a way, an independent organizational signature of cortical processing. It is also important to consider that low-level visual features do not need to be necessarily treated as nuisance variables, as they can play a fundamental role in object recognition and provide information about the object function.

Note also that we are certainly not proposing that our ROIs are uniquely dedicated to processing information on object-directed action (here, MD1- and MD2-related information). In fact, it is clear that these ROIs process of the world in retinotopic coordinates, as it has been widely demonstrated (Dumoulin and Wandell, 2008). Nevertheless, while areas in early visual cortex are predominantly driven by contrast modulation, there is now a large body of data that suggests that these are not the only stimulus modulations and processes that these areas are responsive to. For instance, early visual cortex can encode auditory stimuli (Vetter et al., 2020; Vetter et al., 2014), is driven by vision-based working memory processes (Harrison and Tong, 2009), is characterized by global orientation-tuned maps (Freeman et al., 2011), and as described above, codes for grasp and object-directed action processing. Moreover, mice neurophysiology indicates that early visual cortex responses are modulated by the spatial position of a moving agent in an environment (Saleem et al., 2018). These data suggest that multiple response tunings may be shared by the same portion of cortex.

Contentopy, thus, probably depends on intermediate representations that bridge between early visual properties and complex object representations (Li and Bonner, 2020). Although these mid-level representations are important for visual and visuomotor object processing (Almeida et al., 2020; Almeida et al., 2010; Vannuscorps et al., 2021), their nature is hard to determine (Li and Bonner, 2020). Potentially, they no longer focus on spatial coding, but rather on object feature mapping (Almeida et al., 2020; Barlow, 1986) and object-specific parameter spaces (Ballard, 1987). These spaces and maps should be dependent on the aggregation of information from upstream simpler cells (e.g., edge orientation) into more global representations (e.g., surface orientation). This would be done via highly non-linear transformations (Li and Bonner, 2020; Op de Beeck, Haushofer, et al., 2008) that group lower-level information using gestalt principles applied to visual and visuomotor (Kootstra et al., 2012; Wagemans, 2013) processing. Interestingly, and related to the dimensions tested, models for object grasp have been proposed that rely precisely on gestalt principles to determine the kind of grasps to be applied to a stimulus through the computation of visual surfaces from low-level visual properties and focusing on action as a composite of chunks that respects surface properties (Kootstra et al., 2012).

How do these maps relate to the underlying retinotopic organization?

Although contentopic maps are not reducible to the retinotopic signatures of the stimuli used, low-level visual information is going to be highly informative and important for the definition of these maps and the dimensions that drive them (as they are of the response profiles of more anterior areas (Arcaro et al., 2019; Malach et al., 2002)). In fact, the very nature of the areas that encompass v-occ and d-occ ROIs – i.e., retinotopically organized hV4, LO, V3, V2, and V1 – suggests that there is a relationship between these retinotopic maps and contentopy.

What this relationship is, is yet to be fully fleshed out, but there may be some indications from our data and extant data sets. First and foremost, it is now clear that many topographies can coexist within a 2D cortical sheet – in fact, it probably has to be the case given the limited amount of 2D space in the cortex and the complexity of the world that surrounds us. This was beautifully demonstrated by work of Aflalo and Graziano on the multiple superimposed body, effector and action maps within the motor cortex of the monkey (Aflalo and Graziano, 2006; Graziano and Aflalo, 2007), by the demonstration of superimposed maps in parietal cortex for the processing of number, quantity, and time (Harvey et al., 2013; Harvey et al., 2020; Kristensen et al., 2021), or even the superimposition of several maps within early visual cortex (e.g., ocular dominance, orientation selectivity, eccentricity and polar angle) that can be modelled into the 2D cortical surface via dimensionality reduction, suggesting a final observable topography that is actually the result of this amalgamation of topographical maps (Durbin and Mitchison, 1990).

Interestingly, in these examples, some of these maps are truly orthogonal (e.g., orientation and location in the visual field). Nevertheless, our prediction is that mid-level contentopic maps that may lay, in part, superimposed onto strong retinotopic maps in the most posterior areas, are computationally related to retinotopic information. Firstly, eccentricity may well correlate, in the real world, with an object’s grasp – power grasp objects may require more eccentric attention towards the handle of an object, while maintaining attention towards the functional tip in order to properly use an object (Pilacinski et al., 2021). This is potentially in line with our own data where, despite the fact that within-dimension maps significantly outperform eccentricity maps, there is a part of the variance of contentopy that is explained by eccentricity through a negative relationship between grasp type and eccentricity location (as measure by the negative slope of the linear fit between MD1 and our eccentricity maps). This suggests a relationship in the direction of the importance of more peripheral stimulation for objects more dependent on power grasps.

Perhaps more speculatively, grasping and complex object directed-action require detailed information about object features that are necessary for hand pre-shaping and finger placement. Eccentricity may provide exactly the type of low-level information that can then be integrated and processed and give rise to this mid-level grasp-related information. Interestingly, our data and maps may actually reconcile the large body of work showing that early visual cortex is implicated in high-level complex object-directed action and holds enough (mid-level) information to disentangle between different grasp types (Gallivan et al., 2019; Gutteling et al., 2015; Monaco et al., 2017,; 2020; Singhal et al., 2013), with the existence of strong retinotopy within these regions, and the relationship of peripheral stimulation and power grasp objects. That is, these contentopic mid-level maps are the transformation of eccentricity in the service of object-directed action – the contentopic maps herein are protomaps of mid-level information important for complex object-direct information that allows for hand pre-shaping, finger placement and ultimately grasp type differences and complex action.

Contentopy and other object-related topographies

But how does this possibility fit with many of the coarse topographies that have been suggested? Speculatively, transformations over the readouts of the (multiple and potentially correlated) contentopic maps of the kind shown here would lead to composite higher order maps (Op de Beeck, Haushofer, et al., 2008) (within more anterior areas). In fact, it may be the case that domain selectivity within ventral temporal cortex follows precisely from the members of a domain satisfying a particular set of dimensions (and their associated contentopic maps; e.g., grasp type, elongation, presence of metal; Almeida et al., 2023) that relate strongly with that domain (e.g., manipulable objects). Thus, these downstream composite higher-level maps would arise dynamically from sampling upstream contentopic maps that are important for different domains of stimuli. This seems to be in line with there being finer parameter spaces within domain-specific regions (Sato et al., 2013), and with the role of connectivity in the definition of the conceptual representation in ventral temporal cortex (Mahon and Almeida, 2024; Mahon and Caramazza, 2011; Walbrin and Almeida, 2021).

Thus, topography at more higher-level areas should emerge from the non-linear aggregation of upstream contentopy mid-level maps into composite maps (Op de Beeck, Haushofer, et al., 2008). For instance, domain specificity (and potentially real object size, a topography that is highly correlated with domain organization; Grill-Spector and Weiner, 2014; Konkle and Caramazza, 2013; Mahon and Caramazza, 2011) would emerge from the fact that members of a domain would evoke a large set of important dimensions, and thus mid-level contentopic maps, that are typical of that domain. In these domain-specific (or size-dependent) regions, traversing single dimensions (e.g., our MD1 dimension) would lead to weak or no selectivity (Op de Beeck, Haushofer, et al., 2008). However, evoking the right pattern of dimensions would lead to the specific response preferences observed. Similarly, because non-linear integration of contentopic maps into composite higher-level maps follows dynamically from the requirements of the different domains, then regions that do not show particular domain preferences (the so called “NoManLand” regions; Bao et al., 2020) should show more unidimensional maps, potentially tuned towards highly informative mid-level properties such as curvature singularities (Wagemans, 2013), as they do (Bao et al., 2020; Coggan and Tong, 2023). Finally, this hypothesis is also compatible with the putative role of eccentricity at these higher-level topographies (Arcaro et al., 2019; Malach et al., 2002). Eccentricity maps would work as protomaps (Arcaro et al., 2019) to guide the dynamical integration of contentopies. Moreover, because contentopy is built from lower-level representations, eccentricity would be inherently passed to these higher-level maps. In fact, here we show effects of eccentricity for the stimuli presented, in line with the potential effects of eccentricity on higher-level maps

Interestingly, this multidimensional flexibility in the topographical organization of object information may be central for the kinds of processes that underlie object recognition. The multidimensional flexibility that we show could be the mechanism by which object representations are built in the service of object recognition – neural populations would be tuned to different mid-level dimensions and flexibly superimpose different topographic maps. Potentially, these maps impose an object-centric reference frame to visual processing, just as retinotopy imposes a spatial reference to cognition (Groen et al., 2022). One potential consequence of such composite maps is that they allow for flexibility and discriminability when processing individual items: a particular hammer may have some properties that differ from a prototypical hammer, but because of the intersection of the readouts of multiple contentopy maps, in the end, a hammer will still be a hammer, even if differing from a prototypical hammer in some dimensions.

Importantly, here we show that topography is (at least) one of the central organization principles that the brain uses to represent and store information about objects. Note that although we show contentopy for one type of objects – i.e., manipulable objects – and a particular set of dimensions, we predict that this contentopic organization should be present for other types of stimuli (e.g., faces, animals, scenes; provided that we have structuring dimensions for the object categories to be tested) and for other object-related dimensions. For instance, mid-level dimensions such elongation (Almeida et al., 2023), or particular surface properties (Cavina-Pratesi et al., 2010), should also lead to relatively independent contentopic maps.

The relationship between different object-related dimensions as a limitation for studying contentopy

One might question whether and how much these object-related dimensions might be independent from one another – for instance, in the real world, real-object size, elongation and grasp type will be correlated. This is probably the result of the complexity of the world, and of the multidimensional and multimodal nature of the objects that surround us – different mid-level information co-occurs, and those co-occurrences may actually define different objects as described above. This relates to a limitation of this study – are these maps based on MD1 and MD2, or are they shared with (or even driven by) other object-related dimensions? This confound is rather hard to completely rule out. Nevertheless, our maps appear to be independent from some of the coarser topographies that have been put forth in the literature. For example, real-object size seems to show categorical differences between small and large objects (Konkle and Oliva, 2012) (but see Magri et al., 2021) for a different object-size topography) within areas in the proximity of those presented here. However, the potential topographical orientation that could be derived from the categorical distinction between large and small objects is at odds from the direction of the finer topography shown here for MD1 – for instance, in the dorsal stream, Konkle and colleagues (Konkle and Oliva, 2012) show that the anatomical boundary between large and small objects happens to be the occipital sulcus (with large objects going posteriorly), whereas that same sulcus is the anterior border of our MD1 map. Note, however, that we do not claim that these maps are not correlated, but rather that they do follow different orientations, and importantly that these object-related dimensions, whether or not correlated, seem to be coding for different (and dissociable) aspects of the mid-to-high-level representational content humans hold about objects. To this point, the dissociation between patient DF’s performance in determining the real size of an object and adjusting her grasp to objects of different sizes (Goodale et al., 1994) is a demonstration of the functional and anatomical independence of these dimensions.

Conclusion

Our results demonstrate the ubiquity and non-incidental nature of topographical organization of information in the brain. Irrespective of the kind of information – whether sensorimotor (Formisano et al., 2003; Graziano and Aflalo, 2007; Sereno et al., 1995), or more abstract such as numerosity (Harvey et al., 2013) or, as demonstrated here, object information – the brain will likely organize information in continuous topographical maps that follow particularly relevant dimensional solutions for the kinds of information being coded. The parameters that may change for different kinds of information relate to the multidimensional flexibility that is implemented, and how this flexibility is rendered as a composite neural response. The benefits of such an organizational principle go from reducing metabolically costly long-distance connectivity between neurons processing similar information, to more cognitively focused aspects relating to processing specialization and stimuli discrimination (Kaas, 1997; Wandell et al., 2007). Overall, our results raise the question of whether all kinds of information are amenable to topographical mapping. Although our data cannot fully address this, it clearly brings a new layer of understanding to both the general question of how information is processed in the brain, as well as to the quest of understanding what underlies our formidable capacity to recognize objects.
