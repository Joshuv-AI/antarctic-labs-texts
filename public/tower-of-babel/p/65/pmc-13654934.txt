Improving FAIRness of Geospatial data using Large Language Models
Authors: Blessing Kavhu, Ville Mäkinen, Panu Muhli, Juha Oksanen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The FAIR principles- Findability, Accessibility, Interoperability and Reusability have become central to enhancing the value, transparency, and longevity of scientific data. Yet in the geospatial domain, implementing FAIR practices often remains uneven, reflecting gaps between formal standards compliance and practical usability within complex data infrastructures. Here, we examine how large language models (LLMs) can support the operationalisation of FAIR-aligned geospatial data stewardship. Drawing on practical experiences in geospatial FAIR implementation and a targeted synthesis of emerging LLM-enabled applications, we discuss how LLMs can assist various FAIR-related elements, including metadata enrichment, semantic alignment, multilingual discovery, and natural-language interaction with geospatial services. We further address technical, institutional and epistemic challenges associated with using LLMs and propose solutions that include hybrid validation approaches, confidence-aware workflows and transparent AI provenance indicators to mitigate automation bias and support trustworthy reuse. Building on these insights, we outline a research agenda for AI-supported FAIR (fAIr) centred on evaluation benchmarks, domain-tuned and resource-efficient models, explainable and auditable AI, and standards-integrated system architectures. We frame LLMs as assistive technologies that can help scale and embed FAIR principles within evolving geospatial research infrastructures, rather than replacements for traditional stewardship practices.

Introduction

The Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) data principles have become a cornerstone of open science since their introduction1. They have been adopted across disciplines as a framework for enhancing the long-term usability, transparency, and reproducibility of scientific data2,3. In this sense, FAIR practices represent an extension of good scientific data stewardship rather than an additional requirement imposed on data providers. However, translating these principles into operational practice remains challenging, particularly within established geospatial ecosystems. The difficulty lies less in the FAIR principles themselves than in the practical work required to embed them into existing workflows and infrastructures. Tasks such as metadata enrichment, semantic harmonisation, and documentation often demand sustained expert effort, especially when applied retrospectively to previously published data resources. This is concerning, given the unprecedented growth of geospatial information. For example, earth observation satellites alone generate more than 250 terabytes of raw data daily4, while platforms such as OpenStreetMap add millions of new features each month5. Without robust FAIR practices, much of this information remains siloed, underutilised, non-reusable, or effectively invisible to broader scientific and policy communities.

Studies have demonstrated several areas where translating FAIR principles into operational geospatial data practices remains uneven. These include many situations where metadata is inconsistent, incomplete, or in some cases missing altogether6. Data catalogues and APIs often require specialised technical knowledge to query and integrate effectively7. Standards for interoperability, such as ISO 191158 or the Open Geospatial Consortium’s (OGC) protocols9, are often unevenly adopted, resulting in fragmentation across institutions and regions worldwide10,11. Moreover, implementing FAIR-aligned workflows often requires institutional investment, which many repositories, particularly in developing countries, struggle to provide12. These bottlenecks highlight why, despite nearly a decade of FAIR advocacy, progress in many geospatial data infrastructures remains lagging.

Emerging Large Language Models (LLMs) offer assistive capabilities for addressing challenges of operationalising FAIR practices. Unlike traditional isolated tools designed to enhance FAIR-aligned operationalisation, LLMs excel at interpreting, generating, and translating natural language, making them uniquely suited to address multiple long-standing barriers to FAIRness13,14. For instance, they can: (i) enhance findability and reusability by automatically enriching metadata with semantic descriptions extracted directly from manuscripts and datasets, (ii) improve accessibility by enabling conversational search across APIs and portals, lowering technical barriers for non-expert users, (iii) promote interoperability by further aligning heterogeneous terminologies and ontologies across domains, and (iv) facilitate reusability by generating multilingual documentation, and contextual knowledge graphs that situate datasets in broader scientific workflows15–17. These affordances indicate that LLMs can complement existing FAIRification strategies by directly targeting some of the weak links in current geospatial data practices.

Despite growing experimentation, the systematic application of LLMs to improve FAIRification of geospatial data remains nascent. Recent years have seen several important technical contributions that hint at what is possible. Recent GeoAI developments (such as GeoGPT) prove how LLM-driven agents can improve geospatial workflows from natural language prompts. This enables users to discover, fetch, and preprocess spatial data without writing technical code18–20. Parallel efforts on domain-tuned models, such as GeoCode-GPT, and the creation of geospatial code-generation benchmarks demonstrated that LLMs can be adapted for spatial reasoning tasks and evaluated systematically21. Other strands of research focused on metadata enrichment and dataset profiling19. In the semantic web and data stewardship communities, LLMs were adopted for schema matching and column-header tagging22. Complementary systems such as Topio illustrate the feasibility of automated profiling at scale, extracting structural descriptors from large geospatial collections23. Beyond geospatial data, data management in other data-intensive domains with comparable metadata challenges offers promising analogies. For instance, in law, LLMs are already used to generate plain-language summaries of complex licenses24,25; in biomedicine, auto-tagging workflows improve discoverability across fragmented databases, and in the broader data ethics community, tools like “datasheets for datasets” provide structured pathways for documenting provenance and reuse26. Together, these advances demonstrate a growing toolkit that could be harnessed for FAIRification.

Although progress has been made, existing contributions fall short of a coherent FAIR strategy. Most initiatives focus on isolated tasks such as code generation, single-dataset enrichment, or descriptive profiling rather than pursuing end-to-end FAIR outcomes14,27,28. Critical gaps in FAIRification persist. These include evaluating LLM-generated metadata against FAIR maturity metrics and integrating data-aware profiling, particularly for attributes and geometries, with OGC/ISO standards. Other important gaps involve embedding conversational and multilingual access into operational repositories, supported by human-in-the-loop validation, and provenance summarisation to ensure reusability. Unresolved concerns regarding hallucinations, AI output provenance, compliance with community standards, and equitable access to LLMs further highlight the risks of premature adoption. Given these challenges, consolidated research that synthesises existing efforts on the use of LLMs and critically evaluates them against FAIR objectives, and proposes a research and action agenda is needed. Such an approach would help the community move from proof-of-concept demonstrations toward measurable, trustworthy, and operational LLM-assisted FAIRification of geospatial data.

Based on our knowledge of advancing FAIRification of geospatial data projects in large scale research infrastructures, we argue that unlocking FAIR geospatial data with LLMs requires a transformative approach, organized around four themes of change, including: (i) metadata enrichment and semantic discoverability, (ii) human–machine accessibility through conversational interfaces, (iii) ontology alignment and cross-domain interoperability, and (iv) contextual reusability through provenance, and workflow support. By structuring our analysis around these themes, we aim to offer a research agenda that highlights pragmatic pathways for leveraging LLMs in geospatial data. Rather than presenting LLMs as a technological substitute for FAIR practices, we identify where they can responsibly augment existing standards-driven workflows, borrowing lessons from data-intensive domains with comparable metadata challenges while identifying opportunities unique to the geospatial fraternity.

Analytical framework and evidentiary basis

We used a conceptual synthesis approach to examine how LLMs may support the implementation of FAIR principles within geospatial data stewardship. Conceptual and integrative syntheses are commonly used in emerging interdisciplinary domains where empirical evidence would be fragmented and rapidly evolving. This enables researchers to interpret technological developments through established practice-oriented frameworks29,30. Rather than presenting a systematic review of LLM applications, our assessment is grounded in our practical experiences with FAIR implementation in geospatial research infrastructures, complemented by a targeted examination of emerging LLM-enabled tools and research prototypes. Our objective is to evaluate how recently developed capabilities in LLMs intersect with persistent challenges observed in operational geospatial data stewardship1,31.

Our analytical framework emerged from sustained engagement with FAIR implementation across geospatial infrastructures, including metadata standardisation, spatial data infrastructure (SDI) development, ontology alignment, and repository governance. Practice-informed research has been widely recognised as a reliable methodological basis in infrastructure and data stewardship studies, where operational knowledge provides critical insight into socio-technical constraints not fully captured by formal evaluations32,33. Through these activities, recurring limitations were identified across the FAIR pillars, and other gaps that are frequently documented in FAIR adoption earth studies and geospatial data management literature6,10,34. To contextualise our observations, we conducted a targeted scoping review of academic and grey literature addressing (i) FAIR data practices, (ii) geospatial data infrastructures, and (iii) emerging applications of LLMs relevant to data discovery, documentation, and semantic interpretation. We identified literature through iterative searches in Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar, combined with domain knowledge accumulated through ongoing research and participation in geospatial data initiatives. Our purpose was not exhaustive coverage of all LLM research, but rather identification of representative developments illustrating how LLM capabilities align with documented FAIR implementation challenges.

First, we mapped persistent implementation challenges to each FAIR pillar based on established FAIR literature and geospatial stewardship experience1,31,35. Second, we analysed emerging LLM applications to determine whether and under what constraints their capabilities could plausibly address these challenges. Our approach follows analytical synthesis strategies used in technology assessment studies, where emerging tools are evaluated against existing governance and workflow frameworks rather than through performance benchmarking alone36–38. We don’t claim operational deployment outcomes but instead develop a foundation for evaluating opportunities, limitations, and governance implications associated with LLM-assisted FAIRification.

Themes of transformation in FAIRification

We explore how LLMs can act as catalysts for the operationalisation of FAIRification of geospatial data. We argue that increasing operationalisation of FAIR principles requires re-imagining geospatial data infrastructures. We propose themes of transformation that align with the FAIR-aligned geospatial data, positioning LLMs as enablers of more comprehensive, usable, and sustainable data ecosystems (Fig. 1). From hidden to discoverable (Findable)

A persistent challenge in operationalising FAIR principles for geospatial data is the gap between nominal catalogue compliance and genuine discoverability. Many datasets are technically registered in portals but remain effectively invisible because metadata often provide only coarse descriptors, such as titles, coordinate reference systems, spatial extent, and basic keywords, while omitting the semantic and structural information necessary for evaluating usability. Specifically, metadata frequently lack: (i) detailed thematic descriptions and variable-level semantics (e.g., clear definitions of attributes, units of measurement, classification schemes); (ii) provenance and processing lineage (e.g., sensor characteristics, preprocessing steps, uncertainty estimates); (iii) temporal granularity and update frequency; (iv) data quality indicators, including accuracy assessments and completeness metrics; and (v) explicit usage constraints or licensing clarity.

As emphasised by Wilkinson et al.1, Findability (F) requires that data are assigned globally unique and persistent identifiers (F1), are described with rich metadata (F2), and are indexed in searchable resources (F4). Without these elements, users cannot reliably locate or assess datasets. Insufficient semantic depth forces users into inefficient workflows of downloading and manually inspecting multiple datasets, while heterogeneity in portal interfaces and metadata schemas increases cognitive and technical barriers39–43.

Recent advances in LLMs provide opportunities to address these deficits primarily by enriching metadata. LLMs can automatically generate informative abstracts, standardised keyword tags, multilingual descriptions, and concise semantic summaries from dataset documentation and provenance records, improving catalogue indexing and discoverability44. This represents an area where LLM capabilities offer assistive support that aligns closely with existing needs, as discovery relies heavily on language, description, and semantic interpretation rather than deterministic infrastructure alone. For vector datasets, LLMs can parse attribute tables, canonicalise attribute names against controlled vocabularies, summarise geometry patterns, and translate raw descriptors into human-readable tags that are machine-actionable. Such functionality enables datasets to be described in richer detail, supporting F2 (rich metadata) and indirectly F4 (indexed in searchable resources).

Analogues from other scientific data-intensive domains with comparable metadata challenges highlight the transformative potential of this approach. In biomedicine, automated tagging pipelines that expand indexing using controlled vocabularies such as Medical Subject Headings45 have significantly improved article discoverability45, enhancing recall and precision for domain-specific queries46. Early geospatial demonstrations include GeoGPT47 and GeoCode-GPT21, which support conversational discovery and metadata enrichment, while existing structural profiling systems, such as Topio23, demonstrate scalable approaches to attribute extraction. Embedding these LLM capabilities into established infrastructures, such as Copernicus DIAS48, or national spatial data infrastructures and INSPIRE protocols49, could yield substantial improvements in Findability.

Using an example of a practical task for the discovery of global land-cover datasets within the Copernicus Programme Earth observation ecosystem. The Copernicus Global Land Cover Layers product provides annual global land-cover classifications derived from satellite imagery and is distributed through multiple catalogues, including the Copernicus Climate Data Store (https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/). While technically discoverable through catalogue search interfaces, users searching for datasets relevant to concepts such as “urban expansion monitoring” or “impervious surface growth” may struggle because these terms do not explicitly appear in the dataset’s metadata, which primarily reference land-cover classes and sensor processing documentation. An LLM integrated with the catalogue could analyse dataset documentation, product user guides, and classification schemas to automatically generate expanded semantic metadata linking concepts such as “urban expansion,” “built-up areas,” and “impervious surfaces” to the dataset’s classification categories. However, it is important to note that such semantic expansion is not a novel contribution of LLMs in itself. Established approaches, including ontology alignment, thesauri mapping, and controlled vocabularies, already provide robust mechanisms for relating concepts across datasets and improving semantic interoperability.

In this context, LLMs should be understood as assistive tools that can help operationalise and scale these existing approaches, particularly when relating existing metadata with terms from controlled vocabulary that cannot be easily processed through deterministic methods alone. For example, LLMs can extract latent concepts from free-text documentation and propose candidate links to controlled vocabulary terms, which can then be validated against authoritative ontologies.

Similar semantic enrichment approaches have been demonstrated in geospatial data discovery prototypes such as GeoGPT and GeoCode-GPT21,47. By automatically generating richer thematic descriptors and contextual summaries, the system could improve dataset retrieval across catalogues while maintaining compliance with FAIR Findability requirements (F1–F4). Thus, rather than replacing established semantic technologies, LLMs act as a complementary layer that bridges content from existing metadata and formal knowledge organisation systems, enhancing the efficiency and scalability of FAIR-aligned metadata enrichment.

LLMs also contribute to more consistent use of persistent identifiers (PID) by parsing and validating identifiers in structured queries or citations, ensuring datasets remain globally identifiable (F1). The contribution of PIDs to machine-actionable discovery, however, extends beyond their use as stable references. Their effectiveness is closely linked to the structure and semantic interpretability of the metadata in which they are embedded. In particular, interoperability can be strengthened when PID-associated metadata fields and values follow well-defined and machine-interpretable structures, enabling systems not only to resolve identifiers but also to interpret their roles, relationships, and attributes in context.

This perspective aligns with broader practices in metadata standardisation, where controlled vocabularies and formal representations are used to enhance semantic clarity. Extending similar principles to PID-associated metadata supports more robust automated processing and integration, which can strengthen the overall machine-actionability of geospatial resources.

At the same time, relating metadata with controlled vocabularies or independent terminologies does not inherently guarantee strict compliance. Without constrained validation, outputs may be overgeneralised, misclassified, or contain hallucinated assertions50. For this reason, metadata expanded by LLMs should be programmatically validated against authoritative schemas, with provenance documentation specifying which fields were inspected and confidence indicators provided. Human-in-the-loop review remains essential to ensure semantic consistency and adherence to FAIR principles. By enriching metadata, standardising descriptions, and integrating persistent identifiers, LLMs enhance the practical discoverability of datasets while preserving compliance with F1-F4. This approach shifts geospatial portals from technically compliant but opaque catalogues to resources that are both machine-actionable and semantically interpretable, thereby improving accessibility downstream without conflating the two principles.

From exposure to enablement (Accessible)

Within the FAIR framework, Accessibility ensures that data and metadata can be retrieved by their identifiers using a standardised communication protocol, independent of the user. Key subprinciples include: A1: data and metadata are retrievable by their identifier using a standardised protocol, A1.1: the protocol is open, free, and universally implementable, A1.2: authentication and authorisation procedures are supported where necessary, A2: metadata remain accessible even when the data are no longer available.

In practice, however, barriers to accessibility extend beyond the availability of standardised endpoints. Many spatial data infrastructures and thematic portals provide technically retrievable services, but interacting with these systems often requires specialist knowledge of query syntax, portal-specific authentication procedures, and service semantics51. Heterogeneous APIs, sparse or inconsistent documentation, and irregular parameter naming conventions can render datasets nominally “accessible” but practically challenging for broader communities35,52,53. Linguistic limitations further exacerbate these challenges as the majority of geospatial metadata is published in English, potentially excluding non-English-speaking researchers, while datasets described in national languages may be less discoverable by English-speaking users54–57. This gap illustrates that accessibility is not only a matter of technical compliance with protocols but also a question of practical retrievability and enabled access for diverse user communities (A1.1).

LLMs offer promising avenues to mitigate these barriers by enabling full practical access while remaining consistent with the formal requirements of Accessibility. By translating natural-language queries into standardised API calls, LLMs enable users to retrieve data without requiring in-depth knowledge of syntax or portal-specific semantics. For instance, a researcher could request “all land-cover polygons classified as forest in Nairobi, 2020–2022,” and an LLM integrated with a geospatial middleware layer could generate the corresponding OGC-compliant spatial query. LLMs are not inherently “aware” of data portals in a persistent or agentive sense; rather, their familiarity derives from statistical patterns learned during pretraining and, more reliably, from structured integrations that enable retrieval and tool use. When combined with retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) or middleware agent-based frameworks, LLMs can access portal documentation, API schemas, and catalogue endpoints in real time, translating natural-language queries into executable API calls or structured database queries58,59. In doing so, LLMs operationalise A1 and A1.1, supporting technically compliant retrieval through open and standardised protocols, while remaining dependent on the underlying infrastructure for authoritative execution.

This functionality demonstrates that Accessibility is not only about machine retrievability but also about ensuring that users from diverse communities with different technical abilities have full access to open and free protocols, thereby operationalising both A1-A2 while preserving the authority of protocols and authentication mechanisms. Similar conversational query systems have begun to emerge in scientific data infrastructures and research prototypes using RAG and tool-enabled LLM agents58,59. Analogues from other scientific data-intensive domains further illustrate the feasibility and impact of these interventions. In astronomy, conversational assistants have been deployed to translate natural-language queries into metadata filters and service requests, enabling broader access to telescope archives for non-specialist researchers60,61. In geospatial contexts, integrating LLMs into platforms such as Copernicus DIAS48 GEOSS62 could transform technically accessible datasets into resources that are practically accessible, allowing multilingual, user-friendly interaction while maintaining compliance with standardised retrieval protocols.

While LLMs substantially enhance accessibility, risks remain. Models may mistranslate queries, return incomplete data subsets, or produce outputs that appear authoritative without guaranteed correctness. Addressing these risks requires careful safeguards, including transparent reporting of LLM-generated queries alongside the original natural-language request, provenance tracking linking results to metadata or portal endpoints, and human-in-the-loop validation. Empirical evaluation of task performance and error rates is also essential to ensure that LLM augmentation genuinely improves practical accessibility without introducing new barriers. In this framing, LLMs function as assistive mediators that expand the reach and usability of FAIR-compliant protocols without altering the protocols themselves. By bridging cognitive and procedural gaps, they operationalise accessibility in practice, complementing deterministic infrastructures while respecting the formal requirements of A1, A1.1, A1.2, and A2.

From syntactic to semantic (Interoperable)

Interoperability constitutes a central pillar of the FAIR principles, ensuring that data can be integrated, interpreted, and reused across systems, disciplines, and infrastructures. Within the FAIR framework, Interoperability is defined through three subprinciples: that (meta)data use a formal, accessible, shared, and broadly applicable language for knowledge representation (I1); that they use vocabularies that themselves follow FAIR principles (I2); and that they include qualified references to other (meta)data (I3)1. Together, these requirements aim to ensure that data are not merely exchangeable at a technical level but semantically integrable across heterogeneous environments.

In geospatial data ecosystems, interoperability has historically been operationalised primarily at the syntactic level. Established community standards, such as ISO 19110 feature catalogues63, ISO metadata specifications, and Open Geospatial Consortium (OGC) service interfaces9, enable datasets to be encoded and transmitted in mutually intelligible formats. These standards satisfy important aspects of I1 by providing structured and widely adopted representation frameworks. However, syntactic compatibility alone does not guarantee semantic coherence. Datasets that conform to identical technical standards may nonetheless rely on divergent vocabularies, classification systems, or conceptual models, limiting meaningful integration.

This distinction between syntactic and semantic interoperability is particularly salient in geospatial science. A land-cover polygon may be labelled “cropland” in one dataset, “arable land” in another, and “agriculture” in a third. Although conceptually related, such differences complicate automated integration and reduce practical reusability. Traditional solutions have addressed these challenges through ontology engineering and controlled vocabularies such as General Multilingual Environmental Thesaurus (GEMET)64, INSPIRE thematic vocabularies65, and AGROVOC66, as well as manually curated crosswalks between classification systems including CORINE, IGBP, and Olson land-cover schemes67. These approaches operationalise I2 by grounding data descriptions in shared, community-endorsed vocabularies. However, ontology alignment and vocabulary harmonisation remain challenging to sustain at scale in practice. While many alignment procedures are not computationally intensive and can be executed efficiently offline, scaling these approaches is constrained less by compute than by the need for domain expertise, iterative validation, and ongoing governance. Ensuring semantic consistency, contextual validity, and long-term maintainability requires substantial expert involvement, particularly in interdisciplinary settings where concepts do not map cleanly across domains. As a result, these processes remain iterative and only partially automated when integrating heterogeneous datasets.

Geospatial data introduces further complexity because many geographic categories are inherently context-dependent. Concepts such as “urban,” “rural,” “forest,” or “shrubland” vary across spatial resolution, national definitions, ecological zones, and methodological assumptions68–70. Even carefully designed ontologies cannot eliminate interpretive ambiguity. Consequently, semantic reconciliation remains an ongoing, partially manual activity in FAIR implementation, especially when datasets are integrated across domains such as climate science, public health, and urban planning.

A common interoperability challenge arises when integrating land-cover datasets that use different classification schemes. For instance, the European CORINE Land Cover dataset employs a hierarchical classification tailored to European environmental monitoring, while global land-cover datasets often follow schemes derived from the International Geosphere-Biosphere Programme land-cover classification system. Although both datasets describe broadly comparable environmental categories, differences in terminology and class definitions complicate automated integration. An LLM constrained by domain ontologies could analyse the textual descriptions of classification classes and propose candidate mappings between categories such as “non-irrigated arable land” in CORINE and “cropland” in the IGBP system. Similar ontology-alignment support has been explored in other data-intensive scientific domains using transformer-based language models to identify semantic correspondences across heterogeneous vocabularies71,72. In a geospatial infrastructure, such suggestions could be presented to domain experts as candidate crosswalks, accelerating the creation of formal mappings while preserving expert validation and governance. In this role, LLMs function as assistive tools that help surface potential semantic correspondences, supporting the operationalisation of FAIR Interoperability principles (I1–I3). Within this bounded problem space, LLM offer an assistive but not authoritative contribution. By learning contextual associations across large textual and technical corpora, LLMs can propose candidate correspondences between heterogeneous terms, suggest hierarchical relationships, and identify potential inconsistencies between vocabularies. When fine-tuned or constrained using domain ontologies or feature catalogues, LLMs may help surface relationships such as the conceptual proximity between “cropland” and “arable land,” situating them within broader taxonomic structures. In doing so, they can accelerate preliminary stages of ontology alignment, reducing the manual burden on domain experts while preserving expert validation as the final authority.

Evidence from adjacent disciplines supports this assistive framing. In bioinformatics, transformer-based models such as BioBERT71 have demonstrated improvements in ontology alignment and semantic relation extraction, identifying correspondences overlooked by rule-based approaches72. Analogously, in geospatial contexts, LLMs could assist in generating candidate mappings across established frameworks73 or disciplinary vocabularies74. Such capabilities are particularly relevant for cross-domain infrastructures such as GEOSS62 and EOSC75, where integration increasingly spans environmental, climate, and health data. For example, an LLM might highlight conceptual links between geospatial descriptions of “urban heat islands” and public-health terminology related to heat exposure or morbidity, thereby supporting the formalisation of qualified cross-domain references consistent with I31,6.

Importantly, however, LLM-generated alignments are probabilistic rather than formally grounded. Models may overgeneralise or conflate superficially similar yet conceptually distinct terms, for instance, equating “forest plantation” with “natural forest,” despite substantial ecological differences. Because LLM reasoning is embedding-based and not inherently transparent, reproducibility and traceability become critical concerns. Mitigating these risks requires embedding LLM outputs within transparent governance workflows that include provenance tracking, confidence reporting, constrained decoding against authoritative vocabularies, and human-in-the-loop evaluation. Benchmarking candidate mappings against established crosswalks, such as INSPIRE theme mappings, remains essential to ensure that LLM assistance strengthens rather than undermines semantic interoperability76,77.

Viewed through the FAIR lens, LLMs do not redefine Interoperability. They do not replace formal knowledge-representation languages (I1), nor do they substitute for community-governed vocabularies (I2), nor automatically generate authoritative cross-dataset references (I3). Instead, they function as semantic accelerators within a standards-based ecosystem. By assisting experts in identifying potential correspondences, inconsistencies, and cross-domain linkages, LLMs can reduce the interpretive burden of semantic alignment while reinforcing, rather than bypassing, the deterministic infrastructures that underpin interoperable geospatial data stewardship.

From availability to usability (Reusable)

Reusability represents the culminating objective of the FAIR principles, aiming to ensure that data can be interpreted, combined, and applied beyond their original context of production1. Within the FAIR framework, Reusability is articulated through subprinciple R1, which requires that (meta)data be richly described with a plurality of accurate and relevant attributes. This requirement is further specified through R1.1, mandating clear and accessible usage licenses; R1.2, requiring detailed provenance; and R1.3, emphasising compliance with domain-relevant community standards. Together, these elements establish the structural and governance conditions necessary for responsible and reproducible reuse.

In geospatial science, substantial progress has been made in operationalising these requirements. Structured metadata standards such as ISO 19115 lineage models78 and provenance frameworks such as PROV-O79 provide formal mechanisms for documenting data origins, processing workflows, and methodological assumptions, directly supporting R1.280,81. Licensing schemes and open-data policies increasingly clarify permissible reuse conditions, aligning with R1.1. Community standards governing spatial reference systems, classification systems, and feature catalogues reinforce R1.3 by embedding datasets within shared disciplinary conventions. From a governance perspective, the structural foundations for reuse are therefore well established.

Yet empirical studies demonstrate that formal compliance with R1–R1.3 does not automatically guarantee practical reuse82. Researchers frequently encounter difficulties interpreting lineage statements, understanding methodological assumptions, and assessing contextual limitations embedded in metadata records31,83. In geospatial contexts, reuse often spans disciplinary boundaries, for example, applying satellite-derived land surface temperature products in epidemiology or using climate reanalysis data in urban planning. Differences in spatial resolution, classification systems, uncertainty reporting, and modelling assumptions can introduce interpretive ambiguity, complicating fitness-for-purpose assessment6. In many such cases, the metadata required under R1.2 and R1.3 are present and formally compliant, yet cognitively demanding to interpret.

The remaining barrier to reuse is thus frequently interpretive rather than structural. While provenance documentation and standards references may exist, users must still synthesise dispersed technical information to assess suitability for a new analytical context. It is at this interpretive layer that LLMs may offer a carefully bounded contribution. Unlike in Findability, where LLMs may enrich metadata descriptions, or Accessibility, where they mediate retrieval through standardised protocols, Reusability depends fundamentally on authoritative documentation and governance frameworks that must remain intact. LLMs should not generate formal provenance records or substitute for community standards. Rather, their contribution lies in enhancing the operational clarity of what is already documented.

In relation to R1, LLMs can assist by synthesising complex metadata fields into task-oriented explanations that foreground attributes most relevant to a given reuse scenario. When metadata includes detailed descriptions of uncertainty metrics, classification schemes, spatial resolution, and temporal granularity, an LLM can contextualise these attributes in light of a proposed research question, thereby making the “plurality of relevant attributes” more intelligible without altering their authoritative content. A practical example includes the reuse of climate reanalysis datasets such as the ERA5 Reanalysis Dataset produced by the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts84 and distributed through the Copernicus Climate Change Service85. These datasets provide globally consistent atmospheric variables derived from complex data assimilation workflows that integrate satellite observations, numerical weather prediction models, and observational archives. Although detailed provenance documentation and methodological descriptions are available, researchers from other disciplines, such as urban planning or public health, may find it difficult to interpret how processing decisions, spatial resolution, or temporal aggregation influence analytical suitability. An LLM constrained to reference the dataset’s metadata and documentation could synthesise lineage information into structured explanations highlighting key factors such as temporal resolution, uncertainty characteristics, and modelling assumptions relevant to a specific research question. Similar approaches have been explored in scientific data interpretation tools where LLMs summarise methodological documentation to support dataset reuse31,83. In this role, the LLM does not replace formal provenance records but assists users in interpreting them, thereby reducing cognitive barriers to responsible reuse and supporting the practical operationalisation of FAIR Reusability principles (R1–R1.3).

Similarly, LLMs may particularly support the practical interpretation of R1.2. Lineage documentation often contains technically precise but dense accounts of preprocessing steps, sensor calibration procedures, resampling strategies, or modelling assumptions. When constrained to cite specific metadata fields, an LLM can translate such descriptions into structured explanations of how methodological decisions might influence downstream analyses. In this sense, the model does not replace provenance frameworks but reduces the cognitive effort required to interpret them, facilitating more informed reuse decisions. For R1.3, LLMs can help situate referenced standards or classification schemes within broader disciplinary practice, supporting cross-domain users in assessing compatibility while leaving the authority of community standards untouched.

Across these cases, the contribution of LLMs is inherently asymmetric and supportive. They do not establish reusability, which remains grounded in clear licensing, detailed provenance, and adherence to domain standards. Instead, they enhance the practical operationalisation of R1 and R1.2 in particular, while offering secondary interpretive support for R1.1 and R1.3 by lowering the cognitive barriers to understanding formally compliant metadata.

At the same time, LLM-assisted interpretation introduces risks. Models may oversimplify methodological nuances, omit critical caveats, or generate explanations that appear authoritative without faithfully reflecting source documentation. Because reuse decisions may influence scientific conclusions or policy outcomes, safeguards are essential. These include strict traceability linking generated explanations directly to metadata fields or workflow records, transparent disclosure of LLM involvement, and human-in-the-loop review, particularly in high-stakes contexts86. Embedding LLM outputs within provenance-aware and reproducible workflows ensures that interpretive assistance reinforces rather than undermines trust.

From FAIR as an external requirement to fAIr as an embedded scientific practice powered by Large Language models

While the FAIR principles have gained broad acceptance across scientific and policy communities1,31, persistent challenges remain in their operationalisation, particularly in geospatial research environments where analytical workflows are technically demanding, FAIR standards are implemented retrospectively, and data production often occurs under tight operational constraints87,88. Empirical evidence consistently shows that the difficulty lies not in conceptual agreement with FAIR but in embedding FAIR-aligned practices within existing research routines89,90.

In many settings, FAIR-related tasks, metadata creation, ontology alignment, and provenance documentation are introduced late in the data lifecycle. This sequencing fosters the perception that FAIR constitutes an external compliance layer rather than an intrinsic part of scientific practice91,92. Cross-disciplinary analyses demonstrate that this perception stems largely from workflow design rather than researcher resistance83,93. When FAIR implementation is retrospective, metadata may achieve formal compliance while offering limited practical support for discovery or reuse94. The challenge, therefore, is not to reduce FAIR requirements but to integrate them more naturally into everyday data production processes. A growing response to this challenge is the concept of born-FAIR data, in which metadata, semantics, and provenance are generated deterministically at the point of data creation through structured standards and controlled vocabularies. In geospatial domains, frameworks such as SensorML95, ISO 19115 metadata models78, and established domain vocabularies96 can enable sensor characteristics, observational context, lineage information, and ownership to be recorded automatically and reproducibly. Deterministic approaches remain the preferred foundation for FAIR implementation because they provide transparent, auditable, and standards-governed documentation without reliance on interpretive inference.

However, a substantial proportion of geospatial data predates such workflows or lacks sufficient semantic characterisation. Legacy datasets, heterogeneous archives, and minimally documented collections often contain essential contextual information embedded in free text, technical reports, or tacit domain knowledge97,98. In these circumstances, deterministic methods alone are insufficient, and retrospective FAIRification becomes necessary. Here, LLMs may provide complementary assistance. Rather than replacing born-FAIR mechanisms, they can help reconstruct descriptive metadata, suggest semantic relationships, extract structured information from unstructured documentation, or summarise provenance narratives derived from existing records. Similar assistive approaches are emerging in digital curation, dataset documentation initiatives such as datasheets for datasets, and domain-specific archives99. Importantly, LLM outputs in this context must be treated as candidate interpretations subject to expert validation rather than authoritative metadata generation. This constraint preserves the primacy of standards-based governance while addressing practical documentation gaps.

More fundamentally, the integration of LLMs into geospatial workflows may shift FAIR from a structural external requirement toward an embedded scientific practice (Fig. 2). When integrated directly into GIS platforms, repository submission interfaces, or cloud-based geospatial analysis environments, LLM-enabled services can provide real-time assistance aligned with specific FAIR dimensions. Metadata enrichment and semantic tagging may support Findability; natural-language translation into OGC-compliant API queries may facilitate Accessibility; vocabulary alignment suggestions may assist Interoperability; and contextual explanations of lineage and methodological implications may enhance Reusability. At the repository level, FAIR-by-design architectures may combine deterministic ingestion pipelines with assistive LLM components. Upon dataset submission, structured metadata can be extracted automatically where available, while LLMs generate provisional summaries (with a human in the loop), multilingual descriptions, or contextual explanations that contributors subsequently review and refine. Comparable assistive paradigms are emerging in computational science and software engineering, where LLMs are used to promote best practices without displacing human responsibility or formal validation mechanisms100.

Embedding such automation does introduce governance risks. Research on automation bias and decision-support systems demonstrates that users may over-trust machine-generated outputs, particularly when systems appear authoritative86. In the context of FAIR workflows, this could result in uncritical acceptance of generated metadata or summaries that inadvertently omit important caveats. To mitigate these risks, LLM-generated content must remain explainable, explicitly linked to source materials or workflow logs, and clearly identified as assistive. Human-in-the-loop validation remains essential, particularly in high-stakes scientific or policy applications. Under this framing, LLMs do not redefine FAIR, nor do they replace deterministic standards. Instead, they function as catalytic intermediaries that reduce friction between scientific practice and FAIR compliance. Deterministic infrastructures remain the backbone of trustworthy geospatial data stewardship, while LLMs support interpretive mediation and legacy recovery. The result is not a relaxation of FAIR requirements but a potential transition from retrospective compliance toward workflow-integrated, embedded FAIR practice.

We provide an architecture illustrating how LLMs can be practically embedded within geospatial data infrastructures to support FAIR-aligned data interaction while preserving existing standards-based systems (Fig. 2). The process begins at the user and application layer, where researchers, policy analysts, or automated workflows submit natural-language or machine-generated queries to discover or access geospatial datasets. These requests are interpreted within the FAIR interaction and interpretation layer, where LLM-orchestrated services assist with tasks aligned to the FAIR principles, including metadata enrichment and semantic tagging (Findable), translation of natural-language queries into standardised service requests (Accessible), vocabulary and ontology alignment suggestions (Interoperable), and synthesis of provenance or contextual information to support dataset interpretation (Reusable). Outputs from this layer are treated as assistive artefacts rather than authoritative records and therefore pass through a validation, trust, and governance pipeline, where schema conformance checks, ontology consistency reasoning, provenance traceability, and human steward review ensure compliance with established standards. Once validated, requests and derived artefacts interact with standards-based geospatial services such as OGC APIs and catalogue services, which in turn access the underlying data and metadata infrastructure comprising spatial databases, sensor streams, metadata repositories, and controlled vocabularies. This layered approach enables LLMs to enhance interpretive and interaction capabilities while preserving deterministic, standards-governed operations and the integrity of the authoritative scientific record.

Challenges to realising this vision

Despite the promise of LLMs in operationalising the FAIR principles for geospatial data, several challenges remain before this vision can be realised at scale. Technical challenges

Perhaps the most visible obstacle lies in the well-documented problem of hallucination. LLMs frequently generate outputs that are syntactically plausible but semantically incorrect, a tendency that is particularly problematic in the geospatial domain, where precision is non-negotiable101,102. Mislabeling a coordinate reference system, confusing land cover categories, or producing incomplete provenance narratives can introduce cascading errors that compromise downstream analyses and decision-making. While RAG and fine-tuning with domain-specific corpora show promise in reducing hallucination103,104, systematic benchmarking against authoritative geospatial established standards remains limited, leaving questions of reliability unresolved.

A related technical barrier concerns standards alignment. FAIR principles in geospatial data are mediated through complex metadata schemas such as GeoDCAT-AP105, INSPIRE106, and the OGC’s evolving suite of standards107. Current LLMs, trained primarily on general web text, lack an intrinsic awareness of these schemas and often produce fluent but shallow descriptors of metadata108. This necessitates post-processing and undermines the efficiency gains that automation promises. Research on ontology alignment and schema mapping109,110 offers potential pathways, yet integration into LLM-assisted workflows remains nascent. There lies an opportunity to use LLMs for structured enrichment of metadata through LLM integration with tools and platforms capable of schema mapping. Configuring these LLM models to follow and adhere to specific metadata standards would be required to produce compliant output.

Finally, the computational and environmental costs of LLMs cannot be overlooked. Training and fine-tuning models require huge computational resources, energy, and cooling, generating significant carbon emissions111. For many geospatial applications, especially in low-resource contexts where urgent data needs relate to climate adaptation or biodiversity conservation, reliance on large proprietary models risks exacerbating digital inequities112. While lightweight architectures and federated approaches are emerging113, their maturity for geospatial FAIRification remains to be evaluated, particularly regarding the trade-off between costs and benefits.

Institutional challenges

Technical innovation alone will not guarantee adoption. A central institutional challenge is trust and governance. Many data providers and agencies remain hesitant to entrust critical tasks like metadata generation or provenance documentation to “black-box” AI systems. Without mechanisms for transparent auditing and accountability, it is unclear whether automated FAIR outputs satisfy legal, ethical, or scientific requirements26,114. Although efforts in explainable AI and dataset model cards115,116 provide useful precedents, their integration into geospatial infrastructures is minimal.

Policy alignment further complicates matters. Major research infrastructures, including EOSC and NASA’s Earth Science Data Systems117, increasingly promote FAIR-aligned data stewardship and provide frameworks and evaluation tools that encourage progressive improvement toward FAIR outcomes. However, these frameworks were not designed before the advent of LLM-enhanced FAIRification. Questions of liability, for example, if an LLM misrepresents data terms or produces misleading provenance, remain unaddressed in existing data governance regimes118,119. This policy lag risks leaving practitioners without clear guidance on how to integrate automation responsibly.

Capacity and equity are equally pressing. Even if LLM-based FAIR assistants become available, disparities in access to computational infrastructure, training, and linguistic coverage may reinforce rather than reduce inequities between well-resourced and under-resourced institutions120,121. These dynamics mirror earlier experiences with the adoption of geographical information systems, where technical gaps translated into uneven scientific participation and constrained the global reach of geospatial data stewardship.

Epistemic challenges

Beyond technical and institutional issues, the integration of LLMs into FAIR practices raises epistemic concerns about the very nature of expertise and stewardship. Automation bias is one such challenge: users may come to over-rely on AI-generated metadata, accepting them uncritically and institutionalising subtle but consequential errors122,123. Embedding human-in-the-loop validation checkpoints is therefore essential to maintain rigour and accountability.

Equally problematic is the opacity of LLM reasoning. Metadata, a cornerstone of geospatial data discovery, depends on clear documentation of how data were generated, transformed, and validated. Yet the internal workings of LLMs are often opaque, offering little transparency into why particular outputs are produced124,125. Without interpretable models that link generated metadata back to workflow logs or authoritative references, the trustworthiness of FAIRification pipelines risks erosion.

Finally, perhaps the most profound epistemic challenge is the shifting role of human expertise. Historically, geospatial data stewardship has relied on human curation, interpretation, and domain-specific judgment to ensure the credibility of shared datasets126–128. Embedding LLMs into these workflows may enable machines to handle portions of repetitive documentation or metadata generation, but it does not eliminate the need for expert oversight and governance. Instead, it shifts the balance of labour and blurs the boundary between human and machine contributions. This transformation raises important questions about which forms of expertise should be preserved, redefined, or selectively delegated to automation. Addressing this tension between efficiency and epistemic integrity will be critical if FAIRification is to be responsibly scaled in the era of LLMs.

Research and action agenda

Realising the transformative potential of LLMs for FAIR geospatial data requires more than technical advances alone. A structured agenda that combines research, infrastructure development, and policy innovation is necessary to ensure these tools deliver both scientific and societal value. Benchmarks and evaluation frameworks

Currently, there are no standardised benchmarks for assessing the quality of LLM-generated metadata, ontology alignments, or provenance narratives in the geospatial domain. This challenge is not isolated; rather, it reflects a broader and more fundamental gap in the availability of authoritative, operational tools for assessing FAIRness itself, particularly in complex and domain-specific workflows. As such, the absence of evaluation frameworks for LLM outputs should be understood as part of a larger systemic limitation, where both FAIR assessment and AI-driven FAIR implementation lack robust and widely accepted validation mechanisms. Addressing this issue, therefore, requires advancing benchmarking capabilities at two interconnected levels: 1) strengthening FAIRness assessment frameworks and 2) developing dedicated evaluation approaches for AI-assisted FAIR processes. While FAIR maturity indicators2 and stewardship frameworks such as the TRUST principles129 offer partial guidance, they were not designed with AI-generated content in mind and do not fully support automated or scalable evaluation. Developing shared benchmarks, annotated corpora, and domain-specific evaluation tasks will be essential not only to assess LLM outputs but also to advance more robust and operational FAIR evaluation practices. Experiences from machine translation130 and biomedical NLP131 illustrate the value of community-agreed testbeds for building confidence in automated systems. Without such benchmarks, LLMs risk producing metadata that is superficially fluent yet functionally unreliable, undermining their utility in high-stakes geospatial contexts.

A key requirement for future evaluation frameworks is the further development and operationalisation of confidence and validation metrics for AI-assisted stewardship. Rather than treating confidence as a single model output, recent work in uncertainty estimation emphasises combining multiple signals reflecting both model uncertainty and external verification132,133. Two complementary categories can be distinguished (Intrinsic and extrinsic confidence). Intrinsic confidence indicators originate from the LLM itself, including token probability distributions, output likelihoods, or agreement across repeated inference runs and model variants. Consistency-based approaches and ensemble prompting have been shown to provide practical proxies for uncertainty estimation in generative models134,135. However, such signals primarily reflect linguistic certainty and do not guarantee semantic or domain correctness. Extrinsic validation signals assess generated outputs against structured constraints and established standards. In geospatial FAIR workflows, this includes schema validation, ontology consistency checks, controlled vocabulary alignment, and provenance verification against existing metadata requirements. Rule-based validation remains central to trustworthy data stewardship and complements probabilistic AI outputs136–139.

Building on these complementary approaches, we suggest research on hybrid validation metrics in which confidence scores integrate intrinsic uncertainty estimates with external compliance metrics. Such scores function not as correctness guarantees but as decision-support indicators within stewardship workflows. Operationally, confidence metrics may support differentiated review pathways rather than automated approval. High-confidence outputs, characterised by model agreement and successful standards validation, can enter expedited expert review, while intermediate or low-confidence results trigger mandatory human assessment. Similar risk-tiered validation strategies are widely used in machine-assisted annotation and curation pipelines140–142. Future benchmarking efforts should therefore evaluate not only output quality but also validation transparency.

Domain-tuned and resource-efficient models

Most existing LLMs are trained on general-purpose corpora, resulting in minimal representation of domain-specific terminologies from OGC standards, INSPIRE metadata profiles, ISO 19115 descriptors, and associated portal or database schema. This mismatch leads to poor alignment with geospatial ontologies and inconsistent terminological precision68,143. Addressing this requires fine-tuning and instruction adaptation on curated geospatial corpora, drawing on precedents from climate science144 and biomedicine145, where domain-specific models have shown substantial performance gains. At the same time, resource efficiency must remain a priority. Large proprietary models carry prohibitive computational and environmental costs146, raising concerns about equity in low-resource settings147. Research into lightweight architectures, modular fine-tuning, and federated approaches could ensure that FAIRification powered by LLMs is accessible beyond a handful of well-funded institutions.

Human-in-the-Loop FAIRification

Rather than replacing human expertise, LLMs should be deployed as augmentative tools that reduce drudgery while preserving accountability in data stewardship. Embedding human-in-the-loop mechanisms allows experts to validate AI-generated metadata and provenance summaries before submission to repositories. Such oversight not only mitigates automation bias148 but also strengthens user trust in outputs.

To operationalise this, human-in-the-loop FAIRification can be implemented as a structured validation pipeline consisting of multiple stages. First, LLM-generated outputs (e.g., metadata records, ontology mappings, or summaries) can undergo automated pre-validation, including schema conformance checks (e.g., against GeoDCAT-AP149), controlled vocabulary matching, and completeness assessment. Second, outputs can be assigned confidence scores based on model certainty, provenance traceability, and alignment with existing standards. Third, these outputs are routed to human reviewers according to a tiered review model, where low-risk or high-confidence outputs receive lightweight verification, while complex or ambiguous cases are escalated to domain experts.

Research on the threshold for human involvement, cost-effective validation strategies is needed, ranging from tiered review systems in large repositories150 to citizen science–based curation models that leverage distributed expertise151. In addition, feedback loops can be established whereby corrections made by human reviewers are systematically captured and used to refine prompts, update validation rules, or retrain domain-adapted models. This creates a continuous learning cycle that improves both model performance and metadata quality over time. By situating FAIRification within collaborative workflows, LLMs can normalise best practices without eroding professional judgment. Such workflows also align with existing data stewardship practices by clearly assigning responsibility for final approval to human curators, ensuring that accountability remains explicit even in semi-automated environments.

Explainable and auditable AI for FAIR

Transparency and explainability are central to building trust in LLM-assisted FAIRification. Without traceable rationales, users may be left unable to verify whether generated metadata or ontology mappings are correct. Drawing from explainable AI research in health and legal domains152–156, LLM systems for geospatial data should provide explicit references for ontology choices, structured rationales for documented links between provenance narratives and underlying workflow logs.

Concretely, explainability can be achieved by requiring LLM systems to produce structured outputs that include (i) the source text or data used for metadata generation, (ii) the specific ontology terms or vocabularies selected, and (iii) a machine-readable justification linking input content to selected terms. These explanations can be stored alongside metadata records and exposed through user interfaces or APIs, enabling both human inspection and machine-actionable verification.

Auditability through versioning, model cards157, and dataset transparency practices such as datasheets for datasets26 further support accountability, enabling users to interrogate the provenance of AI outputs as rigorously as the datasets themselves. These practices align with data reusability requirements that emphasise provenance, contextual metadata, and transparent processing histories3. Also, auditability can be implemented through persistent logging of FAIRification workflows, including prompt versions, model configurations, timestamps, and transformation steps. These logs can be linked to metadata records using provenance standards such as W3C PROV, allowing complete reconstruction of how a given metadata element was generated or modified. Version control mechanisms can further ensure that all changes to metadata, whether human or AI-generated, are recorded and reversible.

However, explainability mechanisms alone are insufficient if transparency remains visible only to system operators. A critical requirement for trustworthy AI-assisted stewardship is research into reliable user-facing disclosure that indicates when metadata or descriptive elements have been generated or modified by AI systems. Studies of automation bias demonstrate that users frequently over-trust algorithmic outputs when uncertainty or authorship is not explicitly communicated158–160. To mitigate this risk and support informed reuse decisions, we propose the adoption of standardised AI provenance and disclosure labels embedded directly within AI-assisted metadata records. Such indicators could function as persistent “AI-assisted” flags or labels that travel with datasets across catalogues and services, extending provenance annotation practices already recommended in FAIR-aligned infrastructures and workflow provenance models such as W3C PROV161,162.

Operationally, these indicators may include machine-readable metadata fields specifying (i) whether AI contributed to metadata creation or enrichment, (ii) the type of task performed (e.g., summarisation, ontology alignment, translation), (iii) the model or workflow version used, and (iv) associated confidence or validation status.

These fields can be integrated into existing metadata schemas (e.g., as extensions to DCAT or GeoDCAT-AP), ensuring interoperability with current catalogue infrastructures while enhancing transparency. In user-facing systems, visual indicators (e.g., badges or labels) can signal AI involvement, while expandable views provide access to detailed explanations, provenance traces, and validation status.

Visible labels within discovery interfaces would allow users to immediately recognise AI-derived components while preserving access to supporting explanations and validation evidence. Comparable transparency mechanisms have been advocated within responsible AI governance frameworks, emphasizing disclosure, traceability, and accountability as prerequisites for trustworthy AI adoption163–165. Importantly, such labelling should not function as a warning against AI use, but as a mechanism for informed interpretation consistent with open science principles of reproducibility and methodological transparency166,167. Embedding persistent AI provenance signals within metadata, therefore, represents a practical extension of FAIR principles, ensuring that increased automation does not compromise trust, reproducibility, or responsible reuse.

Integration pilots and living labs

The practical value of LLMs for FAIRification can only be demonstrated through integration of pilots in real-world infrastructures. However, such capabilities can first be validated in small-scale or contained environments before being progressively upscaled as confidence and maturity increase. Embedding conversational FAIR assistants, multilingual discovery interfaces, or auto-generated dataset cards within these systems would create “living labs” where technical feasibility and user acceptance can be jointly assessed. Lessons from biomedical repositories, where AI-assisted metadata enrichment has been piloted successfully168, suggest that usability and compliance both improve when such assistants are deployed at the point of data ingestion. Geospatial data stands to benefit from similar experimental deployments.

Policy and governance innovations

Finally, technical progress must be accompanied by governance reforms that clarify the role of AI in data stewardship. Questions of liability for AI-generated outputs, such as misrepresented descriptions, remain unresolved under current frameworks. Establishing minimum quality standards for automated metadata, defining shared accountability models, and embedding equity considerations into AI-assisted FAIRification are therefore urgent tasks. International initiatives such as GEOSS and the UN-GGIM169 provide venues where these issues can be debated and codified at a global scale170. Without such policy alignment, LLM-assisted FAIRification risks reinforcing institutional fragmentation rather than enabling the open, trusted geospatial commons envisioned by the FAIR principles.

Conclusion and recommendations

We document how LLMs offer a practical pathway to accelerate FAIRification, transforming it from some of its theoretical guidelines into an integrated, user-friendly practice. By providing assistive functions to processes such as metadata enrichment, conversational access, semantic alignment, and automated provenance documentation, LLMs can lower barriers to adoption and expand the scientific and societal value of geospatial data.

We discussed that realising this potential requires addressing key challenges, including the reliability of AI-generated outputs, the absence of FAIR-specific evaluation benchmarks, risks of semantic drift in ontology alignment, and institutional hesitancy toward black-box systems. We propose a research agenda, covering benchmarking, domain-specific tuning, human-in-the-loop governance, explainability, pilot deployments, and policy innovation, which provides a roadmap for responsible adoption.

At the same time, the rapid emergence of LLM-based tools in the geospatial domain highlights the need for a more systematic assessment of the evolving research landscape. We recommend that future studies would benefit from conducting comprehensive reviews/surveys that quantitatively map how existing work addresses the four FAIR principles, identifying areas of concentration as well as gaps that remain underexplored.

We conclude that thoughtful deployment of LLMs can shift FAIRification from a compliance task to a practical, embedded component of research. Achieving this vision, however, depends on careful evaluation, interdisciplinary collaboration, and sustained institutional support.
