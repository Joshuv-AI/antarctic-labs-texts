Impact of recording technologies on cardiac electrogram morphology: implications for mapping and interpretation
Authors: Mathijs S. van Schie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cardiac electrograms form the foundation of contemporary clinical electrophysiology and are essential for activation mapping, substrate characterization and catheter ablation of cardiac arrhythmias. Electrogram morphology, however, is not solely determined by myocardial electrophysiology, but also by multiple technological factors related to signal acquisition and processing. Electrode size, interelectrode spacing, electrode orientation, tissue coupling, filtering settings and annotation algorithms all influence electrogram morphology, voltage and activation timing. Consequently, identical myocardial activation patterns may generate markedly different electrograms depending on the recording configuration used. Recent advances in high-density mapping technologies, multi-electrode electrogram reconstruction and advanced signal-processing techniques have further expanded the possibilities and complexity of electrogram interpretation. This review discusses the biophysical principles underlying cardiac electrogram formation and examines the technological determinants that influence electrogram characteristics. In addition, the properties, advantages and limitations of different electrogram types, including unipolar, bipolar, multipolar, omnipolar and Laplacian electrograms, are reviewed together with their implications for voltage and activation mapping. A comprehensive understanding of both the physiological basis and technological limitations of electrogram acquisition remains essential for accurate electrogram interpretation and responsible advancement of cardiac arrhythmia mapping and ablation strategies.

Introduction

Cardiac electrograms form the foundation of contemporary clinical electrophysiology; their interpretation underlies virtually every aspect of invasive arrhythmia diagnosis and catheter ablation procedure. Over the past decades, advances in mapping technologies have dramatically increased the spatial and temporal resolution with which cardiac activation can be recorded. At the same time, interpretation of electrograms has become increasingly complex.

Traditionally, electrogram interpretation focused primarily on the relationship between electrogram morphology and myocardial substrate [[1], [2], [3]]. Features such as low-voltage, fractionation, double potentials and prolonged potential duration have been linked to fibrosis, conduction abnormalities and arrhythmogenic substrate [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. However, electrogram morphology is also strongly influenced by the recording technology, including electrode size, interelectrode spacing, electrode orientation, tissue contact, filtering settings and signal processing algorithms [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16]].

These effects have become increasingly relevant with the introduction of high-density mapping systems and novel catheter technologies. Conventional bipolar recordings are inherently dependent on wavefront direction and catheter orientation, whereas electrode size and interelectrode spacing influence spatial averaging and incorporation of far-field activity [11,12,17,18]. As a result, electrograms and voltage maps are not simply interchangeable across mapping technologies.

Novel recording approaches have therefore emerged to improve electrogram characterization. Still, electro-anatomical mapping predominantly relies on unipolar and bipolar electrograms, which additionally form the basis for more advanced signal reconstructions including multipolar, omnipolar and spatial derivative-based mapping techniques [[19], [20], [21]]. High-density multi-electrode catheters enable more detailed spatial sampling of activation [20]. Omnipolar mapping techniques aim to reduce directional sensitivity by reconstructing orientation-independent electrograms from multiple orthogonal bipoles [21]. In addition, advanced signal-processing and annotation algorithms increasingly influence how electrograms are displayed and interpreted.

Understanding the physiological and technological basis of electrogram formation is therefore essential for accurate interpretation of intracardiac recordings. This review discusses the biophysical principles underlying electrogram formation, the technological determinants influencing electrogram morphology and the characteristics and clinical implications of different electrogram types.

Biophysical basis of cardiac electrograms

Cardiac electrograms represent extracellular voltage differences generated by the propagation of electrical activation through myocardial tissue. During depolarization and repolarization, transmembrane ionic currents produce local extracellular voltage gradients that can be detected by recording electrodes positioned on the myocardium [22]. The morphology of recorded potentials therefore reflects the spatial and temporal relationship between propagating activation wavefronts and the recording electrode. Consequently, potential morphology reflects not only local activation itself, but also the way in which activation propagates through the surrounding myocardium.

At the cellular level, activation of cardiomyocytes is initiated by rapid inward sodium current, resulting in depolarization and propagation of the action potential through the myocardium. These transmembrane currents generate extracellular voltage fields in the surrounding tissue. As an activation wavefront propagates, it creates positive extracellular potentials ahead of the activation wavefront and negative extracellular potentials behind it. Because this extracellular voltage field moves together with the propagating wavefront, a recording electrode initially registers increasingly positive potentials as the wavefront approaches, followed by a rapid negative deflection as the wavefront passes beneath the recording electrode, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Experimental studies demonstrated that potential morphology is strongly influenced by wavefront direction, conduction velocity, myocardial anisotropy and tissue architecture [6,8,[23], [24], [25], [26]]. Rapid and uniform conduction generally produces sharp, high-frequency potentials, whereas slow or heterogeneous conduction results in broader and more fractionated potentials [8,27]. Preferential myocardial fiber orientation results in direction-dependent conduction properties and corresponding alterations in potential morphology [24].

Structural discontinuities within the myocardium further contribute to electrogram complexity. Fibrosis, adipose infiltration, connective tissue separation and variations in fiber orientation may interrupt uniform wavefront propagation and generate localized conduction delay or block [1,24,25,28,29]. These disturbances may result in fractionation, double potentials and prolonged potential duration [1,8,24,28,29]. In arrhythmogenic tissue, such abnormalities often reflect increased conduction inhomogeneity and complex activation patterns [25,30].

Extracellular potentials are additionally influenced by the conductive properties of surrounding structures, including blood, myocardium and connective tissue [1,2]. Consequently, intracardiac electrograms generally consist of both near-field and far-field components. Near-field potentials arise from myocardium immediately adjacent to the recording electrode and primarily reflect local electrical activation. These signals are typically characterized by sharp, high-frequency deflections with steep intrinsic negative slopes and relatively high temporal resolution. In contrast, far-field potentials originate from more distant (cardiac) structures and generally appear as broader, smoother and lower-frequency deflections [2,22,23]. A common example is ventricular far-field activity that may be observed in atrial electrograms, as shown in the upper left panel of Fig. 2. Also, endocardial recordings may contain contributions from epicardial activation and vice versa [31]. Asynchronous activation of adjacent tissue bundles may additionally generate multiple near-field components, resulting in fractionation or double potentials, as shown in the upper left panel of Fig. 3 [25]. The distinction between near-field and far-field activity is clinically important for accurate electrogram interpretation and local activation annotation. Near-field potentials generally provide the most reliable information regarding local activation timing and substrate characteristics, whereas far-field signals may obscure or distort electrograms [1]. Electrode size

The dimensions of a recording electrode substantially influence electrogram morphology and spatial resolution. Rather than measuring electrical activity at a single point, electrodes record the average extracellular potential across their conductive surface [11,13]. Electrode dimensions used in contemporary clinical mapping studies typically range from approximately 0.4–1 mm for high-density multi-electrode catheters to 3.5 mm tip electrodes for conventional ablation catheters. Although electrograms recorded with high-density mapping catheters and conventional ablation catheters are not directly interchangeable, both catheter types are often used complementarily during clinical procedures.

Larger electrodes sample electrical activity over a broader myocardial area, thereby smoothing local voltage gradients and attenuating high-frequency signal components, acting as a spatial low-pass filter [11,13]. As a result, electrograms recorded with larger electrodes generally appear broader and less fractionated, with greater susceptibility to far-field activity. In contrast, smaller electrodes preserve greater local detail and typically produce sharper electrograms with steeper intrinsic deflections and increased high-frequency content, at the expense of a lower signal-to-noise ratio [11,13,14,18].

Experimental and computational studies demonstrated that increasing electrode size progressively reduces electrogram complexity and alters activation map characteristics, particularly in regions with heterogeneous conduction [11,13]. Using computational modeling, Abdi et al. [13] demonstrated that increasing the electrode size increased errors in estimation of local activation time and reduced the apparent length of conduction block lines. The resulting activation maps generated by larger electrodes were more homogeneous with a lower number of observed activation wavefronts. The increase in electrode size also increased the extent of identified low-voltage areas while decreasing potential slopes and number of detected deflections. These effects were most pronounced at sites with more complex activation patterns. High-resolution clinical mapping studies further showed that smaller electrodes indeed improve delineation of low-voltage areas and facilitate identification of localized abnormal electrograms compared with conventional ablation electrodes [18,32]. The impact of electrode size becomes increasingly important with modern high-density mapping technologies, which use catheter-specific electrode dimensions and configurations.

Interelectrode spacing

Interelectrode spacing is a major technological determinant of electrogram morphology and spatial resolution in multi-electrode recording configurations, including bipolar, omnipolar, multipolar and Laplacian electrograms. These electrograms are derived from combinations or spatial processing of signals recorded by two or more adjacent electrodes and therefore represent a spatial derivative of the extracellular potential field, acting as spatial high-pass filters [11]. Interelectrode spacing therefore directly determines the spatial scale over which extracellular voltage gradients are sampled. Contemporary clinical mapping systems use interelectrode spacings ranging from approximately 2-6 mm in conventional mapping and ablation catheters to ≤1-3 mm in modern high-density multipolar catheters [14,17,18,33].

Narrow interelectrode spacing preferentially emphasizes localized electrical activity, whereas wider spacing samples a broader myocardial region and increases susceptibility to far-field activity [14,17]. Experimental and computational studies demonstrated that increasing interelectrode spacing generally increases bipolar voltages due to sampling of extracellular voltage gradients over a larger spatial area [11,12,17,34]. Hwang et al. [11] demonstrated that interelectrode spacing interacts closely with wavefront direction and tissue characteristics in determining bipolar electrogram morphology, whereas Beheshti et al. [34] showed that wavefront angle substantially modulates spacing-dependent effects on bipolar voltage amplitudes. Takigawa et al. [17] further demonstrated that wider spacing enlarges the effective recording field and alters local electrogram characterization. Earlier experimental studies additionally demonstrated that variations in electrode spacing influence electrogram resolution and the ability to detect localized activation patterns [35]. More recent investigations confirmed that even small differences in interelectrode spacing may substantially alter voltage maps and low-voltage area delineation [[36], [37], [38]].

Interelectrode spacing additionally influences potential duration and complexity. Wider spacing may merge asynchronous local activations into a single broader bipolar potential, whereas narrow spacing may resolve distinct deflections that would otherwise remain obscured [11,17]. These effects become particularly relevant in arrhythmogenic tissue characterized by anisotropic propagation and complex activation patterns. Similar spacing-dependent effects have also been described in multipolar, omnipolar and Laplacian recording configurations, where spatial sampling characteristics directly influence electrogram reconstruction and voltage characterization [[19], [20], [21]]. In omnipolar mapping, interelectrode spacing determines the spatial scale over which local electric field vectors are reconstructed and may therefore influence estimation of activation direction and conduction velocity [21,38,39].

Interelectrode spacing is closely related to electrode size, as both jointly determine the spatial resolution and near-field selectivity of the recording configuration. Smaller electrodes combined with shorter interelectrode spacing generally improve localization of near-field activity and detection of localized conduction abnormalities, whereas larger electrodes with wider spacing increase spatial averaging and far-field incorporation [14,17,18]. Therefore, electrograms obtained using different catheter configurations cannot be simply combined, particularly when fixed voltage thresholds are used for substrate definition. Several experimental and clinical studies suggested that shorter interelectrode spacings of approximately 1-3 mm improve near-field selectivity and high-resolution characterization of localized activation abnormalities, whereas slightly wider spacing may improve substrate delineation during scar mapping by increasing sensitivity to broader voltage gradients [14,[37], [38], [39]]. The optimal electrode size-spacing relationship therefore depends on the intended mapping objective, underlying substrate complexity and targeted spatial scale of activation.

Electrode orientation and directionality

Electrode orientation relative to the propagating activation wavefront is an important technological determinant of potential morphology in bipolar electrograms. Because these electrograms are derived from spatial voltage differences between two recording electrodes, potential morphology and voltage depend strongly on wavefront direction relative to the bipole axis, as shown in the middle and right panels of Fig. 2 [11,12]. Bipolar voltages are maximal during wavefront propagation parallel to the bipole and attenuated during perpendicular propagation [11,12,40]. In the atria, bipolar potential voltages were demonstrated to be on average 1.66 (range: 1.0–59.0) times larger than corresponding perpendicular bipolar potential voltages using a 2.25 mm grid-electrode [40]. Consequently, identical activation patterns may be represented by markedly different bipolar potentials depending solely on catheter orientation relative to local activation direction [11,12,17,40].

Directional sensitivity becomes particularly important during complex arrhythmias characterized by continuously changing activation wavefront directions. Under such conditions, catheter orientation may substantially influence potential voltages, morphology and low-voltage area delineation [40,41]. Recognition of these limitations contributed to the development of orientation-independent mapping approaches, including omnipolar electrogram reconstruction techniques.

Contact force and tissue coupling

Adequate electrode-tissue coupling is essential for accurate electrogram recording. Insufficient tissue contact reduces the contribution of local electrical activity and increases susceptibility to far-field [14,[42], [43], [44]]. Poor tissue contact generally results in lower potential voltages and reduced high-frequency content, whereas improved electrode-tissue coupling increases the contribution of near-field activity and typically produces steeper intrinsic deflections [14,16,[42], [43], [44]]. Recent quantitative analyses by Schlageter et al. [16] further demonstrated that potential voltage, slope and high-frequency content progressively decreased with increasing electrode-tissue distance, whereas potential duration increased with increasing distance from the myocardial surface. These relationships were observed for both unipolar and bipolar electrograms. Whereas Schlageter et al. primarily investigated progressive loss of electrode-tissue coupling with increasing electrode distance, Ullah et al. [44] demonstrated that increasing contact force also significantly alters potential voltage and fractionation characteristics during both sinus rhythm and atrial fibrillation.

Besides contact force itself, catheter orientation relative to the tissue surface also influences electrogram characteristics. Variations in contact angle alter the effective electrode-tissue interface and thereby influence potential morphology and voltage [45]. The effects of tissue coupling become particularly important during substrate mapping and catheter ablation procedures. Variability in tissue contact may contribute to inaccurate voltage characterization and inconsistent delineation of low-voltage areas [[42], [43], [44], [45]].

Filtering and signal processing

Filtering and signal processing are essential components of contemporary electrogram recording systems. Intracardiac electrograms contain a broad spectrum of electrical signals originating from local myocardial activation, distant cardiac structures, skeletal muscle activity, baseline drift and environmental noise. Therefore, recorded signals are routinely processed using analog and digital filtering techniques to improve signal quality and interpretation [1,2,46].

High-pass filtering suppresses low-frequency components such as baseline wander and motion artifacts, whereas low-pass filtering attenuates high-frequency noise, as illustrated in Fig. 2 and Fig. 3 [1,2]. A study by Starreveld et al. [46] demonstrated that increasing high-pass filter frequencies on unipolar electrograms progressively reduces potential voltage, fractionation and fractionation delay time, whereas low-pass filtering may smooth deflections and suppress fractionated potentials. Notch filtering could even introduce fractionation. Takigawa et al. [15] further demonstrated that bipolar electrograms are also strongly influenced by filter settings and suggested that bandwidths of 30-250 Hz or 20-500 Hz without additional 50 Hz notch filtering provide optimal signal characterization. Filtering effects become particularly relevant during substrate mapping and electrogram-guided ablation procedures, where potential fractionation, duration and voltage may vary substantially depending on the applied filter settings [1,15,46,47]. Electrograms identified as abnormal using one filtering configuration may not necessarily remain abnormal when different signal-processing parameters are applied. These effects become especially important in complex arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, where potential morphology already demonstrates substantial temporal and spatial variability.

Sampling frequency additionally influences electrogram fidelity and temporal accuracy. Insufficient sampling rates may result in loss of high-frequency information and inaccurate representation of small deflections, whereas higher sampling frequencies improve preservation of detailed potential morphology at the cost of more storage consumption and heavier processing loads [1,2]. Modern high-density mapping systems therefore utilize sampling frequencies of 1-2 kHz combined with advanced digital signal processing techniques to optimize electrogram characterization.

More advanced signal-processing approaches, including spectral analysis, phase mapping and wavelet decomposition, have further expanded electrogram interpretation beyond conventional time-domain assessment by enabling characterization of signal organization, frequency content and spatiotemporal activation behavior [[48], [49], [50]]. Spectral analysis characterizes the frequency content and temporal organization of electrograms, whereas phase mapping reconstructs spatiotemporal activation dynamics to identify repetitive activation patterns and rotational activity. Wavelet decomposition enables multiscale analysis of electrogram morphology by separating signals into different frequency and temporal components. These techniques are widely applied in the analysis of complex arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation.

Annotation algorithms and mapping system processing

Contemporary electro-anatomical mapping systems increasingly rely on automated annotation algorithms and advanced signal-processing pipelines to facilitate rapid electrogram interpretation and map construction. Modern mapping platforms continuously process electrograms through multiple computational steps, including filtering, interpolation, beat selection, activation annotation and voltage calculation [1,2,51].

Automated annotation algorithms identify local activation times based on predefined potential features, including maximal bipolar amplitude, steepest unipolar slope or peak negative derivative [1,2]. Although these approaches substantially improve mapping efficiency, annotation accuracy may be reduced in regions with fractionation, low-voltage potentials, far-field activity or complex activation patterns [1,51,52]. In such regions, small differences in annotation criteria may produce substantial variations in activation timing and reconstructed activation maps. These maps may contain artificial conduction delay, wavefront discontinuities or apparent rotational activity generated by processing limitations rather than true electrophysiological phenomena [50,52,53]. Recent developments additionally incorporate machine learning and artificial intelligence-based processing approaches for electrogram annotation, activation pattern recognition and substrate identification [50,54,55]. A recent study of Seno et al. [54] additionally explored deep learning-based reconstruction of activation patterns directly from unipolar electrograms, potentially enabling annotation-independent characterization of complex atrial activation dynamics. However, artificial intelligence-based approaches remain fundamentally dependent on the quality of the underlying electrograms and the assumptions embedded within training datasets and annotation frameworks.

Modern mapping systems additionally incorporate interpolation algorithms to reconstruct activation and voltage maps from discrete electrode recordings [1,2]. Although interpolation improves map visualization and facilitates identification of large-scale activation patterns, excessive interpolation may artificially smooth conduction abnormalities or create misleading propagation patterns in regions with limited spatial point density [1,53,56,57].

Types of electrograms and their technological characteristics

Electrograms can be broadly categorized into unipolar and bipolar recordings, which form the basis of most contemporary mapping techniques. Unipolar electrograms represent extracellular potentials recorded relative to a distant reference electrode, whereas bipolar electrograms are generated by calculating the voltage difference between two adjacent recording electrodes. More advanced electrogram configurations, including omnipolar, multipolar and Laplacian electrograms, are largely derived from combinations or spatial processing of these fundamental signal types. Unipolar electrograms

In the unipolar recording mode, an exploring electrode is positioned on the myocardial tissue, while the indifferent electrode is positioned at a large distance from the heart or connected to the Wilson's Central Terminal. Because unipolar recordings measure absolute extracellular potentials, they provide a broad representation of surrounding myocardial activation and are strongly influenced by both local and distant electrical activity.

The morphology of unipolar electrograms directly reflects the spatiotemporal relationship between the recording electrode and propagating activation wavefronts. As a depolarization wavefront approaches the recording electrode, extracellular current flow initially generates a positive deflection (R-wave) followed by a steep negative intrinsic deflection when activation passes beneath the electrode, after which the signal gradually returns toward baseline as activation propagates away from the recording site (S-wave), as illustrated in Fig. 1 [25,58]. The maximal negative slope of a unipolar potential, commonly referred to as the steepest negative dV/dt, closely reflects local activation timing and therefore forms the basis of activation mapping in both experimental and clinical electrophysiology [4,25,58]. Unipolar electrograms are characterized by relatively straightforward interpretation of the potential morphology, which is independent of the direction of the activation wavefront.

At a site where an activation wavefront arises, a unipolar potential will only be negative as the activation wavefront only propagates away from the recording electrode (monophasic S-wave or QS-morphology). At a site of conduction block or at the end of an activation wavefront, a unipolar potential will only be positive as the activation wavefront only propagates toward the recording electrode (monophasic R-wave). However, in the majority of cases the potentials will be biphasic (RS-morphology), as the activation wavefront passes the recording electrode. The morphology of unipolar potentials therefore also contains information on local conduction characteristics [6,26,59].

Experimental studies by Spach et al. demonstrated that unipolar potential morphology is strongly influenced by conduction velocity, anisotropy and myocardial microstructure [25,58]. Structural discontinuities and nonuniform anisotropic conduction may produce complex unipolar potential morphologies, including fractionation, double potentials and prolonged potential duration [25,58]. High-resolution mapping studies by Konings et al. [8] demonstrated that unipolar fibrillation potentials exhibit considerable morphological variability reflecting local activation complexity and conduction heterogeneity. Distinct morphologies including single, double and fractionated potentials were associated with varying degrees of wavefront collision, conduction delay and asynchronous activation. These unipolar potential morphologies were also shown to reflect varying degrees of conduction discontinuity and local activation complexity during sinus rhythm [7].

Unipolar electrograms have regained considerable interest in contemporary clinical electrophysiology because of their ability to characterize deeper and transmural substrate components that may remain undetected by bipolar recordings [13,40,[60], [61], [62], [63], [64], [65]]. In ventricular tachycardia ablation, the morphology of unipolar potentials is increasingly used to identify intramural and epicardial substrates or the depth of ectopic foci [66,67]. In addition, loss of S-wave amplitude during radiofrequency and pulsed-field ablation have been investigated as markers of lesion transmurality and lesion durability [61,64,65,68,69]. However, because peak-to-peak unipolar voltages are determined by both the R- and S-wave components and therefore strongly depend on wavefront propagation characteristics, unipolar potential morphology should always be interpreted alongside voltage measurements.

Bipolar electrograms

Bipolar electrograms are generated by calculating the voltage difference between two closely spaced recording electrodes using either a differential amplifier or post-processing of unipolar electrograms [1,11]. In contrast to unipolar electrograms, bipolar recordings emphasize local differences in extracellular voltage and thereby accentuate local myocardial activation while attenuating distant far-field activity [1,2,11]. A defining characteristic of bipolar electrograms is their strong dependence on the spatial relationship between the propagating activation wavefront and the electrode pair, with maximal bipolar voltage observed when activation propagates parallel to the bipole axis [1,11,12]. Because of their relative resistance to remote electrical interference and ease of clinical interpretation, bipolar electrograms have become the predominant signal type used in contemporary clinical electrophysiology and electro-anatomical mapping systems.

Experimental and computational studies demonstrated that bipolar electrograms function as spatial high-pass filters that emphasize localized activation gradients while suppressing more homogeneous distant electrical activity [15,17,47]. Consequently, bipolar electrograms often provide improved discrimination of local activation compared with unipolar recordings in regions with overlapping far-field signals. However, interpretation of bipolar electrogram morphology is less straightforward than interpretation of unipolar electrograms. Local activation time is generally determined by identifying the largest bipolar deflection or peak amplitude. Potential polarity and morphology strongly depend on wavefront direction relative to the electrode pair, with predominantly positive deflections generated when activation propagates toward the negative electrode and predominantly negative deflections generated when activation propagates toward the positive electrode.

The morphology of bipolar potentials during arrhythmias may become highly complex due to wavefront collision, conduction slowing, anisotropy and structural discontinuities within the myocardium. Fractionated bipolar potentials, prolonged durations and low-voltage signals have therefore been extensively investigated as markers of arrhythmogenic substrate in atrial fibrillation and ventricular tachycardia. However, many conflicting definitions and methodologies to identify bipolar fractionation exist [70].

Recent developments increasingly focus on overcoming the inherent directional sensitivity and configuration dependence of bipolar electrograms. Approaches including omnipolar mapping, high-density multipolar catheters and advanced signal-processing algorithms aim to improve reproducibility and reduce orientation-dependent variability [20,21,40]. Despite these limitations, bipolar electrograms remain the clinical standard for electro-anatomical mapping because of their robust local signal representation, reduced susceptibility to far-field activity and extensive validation in clinical electrophysiology.

Multi-electrode and high-density mapping

Multi-electrode and high-density mapping technologies were developed to improve spatial sampling of complex cardiac activation patterns by simultaneously recording electrograms from large numbers of closely spaced electrodes [3,18,42]. The increased spatial sampling density reduces interpolation distances between recording sites and thereby improves characterization of localized activation patterns and conduction heterogeneities. However, increasing mapping density also increases the complexity of electrogram interpretation and dependence on catheter configuration and signal-processing characteristics [1,32,42,57].

Contemporary catheter designs include linear, circular, grid and basket configurations, each with distinct spatial sampling properties and directional sensitivities. Grid-based configurations provide more uniform spatial sampling and facilitate assessment of activation propagating along multiple directions, whereas spline-based or linear catheter designs are more susceptible to directional bias depending on wavefront orientation.

The development of multi-electrode and high-density mapping arrays also laid the foundation for more advanced electrogram configurations and signal-processing approaches. Simultaneous recordings from multiple closely spaced electrodes enable spatial reconstruction of local activation patterns, electric field vectors and higher-order spatial derivatives that cannot be derived from conventional unipolar or bipolar recordings alone. Therefore, advanced electrogram configurations and reconstruction techniques such as omnipolar, multipolar and Laplacian-like electrograms are increasingly incorporated within high-density mapping systems.

Omnipolar electrograms

Omnipolar electrograms were developed to overcome the directional dependence of bipolar recordings [21]. Rather than relying on a predefined electrode pair, omnipolar mapping utilizes multiple closely spaced electrodes to reconstruct the local extracellular electric field (E-field). By combining unipolar electrograms within a localized electrode clique, typically consisting of three or four electrodes arranged in a triangular or square configuration, the local E-field vector can be estimated and used to determine the direction of maximal voltage change, corresponding to the local activation direction, as illustrated in Figs. 4E and 5E. The local E-field represents the spatial gradient of the extracellular potential and therefore reflects the direction of maximal voltage change within the sampled tissue region. A virtual bipolar electrogram can subsequently be reconstructed along this direction, resulting in an orientation-independent representation of local activation that is less affected by catheter rotation, as illustrated in Figs. 4F and 5F. Under the assumption of locally planar wavefront propagation, omnipolar mapping additionally enables estimation of activation direction and conduction velocity directly from a single electrode clique [71,72].

Experimental and clinical validation studies demonstrated that omnipolar electrograms provide more consistent measurements of potential voltage, activation timing, activation direction and conduction velocity than conventional bipolar electrograms [40,72]. Omnipolar potential voltages are generally larger than corresponding maximal bipolar clique voltages (±11%) [40]. Consequently, omnipolar mapping improves delineation of low-voltage areas [40,41,73]. Furthermore, because omnipolar electrograms are reconstructed from local electrode cliques rather than predefined bipoles, a larger proportion of recorded electrode data can be incorporated into voltage and activation maps, thereby increasing mapping density and the number of analyzable electrograms derived from a given catheter configuration [73].

The performance of omnipolar mapping is strongly influenced by electrode geometry and spatial sampling density. Reconstruction of the local E-field assumes that activation behavior can be adequately represented within the selected electrode clique. Triangular configurations represent the minimum electrode arrangement required for direct estimation of two-dimensional voltage gradients, whereas square or orthogonal configurations provide additional redundancy at the expense of increased dependence on assumptions regarding local wavefront behavior [39]. Similarly, increasing interelectrode spacing enlarges the effective sampling area and may violate the assumption of locally planar wavefront propagation, particularly in regions with wavefront curvature, collision, conduction slowing or fractionation [38]. Consequently, although omnipolar electrograms substantially reduce orientation dependence, they remain dependent on electrode geometry, spatial sampling density and the validity of assumptions regarding local wavefront behavior.

Multipolar, laplacian and advanced spatial derivative techniques

Several advanced electrogram configurations have been developed to improve characterization of local myocardial activation by mathematically emphasizing local extracellular voltage gradients while suppressing distant far-field components. These approaches extend the spatial-derivative principles underlying bipolar electrograms by incorporating information from multiple surrounding electrodes to reconstruct higher-order representations of the extracellular potential field.

Recently, multipolar electrograms have been introduced as a novel signal configuration derived from simultaneous analysis of multiple adjacent electrodes [20]. Rather than relying on a single electrode pair, multipolar electrograms combine information from several neighboring electrodes to improve measurement accuracy and robustness of local electrogram characterization. The unipolar potential of each electrode and its cluster of surrounding electrodes are sampled within a window of interest, and fed into a Principal Component Analysis algorithm to calculate a score based on similarity of voltage measurements to all other electrodes within the cluster. Each unipolar potential is multiplied by its similarity score to obtain weighted unipolar potential. A far-field component is constructed by summing the weighted components and normalizing it by the number of evaluated electrodes. The multipolar electrogram is then generated by subtracting the common far-field component from the unipolar electrogram, resulting in an electrogram that retains the shape of the local unipolar potential, as illustrated in Figs. 4G and 5G. Initial studies demonstrated improved signal-to-noise ratio, reproducibility of voltage measurements and activation characterization compared with conventional bipolar electrograms, particularly in regions with complex activation patterns or unfavorable electrode orientation.

The Laplacian electrogram represents a fundamentally different approach and is typically calculated by combining unipolar recordings from a central electrode with neighboring surrounding electrodes, thereby approximating the second spatial derivative of the extracellular potential. Whereas bipolar and omnipolar electrograms are based on first-order spatial voltage gradients, Laplacian electrograms emphasize local curvature of the extracellular potential field. Consequently, spatially distributed electrical activity contributes similarly to neighboring electrodes and is largely suppressed, whereas highly localized voltage gradients are amplified, as illustrated in Figs. 4H and 5H. Experimental studies demonstrated improved localization of activation wavefronts and conduction abnormalities compared with conventional unipolar and bipolar recordings [19,74]. However, these techniques may also increase susceptibility to recording noise and signal-processing artifacts.

As electrogram processing becomes increasingly sophisticated, recordings become progressively less dependent on individual electrodes and increasingly dependent on spatial reconstruction algorithms and underlying mathematical assumptions.

Clinical implications for electrogram interpretation

As discussed above, electrograms are influenced by a complex interaction between myocardial electrophysiology and recording technologies. Therefore, electrograms should not be simply regarded as direct measurements of myocardial substrate, but rather as technological representations of underlying electrical activity. Accurate interpretation therefore requires consideration of both the physiological processes generating the signal and the technological factors influencing its recording and reconstruction. Implications for voltage mapping

Voltage mapping is widely used for identification of low-voltage areas and characterization of arrhythmogenic substrate. However, potential voltage is strongly influenced by electrode size, interelectrode spacing, wavefront direction, tissue contact and signal-processing settings. As a result, identical myocardial tissue may generate substantially different potential voltages when recorded using different catheter configurations or mapping systems. Therefore, fixed voltage thresholds should be interpreted cautiously and always in the context of the recording technology used. Comprehensive substrate characterization should additionally incorporate potential morphology, duration and fractionation rather than relying exclusively on peak-to-peak voltage thresholds.

Implications for activation mapping

Accurate activation mapping depends on reliable annotation of local activation times and correct interpretation of activation wavefront propagation. However, activation timing may be influenced by potential morphology, annotation algorithms, electrode spacing and recording configuration. Fractionated potentials, far-field activity and complex activation patterns may introduce uncertainty in activation annotation and consequently affect reconstructed propagation maps. Advanced electrogram configurations such as omnipolar and spatial derivative-based approaches may improve estimation of activation direction and conduction velocity, but remain dependent on assumptions regarding local wavefront behavior and spatial sampling. Therefore, activation maps should always be interpreted in conjunction with the underlying electrograms rather than as independent representations of cardiac activation.

Collectively, these observations highlight that meaningful interpretation of electro-anatomical maps requires understanding of both the underlying electrophysiology and the technological factors governing electrogram acquisition, reconstruction and processing.

Future directions

Contemporary electrogram analysis is rapidly evolving from relatively simple voltage-based interpretation toward increasingly integrated, high-resolution and physiology-driven characterization of cardiac activation. One major direction is the continued development of ultra-high-density mapping technologies with smaller electrodes and increasingly dense multi-electrode arrays. These systems improve spatial sampling of myocardial activation and may facilitate identification of highly localized conduction abnormalities and microstructural arrhythmogenic substrate. Simultaneously, novel flexible and conformable electrode arrays may improve tissue coupling and reduce variability introduced by catheter orientation and contact instability.

The increasing complexity of contemporary mapping systems also highlights the need for standardization and validation of electrogram acquisition, processing and interpretation. Increasing diversity in catheter designs, electrogram configurations, filtering strategies and mapping algorithms complicates direct comparison between studies and mapping systems. Future efforts should therefore focus on establishing standardized methodologies, validation frameworks and clinically meaningful reference values to facilitate reproducibility and translation of emerging technologies into routine clinical practice.

In parallel, electrogram reconstruction techniques are increasingly moving beyond conventional bipolar recordings toward more orientation-independent approaches. These methods aim to reduce directional sensitivity and improve reproducibility of voltage mapping and activation analysis. Future mapping systems will likely increasingly integrate multidirectional electrogram analysis with local conduction vector estimation and dynamic activation reconstruction.

Artificial intelligence and machine learning are also expected to play an increasingly important role in electrogram interpretation. Emerging approaches already explore automated electrogram classification, annotation-independent activation reconstruction and identification of complex arrhythmogenic patterns using deep learning techniques. Future artificial intelligence-based systems may facilitate real-time substrate characterization, automated detection of conduction abnormalities and individualized prediction of arrhythmia mechanisms or ablation outcome. However, predictive performance alone is unlikely to be sufficient for widespread clinical adoption. Increasing emphasis is therefore being placed on explainable and physiologically interpretable artificial intelligence capable of providing mechanistic insights into the electrophysiological features underlying model predictions.

Increasing integration of multimodal data may further improve electrogram interpretation. Future electro-anatomical mapping systems will likely combine electrogram analysis with structural imaging, fiber orientation, computational modeling and potentially noninvasive mapping technologies. Such integrated approaches may improve understanding of the relationship between structural remodeling, conduction abnormalities and arrhythmogenesis. Advances in patient-specific computational modeling and digital-twin technologies may further facilitate mechanistic interpretation of complex activation patterns and prediction of therapeutic response.

In addition, increasing interest exists in personalized and patient-specific electrogram interpretation. Rather than relying on fixed voltage thresholds or universal substrate definitions, future mapping strategies may increasingly incorporate individualized characterization of electrogram morphology, conduction heterogeneity and activation behavior. Such approaches may better account for interindividual differences in anatomy, substrate distribution and disease progression.

Despite rapid technological progress, interpretation of electrograms will remain fundamentally dependent on understanding the underlying electrophysiological principles and limitations of signal acquisition. Future innovations should therefore not only improve technological sophistication, but also preserve physiological interpretability and clinical relevance. Ultimately, the integration of high-resolution mapping, advanced signal processing, multimodal data integration and explainable artificial intelligence may enable more mechanistically grounded and individualized approaches to cardiac arrhythmia mapping and ablation.

Conclusion

Electrogram interpretation remains at the core of contemporary cardiac electrophysiology. Although technological innovations continue to rapidly expand mapping capabilities and improve spatial resolution, electrograms should not be regarded as isolated signals or purely automated outputs. Meaningful interpretation requires continuous integration of electrophysiological principles, myocardial structure and technological context. A thorough understanding of both the physiological basis and technical limitations of electrogram acquisition therefore remains essential for accurate substrate characterization, mechanistic insight and responsible advancement of cardiac arrhythmia mapping and ablation.
