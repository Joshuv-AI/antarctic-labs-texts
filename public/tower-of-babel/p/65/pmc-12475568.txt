Clinical and angiographic characteristics of below-the-knee arterial disease: A case series study from Vietnam
Authors: Nguyen Minh Tan, Lam Thao Cuong, Le Phi Long, Tran Minh Bao Luan, Tran Thanh Vy, Nguyen Hoai Nam
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBelow-the-knee peripheral arterial disease (BTKPAD) is a major cause of chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), yet data on its clinical and angiographic presentation in resource-limited settings remain scarce. This study aimed to characterize the clinical staging and angiographic features of patients with BTKPAD at the time of hospital admission.MethodsA retrospective consecutive case series study was conducted at the University Medical Center Ho Chi Minh City from January 2022 to September 2024. A total of 127 patients diagnosed with BTKPAD were included. A standardized diagnostic work-up was performed for all patients. Clinical staging was assessed using the Rutherford and Fontaine classifications. Diagnostic angiography (DSA) was performed to evaluate lesion morphology, number, length, and runoff scores. Ischemia severity and lesion complexity were assessed using the ankle-brachial index (ABI) and TASC II classification, respectively.ResultsMost patients presented with advanced CLTI: 96.1 % were classified as Rutherford category 5–6 and Fontaine stage IV. The median ABI was 0.55, with over 70 % having ABI < 0.90. Tissue loss or gangrene was the primary cause of admission in 93.7 % of cases. DSA revealed multisegmental disease, with 77.2 % of patients having two to three lesion sites and a median lesion length of 14 cm. TASC classification showed 68.5 % had type C and 25.2 % had type D lesions. The median runoff score was 8.0, indicating severe distal hypoperfusion.ConclusionsPatients with BTKPAD in this cohort predominantly presented with late-stage disease and complex vascular lesions. These findings underscore the need for earlier diagnosis and timely revascularization strategies to improve limb salvage in similar healthcare settings.

Introduction

Below-the-knee peripheral arterial disease (BTKPAD) represents a severe and clinically significant manifestation of atherosclerosis, often culminating in chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI). This advanced stage is characterized by persistent ischemic symptoms such as rest pain, non-healing ulcers, and gangrene, and is associated with high morbidity, limb loss, and mortality [1]. BTKPAD primarily involves the tibial and peroneal arteries, where progressive stenosis and occlusion impair distal perfusion. Diabetic patients are particularly vulnerable, frequently exhibiting diffuse multivessel involvement and long-segment occlusions exceeding 10 cm, which complicate both diagnosis and intervention [2]. Angiographic studies further confirm that BTK disease often entails severe and complex lesions, highlighting the need for detailed vascular assessment prior to any revascularization strategy [3].

Globally, the burden of peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is substantial and continues to rise. As of 2019, an estimated 113 million individuals aged 40 years and older were affected worldwide, corresponding to a global prevalence of 1.52 % (95 % CI: 99.2–128.4 million cases). Notably, 42.6 % of these cases occurred in countries with low or moderate sociodemographic index (SDI) levels, reflecting significant health disparities and limited access to early diagnosis and vascular care [4]. In resource-constrained settings, patients often present at advanced stages due to delayed symptom recognition and lack of specialized vascular services. Risk factors such as diabetes mellitus, smoking, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and aging further accelerate PAD progression and worsen clinical outcomes [5,6].

In Vietnam, despite the growing burden of diabetes and age-related vascular disease, evidence on the clinical and angiographic characteristics of BTKPAD remains scarce. Early recognition of cardinal symptoms, such as intermittent claudication, rest pain, chronic non-healing ulcers, and distal gangrene, is essential to prevent irreversible limb damage. Therefore, this case series study was conducted to comprehensively describe the clinical and angiographic features of patients with BTKPAD admitted to a tertiary university hospital in Vietnam. The findings aim to inform local clinical practice by identifying patterns of late-stage presentation and emphasizing the need for systematic screening and early revascularization in high-risk populations.

Methods

Study design and participants

A retrospective consecutive case series study was conducted at the University Medical Center Ho Chi Minh City from January 2022 to September 2024. A total of 127 patients diagnosed with stenosis or occlusion of below-the-knee arteries were consecutively included. All patients were hospitalized and underwent endovascular treatment during the study period. Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met both of the following criteria: (1) clinical symptoms of lower limb ischemia persisting for more than two weeks, and (2) a clear indication for endovascular intervention, which was subsequently performed at the study site.

Data collection

A standardized diagnostic work-up was performed for all patients. The collected information included demographic, socio-clinical, clinical, and subclinical characteristics. Demographic and socio-clinical data encompassed age, sex, body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, and smoking history. Clinical parameters included reasons for hospital admission, presenting symptoms, physical examination findings, ankle-brachial index (ABI), Fontaine stage, and Rutherford classification.

Subclinical data were obtained from digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which was uniformly performed for all cases, including assessment of lesion morphology, degree of stenosis or occlusion, anatomical location, lesion length, number of lesions, presence of concomitant suprapopliteal artery disease, and status of collateral circulation.

Measurement and classification of clinical parameters

The ankle-brachial index (ABI) was measured using a handheld Doppler ultrasound device [7]. Systolic blood pressure was recorded at the brachial artery and at both the dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial arteries in the lower extremities. ABI was calculated as the ratio of the highest ankle systolic pressure to the highest brachial systolic pressure. Values were subsequently categorized into clinically relevant subgroups to assess the severity of peripheral arterial disease (PAD). Non-compressible arteries were defined as ABI >1.40, according to the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline [8].

Limb ischemia was clinically staged using both the Fontaine and Rutherford classifications [9]. The Fontaine classification stratifies patients into four stages (I–IV) based on the presence and severity of symptoms such as intermittent claudication, rest pain, ulceration, and gangrene. The Rutherford classification offers a more granular system, ranging from Category 0 (asymptomatic) to Category 6 (severe ischemic ulcers or gangrene), enabling comprehensive assessment of ischemic severity and associated functional impairment.

Subclinical data were obtained from digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which was uniformly performed in all cases. Lesion morphology, degree of stenosis or occlusion, anatomical location, lesion length, number of lesions, presence of concomitant suprapopliteal artery disease, and collateral circulation were assessed. Lesion characteristics were classified according to the Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus II (TASC II) guidelines. This system categorizes infrainguinal arterial lesions, including those of the femoropopliteal and below-the-knee arteries, into four types (A–D), based on lesion morphology, length, and anatomical location. TASC II classification was used to evaluate lesion complexity and to inform the selection of endovascular versus surgical treatment strategies [10]. Lesion length was measured using the straight-line method along the arterial axis. Two experienced vascular interventionalists independently reviewed all angiograms, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus, although formal interobserver agreement was not calculated. Runoff scores were determined according to the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) runoff scoring system, without local modification [8].

Statistical method

Statistical analysis was conducted using R software, version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed data, or as median and interquartile range for non-normally distributed data.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee for Biomedical Research of the University of Medicine and Pharmacy at Ho Chi Minh City (Aproval number 2258-DHYD). All procedures were carried out at the University Medical Center Ho Chi Minh City in accordance with ethical standards and the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants after they were fully informed of the study's purpose, procedures, potential benefits, and risks. Participation in the study was entirely voluntary and did not affect the quality of medical care provided. All collected data were anonymized and securely stored to ensure confidentiality.

This case series has been reported in line with the PROCESS Guideline [11].

The study population was predominantly elderly, with 73.2 % aged ≥65 years, and showed a slight male predominance (53.5 %). The mean BMI was 22.2 ± 3.7 kg/m2. Most patients fell within the normal BMI range; however, 32.3 % were classified as overweight or obese, indicating a heterogeneous nutritional status.

A high prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities was noted. Diabetes mellitus and hypertension were the most common, affecting 82.7 % and 85.0 % of patients, respectively. Among diabetic patients, poor glycemic control was frequently observed, with 76.6 % having HbA1c levels ≥7.0 %. Additionally, 31.5 % of participants reported a history of smoking. Other notable comorbidities included dyslipidemia (59.8 %), chronic kidney disease (37.0 %), and coronary artery disease (27.6 %).

Clinical characteristics

Most patients were admitted due to severe complications such as ulcers and tissue necrosis (93.7 %), with rest pain reported in over 90 % of cases. Foot ulcers were prevalent (95.3 %), mainly affecting anterior and posterior tibial artery territories. Necrosis was observed in 79.5 % of patients, and median walking distance was severely limited to only 20 m, indicating significant functional impairment.

The ABI values indicated severe lower limb ischemia in the majority of patients, with over 70 % having ABI < 0.90 and more than 20 % having ABI < 0.50 in each leg. Clinically, most patients were classified as Fontaine stage IV (96.1 %) and Rutherford category 5 or 6 (96.1 %), reflecting advanced critical limb ischemia with tissue loss or necrosis.

Angiographic characteristics

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) revealed that the majority of lesions were severe (93.7 %), with a predominant pattern of combined stenosis and occlusion (76.4 %). Multisegmental involvement was frequently observed, particularly affecting both the anterior and posterior tibial arteries (43.3 %), and additionally involving the peroneal artery in 42.5 % of cases. Most patients (77.2 %) had two to three lesion sites, with a median lesion length of 14 cm, reflecting a substantial disease burden. Notably, 87.4 % of patients retained collateral circulation from above-the-knee arteries, indicating preserved limb viability despite critical ischemia.

The median runoff score was 8.0 (interquartile range: 7.5–9.5), with values ranging from 3.5 to 10, suggesting marked hypoperfusion of the lower extremities. Based on the Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC II) classification, the majority of lesions were complex, with 68.5 % categorized as TASC C and 25.2 % as TASC D. Only 6.3 % of patients had simpler lesions classified as TASC A or B.

Discussion

Characteristics of patients with below-the-knee arterial stenosis and occlusion

The mean age of patients in this study was 71.7 ± 9.8 years, with 73.2 % aged 65 years or older, reaffirming that below-the-knee (BTK) arterial disease predominantly affects the elderly, particularly in countries with low-to-middle sociodemographic indices such as Vietnam. This observation is consistent with global estimates by Yon et al., who reported the highest prevalence of peripheral artery disease (PAD) (14.91 %) in individuals aged 80–84 years [4].

A slight male predominance was observed (53.5 %), aligning with previous findings by Suzuki (67.8 %) and Sayfo (72 %) [6,12]. The higher prevalence among males may reflect greater exposure to modifiable risk factors such as smoking and delayed healthcare-seeking behavior. In contrast, women often present at more advanced stages due to atypical symptoms and later disease onset.

In terms of nutritional status, the mean BMI was 22.2 ± 3.7 kg/m2, with 55.9 % of patients within the normal range and 32.3 % classified as overweight or obese. Although underweight patients were rare in our cohort, previous studies—including those by Paula and Lim—have shown that low BMI is associated with increased risks of mortality, limb loss, and major adverse limb events (MALEs) in PAD patients [13,14]. These findings suggest that formal nutritional assessments should be integrated into both pre- and post-intervention care pathways to optimize clinical outcomes.

Our cohort exhibited a high burden of cardiometabolic comorbidities, with diabetes mellitus (82.7 %), hypertension (85.0 %), dyslipidemia (59.8 %), and chronic kidney disease (37.0 %) being most prevalent. Notably, 76.6 % of diabetic patients had suboptimal glycemic control (HbA1c ≥ 7.0 %). These results are in line with reports by Sayfo in the United States (diabetes: 76.5 %) and Vivekanand in India (diabetes: 100 %) [12,15]. Additionally, 31.5 % of participants had a history of smoking, similar to Rathod's report of 64 % in Indian patients, further highlighting smoking as a major modifiable risk factor for PAD. Coronary artery disease was present in 27.6 % of patients, comparable to findings by Mehta, who emphasized the adverse outcomes associated with polyvascular disease [16]. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment and aggressive management of comorbidities in patients with BTKPAD. Early recognition and intervention may improve limb salvage and overall prognosis.

Clinical characteristics and disease stage at admission

A notably high proportion of patients in our study (93.7 %) were admitted with advanced ischemic complications, primarily presenting with lower limb ulcers and gangrene. In contrast, only 4.7 % were hospitalized solely for rest pain, and 1.6 % for a combination of pain and tissue loss. These findings stand in stark contrast to those of Jihun Ahn et al. in South Korea, where only 39.2–41.6 % of patients with BTK lesions presented with tissue loss, likely reflecting earlier detection and better healthcare accessibility [17].

Rest pain, muscle atrophy, and trophic changes

Rest pain was reported by 90.6 % of patients, predominantly located in the foot (91.3 %) and less frequently in the calf (10.2 %). Clinical signs of prolonged ischemia were widespread, with 65.4 % of patients exhibiting muscle atrophy and 78.7 % demonstrating trophic skin changes. These findings reflect the chronic and severe nature of ischemia in advanced BTK disease.

Compared to the cohort studied by Ahn et al., where only 13.7–14.5 % reported claudication and 4.7–5.9 % had rest pain [17], our data suggest significantly more advanced disease presentation in the Vietnamese context. This disparity may point to delays in detection, referral, and intervention. Both rest pain and muscle wasting are well-recognized markers of advanced ischemia and are strongly associated with increased risk of limb loss if not treated promptly.

Ankle-brachial index (ABI)

The distribution of ABI values in our cohort indicated a substantial burden of severe ischemia. Over 70 % of patients had ABI < 0.90 in both legs, with 20.5 % (right) and 26.8 % (left) falling below 0.50, consistent with critical limb ischemia. Conversely, non-compressible arteries (ABI ≥ 1.40) were identified in 14.2 % (right) and 18.1 % (left) of patients. These findings are comparable to a U.S. cohort study by Moussa Pacha et al. (2018), which reported 20.0 % of patients with ABI < 0.50 and 24.8 % with ABI > 1.4 [18]. Importantly, ABI values <0.50 have been linked to higher revascularization rates, while ABI values >1.4 are associated with increased risk of limb loss due to medial arterial calcification and falsely elevated readings. These results underscore the prognostic value of ABI in stratifying ischemic severity and guiding revascularization decisions.

Rutherford classification

In line with ABI findings, clinical staging revealed that 96.1 % of patients were classified as Fontaine stage IV and Rutherford category 5 or 6. Specifically, 75.6 % of patients exhibited minor tissue loss (category 5), while 20.5 % had major tissue loss (category 6). Only 3.9 % remained in earlier stages (category 3 or 4). This distribution is consistent with findings by Pitoulias, who reported that 92 % of patients with below-the-knee lesions were classified as Rutherford 5 or 6, and with Suematsu, whose study among dialysis patients showed comparable proportions (60.8 % in category 5 and 20.3 % in category 6) [19,20]. These findings highlight the predominance of advanced tissue loss in patients with below-the-knee PAD and reflect delayed presentation with poor limb prognosis.

Lesion morphology and severity

Angiographic analysis revealed that the predominant lesion morphology was a combination of stenosis and occlusion (70.1 %), with 89.0 % of patients exhibiting high-grade (severe) lesions. This pattern differs from a study by Francesco Liistro in Italy, which reported a higher prevalence of total occlusions, approximately 70 %, among patients with critical limb ischemia [21]. Similarly, a study by Rathod in India found that 100 % of BTK lesions exhibited either ≥50 % stenosis or complete occlusion [22]. The comparatively lower rate of total occlusion (4.7 %) in our study may be attributed to variations in disease detection thresholds, referral patterns, or differences in healthcare access and imaging protocols. Nonetheless, the predominance of complex, multisegmental lesions emphasizes the severity of BTK disease and the need for tailored revascularization strategies.

Lesion length, complexity, and anatomic distribution

The median lesion length in our cohort was 14 cm (interquartile range: 10–16 cm), with some lesions extending up to 35 cm, reflecting anatomically complex disease. Compared to Patel et al. in Singapore, who reported mean lesion lengths ranging from 81.8 to 90.3 mm [23], and Liistro et al. in Italy with a mean length of 180 mm [21], our findings suggest a relatively greater segmental involvement. These longer lesions pose increased procedural challenges and may impact the technical success and durability of endovascular interventions.

Multivessel involvement was common, with 43.3 % of patients exhibiting disease in both the anterior and posterior tibial arteries, and 42.5 % showing involvement of all three infrapopliteal vessels. This rate of triple-vessel disease was not observed in prior studies by Ahn in Korea or Liistro in Italy, where anterior tibial artery involvement was predominant (46.8 % and 65.6 %, respectively) [17,21]. The more extensive anatomic distribution observed in our population likely reflects higher disease complexity and may be associated with poorer limb outcomes if not adequately revascularized.

Number of lesion sites and TASC classification

A substantial proportion of patients (73.1 %) presented with three or more lesion sites, significantly exceeding the figures reported by Patel et al., where only 8.9 % of patients in the drug-coated balloon group and 3.1 % in the plain balloon group had ≥3 lesion sites [23]. This further illustrates the advanced disease burden in our study population and underscores the need for comprehensive revascularization strategies.

According to the Trans-Atlantic Inter-Society Consensus (TASC II) classification, 93.7 % of patients were categorized as having complex lesions (TASC C or D). This distribution is consistent with Patel et al., who reported 54–58 % of patients with TASC D and 24–26 % with TASC C lesions [23]. In contrast, a retrospective cohort study by John et al. reported a higher prevalence of simpler lesions, with 31.9 % of cases falling under TASC A [24]. Their findings also demonstrated a significantly lower risk of reintervention in TASC A lesions, reinforcing the prognostic significance of TASC classification in clinical decision-making and outcome prediction.

Reference to contemporary guidelines

Our findings are also consistent with the 2024 ACC/AHA guideline for the management of lower extremity peripheral artery disease [8], which emphasizes endovascular revascularization as the preferred strategy for patients with chronic limb-threatening ischemia, particularly those with below-the-knee multivessel involvement. This alignment highlights the importance of timely revascularization in advanced disease, even in resource-limited settings such as Vietnam.

Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the clinical and angiographic characteristics of below-the-knee peripheral arterial disease in a resource-limited setting, contributing novel data from Vietnam where such information is scarce. However, the retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. Potential selection bias may exist due to inclusion of only hospitalized patients undergoing intervention. Additionally, long-term outcomes and limb salvage rates were not assessed, which limits conclusions regarding prognosis and treatment effectiveness. Another limitation is the lack of WIfI (Wound, Ischemia, and foot Infection) classification data [25], which could have offered additional insights into the severity and prognosis of limb ischemia. Moreover, complementary perfusion assessments such as toe-brachial index (TBI) or transcutaneous oxygen pressure (TcPO₂) were not available in our center and could not be performed in patients with non-compressible arteries (ABI >1.40) [8]. Finally, although short-term procedural and clinical outcomes (e.g., technical success, peri-procedural complications, early adverse events) were recorded, these data were not comprehensively analyzed in the present study and will be reported separately in a subsequent publication.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the majority of patients with below-the-knee arterial stenosis or occlusion presented at an advanced stage, characterized by high rates of tissue loss, rest pain, and extensive multivessel disease. Most patients were classified as Fontaine stage IV, Rutherford categories 5–6, and TASC C—D, indicating severe ischemia and complex vascular lesions. These findings underscore the urgent need for earlier diagnosis, systematic screening of high-risk populations, and timely revascularization strategies to reduce the risk of limb loss and improve long-term clinical outcomes.
