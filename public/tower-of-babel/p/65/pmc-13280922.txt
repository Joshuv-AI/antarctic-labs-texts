ATR-FTIR Assessment of the Antioxidant and Anti-Inflammatory Effects of (−)-Epicatechin in MASLD
Authors: Marija Hefer, Nikolina Filipović, Stjepan Šarić, Nela Malatesti, Ana Petrović, Elvira Kovač-Andrić, Martina Smolić
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Lipid peroxidation is a contributor and a result of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), related to oxidative injury and inflammatory/fibrogenic signaling, which eventually results in disease progression. Early (conjugated dienes, CDs) and propagation (lipid hydroperoxides, LOOHs) products, along with attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy assessment, could possibly be used to quantify the extent of this process, while TNF-α and TGF-β1 reflect downstream inflammatory and profibrotic responses. (−)-Epicatechin (EC), a dietary polyphenol, shows antioxidant activity and could decrease the extent of lipid peroxidation and inflammation/fibrogenesis, thereby limiting MASLD progression. The aim of this study was to determine whether EC attenuates lipid peroxidation and pro-inflammatory/profibrotic signaling in an in vitro model of steatosis and whether ATR-FTIR could be used for such assessment in the context of steatosis and lipid peroxidation. Steatosis was induced in HepG2 cells with sodium oleate. EC (10–50 μM) was applied either as pretreatment before oleate exposure or as posttreatment during already established steatosis. Lipid peroxidation was quantified according to CDs, LOOHs, and the LOOH/CD ratio. ATR-FTIR band ratios and intensities related to lipid peroxidation (CO and C–H bands) were evaluated alongside biochemical results. TNF-α and TGF-β1 concentrations were measured as they are potential indicators of inflammation and fibrogenesis in MASLD, respectively. Sodium oleate increased CDs, LOOHs, and the LOOH/CD ratio, consistent with enhanced lipid peroxidation, and elevated TNF-α and TGF-β1. EC pretreatment reduced CDs and LOOHs and lowered the LOOH/CD ratio and the TNF-α and TGF-β1 levels relative to sodium oleate control groups in a concentration-dependent manner. Although the posttreatment effects were less pronounced, EC posttreatment still decreased all these parameters in a concentration-dependent manner in steatotic HepG2 cells. ATR-FTIR spectra were consistent with the biochemical results where the ATR-FTIR carbonyl (CO) band intensity showed a positive correlation with the LOOH/CD ratio, TNF-α, and TGF-β1 in the EC Pretreatment and Posttreatment models. Therefore, these results suggest that ATR-FTIR readings reflect quantitative changes in lipid peroxidation and associated inflammatory and fibrotic processes in steatosis.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents one of the most prevalent chronic liver disorders. Due to its predisposition to progress toward metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), MASLD has become a significant global health burden and a major challenge for healthcare systems. −

The majority of patients with MASLD or MASH are obese and frequently present with additional metabolic comorbidities, most commonly insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia. − Furthermore, most conditions associated with MASLD and MASH are characterized by lipid metabolism impairment, with elevated levels of oxidative stress. Impaired lipid metabolism, together with prolonged oxidative stress, promotes hepatocellular lipotoxicity and mitochondrial dysfunction. These alterations affect the antioxidant defense system and increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, thereby contributing to the onset of MASLD and its progression to MASH, along with more advanced liver diseases. ,

One of the main results of oxidative stress is lipid peroxidation, in which ROS attack (poly)­unsaturated fatty acids within membrane lipids. This initiates a series of structural and functional alterations, where the formation of conjugated dienes (CDs) reflects early double-bond rearrangements in fatty acyl chains, while lipid hydroperoxides (LOOHs) represent key propagation-phase products that destabilize cell membranes and increase oxidative injury. , Therefore, determining LOOH and CD levels, along with their overall LOOH/CD ratio, might be useful for determining the lipid peroxidation dynamics and the progression of MASLD.

Along with oxidative damage to the cell structures, inflammation is also a key regulator of MASLD progression. In this context, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) is suspected to play a central role as its levels are found to increase alongside MASLD to MASH progression. − It has also been found that ROS and TNF-α are closely interconnected, where elevated TNF-α levels could serve as an indicator of oxidative cellular damage and enhanced lipid peroxidation. − Often, one of the results of MASLD progression is the development of liver fibrosis, which is usually mediated by transforming growth factor beta 1 (TGF-β1) as its levels are frequently elevated in MASH and contribute to the worsening of hepatic steatosis. Figure summarizes the pathways associated with MASLD to MASH progression, with emphasis on lipid peroxidation (CDs, LOOHs) and inflammatory/fibrogenic signaling (TNF-α, TGF-β1).

Schematic representation of lipid peroxidation and its role in MASLD progression. Lipid accumulation in MASLD leads to the release of free radicals and ROS, which attack (poly)­unsaturated fatty acids within membrane lipids, leading to lipid peroxidation, inflammation (via TNF-α), and fibrotic changes (via TGF-β1), thereby contributing to the transition from MASLD to MASH and further progression to cirrhosis and HCC. In lipid peroxidation, reactive radicals (R·) subtract the hydrogen from the (poly)­unsaturated lipid, thereby resulting in the formation of a lipid radical, which undergoes rearrangements to form a CD. The lipid radical then reacts with molecular oxygen to form an unstable lipid peroxyl radical, which further subtracts a hydrogen from another (poly)­unsaturated lipid, thereby resulting in the formation of a LOOH. The subtraction of hydrogen from the (poly)­unsaturated lipid also results in the formation of a lipid radical, thereby perpetuating the chain reaction. However, the exact role of different lipid peroxidation indicators and their contribution to MASLD progression toward MASH have not yet been fully elucidated, with the number of studies addressing this issue remaining limited. Given these limitations in research, attention has increasingly turned toward dietary antioxidants, particularly polyphenols or mixtures of polyphenol-rich plant extracts, as potential modulators of oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in hepatic steatosis. −

Among available polyphenols, we selected (−)-epicatechin (EC) because of its strong antioxidant and radical scavenging capacity, with its documented effects on inflammatory signaling. − One study even found that the antifibrotic properties of EC were comparable to those of silymarin, with EC showing even stronger reducing effects on malondialdehyde (MDA) formation in vivo. Since MDA is a secondary product of LOOH breakdown, these results could link EC’s potential antifibrotic properties to its ability to attenuate lipid peroxidation, thereby potentially mitigating the progression of MASLD to MASH. Unlike more extensively studied compounds such as epigallocatechin gallate, , EC has been less investigated in the context of MASLD, providing an opportunity to explore a novel candidate. At the same time, the growing use of advanced spectroscopic techniques offers new opportunities to monitor biochemical alterations in cells and tissues.

Attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectroscopy recently emerged as a promising tool for monitoring such changes. The application of ATR-FTIR spectroscopy and similar techniques for steatosis and lipid quantification, along with determining the lipid peroxidation extent, has been introduced, especially in the context of hepatic steatosis. Determining the intensity of CO vibrational stretching at ∼1740 cm–1 (triacylglycerols, cholesteryl esters, lipid oxides), as this type of bond usually occurs in lipid peroxidation, could also be valuable for determining the overall extent of steatosis. − Along with lipid peroxidation extent, determining the degree of unsaturation in MASLD models is crucial for understanding the changes in cellular components as the degree of unsaturation is often found to decrease due to steatotic changes in cells and tissues. (Poly)­unsaturated fatty acids contribute to membrane fluidity and are essential for maintaining membrane integrity and signaling, while their depletion leads to increased membrane rigidity and impaired cellular function. , Given their high susceptibility to peroxidation, a reduction in their levels could indicate either an adaptive response to oxidative stress or a disruption of lipid homeostasis. Therefore, correlating the degree of unsaturation (∼3010 cm–1 for C–H) and the intensity of carbonyl stretching (∼1740 cm–1 for CO) with the LOOH/CD ratio and cytokines, TNF-α and TGF-β1, could be valuable for the noninvasive monitoring of disease progression.

Furthermore, applying EC treatment alongside ATR-FTIR analysis could allow us to evaluate whether changes in lipid peroxidation extent by this dietary polyphenol can be detected spectroscopically, thereby offering translational potential for the noninvasive assessment of hepatic oxidative stress. While FTIR has been applied in a few hepatic steatosis models, its correlation with cytokines and biochemical indicators of lipid peroxidation remains insufficiently explored. Most published studies that apply FTIR, or similar methods, in the context of hepatic steatosis or MASLD focus primarily on spectral characterization with staining or lipidomic analysis for validation, not on correlating spectral findings with LOOHs and CDs, or cytokines, which are especially important for MASLD to MASH progression. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of EC on lipid peroxidation and cytokine levels in a sodium-oleate-induced HepG2 steatosis model. Specifically, the main objectives of this study were to (1) quantify lipid peroxidation by measuring the levels of CDs and LOOHs, and the LOOH/CD ratio; (2) assess TNF-α and TGF-β1 as inflammatory and profibrotic mediators; and (3) compare these findings with FTIR spectral changes to determine whether this technique can serve as a complementary approach for detecting oxidative and inflammatory/fibrogenic modifications in steatosis. As part of the novelty of this work, we compared the LOOH/CD ratio and TNF-α and TGF-β1 levels with specific FTIR spectral changes corresponding to CO (1744–1735 cm–1) and C–H (3010 cm–1) vibrations.

Methods

Chemicals

Sodium oleate was used for the induction of a steatosis model in a HepG2 cell line, while EC ≥ 90% (HPLC) was used as a testing compound to evaluate its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory/fibrotic effects. Both products were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). The dissolution solvents used for the preparation of solutions are described in ref .

Cell Culture

HepG2 cells (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) were cultivated in a low-glucose medium with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C under 5% CO2. After the cells reached the desired confluence, they were resuspended in a low-glucose medium without FBS and seeded in multiwell plates for further experiments.

Experimental Design

Two experimental models were introduced for the experiments: EC pretreatment and EC Posttreatment models. In both models, the untreated cells (UT) group was used as a negative (vehicle) control group, while the sodium oleate-treated group (OA) was used as a positive control group, with EC (control) being the highest concentration of EC used as a control for the tested compound. In the Pretreatment model, the cells were pretreated with 10, 30, and 50 μM EC for 4 h, followed by a 24 h posttreatment with 1.5 mM sodium oleate. In the Posttreatment model, the cells were treated with 1.5 mM sodium oleate for 12 h, followed by the addition of the same concentrations of EC in the presence of 0.75 mM sodium oleate. All of the experimental groups were labeled as EC50+OA, EC30+OA, and EC10+OA, according to the concentration used in the experiments. EC concentrations of 10, 30, and 50 μM were chosen based on our previous MTS assay and Oil Red O staining results, where we tested 10–100 μM EC to evaluate its effect on MASLD in the Pretreatment model. As it was proven effective in the Pretreatment model, the Posttreatment model was added to evaluate whether EC could also mitigate oxidative and inflammatory/fibrotic changes once steatosis was already established. Another important consideration was to assess the potential cytotoxicity of the concentrations used, as EC and other polyphenols are often consumed as dietary supplements with poorly regulated dosages. As these cytotoxic effects are often subtle and overlooked in conventional biochemical assays, combining FTIR spectral analysis with complementary assays could provide a more comprehensive assessment of potential cytotoxicity.

DPPH Assay

The DPPH assay kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) was used to determine the antioxidant capacity of the direct radical scavenging of EC alone, as well as the overall antioxidant capacity of HepG2 cells after treatment with sodium oleate. 107 cells were collected after the experiment, and cell lysis was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To obtain the results, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm, and the results were presented accordingly. Undetectable values were presented as 0 μmol CV/L.

TNF-α and TGF-β1 Quantification

To determine the levels of TNF-α and TGF-β1, the cells were collected after the experiment and an ELISA assay (Elk Biotechnology, Denver, CO, USA) was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance was read at 450 nm, and the results were presented as pg/mL of TNF-α and TGF-β1 in the samples. Undetectable values were presented as 0 pg/mL.

Preparation of Lipid Extracts from HepG2 Cells

To obtain the lipid extracts from the cells after the experiment, a Folch method with certain modifications was used. A total of 107 HepG2 cells were collected from each tested group, and the lipid samples were weighed and stored at −20 °C for the experiments.

Determination of CDs and LOOHs

To determine the levels of lipid peroxidation products, CDs, and LOOHs, UV–vis spectroscopy was used. The intensity of absorbance at 234 nm for CDs and 480 nm for LOOHs was measured, and the lipid content was expressed as the absorbance value per mg of lipids, according to the weighed mass of lipids for each group.

ATR-FTIR Assessment of Lipid Peroxidation

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was used to determine the extent of lipid peroxidation and steatosis. The lipid samples were dissolved in chloroform and normalized to a lipid concentration of 20 mg/mL. After the evaporation of the solvent, the ATR-FTIR spectra were recorded using an Agilent Cary 630 FTIR spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), at the Faculty of Biotechnology and Drug Development at the University of Rijeka. All ATR-FTIR spectra were processed using the automatic least-squares baseline correction algorithm implemented in Agilent MicroLab Expert v.10.5 software (4000–650 cm–1; clean scans: 4; threshold: 0.002; background scans: 2; sample scans: 100; resolution: 16 cm–1; apodization: HappGenzel; phase correct: Mertz; method gain: 221). Spectra of pure chloroform were recorded under identical conditions and subtracted from sample spectra to remove any residual solvent contributions. Chloroform was recorded to be excluded in the background, and the data were collected in the spectral range 3200–1600 cm–1 on an ATR device. To ensure comparability between samples, the spectra were normalized against the asymmetric CH2 stretching vibration of lipid acyl chains (2925–2922 cm–1), which was used as an internal reference band due to its relative stability across treatments and minimal alteration during lipid peroxidation. Baseline correction was applied prior to normalization to minimize background variability. Relative intensities of lipid peroxidation-associated bands (CO stretching at 1744–1735 cm–1 and C–H stretching at 3010 cm–1) were then expressed as a ratio to the CH2 reference peak, allowing quantitative comparison of the lipid peroxidation extent between experimental groups. The data were presented as percent relative intensities (%I rel).

Statistical Analysis

Differences between the groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA, and post hoc comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) test to account for multiple comparisons. Statistical significance was set at α = 0.05, where *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001 were statistically significantly different. All experiments were conducted in biological triplicates, and the results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Pearson’s correlation coefficients (r) were calculated using group mean values (n = 6 per model) to evaluate the relationship between biochemical and FTIR parameters. Considering multiple testing across biologically related correlations within each experimental model, Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Because the number of data pairs was limited, both raw p-values and FDR-adjusted q-values are reported together with the corresponding correlation coefficients (r), and interpretation was primarily based on the strength and direction of the correlations.

Results and Discussion

Antioxidant Capacity of EC and HepG2 Cells

To determine the antioxidant capacity of EC, its radical scavenging capacity was first evaluated using the DPPH assay. As expected, sodium oleate, used as a negative control, showed no detectable antioxidant activity, while EC exhibited a strong concentration-dependent antioxidant capacity at all tested concentrations (10, 30, and 50 μM), with EC50 showing the highest radical scavenging activity (Figure a). Therefore, this confirmed the antioxidant properties of EC which was used for further evaluation in the hepatic steatosis model.

Antioxidant capacity of tested compounds (sodium oleate and EC) and HepG2 cells after treatment. (a) Antioxidant capacity of EC determined by the DPPH assay at concentrations of 10, 30, and 50 μM, with sodium oleate used as a negative control. (b) Antioxidant capacity of HepG2 cells following sodium oleate and EC treatment in both models. Data are presented as mean ± SD; *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001 vs the OA group (positive control).

Figure b shows the overall antioxidant capacity of HepG2 cells which was determined after induction of steatosis with sodium oleate and treatment with EC under pretreatment and posttreatment conditions. Sodium oleate treatment alone (OA group) slightly increased the antioxidant capacity of HepG2 cells compared to untreated controls (UT) in both models. Although an elevation in antioxidant capacity is not typically expected under steatotic conditions, it is worth noting that oleic/palmitic acid combinations can cause the upregulation of cellular endogenous antioxidants in response to stress, as reported in ref . These results are also consistent with our previous work where 1.5 mM sodium oleate caused the greatest increase in GSH concentration when compared to all groups. Therefore, this slight increase in the antioxidative capacity of sodium oleate-treated cells could suggest an increased cellular defense due to oxidative stress caused by fatty acid accumulation. Pretreatment with EC at 30 and 10 μM (EC30+OA and EC10+OA) significantly increased the antioxidant defense of HepG2 cells compared to the OA group; however, posttreatment with EC was not proven effective in this context. These results could suggest that EC exerts a stronger protective effect when administered prior to steatosis induction.

Lipid Peroxidation Extent: CD and LOOH Accumulation

The extent of lipid peroxidation was assessed by measuring CDs, LOOHs, and their ratio (LOOH/CD), with higher LOOH/CD values indicating enhanced propagation of peroxidation (Figure ).

(c) LOOH/CD ratio as an indicator of lipid peroxidation extent. Cells were treated with sodium oleate (OA group) to induce steatosis, along with EC pretreatment or posttreatment at 10, 30, or 50 μM. Data are expressed as absorbance per mg of lipid (mean ± SD) where *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 vs the OA group (positive control).

Relative CD Content (Figure a)

Sodium oleate treatment (OA group) increased CD levels compared to the UT group, which confirms enhanced initiation of lipid peroxidation in both OA groups. Pretreatment with EC at 50 μM (EC50+OA) significantly reduced CD formation relative to OA, while lower concentrations (EC30+OA and EC10+OA) also showed consistent, but slightly less pronounced, decreases. On the other hand, in the Posttreatment model, CD levels remained elevated, similar to the OA group. This could indicate a stronger preventive rather than restorative effect of EC on early lipid peroxidation products. Also, posttreatment with 50 μM EC in both EC (control) and EC50+OA showed a significant increase in the CD content when compared to the OA group. However, this did not reflect negatively on the overall LOOH/CD content (Figure c).

Relative LOOH Content (Figure b)

LOOH levels were significantly elevated in both OA groups compared to UT, confirming enhanced propagation-phase lipid peroxidation. Both pretreatment and posttreatment with EC significantly reduced LOOH accumulation, with the most pronounced effect observed at 30 and 10 μM in the Pretreatment model, and slightly less, but still significant reduction, at 50 μM in the Posttreatment model. These results also suggest that EC more effectively suppresses oxidative propagation damage when present prior to steatosis induction.

LOOH/CD Ratio (Figure c)

The LOOH/CD ratio, as a proposed indicator of the lipid peroxidation extent, was substantially increased in both OA groups, consistent with enhanced oxidative stress and advanced lipid peroxidation. Both models showed that EC treatment decreased the LOOH/CD ratio when compared to the OA group (except for the EC50+OA group in the Pretreatment model), which could suggest that EC could balance between initiation and propagation of lipid peroxidation in HepG2 cells. Even though there was no statistically significant difference, the EC50+OA pretreatment group showed an increased overall LOOH/CD ratio, which might indicate possible cytotoxicity of this concentration when applied in combination with sodium oleate. Therefore, this observation needed further addressing to confirm its context-based cytotoxicity, which was assessed by ATR-FTIR spectral changes.

TNF-α and TGF-β1 Levels in Hepatic Steatosis Progression

To evaluate the relationship between oxidative stress and inflammatory/fibrotic mediators, TNF-α and TGF-β1 levels were quantified following sodium oleate and EC treatments (Figure ).

Effects of sodium oleate and EC on inflammatory and fibrogenic mediators in HepG2 cells. (a) TNF-α levels measured by ELISA following OA and EC treatment in Pretreatment and Posttreatment models. (b) TGF-β1 levels measured by ELISA under the same experimental conditions. Data are presented as pg/mL (mean ± SD), where *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 vs the OA group (positive control).

TNF-α Levels (Figure a)

Sodium oleate treatment (OA group) led to a significant increase in TNF-α levels compared to UT, thereby confirming increased inflammation due to fatty acid accumulation. These results also show that 1.5 mM sodium oleate (24 h) induced a greater inflammation than a combination of 1.5 mM (12 h) + 0.75 mM (24 h) used in the Posttreatment model, which is consistent with the lower sodium oleate concentration used in the Posttreatment model. Furthermore, in both Pretreatment and Posttreatment models, EC significantly reduced TNF-α concentrations in a similar manner. These results suggest that EC can effectively reduce sodium oleate-induced inflammatory activation, independent of its period of application.

TGF-β1 Levels (Figure b)

TGF-β1 levels were also significantly elevated in OA groups relative to untreated controls, which could correspond to enhanced fibrogenic signaling. In both models, treatment with EC was proven effective in reducing TGF-β1 levels, whereas posttreatment produced a stronger overall inhibitory effect, depending on the EC concentration. Therefore, along with its antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, this also indicates that EC could attenuate profibrotic signaling pathways linked to MASLD progression. As this effect was more prominent in the Posttreatment model, this could suggest that EC exerts stronger antifibrogenic activity when applied therapeutically rather than as a preventive measure.

ATR-FTIR Spectroscopy for the Assessment of Lipid Peroxidation and Steatosis Extent

To evaluate the overall steatosis and lipid peroxidation extent in HepG2 cells, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was used. Figure shows the 3200–1600 cm–1 spectral region, including the key CO (1744–1735 cm–1) and C–H (3010 cm–1) stretching bands, which correlated with the biochemical parameters measured in this study. The increased intensity of the CO band in sodium oleate-treated groups corresponds to the accumulation of oxidized lipid species derived from peroxidation of (poly)­unsaturated fatty acids. On the other hand, a decrease in the C–H stretching band near 3010 cm–1 indicates a progressive depletion of double bonds which undergo rearrangement to form CDs or further transformations associated with lipid peroxidation.

In Figure , the changes in the spectra are visible after the treatment with sodium oleate, compared to control groups UT and EC (control). The broad vibrational band observed between 3300 and 3200 cm–1 predominantly originated from O–H stretching of residual water molecules and N–H stretching associated with possible trace amounts of proteins left in the lipid extracts. EC Pretreatment Model (Figure a)

As seen from Figure a, the OA group exhibits an increase in the %I rel for CO stretching, which indicates an increase in the lipid peroxidation extent when compared to all groups. EC50+OA, EC30+OA, and EC10+OA have lower mean %I rel for CO stretching, which shows that EC effectively decreased the lipid peroxidation extent caused by sodium oleate. However, it can be noticed that the EC50+OA group has a lower mean %I rel for unsaturated lipids, which can be attributed to the results obtained in Figure , where this group exhibited a lower CD content, along with an overall higher LOOH/CD ratio, when compared to the OA group. Even though 50 μM EC possibly shows a slight cytotoxicity when applied along with sodium oleate as a pretreatment, its pretreatment alone did not cause the same effect in the EC (control) group in healthy cells.

EC Posttreatment Model (Figure b)

In Figure b, the OA group again exhibited an increase in the mean %I rel for CO stretching, which indicates an increased lipid peroxidation extent. All of the EC groups exhibited lower mean %I rel for CO stretching, which shows that EC effectively decreased the lipid peroxidation extent in a similar manner as in the Pretreatment Model. However, there was no overall change in the %I rel for unsaturated lipids between the controls and the experimental groups, possibly due to a combination of a 12 h 1.5 mM and a 24 h 0.75 mM sodium oleate.

Correlation Analysis between Biochemical and FTIR Parameters

To examine the relationship between biochemical parameters and FTIR spectral changes, Pearson’s correlation analysis was performed using mean values obtained for each treatment group. This approach was used to assess whether the changes in FTIR bands corresponding to carbonyl and olefinic functional groups quantitatively reflected biochemical parameters. In addition, potential associations between oxidative (LOOH/CD) and inflammatory/profibrotic (TNF-α, TGF-β1) parameters were analyzed within both pretreatment and posttreatment models. Table summarizes these correlations, which were calculated based on six mean values per model (n = 6). Considering the limited number of groups, raw and adjusted p-values were presented for completeness, while emphasis was placed on the strength and direction of correlations (r). Correlation strength was interpreted according to conventional criteria, where r values below the magnitude of 0.39 were considered weak and not biologically relevant. After FDR correction, none of the correlations remained statistically significant at q < 0.05. However, several parameters retained moderate to strong correlation coefficients with consistent biological directionality.

In the EC Pretreatment model, the proposed lipid peroxidation extent indicator (LOOH/CD) showed a strong positive correlation with the FTIR carbonyl band relative intensity, %I rel (CO), indicating that increased lipid (per)­oxidation was accompanied by higher carbonyl intensity in the spectra. On the other hand, LOOH/CD exhibited a negative trend with the olefinic band relative intensity, %I rel (C–H), consistent with the expected loss of unsaturation during lipid peroxidation. LOOH/CD also showed high positive trends with both TNF-α and TGF-β1, which supports the fact that the lipid peroxidation extent and inflammatory/profibrotic signaling are connected.

In the EC Posttreatment Model, correlations retained the same directionality but were mostly weaker in magnitude, with the exception of TGF-β1 vs %I rel (C–H), which showed a weak negligible correlation. However, this is consistent with percent relative intensities (%I rel) of C–H stretching in this model, where no changes in the degree of unsaturation were seen. This is probably due to a combination of a lower sodium oleate concentration, which was not sufficient to cause a significant decrease in the degree of unsaturation. Therefore, these relationships indicate that, even though the two models are quite different, FTIR readouts did quantitatively track lipid peroxidation extent, where oxidative changes also aligned with cytokine responses. This aspect is particularly important for assessing disease progression, as cytokines play a central role in mediating the transition from MASLD to MASH. − , Considering that TNF-α and TGF-β1 both increase consistently in correlation with ↑ %I rel (CO) and ↓ %I rel (C–H), these results indicate that FTIR spectroscopy could be used as a complementary tool for monitoring cytokine-related changes in MASLD/MASH.

Also, these results further indicate that EC is able to modulate oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, and pro-inflammatory/profibrotic signaling in an in vitro model of hepatic steatosis. In agreement with previous reports that polyphenols exert radical scavenging and hepatoprotective effects, , our results indicate that EC reduces sodium oleate-induced oxidative injury, as shown by decreased LOOH accumulation and a lower LOOH/CD ratio. Specifically, the LOOH/CD ratio could be valuable for measuring the lipid peroxidation dynamics by reflecting the balance between the initiation and propagation phases of the peroxidation cascade. Therefore, an increased LOOH/CD ratio can indicate both accelerated propagation and reduced clearance of LOOHs. In this context, antioxidants are important as they can halt this process in the termination phase by donating hydrogen atoms to lipid peroxyl radicals, thereby preventing further hydrogen abstraction from other lipids. This potentially leads to a lower LOOH/CD ratio as LOOHs decrease, which was consistent with the strong positive correlation between the LOOH/CD ratio and %I rel(CO) (r = 0.8465).

However, 50 μM EC administration in the Pretreatment model possibly shows some cytotoxicity as indicated by the increased LOOH/CD relative content and decreased %I rel for unsaturated lipids. The significance of these results was also confirmed by the negative correlation between the LOOH/CD ratio and %I rel (C–H) (r = −0.6819). As this cytotoxicity was observed only when EC was applied as a pretreatment prior to sodium oleate administration (with no effect observed when applied alone or in the posttreatment model), it can be speculated that EC exhibits either anti- or pro-oxidant behavior depending on the context. These results could also be interpreted in relation to glutathione (GSH) or other endogenous antioxidants dynamics. Our previous study showed that the administration of the highest pretreatment dose (100 μM EC) alone resulted in decreased GSH levels when compared to the untreated group, which could suggest that 50 μM EC similarly lowered the GSH levels. This reduction could compromise the cellular antioxidant defense system, thereby limiting the capacity of HepG2 cells to neutralize sodium oleate-induced oxidative stress. On the other hand, another possible explanation is that EC initially lowered intracellular ROS levels, thereby leading to a compensatory downregulation of GSH biosynthesis. Also, the results from other studies suggest that antioxidants can act as both anti- and pro-oxidants, , so careful dosing is important, as well as considering the aspects of the disease itself or dietary habits. However, whether they can act as pro-oxidants directly or simply cause compensatory downregulation of endogenous antioxidants by lowering ROS remains an important question to assess in future work.

Furthermore, the positive effect seen with 30 and 10 μM EC concentrations was more pronounced when EC was administered as a pretreatment, suggesting that it could be more effective in preventing rather than reversing lipid peroxidation. This is consistent with previous studies showing that polyphenols are effective in modulating early oxidative changes. ,, However, once lipid peroxidation is established, cellular recovery tends to be more challenging as the process itself is self-propagating and causes further damage to the cells, which might not be reversible.

The observed reduction in TNF-α and TGF-β1 levels further confirms the potential role of EC in the inflammatory and fibrogenic pathways associated with hepatic steatosis progression. Therefore, the ability of EC to reduce the levels of both proteins suggests that its protective effects are not only due to its direct antioxidant capacity. Similar anti-inflammatory and antifibrotic effects of polyphenols have been reported in both in vitro and in vivo models, where levels of TNF-α, TGF-β1, and other cytokines were decreased as a result of treatment with polyphenols. ,

Also, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy confirmed biochemical results by detecting changes in lipid-associated CO (1744–1735 cm–1) and C–H (3010 cm–1) percent relative stretching intensities, thereby confirming that EC alleviates steatosis and lipid peroxidation in a concentration-dependent manner. Moreover, the OA group showed an increase in the band intensities associated with lipid peroxidation and steatosis. Similar results were observed in other lipid peroxidation and steatosis models. ,, Therefore, this shows that ATR-FTIR has a great potential to be used as a complementary, nondestructive tool for assessing lipid peroxidation and steatosis progression, as well as for evaluating the protective effects of polyphenols in hepatic steatosis models.

However, there are several limitations to the research design. Even though HepG2 cells are convenient for modeling hepatic steatosis, they do not fully reflect the complexity of liver physiology, especially regarding interactions with Kupffer cells and hepatic stellate cells. Therefore, it should be emphasized that the translational potential of this research could be examined in coculture models that incorporate both parenchymal and nonparenchymal liver cells, as shown in ref , thereby providing a model that more closely mimics the cellular interactions occurring during MASLD progression. Furthermore, although EC treatment showed significant results, the lower concentrations (10–30 μM) should be tested in vivo to confirm whether they could reflect physiologically achievable levels of EC. As the therapeutic translation of antioxidants in general may be limited, future studies could therefore explore EC encapsulation within nanoscale delivery platforms to improve its therapeutic efficacy, as highlighted in a recent report. Moreover, as a part of our future experiments, we plan to introduce coculture models including a recovery phase with oleate removal prior to EC administration, as well as lower 1–10 μM EC concentrations.

Conclusion

In a sodium oleate-induced hepatic steatosis model, EC showed a concentration-dependent effect on the overall radical scavenging ability of HepG2 cells. However, when EC was given as a posttreatment, these results were not achievable. EC also reduced propagation-phase lipid peroxidation by reducing relative LOOH levels in both models, lowering the LOOH/CD ratio (except for the EC50+OA pretreatment group), and effectively decreasing sodium oleate-induced increases in TNF-α and TGF-β1 concentrations. ATR-FTIR results were consistent with changes in lipid (per)­oxidation and reflected the overall antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects of EC observed through biochemical assays. Compared with biochemical assays, ATR-FTIR spectroscopy has several advantages, including rapid and nondestructive analysis that requires minimal sample preparation. Considering that it can simultaneously capture multiple molecular changes, this represents a reliable approach for accurate and complementary insights into lipid peroxidation and redox alterations in MASLD. However, it is important to correlate ATR-FTIR spectral changes with biochemical assays to further strengthen its interpretation.
