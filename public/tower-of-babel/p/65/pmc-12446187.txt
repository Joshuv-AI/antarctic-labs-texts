Potential vulnerability of US green hydrogen in a world of interdependent networks
Authors: Shokoufeh Pourshahabi, Shade T Shutters, Rachata Muneepeerakul, Suleyman O Altiparmak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryGreen hydrogen is viewed as a promising pathway to future decarbonized energy systems. However, hydrogen production depends on a few critical minerals, particularly platinum and iridium. Here, we examine how the supply of these minerals is subject to vulnerabilities hidden in interdependent global networks of trade and investment. We develop an index to quantify these vulnerabilities for a combination of a target country, an investing country, an intermediary country and a commodity. Focusing on the US as the target country for the import of platinum and iridium, we show how vulnerability-inducing investing countries changed between 2010 and 2019. We find that the UK is consistently among investing countries that can potentially induce US vulnerabilities via intermediary exporters of platinum and iridium, with South Africa being the primary intermediary country. Future research includes incorporating geopolitical factors and technological innovations to move the index closer from potential to real-world vulnerabilities.Subject areas: Biophysics, Cell biology, Molecular biology, Molecules, Pharmacology

Introduction

Hydrogen is projected to play a pivotal role in achieving long-term sustainability goals.1,2 It not only helps with decarbonization but also offers advantages in terms of long-term storage, long-distance transport, and wide domestic applications.3,4 According to projections, depending on the cost reductions in renewables and electrolyzers, green hydrogen is expected to dominate the global supply mix, with a share of between 50 and 65 percent of the global energy portfolio by 2050.5

However, the transition to a hydrogen-based economy is not without challenges. This growing market for hydrogen has complicated economic1,2,6 and geopolitical dynamics.7,8,9,10,11 On the one hand, the hydrogen economy aims to strengthen energy security together with a more environmentally friendly, sustainable energy supply.2 On the other hand, an international technology race and geo-economic competition may create new dependencies and confrontations with novel trade patterns in the emerging hydrogen market.10 Importantly, hydrogen production is a conversion activity, not an extraction business, so its geopolitical aspects depend heavily on the critical minerals needed to initiate its supply chain. Here, the dynamics we examine are not the commonly discussed issues like the cost of hydrogen production itself (economic) or how fossil fuel-rich countries strongly influence the hydrogen market (geopolitics). Instead, we analyze hidden vulnerabilities to which countries are indirectly exposed through international trade of the critical minerals and flows of foreign direct investment (FDI).

Hydrogen transformation, in which electrolyzers and fuel cells convert renewable electricity into hydrogen and then into electricity and heat again, require catalysis to control reactions during the conversion process. Platinum group metals are the most effective electrocatalysts for both the cathode (platinum) and anode (iridium and titanium).12,13,14 Platinum and iridium happen to be two of the scarcest and emission-intensive metals in the world.15 Though alternatives to platinum and iridium are currently being researched, readily available substitutes are still many years away.16 For example, in February 2024, Toshiba partnered with Bekaert to develop technology that reduces iridium use by 90%. Production of iridium, a by-product of platinum and palladium mining, is largely concentrated in South Africa (more than 80%) and Zimbabwe (∼10%).15 South Africa also dominates mining for platinum with a share of about 70%, followed by Russia with a market share of 10%.17 This creates a large import dependency of other countries for these metals. For example, both the US Geological Survey and the US Department of Energy classify platinum as a critical mineral to the security of the United States,18 yet the US was dependent on other countries for 83% of its consumed platinum in 2023.19 Such risks arising from limited global supply of critical minerals are what we refer to as direct vulnerabilities. However, the coupling of such trade with investment flows creates a complex, multi-layer network through which new and less obvious vulnerabilities can arise.

Indirect vulnerabilities through trade and investment

Economic globalization has made countries more dependent on each other through trade and financial flows, creating complex interdependent network where a change in one country can affect many others.20,21,22,23,24 While this interdependency has benefits,25,26 it also creates vulnerabilities.27,28 In the context of network science, vulnerability can emerge through both direct and indirect dependencies, within a single layer or between multiple interdependent layers.29,30

Direct vulnerability occurs when one node in the network is explicitly reliant on another. For example, the US depends directly on South Africa for the imports of platinum group metals.31 If South Africa experiences a disruption, the US is directly impacted. Indirect vulnerabilities, however, involve a chain of connections where the impact of a disruption propagates through intermediate nodes. For example, Germany may import batteries from South Korea, but these batteries rely on Chinese lithium.32 In this case, China could indirectly affect Germany if it alters its trade with South Korea. In these two examples, direct and indirect vulnerabilities occur in a single network of trade. Most existing studies focus on these types of vulnerabilities—those arising in an isolated, single-layer network such as a trade network,33,34,35,36,37 infrastructure network,38,39 communication network,40 or transportation network.41,42,43,44,45 In reality, modern systems operate across interdependent multi-layer networks.

Buldyrev et al. (2010) provided a theoretical foundation for understanding propagation of risk in interdependent multi-layer networks. They introduced a model in which a failure in a power grid can cause failures in an interdependent communication network, as illustrated by the case of Italy.46 Vespignani (2010) emphasized that while such models capture structural fragilities, real-world failures depend not only on network structure or connectivity, but also on dynamic interactions by the flow of resources.47 Although interdependent multi-layer networks have been explored extensively,27,28,29,46,47,48,49 existing studies have not yet addressed how to quantify the indirect vulnerabilities that arise from these interdependencies.

We develop and apply the potential indirect vulnerability index (PIVI) (Equation 1 in the method details) to assess hidden vulnerabilities in interdependent multi-layered networks of trade and foreign direct investment (FDI), where the nodes of our networks are countries. The term “potential” refers to latent vulnerabilities that may not have yet realized but could be triggered under supply chain shocks or geopolitical disruptions. When country X imports goods from Y, and Y is the recipient of substantial investments from Z, then Z has potential indirect influence over X’s import supply chains. In other words, X can be impacted by changes in the relationships between Y and Z. For a given country importing goods (the target node) we quantify the country’s potential exposure to risks arising from its interdependence with other countries. Furthermore, we focus our analysis on two critical minerals—platinum and iridium—due to their essential role in the production of green hydrogen, while considering all potential investing countries in the FDI layer. Taking the US as the target country we develop a comprehensive global view of the US’s indirect vulnerability associated with the two critical minerals, highlighting sources of potential supply chain insecurity.

The main objectives of our analysis are to: (1) quantify the hidden vulnerabilities to US supplies of platinum and iridium using PIVI; (2) identify investing countries in the FDI network that induce the largest vulnerabilities to US platinum and iridium supplies; and (3) examine the temporal patterns of the critical investing countries over the study period of 2010–2019 and their rank order.

Results

Using the PIVI (Equation 1), for each investing country (in the FDI network), we obtain the aggregated PIVIs across intermediary countries between the US and the investing country. We then repeat this process for all investing countries in the FDI network for each year (Objective 1). Results show that platinum and iridium have similar annual variation of the aggregated PIVIs, considering all investing countries (Figure 1). During the study period, the highest values of the aggregated PIVIs belong to the UK and the Netherlands.

PIVI is a nondimensional index (Equation 1), not affected by FDI or trade units of measure, nor by volatility in platinum or iridium prices. The highest values of the log transformed aggregated PIVIs are near 4.00 (Figure 1). A value near 4.00 on the log transformed scale corresponds to an extreme scenario—for example, if the US relies on a single intermediary country for platinum or iridium imports, and that country depends entirely on one investing country for FDI, the PIVI approaches N2, which equals 4.7 for N = 222 by applying log transformation. In this case, the PIVI values for other intermediary countries are negligible.

Vulnerability-inducing investing countries over time

We identify the investing countries with the greatest potential to induce indirect vulnerability to the US economy by comparing the aggregated PIVIs of all investing countries (Objective 2). Our findings demonstrate the high potential of the UK for inducing vulnerability through intermediaries for both platinum and iridium in most of the years (Table 1). However, in 2015, 2016, and 2017, the Netherlands surpassed the UK. While almost 60 percent of all US trade partners with net exports of platinum and iridium were European countries, South Africa has been the primary intermediary country between the US and these vulnerability-inducing investing countries. This indicates that (a) one of the main sources of platinum and iridium for the US is South Africa, and (b) South Africa is highly dependent on FDI from these vulnerability-inducing investing countries.

The last column of Table 1 presents the percentage of net import of platinum and iridium from these vulnerability-inducing investing countries to the US directly. These values indicate that, in addition to the indirect vulnerability, these investing countries also have potential for direct vulnerability against the US economy via the trade of platinum and iridium. For example, in 2010, the UK supplied 11.10% of platinum to the US, making this country a potential source of direct vulnerability for the US; in the same year, the UK transferred FDI to intermediary countries that exported platinum to the US, which made the UK a potential source of indirect vulnerabilities for the US through these intermediaries. Therefore, the UK represents both a direct and indirect source of vulnerability for the US, as it directly supplies a portion of platinum and also has potential to influence intermediary countries that export platinum to the US. The direct import of platinum from the UK has decreased over the period, while for iridium, the UK has provided higher percentage of export to the US without any reduction over time.

The Netherlands ranked first in 2015—mainly due to a sudden increase in its FDI flows to intermediary exporters of platinum and iridium, e.g., Belgium, Brazil, Canada, China, France, Germany, Italy, South Africa, Switzerland, and UK—and maintained this position through 2017. However, it experienced a sharp decline, falling to 22nd for platinum and 15th for iridium in 2019. These fluctuations were largely attributed to special purpose entities (SPEs), which are often used for tax planning and financing purposes. The World Investment Report (UNCTAD, 2016) noted that such entities in the Netherlands exhibited complex networks, with capital flowing rapidly in response to financing needs and tax considerations. In response to these dynamics, the Netherlands implemented new substance requirements for group financing and licensing companies. These measures aim to ensure that entities have actual economic activity in the country, aligning with international efforts to reduce tax avoidance. Additionally, the Netherlands introduced provisions for the automatic exchange of information about entities with minimal substance, enhancing transparency and cooperation with tax authorities.50

In contrast, Switzerland has experienced a drop in 2017 from rank 3rd to 20th for platinum and 22nd for iridium, largely due to a decline in its investment ties with intermediary exporters of these critical minerals such as France, Netherlands, South Africa, and UK. This is consistent with the World Investment Report (UNCTAD, 2018), which documents that Switzerland’s FDI outflows declined by $87 billion in 2017. This sharp drop was primarily driven by a reduction in equity investments (-$47 billion) and a decline in intra-company loans (-$42 billion).51 Note that the trade component of Equation 1 may also contribute to these changes.

Japan consistently ranks highly (generally in the top 5), but it has dropped to rank 11 in 2019 for iridium. The main reason for this decline is the loss of FDI ties with South Africa, a major source of iridium in that year. As a result, the trade component of Equation 1 decreased in 2019. Australia rose from rank 13 in 2016 to 3 in 2019 for iridium, mainly due to its FDI ties with South Africa in 2019, which it did not have in 2016. The ranking of Germany remains in the top 10 in 8 out of 10 years, with some volatility. Different factors such as trade dynamics, economic conditions, policy changes, financial market dynamics, and geopolitical shifts, may have contributed to this volatility.

Among the top 20 investing countries, 13 and 12 are located in Europe for platinum and iridium, respectively. This strong European representation (60% of total) reflects Europe’s significant role in inducing vulnerability to US platinum and iridium supplies.

Discussion

The role of green hydrogen in the US energy transition has been increasingly emphasized in recent studies. It highlights its potential to support energy supply, contribute to net-zero emission goals, and address the variability of renewable energy sources such as wind and solar.52 The US government has implemented several policies to advance green hydrogen, including the DOE hydrogen program plan—which supports innovative solutions—and hydrogen shot, aimed at reducing production costs.53 These efforts position green hydrogen as a critical component of the national clean energy strategy. While US efforts such as The Inflation Reduction Act and regional clean hydrogen hubs program have provided significant breakthroughs in the field of domestic (economic) policy, security of access to minerals that are relevant upstream in global supply chain-related issues cannot be ignored. Given this policy, the current study contributes to the underdeveloped literature on vulnerabilities within interdependent economic networks, particularly in the context of green hydrogen and clean energy supply chains.

Our analysis reveals a more distributed and complex landscape. Countries such as the UK emerge as critical nodes in the FDI network as sources of high indirect vulnerability. South Africa, which is a major exporter of both platinum and iridium, functions as the primary intermediary country between the US and the UK. This is consistent with another study, which shows that South Africa produces 85.05% of the world’s platinum, with a large portion of that production controlled by the UK-based company Anglo American.54 In 2019, South Africa supplied nearly 36% of the US net imports of platinum and iridium. Notably, the UK has provided almost 42% of South Africa’s FDI, compared to only 6% from the United States.

Investment (industry or refining, mobility, electricity storage, H2-based fuels, or trade) in hydrogen electrolysers is expected to increase by more than 140% in 2024.55 The findings suggest that future green hydrogen policy should focus not only on reducing production cost and advancing technological innovations, but also on addressing supply chain vulnerabilities arising from interdependent economic networks. Since technology already has a geoeconomic structure, that is, since development in any country has a characteristic that spreads to other countries and even receives contributions from them, which makes it accessible to all parties, the dependence on minerals that initiate the green hydrogen supply chain is of key importance. For instance, exploration spending for selected nonferrous mineral resources between 2019 and 2023 increased by 15%, including the platinum.56

Policy framework should expand to include strategic investment in key supplier countries like South Africa and diversification of the US partners for imports of critical minerals essential for green hydrogen production. Our study, which focuses on indirect vulnerability, is not limited to relations with mineral supplier countries alone; it also considers FDI, a key component of the interdependent economic market. This contrasts with studies that examine only the trade network and, therefore, that could address only direct vulnerability.57 This situation necessitates a reassessment of global political and economic policy decisions by the US, which has recently emphasized global isolationism and has weakened ties with global free trade organizations such as the WTO. Our study shows that traded commodities, including critical minerals, have a complex network of relationships that is not limited to just the biggest exporters and importers. Therefore, strong energy security policies require establishing and maintaining strong diplomatic and economic relations.

Limitations of the study and future work

Although the proposed PIVI represents interdependencies across multi-layered networks, it is important to acknowledge that the PIVI measure does not include qualitative factors such as political relationships between countries or geopolitical tensions. For instance, the fact that the most critical source of vulnerability in the FDI network (UK) is also a close ally of the U.S. will likely mitigate the risk that the UK will use its position to manipulate US supplies of platinum or iridium, but recent shifts in the US trade policies can affect this assessment. However, incorporating such aspects lies beyond the scope of this study. Future research could build on the PIVI framework by incorporating these qualitative factors to enhance the index in addressing real-world risks.

In this study, we use aggregate FDI data, which reflects total investment across all sectors of a country’s economy partly because publicly available sector-specific FDI data are lacking and because the total investments reflect the overall strength of the economic coupling between two countries, which in turn more effectively captures the potential influence that one country may exert on the other. As a result, we do not apply different weights to FDI in specific sectors, such as critical minerals relevant to green hydrogen production. When data availability permits, incorporating sector-specific FDI weights in future work could supplement a more nuanced assessment of vulnerability. Another interesting future research direction is to examine how perturbations in FDI inputs influence PIVI outcomes and resulting supply chain dynamics. Such an analysis would require scenario-based modeling beyond the scope of the current study, but we consider it a potential next step in the methodological development. Policy simulations—for example, modeling targeted increases in US FDI toward South Africa’s mineral sector—could provide important insights into risk mitigation strategies. Although such simulations are beyond the scope of this study, we view them as a promising direction for future research. Technological advances such as iridium-free electrolyzer designs58 could alter future vulnerability projections by reducing dependence on—and thus import of—critical minerals like iridium and platinum, thereby lowering the vulnerability outlook. Therefore, technology-adjusted projections into the PIVI framework is an important and interesting direction for future research.

Method details

In multi-layered networks, countries in one layer can be exposed to vulnerabilities from other nations in another interdependent layer, even if they are not directly involved with those countries. If a target country k imports commodity j from an intermediary country i and i has economic ties with an investing country h through financial flows, country k can be impacted by changes in the relationships between i and h (Figure S1).

In our previous published paper, we developed potential indirect vulnerability index (PIVI) to quantify potential indirect vulnerabilities in economic multi-layered networks.30 In this research, we modified PIVI by considering both incoming and outgoing FDI to quantify the economic ties between countries. Even though inflows and outflows are different in nature, they both contribute to creating mutual economic interests and financial ties. Our modified PIVI is defined as:

where PIVIi,j(h,k) signifies potential indirect vulnerability of a target country k induced by an investing country h for the net import of commodity j (platinum or iridium) from the intermediary nations like i; N denotes number of counterparty economies; Ini(h) and Outi(h) represent the investment inflow and outflow between investing country h and the intermediary country i; Imi,j(k) and Exi,j(k) show the import and export of commodity j (platinum or iridium) between target country k and the intermediary country i, respectively.

Equation 1 captures two key dependencies: (1) the target country’s reliance on imported commodities from each intermediary country and (2) the intermediary’s reliance on FDI from the investing country. The product of these terms allows the index to quantify potential vulnerabilities of the target country to the investing country—mediated through the intermediary exporters. Although some of these potential vulnerabilities may not have yet manifested, they could be activated due to critical shocks or disruptions in any stage of the investing → intermediary → target pathway. Consequently, the index will provide a tool for policymakers using real-world trade and FDI flows to track how the market dynamics or policy changes impact the economic vulnerability of the target country.

For achieving the first objective, we considered the US as the target country k in the trade network of platinum and iridium, one potential investing country h in FDI network, and all intermediary countries between the US and the investing country with net exports of platinum and iridium to the US as well as economic ties with the potential investing country (Figure S2). We obtained the aggregated PIVIs of all intermediary countries between the US and the investing country (∑i=1IPIVIi,j(h,US)).

We repeated this process for all potential investing countries to identify the most critical investing country h as a source of indirect vulnerability to the US economy (objective 2). We performed the procedure for all years from 2010 to 2019 to identify the most critical investing country in each year and explore the pattern over time (Objective 3).

Trade and FDI data

We analyzed annual trade data of platinum and iridium from 2010 to 2019 which published by the United Nations (https://comtrade.un.org). We extracted import and export data of platinum (HS-711011: unwrought or powder; HS-711019: semi-manufactured) and iridium (HS-711041: unwrought or powder; HS-711049: semi-manufactured) to quantify the dependency of the US to its partners through the net import of platinum and iridium, respectively. To calculate the economic dependency between the US trade partners and any potential investing country, we used FDI data from 2010 to 2019 provided by the United Nations (https://unctad.org/).

When there was a flow of trade or FDI between two countries, both nations typically reported the value of these exchanges to the United Nations. However, the reported values were often not identical. In such cases we calculated the value by averaging the quantities reported by both countries. If only one country provided a value while the partner country did not, we relied on the available reported value instead.59

Quantification and statistical analysis

The PIVI analysis is applicable to any combination of target, intermediary, and investing countries, as well as any traded commodity. The analysis code for PIVI was implemented in Python 3.7.1 and is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/SPShahabi/PIVI). The results are summarized in Table 1 and Figures 1, 2, and 3.
