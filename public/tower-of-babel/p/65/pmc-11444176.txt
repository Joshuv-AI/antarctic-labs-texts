An old dog with new tricks: TFEB promotes syncytin expression and cell fusion in the human placenta
Authors: Stephen J. Renaud
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Renaud discusses a study in this issue of Genes & Development by Esbin et al. that shows that the transcription factor TFEB plays a critical role during placental development, independent of its other known cellular functions. Renaud highlights the mechanisms involved in cell fusion leading to syncytiotrophoblast formation and comments on TFEB-dependent and TFEB-independent processes.

Cells fusing with other cells is a phenomenon that occurs in a select few biological contexts. In the human placenta, the merging of trophoblasts into a singular fused entity is of vital importance for fetal development and pregnancy health. This structure, called the syncytiotrophoblast (syncytium = fused multinucleated cell, and trophoblast = placental epithelial cell), facilitates implantation into the uterus and forms the outer lining of the placental villi. As the main “fetal” cell type interfacing with maternal blood, the functions of the syncytiotrophoblast are vast and include the regulation of nutrient and gas exchange between maternal and fetal blood, production of numerous hormones and other substances needed for pregnancy maintenance and maternal–fetal communication, and protection from toxins, pathogens, and other substances that can harm the fetus. Dysregulated syncytiotrophoblast formation is detrimental to pregnancy success and is implicated in several obstetrical complications, with long-term health repercussions for the gestational parent and newborn.

The syncytiotrophoblast has a limited life span and needs to be continuously replenished with fresh cytoplasm and nuclei from underlying cytotrophoblasts to maintain the integrity of the maternal–placental interface. Cytotrophoblasts are self-renewing mononuclear cells that, under the correct cues (which are not yet well defined), stop proliferating and fuse into the overlying syncytiotrophoblast. This fusion event is catalyzed by syncytin-1 and syncytin-2, encoded by ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1, respectively. ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1 are the remnants of envelope genes from retroviral infections that became embedded in human ancestral DNA <50 million years ago (Mi et al. 2000; Blaise et al. 2003). Syncytin-1 and syncytin-2 are expressed in the placenta, bind to their corresponding receptors (SLC1A4/5 and MFSD2A, respectively) on adjacent cells, and promote cytotrophoblast fusion into the syncytiotrophoblast. Remarkably, although these particular syncytins are restricted to primates, exaptation of syncytin-like genes from distinct retroviruses has evolved independently in multiple species to facilitate trophoblast fusion at the maternal–placental blood barrier (Dupressoir et al. 2012).

Coordinated expression of syncytin-1, syncytin-2 and their receptors is vital for syncytiotrophoblast formation; dysregulation can disrupt the integrity of the placental barrier or prematurely deplete the cytotrophoblast pool. So, what controls the expression of these genes? A strong case has been made for glial cells missing-1 (GCM1), a placenta-enriched transcription factor that transcriptionally regulates ERVW-1, ERVFRD-1, and MFSD2A (Liang et al. 2010). Intriguingly, GCM1 has an analogous role in regulating syncytin-related genes in mice despite their sequence dissimilarity from human syncytins. Consequently, mouse embryos lacking GCM1 are nonviable because of inadequate placentation (Anson-Cartwright et al. 2000). Beyond GCM1, other transcriptional regulators of syncytins have not been well characterized.

In this issue of Genes & Development, Esbin et al. (2024) explore the involvement of TFEB in the transcriptional regulation of syncytin genes. TFEB is a basic helix–loop–helix leucine zipper transcription factor that is considered a master regulator of genes involved in autophagy and lysosomal biogenesis during nutrient stress. Interestingly, mice lacking TFEB are embryonic-lethal at around the same time as mice lacking GCM1, suggesting that TFEB is required for placentation (Steingrímsson et al. 1998). Using human BeWo choriocarcinoma cells, which can be induced to undergo fusion into syncytiotrophoblast-like cells in the presence of forskolin, Esbin et al. (2024) observed that TFEB translocates to the nucleus and exhibits increased chromatin interactions following forskolin stimulation. The investigators generated cells deficient in TFEB alone as well as cells doubly deficient in TFEB and the closely related transcription factor TFE3 using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genomic editing. Compared with cells with intact TFEB, TFEB-deficient cells had a profound reduction in ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1 expression following exposure to forskolin. The investigators used several innovative methods such as dual-color fluorescence (in which a secondary color emerges when two primary colors intermingle following cell fusion) and split green fluorescent protein (GFP) assays (GFP assembles and fluoresces after cells containing different components of GFP merge) to demonstrate a reduction in cell fusion in the absence of TFEB, which was rescued by reintroduction of TFEB. Using a heterologous fusion assay involving BeWo cells and human embryonic kidney 293T cells, the investigators also showed that the lack of syncytin expression is the most likely reason why TFEB-deficient cells fail to fuse.

Interestingly, not all syncytiotrophoblast-related factors were impacted by TFEB deficiency. For instance, there was no difference in the expression of GCM1 between control and TFEB-deficient cells, indicating that the effect of TFEB on ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1 expression is likely independent of action on GCM1 transcription. TFEB deficiency also had no effect on the production of human chorionic gonadotropin, a key syncytiotrophoblast hormone, supporting the notion that trophoblast fusion and hormone production are uncoupled and regulated by distinct pathways. Surprisingly, also unaffected in TFEB-deficient cells was the expression of genes associated with autophagic processes and lysosome biogenesis despite the well-established importance of TFEB for controlling their expression. Chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing identified TFEB binding within the promoter-proximal region of many autophagy and lysosome-associated genes in addition to ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1, suggesting that (1) TFEB is a direct upstream regulator of ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1, and (2) the regulation of lysosome biogenesis and autophagic processes in these cells may be more complex.

The findings reported by Esbin et al. (2024) complement several recent studies highlighting TFEB's critical role in syncytiotrophoblast formation. For instance, a recent CRISPR screen identified TFEB as a key regulator of syncytiotrophoblast development in human trophoblast stem cells (Shimizu et al. 2023). Two studies published in July of this year (Cesana et al. 2024; Zheng et al. 2024) showed that TFEB deficiency impairs syncytiotrophoblast formation in various trophoblast models without affecting lysosome number, acidity, or autophagic activity. Consistent with the findings of Esbin et al. (2024), ERVFRD-1 was identified as a transcriptional target of TFEB in these other studies, with no reciprocal regulation with GCM1. TFEB binding upstream of ERVW-1 was also reported by Cesana et al. (2024). Some differences were noteworthy between these studies, such as the effect of TFEB deficiency on syncytiotrophoblast hormone production. Nevertheless, these studies collectively underscore TFEB's essential role in the control of syncytin gene expression and syncytiotrophoblast development, distinct from its well-known functions in autophagy and lysosome biogenesis.
