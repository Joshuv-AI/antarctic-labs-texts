Childhood obesity management in primary care: Bridging guidelines and practice

Introduction

Pediatric obesity is a crisis of unprecedented scale in the United States. According to the CDC, nearly 20 % of children and adolescents aged 2–19 years have obesity, and approximately 6 % are classified as having severe obesity [1]. Recent analyses using NHANES data demonstrate a sharp rise in extreme forms of obesity, with the prevalence of “extremely severe obesity” (BMI ≥160 % of the 95th percentile) increasing by 253 % between 2008 and 2023 [2]. These trends are not evenly distributed, as adolescents, and non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic youth are disproportionately affected, compounding inequities in long-term health outcomes [2]. Globally, approximately one in five children and adolescents are affected by excess weight, with obesity prevalence ranging from less than 1 % in some low-income countries to over 25 % in high-income regions [3].

The health consequences of pediatric obesity emerge early and persist across the lifespan. Children with obesity face an increased risk of insulin resistance, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), dyslipidemia, hypertension, and type 2 diabetes before adulthood [4]. Psychosocial sequelae further burden patients and families, including stigma, depression, bullying, and reduced quality of life [5]. Longitudinal evidence also confirms that effective treatment in childhood reduces long-term risks of diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and even premature mortality, underscoring that early intervention can change life trajectories [6].

Despite this evidence, pediatric primary care providers remain under-prepared to address this crisis. Traditional residency curricula emphasize anticipatory guidance and lifestyle counseling but rarely include pharmacotherapy, bariatric surgical referral processes, or advanced behavioral approaches. As a result, many pediatric primary care providers lack the preparation needed to deliver the full spectrum of evidence-based care. Addressing this gap requires two approaches: integrating obesity medicine into pediatric trainee curricula and providing structured opportunities for practicing clinicians to build competency. Options such as continuing medical education, professional society workshops, and certification through the American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM) can help build capacity; however, the ultimate goal is to ensure that all pediatric primary care providers are prepared to manage childhood obesity as a core component of routine care. Methods

This commentary is a narrative synthesis designed to bridge existing guidelines and real-world clinical practice in the management of childhood obesity. The authors reviewed current literature from PubMed, CDC, and AAP databases between 2017 and 2025, emphasizing clinical practice guidelines, consensus statements, and major peer-reviewed studies relevant to pediatric obesity care in the primary care setting. The manuscript also integrates expert perspectives from practicing pediatricians and obesity medicine specialists on implementation barriers, workforce gaps, and opportunities for curriculum and policy reform.

Epidemiology and paradigm shift

Pediatric obesity prevalence has more than quadrupled worldwide since 1990, with the U.S. at the epicenter of this epidemic. Munte et al. describe it as a “public health emergency,” reflecting its profound medical and economic consequences [2]. In 2024, the CDC estimated the annual U.S. healthcare costs of childhood obesity at $1.3 billion, a figure that is expected to rise without more decisive intervention [1]. The persistence of obesity across the life course makes early treatment critical. For a child with severe obesity at age two, approximately 80 % will still have obesity by age thirty-five. Even more concerning is an adolescent with obesity at age nineteen has about a 94 % chance having obesity as an adult, highlighting the strong likelihood that obesity tracks forward across decades [7]. In parallel, obesity-related cancers are increasingly diagnosed at younger ages, with colorectal, kidney, pancreatic, and uterine cancers showing earlier onset temporally associated with rising childhood obesity [8]. Together, these findings underscore that pediatric obesity is not a self-limiting condition but one that shapes health trajectories throughout life.

Historically, pediatric obesity treatment emphasized lifestyle modification and counseling, which often produced limited short-term and long-term results. Over the past decade, however, a paradigm shift has occurred: obesity is now recognized as a chronic, complex disease that requires multimodal care. Pharmacotherapy and bariatric surgery, once considered last-resort options, are now recognized as appropriate treatments for selected youth. The 2023 American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) Clinical Practice Guidelines represented a watershed moment by affirming that intensive health behavior and lifestyle treatment (IHBLT) may be foundational but frequently insufficient on its own [4]. The guidelines acknowledged that obesity arises from complex genetic, physiologic, socioeconomic, and environmental drivers that cannot be addressed by diet and exercise advice alone.

This chronic disease model has direct implications for pediatric primary care providers. Just as asthma, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), chronic constipation, and musculoskeletal problems are managed as chronic illnesses in primary care, obesity must be approached with the same seriousness, continuity, and expectation of long-term management. Yet, the training of most pediatric providers has not kept pace with this new paradigm. Residency programs continue to focus primarily on anticipatory guidance and lifestyle counseling, leaving many clinicians unprepared to implement pharmacotherapy, coordinate bariatric referral, or manage complex comorbidities.

The gap between guidelines and practice is stark. Despite strong evidence and explicit recommendations, uptake remains extremely limited. In 2023, even after expanded FDA indications, fewer than 1 % of adolescents with obesity received a prescription for an anti-obesity medication [9]. Referrals for bariatric surgery are similarly rare, even among eligible youth [10]. This disconnect highlights the urgent need to equip primary care providers with the tools, training, and confidence to deliver modern obesity care. Without such action, the promise of evidence-based guidelines will remain unrealized in clinical practice.

Training gaps and opportunities

Traditional pediatric training has emphasized acute care and disease prevention, with chronic disease management, such as childhood obesity, receiving minimal attention in medical school and residency. Most graduates enter practice with a limited understanding of obesity pathophysiology and virtually no exposure to advanced behavioral interventions, pharmacotherapy, or bariatric surgical pathways. In many programs, fewer than 10 hours are devoted to obesity-related education [11], with a focus mainly on interpreting growth charts and providing basic counseling. National data quantifying the extent of obesity-focused curricula in U.S. medical schools and residency programs are limited, underscoring the need for future studies to systematically evaluate and expand training opportunities in this area. The consequences of this gap are evident in practice. Pediatric primary care providers often feel uncomfortable raising the subject of weight, risk using stigmatizing language, and often feel uncertain about managing comorbidities. Many are unsure how to initiate pharmacotherapy, when to refer for bariatric surgery, or where to find resources for comprehensive care. Fellowship opportunities in pediatric obesity medicine are scarce, further limiting mentorship and workforce development.

This gap requires a two-pronged strategy. First, obesity care must be integrated into the training pipeline. Medical students and residents should be taught to view obesity as a chronic disease, not as a lifestyle choice, and should be trained in modern management strategies. Curricula must move beyond growth chart interpretation to include obesity staging, comorbidity screening, behavioral approaches, pharmacotherapy, and surgical referral processes. Weight bias training should also be embedded early, modeling patient-centered, respectful, and compassionate care. Without these reforms, another generation of pediatricians will graduate unprepared for the most urgent health crises they will face. Second, the existing workforce must be equipped to meet current demand. Tens of thousands of pediatric primary care providers completed their training before recent advances, leaving them unwilling or unable to provide evidence-based interventions. Structured opportunities are urgently needed. Continuing medical education, professional society workshops, and mentorship can help providers acquire skills in pharmacotherapy, comorbidity identification and management, and surgical coordination. For clinicians seeking a comprehensive pathway, the American Board of Obesity Medicine (ABOM) certification establishes a solid foundation in comprehensive obesity care, while the broader goal is ensuring that all pediatric primary care providers are prepared to deliver evidence-based treatment. Interprofessional education is also essential. Pediatric obesity is thought to be best managed within a team that includes dietitians, behavioral health providers, and social workers if these resources are available. Yet the reality is in many communities such teams are unavailable, and the number of hours required to meet with all providers can be untenable for the average family. In those circumstances, it is critical that primary care providers feel prepared to initiate treatment independently and connect families to resources when possible. Training programs and continuing education should reflect this reality, empowering providers to act rather than defer care due to the absence of a “perfect” team.

The analogy to other chronic diseases such as asthma or ADHD is instructive. When complexity arises within these disease paradigms, referrals are made. However, the majority of children receive ongoing treatment from their primary provider. Pediatric obesity should be no different. It is a chronic disease with interventions that can and should be delivered in primary care. Waiting for subspecialists to absorb the demand is unrealistic; the need is simply too great. Ultimately, the future of pediatric obesity care depends on a workforce that sees this disease as part of its core responsibility. By embedding obesity as a core topic into medical education, providing structured opportunities for practicing clinicians, and normalizing obesity treatment as routine, we can move from a system of under-treatment to one where children receive timely, effective care in their medical home. Operationalizing this vision will require coordinated national efforts – such as standardized curriculum modules endorsed by pediatric and obesity medicine societies, integration of obesity content into CME frameworks, and academic–community partnerships that expand hands-on training opportunities.

Disparities and access barriers

The pediatric obesity epidemic does not affect all children equally. Prevalence is highest among non-Hispanic Black and Hispanic children, those from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, and children in rural areas [2]. These disparities stem from inequities such as limited access to healthy food, safe outdoor spaces, and quality care. Rural and underserved regions often lack specialists, structured behavioral programs, and bariatric centers, leaving families to contend with long travel distances, high costs, and fragmented referral pathways. Equipping primary care providers to deliver evidence-based treatment locally can help bridge this gap. Language and literacy barriers also impede care, underscoring the need for culturally responsive communication, interpreters, and tailored education. Insurance coverage further widens disparities: many plans exclude anti-obesity medications or impose restrictive prior authorization. For families without insurance coverage, the pursuit of advanced behavioral interventions is further complicated by barriers such as limited transportation, rigid work schedules, and competing demands, including childcare or household responsibilities. These challenges also contribute to increased school absenteeism, which can negatively affect academic performance and place additional stress on families balancing work and caregiving responsibilities. However, telehealth offers one strategy to mitigate these inequities. Virtual visits can extend care into rural and underserved communities, reduce transportation barriers, and allow families to engage in follow-up without missing work or school. While perhaps not a substitute for all in-person services, telehealth can be a powerful adjunct when supported by reimbursement and broadband infrastructure.

Overcoming these barriers requires cultural and structural change. Pediatric primary care providers must be empowered to treat obesity with the same confidence as other chronic diseases, supported by resources, team-based approaches when possible, and innovative tools like telehealth and pediatric obesity algorithms that can guide pediatricians in best practices in obesity medicine. Institutions should consider developing pediatric weight management pathways to support primary care providers in delivering consistent, evidence-based care while also supporting specialty pediatric obesity treatment programs. Health systems and professional organizations must advocate for improved reimbursement, promote continuing education, and challenge the misconception that obesity care requires specialized centers. The reality is that most children with obesity will never see a subspecialist; their care depends on primary care providers who are willing and able to act.

Limitations

This work presents a narrative commentary with perspectives based on a synthesis of current literature, expert opinion, and guideline recommendations, which may introduce interpretive bias. Because most cited evidence originates from U.S.-based sources, the findings and policy implications may not fully reflect international contexts or health system variations. Quantitative data were included only where available in the literature; therefore, prevalence and cost estimates should be interpreted as approximate. Future empirical studies and implementation research are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of proposed training reforms, clinical pathways, and policy strategies across diverse pediatric care settings. The implementation of training reform may also be constrained by factors such as limited time within existing curricula, a lack of reimbursement for clinical obesity counseling, and provider burnout. Incremental approaches – such as integrating concise obesity management modules into pediatric rotations or offering CME-based incentives, may help overcome these barriers and promote gradual system-wide adoption.

Conclusion

Pediatric obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease that now affects nearly one in five U.S. children, with severe forms rising at an alarming pace. Its consequences—spanning diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality—make clear that treatment cannot be delayed. The central question is not whether effective therapies exist, but whether the pediatric workforce is prepared to use them. Since most children will never access a specialty clinic, primary care must be the foundation of the response. Just as pediatricians manage asthma and ADHD, they must also view obesity as a core responsibility of their practice, delivering treatment where children already receive care: their medical home.

Meeting this challenge requires reform on two levels. Medical education must integrate obesity training across the continuum—from medical school through residency—emphasizing staging, comorbidity management, pharmacotherapy, bariatric referral, and stigma reduction. Practicing clinicians also need accessible continuing education, mentorship, and structured pathways such as ABOM certification to build confidence and expertise. At the system level, health care organizations should advocate for reimbursement models, expand telehealth for underserved regions, and provide tools that make evidence-based care feasible in everyday practice. The call to action is clear: empower pediatric providers with the skills, confidence, and resources to treat obesity as the chronic disease it is. The health of millions of children depends on it.
