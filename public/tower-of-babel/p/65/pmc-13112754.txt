Association between access to care and direct healthcare costs in people with and at high-risk of chronic kidney disease in the United States: a pooled cross-sectional study using the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey
Authors: Satabdi Chatterjee, Thomas Flottemesch, Lindsay G S Bengtson, Shelby L Corman, Bonnie M K Donato
Published: 2026

Background

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects 35.5 million adults in the United States (US) and is associated with nearly $77 billion in healthcare costs to the Medicare system per year [1]. Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and hypertension are the leading causes of CKD in the US, and development of CKD in patients with these diseases increases direct healthcare costs by more than two-fold [2, 3]. Adequate control of T2DM and hypertension slows progression to CKD [4–6], resulting in longer survival and lower costs to the healthcare system [7, 8]. Optimal management of these cardiometabolic conditions is therefore an important tool for reducing the prevalence of CKD, a goal of Healthy People 2030 [9].

Racial and ethnic minorities and those with indicators of low socioeconomic status are disproportionately affected by cardiometabolic diseases including CKD [10–12]. Non-White race, lower level of education, neighborhood disadvantage, and low income are associated with increased prevalence and poorer control of hypertension and T2DM [13–20], and these same risk factors are associated with higher CKD prevalence, faster progression, and increased mortality [1, 21–23].

The impact of socioeconomic factors on US healthcare costs has also been reported. A 2018 study estimated the economic burden of racial and ethnic and educational health inequities across all health conditions at $451–481 billion and $940-$978 billion, respectively, with most of this burden attributable to poor health in the Black population [24]. Within the CKD population, the associations between socioeconomic factors and costs have varied, with one study finding that an income < 125% of the federal poverty level (vs ≥ 125%) and being publicly insured (vs privately insured) were associated with higher healthcare costs, while being Black or Hispanic (vs non-Hispanic White) or uninsured (vs privately insured) was associated with lower healthcare costs [25]. Another study reported that Black race (vs. non-Black), income < $45,000 (vs ≥ $45,000), and being publicly insured (vs private) were all associated with higher healthcare costs [26].

In recognition of the importance of social determinants of health in CKD prevention, the National Kidney Foundation highlighted developing and testing interventions to reduce health disparities among its highest clinical research priorities in 2021 [27]. The ability to address these barriers requires an identification of the relative contribution of factors like financial hardship, insurance status, and ability to access healthcare providers in order to tailor interventions appropriately.

The objectives of this study were 1) to evaluate factors associated with five access barriers in people at high risk of CKD due to T2DM and/or hypertension and those with CKD, and 2) to evaluate factors associated with healthcare costs among these populations.

Methods

Study design

This was a pooled, retrospective, cross-sectional analysis using data from the Medical Expenditure Panel Survey (MEPS), a nationally representative survey of the non-institutionalized US population. MEPS is the most complete, publicly available source of data regarding healthcare cost, utilization, and insurance coverage (including the uninsured) in the US. The sampling frame of the MEPS is designed so that reliable estimates, representative of the non-institutionalized civilian population are possible. The analytic weights provided by MEPS are adjusted so that the analysis reflects a single year. Due to the complex survey design, accurate analysis of data from the MEPS requires careful application of appropriate weights according to the question, timeframe, and survey component [25, 26]. Here, in order to increase sample size, multiple years were combined and sampling weights adjusted accordingly [28, 29].

Study data were sourced from the MEPS Household Component (HC), which collects self-reported sociodemographic characteristics, access barriers, insurance status, medical conditions, and healthcare resource use and costs directly from participants, and validates medical and financial information against provider and healthcare facility records [30].

Study population

The study population consisted of those who participated in MEPS in 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, or 2020, who were at least 18 years old at the time of survey completion. The study population consisted of two mutually exclusive cohorts: the CKD/kidney failure (KF) cohort, who had at least one record with a 3-digit International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) code of N18 (CKD) and/or N19 (KF); and the high-risk cohort, who had at least one record with an ICD-10-CM code of E11 (T2DM) and/or I10 (hypertension) and no code for CKD or KF.

Study measures

The primary outcomes for the first and second objectives were the prevalence of access barriers and total healthcare costs. The key exposure of interest was status of chronic kidney disease (i.e. CKD/CKF versus high-risk of CKD/CKF). Sociodemographic variables included age, sex, race/ethnicity, US Census region, highest level of education, annual income, and marital status, which were considered covariates in final regression models.

Objective 1 outcomes: access barriers

Access barriers collected in the MEPS HC and assessed in this study included: presence of financial barriers, defined as being unable to pay family medical bills (yes or no); insurance coverage (public, private, uninsured, or missing); having a usual source of care, defined as a particular medical professional, doctor’s office, clinic, health center, or other place to go if sick or in need of advice about his or her health (yes or no); type of usual care (hospital clinic/outpatient, non-hospital, or emergency department [ED]); and ability to schedule care appointments when needed (never, sometimes, usually, or always). Due to a large number of non-responses, listwise deletion was used.

Objective 2 outcomes: healthcare costs

Healthcare costs included costs for office and hospital-based care, home health services, prescribed medicines, dental services, and other medical equipment and services, capturing direct payments from all sources such as out-of-pocket, private insurance, Medicaid, and Medicare [31]. Costs were adjusted to 2022 US$ using the Consumer Price Index for Medical Care.

Data analysis

Bivariate analyses were conducted to compare demographic characteristics and prevalence of access barriers between the two groups using Chi-square tests. For Objective 1, thirteen multivariable logistic regression models were used to estimate the relative odds of having each access barrier in the CKD/KF compared to the high-risk population, reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For Objective 2, seven multivariate regression models with an indicator of high-risk CKD status and interaction terms assuming gamma-distributed errors were estimated to assess the association of each access barrier on annual healthcare costs while controlling for other patient characteristics. Results are reported as the marginal impact, along with associated 95% CIs, on total costs. Listwise deletion was used and analytic sample adjustments were made for each model to address missingness. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Additional details are provided in the appendix.

Results

The study population consisted of 34,251 MEPS participants across the five study years (CKD/KF, 596; high-risk, 33,655), with weighted sample sizes of 1,185,517 in the CKD/KF cohort and 69,695,536 in the high-risk cohort.

Unadjusted, bivariate comparisons

In unadjusted comparisons of sociodemographic characteristics, individuals with CKD/KF were more likely to be at least 65 years of age (60.7% vs 46.9%; p < 0.001), have less than a college education (66.0% vs. 61.4%; p = 0.045), have an annual income < $25,000 per year (37.2% vs. 22.8%; p < 0.001), and be separated, widowed, or divorced (39.9% vs. 31.9%; p < 0.001), compared to the high- risk population (Table 1).

Unadjusted mean (95% CI) total annual healthcare costs were $36,177 ($31,086, $41,269) higher in the CKD/KF cohort ($49,694) compared to the high-risk cohort ($13,516; 95% CI: $31,086, $41,269; data not shown).

Results from multivariable regressions

Following adjustment for demographic characteristics, the CKD/KF cohort had greater odds of reporting inability to pay medical bills (OR, 1.83; 95% CI, 1.47, 2.28), being publicly insured (OR, 3.97; 95% CI, 3.09, 5.06), reporting that their usual source of care is a hospital clinic/outpatient department (OR, 1.29; 95% CI, 1.10, 1.50) compared to the high-risk cohort. There were no differences in the odds of having a usual source of care or being able to schedule care appointments between the CKD/KF and high-risk cohort.

After adjustment for sociodemographic characteristics, average annual healthcare costs were $25,042 ($19,578, $30,503) higher in the CKD/KF cohort compared to the high-risk cohort (Table 3). Other characteristics associated with higher costs included being publicly insured compared to privately insured ($2,352; $1,807, $2,898); age 45–64 years ($2,353; $1,966, $2,739) or ≥ 65 years old ($2,064; $1,551, $2,578) compared to 18–44 years old; being non-Hispanic White ($2,194; $1,805, $2,584) or non-Hispanic Black ($577; $100, $1,054) compared to Hispanic; having a college education ($967; $569, $1,366), graduate school ($1,591; $1,000, $2,182), or other level of education ($1,064; $416, $1,712) compared to less than college; and being never married ($1,039; $646, $1,431) or separated, divorced, or widowed ($1,409; $941, $1,877) compared to married (Table 3). Characteristics associated with lower costs included being uninsured compared to privately insured (-$3,220; -$3,524, -$2,917), being male compared to female (-$1,846; -$2,115, -$1,578), and having an annual income of $25,000-$49,999 (-$1,178; -$1,567, -$790), $50,000-$74,999 (-$884; -$1,368, -$399), or ≥ $75,000 (-$745; -$1,223, -$267) compared to an annual income of < $24,999.

Discussion

Using a nationally representative, publicly available data source, this study examines and compares self-reported barriers to access to care among individuals with CKD/KF and those at high risk of CKD due to the presence of T2DM and/or hypertension and highlights the impact of access barriers on costs in these populations. Compared to the high-risk population, those with CKD/KF were more likely to report being unable to pay medical bills, to be publicly insured, and to receive care usually at a hospital clinic/outpatient department. Higher adjusted costs in individuals with CKD/KF compared to high-risk individuals suggest the potential for cost avoidance of progression to CKD in those with hypertension and/or T2DM. In individuals with CKD/KF, costs were $22,701 higher among those who self-reported the inability to pay medical bills compared to those who did not, further compounding economic disadvantage. In this same population, being uninsured was associated with $39,660 lower costs compared to those with private insurance.

Individuals in the CKD/KF cohort were more likely than the high-risk cohort to experience barriers related to care affordability. Within the CKD/KF cohort, higher prevalence of self-reported inability to pay medical bills was coupled with a dramatic increase in annual costs among those affected, making affordability a key target for intervention. Interventions aimed at improving quality of care, such as specialist care and use of medications to prevent disease progression, may be cost-prohibitive in populations with lower incomes. For example, people with CKD who live in areas with lower median income are less likely to be referred for specialty care [32]. Lower income, in addition to Black or Asian race and female sex, is associated with lower use of guideline-recommended therapies such as sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT2) inhibitors [33]. Among those prescribed medications, people with lower incomes or who are uninsured experience cost-related non-adherence to medications in hypertension and T2DM [34]. System-level interventions to mitigate these barriers include increased community-based care and case management [35]; incorporation of health equity incentives into value-based care models [36]; and increased Medicaid funding for federally qualified health centers which have been shown to improve control of T2DM and hypertension [37, 38].

Conversely, individuals with CKD/KF were not more likely than high-risk individuals to have challenges with accessibility. Having a usual source of care was more common in the CKD/KF cohort than in the high-risk cohort, and both cohorts rarely reported never or sometimes being able to schedule care appointments. However, using a hospital clinic/outpatient or ED as the usual source of care was associated with substantial increases in costs. While use of the ED as a usual source of care is typically linked to poor access to healthcare, it is also associated with an increased risk of hospitalization and all-cause mortality, after controlling for demographic and clinical characteristics such as race/ethnicity, education, income, smoking status, blood pressure, and hemoglobin A1c [39]. EDs and other urgent care settings are not designed to deliver care for chronic conditions, nor are they equipped to manage multiple comorbid conditions that are often present in people with CKD. Efforts to improve continuity of care through structural improvements in healthcare delivery have been shown to reduce ED use in patients with CKD [40, 41], further supporting the bidirectional relationship between use of the ED as a usual source of care and poor continuity of care.

While being uninsured was uncommon in the CKD/KF cohort, the dramatic reduction in costs in this population should be interpreted with caution, as it is more likely to be due to foregoing needed care rather than efficient delivery of care. Those who are uninsured, even temporarily, are less likely to receive preventive care, to have seen or spoken to a healthcare provider and are more likely to report delaying or not receiving healthcare due to costs, compared to the insured [42–44]. While safety-net providers such as community health centers, county health departments, and public hospital programs are available to uninsured and under-insured patients with CKD, health outcomes are poorer in these settings compared to those accessed by the insured due to factors such as lack of provider awareness of CKD, suboptimal risk factor management, and lack of specialist providers [45].

Healthcare costs (2022 US$) in the CKD/KF population in this study (mean, $49,694) were higher than those reported in previous studies. In one study using claims-linked electronic health record data, annual healthcare costs (2019 US$) in commercially insured patients were higher than in those covered by Medicare (mean $37,843 vs. $22,660 per year, respectively) [46]. Annual healthcare costs (2019 US$) of $35,649 and $25,010 have been reported in commercially insured patients with CKD with and without diabetes, respectively [47].

Recently, in patients with CKD and T2DM with commercial or Medicare Advantage coverage, annual healthcare costs (2020 US$) were estimated $28,636 [48]. Key differences between this study and previous ones include having a predominantly publicly insured population and the inclusion of patient out-of-pocket costs. Because Medicare provides coverage for patients with end-stage renal disease regardless of age, [49] this study provides a more comprehensive assessment of costs than those with commercially insured populations, particularly in those with KF. In patients with CKD who are not dialysis-dependent, annual out-of-pocket costs were shown to exceed those in patients with cancer or stroke [50].

This study has some limitations which can be attributed to the nature of data and the disease under investigation. The MEPS is a repeated cross-section of the US population. MEPS data do not allow for examination of disease progression and its association with access barriers or costs. While we attempted to compensate for this by pooling multiple years of data, the final sample of our population of primary interest, those with CKD/KF, was relatively small. The level of non-response (i.e., missingness) compounded this challenge. Future investigations revisiting these questions may consider imputation methods such hot-decking, mean/median/mode substitution, and/or multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE). We refrained from taking the additional analytic steps and thus some finding should be considered exploratory rather than definitive [51]. Despite these limitations, MEPS is considered to be a robust and comprehensive source of information on a broad range of topics and has been used in numerous previous studies in CKD [25, 26, 52–54]. Disease severity measures such as CKD stage and T2DM control are not available in MEPS, and thus the analysis could not evaluate or control for differences in disease severity among those with and without access barriers. The 3-digit ICD-10 code for kidney failure (N19) does not provide insight into the use of dialysis or kidney transplant, which are known cost drivers in this population [26, 55, 56]. Finally, because early-stage CKD is often undiagnosed [57], there could be a potential misclassification of early-stage patients into the high-risk group, which could have resulted in an underestimation of cost differences between the high-risk and CKD/KF groups.

Conclusions

People with CKD or KF are a vulnerable population with high healthcare costs and financial challenges in accessing appropriate medical care. Especially disadvantaged populations include those who report an inability to pay medical bills, whose higher healthcare costs may be unaffordable; and those who are uninsured and may not receive routine care they cannot afford. Given the increase in healthcare costs in patients with CKD/KF compared to those at risk, and the impact of access barriers on costs in those with CKD/KF, prevention of CKD is likely to both decrease healthcare costs and reduce financial hardship. Resources for early intervention in people with T2DM and hypertension are needed to promote disease management and slow progression to CKD in this population.
