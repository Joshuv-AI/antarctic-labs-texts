Addressing equity through integration of child health services in North West London disadvantaged neighbourhoods: a mixed-methods evaluation
Authors: Bina Ram, Kajl Ahmad, Dougal Hargreaves, Mitch Blair
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGeneral Practitioner (GP) Child Health Hubs (CHH) have been established in North West London (NWL), UK, for over a decade, yet gaps remain in reaching underserved families with children aged <5 years. To address this, the Early Years GP CHH Pilots (April 2023–March 2025) were set up in three NWL boroughs (Harrow, Brent and Ealing). Each site aimed to establish hyper-local preventative care teams (HLPCTs), identify their target population and health need, and design their evidence-based intervention programme. Our study aimed to describe the processes each site undertook for programme implementation and identify early impact.MethodsWe focused on Harrow and Brent. Using the Medical Research Council framework and the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour framework for behaviour change, we collected data from each site on workforce engagement, volunteer organisation collaborations, community engagement, case studies and specialist clinics. In addition, we conducted interviews with members of multidisciplinary teams (MDTs). Data analyses are descriptive, narrative and thematic.ResultsBoth sites established HLPCTs. Harrow used the Community Health and Wellbeing Worker (CHWW) model and focused on the first 1000 days and Brent used Family Link Workers (FLWs) focusing on respiratory health. Workforce and community engagement were successful for networking and reaching communities. Collaborations with voluntary services aligned services to local needs and public health priorities. Case studies identified both holistic and medical needs, and specialist clinics increased parent confidence (Harrow) and assessed, diagnosed and prescribed medication accordingly for asthma (Brent). ‘Community connectors’ (CHWWs, FLWs) were crucial to increase family engagement and brought more urgent cases to MDT meetings. However, a key finding was that engaging with families was complicated and may be affecting reach. Interviews highlighted enablers as well as challenges of MDT meetings.ConclusionCollaborative approaches and community connectors are key to engaging with families but challenges remain. Sufficient maturity of interventions is needed to fully establish impacts.Keywords: Child Health, Epidemiology, Infant

Introduction

Health disparities among children and young people (CYP) and those from disadvantaged backgrounds have remained persistent over many years despite a changing landscape of health and local authority (LA) service provision.1 Current pressures on the United Kingdom’s (UK) National Health Service (NHS) for CYP include increasing General Practitioner (GP) caseloads, inadequate community nursing, year-on-year rise in unscheduled and emergency care,2 overwhelmed mental health services and long waiting lists for assessment of neurodevelopmental disorders and allergic conditions.3 These pressures have significantly worsened since the COVID-19 pandemic. The UK has one of the poorest child health outcomes compared with other high-income countries and families already facing barriers to access healthcare continue to face challenges including missed opportunities for preventive care and early intervention and timely management of health conditions, leading to increased hospitalisations and higher healthcare costs.4 Young children, particularly those aged 5 years and under, are the highest attenders to hospital urgent and emergency services.5 Early identification and support for development issues are known to improve life chances.6

Integrated approaches to improving child health and well-being have shown to be effective both nationally and internationally. Evidence from Australia,7 UK8 and America9 suggests that attempts at integrating health and social care services can positively impact vulnerable populations and address the wider social determinants of health.10 11 Through effective relationships between health and social service providers, integrated services can offer a more holistic family-centred and community-centred approach to care. Current UK initiatives include Family Hubs, and Child Health Hubs (CHHs) which provide a local health service bringing together both medical and holistic approaches in one place.12 ‘Community Connectors’, for example, Community Health and Wellbeing Workers (CHWW) and Family Link Workers (FLWs), work closely within integrated care systems to link communities to local health services and support wider social, practical and emotional factors that affect a child’s health and wellbeing.13 14 Evidence suggests that community connectors are effective in improving parental self-efficacy, engagement with early years services and recall of early years information for children aged under 5 years, and there have been demonstrated impacts of increased breastfeeding rates and vaccination uptake11 and reducing overall morbidity.11 15 However, though these initiatives mitigate social determinants of poor health, especially among underserved population groups at high risk, for example, those experiencing domestic abuse, poor housing, financial issues or food poverty,16 17 there is also evidence to suggest that the most vulnerable families in underserved communities are not being reached.16 18

The Fuller (2022)19 and Hewitt Reports (2023)20 highlighted the need for integrated care systems (ICSs) to improve the use of data, community engagements and LA partnerships19 and recommended that ICSs required cultural and systematic changes to support transformation to show improvement in health outcomes.20 Despite widespread investment in integrated health and care systems in England, benefits to CYP in underserved communities remain inconsistent.21

For over a decade, North West London (NWL) has well-established GP CHHs within primary care networks. Based on the Connecting Care for Children (CC4C) model,22 the hubs are based in a population of around 50 000 families and bring together specialists to provide regular community based, joint specialist and primary care clinics and monthly multidisciplinary team (MDT) meetings, where patient cases are discussed with a wider group of health and non-health professionals. This model and other similar models have been shown to be efficient and cost-effective23 and are recommended as part of the latest NHS England Guidance on Neighbourhood Multi-Disciplinary Teams for CYP.24 Building on previous reports19 20 and the success of CC4C, NHS England funded the ‘Early Years Pilots’ in three NWL boroughs (Harrow, Brent and Ealing) to establish hyper-local preventive care teams (HLPCT) to reach families with children aged 0–5 years living in the most disadvantaged neighbourhoods.

The Early Years Pilots (context)

The Early Years Pilots were funded for 2 years from April 2023 to March 2025. Funding was allocated per site (online supplemental material 1) to determine health priority areas based on an interagency needs assessment and create an intervention programme. Local community involvement was achieved through extensive stakeholder engagement; health and well-being fairs where professionals, voluntary sector organisations and families were able to exchange information and participate in workshops focusing on key health and social care priorities. The core intervention components common to all three sites are centred around six design features (online supplemental material 2). Our study has included details from two of three pilot sites (Harrow and Brent). The third site, Ealing, started the intervention much later than expected and had little time to develop their programme and implement the intervention within the time period of the project. The Early Years Pilots intervention provided a unique opportunity to evaluate the implementation processes that sites undertook to engage with stakeholders and the local voluntary and community organisations and community. The aim of our evaluation was to identify whether the pilot sites were successful in establishing HLPCTs, the processes undertaken to reach underserved families in their localities and impacts.

Methods

Study design

Our study used an observational mixed-methods approach of quantitative descriptive data and qualitative data from case studies and interviews. Our design aligns with the Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions.25 We created a theory of change logic model for the early years pilots informed by the Donabedian structure26 for evaluating quality of care: structure (input), process (activities, measures) and outcomes (impact) (figure 1).

North West London Early Years Pilots theory of change logic model.

Guided by components of our logic model and where data were available from the sites, we aimed to assess whether the Early Years Pilot sites established HLPCTs, as indicated by: (1) workforce engagement; (2) collaborations with volunteer organisations; (3) local community engagement, and to evaluate the early impact of HLPCTs on service delivery, as indicated by: (4) case studies; (5) specialist clinics and (6) interviews with MDT health professionals to identify their views on how MDTs work, including any challenges.

Data collection

Quantitative data

We collected data on ‘workforce engagement’, for example, different professionals attending MDT meetings and the number of cases discussed, events aimed at those delivering early years services in their respective boroughs and attendance. Sites also provided information on: the number of applications received for collaborative local community projects and how many were funded; case studies; the number attending community events; feedback on how the community events were received by local residents (eg, how useful / informative the event was) and the most common health issues affecting families; the number of patients seen in specialist clinics and clinical indicators of impact where available.

Qualitative data

Qualitative data included case studies bought for discussion at MDT meetings to assess the range of support families required.

We designed a semi-structured interview guided by previous research27 that aimed to understand how MDTs contribute to delivering early years services as part of integrated health systems. We distributed an email through our networks to invite health professionals who are part of MDTs in NWL to participate in an online (Microsoft Teams) interview.

Analysis

Quantitative descriptive data are presented as n and/or percentages. Narrative synthesis of case studies is presented by applying the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation – Behaviour (COM-B) framework for Behaviour Change.28 Each transcript from the qualitative interviews was read independently by two researchers. Thematic analysis was used to code common themes both inductively (what emerges) and deductively (based on the COM-B framework). To reduce researcher bias of themes identified, both researchers met to discuss their codes and agree the final themes. A third researcher was consulted for any discrepancies or disagreements.

Patient and public involvement

Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) was undertaken through a series of workshops and public events designed to inform local communities of the services available and to ascertain health and care priorities in the local areas.

Results

We present our results under each indicator (as described in our methods). To assess whether HLPCTs were established, results are presented by (1) workforce engagement, (2) collaborations with volunteer organisations and (3) local community engagement. To evaluate early impacts of HLPCTs, we describe (4) case studies, (5) specialist clinics and (6) interviews with MDT health professionals. Each section describes findings from Harrow, followed by Brent.

Workforce engagement

Multidisciplinary teams

MDT meetings were attended by different professionals including GPs, practice nurses, paediatricians, pharmacists, health visitors and subspecialists from Child and Adolescent Mental Health Services (CAMHS). Attendance ranged from 10 to 23 attendees per meeting, and the number of cases discussed varied.

In Harrow, between January and November 2024, 42 cases specifically for children aged under 5 years were discussed during nine different MDT meetings. Issues were sleep disorders, asthma diagnoses, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), attachment problems, eye concerns and constipation. Case discussions led to referrals for children and/or parents to occupational therapy, Multi Agency Safeguarding Hubs, Speech and Language Therapy, children’s centres, sleep courses, therapists for social stories work and medication reviews. One case involved a child at a high risk of developing a megacolon and needing possible surgery.

Cases for children aged under 5 years discussed in MDTs in Brent between April 2022 and February 2024 reached a peak in May 2023 (67%). Two children who had ADHD, global developmental delay and ASD were discussed with the CAMHS and the paediatrics team. Management for both cases involved referral criteria for the child and the need for further information about the families to offer parents advice on strategies to manage their children’s needs.

The breadth of issues discussed during MDTs highlighted the need for holistic approaches to ensure appropriate early years support (table 2).

Events

Harrow conducted two events: a one-off launch in July 2024 to publicise the Early Years Pilot, and four ‘Munch & Learn Clubs’ (an open invitation to professionals and voluntary sector staff focusing on themed topics, eg, child development, immunisation, oral health, housing, asylum seeker health). The launch was attended by 36 professionals and HLPCT teams, who expressed their appreciation for networking with local community Early Years services. Four ‘Munch and Learn’ Clubs (January, March, May and June 2024) offered opportunities to share learning around topics including housing, legal services, asylum health, healthcare plans and immunisations. Each club was attended by up to 15 varied local professionals.

Collaborations with local volunteer organisations

The pilot sites invited bids from local volunteer organisations to develop community projects targeting early years child health. Harrow received seven applications and funded six community projects. Brent received 10 applications and funded two projects. Individual grants varied from £7000 to £25 000. Harrow’s community projects engaged and supported just under 250 families and over 300 individuals with a range of issues including social isolation, caring responsibilities, financial difficulties and mental health problems (online supplemental material 6).

Local community engagement (PPIE)

Health and wellbeing fairs

The Harrow Association of Somali Voluntary Organisations (HASVO; a community project partner) hosted the fair and distributed posters/leaflets to the local community. The fair took place in February 2024 to raise awareness around local healthcare services (eg, vaccinations, CAMHS, legal advice). Workshops led by professionals provided information and guidance around child speech, communication, development and mental health. A total of 256 local community residents from a diverse range of backgrounds attended; 90% scored the event a success (5/5), 94% reported learning of a service that they were not previously aware of, 33% reported information about mental health as the most useful, and all attendees (100%) reported that the fair increased their confidence in seeking support. Stallholder feedback included opportunities for networking and shared learning. The fair led to four areas of impact: (1) parents’ increased understanding around vaccinations; (2) an increased awareness among voluntary community services of GP services and support (e.g., Providing Assessment and Treatment for Children at Home System); (3) increased awareness among GPs about community services leading to direct referrals for families with concerns around their child’s development; (4) opportunities to introduce the local Children’s Centre staff and Family Hub activities for parents and infants.

Brent Health Matters, a borough-wide partnership programme, hosted the Brent fair which took place in June 2024 to raise awareness around early years health and well-being services, risks of household smoking, undiagnosed asthma attacks, developmental delay and vaccine education. A total of 110 local community residents attended; 85% were female, 67% were under the age of 40 years, and 25% were from the most deprived Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) quintile. A total of 74 attendees completed feedback questionnaires; the fair was rated 4.6 on a scale of 1=poor to 5=excellent. There was increased parental confidence: 63% reported learning about services available for household smoking; 75% reported learning around childhood development; and 93% reported learning about health and well-being services available to them. The most popular stalls were for healthy eating and vaccinations. Clinical teams screened 57 people for diabetes (leading to two diagnoses of cases for type 2 diabetes mellitus), blood pressure, weight management and hypertension (one of severe hypertension leading to ambulance attendance). Most stallholders expressed that families needed opportunities to ask questions before making decisions about their child’s health, especially around vaccinations. Opportunities to vaccinate young infants on the day were not taken up by parents; however, most felt better informed after discussing their concerns.

Specialist clinics

Postnatal group consultations (Harrow)

Patient group consultations took place between August 2022 and December 2024. This was aimed at women who met the inclusion criteria of mild to moderate mental health issues or lack of social and emotional support identified from a single GP practice or other primary care staff. The group consisted of monthly 2-hour sessions led by a social prescriber and antenatal educator using evidence-based early parenting interventions. 60 parents were invited; 38 (63%) attended. 13 participants (36%) had complete pre-data and post-data collection which found that there was a significant increase in patient activation but not parenting confidence.

Preschool respiratory health (Brent)

Monthly MDT clinics offering 30–40 min appointments were available for 3–4 patients in the preschool age group (0–5 years) who had attended local emergency department services on more than two occasions within the past 6 months with ‘preschool wheeze’. From a total of 2319 under-fives living in the locality, 214 (9%) had suspected asthma or had asthma medication prescribed to them without a formal recorded asthma diagnosis (data sourced by WSIC and GP system searches). As part of the clinic pilot, 15 of these patients were opportunistically invited to attend the respiratory clinic; 14 (93%) patients were seen (one did not attend) during September 2024 to December 2024. Three (20%) patients had a personalised asthma action plan (PAAP) prior to attending, and all 14 (93%) had a PAAP after attending the clinic. All patients had their inhaler technique checked and reviewed; eight (53%) had medication changed and/or a skin prick test, and at their second visit, four (27%) patients reported that their asthma symptoms had improved (table 4). Feedback from users and the clinicians highlighted the effectiveness of a multidisciplinary and integrated approach to health delivery in underserved populations.

Establishing specialist clinics required strong leadership from highly motivated clinical staff and took longer than anticipated due to the time and capacity to develop collaborative working relationships within teams. Across both sites, professionals reported that engagement with parents was challenging, with approximately one-third of families refusing contact or disengaging after a few successful contact attempts.

Interviews with MDT members

The interviews with MDT members aimed to gain a better understanding of whether those involved in MDTs found it helpful, how the collaborations with other MDT members worked, and any challenges they experienced.

Eight professionals participated (two paediatricians, three GPs, one Early Years Team Manager, one Early Years Worker and one Family Help Manager) who were from five different NWL boroughs (Ealing, Brent, Kensington and Chelsea, Harrow, and Hammersmith and Fulham). Interviews took place between March and April 2024 and lasted an average of 30 min.

Three overarching themes were identified (table 5).29 The first theme of ‘time’ was two-fold, one which highlighted that more time was needed to discuss cases in MDT meetings, while the other was that staff did not always have capacity to join MDT meetings due to workload. The second theme was ‘interprofessional relationships’ which identified power imbalances and the importance of fostering a collaborative multiagency approach allowing for both medical and non-medical cases to be discussed, especially where holistic approaches are needed. Medical professionals attending MDT meetings expressed appreciation of holistic approaches to support families. However, non-medical professionals expressed concerns that their contributions were not always encouraged due to heavily medically-focused cases. The third theme was ‘shared learning’ where most expressed being part of an MDT provided valuable insights from those bringing different expertise to the meetings.

Discussion

Our mixed-methodology allowed for a comprehensive understanding of a complex intervention aiming to reach underserved populations. Our approach, using the MRC framework, supports the need for flexible methodology to evaluate interventions in real-world settings.

Both pilot sites successfully established HLPCTs, reaching many families living in disadvantaged neighbourhoods, and there were several key strengths of how interventions were delivered in both Harrow and Brent particularly around accessibility, integration and user experience. There was improved access to specialist support, reducing the need for hospital referrals. The hubs were also effective in bringing services together, and there was better collaboration between GPs, health visitors and clinicians, increasing confidence and skills in managing cases through closer collaborative working leading to efficiency of care, that is, cases that may have previously been referred to secondary care were managed within the MDTs and hubs. This multidisciplinary approach ensured children could be assessed holistically as well as clinically, and professionals were able to share knowledge and make joint decisions more easily. Furthermore, families reported positive experiences, especially with CHWWs which helped build trust and better communication. This supports other studies where engaging families through the use of ‘community connectors’ (CHWWs and FLWs) facilitated service integration,7,9 a generalisable and an effective universal intervention.11 15 30 Importantly, the pilots showed potential for prevention and early intervention; by addressing issues earlier, the hubs helped prevent escalation of some health concerns and supported better long-term outcomes for children.

Our evaluation highlighted a number of challenges despite the overall positive impact. A key issue was capacity, workforce pressure and limited resources. Clinics were dependent on the availability of paediatricians and already stretched GP staff which would limit how many children could be seen. Operational and logistical difficulties included coordinating schedules across MDTs creating administrative pressures. The hubs required dedicated resources and ongoing investment for which sustainability and funding are required, and raised concerns about how the models could be maintained or expanded without secure funding or workforce capacity. Additionally, there were data related issues; information sharing was not always permitted, and differences in recording practices of data that were shared created challenges with measuring impact. Furthermore, the evaluation identified family engagement barriers, with some families not responding to multiple contact attempts nor attending appointments. This may reflect underlying factors such as childcare and socioeconomic pressures, as well as cultural and language-barriers and trust-related factors which can all influence uptake. These findings align with other studies both nationally14 31 and internationally32 33 which also identify the need for relationship building with families as part of an ICSs approach to reach underserved families. These barriers were not explored in depth and are not yet well understood. This highlights the need for more detailed follow-up research to better identify and address underlying causes.

The NHS is under enormous pressure to meet key missions set out by parliament34 which mostly emphasise demand management to ensure improved access to GP and specialist services. The guidance on Neighbourhood Health reiterates the desire of major shifts, including ‘from hospital to community services’ with a greater use of existing resources within the voluntary sectors.35 The findings from our study support the shift to community services, highlighting how voluntary organisations, community events and the breadth of support (from clinical to holistic) are needed to support diverse local communities. A recent randomised controlled trial in South London has demonstrated benefits of integrated care when focused on specific health conditions such as constipation, headache and allergy,36 but more research is needed to understand benefits of integrating holistic care for families. Future research should build in longer-term cohort approaches, as demonstrated by Sure Start,37 to explore potential latent effects, with a greater focus on family impact over time. Aligning integrated early years services with strong longer term social and economic policies that tackle poverty may help to reduce child health inequalities.38

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of our study include the opportunistic evaluation of an early years pilot to reach underserved families. We used the MRC framework for evaluating complex interventions and COM-B frameworks which helped focus our evaluation’s mixed methodology approach. Our study’s findings align with that of other studies conducted both nationally and internationally which support its generalisability. Limitations include lack of data provided by sites where governance processes prevented timely sharing of data. Our study had limited data on longer term ‘impact’ given most cases were still active at time of evaluation. Not all data were available across sites, and we were unable to include the Ealing pilot in our study due to the intervention (StartWell) commencing much later than anticipated. An important limitation of our work is cost-effectiveness, which we were unable to include due to lack of data. This may have strengthened the evidence base for long-term planning by addressing affordability and sustainability to provide a clearer case for investment and helping prioritise resources within constrained healthcare budgets.

Our evaluation suggests that Early Years GP Child Health Hubs have strong potential to be implemented in other areas, particularly due to their ability to improve access, integrate care and reduce pressure on secondary services. However, successful replication will depend on adapting the model to local context, including population needs, workforce capacity and existing service relationships. Key requirements for this include secure paediatric and GP capacity, clear referral pathways and dedicated coordination support to manage multidisciplinary working. Importantly, consistent data collection and information-sharing systems are essential to demonstrate impact. Additionally, efforts to improve family engagement, particularly among underserved groups, are critical. Overall, while the early years models are transferable in principle, its effectiveness relies on careful local tailoring alongside sufficient resourcing and system-level support.

Conclusion

Early Years support to underserved families require an integrated healthcare services agile workforce which works across organisational boundaries and supports those with varying needs. There is a critical role for trusted community connectors who can bridge traditional hospital or GP services and help optimise family engagement. It is important that families interacting with these services are followed-up for extended periods to better understand the potential impact of integrated health systems to improve CYP health and well-being.
