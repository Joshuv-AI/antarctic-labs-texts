Digital Assessment of Gingival Display During Different Facial Expressions Using AutoCAD Software: An Exploratory Clinical Study
Authors: Rija Anees, Muhammad Aamir Ghafoor Chaudhary, Syeda Kainat Fatima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore and compare the extent of maximal gingival display among four different facial expressions, including the requested smile, Duchenne smile, grimace of disgust, and funnel-shaped expression, using AutoCAD software.Materials and methodsThis exploratory cross-sectional study was conducted in the department of Prosthodontics, Islamic International Dental Hospital, Islamabad, Pakistan, from December 2024 to March 2025. A total of 36 adults (20 females and 16 males), fulfilling the inclusion criteria, were randomly selected. Four different facial expressions, including a ‘requested smile’, a ‘Duchenne smile’, a ‘grimace of disgust’, and a ‘funnel-shaped’ expression, were explained to each participant. A digital camera was then used to capture four standardised photographs for every subject, each photograph showing a specific facial expression. Every photograph was then analysed using AutoCAD software to determine the height of gingival display at all maxillary anterior teeth and first premolars from the lower edge of the upper lip to the gingival zenith point of each tooth. Data analysis was done using SPSS Version 27. The Friedman test with post hoc comparison was used to assess and compare the amount of gingival tissue revealed across the four facial expressions.ResultsWithin the limitations of this exploratory clinical analysis, the most gingival display was produced at the central and lateral incisors by the Duchenne, grimace and funnel-shaped expressions. Similarly, both the Grimace and Duchenne smiles showed the most gingival display among the four facial expressions at the canines and the first premolars.ConclusionsThe Duchenne smile showed more gingival display above all maxillary anterior teeth compared to the other expressions.Clinical relevanceAssessing gingival display across various facial expressions offers objective direction for prosthodontic and aesthetic treatment planning, encompassing smile line evaluation, anterior restoration and implant margin positioning, as well as enhancing aesthetic results and patient satisfaction.Key words: Facial expressions, Facial muscle, Gingiva, Image analysis, Smiling, Software

Introduction

Smiling is a very simple and basic but perplexing facial expression. Though it is a well-recognised expression, it can be challenging to interpret, as it does not necessarily reflect actual emotions.1,2 The lower one-third of the face significantly impacts beauty.1 An effective smile design must include aesthetic conditions for gingival health and appearance. The presence of an inflamed, irregular gingiva diminishes the value of a beautiful smile. Asymmetric gingival crests and a blunt papilla contribute negatively to the overall aesthetic appearance.3

A certain degree of gingival show in the smile is undoubtedly permissible and even aesthetically pleasing. The complete absence of a gingival display is less appealing. On the other hand, a smile can appear harsh and disagreeable if there is an excessive amount of gingival show.1,4

The extent of maxillary gingival display during a smile is regarded by both dentists and patients as a key factor in facial aesthetics and overall appearance; a categorisation method was created to help assess and classify smiles based on the amount of gingival tissue displayed. It was stated that a continuous strip of gingiva over the upper anterior teeth would be visible in a high smile. Only interdental papillae would be visible in a normal grin, and no gingival tissue would be visible in a low smile.2,5 In a nutshell, the replacement of lost maxillary teeth and their auxiliary constructs has always involved a smile assessment due to the significance of the amount of gingival show.1,2,5

Two distinct types of smiles were identified: one results when contracting both the orbicularis oculi muscles and the zygomatic major muscles, and the other occurs without involvement of the orbicularis oculi. Therefore, it was discovered that contracting the orbicularis oculi and expressions of delight and laughter hold an association.2,6,7 The zygomaticus major, which pulls the corners of the mouth up, and the orbicularis oculi, which wrinkles the outer corners of the eyes, are both contracted in a ‘genuine’ smile, also referred to as the ‘Duchenne’ smile. In contrast, a requested smile is a forced or courteous smile that mainly uses the zygomaticus major muscle, without the eye wrinkle.2,6, 7, 8

It is more than just smiles. Other facial expressions could also be useful in figuring out how much of the teeth and gingival tissue are exposed. Research has shown that the central part of the upper lip reaches its maximum extension during a scowl rather than a smile.2,9 Upper lip movement can result from a variety of facial expressions. The dentist must determine which expressions cause the most lip movement when examining the gingival show.2,10,11

Simply asking research participants to ‘give me your biggest smile’ produced the initial facial expression, a requested smile. Additionally, three more facial expressions were employed: a ‘Duchenne smile’, a severe ‘grimace of disgust’, and an experimental ‘funnel-shaped’ face.2 Functionally, ‘funnel-shaped’ expression drives the lips forward in a funnel-like shape by coordinating the contraction of the orbicularis oris, levator labii superioris, and zygomaticus muscles.1,2

Both patients and dentists regard the quantity of maxillary gingival tissue visible in a smile as a crucial component of facial beauty and appearance. This helps dentists establish guidelines and criteria for producing aesthetically beautiful dental restorations for replacing missing maxillary anterior teeth and supporting lost soft tissue structures. Moreover, we chose to compare and evaluate the maximum amount of gingival show above the maxillary front teeth among different facial expressions because, as of yet, no such study has been conducted based on the Pakistani population. Thus, our study aimed to quantify and compare the quantity of gingival show among various facial expressions.

Materials and methods

Study settings and ethical approval

This exploratory cross-sectional study was carried out at the Department of Prosthodontics, Islamic International Dental College and Hospital, Islamabad, Pakistan. All participants provided written informed consent before being enrolled in the study. The research received ethical clearance from the Institutional Ethical and Scientific Review Board (Ref. No.: IIDC/IRC/2024/011/007) on 30 November 2024, before the initiation of the research.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software for the case of Friedman’s ANOVA test. The pilot testing was done by taking 20 participants' pictures to calculate the effect size (f = 0.25), α=0.05, power of the test (1 - β)=0.95having one group with four measurements, the calculated sample size was (n = 36).

Descriptives of participants

A total of 36 young adults, including both genders, with healthy dentition who were between the ages of 18 and 25 years were chosen for the current study. Students, dental professionals, and patients who matched the eligibility of the inclusion criteria and were found in the specified age range and reported to the outpatient department of the prosthodontics department of Islamic International Dental Hospital were among the participants. Among the 36 participants, 16 were men, and 20 were women (Figure 1).

Descriptives of male and female participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

A participant was eligible to participate in this study if they met the following inclusion criteria: 18 to 25-year-old dentate subjects with well-aligned natural maxillary anterior teeth, a fully dentate arch devoid of anterior restorations, absence of interdental spaces, and no history of orthodontic treatment. Excluded from this study were those with periodontally compromised, worn, spaced, misaligned, mobile, or missing maxillary anterior teeth, those with any type of prosthesis or restorations in maxillary anterior teeth, individuals with a history of orthodontic treatment, and those with developmental abnormalities of teeth, cleft lip and palate, facial paralysis, and syndromes.

Head positioning and standardisation

The participants were instructed to sit up straight in the dentist’s chair with the occlusal plane of their maxillary teeth equally parallel to the surface of the floor. To standardise head posture across all participants, the Natural Head Position (NHP) was utilised. Participants were instructed to sit comfortably, look straight ahead, and focus on a fixed point at eye level, thereby adopting a self-balancing, reproducible posture that approximates the physiological position of the head in relation to the true horizontal and vertical. Visual confirmation ensured the Frankfort horizontal plane (From tragus of the ear to the infraorbital rim) was parallel to the floor.12

The use of NHP, as standardised by Broca in the 19th century and validated for photographic studies, provides a reliable substitute for a mechanical cephalostat. This method has been shown to yield a reproducible, standardised head orientation that is comparable to cephalometric radiographs and is essential for accurate soft tissue analysis.12

Instructions to produce different facial expressions

Four different facial expressions, including the ‘requested smile’, ‘Duchenne smile’, ‘grimace of disgust’, and ‘funnel-shaped’ expression, were thoroughly explained, showing reference pictures of the required expressions to each participant. The participants were given instructions on how to precisely create each of the facial expressions. Simply asking them to ‘give me your biggest smile’ produced the initial facial expression, a ‘requested smile’. The ‘Duchenne smile’ is a real smile involving the contraction of ocular muscles, while the funnel-shaped expression was explained as a pouty expression showing teeth. The term ‘funnel-shaped expression’ was explained as an expression in which the lips are pursed and protruded, and the upper lip edges are elevated, exposing the anterior teeth to make a funnel like shape.1,2

Photographic data collection

Four standardised photographs of each participant, including close-ups of four facial expressions (‘requested smile, ‘Duchenne smile’, ‘grimace of disgust’, and ‘funnel-shaped expression’), were captured (Figure 2). Resting the face and lips was advised to every individual between each expression change. All images were captured using a digital camera (Canon 200D). All photographs were taken with a standard lens (18-55 mm) and at a standard distance (close-up: 30 cm). A camera lens having standardised specifications was utilised – ‘Model: EF-S 18-55 mm f/3.5-5.6 IS III, Focal length 18-55 mm, maximum aperture f/3.5-5.6, minimum aperture 36’. According to the height of each subject’s face, the height of the camera was adjusted individually. To resize all the images within the software, an L-shaped scale was used as a reference and was held beside the face in all photographs.

An example of: A, requested smile, B, Duchenne smile, C, intense grimace of disgust, D, funnel-shaped expression.

AutoCAD software analysis

The images were then transferred to a computer (HP ProBook i7, Windows 11 Pro, 10th Gen) and opened and analysed in AutoCAD analysis software (AutoCAD 2020) one at a time. To ensure that accurate measurements were taken across all of the data, every photo was framed identically to the others within the software. Additionally, to minimise error, a scale ratio was taken for each image, which corresponded to the measurements taken digitally to accurately represent real-world values. The entire image analysis was completed by a single trained professional. Using AutoCAD software, the vertical measurements were made, measuring the distances from the lower edge of the maxillary lip to the gingival zenith point. The gingival zenith was visually established to be the most apical point of each tooth's gingival contour. The lower edge of the upper lip was marked at its most superior margin using the visible mucocutaneous junction. All landmarks were digitised manually using AutoCAD's precision measuring tools with 0.01 mm accuracy. These measurements were performed for maxillary central incisors, lateral incisors, canines and first premolar teeth of both sides in all photographs and for all facial expressions. To remove facial asymmetries as a potential confounding factor, the mean dimensions of the right and left tooth types were found before the investigation. If the gingival zenith of a tooth could not be identified, that tooth's measurement for the specified facial expression was marked as 0.00 mm (Figure 3).

Example of measurements of the gingival display above maxillary anterior teeth among four different facial expressions. A, requested smile B, Duchenne smile C, intense grimace of disgust D, funnel-shaped expression.

Intraobserver reliability assessment

To assess measurement consistency, intra-observer reliability was evaluated by selecting 10 random images from the sample of 36. A single trained examiner conducted both the independent measurement rounds for each tooth site and expression. To determine the consistency of the examiner's measurements, we determined the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). We employed two types: ICC1, which shows whether the examiner provides the same pattern of scoring, and ICC2, which shows whether the two measurements are almost identical in value. The intraobserver reliability was excellent. Across all dental sites and facial expressions, ICC1 and ICC2 values were consistently very high (≈0.998-1.000). The maxillary central incisors, our primary outcome, had both ICC1 and ICC2 values of 1.000 for the left and right sides in every expression, demonstrating flawless measurement reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS Version 27. The amount of gingival show across the four different facial expressions was compared utilising the Friedman test, a nonparametric alternative to repeated measures ANOVA, due to the non-normal distribution of the data. Post hoc analyses were conducted with the Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Discussion

A smile is a powerful nonverbal communication tool, a sign of civility, and a reliable indicator of face attractiveness. Prosthodontists value smiles as an essential component of treatment planning and the foundation of treatment goals. A smile is considered very crucial and has a significant impact on both facial expressions and physical appearance, as the mouth serves as the primary means of communication through the face.1,13

Typically, the gingival margins of the lateral incisors are positioned lower than those of the central incisors, while the central incisors' gingival margins are usually at the same height or slightly below those of the canines.1,14 Ideally, a smile is characterised by the top lip that reaches the gingival margins, curved upwards commissures, the top incisal line that follows the contour of the lower lip, minimal to no negative spaces, and a harmonious integration of dental and gingival aesthetics.1,15, 16, 17

Other facial expressions could also help determine the extent of the teeth and gingival tissue displayed. Many different facial expressions might cause upper lip movement.2,9, 10, 11 The dentist must discover which expressions create the most lip movement when analysing gingival show.

Many clinical studies utilise digitally captured photographs as a cost-effective and time-saving technique.18 In the current study, researchers used a Canon 200D digital camera with an 18 to 55 mm lens to take close-up images of all participants, which were altered individually based on the level of each subject's face while maintaining a constant distance and camera settings.

Numerous studies have proved the flexibility and versatility of digital imaging technologies, such as AutoCAD software.19 To examine the amount of gingival display in relation to various facial expressions, the current study made use of the powerful features of AutoCAD software. The use of AutoCAD software provided an objective, standardised, and reproducible method for quantifying gingival display, reducing observer-related bias and enabling precise measurement across dynamic facial expressions. This digital approach offers a feasible and cost-effective tool for clinical aesthetic analysis.

The null hypothesis proposes that there would be an insignificant difference in the amount of maxillary gingival show among the four facial expressions evaluated across the four maxillary tooth types. The findings of this research rejected the null hypothesis. The results demonstrated that the midfacial soft tissues exhibited greater vertical displacement during a ‘Duchenne smile’ compared to a grimace, a finding that contrasts with previously reported studies.2,9,10

The current study also found that a ‘Duchenne smile’ resulted in more gingival show than other facial expressions at the maxillary central and lateral incisors. These findings were in disagreement with the results from earlier studies.1,2,9,10 A similar trend was observed at the maxillary canine and first premolar, with the Duchenne smile showing the maximum gingiva.

While our findings differ from those of another study exploring the gingival show in different facial expressions.2 These changes could be due to population-specific soft tissue dynamics, variances in facial expression execution, or measuring technique variability. Previous research has focused on Western or East Asian cohorts, whereas ours is based on a Pakistani sample, which may exhibit distinct perioral morphological patterns. Furthermore, using AutoCAD for digital measuring may produce different precision than previous photographic grid-based investigations.

The present study's findings cast doubt on the classification scheme for high, moderate, and low smiles proposed in previous literature. According to the definition, a high smile has a continuous layer of gingiva, while an average smile simply displays only the interdental papillae.5 Participants in the present study tended to exhibit greater gingiva. The participant's mean gingival bandwidth at the maxillary central incisor was 0.86 ± 1.10 mm, at the lateral incisor 1.91 ± 1.55 mm, at the canine 1.49 ± 1.68 mm, and at the first premolar, 2.35 ± 1.96 mm when they were asked to produce a Duchenne smile.

Clinicians should be mindful of the average amount of gingiva displayed among different facial expressions when assessing gingival presentation. A dentofacial analysis that incorporates the above results will provide a more comprehensive understanding of how the gingiva of the dental patient serves as a link between the teeth and the face and will aid in creating an aesthetically pleasing smile. This study will contribute to the development of guidelines that allow prosthodontists to evaluate the aesthetic properties of a complete denture prosthesis when creating new denture prostheses or when a patient expresses dissatisfaction with the appearance of their existing prosthesis. This will also help dentists establish guidelines and criteria for producing aesthetically beautiful fixed dental restorations for replacing missing maxillary anterior teeth. Patients with unwarranted gingiva show benefit from it, and the dental technician will be able to add the required aesthetics to the prosthesis as it is being designed and made.

Limitations

As an exploratory clinical analysis with a limited sample size and single-centre design, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive. Moreover, AutoCAD measurements were conducted by a single examiner; therefore, inter‑observer reliability could not be assessed and should be evaluated in future multi‑examiner studies.

Conclusion

In comparison to the other expressions, the ‘Duchenne smile’ displayed a greater gingival display over all maxillary anterior teeth.
