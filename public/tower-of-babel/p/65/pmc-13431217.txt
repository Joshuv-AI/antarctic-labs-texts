Consensus and guidelines on myopia control in children by the Taiwan Myopia Disease Society
Authors: Jui-Yen Lin, Hung-Chi Chen, Pei-Chang Wu, Ken-Kuo Lin, Chih-Chien Hsu, Der-Chong Tsai, Chih-Ping Wang, Chun-Fu Liu, Han-Chih Cheng, Chau-Yin Chen, Yun-Wen Chen, Wei-Yu Chiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:Myopia is a major public health concern in East Asia. Despite available interventions, clinical variations persist in diagnosis and treatment. This study aimed to develop consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in Taiwanese children using a structured Delphi methodology.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A modified Delphi method was used to develop consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in children. Delphi statements were derived from a literature review and were evaluated by an expert panel of 12 ophthalmologists through a two-round anonymous rating process. Agreement was assessed using a five-point Likert scale, and consensus was defined a priori as ≥75% agreement. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize panel responses.RESULTS:Seventeen statements across seven predefined domains were evaluated using a two-round Delphi process. High levels of agreement were achieved for all statements. Key recommendations were as follows: cycloplegic refraction is essential, and premyopia (spherical equivalent > −0.50 D to ≤ +0.75 D) is recognized as a distinct condition requiring active monitoring; family history and environmental factors must be routinely assessed; increased outdoor time is the foundational strategy for all children; low-dose atropine (starting concentration ≤0.05%) and optical interventions are effective primary treatments. Combination therapy is recommended for patients with an inadequate response to single-modality treatment.CONCLUSION:Using a modified Delphi approach, we established comprehensive consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in children and adolescents in Taiwan. These recommendations reflect broad expert agreement and provide a structured framework to support clinical decision-making and future guideline development for the management of childhood myopia.

Introduction

Myopia has become a major public health issue worldwide, with particularly high prevalence in East Asia, including Taiwan.[1234] The increasing prevalence of myopia and high myopia in the pediatric population is alarming, especially because of the association between high myopia and irreversible, vision-threatening ocular pathologies.[35] Therefore, early detection of myopia and timely intervention to slow progression are essential components of pediatric eye care.[1]

Childhood myopia is predominantly axial in nature, and its development and progression are influenced by both genetic and environmental factors.[6] Environmental exposures, such as prolonged near work, insufficient outdoor activity, and intensive educational demands, are especially relevant in East Asian societies.[7] In Taiwan, after-school programs and tutoring environments further increase near-work exposure, contributing to early onset and rapidly progressive myopia in children.[8]

Over the past decade, several interventions have been shown to be effective in controlling myopia. Low-dose atropine, orthokeratology, myopia-control contact lenses, and spectacle lenses designed to induce peripheral myopic defocus have demonstrated efficacy in slowing myopia progression.[91011121314] Behavioral interventions, including increased outdoor time and reduction in continuous near work, are also widely recommended.[7] However, despite growing evidence, substantial variation remains in clinical practice regarding diagnosis, risk stratification, treatment initiation, and escalation strategies.

Currently, there are no standardized consensus-based guidelines that integrate these interventions into a unified clinical framework tailored to the Taiwanese context. The Delphi method provides a structured approach for developing expert consensus, particularly in areas where evidence is evolving and definitive clinical thresholds are lacking. Therefore, this study aimed to develop a set of consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in children and adolescents in Taiwan using a modified Delphi process.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This study employed a modified Delphi method to develop consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in children and adolescents in Taiwan. The Delphi technique is a structured, iterative process designed to synthesize expert opinions through anonymous rating and controlled feedback and is particularly suitable for areas in which evidence is evolving, and clinical practice varies. Development of Delphi statements

The initial Delphi statements were developed by the study investigators based on a structured review of the literature and relevant professional recommendations. The literature review was conducted by members of the research team, who searched PubMed/MEDLINE and Embase for English-language publications related to childhood myopia and myopia control published from January 2015 to December 2025. The search focused on major domains, including the definition and diagnosis of myopia and premyopia, risk factors, screening and follow-up strategies, behavioral interventions, pharmacological treatment, optical interventions, and combination therapy, as shown in Table 1. The draft statements were reviewed and refined through preliminary discussions among the study investigators to ensure clinical relevance and clarity before the Delphi rounds.

Expert panel

From 2024 to 2025, an expert panel of 12 ophthalmologists with more than 10 years of clinical experience in childhood myopia and myopia control was invited to review the updated literature of myopia prevention and control for the consensus. To ensure diversity of clinical perspectives, the panel included three retinal specialists, four clinicians with expertise in clinical refraction, two pediatric ophthalmologists, two corneal specialists, and one glaucoma specialist. Panelists were selected from a range of clinical settings, including tertiary medical centers, regional hospitals, and private practice. After eight review meetings, they were invited to participate in the Delphi process in the beginning of 2026. Participation was voluntary, and responses were collected anonymously to minimize potential bias and the influence of dominant opinions.

Delphi process

The Delphi process consisted of two sequential rounds. In Round 1, the panelists independently rated their level of agreement with each statement using a five-point Likert scale (strongly disagree, disagree, neutral, agree, and strongly agree). In addition, panelists were invited to provide free-text comments and suggestions for statement modification after each item. Because all invited panelists were ophthalmologists with expertise in myopia control, no additional structured response options, such as “not within my area of expertise” or “prefer not to answer,” were provided.

Following Round 1, statements that did not reach the predefined consensus threshold, as well as those that reached consensus but received substantive suggestions, were reviewed by the research team. Revisions were made based on aggregated quantitative results and qualitative feedback from the panelists. The revised statements were then redistributed for re-evaluation in Round 2.

All 12 invited panelists completed both Delphi rounds, corresponding to a response rate of 100% for each round, and all statements were rated by all panelists.

Definition of consensus

Consensus was defined a priori as agreement (agree or strongly agree) by at least 75% of the panelists. Statements that reached consensus after either round were accepted as consensus recommendations.

Ethical approval

This study was performed in line with the Declaration of Helsinki and institutional ethical guidance. The Institutional Review Board of New Taipei Municipal TuCheng Hospital (Built and Operated by Chang Gung Medical Foundation) was consulted regarding the need for ethics review and concluded that formal approval was not necessary, as the study consisted of a noninterventional Delphi consensus procedure and did not involve patient recruitment or the use of human subject data. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating panelists prior to both Delphi rounds.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the level of agreement with each statement in each Delphi round. The proportion of panelists who selected each response category was calculated. No inferential statistical analyses were performed, as the primary objective was to establish an expert consensus.

Results

A modified Delphi process was used to establish consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in children and adolescents. Seventeen statements across seven predefined domains were evaluated in two Delphi rounds.

Round 1 results

In Round 1, consensus was reached for all statements. High levels of agreement were observed for statements related to the definition and diagnosis of myopia (Statements 1–3), screening and follow-up strategies (Statements 4 and 5), risk assessment (Statements 6 and 7), behavioral interventions (Statements 8 and 9), pharmacological treatment (Statements 10–13), optical interventions (Statement 14), and integrated strategies (Statement 15). These statements reached the predefined consensus threshold of 75% agreement without substantial modifications.

Following the expert panel discussion, several statements were refined to improve semantic clarity and provide more explicit definitions. Specifically, Statement 3 was revised to include a numerical definition of premyopia (> −0.50 D and ≤ +0.75 D); Statement 10 was clarified by specifying the concentration of low-dose atropine; Statement 13 was revised with respect to wording and the threshold for myopia progression; and Statement 14 was refined to clarify terminology related to optical interventions. In addition to these revisions, two new statements (10a and 13a) were introduced based on expert feedback, and the revised and newly added statements were evaluated in the second round of voting [Table 2]. Round 2 was conducted to reassess the revised statements and evaluate the newly added statements.

Final consensus recommendations

The expert panel established a cohesive clinical framework summarized as follows:

Definition and diagnosis

The consensus strictly defines myopia as a spherical equivalent ≤ −0.50 D under cycloplegia. Notably, although premyopia was defined in this consensus as a spherical equivalent refractive error of > −0.50 D and ≤ +0.75 D, this threshold should be interpreted in the context of age-specific refractive development. Hyperopic reserve is age-dependent, and a lower-than-expected hyperopic reserve for age may offer additional prognostic value when identifying children at increased risk of future myopia onset. Therefore, the proposed threshold is intended as a practical clinical framework rather than a standalone marker and should be interpreted together with age and other risk factors.

Screening and follow-up

The panel reached 100% agreement that school-based visual acuity screening alone was insufficient for detecting premyopia or early myopia. Consequently, regular cycloplegic refraction is strongly recommended, particularly for high-risk children.

Risk assessment

Clinicians are advised to routinely assess high-risk indicators, including early onset age, rapid progression history, high myopia in parents, and intensive near-work environments, such as after-school programs.

Behavioral foundation

Regardless of refractive status, increasing outdoor time and implementing structured near-work breaks have been established as the foundation of myopia management.

Therapeutic interventions

Pharmacological

Low-dose atropine is the core treatment, with ≤0.05% recommended as the preferred initial concentration in most patients. While the efficacy of 0.01% has been debated, the consensus is that it remains an appropriate option for selected patients, such as older children with slower progression or those receiving combination therapy.

The panel explicitly agreed that tropicamide is not recommended for myopia control, as it lacks sufficient evidence for long-term efficacy compared with that of atropine.

Optical

Orthokeratology and myopia control using spectacle or soft contact lenses have been endorsed as effective alternatives.

Integrated care

For rapid progressors (myopia progression exceeding 0.50 D per 6 months), the consensus supports treatment escalation or combination therapy (including atropine plus optical methods).

Discussion

This study employed a modified Delphi methodology to develop consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in Taiwanese children and adolescents. By integrating current evidence with expert clinical judgment, the consensus addressed key areas of uncertainty across the diagnostic, preventive, and therapeutic continuum of childhood myopia management. Importantly, this framework reflects international evidence and real-world clinical considerations specific to the Taiwanese context.

A major contribution of this consensus is its emphasis on early identification and risk stratification. Recognizing premyopia as a distinct clinical condition underscores the importance of preventive strategies before the onset of myopia.[26] This approach aligns with emerging international perspectives and is particularly relevant in Taiwan, where early-onset myopia and its rapid progression are common. Incorporating environmental risk factors, such as prolonged near work in after-school or tutoring environments, further grounds recommendations in local lifestyle and educational practices.[781516]

Behavioral interventions have been consistently endorsed as a foundational component of myopia control. Increasing outdoor time and reducing continuous near work are broadly applicable, low-risk strategies that complement pharmacological and optical treatments.[71718] Strong agreement with these statements reflects a shared expert view that lifestyle modifications and parental education should form the basis of myopia management regardless of whether additional interventions are initiated.

Pharmacological treatment with low-dose atropine remains the cornerstone of myopia control in Taiwan, a position that is reinforced by clinical evidence and systemic healthcare factors. Its widespread adoption is rooted in decades of clinical familiarity among Taiwanese ophthalmologists and, crucially, its status as the sole myopia control intervention currently reimbursed by the National Health Insurance. Although consensus has been reached regarding its overall effectiveness and safety, expert discussions have highlighted the ongoing clinical debate regarding the choice of initial concentration and escalation strategies. In particular, while the efficacy of 0.01% atropine has been questioned in some studies, panelists agree that this concentration may still be appropriate for selected patients, such as older children with slower progression or those receiving combination therapy.[91019202122] This perspective emphasizes the importance of individualized treatment decisions rather than reliance on a single concentration threshold.

Monitoring and follow-up strategies during atropine treatment were also addressed. Although some experts advocate the use of axial length as a primary outcome measure, the absence of a universally accepted threshold for axial length progression limits its use as a sole criterion in consensus statements.[121323] Consequently, refractive error progression was retained as the primary indicator of treatment adjustment, while axial length measurement was acknowledged as a valuable adjunct for longitudinal monitoring. This approach balances clinical feasibility with evolving evidence.

Optical interventions, including orthokeratology, myopia-controlled contact lenses, and specialized spectacle lenses, have been recognized as effective options for slowing myopia progression when appropriately prescribed and monitored. The consensus further supports the consideration of combination therapy for children with an inadequate response to single-modality treatment, while emphasizing the need to account for treatment burden, adherence, cost, and safety.

Emerging therapies, such as repeated low-level red-light therapy,[24] were evaluated during expert deliberations but were ultimately excluded from formal consensus statements. Although red-light devices have received regulatory approval for myopia control in several countries, they have not yet been approved by the Taiwan Food and Drug Administration. In the absence of local regulatory approval and widespread clinical adoption, the panel agreed that these modalities should not be included in the current recommendations, reflecting a cautious and regulation-informed approach.

This study has several limitations. As a consensus-based approach, the Delphi method reflects expert opinions rather than direct evidence from randomized controlled trials, and recommendations may be influenced by the composition of the expert panel. Nevertheless, the Delphi methodology provides a transparent and systematic means of synthesizing expert perspectives in areas where evidence is evolving or clinical thresholds remain uncertain.

Conclusion

Using a modified Delphi approach, we developed consensus-based clinical recommendations for myopia control in Taiwanese children and adolescents. By integrating current evidence with expert clinical perspectives, the consensus provides a structured and context-specific framework to support evidence-based clinical decision-making in childhood myopia care.

These recommendations emphasize early risk identification, the foundational role of behavioral interventions, and the importance of individualized treatment strategies. While pharmacological and optical therapies play key roles in slowing myopia progression, their application should be tailored to patient characteristics, progression patterns, and clinical context. The areas of ongoing clinical debate identified through the consensus process highlight the need for continued research as new evidence and therapeutic options emerge.

Overall, this consensus serves as a practical reference for clinicians in Taiwan and may inform future guideline development and public health strategies for childhood myopia management.
