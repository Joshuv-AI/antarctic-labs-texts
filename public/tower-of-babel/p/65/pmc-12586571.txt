Network construction using sparse Gaussian graphical model based on GWAS summary statistics
Authors: Megh Subedi, Xuewei Cao, Byung-Jun Kim, Qiuying Sha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn genome-wide association studies (GWAS), thousands of genetic variants are tested to identify their associations with a phenotype. GWAS have identified many strongly associated genetic variants with phenotypes and have greatly enhanced our understanding of the genetic architecture of complex phenotypes and diseases. Joint analysis of multiple phenotypes can increase the overall statistical power to detect genetic associations and allows for the identification of pleiotropic loci. A phenotype-phenotype network (PPN) represents phenotypes as nodes and the relationships between them as edges, which allows for the visualization of complex relationships between phenotypes, making it easier to identify clusters and helps us intuitively grasp how different phenotypes are related. In this study, we propose a new method to construct a PPN using the sparse Gaussian Graphical Model (sGGM) based on GWAS summary statistics. This approach isolates the direct relationship between phenotypes, making it easier to identify clusters of phenotypes conditional on other phenotypes that reflect more meaningful biological or functional connections. We then applied a community detection method to partition phenotypes into disjoint modules based on the partial correlation matrix of phenotypes. For each module, various multiple phenotype association tests can be employed to test the association between a SNP and phenotypes in that module. We conducted a comprehensive simulation study to compare the performance of several multiple phenotype association tests using the network modules obtained from sGGM, the correlation matrix, as well as using all phenotypes without modular segmentation. The simulation results demonstrated that most of the multiple phenotype association tests based on network modules from sGGM not only effectively control the Type I error rate but also exhibit higher power compared to network modules derived from the correlation matrix and the association tests on all phenotypes without modular segmentation. We applied this method to the GWAS summary statistics of 92 phenotypes derived from Chapter IX (Diseases of the circulatory system) of ICD-10 codes in the UK Biobank. Introduction

In genome-wide association studies (GWAS), thousands of genetic variants are tested to identify their associations with phenotypes. This approach has identified many strongly associated variants for various traits and diseases. The most commonly used genetic variants in the GWAS are single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); however, other genetic variants such as copy number variants (CNVs)1 or short tandem repeats (STRs)2 are also used. The first GWAS was conducted in the year 2005 on Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)3, and thousands of GWAS have been conducted since then (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/downloads/summary-statistics).

GWAS usually examine one phenotype at a time. However, some genetic variants may be associated with multiple phenotypes, a phenomenon known as pleiotropy. An examination of genome-wide significant SNPs showed that 4.6% of these SNPs and 16.9% of these genes are associated with multiple correlated phenotypes, which indicates probable biological pleiotropy4. Most complex diseases’ genetic architecture is highly polygenic4–6. There has been an increasing interest in testing the association of a single genetic variant with multiple phenotypes. Joint analysis of the associations from different studies allows us to inspect the cross-phenotype associations7,8. The measurements of disease-related phenotypes are generally taken simultaneously as disorders or risk factors of a complex disease. Combining these correlated phenotypes aggregates the effects of multiple genetic variants on complex diseases and can increase the statistical power to identify associated genetic variants.

Numerous statistical methods have recently been developed to assess the relationship between a SNP and multiple correlated phenotypes. These multiple phenotype association tests can be broadly classified into three main categories. The first category of tests combines the test statistics or p-values from the univariate tests, such as the O’Brien method9, adaptive Fisher’s Combination (AFC)10, the adaptive sum of powered score test (aSPU)11, and Trait-based Association Test that uses Extended Simes procedure (TATES)12. The second category of methods includes multivariate analysis based on regression approaches, such as Multivariate Analysis of Variance (MANOVA)13, reverse regression method (MultiPhen)14, Linear Mixed Models (LMM)15, and Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE)16. The third category of methods consists of dimension reduction methods, such as clustering linear combinations (CLC)17, canonical correlation analysis (CCA)18, principal components of traits (PCT)19, and principal components of heritability (PCH)20–22. The vast majority of the human complex traits and diseases are influenced by many genetic variants that work together to modify cellular functions23. Different research studies have demonstrated that most of the trait-associated genetic variants are predominant in regulatory elements24,25. Network analysis offers a comprehensive approach to understanding complex genomic associations26 and will help identify genetic variants and genes that impact cellular processes and give rise to phenotypes27,28.

Exploring network-based approaches in the study of human disease facilitates the identification of key genes, pathways, and biomarkers, which offer potential targets for drug development26. Biological networks exhibit a high degree of modularity, wherein closely linked genes form cohesive groups known as communities, modules, or clusters, sharing similar biological functions29. Therefore, community detection methods are crucial in identifying these community structures. Many community detection methods have been developed and applied across various domains, from social networks to human disease networks30–32.

A Phenotype-Phenotype Network (PPN) is a graphical representation in which nodes represent different phenotypes and edges represent the association or relationship between phenotypes. This network structure is used to explore and understand the complex relationships among multiple traits, particularly in the context of genetic association studies. Community detection within PPNs is crucial for identifying groups of phenotypes that share common genetic or biological bases. This helps to discover pleiotropic effects and improve the statistical power of multiple phenotype association tests, revealing how a single genetic factor contributes to multiple traits and diseases.

A PPN can be established by considering the correlations between phenotypes, including those mediated by intermediate variables. Emphasizing the direct relationships between phenotypes is vital, as it removes the influence of intermediate variables and ensures that the network accurately reflects the true associations, leading to more precise insights into genetic and biological mechanisms. Identifying direct associations between phenotypes in the PPN is crucial because a traditional correlation-based PPN can yield misleading results if an intermediate phenotype confounds the relationships33. By focusing on direct associations, the network more accurately captures the real interactions between phenotypes, avoiding the distortions caused by indirect influences.

Materials and methods

Overview of methods

Clustering is a widely used technique in data analysis that aims to group similar objects. When applied to multiple phenotypes, clustering can identify groups of phenotypes exhibiting similar properties. One common approach to clustering is based on the correlation matrix, which captures the pairwise linear association between phenotypes. The correlation matrix reveals associations, enabling the identification of clusters based on correlation strength and direction. Using a correlation matrix for clustering enables us to uncover the underlying structure of the data, leading to insights into the relationships and potential interactions between the phenotypes. However, there are different possible ways in which correlation can exist between variables, even though they do not directly relate to each other (see Figure A1). Incorporating indirect correlations in clustering methods can significantly impact the accuracy and interpretability of the resulting clusters. For example, phenotypes that appear correlated due to mediating variables might be clustered together even though their direct relationship is weak or non-existent. By controlling for these indirect correlations, clustering methods can more accurately reveal the true structure of the data, leading to clusters that are more meaningful and reliable for analysis and decision-making. The partial correlation coefficient removes the influence of indirect associations, enabling us to uncover the direct associations. This leads to more accurate and meaningful insights into the true relationships between the phenotypes. Figure A2 illustrates the comparative cluster structures obtained from the correlation (Figure A2(b)) and partial correlation matrix (Figure A2(c)). The clustering method based on the correlation matrix groups phenotypes based on their pairwise correlations, regardless of the specific correlation path among them. However, clustering based on the partial correlation matrix offers the advantage of isolating the direct relationship between phenotypes by controlling for the influence of other variables, resulting in smaller but meaningful clusters. Figure 1(a) exhibits the network structure obtained from the correlation matrix, and Fig. 1(b) shows the network structure obtained from the partial correlation matrix. The non-zero elements in both the correlation matrix and partial correlation matrix represent edges between the variables in the network. From Fig. 1(b), it can be observed that indirect associations are eliminated in the partial correlation matrix.

Power

We consider varying numbers of phenotypes and different simulation models for power comparison. We consider 100 causal SNPs for models 1,2, and 3, and 200 causal SNPs for model 4, with a sample size of 4,000 and a total of M = 10,000 SNPs. The power in each simulation model is evaluated using 10 MC runs. The number of replications is equivalent to 1,000 for models 1–3 and 2,000 for model 4. In each MC run, the power is assessed at a Bonferroni-corrected significance level of 0.05. Accordingly, the Bonferroni-corrected significance level is for models 1, 2, and 3, and for model 4. Figure 2 and supplementary Figures A4–A5 show the power comparison of the five multiple phenotype association tests (ACAT, Wald, PCFisher, HCLC, and O’Brien) under four different models for various effect sizes and various numbers of phenotypes. From Fig. 2, we can see that the power of ACAT is almost the same in all four models. Additionally, we observed that Wald, PCFisher, and HCLC exhibit higher power when utilizing network modules obtained from sGGM, compared to network modules from the correlation matrix or analysis that do not incorporate network modules. We also observed that O’Brien’s method shows slight decreases in power when utilizing network modules derived from sGGM, compared to those derived from the correlation matrix in model 2. However, across the remaining three models, O’Brien consistently exhibited higher power when using network modules obtained from sGGM compared to those based on the correlation matrix or when network modules were not considered. A similar power pattern was observed when the number of phenotypes was 60 and 80 (see Figure A4 and Figure A5).

Real data application

The UK Biobank project is a major prospective cohort study with around 500,000 participants across the United Kingdom, aged between 40 and 69 at the time of recruitment, and it contains a wide range of genetic and phenotypic information46. The UK Biobank studies aim to improve the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of a wide range of severe and life-threatening illnesses like cancer and heart disease. The project seeks to understand the genetic, environmental, and lifestyle factors contributing to various diseases by collecting and analyzing health data from a large and diverse group of participants47. We considered 92 phenotypes associated with circulatory system diseases from Chapter IX (Diseases of the circulatory system) of ICD-10 codes in the UK Biobank. The ICD offers a globally standardized framework for categorizing and coding diseases, disorders, injuries, and various health conditions. Table A4 lists the corresponding ICD-10 (International classification of Diseases,10th revision) codes of the different diseases of the circulatory system included in our analysis. Following the similar data preprocessing steps described in39,48 the final analysis utilizes 322,607 individuals, 288,647 variants, and 92 phenotypes.

We applied five multiple phenotype association tests (ACAT, Wald, PCFisher, HCLC, and O’Brien) to evaluate the association between these 92 phenotypes and each of the 288,647 SNPs. We used commonly accepted genome-wide significance level 5 × 10− 8. We first applied these five tests without considering the network modules. We then applied the community detection method to partition these 92 phenotypes into disjoint network modules based on the correlation matrix and sGGM. Next, we applied the same five multiple phenotype association tests using the network modules derived from the correlation matrix and sGGM.

Table A5 presents 15 network modules obtained based on the correlation matrix, with each module containing 1 to 17 phenotypes. Conversely, Table A6 shows 24 disjoint network modules obtained from sGGM, each containing 1 to 8 phenotypes. We observed that network modules obtained from the correlation matrix and sGGM are inconsistent with the ICD-10-based categories (https://biobank.ctsu.ox.ac.uk/showcase/field.cgi?id=41202) as the latter are constructed based on the underlying cause of death rather than the genetic architecture of the diseases. Since the true underlying cluster structure of these 92 phenotypes is unknown, we compared the similarity of these modules obtained from the correlation and partial correlation matrix using Normalized Mutual Information Criteria (NMI)49 .The NMI value obtained from these two partitions is 0.728 which shows that these two partitions are substantially in agreement with each other. The clusters based on the partial correlation matrix are sparser compared to the clusters from the correlation matrix. Table A5 and Table A6 illustrate that traits originally grouped together in a single correlation-based cluster are often redistributed across multiple clusters in the partial correlation. For example, the cluster number II and XIV from the partial correlation matrix are subclusters of cluster II from the correlation matrix. Similarly, correlation cluster IV, which has large group of 17 traits, has split into 8 distinct clusters: III, IV, V, VI, VII, VIII, IX, and XVI in partial correlation. Each of these clusters from the partial correlation matrix contains a subset of traits from cluster IV. These examples highlight how partial correlation clusters reveal a more detailed structure within the broader clusters identified by the correlation matrix.

Direct functional follow-up of significant SNPs

While pathways and functional enrichment analysis provide valuable insights into broader biological processes, it is equally important to assess the functional impact of individual variants. We performed a direct functional follow-up analysis on those independent significant SNPs using multi-faceted variant functional annotation from the FAVOR database (available at https://favor.genohub.org/)55. FAVOR uses comprehensive annotations derived from different resources across 13 major attributes: Basic, Variant Category, Allele Frequencies, Integrative Score, Protein Function, Epigenetics, Conservation, Transcription Factors, Chromatin States, Local Nucleotide Diversity, Mutation Density, Mappability, and Proximity. The detailed annotation results of those SNPs are available in the Supplementary Data Excel file (Sheet Favor Annotation Result).

Given the majority of the significant SNPs were located in the noncoding regions (84 non-coding, 6 coding (Supplementary Data Excel file (Sheet Favor Annotation Result)), we seek to assess their potential functional roles using annotation principal components (aPCs), which integrates diverse annotations into a unified measure of SNP functionality. We used nine annotation principal components Apc-ConservationV2, Apc-EpigeneticsActive, Apc-Epigenetics-Repressed, Apc-Epigenetics-Transcription, Apc-LocalNucleotideDiversityV3, Apc-Mappability, Apc-MutationDensity, Apc-ProteinFunctionV3, and Apc-TranscriptionFactor. These scores are expressed as Phred -scaled values, defined as ), where M is the number of variants in the FAVOR database. A higher Phred value indicates increased functionality of the annotations. We selected SNPs with at least one Phred-scaled annotation principal component (aPC) score exceeding 20, corresponding to the top 1% of all variants in the FAVOR database55.

Figure 5(c) shows the heatmap of the 20 SNPs belong to top 1% of all variants in the FAVOR database indicating strong biological relevance. Several SNPs exhibit high scores across multiple aPCs indicating their potential functional roles through diverse regulatory mechanisms. For example, SNP rs57374020 exhibits high Phred-scaled score across four aPCs: ApcTranscriptionFactor, ApcMappability, ApcEpigeneticsTranscription, and ApcEpigeneticsActive. This finding suggests that SNP rs57374020 may regulate gene expression by altering transcription factor binding, modulating enhancer activity, or influencing transcriptional processes. To further evaluate the potential functional roles of significant SNPs, we did an enrichment analysis of chromatin states leveraging the chromatin state annotations derived from the ChromHMM 25-state model across 48 tissues56 from the FAVOR database. Detailed descriptions of these annotations are available at https://docs.genohub.org/data. Figure A15 represents the heatmap showing the number of tissues each SNP overlaps out of 48 tissues across different chromatin states. Lighter colors in the heatmap represent higher tissue overlap implying broader activity of the chromatin contexts for the SNP.

For enrichment analysis, we converted each chromatin state column into a binary indicator by assigning a value of 1 if a SNP overlapped that state in at least one of the 48 tissues, and 0 otherwise. We compared how often each chromatin state was observed in the independent significant SNPs versus the remaining SNPs. For each chromatin state, we used Fisher’s exact test to evaluate the difference in the proportion of the SNPs overlapping that state between independent significant and non-significant groups and applied the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate at 5%. The detailed enrichment result is provided in Table A10. The chromatin state category was considered significantly enriched if the false discovery rate is less than 0.05. Out of 25 chromatin states, 12 states were significantly enriched. Significantly enriched chromatin states are displayed in Fig. 5(d).

Figure A16 represents the correlation heatmap of epigenetics, chromatin states, local nucleotide diversity and integrative functional annotations. The strong correlation pattern observed across these annotations highlights their interdependence, reflecting shared biological mechanisms such as co-regulation of gene expression, chromatin accessibility and sequence conservation. These relationships suggest that the SNPs with consistently high values across multiple correlated annotations are more likely to be functionally important.

Discussion

Statistical methods for jointly analyzing multiple phenotypes have become indispensable tools for increasing the statistical power to detect the significant associations between multiple phenotypes and SNPs. Analyzing multiple related phenotypes simultaneously can detect SNPs that influence more than one phenotype, called pleiotropic SNPs. Phenotypic correlation refers to the statistical relationship between two or more phenotypes in a population, measuring the degree to which variation in one phenotype is associated with variation in another phenotype. When individual level phenotype data are available, phenotypic correlation is estimated using the Pearson correlation coefficient across multiple phenotypes, which can subsequently be used to construct a PPN. A PPN visualizes phenotypes as nodes and their interrelationships as edges. This structure facilitates the visualization of complex relationships among phenotypes and enhances the identification of clusters and helps researchers intuitively understand how different phenotypes are interconnected. One drawback of constructing networks based on correlation coefficients is that they may capture indirect correlations, including connections that do not accurately represent direct biological relationships. The partial correlation coefficient, which measures the direct relationship between two phenotypes while conditioning on the other phenotypes, offers an alternative approach for constructing PPN that addresses the limitation of correlation-based networks. Direct associations between phenotypes allow the network to more accurately illustrate true interactions between phenotypes, effectively avoiding distortions caused by indirect influences.

Given the challenges associated with accessing individual-level data from the studies, developing statistical methods that leverage GWAS summary statistics has become increasingly important. In this study, we proposed a novel strategy, sGGM, to construct a PPN based on GWAS summary statistics. We utilized a robust community detection method to identify network modules and subsequently applied multiple phenotype association tests using these modules. Through simulation studies and real data analysis using UK Biobank chapter IX summary statistics, we demonstrated that sGGM is a valid and powerful approach for multiple phenotype tests. Multiple phenotype test methods based on sGGM-derived network modules demonstrated higher power than those based on the network modules derived from the correlation matrix and those without considering network modules. We evaluated the performance of the various multiple phenotype association tests based on network modules obtained from sGGM through a set of 92 EHR-derived phenotypes in the disease of the circulatory system across 322,607 samples from UK Biobank. The multiple phenotype association test utilizing network modules obtained from “sGGM” detected more significant SNPs than those obtained from the correlation matrix or those without considering network modules. Further analysis of the significant SNPs and the genes associated with those SNPs has revealed enriched biological and functional significance.

Recent advances in next generation sequencing technologies and the decreasing cost of whole genome/exome sequencing have enabled the study of rare genetic variants underlying complex human traits. There is growing interest in identifying pleiotropic rare variants in whole-genome sequencing studies. Leveraging the correlation structure among multiple traits enhances the power of association tests to detect pleiotropic genes over single-trait analyses57. The proposed method sGGM can be extended to the rare variant set-based test like Meta-Tow-S58 and Multi-STAAR59 by partitioning multiple traits into distinct clusters based on their correlation structure. Association tests are then performed within each cluster, and the resulting p-values are combined to obtain a single p-value for association. However, the performance of methods incorporating sGGM approach need further investigation.

Construction of PPN using sGGM requires only GWAS summary statistics. The determination of network modules is independent of the multiple phenotype association tests and is performed only once during real data analysis. This procedure can be efficiently implemented using GGMncv R package (available at https://github.com/donaldRwilliams/GGMncv). One key limitation of this study is that both simulations and real data analysis using the UK Biobank were restricted to complete sample overlap, where all the phenotypes were measured on the same set of individuals. In future work, we aim to extend our methodology to accommodate scenarios involving partially overlapping and non-overlapping samples to broaden the applicability of our method.
