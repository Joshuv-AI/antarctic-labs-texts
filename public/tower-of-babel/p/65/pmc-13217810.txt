Water-soluble yeast β-glucan accelerates oral ulcer healing in rats via collagen synthesis and epithelial regeneration
Authors: Yeting Tu, Hua Huang, Mengyun Zhang, Xiaomin Yu, Zixue Lei, Zhong Zhang, Xingyan Tan, Xiaorui Zhang, Chongyun Bao, Yang Yao, Yuqing Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral ulcer is a common, painful lesion that heals slowly and easily becomes secondary-infected. β-glucan, a well-known immunomodulator, has shown potential in promoting wound repair. However, the therapeutic efficacy of its water-soluble form on oral mucosal wounds remains insufficiently studied. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the effects of water-soluble yeast β-glucan on the healing of oral ulcers in a rat model.MethodsForty rats were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 8): (1) negative control (2), standard care control (0.12% chlorhexidine), and (3–5) experimental groups treated with 0.1%, 0.5%, or 1.0% water-soluble yeast β-glucan, respectively. Oral ulcers were chemically induced, and lesion size and clinical status were monitored daily. Histopathological changes were assessed by hematoxylin–eosin, Masson trichrome, and immunohistochemical staining. Data were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance using GraphPad Prism 10.0; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.ResultsOn day 6, water-soluble yeast β-glucan- and chlorhexidine-treated rats exhibited significantly smaller ulcer areas, faster healing rates, complete epithelialization, and improved histological scores compared with the negative controls (P < 0.05). Water-soluble yeast β-glucan treatment also increased collagen deposition (P < 0.05). Moreover, water-soluble yeast β-glucan-treated animals showed significantly greater weight gain than the negative controls (P < 0.0001).ConclusionsThis study establishes the beneficial effect of water-soluble yeast β-glucan on oral mucosal repair and provides a clear rationale for its clinical evaluation in human oral ulcers.

Introduction

Oral ulceration is a common mucosal condition with multifactorial origins, including infection, trauma, dental procedures, medications, cancer therapies, dehydration, systemic disorders, and autoimmune diseases. The resulting pain impairs eating, speaking, and swallowing, significantly diminishing quality of life. Moreover, as chemo- and radiotherapy have become primary treatments for head and neck cancers, oral ulcers have emerged as one of the most frequent acute toxicities of oncological therapy [1, 2].

Topical pharmacotherapy continues to constitute the primary strategy for managing oral ulceration, with the objectives of accelerating epithelial closure, alleviating pain, and extending the interval between recurrences [3]. Current pharmacological options include topical corticosteroids, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, immunosuppressants, and local anesthetics. However, adverse effects such as mucosal atrophy, systemic absorption, or secondary candidiasis limit their long-term use, necessitating safer alternatives [4]. Hence, attention has turned to natural products with favorable safety profiles.

β-glucan is a glucose polymer present in fungal cell walls, oats and barley, and has attracted interest for its immunomodulatory and anti-infective activities [5]. Together with mannan (a yeast cell-wall mannose polymer), it modulates innate and adaptive immunity by engaging pattern-recognition receptors on macrophages and dendritic cells [6]. The immunomodulatory potential of polysaccharides was first reported 80 years ago, when Shear and Turner isolated a polysaccharide from the “G.W.” strain of Serratia marcescens that induced tumor necrosis [7]. Subsequent studies confirmed that yeast β-glucan likewise activates macrophages [8].

In vivo evidence extends these findings: glucan administration reduced neutrophil infiltration and bacterial burden in a murine pneumonia model [9], and orally delivered soluble β-glucan attenuated ligature-induced periodontal bone loss. This attenuation was achieved by reducing proinflammatory cytokines (including TNF-α and IL-1β) and inhibiting oxidative stress, thereby suppressing the recruitment of inflammatory cells to the periodontium [10]. Notably, yeast β-glucan has the potential for application in oral health care, including maintaining oral cleanliness and moisture, preventing infections and inflammation, alleviating halitosis and tartar buildup, enhancing patient comfort, and preserving healthy oral function [11–13]. However, its direct role in ulcer healing remains to be established. Therefore, the present study evaluates the therapeutic efficacy of water-soluble yeast β-glucan in a rat model of oral ulceration.

Methods

Animals and reagents

Male SPF Sprague-Dawley rats (6–8 weeks, 180–220 g) were purchased from Chengdu Dashuo Experimental Animal Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). After a 5-day acclimation under controlled temperature (22 ± 2 °C), humidity (40–70%) and a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, all procedures were approved by the Institutional Review Board of West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-D-2021-428, Sichuan University, China) and conducted in accordance with the Chinese National Laboratory Animal Guidelines for Ethical Review of Animal Welfare.

Water-soluble yeast β-glucan (SG90, 500 kDa mean MW) was provided by Angel Yeast Co. (Hubei, China) and dissolved in sterile saline to obtain 0.1%, 0.5% and 1.0% (w/v) solutions on the day of use. A commercial 0.12% chlorhexidine mouth-rinse (MedChemExpress, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) was used as supplied.

Oral ulcer induction

Rats (n = 40) were weighed and randomly allocated to five groups (n = 8 each). Rat oral ulcer models were established as the reported method [14]. Animals were ventrally anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 0.2 mL per 100 g) before a 6-mm-diameter filter paper disc saturated with 50% glacial acetic acid (analytical grade, Sinopharm) was applied to the dried buccal mucosa of both sides for 30s to create symmetrical circular lesions. Twenty-four hours later, the presence of a white fibrin-covered crater surrounded by an erythematous halo confirmed successful modeling.

Drug administration

The modeling process was initiated on day 0 (DAY 0), whereas ulcer formation was designated as day 1 (DAY 1). Each rat received two treatment sessions daily. Each session consisted of three sprays applied to the ulcer surface, with each spray delivering 50 µL of solution. Between the two daily sessions a 30-min interval was observed, and food and water were withheld for 1 h post-application. Treatments continued until complete macroscopic epithelial closure was observed.

Clinical observation and sample collection

Rats were briefly anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD Life Science), and the ulcer area was measured daily with a digital vernier caliper (precision 0.01 mm) under standardized photography until complete re-epithelialization was observed. Body weight was recorded concurrently. At study termination, the animals were generally anesthetized via isoflurane to guarantee that all the animals were painless and euthanized. Well-trained veterinary staff performed cervical dislocation in the animal facility located in the West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University. The full-thickness buccal mucosal specimens containing the lesion were harvested for histological analysis.

Histological analysis

For histological examination, buccal mucosa specimens were collected from n = 4 rats per group on days 3 and 6 post-induction. Rats were euthanized at these time points. Buccal mucosa containing the ulcer was excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial 5-µm sections were cut and subjected to histological staining. Sections were stained with a hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) kit (Beyotime, C0105S) or a Masson’s trichrome staining kit (Solarbio, G1340) according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The slides were observed under a microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

The results were divided into two categories as follows: “evaluations in epithelial tissue” (0 = no ulcer; 1 = ulcer) and “evaluations in connective tissue” (0 = remodeled connective tissue; 1 = fibrosis/ discrete scattered mononuclear inflammatory cells; 2 = fibrosis/granulation tissue + mild mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate; 3 = fibrosis/granulation + moderate or intense mononuclear inflammatory infiltrate; 4 = fibrosis/granulation tissue + mixed inflammatory infiltrate (acute and chronic); and 5 = acute process (ecstatic vessels, edema, inflammatory infiltrate, and predominantly neutrophilic) [adapted from [15] ]. Edema was defined histologically as the presence of interstitial space widening, connective tissue architecture disruption, or eosinophilic protein exudate at 20× magnification. Two blinded pathologists independently evaluated four fields per section, with inter-observer agreement > 95%. Collagen volume fraction was quantified on Masson-stained images using SLIDEVIEW™ VS200 software (Olympus Corporation).

Picrosirius red staining

To evaluate collagen deposition and organization during the active healing phase, Picrosirius red (PSR) staining was performed on tissue sections obtained on day 6 post-induction using a Picrosirius Red Stain Kit (Solarbio, S8060) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PSR staining is the standard method for detecting and quantitatively evaluating collagen in both healthy and pathological tissue sections. PSR F3BA is an anionic dye characterized by sulfonic acid groups, which exhibit a high specificity for basic amino acids in collagen molecules. The alignment of PSR molecules parallel to the longitudinal axis of collagen fibers enhances collagen birefringence under polarized light microscopy. Consequently, this technique allows for the assessment of both collagen abundance and, qualitatively, the architecture and maturation of collagen fibers within the granulation tissue based on their birefringence intensity and morphological appearance under polarized light.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Paraffin-embedded buccal mucosa Sect. (5-µm) were subjected to immunohistochemical staining. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, sections were blocked and incubated overnight at 4°C with the following primary antibodies on separate slides: rabbit anti-IL-1β (1:200; Abcam, ab8227) and mouse anti-IL-6 (1:200; Abcam, ab9324). On the following day, the sections were incubated with species-specific horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h: goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, Beyotime, A0208) for IL-1β and goat anti-mouse IgG (1:500, Beyotime, A0216) for IL-6. Antigen-antibody complexes were visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) Substrate Kit (Beyotime, P0203) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and mounted. When assessing inflammatory markers, immunohistochemical scores were established by two blinded evaluators. The scoring system ranged from 0 to 3, where 0 indicated no positive cells, 1 corresponded to 1% to 33% positive cells, 2 corresponded to 34% to 66% labeled cells, and 3 corresponded to 67% to 100% labeled cells in the connective tissue analyses.

Statistic analysis

Data are expressed as mean ± SEM. Normality and homogeneity of variance were verified using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Levene’s tests, respectively. Group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA. Significant overall effects were followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for all pairwise comparisons among groups. An independent unpaired two-tailed t-test was specifically used to compare the optimal β-glucan group with the chlorhexidine group. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.0.

Results

Yeast β-glucan accelerates ulcer healing

One day post-induction, all animals exhibited symmetrical, crater-like lesions on the cheek mucosa, hemorrhagic bases, surrounding erythema, and a thick gray-white pseudomembrane with central depression and peripheral edema—confirming a successful model. Between day 1 and day 4, the negative control group showed no improvement: the pseudomembrane persisted, accompanied by pronounced edema. In contrast, the 0.12% chlorhexidine, 0.1%, 0.5% and 1.0% yeast β-glucan groups displayed a progressive reduction in ulcer size and severity; nevertheless, irregular borders and white halos still surrounded residual injuries (Fig. 1b).

By day 6, the negative control group retained uneven, bleeding ulcers, whereas the 0.12% chlorhexidine and 0.1% yeast β-glucan groups presented uniform scar formation with regular borders. The 0.5% and 1.0% yeast β-glucan groups showed almost complete resolution, with ulcerated areas becoming invisible and the mucosal surface largely restored to a healthy appearance (Fig. 1b). From day 1 to day 6, ulcer area decreased in all groups, with the 0.12% chlorhexidine, 0.1%, 0.5% and 1.0% yeast β-glucan groups showing significantly steeper reductions than the negative control from Day 3 onward (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1c). To directly address the differences in therapeutic effect, we performed comparative analyses between the treatment groups. On day 3, the ulcer area in the 1.0% β-glucan group was significantly smaller than that in both the 0.5% group (P < 0.05) and the chlorhexidine group (P < 0.01). However, by day 6, no statistically significant differences in ulcer area were detected among the chlorhexidine and the three β-glucan concentration groups (Fig. 1d). From day 3 onward, the negative-control group exhibited a marked and progressive weight loss relative to all β-glucan-treated groups, whereas the 0.1%, 0.5% or 1.0% yeast β-glucan groups maintained steady weight gain or only transient loss, indicating that untreated ulceration impaired feeding behaviour while β-glucan preserved overall nutritional status (Fig. 1e). However, no significant differences were observed among any of the treatment groups (chlorhexidine and β-glucan concentrations) on either day 3 or day 6, suggesting that the nutritional status was similarly preserved across these interventions (Fig. 1f).

Yeast β-glucan enhances histological repair

On day 3, in the negative control group the lesions remained in the acute inflammatory phase: epithelial gaps were still wide, inflammatory cell infiltrates were pronounced, and only scant, loosely arranged collagen was present. By contrast, the standard care control and all yeast β-glucan-treated groups already displayed early signs of repair—namely, re-epithelialization with elongated epithelial ridges, moderate fibroblast proliferation, and notably reduced inflammation. In the 0.1% yeast β-glucan group, reduced inflammatory cell infiltration within the oral mucosal tissue was evident, without signs of edema (absence of interstitial widening, connective tissue disruption, and eosinophilic exudate at 20× magnification). The 0.5% yeast β-glucan group exhibited a partially replaced ulcer surface with new granulation tissue, whereas the 1% yeast β-glucan group exhibited a partially covered ulcer surface with new collagen fiber tissue (Fig. 2a). On day 6, healing indices in every yeast β-glucan group had returned to near-normal levels. Histologically, the ulcer bed was almost completely resurfaced with a mature epithelial layer, and the underlying connective tissue exhibited dense, well-aligned collagen bundles and minimal residual inflammation. The 1% yeast β-glucan group most closely resembled healthy mucosa, whereas the 0.1% yeast β-glucan group still displayed focal, albeit mild, inflammatory foci (Fig. 2a). On day 3, epithelial tissue scores did not differ significantly among groups; on day 6, however, scores in both the standard care control and yeast β-glucan groups were markedly lower than in the negative control group (P < 0.0001). Notably, at both day 3 and day 6, no statistically significant differences were observed in epithelial scores among the chlorhexidine group and the various yeast β-glucan concentration groups. Similarly, connective tissue scores for the standard care control and yeast β-glucan groups differed significantly from the negative control group at both time points (P < 0.0001), and no significant differences were detected among the treatment groups themselves (Fig. 2c).

Masson staining was used to evaluate collagen deposition and overall healing status of the oral ulcers. The ulcer tissue after modeling exhibited epithelial destruction, with the connective tissue characterized by loosely arranged and disordered collagen fibers. The negative control group remained in the inflammatory phase on day 3; on day 6, collagen deposition was still minimal. The standard care control, 0.5%, and 1% yeast β-glucan groups already showed marked collagen deposition on day 3, which became progressively better aligned on day 6. Notably, the 1% yeast β-glucan group closely resembled healthy mucosa, indicating favorable healing. In contrast, the 0.1% yeast β-glucan group still displayed pronounced inflammatory infiltration on day 3, although collagen density had increased on day 6 (Fig. 2b). At both day 3 and day 6, collagen volume fractions in the standard care control and all yeast β-glucan groups were significantly higher than in the negative control group (P < 0.001), confirming superior healing in the treated groups (Fig. 2d). Statistical comparisons among the treatment groups revealed a concentration-dependent effect. On day 3, the collagen volume fraction in the 0.1% group was significantly lower than that in both the 0.5% (P < 0.05) and 1% groups (P < 0.05). Additionally, the 1% group showed significantly higher collagen deposition than the chlorhexidine group (P < 0.05), while no significant difference was observed between the 0.5% and 1% groups. By day 6, the disparity between the 0.1% group and the higher concentrations persisted, with significant differences compared to the 0.5% (P < 0.05) and 1% groups (P < 0.01). However, the difference between the 1% yeast β-glucan group and the chlorhexidine group was no longer significant, and the 0.5% and 1% groups remained statistically comparable (Fig. 2d).

Yeast β-glucan enhances collagen deposition

To further characterize the architecture and maturity of the collagen matrix deposited during healing, tissue sections were assessed using Picrosirius red staining under polarized light. Under these conditions, collagen fibers exhibit enhanced birefringence, the intensity and morphology of which provide qualitative insights into their organization. In the yeast β-glucan-treated groups, the collagen fibers within the granulation tissue displayed notably stronger birefringence and appeared thicker and more densely packed with a more parallel alignment compared to the thin, loosely arranged, and weakly birefringent fibers observed in the negative control group (Fig. 3a). These qualitative observations suggest that yeast β-glucan treatment promoted the deposition of a more mature and structurally organized collagen network.

Quantitative analysis of the collagen area ratio (the proportion of collagen-positive area to total tissue area) corroborated these findings. All yeast β-glucan-treated groups exhibited a significantly higher collagen area ratio than the negative control group (P < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons among the treatment groups revealed specific differences: the 1% yeast β-glucan group showed a significantly higher collagen area ratio than the chlorhexidine group (P < 0.01). Furthermore, the effect was concentration-dependent, with the 0.1% group demonstrating a significantly lower ratio than both the 0.5% (P < 0.01) and 1% (P < 0.01) groups. No significant difference was observed between the 0.5% and 1% yeast β-glucan groups (Fig. 3d). Together, the qualitative and quantitative data indicate that yeast β-glucan not only increases total collagen deposition but also enhances the structural quality of the collagen matrix in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. 3d).

Yeast β-glucan attenuates early expression of IL-1β and IL-6

Immunohistochemical staining for IL-1β revealed a distinct temporal pattern. On day 3 post-induction, IL-1β immunoreactivity was markedly elevated in the negative control group compared to all treatment groups (Fig. 3b). In contrast, IL-1β levels were uniformly lower across the chlorhexidine and the various yeast β-glucan concentration groups, with no statistically significant differences detected among these treatment groups themselves. By day 6, the expression of IL-1β had declined across all groups, and at this later time point, no statistically significant differences in IL-1β immunoreactivity were observed among any of the experimental groups (Fig. 3e).

The expression profile of IL-6 differed from that of IL-1β. On day 3, no statistically significant differences in IL-6 expression levels were found among any of the groups, including between the negative control and the treatment groups (Fig. 3c). This pattern persisted through day 6, by which time IL-6 expression had diminished to minimal or undetectable levels in all groups, with no significant inter-group differences remaining (Fig. 3f).

Discussion

Oral ulcers are the most prevalent mucosal lesions, driven by a complex pathogenesis. In this study, water-soluble yeast β-glucan markedly accelerated ulcer resolution in rats by sustaining inflammatory cytokine environment and enhancing collagen synthesis and epithelial regeneration. Compared with corticosteroids or antibiotics, which can cause hyperglycaemia, blood-pressure swings or antibiotic resistance, yeast β-glucan offers a safer, biocompatible and equally effective alternative.

In this study, we included a group treated with 0.12% chlorhexidine as our standard care control. This decision was made to establish a baseline for oral hygiene maintenance, which is a fundamental aspect of ulcer management [16]. Our model was designed to mimic common oral ulcers, which are typically self-limiting and heal spontaneously over time, albeit at a slower rate without intervention. Although chlorhexidine is not a specific ulcer treatment, it is widely recognized for its antiseptic properties and is commonly used in oral care regimens [17, 18]. Chlorhexidine’s role in this context is to demonstrate that yeast β-glucan can significantly accelerate the natural healing process of oral ulcers, which would otherwise resolve at a baseline rate when managed with standard oral hygiene practices. This comparison highlights the potential of yeast β-glucan as a therapeutic agent that can enhance the body’s natural healing mechanisms.

Moreover, yeast β-glucan, being a natural product, offers several advantages over chlorhexidine. Unlike chlorhexidine, which is a broad-spectrum antimicrobial agent that can sometimes lead to mucosal irritation, dysbiosis, allergic reactions, and the potential for antimicrobial resistance [19, 20], yeast β-glucan is derived from a natural source and has a favorable safety profile [6]. As an immunomodulator with prebiotic-like activity, it modulates the oral microbiome in a targeted manner, for example, by reducing pathogenic biofilm formation and selectively altering microbial composition, and is less likely to cause adverse reactions [11, 21].This study, therefore, not only evaluates the efficacy of yeast β-glucan but also underscores its potential as a safer alternative to chlorhexidine.

Histomorphometry showed significantly higher epithelial scores and markedly reduced ulcer areas in the yeast β-glucan groups than in the negative control, indicating accelerated epithelialization and wound closure. Moreover, epithelial thickness was substantially increased in the yeast β-glucan cohorts, and both the basal layer and lamina propria exhibited normal morphology, confirming that yeast β-glucan effectively promotes mucosal repair. We hypothesise that the acceletated healing observed under β-glucan treatment is attributable to its enhancement of re-epithelialization, angiogenesis, and collagen synthesis [22, 23].

Collagen-the primary extracellular-matrix protein synthesised by fibroblasts-drives connective-tissue repair and is indispensable for the remodelling phase that ultimately closes wounds [24, 25]. Studies have shown that β-glucan not only stimulates fibroblast proliferation but also enhances their migration, thereby accelerating wound healing [5, 26, 27]. In our study, yeast β-glucan-treated lesions exhibited significantly greater collagen fibre density and more orderly alignment than those in the negative control group. Importantly, this pro-reparative effect on the extracellular matrix was accompanied by a marked reduction in early pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-1β). This suggests that β-glucan may facilitate the transition from the inflammatory phase to the proliferative/remodelling phase, creating a more conducive microenvironment for fibroblast activity and collagen synthesis. While our current methodology (Picrosirius red staining) robustly demonstrates improvements in total collagen deposition and matrix organization, it does not allow for definitive quantification of collagen subtypes (e.g., Col I vs. Col III). Future studies employing techniques such as Western blot or immunohistochemistry with type-specific antibodies would be valuable to precisely determine the effects of β-glucan on the synthesis and ratio of different collagen types during oral mucosal repair.

Studies have indicated that yeast β-glucan orchestrates immunomodulation and actively promotes wound healing and tissue repair [28, 29]. However, the immunomodulatory pathways of yeast β-glucans is intricate and remain incompletely elucidated. A prevailing hypothesis posits that the helical conformation of yeast β-glucans enables engagement with multiple pattern-recognition receptors on macrophages, dendritic cells and T lymphocytes [30–32]. Among these receptors, the interaction with the C-type lectin receptor Dectin-1 is particularly well-characterized [33, 34]. This specific binding is known to trigger a downstream signaling cascade involving Syk kinase and CARD9, leading to the activation of the transcription factor NF-κB and the subsequent release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α [35–37]. Our findings highlight that this canonical pathway may yield a qualitatively different outcome depending on the physicochemical presentation of the ligand and the tissue microenvironment. Employing a unique water-soluble, high-molecular-weight formulation, we observed a modulated early response: IL-1β levels were significantly lower in treated groups compared to the control at the early phase (day 3), while IL-6 levels were comparable across groups. This attenuated early inflammatory profile coincided with subsequently enhanced tissue repair.

Translating β-glucans into wound therapies faces formulation challenges due to their inherent physicochemical properties. Typically, insoluble β-glucans can promote wound healing due to their excellent immunoenhancing effects [26, 38]. However, the robust and insoluble nature of the β-glucan cell wall presents significant obstacles for its absorption and delivery as a medicinal agent. Conversely, low-molecular-weight soluble derivatives obtained through extensive hydrolysis may exhibit variable and sometimes reduced immunomodulatory activity [39]. The agent used in this study differs: it is a fully water-soluble yeast β-glucan prepared via a patented enzymatic process that preserves its native high-molecular-weight structure (≈ 500 kDa). This “structure-preserving solubilization” allows for direct and uniform application to moist wounds while maintaining bioactivity. The functional outcome we observed—a modulated cytokine profile at the early phase (characterized by significantly lower IL-1β in treated groups vs. control on day 3) that was temporally coupled with tissue repair, rather than an acute inflammatory burst—may be related to this distinct physicochemical profile [40]. Thus, our findings suggest that both solubility and molecular structure jointly shape the immunomodulatory phenotype of β-glucan, with this soluble high-MW form associated with a mucosal healing process characterized by the observed cytokine and repair dynamics.

To elucidate the precise immunomodulatory mechanism of this specific soluble β-glucan formulation and to define its role in tissue repair, further investigation is warranted. Key future directions include the direct profiling of macrophage phenotypes (e.g., via markers such as iNOS and Arg-1) to assess their potential transition in response to treatment. Concurrent studies to verify the involvement of specific receptors (e.g., Dectin-1) and downstream signaling pathways in this model will be essential. Together, this work will help determine whether the observed reparative outcome is mediated through a modulation of canonical immune pathways or involves distinct mechanisms unique to soluble high-molecular-weight β-glucans. These steps are crucial for advancing from the correlative observations presented here to a definitive mechanistic understanding.

A primary limitation of this study is the inherent physiological differences between rats and humans. Although there are similarities between species, these models do not accurately reflect the pathogenesis of oral ulceration in humans. A second limitation is the exclusive reliance on an acute chemical-trauma model. While this method offers a controlled and reproducible injury, it does not accurately reflect the etiology of most oral ulcers encountered in clinical settings [14]. In practice, oral ulcers are more frequently associated with systemic conditions, autoimmune disorders, or mechanical trauma—such as when the palatal mucosa is used as a donor site in surgical procedures [2]. These scenarios typically involve a compromised healing capacity and altered immune responses, which are not adequately represented by the chemically induced acute trauma model. Specifically, the ability of glucan solutions to accelerate healing under optimal—near-ideal—conditions may not be directly extrapolated to more complex or chronic ulcerative states where host immunity is impaired. In such settings, the regenerative and immunomodulatory effects of glucan may be attenuated or may interact differently with the host environment. Therefore, while the study demonstrates promising results under simplified conditions, future research should incorporate models that better mimic the multifactorial and often immunologically challenged context of clinical oral ulceration.

Several research domains should be investigated to enhance the clinical utilization of yeast β-glucan in treating oral ulcers. First, researchers should elucidate the biological mechanisms by which yeast β-glucan facilitates oral ulcer-associated healing. Second, the development of efficient delivery systems, such as hydrogels or mucoadhesive materials, is crucial to ensure the sustained contact of yeast β-glucan with the ulcerative site, thereby optimizing healing. Furthermore, comprehensive clinical trials are necessary to assess the efficacy, safety, and optimal dosage of yeast β-glucan across diverse patient populations. A comparative analysis of its effectiveness against existing treatments for oral ulcers, including corticosteroids and antibiotics, is imperative to ascertain the treatments relative advantages and potential adverse effects.

Conclusions

In conclusion, water-soluble yeast β-glucan may significantly accelerated oral-ulcer repair in rat models by enhanced collagen deposition and re-epithelialization. Our findings provide a basis for further investigation of β-glucan’s reparative and immune-regulatory potential within the human oral mucosa.
