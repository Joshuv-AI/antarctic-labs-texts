Perspective on alcohol-induced organ damage via autophagy-dependent cellular changes
Authors: Ahmed Bakheet, Murugadas Anbazhagan, Shirish Barve, Srinivasan Dasarathy, John J Dickinson, Wen-Xing Ding, Terrence M Donohue Jr, Thiyagarajan Gopal, Anna Gukovskaya, Kusum K Kharbanda, John J Lemasters, Natalia A Osna, Pal Pacher, Lucy Sloan, Luis F Vilches, Saraswathi Viswanathan, Xiao-Ming Yin, Zhi Zhong, Paul G Thomes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlcohol-induced organ damage is a major global health concern and a leading cause of disability and mortality. In addition to the liver, chronic alcohol consumption adversely affects multiple organ systems, including the cardiovascular and neuromuscular systems, the gastrointestinal tract, adipose tissue, kidneys, pancreas, and the brain. Organ damage begins with necrosis (cell death) followed by tissue injury, which triggers immune responses aimed at tissue repair. However, with continued heavy drinking, these reparative responses transition into inflammatory responses which exacerbate tissue injury and accelerate disease progression.Macroautophagy, commonly referred to as autophagy, is a vital and evolutionarily-conserved digestive process in eukaryotic cells. While autophagy is well known for degrading obsolete proteins, and damaged organelles to prevent their accumulation, autophagy also plays critical roles in regulating innate and adaptive immunity and in modulating inflammation. This review describes evidence that chronic alcohol exposure and chronic alcohol consumption impair autophagy, contributing to dysfunction across multiple organ systems.We and others have explored the mechanisms by which alcohol disrupts autophagy to cause organ damage. This review aims to establish a cohesive understanding of these intracellular processes, which is essential for guiding future research toward targeting autophagy as a therapeutic strategy for alcohol-induced tissue injury.In summary, this comprehensive review integrates evidence from preclinical studies to present a unified perspective on how alcohol induces autophagy dysregulation and contributes to organ damage. It also highlights evidence-based recommendations from leading researchers in the field, offering valuable insights to advance autophagy-targeted therapies for mitigating the effects of alcohol-induced organ injury.

Introduction

Impact of alcohol-associated organ damage

Alcohol-induced organ damage is a serious public health concern with far-reaching consequences [1]. Excessive alcohol consumption damages cells and tissues throughout the body, impairing their normal functions, thereby contributing to disease. Globally, alcohol misuse is linked to significant disability and mortality, contributing to nearly 14 % of deaths among young adults aged 20 to 39 [2]. The World Health Organization now associates excessive alcohol use with over 200 health conditions, highlighting its widespread impact. In the U.S. alone, alcohol-related deaths rank among the leading causes of preventable mortality, with the associated socioeconomic burden continuing to grow [3]. Developing effective treatment strategies requires a deeper understanding of the mechanisms underlying alcohol-induced tissue damage.

Etiology of alcohol-related organ damage

Alcohol-related organ damage stems from multiple factors, including variations in alcohol consumption patterns, the quantity and duration of alcohol use, and an individual's genetic susceptibility to heavy drinking. Additional contributing factors include pre-existing medical conditions, nutritional deficiencies, and lifestyle choices. Prolonged, excessive alcohol use, especially when accompanied by poor nutrition, smoking, other substance use, or infection, heightens the risk of organ damage or failure.

Alcohol-associated organ damage and autophagy

Chronic alcohol consumption damages multiple organs, with the liver being the most vulnerable due to its central role in metabolizing alcohol from alcohol containing beverages. This vulnerability arises from the liver's high concentration of enzymatic machinery necessary for alcohol metabolism. The pathogenesis of alcohol-induced liver injury progresses through distinct stages, beginning with alcohol-associated fatty liver and advancing to hepatitis, cirrhosis, and ultimately, an increased risk of hepatocellular carcinoma [4,5]. Beyond the liver, alcohol-related damage extends to other organs, including cardiovascular dysfunction, gastrointestinal disturbances, pancreatic inflammation (pancreatitis), neural (cognitive) impairments, and an elevated risk of cancer in these tissues.

Although the mechanisms of alcohol-induced organ damage vary across different systems, common pathological factors such as oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell death play pivotal roles in driving tissue injury [1]. Prolonged heavy drinking sustains tissue injury and triggers an aberrant immune response, which not only perpetuates inflammation but also disrupts the tissue repair process, further exacerbating organ dysfunction.

Dysregulation of autophagy has emerged as a key factor in the pathophysiological outcomes associated with alcohol-induced damage in the liver, pancreas, heart, and muscle. The following sections will explore how alcohol influences autophagy dynamics across various organs, highlight autophagy-dependent changes contributing to organ pathology, and identify potential areas for future research aimed at developing autophagy-targeted therapies to mitigate alcohol-related organ injury. In this review, “alcohol” refers specifically to ethanol, the type of alcohol present in alcoholic beverages and used in experimental models.

Overview of the macroautophagy pathway

Macroautophagy (commonly referred to as autophagy) is a highly conserved cellular process essential for maintaining homeostasis by degrading and recycling subcellular components [6,7]. This process begins with the engulfment of cytoplasmic material, including damaged organelles and proteins, by a double-membrane structure termed the autophagosome. The autophagosome subsequently fuses with lysosomes to form autolysosomes, where the contents are degraded and recycled (Fig. 1). Although macroautophagy is the most prevalent and well-studied form of autophagy, it is not the only type. Other forms include microautophagy and chaperone-mediated autophagy (CMA), each utilizing distinct mechanisms to deliver cargo to lysosomes for degradation [[8], [9], [10]].

In microautophagy, cellular components are directly internalized by lysosomes and exposed to lysosomal hydrolases for degradation. CMA, on the other hand, selectively degrades proteins containing a KFERQ peptide motif [11]. This motif is recognized by the Heat Shock Cognate 70 (Hsc70) chaperone, which directs the proteins to the lysosome, where Lysosome-Associated Membrane Protein type 2A (LAMP2A) facilitates their translocation across the lysosomal membrane for degradation (Fig. 1). The roles of microautophagy and CMA in alcohol-related organ dysfunction remain largely unexplored and will not be the focus of this review.

Induction and regulation of macroautophagy

Autophagy is tightly regulated by nutrient availability and stress signals, with the mammalian target of rapamycin complex 1 (mTORC1) serving as a central nutrient sensor. Under nutrient-rich conditions, mTORC1 suppresses autophagy by inhibiting the formation of the Unc-51 Like Kinase (ULK) complex, which includes Ulk1, Atg13, and FAK family kinase-interacting protein (FIP200) [12]. When mTORC1 activity is reduced, such as during starvation or rapamycin treatment, the ULK complex becomes active and initiates autophagy [13,14]. The active ULK complex recruits the class III phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) complex, comprising Vps34, Beclin 1 (Becn1), and Vps15, to the isolation membrane or phagophore, a cup-shaped, single-membrane structure that elongates and closes around cytoplasmic material to form an autophagosome [15]. This recruitment promotes the production of phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P), a key signaling lipid that drives autophagosome nucleation (Fig. 1).

Elongation and maturation

The elongation of the autophagosome membrane involves two ubiquitin-like conjugation systems. In the first system, Atg12 is conjugated to Atg5 via the E1-like enzyme Atg7 and E2-like enzyme Atg10. The Atg12-Atg5 conjugate associates with Atg16L1 to form a complex that localizes to the phagophore, facilitating membrane expansion. In the second system, LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 light chain 3) is processed by Atg4 to expose its C-terminal glycine. LC3 is subsequently lipidated with phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) through the sequential action of Atg7 and Atg3, forming LC3-II [[16], [17], [18]]. This lipidated form of LC3 integrates into the autophagosome membrane and is used as a biochemical/cytological marker of autophagosome maturation (Fig. 1).

Fusion, degradation, and lysosome biogenesis

Once formed, autophagosomes transport their cargo to lysosomes along microtubules. The fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes is a complex process mediated by Soluble NSF Attachment Protein Receptors (SNARE) [19,20] For example, the interaction between Syntaxin 17 on the autophagosome and vesicle-associated membrane protein 8 (VAMP8) on the lysosome is essential for fusion. Lysosome-associated membrane protein 2 (LAMP2) further facilitates the merging of these two organelles [21,22].

Maintaining efficient autophagy also requires a high lysosomal degradative capacity. Lysosomes contain more than 50 hydrolases, enabling the degradation of diverse biological materials, including proteins, nucleic acids, carbohydrates, lipids, and other macromolecules. A key feature of lysosomes is their acidic pH, which aligns with the optimal activity of most lysosomal hydrolases [23].

Over the past decade, our understanding of lysosomes has evolved from viewing them as simple “garbage disposals” to recognizing them as key regulators of fundamental cellular processes such as nutrient sensing, vesicular protein trafficking, endocytosis, and autophagy (Fig. 1). The Micropthalmia/Transcription Factor E (MiT/TFE) family of transcription factors, most notably Transcription Factor EB (TFEB), has been shown to regulate lysosomal biogenesis and autophagic function at a global level [23,24].

Bulk and selective macroautophagy

Macroautophagy can be either non-selective or selective in targeting specific organelles for degradation. While bulk autophagy is typically induced by starvation, selective autophagy removes potentially harmful, damaged organelles such as mitochondria (mitophagy), the endoplasmic reticulum (ER-phagy), and lysosomes (lysophagy) [25]. Other selective autophagy pathways include the degradation of protein aggregates (aggrephagy), lipid droplets (lipophagy), and intracellular pathogens (xenophagy), all of which help maintain cellular integrity by eliminating dysfunctional components and adapting to metabolic demands.

During selective autophagy, substrates marked for degradation express specific receptors on their surface, which interact with LC3 anchored in the autophagosome membrane. This interaction enables cargo sequestration within the autophagosome, followed by lysosomal degradation. Selective autophagy plays a crucial role in maintaining cellular homeostasis, and its dysregulation is a common feature of various diseases [25,26].

Role of autophagy in cellular physiology and alcohol-associated organ dysfunction

Autophagy is fundamental to cellular physiology because it is an evolutionarily conserved mechanism that maintains homeostasis through the removal of damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and toxic aggregates, while recycling essential metabolites. Through this tightly regulated degradative process, cells adapt to stress, preserve energy balance, and sustain organ function. Because nearly all tissues rely on autophagy for quality control and stress adaptation, its disruption by chronic alcohol exposure can have broad systemic consequences. Alcohol metabolism alters redox balance and interferes with autophagy regulation at multiple levels, including initiation, vesicle trafficking, and lysosomal function, leading to tissue-specific vulnerabilities. The following sections examine how alcohol-induced autophagy dysregulation manifests across different organ systems, highlighting both shared mechanisms and organ-specific outcomes that collectively drive multi-organ dysfunction in alcohol-associated disease.

Liver

The liver serves as the central hub for maintaining macro- and micronutrient homeostasis, relying on autophagy to degrade macromolecules and recycle damaged organelles. Dysregulated autophagy plays a pivotal role in the progression of Alcohol-Associated Liver Disease (ALD). Evidence suggests that autophagy protects hepatocytes and macrophages from alcohol-induced injury, whereas in hepatic stellate cells, it may exacerbate ALD pathogenesis by promoting fibrosis. Additionally, the mode of alcohol consumption appears to influence its impact on autophagy, as discussed in the following sections [[27], [28], [29]].

Mechanisms of alcohol regulation on hepatic autophagy

Acute alcohol administration increases essential Atg proteins and their corresponding mRNAs in mouse livers [30,31], enhancing autophagic and mitophagic flux by promoting the formation of autophagosomes and autolysosomes [30,[32], [33], [34], [35]]. Studies using pharmacological inhibitors and siRNAs have revealed that binge alcohol-induced autophagy plays a protective role in hepatocytes by suppressing apoptosis [32,36]. These early protective responses are, however, highly sensitive to the redox environment established by alcohol metabolism.

In this context, alcohol metabolism via cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) generates superoxide radical (O2•-), a type of reactive oxygen species (ROS). CYP2E1 is highly inducible by alcohol and is an “uncoupled” enzyme that leaks electrons to molecular oxygen during alcohol oxidation, generating superoxide (O2•-) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) as primary reactive oxygen species (ROS). These ROS are produced directly as byproducts of CYP2E1's catalytic cycle and can be generated even in the absence of exogenous substrates, owing to its elevated NADPH oxidase activity. Therefore, alcohol-induced upregulation of CYP2E1 protein expression conveniently enhances intracellular ROS production through this mechanism [37]. O2•- is also produced by other cellular/molecular components, including the mitochondrial respiratory chain, peroxisomes, endoplasmic reticulum, NADPH oxidases (NOX), and xanthine oxidase. Superoxide is converted to hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) by superoxide dismutase (SOD), and subsequently to the highly reactive hydroxyl radical (•OH) and hydroxide ion (OH−) via the Haber-Weiss/Fenton reaction. Chronic alcohol exposure also promotes formation of peroxynitrite (ONOO−) [38], a reactive nitrogen species (RNS) formed by the rapid reaction of O2•- with nitric oxide (NO•). ONOO− mediates tyrosine nitration of cellular proteins, impairing their function and contributing to cellular dysfunction. •OH initiates lipid peroxidation, triggering chain reactions that produce various lipid and lipid peroxide radicals. Some of these undergo β-scission to form reactive aldehydes such as malondialdehyde (MDA), acrolein, and 4-hydroxy-2-nonenal (4-HNE). These aldehydes further react with biomolecules including proteins, DNA, and even other aldehydes to form damaging adducts [37].

Collectively, these processes create a redox environment that not only damages liver tissue but also dynamically modulates autophagy. Experimental studies using CYP2E1-expressing hepatocytes and transgenic mouse models suggest that elevated O2•-, H2O2, and ONOO− can influence autophagic flux, disrupt mitochondrial integrity, and promote oxidative inactivation of lysosomal enzymes [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. In addition, acetaldehyde (AcAld), the primary metabolite of alcohol indirectly exacerbates oxidative and nitrosative stress by impairing mitochondrial respiration, depleting glutathione, and promoting nitric oxide production, thereby further amplifying redox imbalance and autophagy dysregulation. Importantly, free radical production involves a complex cascade of reactions, therefore, identifying which specific radical or aldehyde initiates a particular alteration in the autophagy process remains challenging. Nevertheless, CYP2E1-derived O2•-, H2O2, and ONOO− are consistently implicated as upstream modulators of redox-sensitive autophagy disruption during alcohol exposure [[39], [40], [41], [42]]. Moreover, by amplifying oxidant stress, disrupting cellular trafficking events, and modifying the function of proteins involved in autophagy, AcAld can further exacerbate CYP2E1-dependent ROS-mediated outcomes [43]. In the following sections describing how ROS/RNS and oxidant stress influence specific steps in the autophagy process, these free radicals and AcAld should be considered as potential compounding factors, although the precise involvement of each species remains to be determined.

Redox imbalance not only damages cellular components directly but also alters key signaling pathways that govern autophagy regulation. Acute ROS elevation transiently inhibits Mammalian Target of Rapamycin (mTORC1) and activates autophagy. However, chronic oxidative stress inhibits AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), sustains mTORC1 signaling, and impairs autophagy [[44], [45], [46]]. Simultaneously, ROS-induced proteasomal dysfunction leads to LC3-II and SQSTM1(Sequestome 1)/p62 accumulation, compounding autophagic impairment [47]. These redox-sensitive pathways influence autophagy initiation and degradation, highlighting how metabolic byproducts of alcohol indirectly govern the autophagy lifecycle.

While acute alcohol exposure can promote mitophagy and enhance degradation of damaged mitochondria as an adaptive response to transient mitochondrial depolarization and oxidative stress, chronic exposure shifts this balance. As alcohol metabolism proceeds, the accumulation of reactive aldehydes (e.g., AcAld, 4-HNE) and persistent ROS/RNS production collectively overwhelm detoxification systems and redox-buffering capacity, leading to lysosomal dysfunction and disrupted autophagic flux.

Thus, a threshold likely exists at which the accumulating redox burden and aldehyde load disrupt lysosomal homeostasis, driving lysosomal dysfunction and associated pathological outcomes. This transition is influenced not only by direct effects of alcohol and their metabolites on autophagosomes and lysosomes, but also by their disruption of essential mechanisms required for autophagy initiation, progression and completion. These include vesicular trafficking, autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and cargo delivery; both substrates destined for degradation (which can accumulate and overwhelm lysosomes) and Golgi-derived lysosomal components such as LAMP1 and cathepsins, which are critical for maintaining lysosomal structure, acidification, and proteolytic activity. Chronic alcohol exposure may impair these processes in a manner dependent on the duration and intensity of exposure, the extent of alcohol oxidation, and the buildup of reactive metabolic byproducts. In parallel, alcohol-induced injury to organelles that support lysosomal function, particularly the Golgi apparatus and mitochondria, further compromises the integrity and performance of the lysosomal system. Accumulated substrate load, lysosomal membrane destabilization, dysregulation of lysosomal stress responders such as TFEB, as well as defective lysosomal repair mechanisms together drive pathological lysosomal remodeling and hepatocellular dysfunction. Future studies defining the redox and aldehyde thresholds and pinpointing vulnerable redox-sensitive steps in the autophagy-lysosome axis will be essential for understanding disease progression in ALD.

This pathogenic shift aligns with findings from chronic and chronic-binge alcohol models, where autophagosome and mitophagosome formation continues, yet degradative capacity is compromised, leading to impaired lysosomal clearance. In these models, alcohol feeding also increases autophagosome/mitophagosome formation [30,35,48,49] but the subsequent degradative lysosomal processing becomes inhibited due to decreased lysosomal proteolytic capacity and protease activity [44,[50], [51], [52]]. Alcohol-induced oxidative stress compromises lysosomal function by raising intralysosomal pH [53] and impairing cathepsin activity [50], a phenomenon attributed to oxidant damage to lysosomal membranes and vacuolar-type H+-ATPases (v-ATPases), the proton pumps responsible for acidifying lysosomes and enabling optimal protease activity. Although direct evidence is limited, ROS and reactive aldehydes generated during alcohol oxidation are expected to damage lysosomal membranes and proton pumps, leading to these impairments. AcAld-protein adducts are also known to disrupt intracellular trafficking, particularly by modifying α-tubulin [54,55], which hinders autophagosome movement and fusion with lysosomes [43,56]. These changes are linked to a decrease in the nuclear expression of TFEB, the master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and function [48,49,57]. Alterations in TFEB nuclear-cytoplasmic shuttling likely reflect dynamic lysosomal stress responses, which are in turn influenced by oxidants and AcAld as described above. Consequently, damaged organelles and undegraded substrates progressively accumulate, overloading the compromised lysosomal system. In particular, the persistence of mitophagosomes without effective clearance allows damaged mitochondria to linger within hepatocytes, creating a source of metabolic dysfunction and inflammatory signaling. Insufficient/incomplete degradation of mitochondrial contents leads to the release of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs), such as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), succinate, and cytochrome c, into the cytosol and circulation [48,49]. Since mitochondria have a prokaryotic origin, many mtDAMPs are recognized by the innate immune system in the same way as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), triggering cell death, inflammation and fibrosis, thereby contributing to the progression of ALD. Impaired degradative autophagy also leads to the accumulation of misfolded, aldehyde-adducted, and isoaspartyl-modified proteins, as well as damaged organelles including mitochondria. This accumulation activates an immunogenic "self-defense" response that becomes maladaptive with continued alcohol use.

Similarly, chronic alcohol consumption also increases lysosomal fragility [50], leading to cathepsin leakage and apoptosis via the intrinsic pathway [58]. This apoptosis potentiates liver injury by promoting inflammatory responses and fibrosis through the uptake of apoptotic bodies by non-parenchymal cells [5,[59], [60], [61]], further exacerbating hepatic damage. These lysosomal and trafficking defects are further linked to alcohol-induced increases in miR-155, which silences LAMP2, a protein essential for lysosome function. This miR-155-induced lysosome dysfunction also increases exosome release [62]. While exosomes help remove damaged proteins and nucleic acids, they can also transmit harmful signals to other organs. Similarly, alcohol-exposed hepatocytes showed higher exosome release associated with reduced cathepsin activity [58], suggesting that impaired lysosomal degradation contributes to exosome-mediated cellular communication, potentially exacerbating liver injury and systemic inflammation.

One downstream consequence of impaired lysosomal degradation is altered exosome secretion, a process increasingly recognized as a contributor to alcohol-associated liver injury. In CYP2E1-overexpressing Huh7.5 cells, a human hepatoma-derived cell line, AcAld exposure exacerbated lysosomal dysfunction, likely by increasing the generation of superoxide anion and other ROS. This effect was mitigated by N-acetylcysteine (NAC), a thiol antioxidant and glutathione precursor, supporting a redox-sensitive mechanism of exosome regulation [63].

Exosome secretion occurs through the fusion of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) with the plasma membrane. Proteins that undergo specific post-translational modifications (PTMs) are often targeted to lysosomes for degradation; however, when degradation is impaired, these proteins may instead be sorted into exosomes. This sorting process is influenced by specific PTMs, such as K63-linked ubiquitination (UbK63) and ISGylation, the covalent attachment of ISG15, a ubiquitin-like modifier, to substrate proteins. ISGylation of MVB-associated proteins, such as TSG101, promotes their aggregation and targeting to lysosomes for degradation. For example, ISGylation of TSG101 has been shown to suppress exosome release by facilitating its lysosomal degradation [64]. Importantly, ISGylation is enhanced by type I interferon (IFN–I) signaling, but this pathway is disrupted by AcAld exposure, particularly in hepatitis C virus (HCV)-infected hepatocytes. AcAld suppresses IFN-I signaling, thereby reducing ISGylation of proteins, including TSG101 [65,66]. As a result, impaired ISGylation of TSG101 may reduce its lysosomal degradation, leading to increased exosome secretion in hepatocytes exposed to both viral infection and alcohol-derived metabolites such as AcAld. These findings suggest that alcohol- and virus-induced suppression of redox-sensitive ISGylation pathways, in the context of CYP2E1-driven ROS production, promotes exosome release by impairing lysosomal degradation of MVB cargo. However, whether these virus-specific mechanisms of exosome regulation are operative in the context of alcohol exposure alone remains to be experimentally validated.

Aldehydes as drivers of mitochondrial depolarization and consequent mitophagy

Intravital multiphoton microscopy in rodents has demonstrated that alcohol induces reversible hepatic mitochondrial depolarization (mtDepo), primarily in pericentral hepatocytes located near the liver surface, where they are accessible for imaging [[67], [68], [69], [70], [71], [72], [73]]. mtDepo occurs in an all-or-nothing manner within individual hepatocytes. The extent of mtDepo following a single intragastric dose of alcohol is both time- and dose-dependent, affecting up to ∼90 % of pericentral hepatocytes near the liver surface after a high dose of alcohol (5–6 g/kg body weight). This depolarization peaks at 6–12 h and resolves almost completely within 24 h mtDepo has also been observed in chronic and chronic-binge alcohol feeding models [69,73].

Alcohol is primarily metabolized to AcAld by cytosolic ADH and endoplasmic reticulum CYP2E1, with a smaller contribution from catalase in peroxisomes. AcAld is subsequently converted to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase-2 (ALDH2) in mitochondria.

Although the liver is the primary site for detoxifying alcohol metabolite AcAld via ALDH2, recent evidence suggests that the liver alone is insufficient for systemic AcAld clearance. Instead, a liver-gut axis synergistically regulates both systemic AcAld clearance and voluntary alcohol consumption. Mechanistically, a significant portion of AcAld generated in the liver is excreted into the gastrointestinal tract via bile, where it undergoes further metabolism by gut ALDH2 [74].

AcAld is the principal driver of mitochondrial depolarization (mtDepo), as evidenced by the fact that inhibitors of ADH and CYP2E1 (which reduce AcAld formation) and activators of ALDH2 (which enhance AcAld metabolism) decrease mtDepo, whereas ALDH2 inhibition exacerbates it [67,73]. mtDepo serves as an adaptive role by promoting increased mitochondrial respiration and Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide (NAD+) formation to accelerate detoxifying alcohol metabolism by ADH and ALDH2. This mechanism explains the enhanced hepatic alcohol metabolism observed after both acute and chronic alcohol exposure [[75], [76], [77]].

Simultaneously, voltage-dependent anion channels (VDAC) in the mitochondrial outer membrane close to block uptake and release of bilayer-impermeant metabolites but not membrane-permeant AcAld, which prevents futile ATP hydrolysis by depolarized mitochondria, inhibits β-oxidation of fatty acids, and promotes steatosis [78,79].

Depolarized mitochondria are well recognized to be targeted for autophagy, a process called mitophagy [[80], [81], [82], [83]]. After acute, chronic, and chronic binge alcohol treatments, mitophagy increases principally in hepatocytes with mtDepo, and mitochondria can be visualized entering autophagosomes [68]. Chronically, however, an overburden of mitophagy and the impaired processing of mitophagosomes by lysosomes leads to release mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs) like mitochondrial DNA that activate hepatic stellate cells and innate immune system cells like Kupffer cells to cause fibrosis and steatohepatitis [48,73,81,84].

ALD, which can progress to alcohol-associated steatohepatitis (ASH), interacts synergistically with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), a condition that can advances to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Previously, MASLD and MASH were known as non-alcohol-associated fatty liver disease (NAFLD) and non-alcohol-associated steatohepatitis (NASH), respectively [85]. The updated nomenclature also introduces “MASLD and increased alcohol intake” (MetALD) to acknowledge that, in many patients, both alcohol consumption and metabolic syndrome contribute to steatosis and steatohepatitis [86,87].

ALD/ASH and MASLD/MASH share similar histopathological features and synergize in producing steatohepatitis and liver fibrosis in both humans and animal models [[86], [87], [88], [89], [90]]. Human MASLD/MASH is closely linked to obesity and Western diets high in fat, cholesterol and fructose. Similar to alcohol exposure, Western diet feeding in mice induces hepatocellular mtDepo and mitophagy, leading to release of mtDAMPs [91] Aldehydes appear to be key drivers of mtDepo and mitophagy, as they are generated through beta-scission of lipid peroxides formed during oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation [92,93] Thus, aldehyde-driven mtDepo with consequent dysregulated mitophagy leading to mtDAMP release constitute a shared pathogenesis for both ALD and MASLD/MASH. Oxidative stress likely amplifies aldehyde-driven mtDepo and mitophagy in ALD, given that ‘loosely coupled’ CYP2E1 functions as a source of O2•- [[94], [95], [96]]. This CYP2E1-derived oxidant stress not only amplifies aldehyde production but also acts directly to impair lysosomal degradation capacity and autophagic flux [43,56]. ROS generated during alcohol metabolism can denature lysosomal membranes and inactivate hydrolases, promoting accumulation of mitochondrial and protein debris. These redox injuries synergize with aldehyde-induced mtDepo and mitophagy, leading to the buildup of autophagic intermediates and triggering pro-inflammatory responses.

Changes in autophagy-associated with immune responses

The Gao-binge acute-on-chronic alcohol exposure [97] increases autophagosome formation and flux in mouse livers but decreases hepatic TFEB levels, reducing lysosome numbers and impairing lysosomal function [98]. In a related animal model that uses chronic alcohol feeding followed by LPS treatment, pre-exposure to alcohol sensitizes the liver to LPS-induced inflammation and steatosis through dysregulated autophagosome formation and increased Toll-Like Receptor 4 (TLR4) and Myeloid Differentiation Factor 2 (MD2) interaction [99]. Earlier findings showed that suppression of autophagy sensitizes Kupffer cells to endotoxins [100]. Autophagic dysfunction in non-parenchymal hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), enhances liver disease progression, particularly fibrosis. In vitro studies using HSC-T6, an immortalized rat HSC line, suggest that autophagy induction may contribute to alcohol-induced HSC activation. Additionally, blocking autophagy in stellate cells may attenuate fibrosis [101].

Conversely, macrophage autophagy has a protective role against alcohol-induced liver damage. Alcohol increases gut permeability, allowing pathogenic antigens to trigger a pro-inflammatory response in hepatic macrophages, thereby exacerbating liver injury. Suppression of autophagy in Kupffer cells from steatotic livers enhances inflammatory responses to endotoxins [100]. Impaired macrophage autophagy correlates with increased pro-inflammatory M1 polarization and reduced anti-inflammatory M2 polarization, contributing to heightened liver inflammation [102]. Chronic alcohol plus LPS treatment in Atg5Δmye mice and 6-week alcohol-feeding in Atg7Δmye mice causes increased mortality and liver injury, which was attributed to inflammasome activation and elevated Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) production [27,29]. Enhanced injury in Atg7Δmye mice is further linked to defects in interferon regulatory factor 1 degradation and impaired mitochondrial clearance [27]. Additionally, Atg5 deletion in macrophages abolishes the protective role of Cannabinoid Receptor Type 2 (CB2) in ALD [103]. These findings highlight the importance of macrophage autophagy in protecting the liver from alcohol-induced damage by supporting defense mechanisms and preventing further injury.

Direct lysosomal damage in macrophages also contributes to ALD. Aldehyde-adducted proteins resulting from redox changes induced by chronic alcohol oxidation [104], bind to macrophages and cause lysosomal protease leakage, inflammasome activation, IL-1β secretion, and macrophage cell death [105]. Chronic alcohol-induced macrophage cell death has been reported widely, with some evidence suggesting a protective role for Kupffer cell loss in ALD [106] However, the necrotic cells, which are rich in cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns, may exacerbate inflammation. Similarly, autophagy deletion in Cluster of Differentiation 11c Positive (CD11c+) cells increases pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, particularly IL-23 pathway genes [50]. Across immune cell types, redox stress–induced lysosomal remodeling appears to be a unifying driver of inflammatory responses, linking oxidative injury to inflammasome activation and cytokine release.

Second hits

Alcohol exacerbates liver damage in the presence of secondary factors such as hepatitis C virus (HCV) or human immunodeficiency virus (HIV). Alcohol exposure either enhances HCV replication or induces HIV accumulation in hepatocytes, increasing intracellular viral protein expression, oxidative stress, and extracellular vesicle (EV) release [5,107,108]. These EVs, including exosomes and apoptotic bodies, are secreted due to alcohol-induced lysosomal dysfunction. When engulfed by macrophages or Hepatic Stellate Cells (HSCs), EVs activate liver non-parenchymal cells [58,109,110].

For example, combined treatment with AcAld and HIV induces lysosomal damage in hepatocytes, leading to cathepsin leakage, mitochondrial apoptosis, and the release of apoptotic bodies. This lysosomal damage is exacerbated by impaired TFEB trafficking and reduced lysosomal biogenesis [111] Liver macrophages internalize apoptotic bodies, triggering inflammasome activation, while HSCs uptake apoptotic bodies, promoting fibrogenesis [59,112]. Additionally, exosomes from alcohol-exposed hepatocytes under oxidative stress activate macrophages, to drive inflammation and fibrosis even in the absence of viral infection [108]. These findings underscore how extracellular vesicle (EV) release, regulated by lysosomal changes in hepatocytes, contributes to liver disease progression during alcohol exposure by activating non-parenchymal liver cells.

Critical highlights

A key but underappreciated factor in alcohol-induced autophagy dysregulation is redox imbalance. Alcohol metabolism via ADH and CYP2E1 increases oxidative stress, which differentially modulates autophagy depending on exposure duration [43,56]. Acute redox bursts may activate autophagy through mTORC1 inhibition, but sustained oxidant stress inhibits AMPK, impairs lysosomal acidification, blocks autophagosome-lysosome fusion, and dysregulate TFEB-mediated lysosome biogenesis [56]. These redox-sensitive changes drive the shift from protective to dysregulated autophagy in ALD. A schematic summary of these liver-specific mechanisms is presented in Fig. 2.

Schematic illustration of the impact of acute and chronic alcohol on autophagy in the liver.

In hepatocytes, alcohol (ethanol/EtOH) is metabolized by ADH and CYP2E1 into AcAld. CYP2E1 is a major source of ROS during alcohol metabolism, producing superoxide (O2•-) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), with hydroxyl radical (•OH) generated downstream via the Fenton/Haber–Weiss reaction. These ROS, together with reactive nitrogen species (e.g., peroxynitrite) formed from the reaction of O2•- with nitric oxide, contribute to oxidative modification of proteins, lipids, and organelle membranes. Acute EtOH metabolism causes mitochondrial depolarization (mtDepo) and lipid accumulation. It also promotes TFEB nuclear translocation, which activates transcription of autophagy-related genes (ATGs) and lysosome biogenesis. These processes enhance mitophagy and help minimize EtOH-induced liver injury. In contrast, chronic EtOH exposure disrupts lysosomal function and dysregulates TFEB, thereby promoting hepatocellular damage. Persistent AcAld production, together with lipid peroxidation–derived reactive aldehydes (e.g., 4-HNE, MDA), induces tubulin acetylation and protein adduction, impairing microtubule polymerization and disrupting autophagosome (AV)–lysosome (LYS) fusion. This fusion defect impairs degradation of lipids and proteins, contributing to steatosis and proteopathy, which are hallmark features of alcohol-induced liver disease. Chronic EtOH exposure likely increases lysosomal pH by interfering with the proton pump that maintains the acidic environment of the lysosomal lumen, thereby reducing degradative capacity. It also promotes lysosomal membrane fragility. Reactive aldehydes from alcohol metabolism are also likely to damage lysosomal membranes (indicated in the figure with a question mark to reflect this proposed mechanism). Comorbid conditions such as HIV and HCV infection further exacerbate lysosomal dysfunction by damaging the lysosomal membrane, leading to leakage of lysosomal contents. This leakage, particularly of Cathepsins, causes mitochondrial injury, promotes reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, and triggers mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) release. These events initiate apoptosis and lead to the release of apoptotic bodies, mtDNA, and extracellular vehicles (EVs). These components are taken up by hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) and macrophages, amplifying liver injury. EtOH enhances autophagy in HSCs, providing energy and cytoprotection that support their transition to a fibrogenic phenotype. Autophagy's response to alcohol is dynamic and shaped by multiple factors, including drinking patterns, duration of exposure, alcohol clearance rates, oxidative burden, metabolic adaptation, and the specific autophagy component or gene affected [[44], [113]]. This complexity is highly relevant in humans. Autophagy is not a simple on–off process but a coordinated, multi-step pathway involving initiation, cargo recognition, vesicle trafficking, fusion, and degradation. Under sustained alcohol exposure, these steps become increasingly dynamic, and pathology arises not only from the direction of change but also from persistent disturbances in steady-state activity that progressively undermine autophagic and lysosomal integrity.

In experimental models, acute alcohol exposure can transiently enhance autophagy and aid in recycling damaged components, often interpreted as protective. However, organelle injury and adaptive responses occur in parallel. Lysosome-dependent autophagy is particularly sensitive to alcohol, with activity altered within hours of alcohol exposure in liver cells both in vitro and in vivo. These early changes overlap with activation of the lysosomal stress response, including TFEB-dependent lysosomal changes aimed at sustaining lysosomal function within injured hepatocytes.

This stress response is accompanied by early injury to organelles essential for lysosomal function. The Golgi apparatus becomes disorganized, impairing delivery of enzymes and membrane proteins to lysosomes. Mitochondrial function, required for utilizing recycled fatty acids, declines, contributing to ROS/RNS generation and redox imbalance, which further exacerbate lysosomal stress. Other trafficking pathways critical for cargo delivery and autophagosome–lysosome fusion also become dysregulated. As alcohol oxidation continues, the lysosomal stress response persists, shifting from a compensatory mechanism to a driver of pathology. Strategies that attenuate alcohol oxidation and CYP2E1 activity, thereby reducing ROS/RNS production, or antioxidant interventions that alleviate lysosomal stress, may help limit lysosome-dependent hepatocellular dysfunction.

Early disruption by alcohol, regardless of whether autophagy markers appear increased or decreased, can destabilize the pathway and set the stage for disease onset with continued alcohol exposure. The key question is not whether alcohol increases or decreases autophagy in absolute terms, but how the nature, timing, and persistence of disruptions at distinct steps of the autophagy–lysosome pathway align with and drive disease progression. In animal models, interpreting autophagy solely in binary terms may oversimplify the problem, as flux provides a snapshot of net throughput and cannot fully capture asynchronous regulation within the pathway. For example, lysosomal activity may increase during a lysosomal stress response via TFEB, yet biosynthetic lysosomal proteins may be misdirected due to Golgi disorganization, leading to abnormal morphological changes and membrane damage. Such asynchronous changes can temporarily preserve autophagy flux while masking progressive structural and functional deterioration of the lysosomal system which can contribute to tissue injury.

Thus, effective therapy will require pinpointing when the lysosomal stress response shifts from compensatory to harmful, identifying the most vulnerable steps in the autophagy–lysosome pathway, and targeting them to relieve stress and attenuate hepatocellular injury in alcohol-associated liver disease. Achieving this will require a clearer definition of the mechanisms driving lysosomal stress, clarifying their role in disease progression, and understanding the contributions of selective autophagy pathways and cell-type-specific regulation. Rigorous pre-clinical studies in diverse alcohol exposure models, validated with clinically relevant human samples, are needed before effective autophagy-targeted therapies can be developed.

Gastrointestinal tract

Impact of alcohol on the gastrointestinal barrier

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract has a vital role in digestion, nutrient absorption, and immune defense, while its barrier function protects the body from harmful substances and pathogens. The multilayered intestinal barrier system is composed of an extracellular mucus layer, adjacent to the intestinal lumen. Mucus is produced by goblet cells which trap pathogens and shield the underlying epithelial cells. The epithelial layer, (beneath the mucus layer) is tightly sealed by junctional complexes. The epithelial layer includes specialized cells, including enterocytes for nutrient transport and Paneth cells, which release antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) that control intestinal microbial populations. Beneath the epithelial (luminal) layer, the mucosal immune system provides defense and regulates immune responses. Together, these layers ensure selective permeability, microbial homeostasis, and effective immune protection.

The GI system also plays a central role in alcohol absorption. While a small portion (7–9 %) of consumed alcohol undergoes “first-pass” metabolism in the stomach [114], the majority is absorbed by the small intestine, primarily in the duodenum and jejunum. This occurs due to their extensive surface area and rich blood supply. Alcohol then diffuses passively through epithelial cell membranes into the capillaries. Once absorbed, alcohol enters the portal circulation to the liver, where it is metabolized by hepatic alcohol metabolizing enzymes. A recent study also revealed that the liver-gut axis synergistically regulates both systemic AcAld clearance and voluntary alcohol consumption [74].

Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts the delicate mucosal lining of the GI tract. It damages epithelial cell membranes, increases gut permeability, and induces structural changes, such as villus atrophy, goblet cell hyperplasia, and a reduction in tight junction proteins. These disruptions elevate intestinal permeability, allowing endotoxins, bacterial and fungal products to translocate into the systemic circulation. Such translocation triggers systemic inflammation, exacerbates liver damage, and contributes to multi-organ injury [115,116].

This disruption is likely exacerbated by oxidant stress generated locally in the intestinal mucosa. Chronic alcohol exposure induces CYP2E1 protein in the intestinal epithelium [117], which catalyzes alcohol metabolism to produce ROS, including O2•-, H2O2 and downstream reactive nitrogen species such as ONOO−. These ROS impair tight junction integrity and cytoskeletal stability, leading to oxidative barrier disruption and increased paracellular permeability [118]. Importantly, CYP2E1-derived oxidative stress is a key upstream initiating factor that drives overexpression of redox-sensitive circadian proteins Circadian Locomotor Output Cycles Kaput (CLOCK) and Period Circadian Regulator 2 (PER2) in intestinal epithelial cells. Notably, alcohol-induced CLOCK and PER2 upregulation is prevented by CYP2E1 knockdown or antioxidant treatment (e.g., N-acetylcysteine), confirming that this is a ROS-dependent process [119,120]. This circadian dysregulation, driven by alcohol-induced ROS, not only disrupts epithelial homeostasis and junctional protein expression but also interferes with autophagy-related signaling pathways [121]. Emerging evidence suggests that CLOCK and PER2 modulate autophagy machinery by influencing mTOR and AMPK activity; thus, their redox-mediated dysregulation links oxidative stress to impaired autophagy. [122]. Genetic knockdown or pharmacologic inhibition of CYP2E1 or antioxidant treatment prevents CLOCK and PER2 upregulation and attenuates alcohol-induced hyperpermeability in both in vitro and in vivo models [120,123]. These findings highlight an emerging redox–circadian–autophagy axis as a critical regulator of alcohol-induced intestinal barrier failure.

Autophagy in intestinal epithelial cell function and barrier protection

Enterocytes

In enterocytes, autophagy regulates tight junction (TJ) proteins, alleviates cellular stress, and prevents cell death. Toll-like receptors (TLRs) on the cell surface detect microbial ligands and activate autophagy via Myeloid Differentiation Primary Response Gene 88 (MyD88), Beclin 1 (BECN1), and related proteins, leading to autophagosome formation. This process facilitates xenophagy, where ubiquitinated pathogens, such as Salmonella Typhimurium, are recognized by autophagic receptors and eventually degraded in lysosomes, thus, preventing bacterial replication, reducing inflammation, and preserving barrier function [124].

Autophagy also modulates intestinal permeability by targeting claudin-2 (CLDN2), a pore-forming TJ protein, for lysosomal degradation. CLDN2 is upregulated by Tumor Necrosis Factor (TNF) and degraded through ATG16L1-and ATG7-mediated recruitment of LC3. Since CLDN2 facilitates paracellular ion and solute movement, its degradation by autophagy attenuates intestinal permeability. Conversely, mutations in ATG16L1 impair this process, leading to increased pathogen persistence and epithelial damage [125].

Additionally, autophagy mitigates ER stress by degrading misfolded proteins via the BECN1 complex, preventing unfolded protein accumulation [126]. It also inhibits TNF-induced apoptosis and necroptosis by degrading pro-apoptotic proteins like Receptor-Interacting Protein Kinase 3 (RIPK3), thus maintaining barrier integrity during inflammation or injury.

Paneth cells

In Paneth cells, autophagy proteins such as nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein-2 (NOD2), ATG16L1, receptor-interacting protein kinase-2 (RIPK2) and Ras-related protein Rab-2A (RAB2A) regulate the secretion and sorting of antimicrobial peptides and proteins, including lysozyme. NOD2 activation by bacterial muramyl dipeptide recruits ATG16L1 to dense core vesicles via the RIPK2 and RAB2A pathways, ensuring proper sorting of lysozyme [127]. Under stress conditions (e.g. bacterial infections), secretory autophagy bypasses the Golgi apparatus, redirecting antimicrobial peptides for secretion [128]. This process depends on eukaryotic translation factor 2 alpha (EIF2A) and eukaryotic initiation factor 2 alpha kinase-3 (EIF2AK3) signaling pathways, which mediate the ER stress response and autophagy activation. Dysfunctions in autophagy, such as caused by ATG16L1 mutations, impair lysozyme packaging and secretion, leading to dysbiosis and susceptibility to inflammation [129,130].

Goblet cells

In goblet cells, autophagy supports mucin production by mitigating ER stress and ensuring proper folding and secretion of mucin proteins. Proteins like BECN1 and LC3 regulate the degradation of misfolded proteins and maintain ER homeostasis. ER stress induces autophagy activation via AMP-activated kinase (AMPK) and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) signaling pathways, promoting lysosomal degradation of improperly folded mucins. Autophagy deficiencies reduce mucin production and lead to a thinner mucus barrier, exposing epithelium to microbial invasion and inflammation. Mechanistically, LC3-II binds to misfolded mucins, directing them to autophagosomes for degradation [126].

Alcohol-induced autophagy dysfunction in gastric mucosa

Alcohol-induced gastric injury disrupts autophagy through multiple mechanisms, with variations observed across different experimental models and conditions. Alcohol commonly disrupts autophagic flux by inhibiting the AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)/mTOR signaling axis. Specifically, alcohol suppresses AMPK phosphorylation, which prevents the activation of autophagy, while promoting mTOR activation, a known inhibitor of autophagy initiation. This dual effect reduces the expression of autophagy markers such as LC3-II and Beclin-1, while leading to SQSTM1/p62accumulation, indicating impaired autophagic clearance [131,132]. Consequently, autophagy disruption by alcohol increases production of ROS, it exacerbates mitochondrial dysfunction and promotes apoptosis by reducing the B-cell lymphoma 2-Bcl-2-associate X protein (Bcl-2/Bax) ratio and activating caspase-3 [133]. In addition to lysosomal dysfunction, alcohol induces oxidative damage via mitochondrial ROS and Inducible Nitric Oxide Synthase (iNOS) activation [134]. These ROS further destabilize tight junctions and activate redox-sensitive transcription factors like Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), amplifying epithelial injury and inflammation [134]. Moreover, AcAld formed in the gut lumen, along with ROS, synergistically promotes ER stress and tight junction protein degradation, further impairing autophagic flux and epithelial homeostasis [117].

While most studies demonstrate that alcohol disrupts autophagic flux and exacerbates gastric injury, some findings reveal alcohol-induced autophagy as an adaptive response that actually limits oxidative stress, decreases lipid peroxidation, and preserves mitochondrial integrity, particularly when mTOR signaling is suppressed. The differences between these. observations arise from variations in alcohol exposure duration, the alcohol concentration used, and experimental models, such as Wistar rats versus human gastric epithelial cells [131,132].

Pharmacological intervention with rebamipide, a gastroprotective agent commonly used to treat gastric mucosal damage and ulcers, highlights the therapeutic potential of restoring autophagy. Rebamipide enhances autophagy by activating mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways, specifically through phospho-extracellular signal-regulated kinase (p-ERK and independently of Beclin-1 and mTOR signaling. This activation increases LC3-II and Atg5/Atg7 expression, reduces SQSTM1/p62 accumulation, and mitigates oxidative stress and ER stress, thereby protecting gastric epithelial cells from alcohol-induced apoptosis and inflammation [135]. These findings collectively underscore the critical role of AMPK/mTOR suppression and MAPK activation in modulating autophagy and protecting the gastric mucosa during alcohol exposure. The studies converge on the importance of autophagy in maintaining gastric integrity, with the discrepancies primarily reflecting model-specific alcohol effects and signaling pathway activation.

Role of autophagy in immune response

Autophagy changes caused by alcohol significantly impact immune responses in gastric injury by promoting inflammation and disrupting cellular homeostasis. Impaired autophagy flux, characterized by reduced LC3-II levels and SQSTM1/p62 accumulation, activates the Nuclear Factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) pathway and the NOD-, LRR-, and pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, driving the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18 [136]. Defective autophagy also hinders the clearance of damaged mitochondria, leading to the release of DAMPs, like mtDNA and ROS, which amplify innate immune activation via TLRs. Additionally, disrupted autophagy hinders antigen presentation, alters T cell responses, and prolongs inflammation by failing to degrade inflammasome components and misfolded proteins, which exacerbates ER stress and cytokine production. This immune dysregulation contributes to chronic gastric inflammation, microbial dysbiosis, and impaired pathogen clearance. Restoring autophagy, as demonstrated by agents like rebamipide, mitigates these effects by reducing inflammasome activation, oxidative stress, and cytokine release, highlighting autophagy as a therapeutic target in alcohol-induced gastric injury [135].

Critical highlights

Alcohol disrupts autophagy in the gastric mucosa by inhibiting AMPK activation, which promotes autophagy and activates mTOR to suppress autophagy. These competing events result in impaired autophagic flux, oxidant stress, apoptosis, and inflammation. A schematic summary of these gastric mechanisms is presented in Fig. 3. While agents such as rebamipide restore autophagy via MAPK activation and protect against gastric injury, its antioxidant properties and ability to scavenge ROS suggest that targeting oxidative stress may also alleviate gastric inflammation and barrier injury. However, the effects of alcohol on autophagy in the small intestine and colon, key sites of alcohol absorption and injury remain understudied. Future research should focus on chronic alcohol feeding models and human clinical samples to better understand the impact of alcohol on autophagy throughout the GI tract and identify translational therapeutic targets.

Pancreas

Pancreatitis is a common, life-threatening disease of the exocrine pancreas and the third most frequent cause of hospital admissions for gastrointestinal diseases in the United States, imposing a significant burden on the healthcare system, with an annual economic cost of $3.7 billion [137]. It is also a major risk factor for pancreatic cancer [[138], [139], [140]].

The pathogenesis of pancreatitis remains poorly understood, and treatment options are limited to supportive care. The disease progresses through three major forms: acute pancreatitis (AP), recurrent AP (RAP), and chronic pancreatitis (CP). While AP is often a single episode, it can predispose the pancreas to RAP, which may further progress to CP [141]. Growing evidence suggests that all clinical forms of pancreatitis share common underlying mechanisms [142]. The established paradigm posits that AP originates from acinar cell injury, triggering inflammatory cell infiltration, parenchymal necrosis, and systemic inflammation. Key pathological features of AP include elevated serum amylase and lipase levels, acute pancreatic inflammation, and parenchymal cell death. In contrast, CP is characterized by chronic inflammation, pancreatic atrophy, fibrosis, and acinar-ductal metaplasia.

Epidemiological studies indicate that alcohol misuse is a major risk factor for all three forms of pancreatitis, accounting for 40–70 % of cases, with variation across countries [[143], [144], [145], [146], [147]]. Despite extensive research, the precise mechanisms of alcohol-associated pancreatitis remain unclear.

Autophagy in pancreatitis

The primary physiological function of acinar cells is to synthesize, store, and secrete digestive enzymes [142]. This process relies on the coordinated actions of cytoplasmic organelles: the endoplasmic reticulum facilitates enzyme synthesis, the Golgi apparatus sorts and packs these enzymes into secretory granules, and mitochondria provide the necessary energy. With continuous activity, some organelles inevitably become damaged; in a healthy pancreas, autophagy efficiently removes these damaged organelles to maintain cellular function. However, impaired autophagic degradation is a common pathological feature in human, experimental, alcohol, and non-alcohol-associated pancreatitis, contributing to disease progression [[148], [149], [150], [151], [152], [153], [154]].

Mechanisms of autophagy impairment in alcohol-associated pancreatitis

Recent studies have identified several molecular mechanisms that disrupt autophagy in human and experimental alcohol-associated pancreatitis. First, alcohol associated pancreatitis reduces autophagosome formation in the pancreas. The underlying mechanisms include a dramatic decrease in Vacuole Membrane Protein 1 (VMP1), a protein essential for autophagosome closure [155], and upregulation of Atg4B, a cysteine protease that downregulates the autophagy mediator LC3-II [149]. Second, alcohol reduces lysosomal degradative capacity by decreasing the lysosomal membrane protein LAMP-2, a central mediator of lysosomal proteolytic activity [153,156]. Third, alcohol associated-pancreatitis causes defective autophagosome-lysosome fusion, triggered by the pancreatitis-induced translocation of the SNARE protein Synaptosomal-Associated Protein 23 kDa (SNAP23) to the autophagosome, which disrupts this fusion process [157,158]. Importantly, chronic alcohol exposure also impairs autophagy through redox-mediated mechanisms. Alcohol metabolism in pancreatic acinar cells via ADH and CYP2E1 generates AcAld and ROS [159,160], which reduce glutathione levels, damage lysosomal membranes, and destabilize zymogen granules. This oxidative stress compromises lysosome function and autophagosome clearance, thereby blocking flux and promoting the accumulation of undegraded autophagic vacuoles [159]. Alcohol reduces autophagosome formation in pancreatic acinar cells, both in a mouse model of alcoholic pancreatitis induced by a combination of EtOH diet and cerulein (a CCK ortholog) and in EtOH + CCK-treated acinar cells (ex vivo model). Alcohol treatment decreased pancreatic levels of LC3-II, a key marker of autophagosomes. This was attributed to alcohol-induced upregulation of ATG4B, a redox-sensitive cysteine protease that regulates the balance between cytosolic LC3-I and membrane-bound LC3-II [161]. Although specific ROS species involved has not been identified yet, ATG4B has been shown in other systems to be selectively activated or inhibited by hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) via reversible cysteine oxidation [162]. Thus, it is plausible that alcohol-induced redox imbalance, potentially involving H2O2, contributes to ATG4B-mediated LC3-II deconjugation and impaired autophagosome formation.

Finally, alcohol-associated pancreatitis leads to a marked reduction in TFEB, indicating a loss of transcriptional control of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy [163,164]. Of note, autophagy defects in alcohol and non-alcohol-associated pancreatitis are largely similar. Reductions in VMP1, LAMP2, and TFEB have been observed in experimental models of both alcohol and non-alcohol-associated pancreatitis, as well as in human disease [153,155,156,163,164]. Additionally, experimental models of non-alcohol-associated pancreatitis and human disease exhibit defects in the N-Acetylglucosamine-1-Phosphotransferase Subunits Alpha and Beta (GNPTab)-mediated pathway of hydrolase delivery to lysosomes [150], though the effect of alcohol-associated pancreatitis on this pathway has not yet been tested.

Detrimental consequences of autophagy/lysosomal disruption in alcohol-induced pancreatitis

Several genetic models targeting different steps in the autophagy process have been developed to assess the impact of autophagy deficiency on the pancreas. These models consistently demonstrate that the blockade of autophagy in the pancreas triggers pancreatitis [142,165]. Specifically, mice with genetic ablation of autophagy-related proteins such as Atg5, Atg7, or VMP1—which hinder autophagosome formation—mice with the genetic deletion of Lamp2 and Gnptab, which disrupt lysosomal proteolytic activity, and mice lacking TFEB all progressively develop CP. Pancreatitis in autophagy-deficient mice presents the same pathological manifestations as alcohol and non-alcohol-associated pancreatitis, including trypsinogen activation, acinar cell death, fibrosis, and inflammation, thus revealing a primary role of autophagy impairment in disease initiation and progression [[148], [149], [150], [151],153,154].

The blockade of pancreatic autophagy results in the accumulation of dysfunctional organelles, such as mitochondria, ER, lysosomes, protein aggregates, and fragile zymogen granules in acinar cells [142]. In particular, loss of VMP1 leads to the accumulation of abnormal ER structures in acinar cells and induces ER stress markers [155], including X-box Binding Protein 1 (XBP1) and C/EBP Homologous Protein (CHOP), which are known risk factors for alcohol-associated pancreatitis [166]. Lysosomal dysfunction in LAMP2 KO mice causes incomplete degradation of zymogen granule components, leading to inappropriate accumulation of trypsin in acinar cells, a signature response of pancreatitis [153,156]. Inhibition of autophagy in acinar cells with overexpressed ATG4B exacerbated necrosis, a major type of cell death in pancreatitis [149]. Among these dysfunctional organelles, mitochondria play a central role in amplifying pancreatic injury, particularly under alcohol exposure. Treatment with alcohol stimulates opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), resulting in mitochondrial depolarization, ATP depletion, and necrosis in pancreatic acinar cells [167]. Alcohol sensitizes mitochondria to mPTP opening by lowering the NAD+/NADH ratio, a process mediated by alcohol metabolism. While the precise link in pancreatic acinar cells requires further validation, mitochondrial depolarization and NAD+ depletion are well-established triggers of excessive O2−• generation from mitochondrial complexes I and III, which can further react with nitric oxide to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a highly reactive nitrogen species. Without adequate mitophagic clearance, damaged mitochondria persist, leading to sustained ROS/RNS production and worsening of pancreatic injury. These downstream effects place redox imbalance upstream of defective autophagy in the context of alcohol-induced pancreatic injury.

Additionally, TFEB KO mice exhibited a significant reduction in Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma Coactivator 1-alpha (PGC1alpha), a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis [164]. Taken together, these data strongly suggest that organellar dysfunction mediates the detrimental effects of autophagy failure on the pancreas [142].

Autophagy-dependent immune response

Inflammatory infiltration is a major complication of alcohol-associated pancreatitis and a key determinant of disease severity; however, its underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood [152]. Genetic models mimicking autophagy defects in pancreatitis, such as VMP1, LAMP2, and TFEB knockout (KO) mice, develop a massive inflammatory response. Similar to experimental pancreatitis, macrophage-driven inflammation in these models is associated with a surge in cytokines and chemokines. These findings suggest that impaired autophagy plays a mediatory role in triggering the inflammatory response in pancreatitis.

Autophagy serves as a critical antioxidant defense mechanism by removing dysfunctional mitochondria, thereby limiting intracellular ROS accumulation. This function is particularly vital in the pancreas, as demonstrated by multiple genetic models in which autophagy is selectively ablated. Pancreas-specific deletion of ATG5 [168], ATG7 [169], IKKα [170] and VMP1 [171] each result in oxidative stress and pancreatic inflammation. While these studies do not identify specific ROS species, mitochondrial-derived superoxide and hydrogen peroxide are the most likely contributors, based on the role of mitophagy loss in redox imbalance. Elevated ROS in these models activates inflammatory signaling via two major pathways [172]: (1) stimulation of redox-sensitive transcription factors such as NF-kB, and (2) activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to IL-1β production and exacerbation of pancreatic injury.

Critical highlights

Autophagy dysfunction drives alcohol-associated pancreatitis by impairing VMP1, LAMP2, and TFEB, disrupting lysosomal degradation and autophagosome-lysosome fusion. This leads to organelle dysfunction, ER stress, mitochondrial damage, and toxic protein accumulation, exacerbating inflammation and acinar cell injury. These novel therapeutic strategies should be evaluated in preclinical models and clinical trials.

Heart

Role of autophagy in cardiovascular health and disease

Autophagy is a fundamental cellular process that preserves cardiovascular homeostasis by degrading damaged organelles, misfolded proteins, and other cytoplasmic components. In the heart, basal levels of autophagy are essential for maintaining cardiomyocyte integrity, ensuring mitochondrial quality control, and preventing the accumulation of toxic protein aggregates [[173], [174], [175], [176], [177], [178], [179]]. Dysregulation of this tightly controlled process has been implicated in the development and progression of various cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic heart disease, hypertensive cardiomyopathy, and heart failure [[173], [174], [175], [176], [177],179].

Although the importance of autophagy in cardiovascular disease is well established, its precise role remains complex and context dependent [[176], [177], [178]]. While enhanced autophagic activity may attenuate ischemia-reperfusion injury and slow the progression of heart failure, excessive or misregulated autophagy has also been associated with increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis and maladaptive remodeling [180]. Similarly, under conditions of hypertrophic stress, autophagy facilitates cardiac adaptation by eliminating dysfunctional mitochondria and mitigating oxidative injury. Yet, if persistently elevated, autophagy can exacerbate pathological remodeling and compromise myocardial function. In dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), autophagy is often upregulated as an early adaptive response to cellular stress. Initially protective, sustained or excessive autophagic activity may become detrimental, leading to the degradation of essential cellular components, cardiomyocyte loss, and interstitial fibrosis. These paradoxical effects highlight the need for a nuanced understanding of autophagy's role in specific disease contexts—particularly in alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy, where its regulation and consequences.

Alcohol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction: mechanisms

Both acute and chronic alcohol consumption can significantly impact cardiovascular function and microcirculation, with effects that are often time- and dose-dependent [[181], [182], [183]]. Chronic alcohol use is a well-recognized risk factor for cardiovascular disease, contributing to a spectrum of conditions including alcohol-associated cardiomyopathy, hypertension, arrhythmias, and ischemic heart disease. Alcohol-associated cardiomyopathy is marked by left ventricular dilation, myocardial wall thinning, impaired contractility, and mitochondrial dysfunction [[184], [185], [186], [187]].

The pathogenesis of alcohol-induced cardiovascular injury and dysfunction involves a multifaceted network of interrelated mechanisms. Central to this process are alcohol metabolism, increased oxidative and nitrative stress, mitochondrial damage, and disrupted calcium homeostasis. These are compounded by chronic inflammation, parenchymal cell death, impaired protein synthesis, and dysregulated lipid metabolism [181,183,188]. Dysfunction of the autonomic nervous system, along with activation of the renin-angiotensin and sympathetic pathways, and endocrine imbalances, further amplify cardiovascular injury. Emerging research increasingly underscores the pivotal role of the liver in the development and progression of cardiovascular complications [189]. Together, these pathological events contribute to adverse remodeling of cardiac and vascular structures, reduce cardiovascular reserve capacity, and ultimately lead to heart failure when compensatory systems are overwhelmed [186,190].

Redox imbalance is a central upstream mediator of alcohol-induced pathology in cardiac and vascular tissues, characterized by excessive generation of ROS/RNS and compromised antioxidant defense mechanisms [181,183,187,189,190]. Principal sources of ROS/RNS include alcohol metabolism, primarily via CYP2E1 and ADH, mitochondrial respiratory dysfunction, NADPH oxidase activation, and upregulation of iNOS. The intermediate metabolite AcAld, predominantly produced in the liver, further amplifies ROS production. ROS species implicated in alcohol-induced cardiovascular injury include O2•-, H2O2, and •OH [181,183,187,[189], [190], [191]]. In parallel, the reactive nitrogen species ONOO− formed via the near diffusion-limited reaction between O2•- and NO• has been strongly associated with alcohol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction [181,183,192]. Due to NO• high diffusibility and the rapid kinetics of its reaction with O2•-, ONOO− can form even when its precursors are generated in different cellular or subcellular compartments. These ROS and RNS drive mitochondrial depolarization, lipid peroxidation, and the accumulation of reactive aldehydes (e.g., 4-HNE, MDA), which in turn propagate oxidative, nitrosative, and nitrative modifications of critical proteins. These molecular alterations contribute to cardiomyocyte and endothelial cell death, maladaptive structural remodeling, and impaired myocardial contractility mechanisms [181,183,187,189,190,192].

Redox stress modulates autophagy in a context-dependent manner. Acute alcohol exposure causes a transient increase in ROS, which inhibits mTORC1 and activates autophagy to remove damaged mitochondria. In contrast, chronic alcohol exposure results in sustained oxidative stress that impairs lysosomal acidification by disrupting v-ATPase activity and destabilizing lysosomal membranes [193,194]. This impairs autophagic flux, leading to autophagosome accumulation and incomplete mitophagy. Oxidative modifications, such as carbonylation and S-glutathionylation of LC3, Beclin-1, and SNARE proteins, further hinder autophagosome-lysosome fusion [194]. However, alcohol-specific PTM mapping of these regulators remains largely unexplored and represents an important direction for future investigation to strengthen mechanistic links between oxidative injury and autophagy-lysosome dysfunction in alcohol-associated organ damage.

Persistent redox stress also alters autophagy transcriptional regulation. Alcohol inhibits the nuclear translocation of TFEB by inactivating calcineurin and activating GSK3β, thereby suppressing lysosomal biogenesis [195,196]. Reduced TFEB activity exacerbates accumulation of mtDNA and protein aggregates, promoting inflammation and cardiomyocyte apoptosis [190]. Mitochondrial damage induced by ROS and aldehydes triggers mitochondrial depolarization and initiates mitophagy. However, due to lysosomal dysfunction, mitophagosomes are not properly degraded, leading to the release of mtDAMPs, such as mtDNA and cytochrome c. These DAMPs activate TLR9 and the NLRP3 inflammasome [190,191], promoting M1 macrophage polarization and myocardial injury.

Endothelial autophagy is also impaired by ROS generated through CYP2E1 activity, resulting in decreased nitric oxide bioavailability and contributing to atherogenesis [183]. In cardiac fibroblasts, redox imbalance and disrupted autophagy promote myofibroblast activation and fibrosis [190]. Alcohol similarly disrupts autophagy in immune cells. In macrophages, defective mitophagy enhances mtDAMP release and inflammasome activation, increasing IL-1β and TNF-α production. Impaired autophagy in neutrophils and lymphocytes further aggravates myocardial inflammation [191].

Autophagy and alcohol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction

Growing evidence suggests that alcohol consumption can significantly influence autophagy within the cardiovascular system. However, current research remains limited, with only a small number of studies directly addressing this relationship [191,193,197,198]. Interpretation is further complicated by inconsistencies across preclinical models—ranging from in vitro systems to acute and chronic in vivo exposures—and by variations in diet composition, duration of alcohol administration, lack of rigorous functional assessments, and numerous recent retractions in the field.

Preclinical evidence

While acute alcohol exposure may activate protective autophagy, chronic exposure impairs lysosomal function and mitophagy, exacerbating myocardial damage [191,193,194,197,198]. These findings suggest that alcohol may contribute to chronic alcohol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction in part by disrupting autophagic processes. Thus, autophagy appears to play a dual role—serving both protective and harmful functions depending on the context, intensity, and duration of alcohol exposure [190].

Clinical evidence

While direct clinical studies on alcohol's effect on autophagy in human cardiovascular tissues are limited, indirect evidence suggests a correlation. Chronic heavy alcohol consumption is a well-established risk factor for alcohol-associated cardiomyopathy, a condition characterized by weakened heart muscles and impaired cardiac function. The pathogenesis of this disease involves oxidative stress, inflammation, and cellular damage—processes in which autophagy plays a critical regulatory role. Although direct measurements of autophagic activity in human subjects are challenging, the observed clinical outcomes align with preclinical findings that implicate disrupted autophagy in alcohol-related cardiac diseases [190].

In cardiomyocytes, autophagy regulates mitochondrial quality control by removing damaged mitochondria through mitophagy. Acute alcohol exposure may enhance autophagy as a compensatory response to oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction. However, chronic alcohol exposure impairs autophagic flux, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria and increased production of mitochondrial damage-associated molecular patterns (mtDAMPs). This triggers inflammation, promotes cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and exacerbates myocardial damage, contributing to ACM. A key driver of this dysfunction is alcohol-induced oxidative stress, mediated by excessive mitochondrial ROS and the accumulation of reactive aldehydes such as 4-hydroxynonenal (4-HNE) and malondialdehyde (MDA). These aldehydes form adducts with mitochondrial proteins, impair oxidative phosphorylation, and further destabilize mitochondrial membranes [190]. When aldehyde detoxification by ALDH2 is insufficient, redox imbalance worsens, resulting in excessive mitophagy and increased cardiomyocyte loss [199]. Activation of ALDH2 with pharmacologic agents like Alda-1 has been shown to restore mitochondrial redox homeostasis and reduce myocardial injury [190,191,196]. Additionally, nonoxidative alcohol metabolism generates fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs), which accumulate in cardiac mitochondria and induce mitochondrial dysfunction [200], further amplifying oxidative stress and maladaptive mitophagy.

Autophagy in endothelial cells regulates vascular homeostasis, angiogenesis, and inflammation. Chronic alcohol exposure impairs endothelial autophagy, resulting in increased oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability. Alcohol-induced endothelial dysfunction promotes vascular inflammation [183] and accelerates the development of atherosclerosis and hypertension. Autophagy modulates fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition, a critical process in cardiac remodeling. Chronic alcohol exposure promotes fibroblast activation and collagen deposition, contributing to myocardial fibrosis. Dysregulated autophagy in fibroblasts may exacerbate this process by enhancing myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition, further impairing cardiac function.

Autophagy also plays a critical role in modulating immune responses in the heart, which are closely linked to alcohol-induced cardiovascular dysfunction. Autophagy regulates macrophage polarization and limits pro-inflammatory responses. Chronic alcohol exposure impairs macrophage autophagy, promoting M1 polarization and increasing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6. This heightened inflammatory state may contribute to myocardial injury, fibrosis, and remodeling in ACM. Alcohol-induced autophagy dysfunction in neutrophils and lymphocytes exacerbates inflammation and tissue injury. Impaired autophagy in these immune cells reduces their ability to clear pathogens and apoptotic cells, further promoting inflammatory responses in the myocardium.

Clinical and therapeutic implications

Given the complex interplay between ROS/RNS and autophagy in alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy and cardiovascular dysfunction, targeting these interconnected pathways holds considerable therapeutic promise. Redox-based interventions aimed at restoring autophagic flux may help mitigate alcohol-induced cardiac injury.

Mitochondrial ROS, a central mediator of cardiac and vascular damage in alcohol-related disease, represents a particularly attractive therapeutic target. Mitochondria-targeted antioxidants such as MitoQ and SS-31, especially when combined with ALDH2 activators like Alda-1, can effectively scavenge ROS, reduce toxic aldehyde accumulation, and stabilize mitochondrial membranes.

Notably, mitochondrial dysfunction and redox imbalance also contribute to aging-associated cardiac and vascular decline (PMID: 40295347, PMID: 38094682), paralleling mechanisms observed in alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy and skeletal muscle dysfunction [201]. Chronic MitoQ supplementation has been shown to improve vascular function in healthy older adults [202], and more recently, MitoQ was found to protect human cardiomyocytes from oxidative stress–induced mitochondrial dysregulation in vitro [203], further underscoring its translational potential.

Pharmacological activation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) or enhancement of PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy may restore mitochondrial quality control and alleviate myocardial stress. Future studies should prioritize mechanistic investigations into redox-autophagy crosstalk in human alcohol-associated cardiomyopathy and evaluate these targeted therapies in both preclinical and clinical settings.

Additionally, modulating autophagy in immune cells represents a novel avenue to reduce inflammation and tissue damage. Enhancing autophagic activity in macrophages to favor M2 polarization and suppress pro-inflammatory responses may further improve cardiac outcomes in alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy.

Critical highlights and future directions

Alcohol-induced cardiomyopathy is driven by mitochondrial dysfunction, redox imbalance, impaired autophagy, and chronic inflammation. While acute alcohol exposure may transiently activate protective autophagy, chronic exposure disrupts lysosomal function and mitophagy, leading to the accumulation of damaged mitochondria, protein aggregates, and mtDNA. These promote maladaptive remodeling, inflammation, and cardiomyocyte death (Table 1). Autophagy dysfunction also contributes to endothelial dysfunction, fibrosis, and immune activation, particularly via mtDAMP-driven NLRP3 inflammasome activation in macrophages. These mechanisms highlight autophagy as a key regulator of cardiac homeostasis and injury in alcohol-related disease. Therapeutically, restoring mitochondrial function and autophagic flux through agents such as MitoQ, SS-31, Alda-1, or inducers of TFEB and PINK1/Parkin pathways may offer benefit. Enhancing autophagy in immune cells to reduce inflammation is a promising avenue. Future work should focus on clarifying cell-specific autophagy pathways, validating redox-autophagy targets, and translating these findings into effective therapies for alcohol-associated cardiovascular disease.

Adipose tissue

Adipose tissue (AT) is a vital metabolic tissue. AT is recognized for its ability to store excess energy as triglycerides (TG) in lipid droplets and release them as glycerol and non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA) during energy shortages. AT also secretes a range of bioactive substances known as adipokines, including adiponectin, leptin, resistin, cytokines, and chemokines (MCP-1, IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α), which regulate the function of metabolically active peripheral tissues. Alcohol exposure alters both the storage and secretory functions of AT. These effects impact the function of other organs, including the liver.

Alcohol-induced adipose tissue dysfunction

AT dysfunction plays a key role in ALD. Excessive lipolysis and reduced lipogenesis compromise AT storage of lipids. Studies consistently show heavy alcohol consumption reduces AT mass [[204], [205], [206], [207]]. Alcohol consumption increases NEFA release from AT, which is linked to the activation of the hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) and the adipose triglyceride lipase (ATGL) [208], both contributing to ALD pathogenesis [209]. Alcohol consumption also downregulates genes involved in adipogenesis and lipogenesis, such as CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (Cebpa) and Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (Pparg), impairing adipocyte differentiation and function in both subcutaneous (SAT) and visceral AT (VAT) [207,208]. Additionally, alcohol alters fatty acid synthesis enzymes in VAT, further reducing AT mass and increasing NEFA delivery to the liver, exacerbating ALD pathogenesis.

Mediators of adipose tissue dysfunction

Alcohol consumption induces oxidant stress, inflammation, and autophagy changes, which impair AT function. Alcohol increases CYP2E1 expression and activity in adipocytes from VAT, SAT, and after exposure to cultured 3T3-L1 cells [210,211], leading to oxidative stress and DNA damage [210]. In high-fat and alcohol-fed mice, increased CYP2E1 expression in AT is linked to reduced AT mass, decreased perilipin 1 (required for fat storage), and elevated NEFA levels, indicating that oxidant stress drives alcohol-induced lipolysis [212]. Alcohol exposure also activates inflammatory processes in AT. Chronic alcohol feeding promotes lipolysis and increases inflammatory gene expression in rat AT, which can be alleviated by treatment with scopoletin, an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent [213]. Additionally, rosiglitazone, a Peroxisome Proliferator-Activated Receptor Gamma (PPARγ) agonist, reduces alcohol-induced lipolysis and inflammation in AT [207], highlighting the role of inflammation in alcohol-induced lipolysis. Further research is needed to confirm these mechanisms.

Autophagy in adipose tissue dysfunction

Alcohol also induces AT dysfunction by enhancing autophagy. Mice fed chronic alcohol diets showed reduced body weight, AT mass, and protein synthesis in AT, along with upregulated markers of autophagy, including ATG12-5, LC3B–I, LC3B-II, and ULK1 S555. These changes were associated with increased lipolysis, inflammation, and reduced AT lipogenesis [214]. Importantly, chronic alcohol exposure also induces CYP2E1 expression in adipocytes, particularly in visceral fat, leading to increased generation of ROS as described previously. This redox imbalance contributes to lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial stress, and local inflammatory responses, which can trigger or amplify autophagy activation [215]. Oxidative stress in adipocytes also promotes the release of extracellular vesicles (EVs) enriched in miR-155, which impair antioxidant defenses and propagate injury to distal organs such as the liver. Additionally, alcohol-induced ROS and lipid peroxidation are associated with the breakdown of lipid droplets and impaired energy storage, further linking redox stress to dysregulated autophagy and adipose tissue wasting [216]. However, Li et al. demonstrated that mice exposed to chronic and binge alcohol showed decreased p62 levels in AT and increased AT atrophy [217].

Although alcohol stimulates lipolysis in AT and contributes to liver damage, it also activates brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis as an adaptive mechanism against alcohol-induced liver injury. In wild-type mice, alcohol feeding increased thermogenic gene expression in BAT, but this effect was significantly reduced in aged SQSTM1/p62 knockout (KO) mice. These aged KO mice exhibited obesity and impaired BAT adaptation to alcohol, which worsened alcohol-induced liver injury [218]. These findings suggest that autophagy promotes BAT thermogenesis, thereby helping to mitigate ALD in aged mice. Overall, these findings indicate that effects of alcohol on autophagy markers in AT depend on the alcohol feeding regimen and the ages of the mice.

Autophagy and immune regulation in adipose tissue

Increased autophagy following alcohol feeding has been linked to elevated inflammatory markers in AT [214]. The importance of autophagy in inflammation is underscored by the finding that alcohol feeding to conditional knockout mice lacking ATG5 fails to change inflammatory markers like TNFα and IL-6 in AT [217]. In contrast, aged SQSTM1/p62 KO mice exposed to chronic and binge alcohol exhibited increased secretion of proinflammatory and angiogenic adipokines [218], to suggest that blocking autophagy in AT amplifies the inflammatory response to alcohol exposure.

Mechanisms of autophagy dysregulation

Autophagy is negatively regulated by mTOR signaling. However, autophagy induced by alcohol administration was not associated with changes in mTOR phosphorylation. Instead, a distinct upregulation of S6 kinase (S6K1) activity, contributed to enhanced autophagy activation in AT upon alcohol feeding. There were no other alterations in other protein synthesis markers such as mTOR or eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E-binding protein 1 (4E-BP1) reported [214].

Critical highlights

Alcohol disrupts autophagy in adipose tissue (AT) through a mechanism independent of mTOR but linked to S6K1 activity. This dysregulation plays a complex role in AT function, as some studies associate autophagy activation with increased lipolysis and AT atrophy, while others suggest impaired autophagic flux worsens dysfunction (Table 2). Beyond metabolism, autophagy also regulates immune responses—ATG5 deficiency prevents alcohol-induced inflammation, whereas SQSTM1/p62 knockout (KO) amplifies it. Alcohol further triggers brown adipose tissue (BAT) thermogenesis as a protective response against liver injury, but this adaptation fails in aged SQSTM1/p62 KO mice, worsening alcohol-associated liver disease (ALD). Understanding the dual role of autophagy in AT dysfunction is crucial for identifying potential therapeutic targets.

Brain

Chronic excessive alcohol consumption is associated with substantive brain changes, including regional neurodegeneration, cortical thinning, and decreased brain volume, particularly in the cerebellum (motor coordination) and hippocampus (memory deficits) [204,219,220]. These findings highlight the neurotoxic effects of alcohol on the brain, with research increasingly focusing on mechanisms including neuroinflammation and autophagy dysfunction. This review explores alcohol-induced neuroinflammation and subsequent autophagy dysfunction.

Mediators of alcohol-related brain impairment

Chronic alcohol consumption causes neurotoxicity primarily through oxidant stress, driven by alcohol conversion to AcAld, catalyzed by ADH, CYP2E1, and catalase. AcAld generates ROS and 4-HNE, leading to neuronal cell death [221]. AcAld also activates the ER stress response, increasing the levels of apoptotic proteins including CHOP and cleaved caspases 3, 9, and 12 [222]. Chronic alcohol use is most notably linked to neuroinflammation [223], which is regulated through the gut-brain axis. Studies have revealed that alcohol induces gut microbiome dysbiosis in mice, leading to elevated peripheral endotoxins (LPS). This, in turn, activates TLR4 signaling, increasing neuroinflammation and glial activation via increased expression of markers such as Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP), Ionized Calcium-Binding Adapter Molecule 1 (IBA-1), and Cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) [224]. Studies have shown that alcohol-induced neuroinflammation also disrupts brain proteostasis, affecting the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the autophagy-lysosome pathway (ALP) [225,226].

In addition to systemic effects, alcohol is metabolized within the brain by catalase, ADH, and CYP2E1, which generate AcAld and contribute to local redox imbalance. CYP2E1 activity in brain tissues promotes formation of ROS, such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, that leads to oxidative stress and protein adduct formation [227]. This redox stress particularly affects lipid membranes and mitochondrial proteins, inducing lipid peroxidation and compromising mitochondrial integrity.

The accumulation of AcAld in microsomal and peroxisomal compartments, where catalase is localized, further contributes to redox stress, which is exacerbated when mitochondrial ALDH2 activity is overwhelmed or inhibited. Oxidative post-translational modifications of ALDH enzymes during chronic alcohol exposure decrease their catalytic efficiency, sustaining AcAld toxicity and ROS generation [227].

Alcohol metabolism in the brain also generates ROS/RNS, including O2•-, H2O2, and ONOO−, together with lipid peroxidation products such as 4-HNE. This redox stress not only leads to cellular damage but is also implicated in autophagy suppression. Studies show that chronic alcohol oxidation activates mTORC1 and reduces expression of autophagy proteins (ATG5, ATG12, cathepsin B), mirroring the decline in autophagy seen in liver tissues [228]. The accumulation of oxidatively damaged proteins and organelles is associated with reduced autophagic clearance and proteasomal activity, contributing to neurodegeneration [228].

Alcohol-induced autophagy dysregulation

Autophagy is a key degradation pathway, that plays a central role in maintaining neuronal homeostasis. Impaired protein degradation, commonly seen in neurodegenerative diseases and after chronic alcohol consumption, leads to abnormal protein accumulation [229]. Autophagy, specifically via the ALP pathway involves autophagy-related genes (ATGs), receptors like SQSTM1/p62, ULK1/2, Beclin-1, and LC3I/II, all of which mediate vesicle induction, nucleation, maturation, and fusion with lysosomes for degradation [230]. While acute alcohol consumption activates autophagy, presumably as a protective mechanism, chronic alcohol consumption suppresses autophagy, causing accumulation of damaged proteins in neural tissue and subsequent neurodegeneration [225].

One of the primary factors involved in alcohol-induced autophagy dysregulation is TLR4 signaling, activated by lipopolysaccharide (LPS). LPS increases neuroinflammation and microglial activation, which disrupts autophagy in the brain. Studies showed that alcohol-induced neuroinflammation caused by LPS dysregulated autophagic processes in the mouse brain occurs through TLR4-dependent mechanisms [224,228]. In vivo studies revealed that alcohol treatment of TLR4 knockout (KO) mice does not induce neuroinflammation, supporting the role of TLR4 in this process [231]. Chronic alcohol exposure also dysregulate both the UPS and ALP in brain tissues, which is marked by changes in proteasomal β5 and α2 subunits and altered levels of key autophagic proteins including LC3, ATG5, p62, and cathepsin B in wild-type (WT) mice. These effects were not observed in alcohol-fed TLR4 KO mice [228]. The UPS mainly clears short-lived soluble proteins, whereas autophagy removes bulkier substrates such as damaged organelles, protein and lipid aggregates that predominate under chronic oxidative stress, making the ALP more heavily burdened and thus more vulnerable to suppression in this setting. Both the UPS and autophagy are sensitive to alcohol-induced oxidative stress, and both have been shown to be dysregulated in liver tissue [47], suggesting that similar redox-driven disruptions may also occur in the brain.

In adolescent models of intermittent alcohol binge feeding, the dysregulation of autophagy was similarly TLR4-dependent. Alcohol treatment increased mTOR activation, impairing autophagy, as judged by increased p62, ATG5, and decreased LC3-II levels in WT mice. These findings were associated with synaptic pruning deficits, which were restored to normal after treatment with the autophagy agonist rapamycin, an mTOR inhibitor [232]. Rapamycin treatment also restored autophagy and cognitive function in the hippocampi of binge-alcohol-treated mice [226,233]. These findings suggest that gut-derived LPS activating microglial TLR4 signaling, together with local alcohol metabolism-derived ROS/RNS in brain cells, may converge to amplify autophagy inhibition through mTORC1 activation and lysosomal impairment, thereby accelerating neurodegeneration.

Clinical implications

Neuroinflammation and autophagic dysfunction from chronic alcohol use pose significant public health challenges, especially with our growing aging population. Heavy drinking in older adults has increased in recent decades [234,235]. Older adults are more vulnerable to alcohol's neuroinflammatory effects and behavioral changes [235,236]. Age-dependent declines in neuronal autophagy reportedly exacerbate these effects [237]. Studies show a strong correlation between heavy alcohol consumption (≥8–14 drinks/week) and an increased risk of dementia, particularly Alzheimer's disease (AD)-related dementia [[238], [239], [240]]. Experimental evidence highlights the role of alcohol-induced neuroinflammation and autophagy dysregulation in AD. In Triple-Transgenic Alzheimer's Disease (3xTg-AD) mice, chronic alcohol feeding exacerbated neuroinflammation (TLR4, IL-6, IL-1β, and IFN-γ) compared to WT, inhibited autophagy, and promoted tau pathology [241]. Consequently, alcohol consumption is now considered a modifiable risk factor for dementias, including AD [242,243].

Therapeutic implications

The gut-brain axis is a promising therapeutic target for addressing alcohol-induced neuropathology. Chronic alcohol use alters gut microbiota, particularly reducing butyrate-producing bacteria, which play a role in neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration [224,244]. Studies have shown that alcohol-related decreases in butyrate-producing bacteria contribute to AD-related neuroinflammation and tau pathology. Furthermore, alcohol disrupts autophagic processes, including the UPS and macroautophagy, which are both crucial for degrading tau [241,245].

Therapeutic interventions targeting the microbiome-brain axis have shown promise. Sodium butyrate (NaB), a short-chain fatty acid, has been shown to alleviate alcohol-induced gut dysbiosis, reduce neuroinflammation, and prevent neurodegenerative changes in mice [246]. In Parkinson's disease models, NaB restored gut homeostasis and reduced microglial activation [247]. Similarly, oral administration of tributyrin, a butyrate prodrug, prevented age-related cognitive deficits and AD pathology in 3xTg-AD mice by restoring gut microbial balance and reducing neuroinflammation [248]. In parallel, reducing alcohol oxidation-derived ROS/RNS (e.g., via ALDH2 activation or mitochondrial antioxidants) may help restore autophagy and limit neurodegeneration, especially alongside microbiome-based interventions.

Critical highlights

Chronic alcohol consumption disrupts autophagy in the brain, impairing neuronal homeostasis and contributing to neurodegeneration. While acute alcohol may temporarily enhance autophagy, chronic exposure suppresses it, leading to protein accumulation and neuronal damage (Table 3). This dysregulation is linked to TLR4 signaling, activated by alcohol-induced gut dysbiosis and increased LPS, which promotes neuroinflammation and glial activation. In TLR4 knockout (KO) mice, alcohol fails to induce neuroinflammation, preserving autophagic function. Furthermore, alcohol activates mTOR signaling, which suppresses autophagy, leading to impaired synaptic pruning and cognitive deficits—effects that can be reversed with rapamycin. Autophagy suppression also accelerates tau pathology, linking alcohol use to Alzheimer's disease. Restoring autophagy via gut-targeted interventions, such as sodium butyrate, offers a potential therapeutic strategy against alcohol-induced neurodegeneration.

Skeletal muscle

Alcohol and sarcopenia

Skeletal muscle is the largest protein store in mammals, and its mass and function are regulated by proteostasis, the balance between protein synthesis and degradation [249]. The primary proteolytic pathways in skeletal muscle include the UPS, the lysosomal-autophagy pathway, and cysteine proteases like caspases and calpains [250]. When proteolysis is dysregulated and protein synthesis is impaired, muscle protein mass decreases, leading to sarcopenia [251].

Recent evidence suggests that skeletal muscle also contributes to immune responses through secreted molecules, including interleukins like IL-6 and IL-V15 [252]. Muscle proteolysis releases not only amino acids but also cytokines and metabolites that may promote immune senescence. Basal autophagy in skeletal muscle acts as a protective mechanism against subcellular dysfunction, particularly in mitochondria, and helps prevent the accumulation of damaged molecules, including oxidative products [253]. While age-related muscle loss (primary sarcopenia) is linked to immune senescence, there is limited evidence regarding the role of secondary sarcopenia due to disease [252].

Alcohol and secondary sarcopenia

Dysregulated proteostasis is a major factor in secondary sarcopenia, a common complication in individuals with heavy alcohol consumption or liver disease, particularly ALD [[254], [255], [256]]. During acute alcohol intoxication, muscle loss can be reversible [257], but once ALD develops, sarcopenia becomes more severe and progresses rapidly [258]. The lysosomal-autophagy pathway plays a key role in muscle proteolysis in liver diseases and ALD, contributing to sarcopenia alongside impaired protein synthesis [258,259]. Understanding how alcohol increases muscle autophagy in ALD could lead to targeted therapies to prevent or treat sarcopenia and improve clinical outcomes, including immune dysfunction.

Mediators of alcohol-induced sarcopenia

Although alcohol itself is harmful to skeletal muscle, studies using inhibitors of alcohol and ADH suggest that AcAld, rather than alcohol, primarily induces increased autophagy with minimal activation of the UPS [260]. Alcohol's adverse effects on skeletal muscle result from its direct action and oxidative metabolism within muscle cells [259] as skeletal muscle itself oxidizes alcohol to generate AcAld. Thus, both circulating (liver derived) and locally generated AcAld contribute to muscle toxicity of alcohol. Chronic alcohol exposure upregulates CYP2E1 in skeletal muscle, enhancing ROS production, lipid peroxidation, and mitochondrial injury as described above in the liver and other organs. Thus, AcAld and CYP2E1 dependent free radicals should be considered as potential factors influencing autophagy process. These redox changes impair myofibrillar protein integrity, increase protein carbonylation, and lead to mitochondrial dysfunction—all of which promote dysregulated autophagy and myofiber atrophy [261]. In addition to the redox effects of AcAld, fatty acid ethyl esters (FAEEs)—nonoxidative alcohol metabolites—accumulate in muscle mitochondria, further impairing oxidative phosphorylation, disrupting calcium handling, and promoting mitochondrial permeability transition (MPT) [261]. These mechanisms exacerbate oxidative damage and stimulate autophagy in muscle tissue [262]. In addition to the effects of alcohol and AcAld on the skeletal muscle, hepatic urea cycle enzymes are inhibited with alcohol exposure contributing to hyperammonemia [263].

Studies show that alcohol consumption also impairs the metabolism of ammonia, a cytotoxic molecule produced during amino acid and purine catabolism and by gut microbes, leading to muscle damage [259]. Under normal conditions, ammonia is detoxified by hepatocytes through ureagenesis, but alcohol exposure reduces the expression and activity of enzymes in the urea cycle [263]. Hyperammonemia is a recognized cause of sarcopenia through various metabolic and molecular mechanisms and alcohol exposure sensitizes the muscle to adverse consequences of hyperammonemia [264]. Other factors contributing to sarcopenia in ALD include endotoxemia (Sato et al., 2021). These mediators of the liver-muscle axis in ALD also cause mitochondrial dysfunction, increased free radical production, and signaling disruptions, further promoting autophagy and muscle proteolysis [265]. Both alcohol and ammonia lead to cataplerosis (loss of tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates), reduce mitochondrial membrane potential, increase free radicals, and decrease ATP production [266,267].

Molecular mechanisms of alcohol-induced dysregulated autophagy

Alcohol exposure affects two key signaling pathways: mTORC1 and AMPK, both of which regulate protein homeostasis and autophagy. These pathways have opposing effects on autophagy [268]. In ALD and during alcohol exposure, mTORC1 is less phosphorylated, signaling nutrient deprivation and increasing autophagy flux, while protein synthesis is impaired. Unexpectedly, AMPK phosphorylation is also reduced, despite lower ATP levels. AMPK typically inhibits mTORC1 and activates autophagy, but in ALD, mTORC1 signaling is impaired, while AMPK remains insufficiently activated, creating a “pseudofed state” in muscle cells [268]. This impairment in mTORC1 and AMPK signaling occurs due to activation of protein phosphatase 2A (PP2A) in response to alcohol, which also occurs in liver tissue. Alcohol also inhibits mTORC1 through redox-sensitive disruption of upstream insulin and insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) signaling. Oxidative stress reduces Insulin Receptor Substrate 1 (IRS1) phosphorylation and impairs PI3K-Akt pathway activity [269], compounding mTOR suppression and augmenting autophagy.

Alcohol exposure increases the expression of Rhesus Blood Group-Related Glycoprotein B (RhBG), a regulated ammonia transporter in skeletal muscle [259]. Impaired ammonia disposal combined with increased ammonia transporter expression leads to skeletal muscle hyperammonemia. This exacerbates mTORC1 inhibition and autophagy flux, while simultaneously reducing protein synthesis [270,271]. Ammonia also worsens mitochondrial oxidative dysfunction, further amplifying the effects of alcohol-induced muscle abnormalities [259,267]. Endotoxemia, often seen in ALD due to intestinal barrier dysfunction and hepatic Kupffer cell abnormalities, activates TLRs, especially TLR4, which are abundant in muscle [272]. This activation can induce autophagy through the MYD88-p65NFκB pathway and may involve myostatin, a transforming growth factor β superfamily member [254,255,273].

Immune senescence and ALD

While immune dysfunction in ALD is well-documented, recent studies show that peripheral blood mononuclear cells from ALD patients exhibit abnormal mitochondrial oxidative responses and senescence-associated changes. These findings, derived from multiomics analyses, suggest that shorter telomere length is associated with higher mortality [266]. However, it is unclear whether these changes are directly related to sarcopenia, a common complication in ALD.

Therapeutic implications

Despite the clinical significance of ALD and sarcopenia, therapeutic options remain limited. Nutritional supplementation is commonly recommended but has shown limited effectiveness in improving muscle health [267]. Preclinical and pilot human studies suggest that abstinence alone is insufficient to reverse sarcopenia. Targeted therapies, including leucine-enriched branched-chain amino acid supplementation and the non-nitrogenous, highly anabolic leucine metabolite β-hydroxy-β-methylbutyrate, may help by reducing autophagy and reversing sarcopenia through mTORC1 modulation [274,275]. Using non-nitrogenous anabolic agents to target molecular disruptions in ALD are promising, given the compounding effects of alcohol-dependent hyperammonemia on muscle health. Additionally, mitigating redox imbalance, particularly ROS/RNS generated by alcohol oxidation in muscle and the systemic burden of AcAld may help maintain autophagy at steady-state levels and, in turn, preserve muscle mass and function.

Critical highlights

Alcohol exposure and ALD lead to accelerated muscle proteolysis through increased autophagy, while muscle UPS remains largely unaffected, resulting in sarcopenia. Although autophagy is generally protective in tissues, its dysregulation contributes to sarcopenia in ALD and presents a potential therapeutic target (Table 4). Further investigation is needed to explore how alcohol-induced autophagy dysfunction drives sarcopenia and its connection to immune dysfunction.

Lung

According to the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), 29.5 million individuals aged 12 and older were diagnosed with Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD). AUD significantly impacts the prevalence and severity of lung disease, including impairments to consciousness and cough reflexes [276], decreased innate immune responses in pneumonia, and increased lung inflammation in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [277].

AUD and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)

COPD is one of the leading causes of death globally [278] and was the third leading cause in 2019. COPD is associated with an increased risk of infectious complications from community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) and COPD exacerbations [[279], [280], [281]]. While cigarette smoking is the leading cause of COPD, airway infections are the primary driver of COPD exacerbations. The role of alcohol use in influencing COPD remains unclear, although some studies suggest a complex relationship. Research by Sisson et al. demonstrates that heavy alcohol consumption is linked to increased pulmonary obstruction compared to lifelong non-drinkers [282]. However, moderate alcohol consumption may offer a protective effect against COPD, though the risk rises with excessive alcohol use [283,284].

Mechanistically, AUD can exacerbate respiratory infections. One significant factor is the impact of AUD on the mucociliary escalator, which is the primary innate immune defense against respiratory infections. This mechanism involves the movement of mucus and debris up the airway toward the epiglottis for clearance via swallowing and cough [285,286]. Effective mucociliary clearance relies on the functions of motile cilia, which are specialized tubulin-containing structures that rhythmically beat to expel pathogens and mucus. Alcohol consumption impairs cilia function, with prolonged exposure slowing cilia beat frequency and reducing bacterial clearance from the lungs, thereby increasing the risk of infection [[287], [288], [289], [290], [291]].

AUD and host immune cell function

AUD has long been recognized as increasing the risk of CAP [[292], [293], [294]]. Chronic alcohol exposure negatively impacts resident and recruited immune cells in the lung [295,296]. In lung alveoli, bacteria and foreign particles encounter alveolar macrophages, triggering the release of cytokines and chemokines that attract neutrophils to the site of infection. Neutrophils help clear pathogens by secreting proteases and producing ROS [297]. However, alcohol impairs the ability of macrophages to phagocytize bacteria and recruit neutrophils, thereby slowing the immune response [298,299]. Additionally, alcohol slows neutrophil chemotaxis, impairing their ability to reach the infection site [[300], [301], [302]]. These effects increase the risk of lung infection and contribute to the severity of conditions like CAP and COPD exacerbations.

AUD and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS)

ARDS is a severe condition characterized by non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema and hypoxic respiratory failure. It can result from both pulmonary (e.g., pneumonia) and non-pulmonary causes (e.g., sepsis or pancreatitis) [303]. AUD is associated with an increased risk and severity of ARDS, particularly when caused by infection [304,305]. Chronic alcohol exposure contributes to this risk by impairing the alveolar-epithelial barrier. Studies show that individuals with chronic alcohol use have elevated protein concentrations in the epithelial lining fluid (ELF), indicating barrier dysfunction and increased permeability, which exacerbates edema and impairs gas exchange [306]. Additionally, chronic alcohol use leads to dysregulated immune responses, increasing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α, further driving lung damage in ARDS [298,299].

These effects are closely tied to alcohol-induced redox stress in the lung. Alcohol metabolism by CYP2E1, ADH, and catalase in alveolar macrophages and epithelial cells generates ROS and AcAld, which damage mitochondria and ER, deplete glutathione, and promote chronic oxidative stress [307]. These redox changes interfere with signaling pathways essential for autophagy initiation and lysosomal homeostasis [307].

Redox-driven autophagy dysregulation in AUD-related lung disease

Emerging evidence suggests that the autophagy-lysosome pathway also plays a key role in innate immune responses in the lung. Alcohol-related oxidative stress impairs this pathway by inhibiting AMPK activation, promoting ER stress, and reducing TFEB nuclear translocation, all of which suppress autophagy initiation and lysosomal biogenesis. In macrophages, autophagy is essential for the phagocytosis and killing of pathogens like Streptococcus pneumoniae, [308]Group A streptococcus, [309] Mycobacterium tuberculosis, [310,311]and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. [312] Recent studies demonstrate that autophagy is also critical for proper ciliary function [313,314]. In rodent models, alcohol exposure impairs pulmonary cilia function, slowing ciliary beat frequency, and reducing bacterial clearance, which may contribute to the increased risk of respiratory infections. Moreover, chronic redox imbalance leads to oxidative modification and inactivation of autophagy-related proteins (e.g., LC3, ATG5), further reducing autophagosome formation and impeding the clearance of damaged organelles and pathogens.

Furthermore, autophagy-mediated degradation of mucin granules in airway cells is essential for resolving airway inflammation. This process may be disrupted in AUD due to ROS- and AcAld-mediated suppression of autophagic flux, impaired lysosomal acidification, and mitochondrial dysfunction. These changes compromise mucin resolution and perpetuate epithelial inflammation leading to impaired mucous clearance and contributing to the development of lung diseases such as CAP and COPD exacerbations. However, the precise role of autophagy in mucin granule degradation and its impact on AUD-related lung disease remains to be fully characterized.

Critical highlights

Chronic alcohol use disorder impacts lung health, increasing the risk of respiratory infections, exacerbating conditions like COPD and ARDS, and impairing immune responses. Autophagy, a process that regulates protein degradation and immune responses, may play a central role in mediating the effects of AUD on lung disease. Chronic alcohol exposure disrupts autophagy and lysosome function, impairing mucociliary clearance and immune cell function, thus increasing vulnerability to lung infections and exacerbating existing lung conditions (Table 5). Further research into the mechanisms of autophagy in AUD-related lung disease could lead to novel therapeutic approaches for improving lung health in individuals with alcohol use disorder.

Summary and conclusions

This review summarizes current evidence that alcohol-induced autophagy disruption contributes to organ damage. We have highlighted such disruptions in the liver, heart, pancreas, brain, adipose tissue, skeletal muscle, and lung. Autophagy is a critical cellular catabolic process that maintains cell and tissue homeostasis, but it becomes dysregulated after chronic alcohol exposure. While acute alcohol intake may transiently activate autophagic flux as a protective response, chronic, prolonged alcohol consumption impairs lysosome function, reduces selective autophagy, and amplifies inflammatory responses, thereby exacerbating tissue damage. Importantly, these effects are closely linked to the oxidative and nitrative stress generated during alcohol metabolism. Across multiple tissues, TFEB signaling appears dysregulated, suggesting that the lysosomal stress response is a conserved mechanism in alcohol-related pathology. Upstream drivers likely include ROS/RNS and secondary reactive aldehydes that damage lysosomal membranes, triggering compensatory TFEB-driven lysosomal stress response. However, with continuous alcohol oxidation, this adaptive response becomes maladaptive, as described in the liver, and may similarly contribute to disease progression in extrahepatic organs.

Although alcohol metabolism occurs predominantly in the liver via ADH and CYP2E1, many extrahepatic tissues including heart, brain, pancreas, gastrointestinal tract, lung, and skeletal muscle also metabolize alcohol, often via CYP2E1 and, in some cases, catalase or the nonoxidative fatty acid ethyl ester (FAEE) pathway. The relative contribution of these pathways varies by organ, shaping the dominant ROS/RNS species generated in each. For example, hepatic CYP2E1 produces high levels of O2•-, H2O2, and ONOO−, while pancreatic acinar cell injury may be linked to H2O2-driven ATG4B activation and persistent mitochondrial O2•-/ONOO− generation. In the heart, CYP2E1-and mitochondria-derived O2•- and ONOO−, along with aldehyde adducts, drive lysosomal dysfunction and defective mitophagy. In the gut, epithelial CYP2E1-derived ROS may disrupt tight junctions and circadian–autophagy signaling. Furthermore, AcAld generated in the liver can enter systemic circulation, reaching distant organs and compounding local oxidative/nitrosative stress, thereby amplifying autophagy disruption across multiple systems.

Reactive aldehydes such as AcAld, 4-HNE, and MDA arise downstream of ADH and CYP2E1-dependent alcohol oxidation and lipid peroxidation. While the liver is the primary site of their formation, CYP2E1 induction in extrahepatic tissues including heart, pancreas, gut, and skeletal muscle also contributes to local aldehyde production. These aldehydes covalently modify proteins and macromolecules, altering their stability or function, and can form adducts with α-tubulin, disrupting microtubule polymerization [185]. Such cytoskeletal alterations impair autophagosome trafficking and autophagosome–lysosome fusion. Aldehyde adduction has also been associated with lysosomal membrane destabilization, which could compromise lysosomal integrity and initiate lysosomal stress response, although the latter remains to be experimentally verified.

In summary, chronic alcohol consumption disrupts autophagy, shifting it from a protective mechanism to a driver of organ dysfunction and systemic inflammation. Although therapeutic targeting of autophagy holds promise, it is important to note that the majority of data summarized in this review are derived from rodent models. Translation of these findings to human biology remains essential. Preliminary data from ongoing studies in human liver tissue, including work from the Thomes laboratory, suggest that key autophagy-related mechanisms observed in rodents may not be fully conserved in humans. This highlights the need for caution when extrapolating preclinical findings and underscores the importance of validating mechanistic models in human tissues and clinical cohorts. Advancing this line of investigation will be critical for developing targeted interventions that effectively mitigate alcohol-related organ damage in human populations.
