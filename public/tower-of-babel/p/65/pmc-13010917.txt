Chronic stress and fear in fast- and slow-growing broilers as they age and gain weight when raised in simple or complex environments
Authors: Alexandra Ulans, Ignacio T Moore, Leonie Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective was to assess how environmental complexity influences fear and chronic stress in fast- and slow-growing broiler chickens as they age and gain weight. Six-hundred fast-growing (Ross 708) and 600 slow-growing (Hubbard Redbro Mini) broilers were raised in 24 pens with simple (standard) or complex (permanent and temporary enrichments) pens. Tonic immobility (TI) tests were performed on 6 broilers/pen to assess fear, with 3 sampled for primary wing feathers to assess feather corticosterone (fCORT) concentration at 4, 5, and 6 weeks of age and 1, 2, and 3 kg of body weight, resulting in 4 testing points per strain due to overlapping weights and ages (TI n=576, feather n=288). Feather brightness (indicating melanin content) was calculated from images. fCORT was assayed using an ELISA. Fast-growing broilers remained in TI longer (106±6 sec) than slow-growing broilers (82±6 sec) at equal ages (p=0.013). Heavier birds remained in TI longer (105±8 sec) than lighter birds (73±8 sec; p=0.011). Feather brightness and fCORT concentration were not correlated in slow-growing broilers (ρ=0.085, p=0.320), but were negatively correlated in fast-growing broilers (ρ=-0.286; p=0.001). Young (7.5 ng/g; p=0.002) and light (8.8 ng/g; p<0.001) broilers had higher fCORT concentrations than older (5.0 ng/g) and heavier (4.7 ng/g) ones. Fear increased with age, suggesting reduced self-efficacy. fCORT concentration declined with age and weight gain, suggesting stress response downregulation.Keywords: Feather corticosterone, Tonic immobility, Slow-growing broiler, Environmental enrichment, Chronic stress

Introduction

Broiler chickens have been selected for fast growth and increased breast muscle yield (Tavárez and de los Santos, 2016). However, prioritizing these production-related traits has led to increased likelihood of adverse animal welfare outcomes. Fast-growing broiler chicken strains have an increased prevalence of leg disorders, bone deformations, sudden death syndrome, ascites, and mortality rates compared to slower-growing strains (see (Hartcher and Lum, 2020) for a review). Moreover, fast-growing broilers show modified behavioral expression such as increased inactive behavior, and decreased dust bathing, preening, foraging, and perching compared to slow-growing broilers (Dixon, 2020; Rayner et al., 2020). As both growth rate and body weight increase with age, broiler welfare can change, with worsening gait (Freeman et al., 2020; Kestin et al., 2001), worsening footpad dermatitis (de Jong et al., 2014), increased inactivity (Bizeray et al., 2002; Bokkers and Koene, 2004; Evans et al., 2023), and increased anxiety occurring in older, heavier fast-growing broilers (Ulans et al., 2024).

One aspect of animal welfare is fearfulness, which can be assessed using fear tests (Forkman et al., 2007). Tonic immobility (TI) is a death-feigning behavior that occurs when a prey animal has exhausted all other possible means of escape from a predator (Humphreys and Ruxton, 2018). The animal will freeze and remain motionless so that the prey can escape when the predator leaves (Fogelholm et al., 2019; Sargeant and Eberhardt, 1975). The duration of TI has been used to quantify fear in broilers, with longer TI durations indicating more fear (Forkman et al., 2007). TI is preferential over other measures of fear for older broiler chickens, like the touch, novel object, or open field tests, as these can be confounded with lameness (Rasmussen et al., 2022; Vasdal, Moe, et al., 2018; Vestergaard and Sanotra, 1999). Complex environments reduce TI durations, thus fear, in fast-growing broilers. Fear was reduced when birds had access to perches and platforms (Baxter, Bailie, and O’Connell, 2019; Lourenço da Silva et al., 2021; Yildirim and Taskin, 2017), perches and peat dust baths (Baxter, Bailie, and O’Connell, 2019), suspended red plastic balls, mirrors, sand dust baths (Yildirim and Taskin, 2017), a combination of plastic balls, plastic bottles, toys and mirrors (Altan et al., 2013), and a combination of sand dust baths, a PECKstone, perches, and rotating nutrition and occupational enrichments (Anderson et al., 2021). This indicates fear may be reduced by providing a complex environment in fast-growing broilers.

Genotype can impact fearfulness. Some laying hen strains experienced greater fear and distress compared to other strains when other factors were controlled for (Albentosa et al., 2003; Campo et al., 2008; de Haas et al., 2013; Ghareeb et al., 2008; Meuser et al., 2021; Nelson et al., 2020). This may be due to indirect genetic selection for low reactivity in production systems that require close human proximity (Nelson et al., 2020). Similarly, fast-growing broilers had a smaller avoidance distance than slow-growing broilers, suggesting that fast-growing broilers were less fearful of humans (Baxter et al., 2021). However, the authors acknowledged that fast-growing broilers may be less physically capable of expressing fear or less motivated to move due to their rapid weight gain (Baxter et al., 2021; Rasmussen et al., 2022). If a strain of broiler is more fearful, they may experience more fear throughout their production period, resulting in worse animal welfare.

Besides fear, measuring distress can provide valuable insights into animal welfare status (Weimer et al., 2018). Acute stressors can activate the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal axis and release glucocorticoids into the circulation as part of the physiological acute stress response to overcome the stressor and return to homeostasis (Sapolsky et al., 2000). Measuring these glucocorticoids, specifically corticosterone (CORT) in birds, is a common method to quantify the stress that an animal is experiencing (Weimer et al., 2018). While the acute stress response may be adaptive to help the animal survive the stressor, long-term exposure to stressors and glucocorticoids can result in negative affective states (Ohl et al., 2008). Chronic stress can be associated with decreased growth (Wingfield and Ramenofsky, 1999), immune defense (Sapolsky et al., 2000), and cognitive ability (Kitaysky et al., 2003).

Assessing plasma CORT concentrations requires handling and blood collection which can confound results (Wein et al., 2017), and can negatively impact bird welfare. In contrast, feather corticosterone (fCORT) concentrations are validated as a measure of chronic stress in laying hens and fast-growing broilers (Bartels et al., 2021; Carbajal et al., 2014; Häffelin et al., 2020; Weimer et al., 2018). Growing feathers are vascularized and nonfunctional components, including CORT, are incidentally deposited within the keratin structure (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Dauwe et al., 2003). Greater concentrations in the bloodstream will result in more CORT deposition in the feather (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Carbajal et al., 2014; Weimer et al., 2018). As feathers grow over time, fCORT concentrations can reflect an accumulation of acute responses (Bartels et al., 2021; Bortolotti et al., 2008; Jenni-Eiermann et al., 2015). fCORT concentrations were positively correlated with heterophil:lymphocyte ratios and serum CORT concentrations in fast-growing broilers that received synthetic CORT via drinking water, indicating that fCORT concentration is a potential non-invasive alternative to more invasive physiological stress measures (Weimer et al., 2018). While well-validated in previous work (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Carbajal et al., 2014; Häffelin et al., 2020; Weimer et al., 2018), this measure has yet to be applied in broilers to measure chronic stress.

Few studies directly assessed the relationship between environmental enrichment and broiler chicken stress responses. Broilers given laser lights (Meyer et al., 2021) and laying hens hearing classical music (Davila et al., 2011) or given sprouted oats, barley grass, dust baths, platforms, and perches (Ross et al., 2020) showed reduced acute stress responses compared to chickens kept in unenriched environments. However, offering broilers live insects did not reduce acute stress (Oddon et al., 2021). Overall, environmental enrichment may be a means of reducing acute and potentially chronic stress in broilers but needs more evidence.

Beyond the social and physical environment, stress responses can be impacted by genetic factors. Ross 308 broilers had greater plasma CORT concentrations than Hubbard flex or Hybro broilers when other factors were controlled for, indicating greater acute stress experienced (Skomorucha et al., 2009). Plasma CORT concentrations were higher in White Leghorn hens than brown Hyline hens after experiencing a stressful event (Fraisse and Cockrem, 2006). Currently, no studies compare fCORT concentrations as an indicator of chronic stress in different broiler strains.

Feather coloration may impact glucocorticoid levels as melanin pigmentation has been associated with greater CORT deposition in feathers (Jenni-Eiermann et al., 2015). Melanin is pigment that is transferred to the epidermis, hair, and feathers, with higher melanin presence creating a darker color (Riley, 1997). In some bird species, feathers containing more melanin had higher CORT concentrations (Cruz-Miralles et al., 2020; Jenkins et al., 2013; Porterfield, 2016), suggesting that pigmentation can influence chronic stress measures. However, whether this association applies to broiler chickens is unknown. Fast-growing broilers have white plumage while slow-growing broilers have a variety of plumage colors. The association between melanin content and fCORT concentration may limit the application of this measure of chronic stress among differing broiler chicken genotypes, however this is unknown.

Our objectives were to assess how chronic stress and fear differed between fast- and slow-growing broilers when raised in simple or complex environments, to determine how chronic stress and fear changed as these broilers aged and gained weight, and if feather melanin content impacted CORT deposition in chicken feathers. We predicted that due to rapid weight gain, fast-growing broilers would experience more chronic stress and fear than slow-growing broilers, especially as they age and gain weight. Also, we predicted that a complex environment would help to mitigate chronic stress and fear in both strains. We theorized that a greater feather melanin content would be associated with higher fCORT concentrations.

Materials and methods

Ethical statement

This study was approved by the Virginia Tech Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (protocol number 21-221); all methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations and are reported according to the ARRIVE guidelines.

Animals and housing

A 2 × 2 factorial design was used with genetic strain and environmental complexity as factors. We obtained 1,200 one-day-old male broiler chicks (Gallus gallus domesticus) from a commercial hatchery (South Fork, PA, USA), including 600 fast-growing and 600 slow-growing birds that had been vaccinated against Marek’s disease. The initial number of birds placed was to achieve a stocking density within a commercially relevant range. Birds were separated by strain and arbitrarily distributed amongst 24 pens with 50 birds per pen, resulting in 6 replicate pens per treatment. Each pen (3.5 × 2.5 m) contained pine shavings (∼6 cm depth), two water lines with three nipples each, and two galvanized tube feeders. The target maximum stocking density for pens was 20 kg/m2 with an actual final stocking densities being 16.7 kg/m2 for fast-growing broilers and 16.5 kg/m2 for slow-growing broilers. The temperature was 35°C on day 1 and was gradually reduced to 21°C by day 29, remaining at 21°C until the end of the experiment. Broilers were on an artificial lighting program of 24L:0D for the first 4 days due to heat lamps, 20L:4D from day 5-7, and 18L:6D until the end of the experiment, with a light intensity of approximately 15 lux during light hours. Corn-soybean meal diets were formulated to meet fast-growing broiler chicken requirements (Aviagen, 2022a). Birds had access to a cardboard feed flat with mash in the first week to ensure easy access to feed. The same diet was fed to both strains in three phases, with each phase approximately 1/3rd of the birds’ life: starter (mash, 3,000 kcal ME and 23% CP), grower (pellet, 3,100 kcal ME and 21.5% CP), and finisher (pellet, 3,150 kcal ME and 20% CP). Fast-growing birds were fed starter on day 0-16, grower on day 16-30, and finisher on day 30-44, while slow-growing birds were fed starter on day 0-22, grower on day 22-44, and finisher on day 44-58.

Strain

Strains were randomly allocated to each pen and balanced across environmental complexities. Aviagen Ross 708 broilers (Aviagen, 2024) are commonly used in United States’ commercial production. This is a white-feathered fast-growing broiler chicken strain that achieves a live body weight of about 3 kg by day 42, with an average daily gain of 68.1 g (Aviagen, 2022b). Hubbard Redbro Mini broilers (Hubbard, 2024) are less common but are used on small-scale farms. This is a primarily brown-feathered slow-growing broiler chicken strain. In the current study this strain achieved a live body weight of 3.06 kg by day 58, with an average daily gain of 52.1 g.

Environmental complexity

The simple or complex environment treatments were randomly allocated to each pen and balanced across both strains. Broilers were housed in either simple environment pens, which contained new shavings, feeders, and drinkers, or complex environment pens, which contained additional permanent and temporary enrichments. In each complex environment pen, two permanent wooden platforms (488 cm L × 46 cm W × 7.5 cm H total), made of 5 × 10 cm wooden planks, provided 19.5 cm of linear perching space per bird. Pens permanently contained one wooden dust bath (180 cm L × 91 cm W × 10 cm H) filled with 45 kg of playground sand (Quikrete, Atlanta, GA, USA). An additional 23 kg was added on day 30 of age. Complex-environment pens received three pairs of temporary enrichments that were rotated every three days in consistent pairs according to a fixed, repeating sequence to standardize exposure duration. Birds had access to two hanging hand mirrors (19 × 28 cm) and a handful of chicken scratch (MannaPro, Chesterfield, MO, USA) thrown into the litter, or half a cabbage hung at bird height and alfalfa hay provided in two metal cage balls (Darice, Strongsville, OH, USA), or two treat balls (Lixit Corp., Napa, CA, USA) filled with oats (Bob’s Red Mill, Milwaukie, OR, USA) and three hanging bundles of polyethylene white string. Nutritional enrichments were refilled or replaced as needed in the morning and evening.

Measurements

Measurements were conducted at three age-matched and three weight-matched timepoints, where birds either were 4, 5, and 6 weeks of age, more specifically at 27, 34 and 41 days old, or weighed 1, 2, and 3 kg in live body weight. Mean (± SD) body weights for the fast-growing strain were 0.98 ± 0.16 kg (22 days old), 1.96 ± 0.22 kg (34 days), and 2.89 ± 0.26 kg (41 days) for the 1, 2, and 3 kg target weight classes. Mean (± SD) body weights for the slow-growing strain were 1.06 ± 0.09 kg (27 days old), 1.97 ± 0.15 kg (41 days), and 2.94 ± 0.22 kg (56 days) for the 1, 2, and 3 kg target weight classes. Six focal birds per pen were individually weighed to assure target weights were met, allowing 10% deviation. These weight collections started 1 or 2 days before the weight point was achieved. As part of a larger study, researchers used the same broilers after feather collection and TI testing (Ulans et al., 2024). This resulted in experimenters being inside the house additionally on days 28-29, 35-36, 42-43, and 57-58. Some age and weight timepoints overlapped, resulting in a total of four sampling timepoints per genetic strain, with the same observations used in separate datasets for modeling age and weight effects. These broilers were wing-banded on day 3 of age for identification throughout the experiment, ensuring that birds were never tested twice across timepoints

Tonic Immobility (TI)

The TI test quantifies a bird’s fearfulness, with a longer latency to right associated with a greater level of fear (Forkman et al., 2007). The TI test (as described in (Anderson et al., 2021)) was performed on the 6 focal birds per pen (n=36/treatment/time point; n=576). Six birds were chosen as this is consistent with similar focal-bird sampling approaches in broiler studies (Buijs et al., 2009; James et al., 2018; Morrissey et al., 2014). Four trained observers performed the TI test at a single timepoint with a total of seven observers throughout the experiment. The inter-observer reliability using the Cronbach’s Alpha for all observers equaled 97.8%. The observer placed birds in a V-shaped cradle in the hallway of the poultry facility. Afterwards, the observer placed two fingers on the bird’s sternum and applied gentle pressure while they covered the bird’s head by cupping their other hand for 15 sec. Then, the observer removed both hands and observed the latency to right (sec). If the latency was <10 sec, TI was considered to not be induced and the induction procedure was repeated for a maximum of three attempts. If TI was not induced after the third attempt, the latency was recorded as 0 sec. If TI was induced, the duration ended when the bird exhibited any attempt to right itself, regardless of whether the attempt was successful. The maximum test duration was 300 sec, after which the bird was righten and returned to their pen.

Feather brightness and corticosterone concentration

Three of 6 focal birds were arbitrarily selected for feather sample collection to determine fCORT concentration as an indicator of chronic stress (Bortolotti et al., 2008; Weimer et al., 2018; Campbell et al., 2022). Feather sampling was limited to three birds per pen for practical reasons. Birds were only sampled once during the experiment. Primary wing feathers were collected by cutting the calamus (Table 1). Feathers were stored in Ziploc bags (SC Johnson, WI, USA) at -20 °C until further processing. Feathers were visually inspected for damage, with the most intact feather selected for analysis (n=18/treatment/time point; n=288 total).

Feather color is associated with the amount of melanin present (McGraw et al., 2005). Mean brightness of the feathers was assessed using the hue, saturation, and brightness (HSB) values from photographs rather than red-green-blue (RGB) scale as reported in (Jenni-Eiermann et al., 2015). After feathers were washed, feathers were placed on light blue paper and photographs were taken (Samsung Galaxy S20 FE camera, white balance set at 5600K) in a light box (Slow Dolphin, Guangdong, China) at a standardized distance of 22 cm. The photographs were cropped to minimize the background. The remaining background was removed and the image converted to grayscale using Fiji ImageJ (NIH and LOCI, Bethesda, MD, USA). The image brightness was analyzed in the color inspector 3D plugin set in display mode with ≤30 colors. Since feather images were changed to grayscale, hue and saturation values were zero. The brightness assessment provided between 7 to 26 values accounting for the entire feather. Lower brightness values indicate darker pixels, representing more melanin present, while higher brightness values indicate lighter pixels, representing less melanin. The mean brightness value was calculated for each feather.

These feathers were then assessed for fCORT concentration using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) (Cayman Chemical, MI, USA). Feathers were weighed (mg), then rinsed with water, and cut into <5 mm-sized pieces in 20 mL scintillation vials using surgical scissors. Thereafter, 1 mL of methanol was added, and samples were placed in a sonicating water bath (CO-Z, Shanghai, China) for 30 min. Then, samples were placed in a shaking water bath (Jouan Inc., PrecisionSci. Div. Chicago, IL, USA) at 56°C overnight to extract any remaining CORT in the feather. Feather material was separated from the methanol using a vacuum and the filtrate was added to the scintillation vials. The methanol was completely evaporated from the filtrate, 1 mL of ELISA buffer was added, and samples were placed in a -20°C freezer until assayed. The samples were assayed using a commercial ELISA kit following manufacturer protocol. Samples were assayed in triplicate. When CV% was >15%, the outlying triplicate was removed. Resulting CORT values were divided by feather weight to express CORT concentrations as ng/g of feather weight. Bortolotti et al. proposed calculating fCORT concentration by using the length of the feather rather than the feather weight (Bortolotti et al., 2008). However, others consider that calculating by feather weight is a more accurate measure (Lendvai et al., 2013; Grunst et al., 2015; Freeman and Newman, 2018), so we decided to use the latter.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in JMP Pro 16 (SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA). Pen was considered the experimental unit, and bird was considered the observational unit for all response variables. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient tests were used for correlations between melanin and fCORT concentration, and TI duration and fCORT concentration. fCORT concentration and TI duration distributions of residuals were not normal, but variances were homogenous.

A linear mixed model was used for both fCORT concentration and TI durations since the model is robust enough to overcome a lack of normal distribution (Schielzeth et al., 2020). Fixed effects were environmental complexity (simple or complex), strain (fast or slow), and either age (4, 5, 6 weeks) or weight (1, 2, 3 kg), and their interactions. Non-significant interactions (p>0.1) were removed from the model. Pen was included as a random factor. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were done using Tukey-adjusted comparisons of least-square means (Tukey-Kramer method), which accommodate unequal sample sizes across groups. Associations were deemed significant at p≤0.05 and trends at p≤0.1. The data are presented as LSmeans±SEM unless otherwise noted.

Discussion

We aimed to investigate metrics of chronic stress and fear by quantifying feather corticosterone levels and tonic immobility durations in broiler chickens with differing genetic growth potential when housed in simple or complex environments. As feather melanin content in another species has impacted CORT deposition, we quantified feather brightness and evaluated whether those values were associated with CORT concentrations. Body weight, strain, and environmental complexity only tended to impact TI durations, increasing fear with weight gain when fast-growing broilers were raised in a complex environment or when slow-growing broilers were raised in a simple environment. Slow-growing broilers were less fearful than fast-growing broilers when comparing at age testing points, regardless of environmental complexity. TI duration and fCORT concentration tended to show a weak negative correlation. Feather melanin content and fCORT concentration were not correlated. fCORT concentrations decreased as broilers got older and heavier.

TI durations did not differ between ages but did between strains (when comparing age testing points), with fast-growing broilers being more fearful than slow-growing broilers. In our results, fearfulness was greater in birds with higher body weights. This difference tended to be impacted by environmental complexity treatment, with the increase in fearfulness only observed for fast-growing broilers in complex environment pens and for slow-growing broilers in simple environment pens. The TI test induces the death-feigning behavior that is performed when all other options for escape have been exhausted to convince a predator that the kill was successful (Humphreys and Ruxton, 2018). When the predator is distracted, the bird can escape (Fogelholm et al., 2019; Sargeant and Eberhardt, 1975). TI duration may therefore reflect not only fearfulness but also individual behavioral strategies or coping styles. Fear-related coping styles are well-studied in laying hens, with brown-feathered laying hens responding passively (freeze) in fear tests while white-feathered hens showing active avoidance (fleeing) (Rentsch et al., 2024; Peixoto et al., 2021), although the opposite was found too (Nelosn et al., 2020). Behavioral strategies might change over time, and heavier broilers may adopt a more passive behavioral strategy when escape is perceived to be less achievable, reflected in their prolonged TI duration at high weights.

These more passive behavioral fear responses could stem from the birds’ perceived ability to escape, which we frame as self-efficacy. The broilers’ sense of capability to organize and implement actions in novel, unpredictable, and possibly stressful situations (Bandura, 1978, 1982) may be reduced at high body weights compared to lower weights. If they feel they may not escape successfully, they may wait longer for the predator to move away. In humans, a low sense of self-efficacy was associated with higher levels of fear (Bandura, 1983; Bandura et al., 1982; Zlomuzica et al., 2015) and anxiety (Bandura, 1991; Graydon et al., 2012; Haycock et al., 1998; Muris, 2002; Paulhus and Martin, 1987; Williams, 1995). Whether this concept applies to chickens remains unknown. No validated measure of self-efficacy was included in the present study, and TI was not used to determine this construct in chickens. Therefore, this interpretation should be considered a theoretical framework rather than a demonstrated mechanism. This would be a worthwhile mechanism to explore further.

In line with our observations of fear and previous findings in humans, we observed increased anxiety in both fast- and slow-growing broilers as they gained weight (Ulans et al., 2024). If perceived escape capability contributes to behavioral fear responses, fast-growing broilers might be expected to show a greater increase in fear because of their more severe gait issues (Bessei, 2006; Hartcher and Lum, 2020; Rayner et al., 2020). However, slow-growing broilers also experience leg disease as they age, albeit not to the same degree fast-growing broilers do (Bessei, 2006; Hartcher and Lum, 2020; Rayner et al., 2020). Alternatively, the progressive loss of physical ability in both fast- and slow-growing broilers may cause a comparable increase in fearfulness, suggesting that functional decline, rather than growth rate alone, increases fear within the TI test.

Tonic immobility duration did not differ between the two genetic strains studied when assessed at equal weight points. This is in line with Mutibvu et al. (2017) finding comparable levels of fear between Potchefstroom Koekoek, Ovambo, and Naked Neck chickens. However, Hubbard JA57 broilers had longer TI durations compared to fast-growing Ross 308 broilers (Çavuşoğlu and Petek, 2019), showing that slow-growing strain type may impact fear response. Domestication and genetic selection for production likely has resulted in lower reactivity, with Red Junglefowl showing stronger CORT responses to restraint compared to White Leghorn laying hens (Ericsson et al., 2014). As slow-growing broilers have not been selected as strictly for productivity like fast-growing broilers, they may be more reactive (Çavuşoğlu and Petek, 2019). However, our results show that the Redbro Mini strain have a similar reactivity compared to Ross 708, so this is not a concern for this slow-growing strain.

While a trend, we see differences in TI duration across weight, environmental complexity, and strain. Fast-growing broilers raised in a simple environment and slow-growing broilers raised in a complex environment had similar fear over all weights, but fast-growing broilers in a complex environment and slow-growing broilers in a simple environment had less fear at light weights and more fear at heavy weights. A complex environment may have delayed the onset of diseases (Pedersen and Forkman, 2019; Güz et al., 2021; Da Silva et al., 2023) that could have limited the broiler’s ability to perform motivated behaviors and flee from a potential threat. Finding a weight effect on fear in a simple environment for slow-growing broilers was unexpected. This may be due to these birds having fewer novel experiences, thus increased fear in the novel situation of the TI test (Baxter et al., 2019; Anderson et al., 2021).

A potential confounding effect may have been that older broilers were less able to right themselves easily, resulting in longer TI durations. This effect would likely be more pronounced in fast-growing broilers, who develop heavier body weights and wider breast muscles than their slow-growing counterparts (Santos et al., 2021), potentially making self-righting within a cradle more difficult. To address this, TI duration was considered ended when the bird exhibited any attempt to right itself, regardless of whether the attempt was successful. This typically includes a twisting motion of the body or abdomen to reposition their feet beneath them. Because this twisting behavior reliably signals the end of the tonic immobility state (Jones, 1986), it was used as the criterion for terminating the TI measurement. This approach ensured that all broilers, regardless of their physical ability to successfully right themselves, had an equal opportunity to display the end of the TI episode through their first clear attempt to regain posture.

We expected a positive correlation between fear and fCORT, our metric of chronic stress, but no substantial correlation was found. Laying hens continuously given exogenous CORT had longer TI durations compared to the control (Jones et al., 1988). When Japanese quail were selected for either strong or weak plasma CORT responses when exposed to restraint, the high-CORT lines had longer TI durations (Satterlee and Johnson, 1988). Broilers selected for long TI durations showed an overproduction of CORT (Wang et al., 2014). The finding that a high CORT response results in longer TI durations counters our results, as birds had lower fCORT concentrations but longer TI durations as they aged and gained weight. Our results highlight that fear and chronic stress are not inherently positively associated. TI durations reflect acute reactivity of the bird (Carli and Farabollini, 2022), while fCORT concentrations do not capture single acute spikes in circulating plasma CORT effectively (Bortolotti et al., 2008). Our results suggest that birds in our study may experience increasing levels of fear but that did not result in more chronic stress. Similarly, decreases in chronic stress did not result in lower levels of fearfulness.

Following expectations, HSB values were consistent between white feathers (fast-growing broilers), with a small range of mean brightness values, reflecting low melanin content. Surprisingly, there was a weak negative correlation found between fast-growing broiler feathers’ mean brightness and fCORT concentration, indicating that darker feathers had higher fCORT concentrations. Since the fast-growing broilers are white, melanin should not be a factor impacting their mean HSB values. We are currently uncertain as to why this weak correlation is present. Feathers from slow-growing broilers had a much wider range of HSB values, representing the varied and higher melanin content in these feathers. Our methodology was similar to Jenni-Eiermann et al. (2015), but our results do not align, as they reported increased fCORT concentrations in dark sections of feral pigeon feathers compared to light sections, potentially due to higher eumelanin content in these dark sections. Melanin, consisting of both eumelanin and phaeomelanin, is present in dark-colored feathers. Eumelanin seems to play a larger role in CORT deposition than phaeomelanin. This is evidenced by the lack of a correlation between phaeomelanin content and fCORT in female Scops owls (but note that there was a correlation in males; Cruz-Miralles et al., 2020), North American barn swallows (Jenkins et al., 2013), American kestrels, Cooper’s hawks, and Sharp-shinned hawks (Porterfield, 2016). We recorded mean brightness, capturing both types of melanin indiscriminately. This served as a proxy for melanin but did not differentiate eumelanin from phaeomelanin. The specific eumelanin and phaeomelanin content of these feathers is currently unknown, so we cannot rule out type-specific effects on fCORT concentrations. Feather color is also confounded with genetic strain, which may have impacted these results. We recommend that future research should explore the separate effects of eumelanin and phaeomelanin on fCORT concentrations in chicken feathers and use more similar genetic strains.

Broilers sampled at week 4 had higher fCORT concentrations than broilers sampled at weeks 5 or 6. Similarly, broilers sampled when weighing 1 kg had higher fCORT concentrations than those sampled at 2 or 3 kg of live body weight. This was an unexpected finding, as fast-growing broilers experience greater health issues as they age and gain weight (Abeyesinghe et al., 2021; Hartcher and Lum, 2020), which we hypothesized to be resulting in higher fCORT concentrations, indicating increased chronic stress.

One possible explanation could be that broilers at younger ages and lighter weights were more active, increasing plasma CORT concentrations, thus increasing fCORT concentrations. Ducks forced to perform 60 minutes of exercise show increased plasma CORT concentrations directly after exercise compared to before exercise (Rees et al., 1983). However, the ducks habituated and showed diminished increases in plasma CORT concentration and no increase by day 21 (Rees et al., 1983). Another study found that 20 minutes of forced exercised in cockerels resulted in highly variables changes in plasma CORT concentration (David et al., 1986). There have been no studies comparing how plasma CORT changes in regards to voluntary exercise in poultry, but since CORT promotes the breakdown of energy stores to meet heightened energy demands (Hu et al., 2020), it likely increases to some degree. However, it is well established that slow-growing broilers show higher activity than fast-growing broilers in later life (Dixon, 2020; Baxter et al., 2021; Dawson et al., 2021; Güz et al., 2021; van der Eijk et al., 2022; Pearce et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024; Guinebretière et al., 2024). If changes in fCORT concentration over age and weight gain were driven by exercise-induced CORT responses, then we would likely see higher fCORT concentrations in slow-growing broilers at older ages, which we did not observe in this experiment.

The lower fCORT concentrations at older ages and higher weights could potentially indicate habituation, exhaustion, or downregulation of stress responses (Rich and Romero, 2005). Birds might be habituating to the presence of humans as they age and gain weight. In this experiment, broilers did not have much early exposure to humans besides the twice daily husbandry checks. However, starting at day 22 (fast-growing broilers at 1 kg body weight), humans were in the pens substantially more often than before day 22 (19% of days before day 22 and 62% after day 22 until day 44). It is possible that this increased exposure to humans at the beginning of the testing points may have allowed for the broilers to habituate to their presence, overall lowering stress from humans. Broilers who had at least 10 minutes of visual contact with humans for three weeks showed lower fear and stress levels than the control (Zulkifli et al., 2002). Visual contact was defined as the experimenter standing in the pen, not interacting or handling the birds (Zulkifli et al., 2002). This may have been a similar experience for our birds sampled at 2, 3 kg and 5, 6 weeks of age.

The reduction in fCORT levels may be caused by exhaustion, but this is unlikely since broilers were not exposed to invasive and persistent stressors and consistently showed weight gain. European Starlings exposed to six different stressors four to five times a day for 10 days did not even induce an exhaustion of the stress response, indicated by a lack of radical drops in the CORT response nor dangerous declines in body weight (Rich and Romero, 2005). It is most likely that the repeated exposure to negative stressors in our study downregulated acute stress responses and consequently the fCORT levels. Exposure to chronic stress downregulated acute CORT concentrations in European starlings, rather than causing exhaustion of the stress response. The authors argued that this was due to controlled downregulation of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis activity in response to chronic stress (Rich and Romero, 2005). In our birds it is possible that, with chronic stress being relatively mild compared to their treatment, baseline CORT levels reduced with age, resulting in less CORT deposited in feathers. This reduction in fCORT could reflect multiple potentially overlapping mechanisms. Baseline CORT levels decreased as broilers aged (Olanrewaju et al., 2009; Renden et al., 1994; Satterlee and Gildersleeve, 1983). Our fCORT data similarly show a decline with age, which could reflect normal developmental downregulation of baseline CORT, independently of chronic stress. However, this could also be a result of downregulation due to chronic stress, other physiological changes accompanying growth, or a combination of both, which cannot be disentangled in the present study. The decrease in fCORT levels observed between earlier sampling points likely reflects a combination of age-related developmental changes and mild stress-induced HPA downregulation. This may indicate that the broilers still experienced chronic stress with age, but showed a downregulated response to maintain normal functioning (Michael Romero, 2002; Sapolsky et al., 2000; Wingfield and Romero, 2001). Changes in baseline CORT levels in the blood could explain the decrease in fCORT levels between earlier sampling points.

Small feathers at earlier ages may have higher fCORT concentrations at those timepoints. Flight feathers from harlequin ducks that were <15 mg (Berk et al., 2016) and flight feathers from European starlings that were <25 mg (Lattin et al., 2011) showed substantially higher fCORT concentrations per mg of feather than heavier feathers. Week 4 feathers weighed 37 mg and had higher fCORT concentrations compared to week 5 feathers at 46 mg and week 6 feathers at 62 mg. As there was no difference in fCORT concentration between week 5 and 6, it may be that feathers that weigh <37 mg contain substantially more CORT in broilers. Future studies should explore how feather size impacts CORT content.

It was unexpected that fCORT concentration did not differ between fast- and slow-growing broilers or between environmental complexity treatments. We predicted fast-growing broilers would have higher fCORT concentrations as they experience higher rates of disease (Steenfeldt et al., 2019; Çavuşoğlu and Petek, 2019; Dixon, 2020; Rayner et al., 2020; de Jong et al., 2021; Abeyesinghe et al., 2021; Baxter et al., 2021; van der Eijk et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2024) and decreased natural behaviors (Rayner et al., 2020; Baxter et al., 2021; Dawson et al., 2021; van der Eijk et al., 2022; Abdourhamane and Petek, 2023), likely increasing their chronic stress. However, since we see no difference between strains, this suggests that fCORT concentration may not reliably capture welfare impairments related to painful conditions such as lameness (Riber et al., 2021) and contact dermatitis (Weber Wyneken et al., 2015), or the frustration stemming from restricted behavioral expression (Bokkers, 2004). As such, it may not be a precise measure for comparing welfare between broiler strains.

Overall, environmental complexity did not impact fear or chronic stress in our broilers, in contrast with our expectations. While certain types of enrichment have been reported to reduce fear, such as perches or novel objects (Altan et al., 2013; Yildirim and Taskin, 2017; Anderson et al., 2021), the enrichments used in this study did not affect tonic immobility durations. Similarly, some enrichments have been shown to reduce indicators of acute or chronic stress, such as laser lights lowering serum CORT (Meyer et al., 2021), cork panels and plastic grass reducing stereotypic pacing in Japanese quail (Laurence et al., 2014), or dust baths and platforms improving thermoregulation responses in broilers (Ross et al., 2020), but these effects were not observed in our experiment. These results suggest that TI duration and fCORT concentration may not capture all potential welfare benefits of environmental complexity, even when enrichments improve health and natural behaviors in the home pen (Riber et al., 2018).

Conclusion

Fear, measured through tonic immobility, increased with weight gain, which may reflect a reduction in the birds’ sense of self-efficacy, although this interpretation needs more support. At the same ages, fast-growing broilers were more fearful than slow-growing broilers, because they were heavier. Environmental complexity did not mitigate fear. Fear and chronic stress showed a significant but weak negative correlation. fCORT concentration can be used to compare chronic stress levels within and across genetic strains, but we need a better understanding of the role of eumelanin and phaeomelanin on CORT deposition in feathers. fCORT concentrations declined with age and weight gain, likely due to downregulation of the stress response. We detected no difference in fCORT concentrations between strains, and there was no evidence the complexity mitigated chronic stress. Choosing effective environmental enrichments is critical to induce an animal welfare benefits for broiler chickens.
