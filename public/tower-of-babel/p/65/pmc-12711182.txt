G-Mesh for Laparoscopic Pectopexy: A Single-center Evaluation of a Novel Technique for Apical Prolapse Repair
Authors: Sujal Atul Munshi, Mohit Laxmikant Marda, Ami Sujal Munshi, Janki Pranjal Patel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTApical prolapse, characterized by descent of the vaginal vault or uterus, continues to pose significant challenges in pelvic reconstructive surgery. Although laparoscopic sacropexy has long been considered the standard approach, it is often associated with defecatory, urinary, and mesh-related complications. Laparoscopic pectopexy, which anchors the suspension to the iliopectineal ligaments, has emerged as a safer and anatomically favorable alternative. This study evaluated the safety, feasibility, and anatomical and functional outcomes of a modified “G-mesh” laparoscopic pectopexy technique performed at a single tertiary-care center. A retrospective observational analysis was conducted on women who underwent the procedure between 2021 and 2025, assessing demographic characteristics, intraoperative details, and postoperative outcomes. Follow-up included POP-Q assessment, recurrence rates, and complication profile. The mean operative duration was 72 minutes with an average blood loss of 50 mL, and no major complications were recorded. Two minor intraoperative incidents—bladder wall thinning and vesicovaginal bleeding—were successfully managed laparoscopically. At 12-month follow-up, 13.3% of patients demonstrated mild prolapse recurrence, all managed conservatively with pelvic-floor rehabilitation. Overall, the G-mesh laparoscopic pectopexy technique appears safe, feasible, and associated with low morbidity and favorable anatomical outcomes. Further large-scale multicentric studies with extended follow-up are recommended to strengthen the evidence base for this technique.

INTRODUCTION

Pelvic organ prolapse (POP) affects millions of women globally and can significantly impair quality of life. Apical prolapse, involving descent of the vaginal vault or uterus, presents a unique reconstructive challenge. Abdominal or laparoscopic sacropexy has long been considered the gold standard for apical prolapse correction, but it may lead to postoperative bowel dysfunction, back pain, and mesh complications.

Laparoscopic pectopexy, first described by Banerjee and Noé,[1] anchors the vaginal apex or cervix to the bilateral iliopectineal ligaments instead of the sacral promontory. This avoids presacral dissection and potential injury to the hypogastric nerves, bowel, and vessels. Recent meta-analyses have demonstrated that pectopexy yields similar anatomical success with fewer complications and faster recovery compared to sacropexy.[234]

Moreover, recent systematic reviews confirm its safety and efficacy in diverse patient populations, including obese women, where pelvic access is often challenging.[567] The “G-mesh” modification – using a preshaped polypropylene mesh designed for optimal pelvic support – may improve mesh handling, tension distribution, and anatomical alignment. The present study evaluates the surgical outcomes and complications of G-mesh laparoscopic pectopexy in patients with apical prolapse at our center and compares results with recent literature.

The iliopectineal ligament is an extension of the lacunar ligament that runs on the pectineal line of the pubic bone[8] [Figure 1] and is significantly stronger than the sacrospinous ligament and the arcus tendinous of the pelvic fascia. The structure is strong and holds suture well. It is also possible to find sufficient material for a suture in the lateral part of the iliopectineal ligament, facilitating reconstruction of the pelvic floor. This segment of the ligament is situated at the second sacral vertebra (S2) level, which is the optimal level for the physiological axis of the vagina. The S2 level is the anchor point for the physiological axis of the vagina.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Inclusion criteria

A single-center retrospective observational study was conducted at Kalpana Munshi Hospital LLP, Ahmedabad, India (2021–2025). Fifteen mature women with symptomatic apical prolapse (POP quantification ≥ II) underwent laparoscopic G-mesh pectopexy. Ethical approval and informed written consent were taken before surgery.

Exclusion criteria

Patients with pelvic inflammatory disease, malignancy, medical disorders, and patients were unfit for laparoscopic surgery.

Under general anesthesia, patients were placed in the modified lithotomy position. The peritoneum along the right and left round ligaments was incised to identify the iliopectineal ligaments. A precut 15 cm × 15 cm polypropylene G-mesh [Figures 2 and 3] was introduced, sutured bilaterally to the pectineal ligaments, and fixed anteriorly to the uterine isthmus or vaginal apex. Posterior fixation was performed using nonabsorbable sutures, and the peritoneum was closed over the mesh.

Pectopexy technique

We performed this procedure as previously described by Banerjee and Noé.[1] First, we opened the peritoneal layer along the right round ligament toward the pelvic side wall [Figure 4a]. An incision in the medial and caudal direction was made with a harmonic scalpel, and the right external iliac vein was visualized. Soft tissue in this area was dissected with blunt dissection, so an approximately 4–5 cm segment of the right iliopectineal ligament (Cooper ligament) adjacent to the insertion of the iliopsoas muscle could be identified [Figure 4b]. The same procedure was then repeated on the left side of the patient [Figure 4d and e]. The peritoneal layers on both sides were opened toward the vaginal apex, and the anterior and posterior areas of the vaginal apex were prepared for the mesh fixation. In patients with a preserved uterus, the anterior peritoneum of the uterus was dissected, and the lower anterior segment of the uterus was prepared for the mesh fixation [Figure 4c]. After completion of dissections, a nonabsorbable synthetic polypropylene mesh (ETHICON© PROLENE Mesh, 15 cm × 15 cm) was tailored according to “G design” and inserted into the abdominal cavity [Figure 4f]. The anterior part of the mesh was sutured to the isthmic portion of the uterus or vaginal apex, the left end of the mesh to the left ilio-pectineal ligament, the right end of the mesh to the right iliopectineal ligament, and the posterior part of the mesh to the posterior surface of the uterus through the extracorporeal suture technique, using nonabsorbable sutures [Figure 4g-j]. The mesh in the tension-free position was fixed to the vaginal apex or uterus with polydioxanone sutures, and the vaginal apex or uterus was provided with a hammock-like fixation. Finally, the peritoneum above the mesh was sutured with an absorbable suture material [Figure 4k].

Demographic data, body mass index (BMI), operative time, blood loss, and intra- and postoperative complications were recorded. Follow-up at 3, 6, and 12 months assessed anatomical success, recurrence, and functional outcomes. Statistical analysis used Student’s t-test and Chi-square/Fisher’s exact test, with P < 0.05 considered significant.

RESULTS

Mean age was 56.2 ± 10.3 years and mean BMI 27.4 ± 3.2 kg/m². Vault prolapse occurred in 10 (66.7%) and uterine prolapse in 5 (33.3%) cases. Mean operative time was 72.3 ± 11.2 min and blood loss 51.6 ± 20.5 ml. Two intraoperative complications – bladder thinning and vesicovaginal bleeding – were successfully managed laparoscopically. No postoperative hemorrhage, infection, or mesh erosion occurred.

At 1-year follow-up, two cases (13.3%) showed mild recurrence of prolapse, both managed conservatively with pelvic floor rehabilitation, including Kegel’s exercises. No new urinary or defecatory dysfunction was reported. Vault prolapse patients were significantly older (P = 0.003) and had longer operative times (P = 0.012) compared to uterine prolapse cases.

DISCUSSION

Our findings demonstrate that laparoscopic pectopexy with G-mesh is a safe and reliable alternative to sacropexy, especially in overweight women or those with deep pelvic anatomy. The less intraoperative blood loss and short operative duration align with recent prospective studies showing faster recovery and similar success rates.[91011]

Pectopexy avoids presacral dissection, reducing risks of vascular and neural injury. No bowel or bladder dysfunction was reported, consistent with prior trials demonstrating preserved pelvic organ function after pectopexy.[12] The G-mesh provides hammock-like support at the S2 level, distributing tension evenly and maintaining the physiological vaginal axis.

Why choose pectopexy over sacrocolpopexy?

It is less invasive, bilateral fixation of the mesh on the pectineal ligaments works like a hammock, which runs parallel to the levator ani muscles and cardinal ligaments. It maintains the direction of the vaginal axis as in prolapse, where mainly there is the lengthening of the cardinal ligament only, not of the uterosacral ligament. There is no interference with vital structures such as the rectum, ureter, or nerves. It is very good for old, fragile patients as it can be accomplished without a steep Trendelenburg position. Thus, there is less cardiopulmonary compromise, less time-consuming, and less morbidity [Table 1].

Mesh placement is very easy in the anterior compartment, and easy placement of the suture at the ligament leads to avoidance of tackers and possible complications. Placement of mesh in the anterior compartment protects against lateral and central cystocele, and there are less chances of mesh erosion in the anterior compartment than posterior. This procedure has very less postoperative morbidity and a fast recovery with almost nil postoperative complication. In vault prolapse cases, the vaginal apex falls behind, and the vaginal angle between the upper and lower vagina decreases, but the fixation of the apex with the pectineal ligament normalizes it.[13]

Limitations include small sample size, retrospective design, and limited follow-up. Lack of a control group precludes direct comparison with sacropexy. Longer-term studies are recommended to evaluate durability and late complications [Tables 2 and 3].

CONCLUSION

G-mesh laparoscopic pectopexy provides a minimally invasive, anatomically stable, and functionally effective alternative for apical prolapse correction. The procedure demonstrated minimal morbidity, short operative time, and excellent recovery. Multicentric randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up are needed to confirm these results.
