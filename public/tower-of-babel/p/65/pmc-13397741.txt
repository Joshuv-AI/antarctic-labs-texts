The perspectives of healthcare professionals on dignity care for people with dementia: a qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Shanna Jin, Chunxiao Feng, Mengqi Zhang, Yu Xin, Ying Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global aging population has rendered dementia a critical public health challenge, with approximately 47 million people affected worldwide in 2015, a number projected to reach 132 million by 2050. Upholding dignity is widely recognized as a core ethical obligation in dementia care. However, the perspectives of healthcare professionals, who serve as the direct practitioners of dignity care, have not yet been systematically synthesized.AimThis study aims to systematically synthesize qualitative research on healthcare professionals' perspectives regarding dignity care for patients with dementia in order to inform clinical practice, care management, and policy development.MethodsThis study followed the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ). A systematic search was conducted across ten Chinese and English databases, including PubMed, from database inception to November 25, 2025. Two reviewers independently screened articles against eligibility criteria and assessed quality using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) checklist. A total of 13 studies were included, comprising qualitative studies and the qualitative components of mixed methods studies that explored healthcare professionals' perspectives on dignity care for patients with dementia. Data were synthesized using Thomas and Harden's thematic synthesis approach.ResultsThe synthesis generated four analytical themes and twelve subthemes: (1) the ethical foundation of dignity care; (2) multidimensional practice strategies for dignity care; (3) dilemmas and adaptive strategies in dignity care; and (4) influencing factors of dignity care. Across these themes, healthcare professionals described central tensions involving the dynamic balance between authenticity and protection, autonomy support and necessary care, and professional boundaries and emotional connection.ConclusionDignity care for patients with dementia is not a standardized technical procedure, but a dynamic, context-bound practice grounded in ethical relationships. Its realization requires not only individual ethical sensitivity and practical wisdom but also supportive organizational cultures, adequate institutional resources, and broader societal recognition of the value of elderly care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01500-9.

Introduction

In the context of global population aging, dementia has become an increasingly critical public health challenge. In 2015, approximately 47 million people worldwide were living with dementia, accounting for about 5% of the total elderly population [1]. This number is projected to reach 75 million by 2030 and 132 million by 2050 [1]. As dementia progresses, patients with Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias often lose decision-making capacity, giving rise to complex ethical challenges related to autonomy, surrogate decision making, dignity, privacy, and justice [2]. Addressing these challenges requires professionals with experience in dignity care for patients with dementia and ethical sensitivity, working in multidisciplinary teams to provide continuous care from the early stage of the disease through the end of life [1].

In this context, how to effectively uphold patients’ dignity while improving their quality of life has become a central issue in high-quality care. Since the mid-2010s, the concept of dignity has gained widespread attention in the field of dementia care, where it is regarded as a fundamental right and an imperative of care [3]. Dignity is generally understood as the recognition and preservation of an individual’s inherent worth, including respect for their sense of self and capacity for expression, regardless of physical, psychological, or cognitive condition [4]. Conceptual analyses have further distinguished between absolute dignity, referring to the inherent and inalienable value of human beings, and relative dignity, which is shaped and influenced by social and relational contexts [4]. This distinction highlights the importance of care practices in sustaining dignity, particularly for individuals experiencing cognitive decline. For people living with dementia, dignity cannot be understood as a static attribute, but rather as something that is dependent on how others recognize, interpret, and respond to them in care interactions. In this study, we adopt the relational perspective on dignity proposed by Denier and Gastmans [5] as an interpretive lens. This perspective conceptualizes dignity as co-constructed within care relationships, grounded in relational autonomy, vulnerability, and embodied dignity. Importantly, this framework is used to support the interpretation of findings rather than to guide data coding, thereby preserving the inductive nature of the thematic synthesis. Upholding dignity has not only become a key principle of care but also a professional obligation that must be fulfilled by care providers [6].

Previous reviews have explored dignity care for patients with dementia from various perspectives. Tranvag et al. [7] conducted an early meta-synthesis in 2013; however, the included literature is now dated, and the analysis primarily reflected patients’ perspectives. Fang et al. [8] focused on dignity practices across different care settings but their review did not specifically address healthcare professionals’ ethical experiences. Cruise and Lashewicz [9] focused on family caregivers, while Liang et al. [10] examined caregiving more broadly without limiting the analysis to dementia care.

Healthcare professionals occupy a distinct and pivotal role in the enactment of dignity care. Unlike patients or family caregivers, they are directly responsible for translating ethical values into concrete care practices within complex clinical environments. Their perspectives reflect not only experiential understanding but also ongoing ethical decision-making, clinical judgment, and negotiation of contextual constraints such as institutional policies, resource limitations, and workload pressures. For example, previous research has shown that nurses frequently encounter ethical tensions between autonomy and safety, authenticity and protection, and substituted decision making and respect for patient preferences, all of which directly shape whether dignity is upheld in practice [11]. Similarly, decisions regarding the use of restraint or persuasion in the absence of patient capacity have been shown to have direct implications for the preservation or violation of dignity [12]. Institutional factors such as staffing levels, shift patterns, and care environments further influence how dignity can be enacted in practice [13]. Taken together, these findings suggest that synthesizing healthcare professionals’ perspectives is essential for understanding the practical logic of dignity care.

Qualitative research, through in-depth interviews, focus groups, and related methods, provides access to healthcare professionals’ lived experiences and interpretations, offering valuable insights into how dignity is understood and enacted in practice [14]. Therefore, conducting a meta-synthesis of qualitative studies allows for a systematic integration of these perspectives. In this study, we adopt an inductive approach and do not impose predefined dimensions of dignity at the outset. Instead, we aim to synthesize qualitative evidence on healthcare professionals’ perspectives on dignity care for patients with dementia, allowing key dimensions to emerge from the data. This approach enables the identification of important but potentially under-recognized aspects of dignity care, particularly those embedded in everyday clinical practice, thereby providing evidence-based guidance and reference for clinical practice, nursing management, and policy development. To our knowledge, no previous meta-synthesis has systematically examined dignity in dementia care from an inductive, healthcare professional–centered perspective. Addressing this gap represents the central contribution of the present study.

Methods

Aims and design

This study aims to synthesize existing qualitative research on healthcare professionals’ perspectives regarding dignity care for patients with dementia. The review was conducted in accordance with the Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the Synthesis of Qualitative Research (ENTREQ) guidelines [15]. The study protocol was registered with PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251153682).

Search strategy

To ensure comprehensiveness of the search, we conducted systematic searches in ten databases: China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), Wanfang Database, VIP Database, Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (SinoMed), PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Cochrane Library, and Psychological Information Database (PsycINFO). Search terms consisted of MeSH terms and free text words, categorized into three groups based on the target population, phenomenon of interest, and study design, including terms such as dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, dignity, dignity care, and qualitative research. During the pilot search phase, we found that including search terms explicitly referring to healthcare professionals led to the unintended exclusion of relevant studies. Therefore, such terms were not included in the final search strategy, and relevance to healthcare professionals was determined during the screening stage. To enhance coverage, we manually screened the reference lists of all included studies and relevant systematic reviews to identify additional studies not captured by database searches. No date restrictions were applied, and the final search was conducted on November 25, 2025, across all databases to ensure consistency. The full search strategy is presented in Appendix A. Inclusion/exclusion criteria

This study used the PICo (Population, Interest of phenomenon, Context) framework to define eligibility criteria.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) P (Population): healthcare professionals directly involved in care provision, including but not limited to physicians, registered nurses, and therapists; (2) I (Phenomenon of Interest): studies exploring healthcare professionals’ perceptions, understandings, experiences, beliefs, attitudes, or practices related to maintaining or enhancing dignity care for patients with dementia. To ensure conceptual clarity and reproducibility, the concept of dignity was explicitly operationalized in the study selection process. Studies were included if they either (a) explicitly used the term “dignity” or related expressions, or (b) addressed conceptually related constructs such as autonomy, respect, personhood, identity, or privacy, provided that these constructs were clearly linked to the preservation, negotiation, or potential violation of dignity care contexts. Studies that addressed these related constructs in isolation, without an explicit or interpretable connection to dignity, were excluded. In cases where relevance to dignity was unclear, two reviewers independently assessed eligibility based on predefined conceptual criteria, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the research team. This approach ensured alignment between the conceptual framework and the study selection process, thereby enhancing transparency and reproducibility; (3) Co (Context): any setting in which healthcare professionals provide direct care to patients with dementia; (4) Study design: all types of qualitative studies and the qualitative components of mixed methods studies, with publications limited to Chinese or English language and full text available.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) Studies involving only family members or informal caregivers; (2) Studies not addressing dignity care; (3) Quantitative studies, mixed methods studies from which qualitative data could not be extracted, reviews, conference abstracts, commentaries, and other non-original research literature; (4) Duplicate publications or studies for which full text could not be obtained. Grey literature was excluded to maintain methodological consistency and focus on peer-reviewed empirical evidence; however, this may have limited the inclusion of emerging or practice-based insights.

Quality appraisal of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) qualitative studies checklist [16]. The purpose of the quality assessment was to evaluate the methodological rigor, transparency, and applicability of the included studies to ensure the credibility of the synthesized findings. The CASP checklist consists of ten questions, with response options of “Yes”, “No”, or “Can not tell”. Each item was scored as follows: “Yes” (1 point), “Can not tell” (0.5 points), and “No” (0 points) [17]. The overall quality score for each study was calculated by summing the scores across all items, and studies were subsequently classified into three levels: low quality (CASP score 0-5.5), moderate quality (6-8.5), and high quality (9–10) [18]. Two researchers (JSN and FCX) independently assessed each study. The results were compared and discussed, and any disagreements were resolved through consultation with a third reviewer (JY). The quality appraisal was not used as a criterion for study inclusion, but rather to inform the interpretation of findings and to provide contextual understanding of the included studies [19].

Data extraction and analysis

All retrieved articles were imported into EndNote X9, and duplicate records were removed. Two researchers (JSN and FCX), trained in qualitative research and evidence-based nursing, independently screened titles and abstracts according to the predefined eligibility criteria. Studies that did not meet the eligibility criteria were excluded. Full texts of potentially relevant studies were then independently assessed by the same reviewers. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with consultation of a third reviewer (JY) when necessary. The extracted data included authors, year, aim, sample size, methodology, and results. For the purpose of synthesis, the data subjected to analysis consisted primarily of material reported in the “Results” or “Findings” sections of the included studies, including both participant quotations and authors’ interpretive accounts, as these represent the primary carriers of meaning in qualitative research. Where necessary, relevant contextual information from the discussion sections was also considered to support interpretation.

Thematic synthesis was conducted following the three-stage approach described by Thomas and Harden [20]. First, the extracted findings from all included studies were compiled into a single dataset, organized in a Word document, and repeatedly read by three reviewers (JSN, FCX, and JY) to achieve familiarity with the content. Second, line-by-line coding was performed on the extracted data, with codes generated inductively based on the meaning and content of the text. Third, codes were compared and grouped according to similarities and differences, and organized into broader categories. These categories were then developed into descriptive themes that captured healthcare professionals’ perspectives on dignity care for patients with dementia. Finally, analytical themes were generated through an interpretive process that went beyond the content of the original studies, enabling a higher-level understanding of the underlying patterns across studies. In this review, these analytical themes are presented as the synthesized findings.

Throughout the process, discrepancies were resolved through discussion with the third reviewer (JY). All stages of the analytical process were reviewed by the research team to ensure consistency, transparency, and adequacy of the interpretations. An audit trail of analytical decisions was maintained in Word documents to enhance methodological transparency. Importantly, no predefined theoretical framework was applied during the coding process. Instead, themes were generated inductively from the data. Ethical dimensions were not specified as part of the study aim or search strategy, but emerged during the synthesis as recurring patterns in healthcare professionals’ accounts. This inductive approach ensured openness of the analysis and allowed key dimensions of dignity care to be identified from the perspectives of healthcare professionals.

Ethical considerations

The data presented in this review are entirely sourced from previously published studies and do not involve any new experiments on animals or humans. Therefore, this review does not require ethical approval.

Results

Search outcome

Figure 1 presents the study selection process. A total of 4917 studies were identified through database searching. After removing duplicates and screening by title and abstract, 54 records were retained for full-text assessment. In addition, manual screening of reference lists from relevant reviews and included studies identified two additional studies. Ultimately, 13 studies [12, 14, 21–31] met the criteria for inclusion in the meta-synthesis.

Study characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the key characteristics of the included studies. Qualitative interviews were the most commonly used data collection method, with semi-structured interviews being predominant. Some studies incorporated observational methods or focus groups to enrich the data dimensions. With regard to data analysis, thematic analysis and phenomenological hermeneutic approaches were the most frequently applied methods, while other approaches included content analysis. The studies were conducted across six different countries: Israel (n = 2), the United Kingdom (n = 2), Japan (n = 2), and Sweden, Norway, Australia, China, Denmark, Nepal, and the Netherlands (each n = 1). The geographic distribution of studies is illustrated in Fig. 2. Across these studies, healthcare professionals described dignity care as a multidimensional concept, emphasizing aspects such as privacy protection, supportive care environments, communication practices, and patient autonomy and participation.

Synthesis of study findings

The synthesis generated four analytical themes and twelve subthemes that capture how healthcare professionals understand and enact dignity care for patients with dementia. From their perspectives, these themes reflect both the ethical foundations and the practical realities of dignity care, as well as the dilemmas and contextual factors influencing its implementation. The four themes were: (1) the ethical foundation of dignity care; (2) multidimensional practice strategies for dignity care; (3) dilemmas and adaptive strategies in dignity care; (4) influencing factors of dignity care.

Theme one: the ethical foundation of dignity care

Although ethical dimensions were not predefined as an analytical focus, they emerged inductively during line-by-line coding and theme development. Healthcare professionals consistently described aspects such as respect for fundamental dignity, recognition of equal status, and sustaining biographical dignity, which together formed a coherent ethical dimension. This suggests that, in healthcare professionals’ accounts, dignity care is inherently understood and articulated as an ethical practice. Within this theme, healthcare professionals described dignity as being enacted through three interrelated dimensions: respecting fundamental dignity, recognizing equal status, and sustaining biographical dignity. These dimensions reflect how dignity is understood in practice rather than representing predefined theoretical categories.

Respect fundamental dignity

Healthcare professionals described respect for fundamental dignity, corresponding to Nordenfelt’s concept of Menschenwürde [32], as the foundation of care. This form of dignity refers to the inherent and inalienable worth of all human beings, which remains intact regardless of cognitive impairment. From their perspectives, respecting fundamental dignity represents the baseline of care, requiring that the intrinsic value of the patient is never compromised [28]. By ‘baseline,’ we mean the minimum, non-negotiable standard of care that must be afforded to every patient regardless of cognitive status, clinical complexity, or institutional constraints; care falling below this threshold is considered fundamentally undignified. This principle was reflected in multiple aspects of practice. At the level of physical care, maintaining personal hygiene and protecting bodily privacy were described as essential expressions of dignity [23]. In end-of-life care, healthcare professionals emphasized that dignity should be equally upheld even in the final stages of life [23]. In everyday interactions, explaining procedures in advance and guiding cooperation through reasoning rather than coercion were also described as important ways of respecting dignity [30].

Respect the PWD and protect their privacy, especially when using toilet and bath, to reduce Embarrassment [28].

Recognize equal status

Healthcare professionals described recognizing equal status as requiring them to move beyond disease-related assumptions and biases. Despite cognitive impairment, patients with dementia were consistently viewed as equal moral subjects whose dignity must be upheld [31]. In practice, this was reflected in treating patients as individuals rather than defining them by their diagnosis, investing time to listen to their needs, and making efforts to respond to those needs [26, 31]. Healthcare professionals also emphasized the importance of respecting patients’ current values, preferences, and identities, including accepting who they have become over the course of the illness [14]. I think everyone has equal rights to live as a human being. People with dementia need to be treated in the same way we treat other people. Yah, despite dementia, they need to be treated well. They need to have cleanliness and hygiene. We can’t leave them in a dirty condition. They need good food and proper cleanliness like other people. Even though, they don’t understand, their health needs those things [31].

Sustain biographical dignity

Healthcare professionals described sustaining biographical dignity as extending beyond how patients are treated in the present to include recognition of their past experiences and life history. From their perspectives, dignity involves not only protecting patients’ personal identity [26] and sense of self [31] but also acknowledging their pre-disease status [14] as well as their past efforts and achievements [31]. They emphasized that dignity is closely linked to the experiences and relationships accumulated over a lifetime. When healthcare professionals are able to recognize who the patient was in the past, they are better able to understand who the patient is in the present, thereby providing care that more effectively upholds dignity.

Theme two: multidimensional practice strategies for dignity care

Healthcare professionals described multiple interconnected ways of enacting dignity care within their everyday practice. These practices were not presented as isolated technical procedures, but as relationally embedded actions within ongoing care interactions. The synthesis identified four interrelated subthemes: relational practices, physical and daily living care, autonomy and identity practices, and context-specific practices. From healthcare professionals’ perspectives, these practices were characterized by the need to continuously balance multiple ethical considerations in practice. This dynamic balancing was reflected in three recurring tensions described across studies: balancing authenticity and protection, balancing support for autonomy with necessary care, and balancing professional boundaries with emotional connection. The following subthemes illustrate how these tensions are negotiated in practice.

Relational practices

Healthcare professionals described relational practices as central to the enactment of dignity care. They emphasized the importance of building emotional connections with patients by understanding their individual characteristics and personal histories, thereby recognizing them as individuals with unique life trajectories [21, 29]. Communication was described as a key component of these relational practices. This included friendly interactions [26], maintaining patience [27], explaining procedures in advance, and guiding cooperation through reasoning rather than coercion [30]. Healthcare professionals also described the complexity of communication in situations involving truth-telling. When conveying distressing information, such as the death of a family member, they reported the need to carefully balance honesty with the avoidance of harm [22], Similarly, they described the deliberate use of topic avoidance [12], and the ethical challenges associated with the use of so-called benevolent deception in dementia care [23], reflecting an ongoing tension between authenticity and protection. They further identified practices that undermine dignity, including infantilizing language, deceptive manipulation, objectifying conversations, humiliation, and accusatory questioning. From their perspectives, maintaining dignity in communication requires treating patients as equal participants in interaction [30]. In addition, healthcare professionals described the need to interpret patients’ ambiguous or non-verbal expressions, striving to understand and respond to needs that may not be explicitly articulated [29, 30]. They also emphasized the importance of maintaining patients’ social relationships, including supporting family connections [29], facilitating opportunities for social participation [14], and fostering a sense of belonging [31].

Although listening to clients repeat the same thing endlessly can be exhausting, it is crucial to remain patient [27].

Although listening to clients repeat the same thing endlessly can be exhausting, it is crucial to remain patient [27].

Physical and daily living care

Healthcare professionals described the body as a central site through which dignity is expressed in care. From their perspectives, upholding dignity involves protecting patients’ physical privacy [23, 26] and respecting their autonomy when assisting with personal hygiene and grooming [14]. They also emphasized the importance of acknowledging patients’ needs for sexuality and intimacy, noting that these should not be overlooked due to disease severity or age [23]. In addition, they described dignity care as requiring a holistic approach that addresses both physical and psychological well-being, extending beyond disease treatment to promote overall quality of life [14].

Among us, in the Arab sector, let’s say it is disrespectful for a man to expose himself [i.e. his body] to everyone. Maybe in other sectors it is more acceptable. [But in our sector] it is unacceptable, it is difficult for me to expose [the body of] an Arab woman. I always look for a female nurse that will take care of her [26].

Autonomy and identity practices

Healthcare professionals described respect for patients’ thoughts and preferences as involving active listening and efforts to respond to their expressed needs, in order to prevent patients from feeling overlooked or dismissed [12, 21, 29, 31]. They also emphasized the importance of accepting patients’ changing values and abilities as the disease progresses [14, 29]. Enhancing patients’ sense of autonomy was described as involving support for self-efficacy, including encouraging participation in tasks within their capabilities [30], affirming their value [30], and facilitating engagement in personally meaningful activities [31]. Providing individualized attention requires treating patients as unique individuals and being willing to spend time with them [26, 27]. However, healthcare professionals also reported that such practices are often constrained by limited time and resources, leading to what they described as a prioritization dilemma in everyday care [12].

It’s really busy in the mornings. Even chaotic. Eight morning care patients . Those who shout loudest get most help. It’s difficult to prioritize among them . A lot gets done as a matter of routine [12].

Context specific practices

Healthcare professionals described dignity care as highly dependent on specific clinical contexts. In the context of diagnosis disclosure, they emphasized the need to balance honesty with compassion in order to preserve patients’ dignity when confronting illness [23]. In end-of-life care, they stressed that patients should not be devalued on the basis of a dementia diagnosis. Instead, care should focus on ensuring comfort and maintaining dignity throughout the dying process [23, 29].

If you have dementia, it is slightly different from other illnesses. But just because you have dementia, I do not think that the way you die should be different [29].

If you have dementia, it is slightly different from other illnesses. But just because you have dementia, I do not think that the way you die should be different [29].

Theme three: dilemmas and adaptive strategies in dignity care

Healthcare professionals described a range of dilemmas encountered in the practice of dignity care, as well as the strategies they use to respond to these challenges. From their perspectives, these dilemmas often arise from a disconnection between patients’ subjective experiences and objective reality. In response, healthcare professionals reported developing adaptive strategies to navigate these tensions in practice.

The root of the dilemmas

Healthcare professionals described the fundamental dilemmas in dignity care as arising from two interrelated dimensions. First, they reported a disconnection between patients’ subjective experience and objective reality. For individuals with cognitive impairment, there is often a mismatch between their internal psychological world and the external environment, making it difficult for care interventions based on objective assessments to align with patients’ perceived needs [22]. Second, they described the progressive decline associated with dementia as contributing to patients’ concerns about loss of autonomy and independence, which in turn may undermine self-esteem and sense of dignity [27]. These tensions were described as recurring and inherent to everyday care practice.

They really enjoy special activities like excursions or looking at cherry blossoms. When the wife of a former company president said to us ‘You are my employee’, we played along [27].

They really enjoy special activities like excursions or looking at cherry blossoms. When the wife of a former company president said to us ‘You are my employee’, we played along [27].

Adaptive strategies

In response to these dilemmas, healthcare professionals described two main types of adaptive strategies, which they apply flexibly depending on the patient’s condition and care context. The strategy of “guiding back to reality” aims to direct patients toward the real world to ensure safety and health. This includes straightforward medication administration, that is, insisting on medical requirements and explicitly stating the need to take medication [22], as well as behavioral restraint, that is, implementing minimal restraint to ensure safety [12]. In contrast, the strategy of “entering the patient’s world” takes the patient’s subjective world as its frame of reference, aiming to integrate into their cognition to achieve care goals. Healthcare professionals described practices such as redirecting attention, engaging in role play, and, in some cases, administering medication covertly by incorporating it into food, in order to reduce resistance and maintain trust [22].

Theme four: influencing factors of dignity care

Healthcare professionals described multiple factors that influence the implementation of dignity care. These factors were inductively organized into three interrelated levels: individual, organizational, and societal. Together, these levels illustrate the broader conditions shaping how dignity care is enacted in practice.

Individual and professional competence factors

Healthcare professionals described skills and training as important factors influencing their ability to uphold patients’ dignity [24]. Beyond technical competence, they emphasized the role of personal development and moral commitment. In their accounts, experiences of external devaluation and stigma prompted reflection on professional identity, leading them to seek internal validation and reaffirm their ethical stance. Through navigating ethical dilemmas, they described undergoing forms of psychological and moral development that supported a sustained commitment to respectful care [25].

Obviously when you train to be a nurse you go through so many different placements every year and they normally do throw in a health care of the elderly placement. So that’s all the real training you get [24].

Team and organizational culture factors

Healthcare professionals described team collaboration and organizational culture as central to the delivery of dignity care [28]. Effective communication and cooperation within teams were identified as key enablers of dignity care practices. They also emphasized the importance of the physical care environment. For example, the design and conditions of specific settings, such as emergency departments, were described as directly influencing patients’ experiences of dignity [24]. Organizational culture was described as an implicit but powerful factor shaping care practices, influencing how values related to dignity are prioritized and enacted in daily work [24]. In addition, healthcare professionals noted that variations in patient populations may present different risks to dignity, requiring flexible and context-sensitive responses [12].

Societal and systemic factors

Healthcare professionals described broader societal and systemic conditions as shaping the possibilities for dignity care. They highlighted how healthcare system structures, such as emergency department processes, influence both the manner and quality of care delivery [24]. They also pointed to the wider social context, noting that elderly care is often subject to devaluation and stigma, which can affect both professional identity and care practices [25]. Healthcare professionals reported experiencing pressures not only from institutional systems but also from broader societal attitudes toward aging and dementia [25]. At the same time, they emphasized that increased societal awareness, cultural understanding, and acceptance of dementia can create more supportive conditions for dignity care, benefiting both patients and healthcare professionals [28].

Discussion

This meta-synthesis of 13 qualitative studies provides an integrated account of healthcare professionals’ understandings and practical experiences of dignity care for patients with dementia. The synthesis generated four interrelated themes: the ethical foundation of dignity care, multidimensional practice strategies for dignity care, dilemmas and adaptive strategies in dignity care, and influencing factors of dignity care. Together, these findings highlight the multidimensional and context-sensitive nature of dignity care and provide an empirically grounded perspective on how healthcare professionals enact dignity care in everyday practice.

How the relational dignity framework informs interpretation

The findings of this synthesis may also be usefully considered in light of the relational dignity framework proposed by Denier and Gastmans [5]. From this perspective, dignity can be understood not merely as a fixed attribute, but as something that is potentially formed and sustained within care relationships.

From the perspective of relational autonomy, Theme One highlights how dignity is enacted through interaction and shared understanding rather than residing solely in the individual. Healthcare professionals described practices such as respecting fundamental dignity, recognizing equal status, and sustaining biographical dignity. From a relational perspective, autonomy may be understood in similar terms, as enacted through interaction, dialogue, and shared understanding rather than existing as an isolated individual capacity [5].

From the perspective of relational autonomy, Theme Two highlights how this concept may manifest in practice. Healthcare professionals described supporting patients’ sense of agency by fostering responsibility, affirming personal value, and facilitating engagement in meaningful activities. They also described the need to navigate between guiding patients toward reality and engaging with patients’ subjective worlds, which can be seen as reflecting the practical enactment of relational autonomy in complex care contexts. In addition, practices related to physical and daily care, such as protecting privacy, respecting personal hygiene, and ensuring comfort in end-of-life care, can be interpreted in light of the concept of embodied dignity [5]. From this perspective, dignity persists even when cognitive capacity is diminished, as it is grounded in the person’s embodied dignity.

From the perspective of relational autonomy, Theme Three highlights certain tensions that arise when patients’ subjective experiences diverge from objective reality. In such situations, healthcare professionals described the need to engage with patients’ perspectives rather than imposing external interpretations. Practices such as “entering the patient’s world” were not framed as abandoning truthfulness, but as recognizing and responding to patients’ lived experience.

From the perspective of vulnerability, Theme Four highlights how systemic devaluation and stigma may reflect social dimensions of vulnerability that extend beyond the individual. According to Denier and Gastmans [5], vulnerability encompasses inherent human vulnerability, situational vulnerability, and their interaction. In this synthesis, healthcare professionals described how systemic devaluation and stigma toward elderly care reflect forms of social vulnerability that shape the relational conditions necessary for dignity care. From this perspective, the realization of dignity care depends not only on interactions between healthcare professionals and patients, but also on the broader social context within which these interactions take place.

Importantly, the relational dignity framework is applied here as an interpretive lens to support the analysis of findings, rather than as a predefined analytical framework guiding coding. This ensures that the inductive nature of the synthesis is preserved. The framework is therefore used heuristically, and the connections drawn between the framework and the findings represent one possible interpretation rather than the only or necessary reading.

The ethical foundation of dignity care

The synthesis suggests that healthcare professionals consistently described dignity care in terms of three interrelated dimensions: respect for fundamental dignity, recognition of equal status, and sustaining biographical dignity. These dimensions closely align with Nordenfelt’s [32] typology of dignity, although it should be noted that this alignment reflects the present authors’ interpretive reading of the primary studies rather than a claim that healthcare professionals themselves explicitly invoked this typology.

In particular, the emphasis on biographical dignity, referring to the dignity derived from a person’s life history, achievements, and social relationships, was evident across a number of the included studies. Healthcare professionals’ emphasis on recognizing who the patient was before the onset of illness resonates with Kitwood’s [33] person-centered care philosophy, which highlights the importance of acknowledging patients’ life histories and engaging with their experiences in an empathetic manner.

Across the included studies, healthcare professionals described these three dimensions as being enacted within care relationships rather than existing as fixed attributes. From their perspectives, dignity was understood as something that is sustained through interaction, rather than as an inherent and unchanging property of the individual. Reading these accounts through a contemporary ethical lens, one may observe a degree of convergence with the concept of relational autonomy [5] which conceptualizes autonomy as emerging within relationships involving healthcare professionals, patients, and families. In this context, respect for autonomy is not limited to acknowledging individual decision-making capacity, but involves an ongoing relational process that attends to patients’ needs, preferences, and lived experiences. For patients with cognitive impairment, healthcare professionals described recognition and responsiveness as central conditions for sustaining dignity, a finding consistent with Kisvetrová et al. [34]. In this sense, dignity care involves not only acting in the patient’s best interest, but engaging with the patient in ways that support shared understanding and meaningful participation.

Overall, these findings suggest that, from the perspective of the healthcare professionals included in this synthesis, dignity care can be understood as a relational and practice-based ethical process, rather than a set of abstract principles.

Multidimensional practice strategies for dignity care

The synthesis suggests that healthcare professionals described four interrelated domains of practice: relational practices, physical and daily living care, autonomy and identity practices, and context-specific practices. These domains were not presented as isolated technical procedures, but as interconnected aspects of care that require continuous balancing of multiple ethical considerations across different contexts.

One key dimension concerns the balance between authenticity and protection. Healthcare professionals described situations such as delivering difficult information, responding to repetitive questions, or addressing delusional experiences, in which honesty must be weighed against the need to avoid harm. Hertogh et al. [22] highlighted similar ethical tensions when patients’ subjective experiences diverge from objective reality. Healthcare professionals described two adaptive approaches in response to these situations: guiding patients toward reality and engaging with the patient’s subjective world. These approaches were applied flexibly depending on the patient’s condition and care context. This suggests that dignity care is enacted through context-sensitive ethical judgment rather than fixed rules.

A second dimension involves balancing support for autonomy with the provision of necessary care. Healthcare professionals described efforts to preserve patients’ autonomy by encouraging participation, affirming personal value, and facilitating engagement in meaningful activities, even as care needs increase due to cognitive decline. Opportunities to experience positive aspects such as emotional function, sense of meaning, social inclusion, and life satisfaction serve as important psychological resources for patients in coping with the disease [35], helping to prevent potential disease-related harm to dignity [36]. At the same time, healthcare professionals described constraints such as time pressure and resource limitations, which contribute to a prioritization dilemma in practice [12]. In such situations, patients who are more vocal or demanding may receive disproportionate attention, highlighting how institutional conditions can shape ethical decision-making. These findings suggest that the realization of dignity care depends not only on individual ethical awareness but also on supportive organizational conditions.

A third dimension concerns the integration of professional competence and emotional engagement. Healthcare professionals described the importance of combining clinical judgment with genuine emotional connection. Practices such as understanding patients’ individual characteristics, interpreting their ambiguous expressions, and maintaining their social relationships exemplify this integration, contributing to the enhancement of patients’ identity recognition-a direction for the development of dignity care [9]. Walmsley and McCormack [25] noted that when healthcare professionals experience systemic stigmatization, seeking internal validation and redefining professional identity can become important psychological resources that sustain their commitment to dignity care. These findings highlight both the emotional demands and the moral resilience involved in dignity care. They also suggest that the dignity of healthcare professionals themselves, as those responsible for upholding patients’ dignity, requires recognition and support. From healthcare professionals’ perspectives, these are not abstract strategies but practical judgments and trade-offs encountered in everyday care.

Dilemmas and adaptive strategies in dignity care

The synthesis suggests that healthcare professionals consistently described a central dilemma in dignity care arising from the disconnection between patients’ subjective experiences and objective reality. From the accounts provided in the included studies, this tension creates complex ethical challenges in everyday practice. Healthcare professionals described practices such as engaging with the patient’s subjective world, including role play, covert medication administration, and redirection of attention. These practices may appear to conflict with principles such as truthfulness; however, they were framed as ways of responding to patients’ lived experiences and minimizing distress in specific contexts. Within broader ethical frameworks, these practices can be interpreted as context-sensitive applications of principles such as beneficence and respect for persons [37], rather than simple deviations from ethical norms. This interpretation is also consistent with Honneth’s theory of recognition [38], which emphasizes that dignity is sustained through processes of recognition, including care, rights, and social affirmation. Patients with dementia are precisely the subjects most in need of recognition, yet they are also the group most susceptible to being treated as objects. Importantly, healthcare professionals described these dilemmas not as abstract ethical problems, but as practical challenges that require immediate and context-dependent responses in everyday care.

Influencing factors of dignity care

The synthesis suggests that healthcare professionals described three interrelated levels of factors influencing dignity care: individual, organizational, and societal. These levels reflect the complex conditions under which dignity care is enacted in practice.

At the individual level, healthcare professionals described both skills and moral commitment as shaping their capacity to provide dignity care. At the organizational level, factors such as team collaboration, ward culture, and resource allocation were described as influencing the accessibility and continuity of dignity care. At the societal level, broader conditions, including systemic stigmatization of the elderly care sector, public attitudes toward dementia, and health policy orientations, were described as shaping the wider environment for dignity care.

Healthcare professionals further emphasized that systemic devaluation and stigmatization of the elderly care sector [25] affect not only their professional identity but also the quality of care delivered. When elderly care is perceived as low-skilled or low-value work, healthcare professionals described a diminished sense of professional dignity, which may in turn influence their capacity and motivation to provide dignity care. These findings suggest that the realization of dignity care depends not only on individual ethical sensitivity but also on institutional-level support. The appropriate allocation of care resources, recognition of the value of care work, and institutional safeguards for care relationships [39] constitute the foundational conditions for the implementation of dignity care. From healthcare professionals’ perspectives, these factors were not seen as background conditions, but as decisive influences on whether dignity care can be realized in everyday practice.

Comparison with previous studies

The findings of this synthesis complement and extend those of previous reviews. Tranvag et al. [7] primarily focused on patients’ perspective, whereas the present synthesis highlights healthcare professionals’ experiential and ethical perspectives on dignity care. Fang et al. [8] examined variations in dignity practices across different care settings. In contrast, the present synthesis, using an inductive thematic approach, did not predefine ethical dimensions as an analytical focus. Instead, ethical aspects such as respect for fundamental dignity, tensions between truthfulness and protection, and the balance between autonomy and necessary care emerged directly from healthcare professionals’ accounts as cross-contextual themes. These findings suggest that the two reviews are complementary. While Fang et al. [8] provide context-specific insights into care practices, the present synthesis identifies underlying ethical patterns that operate across different care environments. Notably, this ethical dimension has received limited systematic attention in previous syntheses from the perspective of healthcare professionals. Cruise and Lashewicz [9] focused on family caregivers, whereas the present synthesis centers on the ethical experiences of professional healthcare workers within institutional constraints. Liang et al. [10] did not limit their focus to a specific population, whereas this synthesis highlights the distinct ethical needs of patients with dementia arising from cognitive impairment: healthcare professionals’ recognition and responsiveness are critical to sustaining dignity. This inductive finding has dual significance at both the substantive and methodological levels. From a substantive perspective, the findings suggest that healthcare professionals experience dignity care primarily as an ethical practice structured around value tensions, such as those between authenticity and protection and between autonomy and necessary care, rather than as a set of technical procedures. From a methodological perspective, the findings suggest potential value of inductive synthesis in revealing dimensions of care that may be less apparent when reviews are guided by predefined conceptual frameworks. Although ethics holds a prominent place in primary research, previous reviews [7–9] have not systematically highlighted the ethical experiences of healthcare professionals. This suggests that, from the perspective of practitioners, ethical considerations represent a previously under-recognized but central dimension of dignity care.

Ethical and practical implications

This study has multiple implications for clinical practice, nursing management, ethical education, and policy development. The following implications are offered as considerations arising from the findings of this synthesis, drawing primarily on the accounts of healthcare professionals from the 13 included studies. These suggestions are intended to inform reflection and discussion rather than to serve as definitive prescriptions.

At the level of clinical practice, the findings suggest that healthcare professionals may benefit from recognizing the multidimensional and context-sensitive nature of dignity care and develop the ability to maintain a dynamic balance, such as between authenticity and protection. Specifically, attention should be paid to preserving biographical dignity, including expressing empathy toward patients with dementia, strengthening their social connections, and reviewing their life histories [40]. At the same time, infantilizing, objectifying, and degrading forms of communication should be avoided, and patients should be treated as equal participants in interaction. Strategies that involve engaging with the patient’s subjective world should be applied flexibly, in ways that respect patients’ lived experience while ensuring safety.

At the level of nursing management, research conducted in Australia indicates that creating a dedicated environment and having time to engage in conversation with patients with dementia are crucial for dignity care [41]. Accordingly, the findings point to the value of healthcare institutions considering optimized human resource allocation to alleviate time pressures on healthcare professionals and establish organizational conditions that support dignity care. In addition, fostering team cultures that encourage ethical reflection and shared learning is essential.

At the level of ethical education, nursing education and continuing education may incorporate case analysis and situational simulation [42] to enhance learners’ ethical sensitivity and context-specific decision-making. Particular attention should be given to relational perspectives on dignity, enabling learners to understand how dignity is co-constructed within care relationships.

At the level of policy development, systemic discrimination against the elderly care sector should be addressed by improving the social status and economic conditions of care professionals. At the same time, promoting public understanding and acceptance of dementia is necessary to reduce stigma and create a more supportive societal environment for dignity care [43].

Conclusion

This meta-synthesis of 13 qualitative studies provides an integrated account of healthcare professionals’ understandings and practical experiences of dignity care for patients with dementia. The findings suggest that dignity care is not a standardized technical procedure, but a dynamic and context-sensitive practice grounded in ethical relationships. Healthcare professionals described the need to continuously interpret and sustain dignity through attention to patients’ past, present, and evolving identities, while navigating tensions such as authenticity and protection, autonomy and necessary care, and professional distance and emotional connection. The realization of dignity care depends not only on individual ethical sensitivity and practical judgment, but also on supportive organizational cultures, adequate institutional conditions, and broader societal recognition of the value of care. Taken together, the findings suggest that dignity care may need to be expanded from an individual ethical endeavor to a broader social and institutional commitment if the dignity of patients with dementia is to be sustainably upheld in practice.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the included studies were predominantly conducted in high-income or middle-income countries, with limited representation from regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and Latin America, which may restrict the cultural generalizability of the findings. Second, most included studies did not adequately explore the influence of the relationship between researchers and participants on the study findings, reflected in generally low scores on item 6 of the CASP checklist. This may affect the transparency of the primary studies. Third, this synthesis relies on the content reported in the primary studies and cannot capture unreported practice details or contextual factors. Fourth, most included studies relied on interview data, with relatively few employing observational methods. As a result, the synthesis may not fully capture potential discrepancies between reported perspectives and actual practice.

Future research can be extended in the following directions. First, cross-cultural comparative studies could be conducted to explore the universality and cultural specificity of dignity care. Second, research methods combining observation with interviews could be employed to gain deeper insights into the actual clinical practice of dignity care. Third, attention could be directed to dignity care practices in low and middle-income countries to reveal strategies for upholding dignity in resource-limited settings. Fourth, intervention studies could be undertaken to evaluate the actual impact of dignity care training, organizational changes, and policy interventions on patients’ dignity and quality of care.
