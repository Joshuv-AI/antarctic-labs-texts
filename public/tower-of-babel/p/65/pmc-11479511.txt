The association between triglyceride glucose index and suicidal ideation in American adults: Results from NHANES 2005 to 2018
Authors: Yuqiu Lu, Tianmei Zhou, Yueqian Shen, Manxing Zhou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have shown a significant association between the triglyceride glucose index (TyG) and suicidal ideation in Korean women. However, this association has not yet been examined among American adults. This study aims to investigate the association between TyG and suicidal ideation using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey database from 2005 to 2018 was used, including samples ≥20 years old. Suicidal ideation was assessed through questionnaires, and the TyG was derived from fasting triglyceride and glucose measurements. Weighted logistic regression models were used to analyze the relationship between the TyG and suicidal ideation. Generalized additive model curves explored the nonlinear relationship, while stratified and sensitivity analyses assessed potential interactions and the robustness of the findings. A significance level of P < .05 was considered statistically significant. A total of 15,694 participants were enrolled, of whom 3.67% participants showed suicidal ideation. The mean TyG index for participants with or without suicidal ideation was 8.82 ± 0.76, 8.65 ± 0.68, respectively (P < .001). Multivariable logistic regression showed that a higher level of TyG were associated with a higher likelihood of suicidal ideation (OR = 1.20; 95% CI, 1.05–1.38) after full adjustment. Subgroup analysis and interaction tests showed no significant correlation between gender, age, race, marital status, education, body mass index, poverty income ratio, smoking status, drinking status, diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and cancer (P > .05 for all interactions). Our findings confirm a significant and positive association between the TyG and suicidal ideation among American adults. Further large-scale prospective studies are needed to analyze the role of TyG in suicidal ideation.

1. Introduction

Suicide, resulting from prolonged and complex psychological processes, is a potentially lethal behavior where an individual intentionally employs various methods to end their life.[1,2] Suicide is a tragic occurrence, with one person taking their own life every 40 seconds.[3] More than 700,000 people die by suicide each year.[4] This is a complex global public health issue that not only affects the individual, but also has a profound impact on society, communities, and the loved ones of those who have lost their lives to suicide. Suicidal ideation, the contemplation of ending one’s life using fatal methods, serves as a strong predictor of actual suicide attempts.[5,6] Given its gravity, enhancing clinical prevention strategies to mitigate suicidal ideation is crucial.

Insulin resistance (IR) is the inability of exogenous or endogenous insulin to perform its role in glucose absorption and utilization, which can lead to metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes and abnormalities of lipid metabolism.[7] These metabolic abnormalities not only compromise quality of life but also increase the risk of depression and suicidal ideation.[8] Early detection of IR is crucial for identifying and preventing suicidal ideation. The hyperinsulinemic–euglycemic clamp is regarded as the gold standard for measuring IR; however, it is complex and limited in environmental applicability.[9] As an alternative, the triglyceride glucose index (TyG) offers a more accessible method through faster and more economical biochemical analysis, facilitating the identification of surrogate markers for IR.[10,11] Abbasi and Reaven have validated the adequacy of this index for assessing IR from fasting insulin levels.[12]

Previous studies have primarily focused on the association between TyG and suicidal ideation within Korean populations, highlighting a significant research gap in other demographic groups.[13] In response, this study aims to explore the potential association between TyG and suicidal ideation in the United States. To this end, we employed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to assess this association among American adults. Uncovering such association could inform strategies to reduce suicidal ideation, potentially yielding significant social benefits.

2. Methods

2.1. Dataset

Data from the National Center for Health Statistics’s NHANES, a national population-based cross-sectional study of nutrition and health status in the United States, was utilized. NHANES, since 1999, conducts a representative sample of the U.S. population every 2 years. It uses a complex, stratified, multi-stage design and sample weights to estimate disease prevalence. All NHANES study protocols were approved by the National Center for Health Statistics Research Ethics Review Board, and all survey participants or their parents and/or legal guardians for those under the age of 16 provided signed informed consent. The detailed NHANES study designs and data are publicly available at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.[14,15] The study is based on a STROBE guideline (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational studies in Epidemiology).

The present study was based on the 7 survey cycles from NHANES 2005 to 2018, since only these cycles included data on suicidal ideation assessments. Participants having complete data on suicidal ideation assessments and TyG were included in the current analysis. A total of 70,190 participants were enrolled at first, and after the exclusion of missing data about suicidal ideation (n = 33,781), participants aged <20 years (n = 2133), missing data about TyG (n = 18,582), 15,694 eligible participants aged ≥20 years were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of participant selection from the NHANES 2005 to 2018.

2.2. Assessment of TyG

TyG can be utilized as an independent variable in exposure assessment. Fasting glucose and triglyceride levels were measured using an automated biochemical analyzer after an 8.5-hour fasting period. TyG = ln[(fasting triglyceride (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL))/2].[16]

2.3. Assessment of suicidal ideation

To assess suicidal ideation, item 9 of the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was used. A total of 9 PHQ-9 items were utilized to test for depression over the last 2 weeks.[17] Each item is given a score of “0,” “1,” “2,” or “3,” for a total score ranging from 0 to 27. In clinical practice, a PHQ-9 score of 10 or higher is considered indicative of depression, with higher scores corresponding to greater severity of depression.[18] In the ninth item, respondents are asked, “In the past two weeks, how often have you had thoughts of hurting yourself or believing that your life would be better off if you ended it?[19,20]’’ “Not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “almost every day” are the available response options. For analytical purposes, all responses are categorized as either absent (no) or present at any frequency (yes).

2.4. Covariates

Multivariate adjustment models were used to consider potential variables that could introduce confounding effects in the association between TyG and the occurrence of suicidal ideation. Several variations in covariates were considered within this study’s analysis, encompassing gender (male or female), age (years), race, education level (less than high school, high school or GED, above high school), body mass index (BMI), poverty income ratio (PIR) (<1, ≥1 or unknown), marital status (single or living with a partner), smoking status (yes or no), alcohol status (yes, no or unknown), diabetes, stroke, cancer, coronary heart disease, hypertension, glycohemoglobin, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol. Everyone’s BMI was categorized as under 25 kg/m2, between 25 and 30 kg/m2, or above 30 kg/m2, corresponding to normal weight, overweight, and obese, respectively.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analysis were carried out in line with the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention standards, using appropriate NHANES sample weights and accounting for complex multistage cluster surveys. Continuous variables are described using means and standard deviations, whereas categorical characteristics are reported as proportions. A Student t test with weights was used to evaluate differences between groups based on the presence or absence of suicidal ideation. To evaluate associations between categories, a weighted chi-square test was used. Specifically, we chose ‘wtmec2yr’ as the analytical weight, aiming to account for the complex sampling design and provide results that are representative of the broader population. Multivariate logistic regression was used to examine an independent association between TyG quartiles and suicidal ideation in 3 distinct models. In model 1, no covariates were adjusted. Meanwhile, model 2 incorporated gender, age, and race adjustments. Gender, age, race, marital status, level of education, BMI, PIR, smoking status, alcohol status, diabetes, hypertension, stroke, and cancer were among the additional variables incorporated into model 3. The study examined the nonlinear association between TyG and suicidal ideation by implementing penalized spline smooth curve fitting and weighted generalized additive model regression analysis. Subgroup analysis were conducted using stratified multivariate regression analysis, with stratification based on gender, age, marital status, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, alcohol status, BMI, cancer, and coronary heart disease. The loglikelihood ratio test model was employed for subgroup analysis, and statistical significance was determined at P < .05. The analytical procedures were implemented using R 4.1.3 (available at http://www.R-project.org) and Empower (available at www.empowerstats.com) as software applications.

3. Results

3.1. Baseline characteristics

Fifteen thousand six hundred ninety-four individuals made up the study’s sample; 49.27% of them consisted of men and 50.73% were women. The research individuals’ average ages were 49.94 ± 17.77 years. Among them, 15,118 (96.33%) reported an absence of suicidal ideation, while 576 (3.67%) indicated exhibiting ideas of suicide. Significant differences were observed among the groups classified by the absence or presence of suicidal ideation with respect to the following factors: race, education level, marital status, income-to-poverty ratio, smoking status, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, stroke, coronary heart disease, cancer, glycohemoglobin, and HDL-cholesterol (P < .05). The TyG in the group with suicidal ideation was 8.82 ± 0.76, which was higher than that in the group without suicidal ideation, which was 8.65 ± 0.68 (P < .05). Those more likely to have suicidal thoughts are non-Hispanic White, single, better educated, having a higher income status, smokers, obese, having high blood pressure, having not been diagnosed with diabetes, stroke, coronary heart disease, or cancer. They also exhibited elevated levels of glycohemoglobin and TyG. However, they were less likely to develop diabetes, stroke, coronary heart disease, and cancer in the study and had lower HDL-cholesterol levels (P < .05) (Table 1).

3.2. Association between TyG index and suicidal ideation

The study found a positive association between the TyG and suicidal ideation. This association was significant both in our crude model (OR = 1.40; 95% CI, 1.25, 1.57) and minimally adjusted model (OR = 1.40; 95% CI, 1.24, 1.57). In the fully adjusted model, the positive association between TyG and suicidal ideation remained stable (OR = 1.20; 95% CI, 1.05, 1.38), demonstrating that each one-unit increase in TyG scores was associated with a 20% increase in the risk of suicidal ideation, respectively. For the sensitivity analysis, we converted TyG from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (quartiles). Compared with those in the lowest quartile, those in the highest quartile had a 32% increased risk of suicidal ideation, a difference that was statistically significant (OR = 1.32; 95% CI, 1.00, 1.74). Individuals in the second quartile also had a higher risk of suicidal thoughts than those in the lowest quartile, although this association did not achieve statistical significance (OR = 1.11; 95% CI, 0.86, 1.44) (Table 2).

3.4. Subgroup analysis

To assess whether there was concordance between TyG and suicidal ideation in the overall population and to identify potentially different population settings, we conducted subgroup analysis and interaction tests stratified by gender, age, race, marital status, level of education, BMI, PIR, smoking status, alcohol status, diabetes, hypertension, stroke, cancer. However, no correlation with the P for interaction meeting the statistical significance was detected, indicating that there was no dependence on gender, age, marriage, smoking status, alcohol status, diabetes, hypertension, stroke, cancer, and coronary heart disease for this association (all P for interaction > .05). Our results showed this positive association between TyG and suicidal ideation was similar across gender, age, marital status, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, smoking status, alcohol status, cancer, and coronary heart disease and may be appropriate for different population settings (Fig. 3).

4. Discussion

In our cross-sectional study of 15,694 adults, we discovered that a higher TyG is independently associated with an increased likelihood of suicidal ideation, with consistent findings across various subgroups including gender, age, marital status, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, smoking status, alcohol status, cancer, and coronary heart disease. We hypothesize that early treatment and management of IR could potentially reduce suicidal ideation.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to explore the association between the TyG and suicidal ideation in the American population. Previous studies had primarily focused on the association between TyG and depression or suicidal ideation in various demographics. For instance, Shi et al[21] reported that higher TyG levels are significantly associated with depressive symptoms among American adults. Zhang et al[22] also highlighted a nonlinear association between TyG and depression in American hypertensive adults. Similarly, Jin et al[23] revealed that TyG could be closely associated with morbidity in major depressive disorder patients. In addition, a cohort research from China also indicated a similar association between TyG and suicide attempts, which is consistent across different genders.[7]

A systematic review and meta-analysis encompassing 195 studies and 129,123 medical students across 47 countries revealed that 11.1% of these students reported suicidal ideation. The study underscored that the onset of depression and suicidal behaviors not only increases the short-term risk of suicide but also heightens the long-term risk of recurrent depressive episodes and overall morbidity.[24] Moreover, a comprehensive survey in the United States found that suicidal ideation was prevalent in over half of the individuals suffering from depression, with depression itself being a predictor of suicidal ideation.[25] Psycho-anatomical research further confirms that in 77% of suicide samples of older adults suffering from a mental illness at the time of death, the most common being depression,[26] which is the most common mental disorder among suicidal individuals.[27] Suicidal ideation and actions are often symptomatic of depressive episodes.[28] Individuals with depression have an increased risk of suicidal ideation and suicide attempts. Dysregulation of the immune-inflammatory system may contribute to this risk.[29]

Inflammatory processes have been implicated in the pathophysiology of depression.[30] There is increasing evidence supporting a link between suicidal ideation due to depression and inflammatory processes. Mae et al discovered for the first time in 1993 that patients with serious depression had considerably greater levels of IL-6 activity than healthy controls or patients with moderate depression.[31] Recently, it was found that IL-6 acts as a mediator to explain the positive association between long sleep duration and suicidal ideation in adults with depression.[32] This association was further supported by a NHANES study spanning 2005 to 2018, which identified a correlation between high levels of the dietary inflammatory index, depression, and suicidal ideation.[33] Inflammatory processes are further classified as peripheral or central, with the latter includes immune-related responses in the central nervous system, also known as neuroinflammation.[34] Furthermore, the central nervous system inflammations is associated with suicidal ideation and behavior.[35] Animal studies have shown that in lipopolysaccharide or unpredictable chronic mild stress-induced depressed stress mice models, inflammatory markers (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) are highly expressed and released in peripheral blood and depression-associated brain areas.[36] Additionally, another animal study also found that during depression, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels in the hippocampus of a mouse model of unpredictable chronic mild stress-induced depression were significantly upregulated.[37]

The interactions between IR and inflammation are increasingly recognized. IL-1β, for example, has been shown to disrupt insulin signaling, illustrating a direct mechanism through which inflammatory responses can induce IR.[38] Similarly, cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β can activate the c-JUN N-terminal kinase and nuclear factor-kappa B pathways, which are known to contribute to IR in peripheral tissues.[39,40] Additionally, TNF-α can further impair insulin signaling by phosphorylating insulin receptor substrate-1 and reducing the expression of glucose transporter type 4, thereby decreasing glucose uptake by cells.[41] There is also compelling evidence that the NLRP3 inflammasome, activated by both exogenous pathogens and endogenous cellular damage, plays a significant role in obesity-related IR.[39,42–44] NLRP3 detects exogenous pathogen invasion and endogenous cellular damage, leading to the formation of NLRP3 inflammasomes. These supramolecular complexes activate caspase-1,[45] triggering the maturation of the cytokine IL-1β.[38] This cytokine inhibits insulin signaling, causing IR. On the other hand, IR decreases lipoprotein lipase activity in adipocytes, leading to increased production of free fatty acids and inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6, TNF-α, and leptin, resulting in increased inflammation.[46]

The Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance and the TyG are both valuable indicators for assessing the status of IR, which is linked to various metabolic diseases.[47] Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance, a widely used surrogate marker for IR in clinical settings,[48] contrasts with the TyG, which is derived from fasting blood glucose and triglyceride levels and offers a simpler, more cost-effective method for measurement.[49] Several studies affirm the reliability of the TyG as an alternative marker of IR.[8,50,51] Considering the established links between inflammation, IR, and suicidal ideation, it can be hypothesized that there is a likely positive association between suicidal ideation and the TyG.

Our subgroup analysis results showed that the positive association held steady in subgroups stratified by age, gender, marital status, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol status, BMI, cancer, and coronary heart disease (all P for interaction > .05). This is in line with other research that suggests the positive association might be suitable in various population settings.

There are several advantages to our study. Firstly, our study is based on data from a large sample of NHANES and was analyzed taking into account appropriate NHANES sample weights, increasing the reliability of the study. Secondly, to make sure that our findings are trustworthy and applicable to a larger group of people, we fully corrected for confounding variables. However, there are still certain restrictions. Firstly, the cross-sectional research design prevents the establishment of a causal association between TyG and suicidal ideation. Secondly, we did not use multiple imputation to deal with the missing data for the covariates since they were missing at random and the sample sizes were large enough to draw conclusions. This decision may have affected the accuracy of our results. Lastly, since this study focused exclusively on Americans, it is not apparent if the results apply to other countries or demographics. Despite these limitations, this study provides persuasive evidence for a novel index related to suicidal thoughts.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, even after accounting for various confounding factors, our study shows a significant association between high TyG indices and the development of suicidal ideation in the general population. These results show that the TyG can be an effective predictor of suicidal ideation generation. However, additional research is required to determine causation.
