Data-driven machine learning modelling in wire EDM of TiNiCo shape memory alloy
Authors: Rashi Tyagi, Hargovind Soni, Ashutosh Tripathi, S M Nabil Rahman, Charles Mbohwa, C Durga Prasad, Sunil Kumar Tiwari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractShape memory alloys (SMAs) are a specific category of smart materials established for their superior mechanical, physical, and biomedical characteristics. SMAs demonstrate two significant behaviours- pseudoplasticity and shape memory effect which produce them highly beneficial for advanced operational uses. To use these materials in real components, they must be accurately machined into precise shapes. In the present study, WEDM was used to machine a Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀ shape memory alloy, and key machining performance measures namely surface roughness (SR) and material removal rate (MRR) were evaluated. In addition, the machined surfaces were thoroughly characterized in terms of morphology and topography. While ANN is employed to optimize process parameters such as voltage and for the best possible surface roughness, and MRR. ANN findings showed 0.96 for validation, 0.93 for testing, and 0.97 overall, efficient parameter tuning of Wire EDM variables for the SMA, achieving strong correlation coefficients (R-values) of 0.99 for training. The most suitable verification efficiency is obtained with a MSE (Mean Squared Error) of 0.729 at phase 5, after training completion within 11phase. Introduction

Shape Memory Alloys (SMAs) - especially Ni-Ti built upon alloys- denote a class of “smart materials” that integrate distinct mechanical behaviours and physical for example pseudoplasticity (PE) and the Shape Memory Effect (SMEs)1,2. These characteristics produce them appealing for a broad range of uses, biomedical implants, robotics, spanning aerospace and more3.

Nevertheless, traditional machining approaches often challenge with these alloyed materials. Because of their work-hardening tendency, high strength, and the sensitivity of their operational characteristics to mechanical damage and thermal, conventional cutting can break down the favourable shape-memory performance3,4.Therefore, advanced processes, especially Wire Electrical Discharge Machining (WEDM), have appeared as a encouraging route for forming SMAs without encouraging their functionality and internal structure3,5.

As part of WEDM, process variables like pulse-off time, discharge current pulse-on time, (or peak current), wire feed play and servo voltage, vital roles in influencing material removal rate (MRR), the surface quality, and broadly “surface integrity” of the machined element6,7.Surface integrity includes features such as presence (or absence) of micro-cracks, recast layer thickness, surface roughness, and possible impurity or accumulation from the wire electrode3,8. Current studies on WEDM of NiTi-based SMAs (including ternary alloys and binary NiTi like TiNiCu or TiNiCo) have illustrated the responsiveness of surface characteristics to parameter configurations9,10.For example, in one study on TiNiCo (Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀) SMA, investigators showed that wire speed, pulse-on time, pulse-off time, servo voltage and servo feed substantially effect productivity and surface roughness11,12.Other research on nitinol (NiTi) have likewise reported that differences in discharge energy effect occurrence of micro-cracks or globules, surface morphology, and recast layer properties13. Even though investigative work delivers useful insights, the high dimensionality and complicated relationships of WEDM parameters pose difficulty for in a structured manner determining optimal configurations- particularly when multiple output feedback (MRR, microhardness, surface roughness, recast layer thickness, etc. should be simultaneously improved14,15.In this context, integrating data-driven techniques, such as those from machine learning (ML), into experimental investigation becomes an appealing proposition16. Indeed, for EDM processes on composite and advanced materials, ML-driven models have recently been applied to predict surface integrity metrics and optimize process conditions more efficiently than purely empirical or response-surface-based methods17,18.

In wire electrical discharge machining (WEDM) of NiTi-based and TiNiCo shape memory alloys (SMAs), multiple process parameters-including pulse-on time, pulse-off time, discharge current, servo voltage, wire feed rate, and wire tension-have been reported to influence machining performance. Recent studies on WEDM and micro-EDM of NiTi-based SMAs have demonstrated that pulse-on time and servo voltage are among the most influential parameters governing discharge energy, spark stability, and debris flushing efficiency, thereby directly affecting material removal rate (MRR) and surface roughness (SR)19,20. Conventional optimization approaches such as Taguchi, response surface methodology (RSM), genetic algorithms (GA), and particle swarm optimization (PSO), have been widely applied to analyze effects of these parameters on performance. For instance, Taguchi- and RSM-based analyses have been widely applied to identify dominant WEDM parameters and develop empirical regression models; however, these approaches are inherently limited by linear assumptions and restricted interaction modeling capabilities21. However, these methods often rely on limited experimental combinations and lack predictive adaptability under changing machining conditions. While other parameters contribute to machining behavior, their effects are often secondary when maintained at optimized machine-recommended levels. Motivated by these findings, the present study focuses on pulse-on time and servo voltage as dominant control variables and integrates experimental WEDM with ANN-based predictive modeling to achieve reliable performance optimization. Nevertheless, many reported ANN applications rely on limited architectural transparency or are combined with optimization tools in a tool-oriented manner, without sufficient discussion of nonlinear parameter interactions and machining physics20,22. Additionally, comparative investigations on NiTi and TiNiCo SMAs highlight that most existing studies emphasize optimization outcomes rather than predictive adaptability and surface–process interdependence23. In this context, the present work advances existing research by integrating controlled WEDM experimentation with a systematically designed ANN framework to capture nonlinear relationships between dominant machining parameters and performance responses. This data-driven approach overcomes key limitations of conventional Taguchi, RSM, GA, and PSO-based methods and provides improved predictive capability for machining optimization of Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀ SMA. By systematically varying key WEDM parameters and measuring surface integrity outcomes, and then applying ML algorithms for prediction and optimization, this study aims to offer a robust methodology for determining parameter combinations that yield superior surface integrity-thus enabling practical and reliable machining of TiNiCo SMAs for advanced applications20,21.In doing so, this research hopes to contribute to the growing body of knowledge on smart-material machining, and to demonstrate how ANN-assisted frameworks can enhance precision manufacturing processes for materials otherwise considered difficult to machine.

Materials and methodology

Materials

Commercially available titanium (99.3% purity), nickel (99.90%), and cobalt (99.5%) were used to prepare the Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀ shape memory alloy. The weighed elements were melted in a copper crucible using a vacuum arc melting furnace. A tungsten electrode was employed for sparking and melting under an inert argon atmosphere. Prior to melting, the chamber was evacuated to a vacuum level of 10⁻⁵ mbar, after which argon gas was introduced. To ensure chemical homogeneity, the alloy was melted and re-melted six times22. The molten alloy was initially cast into button-shaped ingots and subsequently remelted and cast into rectangular blocks with dimensions of 50 × 12 × 10 mm.

Methodology

Machining of the Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀ shape memory alloy was carried out using a WEDM system (Model ELPULS15 CNC, Electronica India Pvt. Ltd., Pune, India). Figure 1 shows the experimental set up used in the present process. The selected input parameters included pulse-on time (Ton), and servo voltage (SV). Their working ranges and levels were determined through preliminary trials using a response surface design. The final parameter settings and levels used in the study are presented in Table 1. A 0.25 mm brass wire was employed as the tool electrode, as it provides better surface integrity compared to zinc-coated brass wire. Distilled water was used as the dielectric fluid during the Wire EDM process23. The dielectric plays a critical role beyond electrical insulation by governing spark initiation, discharge stability, and energy transfer. Dielectric breakdown strength controls the voltage threshold for discharge initiation and directly influences spark frequency and plasma channel stability. Stable and uniform breakdown prevents premature arcing and enables controlled plasma channel formation, which governs localized melting and vaporization of the workpiece material. The dynamics of plasma channel expansion and collapse significantly affect thermal energy distribution, thereby influencing both material removal rate (MRR) and surface roughness (SR). Effective dielectric flushing is essential for removing molten material and eroded debris from the inter-electrode gap. Poor debris evacuation can lead to unstable discharges, secondary sparking, and localized arcing, resulting in irregular crater formation and degraded surface quality. Additionally, dielectric behavior influences oxidation reactions and recast layer formation, particularly in electrically conductive shape memory alloys such as TiNiCo, where high-temperature plasma interactions promote surface chemical modification. Recent studies emphasize that improvements in MRR cannot be attributed solely to material conductivity; instead, they arise from the coupled interaction of dielectric breakdown strength, plasma channel dynamics, debris dispersion stability, and flushing efficiency, which collectively govern discharge stability and surface integrity in WEDM28,29.

Pulse-on time (Ton) and servo voltage (SV) were selected as the primary control parameters in the present investigation due to their dominant influence on discharge energy input and spark gap regulation, respectively. Pulse-on time directly determines the duration of electrical discharge and thermal energy delivered to the workpiece, whereas servo voltage governs inter-electrode gap stability, discharge frequency, and debris flushing efficiency. Other machining parameters such as pulse-off time, discharge current, wire feed rate, and wire tension were maintained at optimized machine-recommended values based on prior literature and preliminary trials to minimize experimental complexity and avoid confounding interactions. Similar parameter-selection strategies have been successfully employed in recent WEDM studies on NiTi-based SMAs22.

Performance prediction

In the present, an ANN model was designed using MATLAB to forecast and analyse the relationship between input processing parameters (Pulse on time and servo voltage) and the output resulting properties (surface roughness and MRR) of the fabricated samples. The methodology involves data preparation, network architecture design, training, validation, and performance evaluation. MATLAB’s Neural Network Toolbox (now integrated as Deep Learning Toolbox) was utilized for model construction and optimization24. The dataset used for ANN training comprised experimentally obtained inputs such as peak current and Pulse on time while the outputs included material removal rate and surface roughness, before training, the dataset was normalized within the range of 0 to 1 using MATLAB’s built-in normalization function to ensure uniform scaling and prevent bias toward higher-magnitude variables25. The data collection was then separated into three categories: 15% for validation, 15% for testing and 70% for training, after a randomized sampling method to maintain distributional balance. The feed-forward backpropagation network (FFBP) architecture was implemented, as it allows higher efficiency in modelling nonlinear and complex relationships among variables. From Fig. 2, the network architecture comprised of Input layer which depicts Number of neurons equal to the input parameters (peak current and pulse on time). Two hidden layers were used, with the number of neurons determined through iterative optimization26. Output layer shows Number of neurons equal to the output variables (MRR and Surface roughness). The tensing (hyperbolic tangent sigmoid) transfer function was used for hidden layers, and the purlin (linear) function for the output layer. The ANN structure is shown schematically in Fig. 3. This network was trained using the Levenberg–Marquardt (trail) algorithm, which combines the advantages of gradient. descent and Gauss–Newton methods, offering faster convergence and higher accuracy for small to medium datasets. During training, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) was used as the performance metric, and the process continued until the MSE reached a minimum or the validation performance ceased improving, thereby preventing overfitting27. To enhance methodological clarity and reproducibility, the artificial neural network (ANN) architecture (layers and neurons), Levenberg–Marquardt training algorithm, data normalization within the 0–1 range, and performance evaluation metrics including mean squared error (MSE) and regression coefficients (R-values) have been described in detail.

Although the experimental dataset is limited to 25 samples due to material availability and extensive surface characterization requirements, robust generalization was ensured through randomized data partitioning, early stopping based on validation performance, and independent test-set evaluation. Similar dataset sizes have been successfully employed in ANN-based EDM modeling of advanced alloys, where experimental cost and machining complexity restrict large-scale trials. Despite the limited dataset size, the consistency of high R-values across training, validation, and testing subsets, together with low and symmetrically distributed prediction errors, confirms that the ANN model generalizes well within the investigated parameter domain.

Results and discussion

Surface integrity is a broad concept encompassing surface roughness, recast layer thickness, microcracks, microhardness, residual stress, and elemental redistribution. In the present study, the scope of surface integrity evaluation is intentionally limited to surface roughness, surface morphology, elemental composition, and topography, as these parameters are primary indicators of functional surface quality in WEDM-processed SMAs.

Effects of servo voltage and pulse on time on machining responses

Figures 4 and 5 present the effects of servo voltage and pulse-on time on the machining responses. The highest material removal rate (MRR) and surface roughness were observed at a pulse-on time of 125 µs and a servo voltage of 20 V. At higher pulse-on time, the discharge energy and spark intensity increase significantly, resulting in extensive melting and evaporation of the work piece material, which leads to enhanced MRR28. However, confocal 3D microscopy of the machined surface under these conditions reveals the presence of deep craters, irregular molten pools, micro-cracks, and a thick recast layer, confirming the severe thermal loading during machining. The insufficient time for complete flushing and solidification causes resolidified debris to redeposit on the surface, thereby increasing surface roughness29.

Effects of Pulse on time on machining responses.

In contrast, at lower pulse-on time and higher servo voltage, both MRR and surface roughness decrease. An increase in servo voltage enlarges the inter-electrode spark gap, which reduces discharge energy and spark density, resulting in lower material removal30. Confocal microscopy images corresponding to these conditions exhibit a more uniform surface topography with shallow craters, reduced crack density, and minimal recast layer formation, indicating improved surface integrity. The increased spark gap facilitates better debris evacuation and controlled melting, allowing effective removal of molten material before resolidification. Thus, the confocal microscopy findings strongly corroborate the observed trends in machining responses, clearly demonstrating that higher pulse-on time and lower servo voltage promote aggressive material removal at the expense of surface quality, while lower pulse-on time combined with higher servo voltage yields superior surface finish.

The interaction between pulse-on time and servo voltage plays a crucial role in determining machining performance. An increase in pulse-on time raises discharge energy and thermal erosion, while a reduction in servo voltage decreases the inter-electrode gap, intensifying discharge concentration. Their combined effect influences plasma channel stability, dielectric breakdown behavior, and debris evacuation efficiency. These thermo-electrical interactions govern both material removal mechanisms and surface quality, explaining the observed trends in MRR enhancement and SR variation. Recent studies emphasize that MRR improvement cannot be attributed solely to electrical conductivity but also depends on plasma channel dynamics, dielectric behavior, and particle dispersion stability33.

Morphology of machined surface

The machined surface shows a highly irregular and rough texture due to materials processed by Electro-Discharge Machining31. Figure 6 shows that the surface is covered with numerous overlapping molten pools, micro-craters, and re-solidified globules, formed due to the rapid melting and cooling caused by repetitive electrical sparks during machining. A few sphere-shaped fragments splash-like configurations particles and are apparent, showing re-solidification of liquefied metal that was not fully flushed away by the dielectric fluid32,34 . The existence of these re-solidified droplets verifies that localized thermal erosion controlled by the material removal process. Shallow voids and fine micro-cracks are observed throughout the surface layer. These surface layer irregularities occur due to rapid cooling and thermal shock, leading to the formation of internal stresses within the TiNiCo alloy. These characteristics are commonly experienced when above-normal discharge energy levels are applied. The quantitative evaluation EDS results show the weight% (wt%) dispersion of elements current on the WEDM-processed TiNiCo alloy exterior. Among all identified elements, Titanium (Ti) demonstrates the highest content, around 35–38 wt%, verifying that the base material stays Ti-rich also after machining. Oxygen (O) is the second most prevailing element at approximately 26–28 wt%, which highly shows the development of surface oxides at the time of WEDM. This is predicted due to the high-temperature trigger environment and dielectric breakdown enhance oxidation of the melted surface level. The existence of oxygen verifies the development of a recast oxide level, which straight forwardly impacts wear behaviour and corrosion. Nickel (Ni) seems at a substantial layer of about 24–26 wt%, verifying the maintenance of the shape memory alloy structure after machining. Ni performs a vital role in establishing the phase transition mechanical performance and behaviour of TiNiCo alloys. Carbon (C) indicates a medium value of around 6–8 wt%, which is primarily related to dielectric fluid breakdown and carbon coating on the machined surface layer at the time of electrical discharges. Copper (Cu) is identified at around 4–5 wt%, which arises from the brass wire electrode utilized at the time of WEDM. This verifies material movement from the wire to the workpiece surface layer, modified surface properties and leading to surface contamination. Zinc (Zn) is present in a small amount (~ 1–2 wt%), also originating from the brass wire electrode (Cu–Zn).Although low, its presence further confirms electrode material diffusion into the recast layer. Cobalt (Co) appears in trace quantity (~ 1 wt% or less), indicating either minor compositional segregation, slight evaporation during high-temperature discharge, or limited detectability due to overlap and low concentration. The surface level is Ti–Ni–rich with a strong oxide level. Copper, Carbon, and zinc arise from the wire electrode transfer and dielectric. The results verify the development of a thermally impacted recast layer with chemical adjustment because of WEDM.

(a) SEM image, (b) area for EDS analysis, (c) EDS analysis, and (d) Quantitative analysis of principal elements.

The EDS analysis reveals oxygen enrichment and trace impurity elements such as copper and carbon on the machined surface, primarily associated with oxidation and wire electrode material transfer during electrical discharge. These compositional changes are confined mainly to the thin recast layer, while the bulk composition of the Ti₅₀Ni₄₀Co₁₀ alloy remains largely unaffected.

Since shape memory effect and pseudoelasticity are bulk-dominated properties governed by phase transformation behavior, the optimized WEDM parameters are not expected to significantly impair the functional performance of the alloy. Similar observations have been reported for WEDM-processed NiTi-based SMAs, where surface compositional modifications did not adversely affect bulk functional characteristics. Nevertheless, detailed functional testing is identified as an important direction for future research.

Figure 8 shows the regression efficiency of the designed model throughout the validation, training, test, and coupled datasets. The training data show a very strong linear connection between target values (R = 0.99839) and predicted outputs, with the regression line strongly corresponding with the suitable Y = T line, showing outstanding model fitting. Validation efficiency also stays high (R = 0.96894); however, the slope slightly varies from uniformity, indicating minor systematic variations between aims and forecasts. The test dataset indicates a slightly lower but remains robust correlation (R = 0.93202), reflecting predicted variability because of unseen data. When all samples are combined, the overall regression attains an R value of 0.9712, showing that the model extends well across all data segments. In combination, these plots verify strong forecasting capability and consistent model response across separate subsets of the data collection.

Conclusions

The current study combines experimental study of WEDM on TiNiCo shape memory alloy with machine learning-built on optimization and modelling. Crucial WEDM variables were methodically variable, and the ensuing surface integrity properties were examined. These results data were then utilized to create ML models for exact optimization and prediction. The outcomes derived are as follows:

High pulse-on time (125 µs) and low servo voltage (20 V) produce high-energy sparks, increasing MRR but causing deep craters, recast layer, and higher roughness, as confirmed by confocal microscopy.
