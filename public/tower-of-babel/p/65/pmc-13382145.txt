HSPE1 promotes immune evasion in lung adenocarcinoma by mediating mitochondrial oxidative stress via histone lactylation-mediated SDHA transcriptional activation
Authors: Tao Xie, Manxiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Histone lactylation promotes SDHA expression.•SDHA overexpression mediates mitochondrial oxidative stress to promote lung adenocarcinoma cell immune escape.•HSPE1 enhances SDHA lactylation through glycolysis and mediates oxidative stress to promote lung adenocarcinoma immune escape.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, SDHA, Lactylation, HSPE1, Glycolysis

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a highly prevalent and heterogeneous malignancy with significant mortality. Histologically, NSCLC is classified into adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most common type, accounting for about 40% of all lung cancer (LC) cases [1]. In recent years, targeted therapies, such as epidermal growth factor receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitors (erlotinib and gefitinib), have been clinically approved. However, acquired resistance often develops during late-stage treatment, leading to therapeutic failure and increased mortality [2]. Thus, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying LC progression is critical for identifying potential novel therapeutic targets, developing innovative treatment strategies, and ultimately improving patient survival rates.

To sustain rapid proliferation, tumor cells undergo metabolic reprogramming, with glucose metabolism being one of the most extensively studied alterations. A hallmark of cancer metabolism is the preferential use of glycolysis for energy production, even under normoxic conditions—a phenomenon known as the “Warburg effect” [3]. This metabolic shift not only supports tumor growth and metastasis but also enables adaptation to hypoxic microenvironments while generating substantial lactate. Traditionally, lactate was considered merely a byproduct of glycolysis, with limited relevance to cancer progression [4]. However, in 2019, Di Zhang’s team made a groundbreaking discovery, demonstrating that lactate-derived histone lactylation modifications could directly stimulate gene expression in chromatin [5]. This finding established a critical link between tumor metabolic reprogramming and epigenetics, with lactate as a bridge. A recent study has revealed that melanoma produces a large amount of lactate as the substrate of lactylation through glycolysis, facilitates YT521-B homology (YTH) domain 2 transcription through histone lactylation, and recognizes N(6)-methyladenosine-modified period circadian regulator 1 and Tumor Protein P53 mRNA as well as promotes their degradation, thereby accelerating the tumor occurrence of ocular melanoma [6]. Similarly, histone lactylation has been implicated in LUAD development [7]. However, the downstream genes regulated by lactylation in LUAD remain poorly characterized. A deeper exploration of these mechanisms could shed more insights into LUAD pathogenesis and uncover potential therapeutic targets.

Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH), located on the inner membrane of mitochondria, connects the mitochondrial electron transfer chain and the tricarboxylic acid cycle by oxidizing succinate to fumarate and reducing ubiquinone to ubiquinol [8]. SDH’s catalytic core consists of SDHA and SDHB [9], with SDHA playing particularly crucial roles in both normal and cancer-related metabolism. High expression of SDHA can restore the level of oxidative phosphorylation in metastatic uveal melanoma and is linked to the abundant breathing reserve capacity of mitochondria [10]. Abnormal lactate metabolism is observed in LC, which is accompanied by abnormal expression of SDHA [11]. Exogenous lactate treatment can induce a further increase in SDHA transcription levels in LC cells [11]. However, the mechanism of histone lactylation regulating SDHA in LUAD still requires further investigation, which may offer new potential targets for cancer treatment.

Tumor metabolic reprogramming not only drives the malignant progression of tumor cells themselves but also profoundly shapes the composition and biological function of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME). As a critical bridge linking innate and adaptive immunity, dendritic cells (DCs) play pivotal roles in tumor antigen presentation and T cell activation [12]. Nevertheless, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within the TIME impairs DC functionality through multiple regulatory mechanisms. Oxidatively truncated lipids accumulate in tumor-infiltrating DCs and further block the major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I)-restricted cross-presentation pathway [13,14]. Sustained oxidative stress upregulates heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) expression, which suppresses DC maturation and proinflammatory activities, elevates interleukin-10 (IL-10) secretion, and ultimately drives DC polarization toward an immune-tolerant phenotype [15]. Additionally, persistently elevated ROS levels in the tumor microenvironment trigger comprehensive functional impairment of DCs, thereby facilitating tumor immune escape [16,17]. Accordingly, elucidating how metabolic reprogramming remodels the TIME via the ROS-DC axis is of great significance for uncovering the underlying immune evasion mechanisms of LUAD.

This study aims to elucidate that lactylation, as a novel post-translational modification mechanism in LUAD, drives cancer progression through the HSPE1-lactylation-SDHA axis. The core mechanism lies in HSPE1-mediated enhancement of glycolysis, which promotes lactylation modification levels, subsequently activating SDHA and exacerbating mitochondrial oxidative stress. This discovery not only provides a novel mechanistic perspective for understanding the epigenetic regulation and metabolic reprogramming in LUAD but, more importantly, highlights the potential of targeting the heat shock protein family E member 1 (HSPE1)-lactylation-SDHA axis as a therapeutic strategy to intervene in LUAD TIME. The development of inhibitors targeting this pathway may open new clinical avenues for improving patient prognosis.

Materials and methods

All methods were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

Bioinformatics analysis

We downloaded mRNA expression data of LUAD tissue (tumor: 539) and normal lung tissue (normal: 59) from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). We obtained differentially expressed gene SDHA through differential analysis with the edgeR package (|logFC|>1, p.adj<0.05). In addition, the expression levels of glycolysis-related genes such as glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), 3-phosphoinositide-dependent kinase 1 (PDK1), pyruvate kinase M (PKM), and solute carrier family 2 member 1 (SLC2A1) in LUAD tissues and normal lung tissues were obtained from the TCGA database. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on SDHA and HSPE1 genes to further investigate their biological pathways, which can affect LUAD. The bioinformatics online Kla prediction website Auto-PTM (http://origin.tubic.org/Kla/public/index.php/onlineservice) predicted the lactylation sites of SDHA [18]. Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two groups.

Immune cell infiltration was evaluated in 515 tumor samples from the TCGA-LUAD cohort using the CIBERSORT and xCell algorithms. Spearman correlation coefficients between the expression levels of HSPE1 and SDHA and the infiltration scores of various immune cell subsets were calculated, with the Benjamini–Hochberg method applied for multiple testing correction (FDR < 0.05, |r| > 0.2).

Cell lines and culture conditions

The human LUAD cell line A549, purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA), was cultured in F12K medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Human embryonic kidney cell line 293T, purchased from BNCC (China), was cultured in DMEM-H medium containing 10% FBS. The incubator was kept moist at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Exogenous lactate was cultured as described previously [11]. A549 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/cell in a 6-well plate. After 24 h of culture, the medium was replaced with a fresh one containing 5 mM or 10 mM lactate (HY-B2227, MCE, USA). The cells were collected for subsequent testing after 24 h of cultivation. The cell lines used were precisely authenticated through STR profiling to ensure the reliability of the experimental materials and the validity of the research results.

Human peripheral blood DCs were purchased from Wuhan EnkiLife Technology Co., Ltd.

Cell transfection

siRNA targeting SDHA (si-SDHA) and non-targeting siRNA (si-NC) were purchased from Tsingke (China). The sequences of SDHA and HSPE1 were amplified and cloned into the pcDNA3.1 vector to construct overexpression vectors (oe-SDHA and oe-HSPE1) (Tsingke, China). pcDNA3.1 empty vector was transfected as oe-NC. A549 cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 106 cells/well in a 6-well plate. The next day, the fresh basal medium was replaced and stabilized for 30 min. The complex containing siRNA or overexpression vectors and UltraFection3.0 (4abio, China) was added to the culture plate.

For the knockdown of SDHA, the negative control or target gene shRNA was co-transfected with pEnv (transfection index of 10) into HEK293T cells for a duration of 8 h. Viruses were harvested from the transfected HEK293T cells and then used to infect the target cells for two consecutive days. Stable cell lines were selected by treating with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 10 days.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissue and corresponding normal samples from 20 LUAD patients were obtained from the Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University. The slice processing method was conducted as described previously [6]. The tissue sections were incubated with antibodies, including anti-Pan-Kla (Catalog number: PTM-1401RM, 1: 500, PTM-biolab, China), anti-H3K18la (Catalog number: PTM-1406RM, 1: 1000, PTM-biolab, China), and anti-HSPE1 (Catalog number: ET7108–67, 1: 200, Huabio, China) at 4 °C overnight, followed by 30 min of incubation with secondary antibodies and nuclear staining with hematoxylin. We observed and photographed tissue sections with an optical microscope (Mshot, China). After completion of immunohistochemical staining, the sections were randomly numbered and scored by two independent blinded observers. The semi-quantitative method combining staining intensity score (0–3 points) and positive cell percentage score (0–4 points) was used, and the final score was the product of the two. The research was conducted according to the approved protocol of the Institutional Review Board. All patients signed informed consent forms before the study. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Affiliated Hospital of Southwest Medical University, approval number: KY2024127. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Cell viability assessment

Cell viability was detected by CCK-8 assay. In brief, transfected and drug-treated cells (1 × 103 cells/well) were seeded in a 96-well plate. Then, 10% CCK-8 reagent (EZB-CK8, EZB, China) was added to each well at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. The plate was incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 2 h. The optical density (OD) at 450 nm was measured using a Multiskan FC microplate reader (Thermo, USA).

Cell migration and invasion ability detection

The Transwell assay was used to detect cell migration and invasion ability. Specifically, in the invasion experiment, a 1:8 dilution of Matrigel (50 μL) was applied to the upper chamber and solidified at 37 °C for 2 h (no glue was applied in the migration experiment). 200 μL of serum-free cell suspension (2.5 × 10⁵/mL) was added to the upper chamber, and 600 μL of culture medium containing 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. After incubation for 24 h, the upper chamber was removed, washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. An inverted microscope was used for taking pictures.

Flow cytometry

A549 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were seeded in a six-well plate at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well. After treatment, the cell precipitate was collected and washed once with pre-chilled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Then, the cell precipitate was resuspended in 500 μL of 1 × binding buffer. At room temperature and in the dark areas, 5 µL annexin V-FITC and 10 μL propidium iodide (AT101, Liankebio, China) were added to 500 μL cell suspension for 15 min. The apoptosis was detected by utilizing a flow cytometer (NovoCyte Advanteon, USA).

Cells were incubated using the same method and washed twice with PBS after different treatments. We added 10 µM DCFH-DA (S0033S, Beyotime, China) to the cells and incubated them in a 37 °C cell culture incubator for 20 min. Cells were rinsed three times with a serum-free cell culture medium to remove DCFH-DA that did not enter the cells. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) level was detected using a flow cytometer (NovoCyte Advanteon, USA). To detect mitochondrial ROS, MitoSOX Green dye (Invitrogen, M36005, USA) was added to the cell suspension.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL reagent (CW0580, Cwbio, China). The reverse transcription kit of Hifair ®Ⅱ 1st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (11119ES60, Yeasn, China) was utilized for reverse transcription. UltraSYBR Mixture (CW0957, Cwbio, China) was employed for amplification and detection on ABI 7500 (Thermo, USA). β-actin was regarded as an endogenous control. We calculated relative expression levels of mRNA with the 2−ΔΔCT method. Table 1 lists the primer sequences.

Western blot (WB)

Total protein was extracted from treated A549 cells using Western cell lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). Proteins were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Millipore, USA). The membrane was blocked for 1 h at room temperature with 5% skim milk, and then it was incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h. Protein band signals were detected using ECL developing solution (Beyotime, China). The primary antibodies used included HSPE1 (Catalog number: A7437, 1: 1000, abclonal, China), SDHA (Catalog number: A13852, 1:1000, abclonal, China), H3K18la (Catalog number: A21214, 1: 1000, abclonal, China), Pan Kla (Catalog number: A23004, 1: 500, abclonal, China), Histone H3 (Catalog number: M1309–1, 1: 10,000, Huabio, China), SOD2 (Catalog number: A1340, 1: 500, abclonal, China), E-cadherin (Catalog number: A20798, 1: 1000, abclonal, China), N-cadherin (Catalog number: A3045, 1: 500, abclonal, China), Vimentin (Catalog number: A2584, 1: 500, abclonal, China) and β-actin (Catalog number: M1210–2, 1: 20,000, Huabio, China).

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR

Cells were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde at room temperature for 10 min, and the crosslinking was terminated with glycine. After washing with PBS, the cell lysate was collected and chromatin was broken by ultrasonic fragmentation. The sample was incubated with H3K18la antibody (PTM-1406RM, PTM biolab, China) overnight at 4 °C. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated at 4 °C for 2 h to capture immune complexes. Elution Buffer (1% SDS, 0.1 M NaHCO3) was used to crosslink at 65 °C for 4 h. After digestion with proteinase K, purified DNA was used for qPCR analysis. The primer sequences used are shown in Table 1.

Measurement of malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and glutathione (GSH)

A549 cells were collected after treatment by each group and counted. For MDA detection, 3 × 106 cells were taken from per group, followed by the addition of 0.5 mL extraction solution. After mixing for 2 min, the cells were broken and suspended using a handheld homogenizer to obtain the test substance. Blank tubes, standard tubes, and test tubes were prepared according to the instructions (E-BC-K028-M, Elabscience, China). The EP tube was sealed with plastic wrap and thoroughly mixed. A small hole was made in the plastic wrap. The tube was then heated in a water bath at 100 °C for 40 min, cooled to room temperature with running water, and centrifuged at 1078 × g for 10 min. Subsequently, 0.25 mL of the supernatant was transferred to an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) plate. The OD values of each well were measured at 532 nm. The content of MDA is normalized by cell protein content.

For total SOD assessment, we took 1 × 106 cells and added 500 μL of PBS for homogenization. The 10 min of 10,000 × g centrifugation at 4 °C was performed after homogenization. The supernatant was collected and kept on ice for measurement. Then, the supernatant was collected for protein concentration determination. Control wells, blank control wells, and test wells were prepared according to the instructions (E-BC-K020-M, Elabscience, China). We mixed wells and incubated them at 37 °C for 20 min, with the OD value detected at 450 nm with a microplate reader. SOD activity was normalized by the cellular protein content.

For GSH measurement, the test substance was obtained using the above-mentioned method. Standard wells, test wells, and blank wells were prepared according to the instructions (E-BC-K030-M, Elabscience, China). The microplate reader was vibrated for 1 min, then left undisturbed for 5 min, with the OD value measured at 405 nm with a microplate reader. GSH content was normalized by cell protein content.

Lactate measurement

For each treatment group, 1 × 106 cells were taken and homogenized with 500 µL PBS. After homogenization, the sample was subjected to 10,000 × g centrifugation at 4 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, the test substance and the colorimetric reagent (E-BC-K044-S, Elabscience, China) were accurately reacted at 37 °C for 10 min. The amount of lactate production was detected at 530 nm with the OD value on the microplate reader. Lactate production was normalized by cellular protein levels.

Cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assay

Specifically, cells were seeded into a Seahorse XF microplate at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured overnight. Subsequently, a buffer containing 1 mM pyruvate, 10 mM glucose, and 2 mM glutamine was added, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1.5 h. Finally, the OCR was measured using the XF Cell Mito Stress Test Kit (Seahorse Bioscience), with oligomycin at a concentration of 1.0 μM, FCCP at 0.5 μM, and rotenone plus antimycin A each at 1.0 μM.

Detection of ATP and HMGB1

The conditioned medium was collected, and working solutions and standards were prepared following the instructions of the Elabscience kit. Test samples and standards were added to the corresponding microplate wells. After incubation under the conditions specified in the manual, the OD values were measured. A standard curve was plotted with standard concentrations on the x-axis and corresponding OD values on the y-axis, which was used to calculate the concentrations of target molecules in the samples.

Animal experiments

A549 cells were transfected with oe-HSPE1 and sh-SDHA plasmids (pLKO.1-Puro, Newborn Biological, China). Stable overexpression or knockdown of key genes (HSPE1/SDHA) in A549 cells was then selected: oe-NC+sh-NC, oe-HSPE1+sh-NC, oe-NC+sh-SDHA, and oe-HSPE1+sh-SDHA.

Twenty female Balb/c nude mice, aged 6 weeks (20±2 g), were purchased from Hangzhou Qizhen Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (China) and randomly divided into four groups. Before the experiment, the nude mice were randomly divided into 4 groups (5 mice in each group). When establishing the subcutaneous transplant tumor model, mice were first anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection with isoflurane (1.5%−2.5%) to ensure that they were pain-free during the surgical process. After successful anesthesia, a total of 5 × 106 A549 cells were subcutaneously injected into the back of each nude mouse in each group. Postoperatively, the recovery of the mice was closely monitored, and necessary care was provided. Tumor volume changes were monitored, calculated using the formula: Tumor Volume (cm³) = (Length × Width²) / 2.

At the end of the experiment, to quickly and humanely end the lives of the mice, we performed euthanasia via intraperitoneal injection of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (150 mg/kg). After ensuring the death of the mice, we excised the tumor tissues and weighed them, followed by collecting the tumor tissues for WB analysis to detect the expression levels of key protein indicators. The animal experiments were guided and approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Health Science Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University (No. XJTUAE2024–2638). This study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data were presented as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three experiments. Student’s t-test compared the differences between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) compared multi-group differences. Tukey’s test was applied for post-hoc analysis. P < 0.05 refers to statistically significant.

Results

Increased level of histone lactylation in LUAD

TCGA data (Fig. 1A) demonstrated that compared with normal lung tissue (n = 59), the expression levels of glycolysis-related genes (GAPDH, LDHA, PDK1, PKM, and SLC2A1) were upregulated in LUAD tissue (n = 539). qRT-PCR analysis of three pairs of LUAD tissues and their corresponding adjacent normal tissues revealed that the expression of glycolysis-related genes, including GAPDH, LDHA, PDK1, PKM, and SLC2A1, was upregulated (Fig. 1B). These findings are consistent with the analysis results from the TCGA database, indicating elevated glycolytic activity in LUAD patient tissues. Immunohistochemical staining was performed on tumor tissues collected from 20 LUAD patients, revealing positive correlations between HSPE1 expression and the levels of Pan Kla and H3K18la in LUAD tissues (Fig. 1C-F). Therefore, these results indicated an increased lactylation level of histone in LUAD.

Histone lactylation facilitates SDHA expression and LUAD progression

To clarify the role of histone lactylation in the progression of LUAD, we treated LUAD cells with different concentrations of lactate in vitro and analyzed the overall lactylation level and the level of histone lactylation marker H3K18la in tumor cells by WB. The results indicated that the lactylation level in A549 cells was elevated with the increasing lactate treatment concentration (Fig. 2A). Additionally, exogenous lactate induced upregulation of SDHA transcription levels (Fig. 2B). ChIP-qPCR revealed that H3K18la was enriched in the SDHA promoter region by 2.57-fold (Fig. 2C). To explore the potential lactylation sites of SDHA, we used the online Kla prediction website Auto-PTM and downloaded the protein sequence of SDHA from Uniprot (https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb). We then predicted all the sequences 25 residues before and after each lysine (K) in the protein sequence. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the prediction results for each site, among which K46, K179, K182, K485, K541, K547, K550, and K598 of SDHA were the most probable lactylation sites. Although bioinformatics analysis predicted potential lactylation sites on the SDHA protein itself (K46, K179, K182, K485, K541, K547, K550, and K598), this study specifically investigated histone lactylation-mediated transcriptional regulation of SDHA, which represents a distinct biological process from direct protein lactylation. It is noteworthy that both the qRT-PCR experiments and the analysis of the TCGA database revealed a significant upregulation of SDHA expression in tumor tissues compared to normal tissues (Fig. 2D-E). Subsequently, SDHA was knocked down in vitro by 0.5 fold (Fig. 2F). The CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that the cell viability of A549 cells with SDHA knockdown was inhibited by 1.43 fold (Fig. 2G). Moreover, knocking down SDHA reduced the migration and invasion ability of A549 cells by 1.98 and 2.02 fold (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Since epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) is one of the genetic determinants of cellular plasticity [19], the detection of the expression of EMT-related proteins (E-cadherin, N-cadherin, Vimentin) can also reflect the degree of SDHA’s impact on cells. The WB detection results showed that knocking down SDHA reduced the protein expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin, while increasing the expression of E-cadherin (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Flow cytometry analysis revealed that the apoptosis rate of A549 cells deficient in SDHA was increased (Fig. 2H). Therefore, H3K18la is found to positively regulate the transcription of the tumor-promoting effect in LUAD models. Silencing SDHA inhibits LUAD progression.

Overexpression of SDHA mediates mitochondrial oxidative stress to enhance the progression of LUAD cells

SDHA is located in mitochondria. SDHA mediates mitochondrial metabolism and function, which is linked to oxidative phosphorylation [10]. According to the median expression level of SDHA in the TCGA database, LUAD patients were divided into high- and low-SDHA expression groups. Differential gene analysis was performed using edgeR (| logFC |>1, p.aj<0.05) to obtain a set of differentially expressed genes. Subsequently, GSEA enrichment analysis was performed on the gene set, and the results showed that SDHA-related differentially expressed genes were enriched in the KEGG OXIDATIVE PHOSPHORYLATION pathway (Fig. 3A). Therefore, we hypothesized that SDHA might influence cellular oxidative phosphorylation. Overexpression of SDHA in A549 cells increased both SDHA expression and OCR levels (Fig. 3B-C). Mechanistically, the abnormal expression of SDHA affects the function of SDH, inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress by interfering with the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the electron transport chain [20,21]. Cells were treated with the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant SS-31. The results demonstrated that overexpression of SDHA upregulated oxidative stress levels in cells, including increased levels of ROS (total cellular ROS and mitochondrial ROS) and MDA, and decreased levels of SOD and GSH. However, SS-31 treatment reversed the oxidative stress response caused by SDHA overexpression (Fig. 3D-H). Subsequently, we measured cell viability and apoptosis using CCK-8 and flow cytometry, finding that compared with the control group, high expression of SDHA promoted the viability of A549 cells and inhibited apoptosis, while the malignant progression potential of LUAD cells induced by high expression of SDHA was suppressed by SS-31 treatment (Fig. 3I, J). Collectively, high expression of SDHA may promote the progression of LUAD by inducing oxidative stress in mitochondria.

Bioinformatics prediction revealed that HSPE1 and SDHA were correlated with the infiltration of diverse immune cell populations. Among these correlations, SDHA exhibited a negative association with DC immune infiltration (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Collectively, current findings suggest that SDHA may impair DC function by mediating oxidative stress, thereby promoting immune escape in LUAD. Immunogenic cell death (ICD) is accompanied by the exposure and release of abundant damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). These DAMPs exert potent adjuvant effects on dying cancer cells by facilitating the recruitment and activation of antigen-presenting cells. ICD-related DAMPs include CALR, secreted ATP, ANXA1 and HMGB1 [22]. After co-culturing treated A549 cells with DCs, we found that SDHA overexpression reduced the levels of HMGB1 and ATP in the co-culture system, indicating impaired efflux of HMGB1 and ATP from A549 cells. Notably, the reduction in HMGB1 and ATP levels triggered by SDHA overexpression was rescued by SS-31 treatment (Fig. 3K). Cell viability assays revealed decreased DC viability in the SDHA-overexpressing group, and this SDHA-mediated reduction in DC viability was reversed upon SS-31 administration (Fig. 3L). Collectively, these results suggest a correlation between SDHA expression and DC immune infiltration.

HSPE1 enhances p300/CBP-mediated histone lactylation of the SDHA promoter through glycolysis

Based on the TCGA database, LUAD patients were grouped according to the median expression of HSPE1. GSEA analysis showed that the glycolysis pathway was enriched by the high-HSPE1 expression group (Fig. 4A). A positive correlation between HSPE1 and SDHA was detected (Fig. 4B). We speculated that HSPE1 enhanced SDHA expression through glycolysis-mediated lactylation. We thus overexpressed HSPE1 in A549 cells (Fig. 4C) and treated them with 2-DG (MCE, USA), a non-metabolizable glucose analog that can reduce lactate production and histone lactylation levels [6]. The results revealed that compared with the control group, high expression of HSPE1 promoted lactate production, which was downregulated by 2-DG treatment (Fig. 4D). According to WB, HSPE1 overexpression upregulated SDHA protein levels by 1.84-fold (Fig. 4E). Meanwhile, the overall level of histone lactylation and H3K18la level were upregulated under the condition of HSPE1 overexpression (Fig. 4F). However, under 2-DG treatment, the upregulation of SDHA protein levels and histone lactylation levels induced by HSPE1 was greatly repressed (Fig. 4E, F). To verify the inhibitory effect of HSPE1 knockdown on cellular function, we knocked down HSPE1 in A549 cells (Supplementary Fig. 4A), followed by glucose stimulation. Compared with the control group, HSPE1 knockdown suppressed lactate production, whereas glucose treatment elevated lactate concentrations (Supplementary Fig. 4B). Western blot analysis demonstrated that HSPE1 downregulation reduced SDHA protein level (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, global histone lactylation and H3K18la levels were both repressed upon HSPE1 depletion (Supplementary Fig. 4D). Notably, glucose treatment substantially restored the SDHA protein expression and histone lactylation signals that were suppressed by HSPE1 knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 4C–D). The ChIP-qPCR assay revealed that HSPE1 overexpression promoted the enrichment of H3K18la by 1.8-fold in the SDHA promoter region, which was reversed by 2-DG treatment (Fig. 4G). The results confirmed that HSPE1 influenced the process of histone lactylation of SDHA promoter modification by regulating glycolysis.

To further analyze the key enzymes mediating histone lactylation modification, we introduced the p300/CBP inhibitor (A-485, MCE, USA). Previous studies have identified p300/CBP as key genes responsible for mediating histone lactylation modification [5,23]. Therefore, we hypothesized that HSPE1 enhances SDHA transcription through glycolysis-driven histone lactylation. Our data confirmed that HSPE1-regulated glycolysis elevated global histone lactylation levels, particularly H3K18la enrichment on the SDHA promoter, thereby facilitating SDHA transcriptional activation (Fig. 4H). Concurrently, ChIP-qPCR results demonstrated a significant reduction in the enrichment of the SDHA gene by lactylated histone antibodies following inhibition of the p300/CBP axis (Fig. 4I). These experimental findings confirmed that the p300/CBP axis functioned as a lactyltransferase in the HSPE1-mediated lactylation modification of SDHA.

HSPE1 facilitates LUAD progression through SDHA-mediated oxidative stress

To validate whether HSPE1 affected LUAD progression through SDHA-mediated oxidative stress, we knocked down SDHA in HSPE1-overexpressing A549 cells. qRT-PCR validated the knockdown and overexpression efficiency (Fig. 5A). Oxidative stress detection revealed that compared with the control group, HSPE1 overexpression considerably promoted the accumulation of ROS (total cellular ROS and mitochondrial ROS) and MDA in cells by 4.2 and 1.5-fold, while inhibiting SOD and GSH levels. However, the simultaneous knockdown of SDHA downregulated the cellular oxidative stress level (Fig. 5B-G). In addition, CCK-8 and flow cytometry showed that overexpression of HSPE1 promoted cell viability and inhibited apoptosis, which were reversed after SDHA knockdown (Fig. 5H, I). Co-culture of the above-treated A549 cells with DCs revealed that HSPE1 overexpression suppressed HMGB1 and ATP levels in the culture medium. SDHA knockdown boosted the efflux of HMGB1 and ATP, and this enhanced secretion could be rescued by additional HSPE1 overexpression (Fig. 5J). Cell viability assays showed reduced DC viability in the HSPE1-overexpressing group, while HSPE1 overexpression reversed the impaired DC viability induced by SDHA knockdown (Fig. 5K). Taken together, HSPE1 promotes LUAD progression by directly regulating SDHA levels and mediating oxidative stress.

The HSPE1-SDHA regulatory axis affects the in vivo growth of LUAD

To validate the role of the HSPE1-SDHA axis in vivo, this study constructed stable A549 cell lines with overexpression or knockdown of key genes (HSPE1/SDHA) using oe-HSPE1 and sh-SDHA plasmids. Subsequently, the mice were divided into groups (oe-NC+sh-NC, oe-HSPE1+sh-NC, oe-NC+sh-SDHA, oe-HSPE1+sh-SDHA) to establish subcutaneous tumor xenograft models. The experimental results indicated that overexpression of the HSPE1 gene promoted tumor development, while knockdown of the SDHA gene inhibited tumor growth. Notably, the knockdown of SDHA was able to suppress the rapid tumor growth induced by HSPE1 overexpression (Fig. 6A-C). Furthermore, high expression of HSPE1 enhanced both the expression of SDHA protein by 1.31-fold and the level of histone lactylation by 1.92-fold. The protein expression of 4-HNE was increased by 1.34-fold. Importantly, SDHA knockdown did not reverse the expression of HSPE1 or the level of histone lactylation. The expression level of SDHA protein was positively correlated with oxidative stress levels (Fig. 6D), where SOD2 is recognized as a classic antioxidant protein [24]. 4-HNE serves as one of the biomarkers of oxidative stress [25]. HSPE1 overexpression reduced serum HMGB1 and ATP levels in mice, while SDHA knockdown elevated the serum concentrations of HMGB1 and ATP. This upregulation of serum HMGB1 and ATP induced by SDHA knockdown could be rescued by HSPE1 overexpression (Fig. 6E). These results suggested that HSPE1 could promote SDHA expression through histone lactylation modifications, thereby activating oxidative stress to mediate the in vivo growth of LUAD.

Discussion

LC is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, with over 7500 deaths due to cancer every day, and >35% of cancer deaths are due to LC [26]. In this study, we aimed to investigate the functions of HSPE1 and SDHA in LUAD. We observed that HSPE1 promoted excessive lactate accumulation in LUAD by regulating glycolysis and induced enrichment of H3K18la in the SDHA promoter region, thus promoting SDHA expression. Additionally, upregulated SDHA enhanced LUAD progression by mediating oxidative stress.

Cellular metabolic programs have a profound impact on cell fate. Metabolism and gene expression mutually modulate to maintain internal balance and regulate cell growth, survival, and differentiation [27]. Metabolism plays an essential role in tumor progression to affect cell fate by directly or indirectly regulating chromatin through metabolic enzymes and metabolites, modulating gene expression through rewriting genomic DNA and histone modifications [27,28]. In this investigation, we focused on a recently described epigenetic modification, lactylation of histone lysine residues (Kla), which can link cellular metabolism to epigenetic regulation [5,11]. Histone H4 lysine 12 lactylation upregulates PKM2, forming a positive feedback loop with the glycolysis pathway, exacerbating microglia dysfunction in Alzheimer’s disease [29]. To date, only a few studies have revealed the regulatory mechanisms of histone lactylation in tumors. For example, the lack of the Numb/Parkin pathway leads to metabolic reprogramming induction, increases lactate production to induce subsequent histone lactylation, and results in transcriptional upregulation of neuroendocrine-related genes in prostate cancer or LUAD [28]. Pan et al. [30] put forward that suppressing H3 histone lactylation can repress the tumorigenicity of liver cancer stem cells. A recent study also analyzed metabolic dysregulation in NSCLC, where exogenous lactate modulates metabolism through histone lactylation in the HK-1 and IDH3G promoters [11]. However, the regulatory mechanism of lactate in the progression of LUAD still requires further exploration.

Herein, we demonstrated that LUAD patient tissues exhibited stronger glycolysis activity compared to normal tissues. Abnormal histone lactylation levels were observed in the collected LUAD patient tissues. Both extracellular and endogenous lactate can increase the overall protein lactylation level, with endogenous lactate largely derived from glycolysis [5]. Therefore, we first treated LUAD cells with exogenous lactate, finding that overall lactate metabolism and H3K18la were induced in LUAD cells. Meanwhile, H3K18la facilitated SDHA transcription by enriching the SDHA promoter region. Being involved in the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, SDHA has been reported to undergo various epigenetic modifications and exhibit different roles in tumor progression, including acetylation, succinylation, and ubiquitination [[31], [32], [33]]. For example, by activating SIRT3 deacetylase degradation, Myc facilitates the acetylation-dependent inactivation of SDHA, which contributes to tumor growth in vivo and in vitro [32]. However, the role of SDHA in tumorigenesis remains controversial, as previous studies have shown that SDHA functions as a tumor-promoting factor whose overexpression supports metabolic plasticity in metastatic uveal melanoma and ovarian cancer [10,34]. This study, through in vitro experiments, unearthed that SDHA promoted LUAD cell proliferation and inhibited apoptosis. In summary, the substantially elevated levels of glycolysis in LUAD may activate the tumor-promoting effect in LUAD models transcription through H3K18la.

SDHA is a major source of oxidative phosphorylation breathing reserve to support cell survival [35]. Elevated endogenous SDHA facilitates drug resistance of highly metastatic uveal melanoma to oxidative phosphorylation inhibitors [10]. Unlike traditional post-translational modifications (such as phosphorylation or acetylation), lactylation is directly driven by the metabolite lactic acid, which is a rare mechanism in cancer biology in LUAD. This study reveals a previously unrecognized link between histone lactylation and the transcriptional regulation of SDHA in LUAD, providing a novel mechanistic perspective on the metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk driving LUAD progression. Meanwhile, overexpression of SDHA enhances the metabolism of ovarian cancer, which is reflected by increased mitochondrial respiration and ATP production rate [36]. The electron transfer chain of mitochondria utilizes a series of electron transfer reactions to generate cellular ATP through oxidative phosphorylation, inducing the production of ROS, which contributes to the steady signal transduction and oxidative stress during pathology [37]. Although the role of ROS in cancer progression is contradictory, it can be observed that excessive proliferation of tumor cells is accompanied by high ROS production. In addition, ROS is involved in carcinogenic signal induction. For example, ROS-induced phosphorylation of ERK1/2 and MLK3 is necessary for the invasion of oxidative stress-induced colorectal cancer cells [38]. SDH increases the oxidation of succinate in mitochondria, promoting the production of ROS in mitochondria [39]. Therefore, we examined the role of SDHA in intracellular oxidative stress, unearthing that SDHA overexpression can promote LUAD cell progression by mediating oxidative stress in mitochondria. Mechanistically, the abnormal expression of SDHA affects the function of SDH, which further induces mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress by disrupting the tricarboxylic acid cycle and the electron transport chain [20,21].

Subsequently, we attempted to delve into the factors inducing endogenous lactate production, finding a potential target gene, HSPE1, that may be involved in glycolysis. HSPE1 is a heat shock protein that has been proven to be involved in tumor progression [40]. The overexpression of HSPE1 is associated with the levels of HSP60 and Mcl-1, contributing to the prediction of poor prognosis for NSCLC patients [41]. GSEA demonstrated the enrichment of HSPE1 in the glycolysis pathway. Furthermore, TCGA data revealed a strong positive correlation between HSPE1 and SDHA expression levels in LUAD tissues. We hypothesized that HSPE1 may enhance lactylation-mediated SDHA expression through glycolysis. Being consistent with our expectations, experimental evidence in vitro confirmed that high expression of HSPE1 enhanced intracellular lactate accumulation and facilitated LUAD progression through mediating histone lactylation of the SDHA promoter. p300/CBP functions as a lactate transferase. At the same time, we used a mouse model to confirm the impact of the HSPE1-SDHA axis on the in vivo development of LUAD. It is foreseeable that monoclonal antibodies or small-molecule drugs targeting the HSPE1-SDHA axis could inhibit glycolytic metabolism [42] and mitigate mitochondrial oxidative stress [36]. By mediating metabolic reprogramming, drugs targeting the HSPE1-SDHA axis play a crucial regulatory role in the progression of LUAD.

HSP60 is a molecular stress protein primarily located in the mitochondrial matrix [43]. HSPA9 is also essential for mitochondrial biogenesis and the proper functioning of the entire cellular mechanism [44]. Although HSPE1, HSP60, and HSPA9 are all mitochondrial chaperone proteins and are associated with mitochondrial stress responses and oxidative metabolism, they differ in their specific mechanisms in regulating mitochondrial function and tumor progression. The mechanism by which HSPE1 promotes SDHA expression by regulating glycolysis and histone lactylation modifications provides a new direction for studying the metabolic regulation and mitochondrial stress response in LUAD. Future research can further explore the interactions between these mitochondrial chaperone proteins and their synergistic effects in the tumor metabolism and mitochondrial stress network, which will help develop more effective LC treatment strategies.

Immune infiltration analysis based on the TCGA-LUAD cohort revealed a significant negative correlation between high SDHA expression and DC infiltration levels. In vitro co-culture experiments further validated that SDHA overexpression reduces DC viability and suppresses the efflux of HMGB1 and ATP by exacerbating oxidative stress. Collectively, these findings suggest that the HSPE1–SDHA axis may participate in tumor immune evasion via oxidative stress-mediated impairment of DC function. It should be emphasized that the immunological conclusions of the present study are mainly derived from bioinformatic prediction and in vitro co-culture systems. We have not yet verified the specific effects of the HSPE1/SDHA axis on DC antigen presentation, maturation markers (CD80/CD83/CD86), and T cell activation in immunocompetent animal models. Accordingly, the regulatory role of this axis in the TIME remains a preliminary speculation, and its definitive immunological significance requires further investigation. Nevertheless, persistently elevated ROS levels within the tumor microenvironment exert opposite effects. Oxidized lipids accumulate abundantly in tumor-infiltrating DCs and severely compromise the cross-presentation capacity of DCs by blocking the transportation of pMHC-I complexes to the cell surface [13,14]. This mechanism has been validated as one of the predominant causes of functional defects in tumor-associated DCs [45]. Additionally, oxidative stress comprehensively weakens the immune activation capacity of DCs by disrupting key procedures involved in antigen processing and presentation, including proteasome activity and MHC molecule stability [46]. Future research could further clarify the specific mechanism by which this axis modulates the TIME by detecting the antigen-presenting function, maturation markers (CD80/CD83/CD86), and cross-presentation ability of DCs under HSPE1/SDHA overexpression conditions.

This investigation elucidated for the first time the histone lactylation mediated by LUAD metabolic reprogramming, which facilitated cancer progression through the modulation of SDHA transcription. Targeting dysregulated cancer metabolism has become an attractive new approach for cancer therapy. Previous research has mostly focused on the regulation of a single metabolic pathway. This project revealed a regulatory mechanism that simultaneously participates in the oxidative stress of glycolysis and mitochondria. This study has several limitations. First, the precise molecular mechanism by which SDHA regulates oxidative stress remains unclear, including the exact site of ROS generation and downstream signaling pathways. Further investigations using SDHA enzymatic activity mutants, mitochondrial ROS probes, and other tools are required to dissect this process in depth. Second, the clinical sample size is relatively small, and larger patient cohorts are needed to validate the prognostic values of HSPE1 and SDHA in future research. Third, our experiments were primarily performed in the A549 cell line and nude mouse xenograft models; validation using additional LUAD cell lines (e.g., H1975, PC9) is lacking. Moreover, nude mice are immunodeficient, which prevents us from evaluating the impact of this axis on adaptive immunity. Fourth, the direct regulatory mechanism linking HSPE1 to glycolysis has not been elucidated. Whether HSPE1 modulates glycolysis by stabilizing glycolytic enzymes or regulating the HIF‑1α/mTOR pathway requires further exploration. Finally, all immunological conclusions were drawn from in vitro DC co‑culture systems. We did not systematically verify the effects of the HSPE1/SDHA axis on the TIME in immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice bearing orthotopic lung tumors, nor did we conduct functional assays targeting immune checkpoint molecules such as PD‑1/PD‑L1. Therefore, sufficient experimental evidence is currently unavailable to translate the mechanisms identified here to the field of immunotherapy.

For the first time, this study integrates the mitochondrial chaperone HSPE1, histone lactylation modification, and oxidative phosphorylation into a functional regulatory axis, establishing a novel theoretical paradigm for the field of metabolism–epigenetics crosstalk. The core logic of this axis is as follows: HSPE1 boosts glycolysis to drive endogenous lactate accumulation. As a metabolic signaling molecule, lactate activates SDHA transcription via histone lactylation. As a core subunit of mitochondrial complex II, elevated SDHA expression further facilitates oxidative phosphorylation and ROS production, thereby forming a cross-organelle regulatory network spanning cytoplasmic glycolysis to mitochondrial oxidative stress. This mechanism not only deepens our understanding of metabolic heterogeneity in LUAD, but also lays an experimental foundation for subsequent research on the interactive regulation among metabolism, epigenetics, and tumor immunity.
