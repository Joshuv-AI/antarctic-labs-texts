Machine learning framework for early identification of high-spending Crohn’s disease patients using administrative claims
Authors: Lukasz S Wylezinski, Jamieson D Gray, Charles F Spurlock, III
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo develop and evaluate a machine learning (ML) model that predicts Crohn’s disease (CD) patients responsible for the top quartile of healthcare spending.MethodsDe-identified commercial claims (2016–2018) from ~267 000 continuously enrolled members in a Midwestern state were analysed, including 994 CD cases. Monthly data for each patient was aggregated into data points that included healthcare spending amounts, encounter interactions, demographics and binary flags for diagnoses, procedures and drug codes. Seven algorithm families were tuned using five-fold cross-validation (January 2016 to September 2017) and tested prospectively (November 2017 to February 2018). Monthly performance evaluations assessed the accuracy of predicting high-cost healthcare spending, using 4-month and 1-month historical cost analyses for comparison.ResultsML models predicted an average of 80% of the dollars spent by top-quartile members during the 4-month evaluation period, compared with 67% for the 4-month baseline and 62% for the prior-month benchmark. The models identified an average of 51 new members entering the high-cost group each month, nearly double the yield of the 4-month historical method. These ML models more accurately anticipated inpatient encounters that drove excess spending.DiscussionClaims-based ML offers actionable lead time for payers and clinicians to enhance monitoring, adjust biological therapy or schedule elective care before emergency admissions occur. Because this framework relies exclusively on standard claim fields, it can be quickly extended to other episodic, high-variance conditions.ConclusionProspectively tested, claims-only ML models enhance short-term risk stratification in CD by identifying future high-cost patients. Future studies should confirm the clinical impact, cost savings and ensure equitable performance across diverse populations.Keywords: Machine Learning, Artificial intelligence, Medical Informatics, Medical Informatics Computing, Computer Simulation

Introduction

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic immune-mediated condition characterised by relapsing inflammation that affects the entire gastrointestinal tract. If not properly controlled, it can lead to cumulative bowel damage, disability and a reduced quality of life.1,3 Modern management has led to better outcomes, but many patients still face unpredictable flare-ups, extra-intestinal complications and disease progression.4 5 Timely identification of individuals approaching a period of increased disease activity is crucial for preventing irreversible tissue damage and preserving long-term function.6

The economic impact of CD is significant, with annual direct medical costs in the USA estimated at US$12–15 billion. Per-patient medical expenses are three to four times higher than those of matched controls.7 8 Spending is largely attributed to hospitalisations, surgeries and rising costs of biological therapies, with month-to-month expenses fluctuating as patients move between remission and active disease.7 9 10 Biological initiation, opioid use and emergency surgery have each been linked to especially sharp cost inflections.6 7 11 Timely identification of patients about to enter a high-cost, high-morbidity phase could enable targeted outreach, proactive optimisation of therapy and avoidance of potentially preventable admissions.

Most payer and care management programmes assess risk primarily by a patient’s recent claims spending. This retrospective metric underperforms when utilisation patterns change or previously low-cost individuals suddenly become high risk.12 The growing availability of longitudinal insurance claims and pharmacy data presents an opportunity to apply modern machine learning (ML) methods that can model complex, non-linear interactions among diagnoses, procedures and medication patterns.13,16 Although ML models have repeatedly outperformed rule-based cost prediction approaches for identifying patients who will move into the top spending decile, the bulk of this work centres on broadly defined adult or mixed chronic disease cohorts.17 18 Only a few studies have examined immune-mediated conditions such as CD, and virtually none evaluate month-to-month forecasting windows or report performance on fully temporally held-out test sets.19,21

To address these gaps, we developed a disease-agnostic ML framework that uses commercial claims data to predict which CD patients are likely to enter the top quartile of healthcare spending over the next 4 months. We benchmarked ML performance against historical spending and analysed the claim-level features that significantly influenced model predictions. Focusing on a condition characterised by episodic, resource-heavy flare-ups, this study demonstrates how advanced analytics can enhance current care management systems and promote more proactive, personalised care for individuals with CD.

Methods

Study design and data source

We conducted a retrospective prognostic modelling study using de-identified commercial medical and pharmacy claims provided by Blue Health Intelligence. The extract included all 835/837 format claims submitted between 1 January 2016 and 30 June 2018, for members continuously enrolled with a single commercial insurer across all three-digit ZIP codes in one Midwestern state (~267 000 lives). Each record contained service dates, International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnoses, Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes, Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System (HCPCS) codes, National Drug Codes (NDC), healthcare costs and anonymised patient and provider identifiers. The data were inspected for completeness and normalised into structured base tables that complied with HIPAA Safe Harbor requirements (data flow illustrated in online supplemental figure 3).

CD cohort definition

CD cases were identified by the presence of ≥1 ICD-10 K50 code during the study window. No additional phenotype adjudication algorithm was applied. Enrollees without a K50 code formed the non-CD comparator. Baseline characteristics for both groups are summarised in figure 1. Claim lines were aggregated per enrollee each month to create a ‘member-month’ observation that (1) summed paid amounts and claim counts, (2) generated binary flags for individual diagnosis, procedure and drug codes, (3) calculated the days since the most recent inpatient, outpatient or pharmacy encounter and (4) appended age band, sex and three-digit ZIP.

Feature engineering

Each member’s monthly feature set included the total paid amount, distinct counts of facility, professional and pharmacy claims, binary indicators for ICD-10, CPT, HCPCS and NDC codes, and demographics. Instead of grouping codes, the feature set kept individual code-level presence markers, indicating whether each code appeared in the member-month. Granular codes were retained to track the impact of individual features driving predictions. No forward or backfilling was applied.

CD cost outcome

The primary prediction aim for every month t was the member’s paid amount aggregated over months t+1 through t+4. This 4-month forward total spend served as a continuous regression target label. The time horizon smooths short-term fluctuations while maintaining operational relevance for care management teams.

Data partitioning

The time-ordered dataset was divided into a model development partition (January 2016 to September 2017) and a prospective evaluation partition (November 2017 to February 2018), separated by a 4-month buffer (July to October 2017) to prevent data leakage (online supplemental figure 2). Within the evaluation period, month-specific models employed rolling historical windows (online supplemental figure 2B). Five-fold cross-validation and an internal holdout of 20% guided hyperparameter tuning and model selection within the development partition.

ML framework

A competitive grid search pipeline was employed, implemented via custom automated ML pipelines in DataRobot (DataRobot, Inc), training seven algorithm families: k-nearest neighbours, decision trees, random forests, gradient-boosted trees, elastic-net generalised linear models, support vector machines and multilayer perceptrons. This process yielded ~75 candidate models for each evaluation month. The optimal regressors were selected by identifying those with the lowest mean absolute error (MAE) and the highest coefficient of determination (R2) (online supplemental table 2 and supplemental figure 3). Final models were retrained on the complete development dataset before prospective testing. Model processing steps are summarised in online supplemental figure 3.

Benchmark comparators

Two rule-based baseline predictions for top quartile spenders were implemented: (1) a rolling 4-month spend, ranking members by cumulative paid amounts from t−4 to t−1; and (2) a prior-month spend, ranking by costs accumulated in month t−1. To ensure comparability, all strategies, including ML, flagged the top 25% of members predicted to have the highest healthcare spend each month.

Performance evaluation

Prospective performance was assessed monthly in the evaluation partition (results summarised in figures 3–5, online supplemental tables 2 and 3). For each method, we calculated four key metrics: (1) prediction of dollar spend, which measured the total spending generated by the true top-quartile spenders compared with the amount attributed to the members predicted as high-risk across ML and historical benchmarks; (2) capture of the actual top quartile of members and dollars based on the correct prediction of the true top-quartile spenders each month; (3) sensitivity for rising-risk member identification, defined as the percentage of patients who newly entered the top quartile of spend and were accurately identified; and (4) the MAE and coefficient of determination (R2) for continuous 4-month spending predictions.

Model interpretability

Permutation-based feature impact scores were calculated for each optimal gradient-boosted tree regressor and averaged over the four evaluation months. The 20 highest-impact codes are reported in online supplemental table 5.

Statistical analysis

The differences between the machine-learning approach and each rule-based comparator were evaluated using Welch’s t-tests with a significance threshold of p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. ML model development and evaluation were conducted using DataRobot.

Results

Disproportionate financial burden of CD

An analysis of all commercial claims filed in a Midwestern state between January 2016 and June 2018 revealed that only 994 members, representing 0.4% of the population of ~267 000, were diagnosed with CD. Despite this small percentage, these individuals accounted for $108 million (2.7%) of the total $4 billion expenditures. On a per-person basis, this represents a sevenfold increase in medical expenses compared with members without CD. The additional costs were not due to a proportional increase in claims, as the total claims from CD members were less than three times those of non-CD members. Instead, the higher costs resulted from significantly more expensive utilisation: the mean per-member-per-month (PMPM) expense was $3127 for CD versus $665 for non-CD, accompanied by a threefold increase in month-to-month variability (SD $3363 vs $1119) (figure 1). The CD cohort skewed older (69.8% aged 40–69 years) and was evenly divided by sex, confirming that the cost differential was driven by disease and utilisation rather than demographic imbalance (online supplemental figure 1).

Complex multimorbidity among CD members

Online supplemental table 1 details extensive comorbidity in the CD population. Common co-diagnoses include anxiety and other mood disorders, chronic pain and musculoskeletal conditions (eg, lower back pain, cervicalgia), as well as cardiometabolic conditions such as hypertension and hyperlipidaemia. Additional gastrointestinal disorders, including non-infective gastroenteritis and colitis, ulcerative colitis, abdominal pain, diarrhoea and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, are often coded alongside CD. The presence of ulcerative colitis diagnosis codes in some members likely indicates coding overlap or diagnostic uncertainty in administrative claims, reinforcing that the reported performance reflects a claims-defined CD cohort. The co-occurrence of these comorbidities with CD has been previously documented.22 23 This clinical complexity amplifies care coordination challenges and contributes to unpredictable resource use, highlighting the need for tools that can identify members at imminent risk of costly episodes.

Short-term spending dynamics and ‘rising-risk’ CD members

Since CD flares and treatment escalations occur unpredictably, we segmented each calendar month into quartiles of observed spending and tracked members’ movements between quartiles across consecutive months. Across the entire Crohn’s population, most members remained in the same quartile, but those who advanced one or more quartiles, defined as ‘rising-risk’ patients, exhibited the highest PMPM expenditures (figure 2A). During the evaluation period from November 2017 to February 2018, members classified as rising-risk patients represented only 24%–28% of the CD cohort, yet they accounted for 38%–41% of total expenditures across this timeframe. When we focused exclusively on the top-spending quartile, rising-risk members exhibited spending levels comparable to those of persistent high-cost members (figure 2B). Predicting which patients are at risk of an imminent acceleration in acute healthcare spending could provide an opportunity for intervention. Early alerts on deteriorating patient conditions, even offering a few weeks’ advance notice, could enable care management teams to pre-empt or lessen a significant share of expenditures.

ML model development and internal validation

Monthly ML models trained on member-month records were used to forecast patient healthcare spending. In each modelling cycle, the previous 18 months of claims served as the development corpus, while the subsequent 4 months constituted an out-of-sample ‘deployment’ dataset, separated by time to prevent information leakage (online supplemental figure 2). For each month, ~75 regression algorithms (including gradient-boosted trees, random forests, elastic nets, multilayer perceptrons and stacked ensembles) were tuned through five-fold cross-validation and evaluated on a 20% hold-out split. The best models were selected jointly based on the highest R2 (0.53–0.56) and the lowest MAE ($5937–$6181) (online supplemental table 2). These gradient-boosted tree regressor models were retrained on the complete development dataset and then frozen for prospective testing on the deployment dataset (online supplemental figure 3).

Prospective ML performance versus historical baselines

Three risk-stratification strategies were compared in each deployment month: (1) the ML model’s prediction of each member’s 4-month forward spend, (2) a rolling 4-month historical spend total and (3) the prior month’s spend. All methods predicted the expected top quartile of healthcare spending and identified members (198 individuals) as ‘high-cost’, enabling a head-to-head assessment of dollar capture.

During the 4-month deployment period, ML selections captured 77%–82% of the top-quartile dollars (mean 80%), whereas the 4-month and 1-month rule-based methods captured 66%–70% and 60%–64%, respectively (figure 3). Identifying new entrants to the high-cost group is valuable, as they have not yet incurred significant expenses and may respond well to proactive care. ML predicted a significantly higher number of rising-risk members and their associated spending. Of the ML-predicted patients, 23%–29% were rising-risk individuals each month, with their spending accounting for 27%–38% of the total spend, surpassing the 4-month benchmark (15%–18% of members; 21%–29% of dollars) (figure 4). Throughout the deployment period, ML predicted rising-risk members responsible for $2.2 million compared with $1.4 million under the 4-month approach. The average number of rising-risk individuals identified through ML (51) greatly exceeded those identified using a historical 4-month benchmark (33). The ML model effectively identifies more individuals at rising risk, creating greater opportunities for early intervention and cost reduction.

Capture of actual patients each month

Prediction accuracy was further evaluated by measuring how many actual patients in the top quartile were correctly identified (figure 5). ML models captured 63%–68% of these individuals and 68%–72% of the top monthly spending, while the 4-month approach identified only 57%–60% of members and 59%–64% of the total cost. Similar to the 4-month historical benchmark, the 1-month method identified 55%–59% of individuals and captured 55%–60% of top monthly spending. Furthermore, even when the ML model predicted patients outside the actual top quartile, these individuals still incurred substantially higher costs ($3095 average PMPM) compared with those identified by the historical 4-month analysis ($1862 average PMPM) or last month’s high-risk group ($1413 average PMPM) (online supplemental table 3). When specifically focusing on capturing the rising-risk population, ML significantly outperformed the 4-month historical benchmark. ML predictions captured 45%–59% of these individuals and 50%–55% of the rising-risk spending, while the 4-month approach identified only 30%–35% of members and 30%–45% of the total cost (figure 5).

Analysis of predicted CD expenditures

Spending during the 4-month analysis period was categorised into inpatient, outpatient and pharmacy segments to analyse utilisation among actual rising-risk members (N=345), ML-flagged members (N=204) and 4-month predicted members (N=130) (online supplemental table 4A). Outpatient and pharmacy PMPMs were comparable across groups, but inpatient encounters per member were two times as high in the ML cohort compared with the 4-month cohort, aligning more closely with the actual rising-risk rate. These data indicate that unplanned admissions, rather than biological infusion costs, account for most of the spending differences detected by ML. Identifying a potential inpatient episode several weeks in advance may provide gastroenterologists and care coordination teams with an opportunity to reduce the likelihood of emergency hospitalisation by facilitating patient contact, using telemonitoring, initiating timely steroid therapy or accelerating elective procedures.24 25

Model interpretability and drivers of high CD cost

Permutation-based feature impact analysis was performed on each month’s optimal ML model to identify the claim features with the greatest influence on predictions. The 20 most important features, aggregated across these optimal models, are shown in online supplemental table 5. Only eight of these features overlapped with the 20 most frequent codes in the CD population, suggesting that code frequency alone does not capture the strongest risk signals. Nearly half of the high-impact features were HCPCS codes for biological monoclonal antibodies (adalimumab, infliximab, certolizumab, vedolizumab and ustekinumab). Other high-impact features included infusion-related procedures, ileostomy status, therapeutic drug quantification, high-complexity outpatient evaluation and management codes, and comorbidity indicators such as chronic pain syndrome and atrial fibrillation (online supplemental table 5). Pharmacy costs alone did not distinguish ML-flagged members from actual high-risk peers, as the average cost per pharmacy interaction differed by only $2 (online supplemental table 4B). Instead, patterns reflecting comorbidity burden (eg, depressive disorder, hypertension) and complex encounters (eg, gastroenterology admissions, endoscopic dilation) contributed substantial additional signals. Overall, these findings suggest that the models identify near-term high spending by combining biological therapy use with broader clinical context from the claims dataset.

Discussion

ML models predicted approximately 80% of the total dollars spent by the highest-cost CD members over 4 months, which is 10%–20% more than rules-based predictions considering recent spending. ML modelling also identified nearly two times as many patients at risk of entering the top quartile of healthcare spending. Most of the increased costs for these patients were due to inpatient hospitalisations rather than medication expenses, highlighting the importance of early intervention to prevent emergency care.

Comparison with prior claims-based cost prediction studies

Previous claims-based cost prediction studies have mainly focused on mixed chronic disease populations, used longer prediction horizons and relied on cross-sectional validation methods.26 Fewer studies have examined immune-mediated conditions like CD or assessed performance in a completely prospective, time-sequenced setting. In contrast, this monthly framework shows that short-term forecasting is practical and clinically relevant for flare-prone conditions. Feature impact analysis indicates that biological HCPCS codes serve as useful risk signals, but they are most effective when interpreted alongside comorbidity burden and encounter patterns that reflect clinical complexity. This approach also supports the use of gradient-boosted trees, which are well-suited to handling sparse, high-dimensional claims data because they can model nonlinear effects and interactions that are hard to specify in linear regression. Although coefficient-based interpretation is not available as it is with linear models, permutation-based feature importance offers a global view of influential features, and local explanation techniques such as Shapley Additive exPlanations or Shapley values for tree models can enhance case-level transparency and are planned in future deployments.27

Operational implications for payers, providers and care management teams

Risk stratification programmes that rely solely on trailing expenditures can overlook members whose costs are likely to increase soon. Adding a monthly ML risk score could help care teams prioritise outreach and deploy targeted actions, such as initiating telemonitoring, optimising medication plans and coordinating timely elective care to prevent avoidable emergency visits. Since the described framework uses standard administrative claim fields, it can be adapted to other episodic, high-variance conditions with minimal adjustments. Operational deployment should consider claims processing delays and data refresh schedules, integration into current care management workflows and clinician acceptance to avoid alert fatigue. Predictive cost profiling should be managed to facilitate proactive outreach and care coordination rather than restrict access, with ongoing monitoring for performance drift and bias across patient groups.

Strengths, limitations and considerations for generalisability

Key strengths of this work include (1) a large, population-level commercial claims cohort with real-world relevance; (2) a strictly time-ordered split between development and evaluation data that prevents information leakage; and (3) permutation-based feature-impact analysis that makes the model’s decision process transparent and clinically interpretable. However, generalisability is limited by the fact that the dataset comes from a single commercially insured population, the absence of clinical variables such as laboratory or endoscopic data, and the reliance on claims from 2016 to 2018, which predates several newer biological agents. Additionally, CD case status was defined using ≥1 ICD-10 K50 code without additional retrospective or prospective phenotype verification, so performance is reported for a claims-based cohort that may include coding overlap with ulcerative colitis. Musculoskeletal and chronic pain comorbidities were common in this claims-defined CD cohort (online supplemental table 1) and might reflect extraintestinal manifestations or co-occurring pain syndromes. Claims data alone cannot determine the underlying cause. Sensitivity analyses varying the time gap between model development and simulated deployment were not performed, and the inpatient claims processing lag may be longer than the 4-month gap used here. Therefore, prospective validation and recalibration will be necessary as treatment patterns and coding practices have evolved.

Future research: impact validation and enhanced data inputs

Prospective implementation studies should assess whether ML alerts reduce emergency visits, unplanned admissions and total costs, and whether the benefits outweigh the deployment expenses. External validation across different payer types and regions, the integration of electronic health record variables, and fairness analyses by socioeconomic and racial groups are essential. These analyses will ensure robustness and equity throughout the patient population.

Taken together, these findings suggest that monthly ML forecasting may offer a significant advantage over retrospective spending rules-based prediction in identifying CD patients whose costs are likely to increase, thus creating an opportunity for earlier, targeted intervention. However, real-world deployment and rigorous prospective evaluation will be essential to confirm clinical effectiveness, economic value and equitable performance across diverse settings.
