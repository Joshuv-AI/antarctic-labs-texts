Hijacking and subversion of macrophage antiviral functions by viruses
Authors: Zhen Xu, Fengyang Shi, Jun Han, Lei Zhou, Hanchun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMacrophages are frontline effectors of innate immunity, acting as essential elements for clearing pathogens. However, viruses have continuously evolved sophisticated mechanisms to subvert these critical defenses. This review comprehensively decodes how viral pathogens systematically dismantle macrophage microbicidal capacities. It delineates the evasion of pattern recognition receptor surveillance, the hijacking of endocytic trafficking, and the active arrest of phagolysosomal maturation to secure intracellular replication niches. Furthermore, the review explores the paradoxical viral manipulation of the host oxidative burst, where pathogens weaponize reactive oxygen species or exploit antioxidant machineries, driving severe redox dysregulation. Profound metabolic reprogramming, including shifts toward aerobic glycolysis and the skewing of inflammatory polarization alongside cell death pathways, is also examined. Ultimately, these evasion strategies inflict functional paralysis on macrophages, heavily predisposing hosts to severe secondary bacterial coinfections. Mapping this virus-host crosstalk highlights critical vulnerabilities, providing a foundation for novel host-directed antiviral immunotherapies.

Introduction

As macrophages distributed throughout the body, they possess the remarkable ability to rapidly move toward sites of infection through chemotaxis. They are usually among the first immune cells to detect and identify invading pathogens, triggering a cascade of anti-infectious and inflammatory responses that play vital roles in both normal physiological and pathological processes. In innate immunity, macrophages eliminate invading bacteria, viruses, damaged and apoptotic cells, and antigen – antibody complexes through their potent phagocytic and microbicidal activities. Additionally, they facilitate the recruitment of neutrophils, monocytes, and other immune cells, playing an indispensable role in maintaining organismal homeostasis.

Due to their indispensable role in immune defense, macrophages act as the frontline sentinels of the host. Paradoxically, this prominence also renders them prime targets for viral subversion. Intrinsically, macrophages are equipped to recognize and directly eliminate viral particles through robust phagocytosis, while simultaneously orchestrating broader immune responses via the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, acting as the critical bridge to adaptive immunity, they process and present antigens to initiate specific T-cell memory. However, viruses have evolved elaborate strategies to systematically dismantle these defensive pillars. They escape immune surveillance by directly evading phagocytic clearance, suppressing host gene expression, and blunting interferon responses. Additionally, viruses can paralyze the antigen presentation-T cell activation axis by downregulating MHC molecules, thereby shutting down the initiation of specific immunity. Beyond merely neutralizing these defenses, viruses actively hijack the intrinsic biological features of macrophages. The extensive tissue distribution of macrophages, combined with their high expression of specific viral receptors, makes them highly susceptible portals for pathogens [1,2]. Once inside, viruses exploit the profound functional plasticity of these cells, successfully reprograming their hosts. Consequently, the macrophage’s role is fatally inverted: rather than acting as the primary line of viral clearance, the compromised cell is converted into a sheltered reservoir for viral latency, replication, and systemic dissemination [3–8].

To orchestrate this cellular hijacking, viruses employ a multifaceted arsenal to systematically dismantle macrophage antiviral functions. This encompasses suppressing early immune recognition, circumventing endocytic and phagolysosomal processing, paralyzing reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generation, and actively rewiring immunometabolism and cell death pathways. By comprehensively decoding these evasion mechanisms, this review clarifies how viral infections cripple the microbicidal capacity of macrophages and precipitate the ensuing pathophysiological cascade-most notably, the heightened susceptibility to secondary bacterial coinfections. Ultimately, by mapping the intricate molecular crosstalk between viruses and host macrophages, this work highlights critical vulnerabilities in viral evasion strategies and enhanced bacterial co-/secondary infections, providing a robust theoretical foundation for the development of novel, host-directed antiviral immunotherapies.

Viral subversion of macrophage antiviral mechanisms

Modulation of pattern recognition receptor expression and signal transduction

Macrophages serve as a critical frontline defense in innate immunity. They recognize viral pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)-such as viral nucleic acids and surface glycoproteins-and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) through a diverse repertoire of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs). These predominantly include Toll-like receptors (TLRs), RIG-I-like receptors (RLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), cytosolic DNA sensors (CDSs, notably cGAS), and C-type lectin receptors (CLRs). Upon recognition, these PRRs trigger complex intracellular signaling cascades that mediate pathogen uptake, microbicidal responses, and the robust production of inflammatory mediators. To bypass host immune surveillance, viruses execute a coordinated, multi-tiered blockade of PRR pathways, systematically dismantling the cascade from initial sensory recognition to terminal effector functions.

At the sensory level, viruses actively “blind” macrophages by suppressing PRR expression or physically obstructing ligand binding. For instance, the influenza virus NS1 protein represses the expression of TLR3/7/9 and RIG-I [9], while vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV) induces miR-218 to specifically downregulate RIG-I, thereby aborting type I interferon (IFN-I) production at its inception [10–13]. Furthermore, proteins encoded by Hepatitis C virus (HCV) and VSV can directly impede RIG-I from recognizing viral RNA, effectively silencing the early antiviral alarm [14,15].

When initial recognition cannot be completely prevented, viruses strategically sever or distort the downstream intracellular signal transduction networks. This is frequently achieved by targeting crucial adaptor molecules and transcription factors. The HCV NS3/4A protease, for example, effectively cleaves the mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) to decouple RLR signaling [16]. Similarly, viral effectors such as the HIV Vpr protein and the murine cytomegalovirus (MCMV) M35 protein mediate the degradation or antagonism of key immune hubs like IRF3 and NF-κB [17–19]. Influenza virus further exploits this layer by upregulating host miR-144 to target and degrade TRAF6 [10]. Rather than simply silencing these pathways, some pathogens cunningly recalibrate signal intensity to their advantage: chronic HIV-1 infection upregulates TLR2/4 to trigger a pro-viral hyper-inflammatory state [20], whereas highly pathogenic porcine reproductive and respiratory syndrome virus (HP-PRRSV) cripples TLR-dependent TNF-α release by throttling ERK signaling [21].

Traversing down to the final effector stage, viruses directly antagonize the synthesis and autocrine/paracrine functions of crucial cytokines. For instance, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) encodes BCRF1, a viral homolog of interleukin-10 (vIL-10) that potently binds to the host IL-10 receptor on macrophages. This interaction directly suppresses the synthesis of crucial pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IFN-γ, thereby paralyzing their autocrine and paracrine inflammatory networks and establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment conducive to long-term viral persistence [22].

Beyond dismantling signal transduction pathways, viruses employ sophisticated physical and structural camouflage to preemptively evade macrophage recognition and phagocytosis, a concealment strategy that operates comprehensively from molecular masking to intracellular sequestration. At the molecular surface, pathogens exploit steric shielding and molecular mimicry to thwart opsonization; for instance, during HCV infection, the virus associates with host lipoproteins to form complexes that effectively mask viral antigenic epitopes, rendering FcγRs unable to recognize the virus and thereby blocking phagocytic clearance. Similarly, the immense structural variability of the HIV-1 envelope glycoprotein gp120 acts as a decoy, eliciting non-neutralizing antibodies that sterically hinder true epitope recognition [23]. HSV-1 adopts a more active decoy strategy, wherein its glycoprotein C (gC-1) binds with high affinity to the complement component C3b, competitively bypassing the complement activation cascade and drastically reducing complement-mediated opsonization and macrophage phagocytic efficiency [24]. Scaling up to the cellular level, several pathogens physically cloak themselves in host-derived biomaterials. HIV and various herpesviruses, for example, hijack host cell membranes during their assembly and budding processes, wrapping their nucleocapsids in “self” lipid bilayers to efficiently conceal immunogenic PAMPs from extracellular surveillance. Finally, this strategy of physical concealment extends deep into the intracellular space. Upon entry, large DNA viruses like ASFV and poxvirus members do not leave their genomes exposed in the cytoplasm; instead, they leverage biophysical mechanisms such as liquid-liquid phase separation to strictly compartmentalize their replication processes within specialized viral factories or organelles. By building these physical barriers, they successfully sequester their nucleic acids, averting detection by cytosolic pattern recognition receptors such as cGAS [25–27].

Hijacking of endocytic and vesicular trafficking pathways

Rather than passively succumbing to macrophage phagocytosis, viruses actively co-opt host entry mechanisms to dictate their own intracellular fate. Many pathogens, including DENV, JEV, FMDV, and ASFV, exploit clathrin-dependent endocytosis to gain stealthy access to the cytoplasm, thereby bypassing the hostile, degradative environment of the phagolysosomal pathway [28]. Conversely, some viruses deploy enzymatic strategies to modulate host entry mechanisms; for instance, the neuraminidase of the influenza virus hydrolyzes sialic acid residues on the macrophage surface, thereby aborting receptor-mediated phagocytosis and simultaneously preventing subsequent phagosome-lysosome fusion to secure viral survival [29].

Following successful entry, viruses further hijack the host’s dynamic vesicular trafficking network to safely navigate from the cell periphery to specialized perinuclear replication niches. Instead of being trafficked for degradation, viral proteins interact with host transport machineries to route their own components through the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and Golgi apparatus. Hepatitis B virus (HBV), for example, commandeers the early endosome-to-late endosome and multivesicular body (MVB) pathways to facilitate the assembly and transport of HBsAg [30,31]. Similarly, certain RNA viruses actively upregulate the GTP-bound form of ADP-ribosylation factor 4 (ARF4) to accelerate virion transport from the ER to the Golgi, repurposing the host’s secretory pathway for virion maturation and egress [32].

Ultimately, this extensive subversion of intracellular trafficking culminates in the structural paralysis of the macrophage. Viruses such as porcine circovirus 2 (PCV2), classical swine fever virus (CSFV), and Newcastle disease virus (NDV) drive their own replication by aggressively remodeling the host’s actin cytoskeleton. Because functional phagocytosis is profoundly dependent on rapid and precise cytoskeletal rearrangements, this virus-induced structural distortion severely impairs the macrophage’s ability to capture and internalize secondary pathogens. Consequently, the macrophage is rendered functionally paralyzed, thereby permitting extracellular bacterial proliferation and systemic dissemination [33–35]. Subversion of lysosomal degradation and clearance

To effectively eliminate engulfed pathogens, macrophages deploy a highly destructive intracellular trap: the phagolysosome. Following viral internalization, the internalizing endosomes undergo a rigorous maturation cascade, sequentially transitioning from early to late endosomes before finally fusing with primary lysosomes. This critical fusion event triggers a drastic drop in luminal pH and unleashes a concentrated arsenal of proteolytic enzymes designed to aggressively dismantle the viral capsid and degrade its nucleic acids. Ultimately, this creates a hostile, nutrient-depleted microenvironment that is fundamentally incompatible with pathogen survival [36,37]. However, evolutionary pressure has equipped numerous viruses with specialized effector proteins that actively subvert this hydrolytic machinery. Rather than perishing within the phagolysosome, these pathogens execute precise biochemical countermeasures to halt maturation or neutralize acidification. Some also physically rupture the compartment, thereby converting an intended lethal trap into a secure, permissive niche for intracellular replication.

The relationship between viruses and the endolysosomal pathway is highly paradoxical. Viruses frequently exploit the mildly acidic milieu of early endosomes to trigger essential conformational changes. For instance, the SARS-CoV-2 Spike (S) protein and the IAV hemagglutinin (HA) protein undergo such changes to enable membrane fusion and uncoating [38]. However, these pathogens must precisely halt this maturation before encountering the destructive core of the lysosome. To achieve this control, viruses actively sever the fusion machinery. The HIV-1 Nef protein recruits the homotypic fusion and vacuole protein sorting (HOPS) complex and antagonizes Rab5a function. This dismantles the vesicular tethering process and arrests phagosomal maturation [39]. Similarly, chikungunya virus (CHIKV) uses its non-structural protein 3 (Nsp3) to block phagosome-lysosome fusion. This shields viral components from degradative enzymes [40]. Crucially, by paralyzing normal phagosomal maturation, viruses not only secure their own replication niches but consequently convert macrophages into circulating “Trojan horses” that shelter and disseminate engulfed bacterial pathogens.

Beyond merely blocking fusion, viruses deploy aggressive mechanisms to neutralize the functional core of the lysosome itself. A markedly low-pH environment is an absolute prerequisite for optimal hydrolase activity and the generation of toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS). To dismantle this, several pathogens directly sabotage the vacuolar-type H+ -ATPase (V-ATPase) proton pump. For example, Human cytomegalovirus (HCMV) US33A degrades DMXL1, a binding partner of V-ATPase. This degradation inhibits lysosomal acidification and repurposes dysfunctional lysosomes for viral assembly. Additionally, it effectively blocks MHC II-mediated antigen presentation [41]. β-coronaviruses, including SARS-CoV-2, induce the cellular egress of lysosomes and disrupt V-ATPase function, causing profound deacidification and the catastrophic collapse of antigen presentation pathways [42]. Likewise, HIV-1 actively excludes V-ATPase from its specialized endosomal assembly compartments, ensuring the long-term persistence of infectious virions within macrophages [43].

Concurrently, many viruses induce physical destruction of the organelle by inducing lysosomal membrane permeabilization (LMP). Proteins such as HIV-1 gp120 and ASFV effectors precipitate mitochondrial dysfunction, which indirectly leads to lysosomal deacidification and impairs pathogen recognition [44,45]. Other viral factors, including the NDV HN protein [46] and the H5N1 NA protein [47,48], alongside effectors from DENV and AdV [49], actively mediate the deglycosylation and degradation of crucial membrane protectants like LAMP1 and LAMP2. This structural breach provokes the catastrophic leakage of cathepsins (CTSB and CTSD), culminating in lysosomal rupture and inflammatory cell death. Coronaviruses further exploit this axis by pairing fusion-blockade with cathepsin-B-dependent lysosomal rupture and pyroptotic IL-1β release [50]. Ultimately, this orchestrated deacidification and structural rupture not only obliterates the hydrolytic “kill zone.” Furthermore, it fundamentally paralyzes cellular homeostasis, which markedly predisposes the host to severe secondary bacterial infections [51].

Subversion of the oxidative burst: from defensive RONS to cellular self-destruction

During a viral invasion, macrophages rapidly mount a “respiratory burst,” mobilizing the NADPH oxidase (NOX) complex and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) to flood the phagosome with highly reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) [52]. While these molecules are intrinsically microbicidal and critical for amplifying antiviral signaling [53], they operate as a dangerous double-edged sword [54,55]. Rather than simply suppressing RONS, many viruses-including SARS-CoV-2, HCV, and HSV-1-actively exploit this oxidative stress [56,57]. By deliberately inducing mitochondrial dysfunction, viruses trigger a pathological overproduction of ROS. This excessive oxidative storm is then hijacked to incapacitate essential immune sensors; for instance, HSV-1 utilizes RONS to oxidize STING, effectively abrogating the cGAS-STING-IFN cascade [58–60], while the AIV NS1 protein suppresses RIG-I-mediated ROS signaling [61]. Concurrently, this virus-induced RONS activates pathways like NF-κB and Nrf2, creating a hyper-inflammatory, pro-proliferative milieu that paradoxically shields the viruses and promotes their replication [62–68].

To survive the very oxidative storm they provoked, viruses execute a secondary subversion: they selfishly hijack the macrophage’s intrinsic antioxidant defense machinery. Pathogens such as HBV, HCV, and RSV actively upregulate host antioxidant enzymes-including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx)-to specifically scavenge immediate threats to their virions [65,69–72]. This enforced biochemical scavenging massively depletes intracellular glutathione (GSH) pools, fundamentally shattering the cell’s redox homeostasis. In contrast, viruses selectively dismantle these defenses, as seen when AIV downregulates SOD1 to deliberately trigger caspase-dependent apoptosis and the hypersecretion of pro-inflammatory IL-8 [73]. Through this biochemical manipulation, the macrophage’s redox balance is completely uncoupled from its defensive mandate [74–76].

Ultimately, this orchestrated redox dysregulation locks the macrophage into an inescapable cycle of self-destruction. The unmitigated relentless of ROS inflicts catastrophic damage on the host’s own mitochondria, severely curtailing the supply of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [77–80]. Stripped of cellular energy, the macrophage is functionally paralyzed, unable to execute ATP-dependent clearance functions such as chemotaxis or phagocytosis. Furthermore, the toxic accumulation of ROS drives a pathogenic shift toward hyper-inflammation [81,82] and triggers intrinsic necrotic and apoptotic cell death pathways [83,84]. By turning the macrophage’s most potent microbicidal weapon-the oxidative burst-against its own organelles, viruses ensure the complete functional and structural collapse of the cell.

Metabolic reprogramming: hijacking the host supply chain

The execution of macrophage microbicidal functions is inherently energy-intensive, requiring precise metabolic shifts to power the oxidative burst and cytokine synthesis [3]. Viruses, however, aggressively hijack these pathways, diverting cellular energy and substrates to fuel their own replication, nucleotide synthesis, and assembly [80,85]. By fundamentally rewiring host immunometabolism, viruses not only secure their proliferation but also induce a state of “metabolic paralysis” that suppresses antiviral effector functions.

A primary target of this subversion is glucose metabolism. Pathogens such as SARS-CoV-2, IAV, HCV, and PRRSV prevent macrophages from using energy for defense. Instead, they trigger mitochondrial ROS production to stabilize HIF-1α. This forcefully shifts the cell into a state of aerobic glycolysis [56,86,87]. This strategic diversion from oxidative phosphorylation allows viruses to avoid the lethal accumulation of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS) that might otherwise jeopardize their survival. However, this shift creates a hyper-inflammatory yet dysfunctional macrophage state. For instance, both ASFV and Senecavirus A (SVA) promote glycolysis and excessive lactate production. This intracellular lactate accumulation specifically weakens the interaction between MAVS and RIG-I. Consequently, this disruption silences the IFN-I antiviral response [9,85].

Beyond stealing energy, viruses commandeer lipid metabolism to construct physical sanctuaries for replication. Pathogens like SARS-CoV-2, HCV, and HBV actively induce de novo lipogenesis and the formation of lipid droplets (LDs) [30,31,88,89]. Rather than supporting host membrane homeostasis, these stolen lipids are repurposed into replication compartments and viral envelopes [90]. Conversely, the dengue virus (DENV) triggers the autophagic degradation of LDs, violently extracting carbon sources for ATP production to power its own assembly [91]. This virus-enforced elevation of unsaturated free fatty acids (FFAs) and fatty acid β oxidation further facilitates viral immune evasion and complicates the macrophage’s clearance capacity [92,93].

Finally, this metabolic subversion extends to the systematic depletion of crucial amino acids, effectively starving the macrophage of antiviral resources. HIV, for example, strictly relies on glutamine as a primary energy source to maintain latent infection within macrophages [94]. More devastatingly, pathogens like HSV-1 and Sendai virus induce high expression of Arginase-1 (Arg1), which forcibly depletes intracellular arginine [95,96]. This not only suppresses iNOS-mediated nitric oxide (NO) killing functions. Paradoxically, it also provides polyamines essential for robust viral replication. Similarly, HSV-1 and Coxsackievirus B3 (CVB3) utilize amino acid reprogramming to facilitate viral protein synthesis. Concurrently, they drive aberrant pro-inflammatory polarization that exacerbates tissue damage [97,98]. Skewing of macrophage polarization and inflammatory cytokine production

The functional plasticity of macrophages, characterized by a spectrum ranging from pro-inflammatory (M1) to anti-inflammatory (M2) polarization, is a cornerstone of effective pathogen clearance. Typically, viral infections trigger a shift toward the M1 phenotype to unleash microbicidal effectors. However, many viruses exhibit a remarkable ability to act as “molecular infiltrators,” skewing this polarization or inducing a dysfunctional hyper-inflammatory state to evade host immunity [99,100]. To create a permissive environment for long-term persistence, several viruses actively suppress M1 activation while promoting the M2-like “repair” phenotype, which lacks potent killing capacity. For instance, ASFV utilizes its pE248R and pE199L proteins to impair M1-associated gene expression, effectively blunting the macrophage’s early antiviral response [101]. Similarly, PRRSV and Ebola virus (EBOV) drive macrophages toward M2 polarization, characterized by the upregulation of CD163 and CD206, which facilitates viral entry and persistent replication [102–104]. This strategic shift is often mediated by the subversion of key signaling hubs, viruses like HBV and IAV modulate the STAT1/STAT6 balance or hijack the PPARγ pathway to dampen the microbicidal burst and favor a pro-viral, anti-inflammatory niche [105–108].

Conversely, some pathogens-most notably SARS-CoV-2 and highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI)-exploit the M1 axis to induce a “cytokine storm.” Rather than facilitating clearance, this uncontrolled surge of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, drives systemic tissue damage and induces profound macrophage exhaustion [109–111]. Specifically, the SARS-CoV-2 Spike protein and various accessory proteins potently activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, triggering pyroptotic cell death and the massive release of inflammatory mediators [112,113]. This dysregulated inflammatory response often masks concurrent viral inhibition of type I interferons, which constitute the primary defense against viruses [57,114].

Beyond skewing global polarization, viruses execute precise targeted strikes against the signaling cascades that govern cytokine production. To suppress the host’s “alarm system,” viruses such as HSV-1, HIV-1, and DENV deploy effectors to antagonize the NF-κB and AP-1 pathways [115,116]. For example, certain viral proteins function as deubiquitinases, while others act as competitive inhibitors that sequester adaptor proteins like TRAF3/6 or MyD88, thereby silencing the production of essential microbicidal cytokine responses [117–119]. By orchestrating this dual strategy-inducing an ineffective polarized state while simultaneously paralyzing critical defense signaling-viruses ensure that the macrophage remains functionally incapacitated, unable to coordinate the broader immune response and leaving the host vulnerable to secondary bacterial proliferation [120].

Epigenetic reprogramming of macrophage antiviral functions

Viruses have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to subvert host innate immunity, and epigenetic reprogramming of macrophages represents a cornerstone of this immune evasion strategy. Upon entering the host cell, viral pathogens hijack the cellular epigenetic machinery-including DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), histone acetyltransferases (HATs), histone deacetylases (HDACs), and histone methyltransferases (HMTs)-to silence antiviral gene expression and establish a cellular environment permissive for viral replication [121,122]. This epigenetic manipulation encompasses DNA methylation at CpG islands in immune gene promoters, deposition of repressive histone marks such as H3K9me3 and H3K27me3, and removal of activating marks including H3K4me3 and H3K27ac. The consequences extend beyond acute infection, as these modifications can persist long after viral clearance, creating an “epigenetic memory” that shapes macrophage responses to subsequent challenges and contributes to post-viral syndromes.

DNA methylation represents a critical mechanism through which viruses silence macrophage immune genes. SARS-CoV-2 infection induces DNMT1 and DNMT3A upregulation, resulting in hypermethylation of PRR gene promoters including TLR3, RIG-I, and MDA5 in lung macrophages and dendritic cells. Concurrently, hypermethylation of IRF5 and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) genes suppresses M1-like pro-inflammatory programming, skewing macrophages toward regulatory or tolerogenic states that facilitate viral persistence [123,124]. Similarly, HIV-1 establishes unique methylation patterns in macrophage reservoirs through ubiquitin-like with PHD and ring finger domains 1 (UHRF1) -mediated recruitment of DNMT1 to the viral 5’ long terminal repeat (LTR) [125], while the HBV HBx protein counteracts host high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) -mediated silencing of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) by interacting with DNMTs and histone-modifying proteins [126]. These findings highlight DNA methylation as a convergent viral strategy for long-term immune suppression in macrophages.

Histone post-translational modifications provide dynamic yet heritable mechanisms for regulating chromatin accessibility at immune gene loci. SARS-CoV-2 infection decreases chromatin accessibility at IFNB1, ISG15, and IRF7 loci, correlating with increased repressive H3K27me3 and reduced activating H3K4me3 and H3K27ac marks [127]. The viral ORF8 protein functions as a histone H3 mimic, containing an ARKSAP motif that increases global H3K9me3 and H3K27me3 while decreasing H3K9ac, leading to widespread chromatin condensation [128]. Concurrently, non-structural protein 1 (NSP1) recruits the G9a/GLP H3K9 methyltransferase complex via proline rich coiled-coil 2B (PRRC2B) to deposit H3K9me2 at immune gene promoters, and NSP5 delivers HDAC2 to the class II major histocompatibility complex transactivator (CIITA) promoter to silence MHC class II expression [129]. In HIV-infected macrophages, the proviral genome is enriched with H3K9me3 and an unusual bivalent mark of H3K9me3/H3K27ac, establishing a unique epigenetic state that contributes to viral latency [130].

Epigenetic modifications orchestrate the balance between pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2 macrophage polarization, and viruses exploit this regulatory axis to their advantage. M1 polarization is favored by DNMT1/DNMT3b-mediated DNA methylation, HDAC3-mediated deacetylation, SET Domain Bifurcated Histone Lysine Methyltransferase 1(SETDB1) -mediated H3K9 methylation, and enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2) -mediated H3K27 trimethylation. Conversely, M2 polarization requires Jumonji domain-containing 3/ Lysine Demethylase 6B (JMJD3/KDM6B) -mediated H3K27 demethylation, HDAC1/HDAC10 activity, and H3K4 methylation by SET and MYND domain containing 3 (SMYD3). The HBsAg and HBeAg antigens of the HBV can regulate Notch1 signaling through SIRT1-mediated deacetylation, thereby driving macrophage polarization toward an M2-like tolerogenic state [131]. This epigenetic polarization shift not only dampens antiviral responses during acute infection but may also contribute to the immunosuppressive phenotypes.

Beyond acute infection, viral encounters can imprint lasting epigenetic memories in macrophages through two distinct programs: trained immunity and tolerance. Trained immunity is characterized by deposition of H3K4me1 at enhancers and H3K27ac at promoters, maintaining immune genes in a transcriptionally poised state that enables enhanced responses upon restimulation [132,133]. Conversely, tolerance manifests as failure to deposit activating marks, decreased chromatin accessibility, and diminished transcription factor recruitment-resembling the immune paralysis observed in sepsis. Notably, even after mild COVID-19, monocyte-derived macrophages retain H3K27ac at IL-1B, SYK, and NOD2, creating a pro-inflammatory memory that may underlie Long COVID pathophysiology [134,135]. The reversibility of these epigenetic modifications offers promising therapeutic opportunities, with HDAC inhibitors (vorinostat, valproic acid), DNMT inhibitors (decitabine, 5-azacytidine), and histone methyltransferase inhibitors (UNC0638) showing potential for restoring macrophage immune function following viral infection.

Regulation of cell death pathways

The life-or-death decision of a macrophage is a pivotal determinant of the host-pathogen struggle. Programmed cell death (PCD) serves as a potent innate defense mechanism to eliminate the viral replication niche and alert neighboring cells. However, viruses have evolved to manipulate these pathways-either by prematurely executing macrophages to facilitate dissemination and tissue damage, or by delaying cell death to ensure long-term intracellular survival.

Rather than allowing for silent clearance, many viruses aggressively trigger highly inflammatory forms of cell death, such as pyroptosis and necroptosis, to dismantle the macrophage barrier. For instance, ASFV, HBV, and CSFV actively induce caspase-3-dependent apoptosis or NLRP3-mediated pyroptosis, leading to the catastrophic loss of functional macrophages and systemic immunosuppression [136–138]. SARS-CoV-2 accessory proteins and the IAV PB1-F2 protein similarly drive NLRP3 inflammasome activation, unleashing IL-1β release and gasdermin D-mediated pore formation, which culminates in pyroptotic cell rupture [139,140]. Furthermore, some pathogens utilize necroptosis as a “fail-safe” death mechanism; viruses like rotavirus and poxviruses can modulate the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis to ensure the cell undergoes a necrotic exit, effectively bypassing apoptotic checkpoints and promoting a hyper-inflammatory environment [141–143].

Conversely, to establish persistent infections, many viruses execute a precise “anti-death” program, shielding the infected macrophage from host-initiated apoptosis. DNA viruses, such as HPV and various herpesviruses, express viral homologs of the anti-apoptotic Bcl-2 family or directly inhibit caspase-8/9 activity to prevent the “altruistic suicide” of the cell [144,145]. In the context of porcine viruses, PRRSV has been shown to employ its non-structural proteins to delay apoptosis during the early stages of infection, thereby maximizing the window for virion assembly and maturation [146]. This inhibition of PCD is often coupled with the subversion of autophagy; viruses like HIV and HCV hijack the autophagic machinery – not for degradation, but to utilize autophagosomal membranes as protected scaffolds for their own replication complexes, a process termed “proviral autophagy” [147–149].

By orchestrating this complex regulation of cell death, viruses ensure that macrophages either perish in a manner that favors viral spread or survive as exhausted, dysfunctional hosts. This ultimate subversion of the macrophage’s lifespan fundamentally paralyzes the host’s primary antiviral front, ultimately enabling the uncontrolled proliferation of both the primary viral pathogen and opportunistic secondary bacteria.

Bone marrow progenitor education and long-term immune maladaptation

Viral infections do not merely target differentiated immune cells in peripheral tissues-many viruses directly infect or indirectly reprogram hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) resident in the bone marrow. Viruses including HIV, CMV, SARS-CoV-2, and lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) have been shown to infect HSPCs, trigger their apoptotic death, and alter their differentiation trajectories through inflammatory cytokine signaling [150–152]. This progenitor-level subversion has profound consequences: the bone marrow becomes a site of viral persistence and a source of dysfunctional immune cells that can persist long after peripheral viral clearance.

During viral infection, inflammatory cytokines including type I and II interferons drive emergency myelopoiesis-a rapid shift in hematopoietic output toward myeloid cells at the expense of lymphoid differentiation [153,154]. While initially protective, sustained emergency myelopoiesis generates immature myeloid cells with suppressive rather than antiviral functions, including myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) that potently inhibit T cell responses [155]. The expanded MDSC populations observed in severe COVID-19 and chronic viral infections represent epigenetically reprogrammed progeny that actively suppresses adaptive immunity when it is most needed for coordinated pathogen clearance.

A paradigm-shifting concept in immunology is that viral infection can create durable epigenetic memory in bone marrow progenitors. Landmark studies demonstrated that inflammatory stimuli reprogram HSPCs through histone modifications (H3K4me3 enrichment) and metabolic rewiring, with consequences for progeny monocyte function lasting months to years [151,156,157]. In severe COVID-19, HSPC epigenetic alterations persist for up to one year and are transmitted to progeny monocytes through differentiation, creating a self-perpetuating cycle of inflammatory myeloid cell production [150]. Critically, interleukin-6 (IL-6) receptor blockade during acute infection prevents these persistent changes, establishing a causal link between acute inflammatory signaling and long-term progenitor maladaptation.

The clinical consequences of viral progenitor subversion are starkly illustrated by post-influenza and post-COVID susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections. IAV depletes over 90% of tissue-resident alveolar macrophages within one week, while simultaneously reprogramming bone marrow progenitors to produce monocytes with impaired phagocytic functions [158–160]. SARS-CoV-2 induces endotoxin tolerance in monocytes, reducing TLR4 expression and NF-κB responsiveness to bacterial LPS. Together, these mechanisms create a “double hit” where both peripheral innate defenses and bone marrow production capacity are compromised, explaining why patients remain exquisitely vulnerable to bacterial superinfection even as viral titters decline.

Exosomes and tunnelling nanotubes as stealth conduits for viral spread

In addition to cell-free viral particles and direct cell-to-cell contact, viruses have evolved sophisticated “stealth” mechanisms for intercellular spread through exosomes and tunneling nanotubes (TNTs). These host-derived membrane structures allow viral components to travel between macrophages without entering the extracellular space, effectively bypassing neutralizing antibodies and other extracellular immune defenses. Exosomes-small extracellular vesicles (30–150 nm) derived from multivesicular bodies-can carry viral RNA, proteins, and even intact virions packaged through hijacked endosomal sorting complex required for transport (ESCRT) machinery. TNTs, in contrast, are dynamic actin-rich membrane bridges providing direct cytoplasmic continuity between cells, allowing rapid transfer of viral particles and replication machinery [161–163].

The biogenesis of exosomes and the budding of enveloped viruses converge on shared molecular machinery. Both processes recruit ESCRT complexes-particularly tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101, ESCRT-I) and charged multivesicular body protein 4 (CHMP4, ESCRT-III)-for membrane scission. HIV-1 Gag mimics the mechanism of intraluminal vesicle formation to drive viral budding, while simultaneously promoting exosome release from infected cells. Selective packaging of viral components is governed by RNA-binding proteins such as heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein A2/B1 (hnRNPA2/B1), which recognizes specific motifs in HIV trans-activation response (TAR) RNA to direct its sorting into exosomes [164].

HIV-1 exploits exosomes as stealth delivery vehicles for viral components between macrophages. The accessory protein Nef is selectively packaged into exosomes through ESCRT-dependent sorting and, upon uptake by recipient macrophages, triggers lipid raft reorganization and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, creating a pro-inflammatory microenvironment conducive to viral replication. Simultaneously, HIV TAR RNA delivered via exosomes activates TLR8-NF-κB signaling, inducing TNF-α and IL-6 secretion that further primes target cells for infection [165,166]. SARS-CoV-2 similarly leverages exosomes for intercellular spread; plasma exosomes from COVID-19 patients contain viral RNA detectable months after infection, and these exosomes activate endosomal TLRs (TLR3, TLR7, TLR9) in recipient monocytes and macrophages, driving sustained pro-inflammatory cytokine production characteristic of the COVID-19 cytokine storm [167].

Exosomes provide a multifaceted immune evasion advantage. Their host-cell membrane composition-enriched in CD47 (“don’t eat me” signal), complement regulatory proteins, and MHC molecules-cloaks viral cargoes from antibody recognition, complement lysis, and phagocytic clearance [168]. HCV RNA complexed with Argonaute 2 (Ago2) -HSP90-miR-122 within exosomes is shielded from neutralizing antibodies while remaining infectious [169]. HIV spike-bearing exosomes function as immunological decoys, absorbing neutralizing antibodies that would otherwise target authentic virions. Furthermore, viral components delivered via exosomes actively reprogram recipient macrophages: HIV Nef reorganizes lipid rafts to activate NLRP3, while HBV miR-21/29a and HCV-mediated MDSC expansion suppress antiviral effector functions. These dual mechanisms-passive shielding and active immune suppression-create a permissive niche for viral persistence [165,170].

Beyond exosomes, tunneling nanotubes provide a direct physical highway for viral spread between macrophages. HIV-1 Nef protein promotes TNT formation by activating the M-Sec/exocyst/RalA pathway, generating actin-rich intercellular bridges through which viral particles undergo rapid myosin-driven transport. Remarkably, TNTs mediate approximately half of all HIV spread between macrophages, enabling productive infection even in the presence of neutralizing antibodies [162,163]. SARS-CoV-2 has similarly co-opted TNTs as a route for viral spread, including invasion of non-permissive neuronal cells. Using correlative fluorescence and cryo-electron tomography, Pepe et al. demonstrated that SARS-CoV-2 induces TNT formation between permissive epithelial cells and non-permissive neuronal cells, with viral replication machinery and double-membrane vesicles observed within these intercellular conduits [171]. In tuberculosis (TB) -HIV co-infection, Mycobacterium tuberculosis creates a feed-forward loop of viral amplification by driving IL-10/STAT3-dependent TNT formation in macrophages, with TB-associated type I interferon further upregulating Siglec-1 on TNTs to enhance viral capture and intercellular transfer [172,173].

Tissue-resident macrophage subsets and differential viral subversion

The tissue-specific consequences of macrophage viral subversion are profoundly shaped by the local microenvironment and the distinct developmental origins of resident macrophage populations. Unlike circulating monocyte-derived macrophages, tissue-resident macrophages arise from embryonic progenitors and exhibit unique receptor repertoires, metabolic wiring, and functional specializations that determine their susceptibility to viral infection [174,175].

Alveolar macrophages (AMs) reside in the airway lumen and are among the first immune cells encountered by inhaled respiratory viruses. SARS-CoV-2 enters AMs through angiotensin converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) -mediated endocytosis or alternative routes including FcγR- mediated antibody-dependent enhancement. Critically, SARS-CoV-2-infected AMs become drivers of the cytokine storm, producing excessive TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β that propagates lung tissue damage and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [176]. IAV binds to alpha-2,3- and alpha-2,6-linked sialic acid receptors on AMs, and the unique alveolar microenvironment enriched in surfactant protein A modulates IAV endosomal trafficking [177]. PRRSV represents a paradigm of strict macrophage-tropic infection, exploiting sialoadhesin for sialic acid-dependent attachment and CD163 for viral uncoating and genome release-an exquisite restriction that illustrates how viruses hijack macrophage-specific surface molecules for productive entry [178].

Kupffer cells (KCs), the largest population of tissue-resident macrophages, exhibit paradoxical responses to hepatotropic viruses. Rather than mounting a robust pro-inflammatory response, HBV-exposed KCs preferentially produce TGF-β1, promoting fibrogenesis. Remarkably, HBV induces an atypical metabolic state in KCs characterized by high oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) rather than glycolysis, which suppresses IL-1β production and enables viral persistence [179,180]. During HCV infection, KCs produce immunomodulatory mediators including IL-10, TGF-β, galectin-9, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) that suppress T cell responses, creating a pro-viral environment that facilitates persistent infection [181].

Microglia, as the sole immune cells residing in the central nervous system (CNS) parenchyma, deploy an extensive “sensome” of pattern recognition receptors for viral defense. Unlike peripheral macrophages, microglia express all TLRs, robust levels of cGAS-STING, and complement machinery that enable both viral sensing and synaptic pruning. HIV exploits the longevity and immune-privileged location of microglia to establish long-lived latent reservoirs, while Zika virus and HSV-1 trigger microglial responses that can cause more neuronal damage than the viruses themselves [182–185]. cGAS-STING signaling in microglia is essential for controlling HSV-1 spread in the brain [186], yet sustained activation drives complement-mediated synaptic loss and chronic neuroinflammation [182].

The peritoneal cavity exemplifies macrophage heterogeneity, containing two distinct subsets: large peritoneal macrophages (LPMs) expressing GATA Binding Protein 6 (GATA6) and maintained by self-renewal, and small peritoneal macrophages (SPMs) continuously replenished from bone marrow monocytes. During infection, LPMs disappear rapidly via an IFN-γ-dependent macrophage disappearance reaction, while recruited SPMs become the primary inflammatory effectors, producing IL-12, TNF-α, and NO [187]. This subset transition represents a conserved tissue-level adaptation that balances homeostatic maintenance with inflammatory defense.

The developmental origin of tissue-resident macrophages profoundly influences their susceptibility to viral infection. Microglia, exclusively derived from yolk sac progenitors and protected by the blood-brain barrier, represent the most stable macrophage population in the body with turnover rates spanning years or decades. This longevity makes them ideal but devastating reservoirs for HIV and other neurotropic viruses [188]. In contrast, alveolar macrophages show progressive replacement by monocyte-derived cells with aging, potentially contributing to increased vulnerability to respiratory infections in the elderly [189]. Understanding these developmental origins is essential for designing therapeutic strategies that can selectively target infected macrophage populations while preserving tissue homeostatic functions.

Microbiota-macrophage-virus interactions

An emerging dimension in understanding virus-macrophage interactions is the profound influence of the host microbiota on macrophage polarization and antiviral function. The intestinal microbiota exerts systemic effects on innate immunity through the gut-lung axis, wherein commensal bacteria and their metabolites-particularly short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs: acetate, propionate, butyrate)-continuously prime tissue-resident macrophages for antiviral defense. Germ-free and antibiotic-treated mice display impaired type I interferon production and reduced antiviral gene expression in alveolar macrophages, resulting in increased susceptibility to IAV, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and SARS-CoV-2 [190–192]. This microbiota-mediated priming operates through multiple mechanisms, including SCFA-G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR) signaling, histone deacetylase inhibition, and cGAS-STING-dependent interferon induction by bacterial extracellular vesicles [193].

Microbiota-derived SCFAs are key regulators of macrophage polarization during viral infection. During influenza infection, butyrate promotes the differentiation of bone marrow monocytes into alternatively activated macrophages (AAMs) through GPR41/FFAR3 signaling; these AAMs limit neutrophil recruitment to the airways by reducing C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1) production, thereby preventing immunopathology while maintaining antiviral defense [194]. Acetate, the most abundant SCFA, protects against RSV and influenza by boosting IFN-β production in lung macrophages through GPR43 activation [195,196]. Furthermore, butyrate imprints an antiviral program in macrophages through HDAC inhibition, enhancing their phagocytic and microbicidal capacity [195].

Viral infection initiates a bidirectional disruption of the gut-lung axis that creates a self-amplifying cycle of macrophage dysfunction. IAV infection alters gut microbiota composition, reducing SCFA-producing bacteria and increasing Enterobacteriaceae colonization. The consequent drop in acetate impairs alveolar macrophage phagocytosis, increasing susceptibility to secondary bacterial pneumonia-a major cause of influenza mortality [197]. Similarly, SARS-CoV-2 infection depletes immunomodulatory species including Faecalibacterium prausnitzii and Bifidobacterium, and this dysbiosis persists after viral clearance [198]. HIV infection causes more profound disruption, with gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) CD4+ T cell depletion leading to persistent microbial translocation, chronic monocyte/macrophage activation (elevated soluble CD14), and incomplete immune restoration despite antiretroviral therapy.

A recently discovered mechanism by which commensal bacteria prime macrophages for antiviral defense involves the delivery of bacterial DNA to host cells via extracellular vesicles (EVs). Gut commensal bacteria release membrane vesicles containing DNA that enter circulation and activate the cGAS-STING-IFN-I axis in distal immune cells, including mononuclear phagocytes [193]. This priming is independent of TLR signaling and maintains a tonic baseline of IFN-I that enhances resistance to both DNA and RNA viruses. Antibiotic suppression of gut microbiota eliminates this EV-mediated priming, rendering mice susceptible to systemic viral infections.

The most direct evidence for microbiota programming of alveolar macrophages comes from studies of segmented filamentous bacteria (SFB). Ngo et al. demonstrated that SFB colonization-either natural or exogenous-protected mice against IAV, RSV, and SARS-CoV-2 through a striking mechanism: SFB intrinsically altered alveolar macrophages to resist virus-induced depletion and inflammatory signaling. These SFB-transformed macrophages were not quiescent but actively disabled influenza virus through enhanced complement production and phagocytosis. Notably, this protection was independent of interferon and adaptive immunity, and the transfer of SFB-conditioned macrophages into naive hosts recapitulated the protective effect [199]. This work reveals that specific commensal bacteria can directly reprogram lung macrophages for non-phlogistic antiviral defense, positioning the microbiota as both a determinant and a therapeutic target of macrophage function during viral infection.

Impact of viral infection on macrophage antimicrobial function

The orchestrated subversion of macrophage machineries by viruses extends far beyond facilitating local viral persistence; it inflicts a profound “functional paralysis” that shatters the host’s secondary lines of defense. When macrophages-the primary sentinels responsible for clearing opportunistic pathogens-are structurally and metabolically incapacitated, the host is transformed into a highly vulnerable landscape for severe disease progression [200,201].

Amplified susceptibility to secondary bacterial infections

When viruses cripple the intrinsic bactericidal activity of macrophages, they directly induce critical defects in the intracellular processing and clearance of bacteria. Growing clinical and epidemiological evidence strongly underscores this fatal association between viral infections and secondary bacterial pathogens, particularly within the respiratory tract [202]. In cases of viral pneumonia, secondary bacterial coinfections account for approximately one-tenth of the overall deaths [203]. This phenomenon is starkly evident in COVID-19 patients, where approximately one-seventh experience secondary bacterial pneumonia-often involving Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Klebsiella species, or Staphylococcus aureus-which contribute to nearly half of all treatment-refractory deaths [204–207]. Similarly, HIV frequently establishes fatal coinfections with Mycobacterium tuberculosis, driving chronic inflammatory cascades and drastically exacerbating disease progression [208].

In the context of respiratory pathogens, IAV effectively suppresses macrophage bacterial clearance via profound mitochondrial damage and metabolic rewiring, thereby heavily predisposing the host to secondary methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) infections [209–211]. This immunocompromised state is equally devastating in veterinary medicine. For example, PRRSV infection induces severe interstitial pneumonia and actively diminishes the bactericidal and phagocytic functions of alveolar macrophages; this altered cytokine and antigen-presenting landscape renders pigs highly susceptible to Streptococcus suis bacteraemia [212]. Extensive in vitro studies using various models have confirmed that PRRSV infection fundamentally paralyzes the alveolar macrophage, paving the way for secondary bacterial dissemination [200,201,213–218]. Furthermore, pathogens like BVDV and parainfluenza virus systemically reduce the capacity of macrophages and neutrophils to phagocytose opsonized bacteria, firmly establishing that viral interference with macrophage signaling is a primary driver of opportunistic bacterial pathogenesis [219,220].

Transformation into viral reservoirs for persistent infection

Rather than eliminating invading pathogens, macrophages with compromised microbicidal functions are frequently hijacked and repurposed as secure vehicles for viral replication, systemic dissemination, and long-term persistence. Tissue-resident macrophages, owing to their longevity and distribution, are notoriously exploited as major sanctuaries for HIV. Similarly, SARS-CoV-2 has been shown to establish persistent replication niches within infected macrophages; within this sheltered environment, the virus not only evades neutralizing antibodies but may also accelerate viral evolution and drive the emergence of novel variants [221]. In other chronic infections, such pathogens actively manipulate the host cell phenotype; for instance, certain viruses (such as HBV and Epstein-Barr virus) drive macrophages into a stably immunosuppressive, M2-polarized state that prevents viral clearance and instead drives chronic inflammation and long-term tissue fibrosis [221–224].

Aberrant inflammation and exacerbated tissue damage

The impairment of normal macrophage antiviral functions frequently shifts the cellular response from a protective state to a highly pathogenic one. When viruses disrupt orderly clearance and apoptotic mechanisms, infected macrophages can trigger severe, uncontrolled systemic inflammation. For instance, SARS-CoV-2 infection can provoke massive macrophage-mediated cytokine storms, which are the primary drivers of severe lung damage, septic shock, and acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [220,225]. Furthermore, viruses such as PRRSV not only cause a precipitous decline in the bactericidal activity of alveolar macrophages but also pathologically synergize with bacterial endotoxins. This dangerous synergy markedly amplifies the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, converting a localized infection into a systemic cytokine release that massively exacerbates inflammatory tissue damage [200,201,214–218,220,225].

Prospect

Future directions in restoring macrophage antiviral function

To successfully reverse the viral subversion of macrophage antiviral functions, therapeutic strategies must transition from merely targeting the virus to actively restoring host immune competence. Moving forward, research should prioritize several critical frontiers.

While the broad strokes of viral evasion are known, the precise molecular interactome between viral effectors and host machineries remains incompletely mapped. Leveraging artificial intelligence-specifically deep learning algorithms tailored for predicting complex virus-host protein interactions-will be instrumental in uncovering novel therapeutic targets. Concurrently, integrating single-cell technologies and spatial transcriptomics will allow researchers to accurately map the profound heterogeneity of macrophage responses within the complex, three-dimensional architecture of infected tissues.

Gene-editing technologies, notably CRISPR-Cas9, offer unprecedented potential to fundamentally rewire macrophage defense mechanisms. By precisely modifying genes encoding pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs) or metabolic checkpoints, researchers could theoretically engineer macrophages with a heightened intrinsic resistance to viral reprogramming and a fortified microbicidal capacity.

To maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing systemic toxicity, the development of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems is paramount. These platforms can specifically target infected macrophages, delivering antivirals or immunomodulators directly to the hijacked intracellular niches. Furthermore, pairing traditional direct-acting antivirals with host-directed immunomodulators-potentially tailored to the specific multi-omic profile of the patient-represents a highly promising synergistic approach to simultaneously neutralize the virus and restore phagocytic and cytotoxic functions.

Recent preclinical advances have demonstrated significant promise in translating macrophage-targeted strategies from bench to bedside/farm. Nanoparticle-mediated delivery systems, including liposome-encapsulated siRNA and mRNA-loaded polymeric nanoparticles, have achieved selective macrophage targeting through surface modification with mannose, folate, or scavenger receptor ligands. In murine models of influenza and SARS-CoV-2 infection, mannose-conjugated liposomes delivering RIG-I agonists or STING activators effectively restored macrophage antiviral competence while reducing systemic cytokine storms. CRISPR-Cas9-mediated gene editing of macrophages, delivered via lentiviral vectors or ribonucleoprotein complexes encapsulated in targeted nanoparticles, has shown proof-of-concept in animal models. For instance, CRISPR-edited macrophages with knockout of viral entry receptors (e.g. CD163 for PRRSV) or metabolic checkpoints (e.g. HIF-1A) exhibited enhanced resistance to viral infection and restored bactericidal function. Chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-M), engineered to express receptors targeting viral envelope proteins, represent an emerging frontier, with preclinical studies demonstrating enhanced viral clearance in HBV and HIV animal models. These advances collectively underscore the translational potential of precision macrophage engineering, though challenges remain in scaling manufacturing, ensuring long-term safety, and navigating regulatory frameworks for cell-based therapies.

Challenges in therapeutic translation

Despite these promising therapeutic avenues, developing targeted interventions at the highly dynamic virus-macrophage interface is fraught with formidable biological hurdles.

Viruses rarely rely on a single evasion tactic; they deploy a redundant arsenal involving antigenic variation, metabolic hijacking, and structural camouflage. Consequently, neutralizing a single evasion pathway is often therapeutically insufficient. Furthermore, the rapid evolutionary dynamics of viruses constantly threaten the emergence of drug-resistant variants, which can swiftly render highly targeted therapies obsolete and exacerbate disease severity.

Re-arming the macrophage killing machinery carries the inherent risk of off-target tissue damage. For instance, forcefully reversing immunosuppression via TLR agonists can inadvertently trigger a hyper-inflammatory cascade, culminating in systemic cytokine storms. Similarly, genetic interventions risk disrupting delicate cellular homeostatic networks, highlighting the razor-thin therapeutic index between effective viral clearance and host-detrimental immunopathology.

Macrophage biology is profoundly context-dependent. Therapeutic interventions optimized for circulating monocyte-derived macrophages may completely fail when applied to highly specialized, tissue-resident populations (such as alveolar macrophages or Kupffer cells). This extreme functional and phenotypic heterogeneity dictates that different viruses will exploit different macrophage subsets via entirely distinct regulatory mechanisms, complicating the development of universally effective immunomodulators.

Targeting viral metabolic hijacking presents unique therapeutic challenges that warrant careful consideration. A fundamental obstacle lies in the extensive overlap between viral and host metabolic pathways. Viruses exploit core cellular machinery-such as glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and lipid synthesis-that is equally essential for uninfected host cells and bystander immune populations. Consequently, broad metabolic inhibitors risk causing severe off-target toxicity. For example, 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG), a glycolysis inhibitor with demonstrated antiviral efficacy in preclinical models, disrupts glucose metabolism not only in infected macrophages but also in neurons, cardiomyocytes, and activated T cells, raising significant safety concerns. Similarly, statins and fatty acid oxidation inhibitors, while capable of disrupting viral replication compartments, can induce hepatotoxicity and myopathy. The metabolic flexibility of both viruses and macrophages further complicates therapeutic design: inhibition of one pathway often triggers compensatory upregulation of alternative routes. Moreover, tissue-resident macrophage subsets exhibit distinct metabolic profiles-alveolar macrophages rely heavily on fatty acid oxidation, while Kupffer cells prioritize glycolysis-complicating the development of universally applicable metabolic interventions. These considerations necessitate the development of highly selective delivery systems, such as nanoparticle-encapsulated metabolic modifiers with infection-responsive release mechanisms, to achieve therapeutic efficacy while preserving systemic metabolic homeostasis.

Conclusion

Viruses dictate the trajectory of pathogenesis by systematically crippling macrophage functions – from acute disruption of endolysosomal and metabolic clearance to long-term epigenetic and progenitor maladaptation. These evasion strategies not only secure viral replication but frequently predispose hosts to severe secondary bacterial coinfections. As emerging evidence on tissue heterogeneity and microbiota crosstalk adds systemic complexity, future therapeutics must pivot from traditional antivirals to host-directed strategies. Ultimately, re-arming the macrophage’s innate capacity to sense and destroy pathogens remains a critical frontier in infectious disease treatment.
