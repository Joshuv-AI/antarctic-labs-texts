Identification anatomical structures in the interforaminal region as part of pre-surgery evaluation
Authors: Mehmet Akyuz, Guldane Magat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this research is to meticulously explore the three-dimensional positioning, form, length, diameter, and interrelations with adjacent anatomical landmarks within the mandibular interforaminal area, while also discerning the influence of factors such as age, gender, and dental status on these anatomical features.MethodsIn this retrospective analysis, we assessed 400 cone-beam computed tomography images focusing on the mandibular lingual canal (MLC), labial foramen (LaF), and median perforating canal (MPC) and their associated features. The data was then statistically correlated with age, dental status, and gender using IBM SPSS v.23. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe study revealed that MLC was present in 98.8% of cases, most commonly with an oblique course (64.1%) and type G canal. MPC was noted in 33.3% and LaF in 26.3% of cases. Age influenced the distance of MLC from labial and lingual terminals to the alveolar crest, and LaF’s distance to tooth apices and the mandibular margin (p < 0,05). Dental condition affected the labial terminal diameter of MLC and its distances from terminals to the alveolar crest (p < 0,05). As teeth count reduced, distances from the terminals to the alveolar crest decreased. Gender impacted the labial terminal diameter of MLC, its distances to the alveolar crest, mandibular margin, and buccal cortical bone, and the distance from LaF to the alveolar crest. Other morphometric aspects showed no significant association with dental condition, age, or gender.ConclusionThe identification of MLC, MPC, and LaF canals in the mandible underscores their importance in implant surgery. It’s vital to consider the bone thickness and neurovascular structure locations. Hence, the interforaminal region presents potential risks in surgical procedures due to these specific structures.Keywords: Interforaminal region, Cone-beam computed tomography, Labial foramen, Median lingual canal, Median perforating canal

Introduction

Surgical procedures in the mandibular interforaminal region are generally considered safe, but preoperative preparations and anatomical knowledge are essential. Operations in the anterior mandible area can lead to bleeding, wound stabilization issues, and loss of sensitivity. A study spanning 1986–2010 found that among 19 individuals with dental implants, there were cases of life-threatening bleeding. All these bleedings occurred in the interforaminal area and required additional surgical interventions like vascular ligation and hematoma drainage [1].

Close to the mandibular lingual cortical bone, there’s a plexus formed by the rich anastomosing lingual artery, a branch of which is the sublingual artery. Additionally, the submental artery, a branch of the facial artery, can be easily damaged in the mouth’s base [2]. Branches of these arteries from the anastomosis also penetrate the mandible through lingual foramina. As defined in most previous studies, lingual foramina often have anastomosing branches of the sublingual and/or submental arteries in the midline and bilateral premolar regions [3]. During surgical procedures, if there’s a perforation of the mandibular anterior lingual periosteum, a large hematoma can develop. This blood can accumulate in the submandibular space’s supramylohyoid section, pushing the tongue backward and upward, potentially obstructing the upper airway [4].

Anatomical structures like the Median perforating canal (MPC), Median lingual canal (MLC), and Labial foramen (LaF) are found in the midline of the interforaminal region. The MPC connects the MLC and LaF [5]. While two-dimensional radiographs might not clearly display these structures, three-dimensional imaging techniques such as Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) can visualize the detailed anatomy of the jaws in high resolution. Importantly, CBCT presents a lower radiation dose compared to medical CT, making it a preferable option for detailed tomographic evaluation in dental studies [3].

Identifying the detailed anatomical features of this region before surgeries involving it is vital to prevent possible complications. Studies have shown significant anatomical variations in this region. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the localization, morphology, length, diameter, and other morphometric features of anatomical formations like MLC, MPC, and LaF in the CBCT images of the mandibular anterior region. Moreover, the influence of age, gender, and dental status on these anatomical formations will be determined.

Material and method

Study design

For the study, ethical committee approval was obtained from Necmettin Erbakan University Faculty of Dentistry Non-Drug and Medical Device Research Ethics Committee, dated 30.06.2022, with the permission number 2022/158.

Based on the power analysis for a two-sided independent samples t-test, with 95% confidence (1-α), 95% test power (1-β), and an effect size of d = 0.429, it was determined that a minimum of 238 observations should be taken, with 119 in each group [6].

Images from patients who are older than 16 years, with optimal CBCT scans in a 100 × 100 FOV, who do not have impacted teeth, fractures, lesions, cysts, or tumors in the mandibular anterior region, who don’t have metabolic or systemic diseases, and who have no surgical history in this area were included in the study.

Our study was conducted by retrospectively examining the CBCT images of 201 female and 199 male patients, totaling 400 patients, taken for various diagnostic reasons at the Necmettin Erbakan University Department of Oral, Dental, and Maxillofacial Radiology between 2020 and 2022.

In our study, we used the Morita 3D Accuitomo 170 (J Morita Mfg. Corp., Kyoto, Japan) brand CBCT device. Patient positioning was done according to the references specified by the manufacturer. Imaging parameters of 90 kVp, 5 mA, and 17.5 s were used. Images with a 100 × 100 millimeters (mm) FOV (field of view) size, voxel size of 160 micrometers (µm), and slice thickness of 0.25 mm were examined.

Measurements

All evaluations were performed by a single radiologist with 3 years of experience (M.A.) using i-Dixel software (J Morita Mfg. Corp., Kyoto, Japan) on a 3.7 MP, 68 cm, 2560 × 1440 resolution, 27-inch color Ultra Sharp LED TFT display (Dell, DellInc. Round Rock, TX, USA). The examinations were performed on axial, sagittal, coronal and cross-sectional images obtained with CBCT. Gender, age and mandibular anterior dental status were recorded for 400 patients whose CBCT images were analyzed. The individuals included in the evaluation were between 16 and 73 years of age. The individuals included in the study were divided into 5 groups as 16–30, 31–40, 41–50, 51–60, 41–50, 51–60 and 61 and over, paying attention to the homogeneous distribution of age groups. The dental status of the individuals was analyzed in 3 categories: with teeth, partial teeth and edentulous. The dental status of the individuals was analyzed in three categories: with teeth, partially dentate, and edentulous. Individuals with teeth are those who have preserved all their natural teeth, excluding the third molars (wisdom teeth). Partially dentate individuals refer to those who have one or two natural teeth in the anterior region between the canines. Completely edentulous individuals are those missing all canines and incisors, meaning they have lost all teeth in the anterior region.

MLCs were evaluated in sagittal sections according to the location of the genial tubercle: above (s-MLF), in line with (m-MLF) and below (i-MLF) the tubercle (Fig. 1A) [7]. In the presence of more than one canal, the entire location of the foramen was recorded. According to the intraosseous course of the MLCs, they were classified as vertical, horizontal and oblique (Fig. 1C, D, and E) [8, 9]. Types of MLCs: The classification described by Abesi et al. was used as a basis, but an additional category was added for individuals with more than 3 foramen (Fig. 2) [10]. All morphometric measurements were calculated in mm. The length of the MLC was measured from the beginning to the end of the canal on the lingual surface (Fig. 3A) [9]. The diameters of the MLC at the lingual terminal and labial terminal points were measured separately. In the presence of more than one canal, the most prominent canal length diameter was selected (Fig. 3B) [11, 12]. The distances between the lingual and labial terminal points of the MLC, the alveolar bone and the mandibular margin were measured (Fig. 4A and B). The distance between the labial terminal point of the MLC and the labial cortical bone and the distances between the canal and the tooth apices in patients with teeth were measured (Fig. 4C and D) [9].

The presence of LaF, its diameter, and its distance to adjacent anatomical structures was examined in the sagittal section. The diameters of the detected canals were divided into 2 categories as “less than 1 mm” and “greater than 1 mm”. The distance to the inferior mandibular margin (a) and the distance to the superior alveolar crest (b) were measured (Fig. 5).

Measurement of the distance from LaF (arrow) to the inferior mandibular margin (a) and the crest of the superior alveolar ridge (b)

Presence and diameter of the MPC: The MPC is the canal between the MLC and LaF. When examining the MPC, its presence was first determined in the sagittal section and the diameters of the identified canals were measured. The canals were classified into 2 categories as “less than 1 mm” and “greater than 1 mm” according to their diameters (Fig. 1B).

In order to check intra-observer agreement, measurements of 20% of the patients were repeated with at least a three-week interval. For quantitative measurements, the intraobserver agreement between the two measurements was evaluated by intraclass correlation (ICC) analysis, and categorical data were evaluated by kappa agreement analysis. ICC and kappa values for all parameters ranged from 0.86 to 0.94.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS v.23 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for statistical evaluation of the relationships of MLK, MLF, MPK and LaF with surrounding anatomical structures in terms of age, gender and dental status. Compliance with normal distribution was analyzed by Shapiro-Wilk and Kolmogorov tests. Chi-square, Yates correction and Fisher’s Exact tests were used for group comparisons of categorical variables. Independent two sample t-test was used for normally distributed data between two groups and Mann Whitney U test was used for non-normally distributed data. One-way analysis of variance and Duncan and Tamhane’s T2 tests for multiple comparisons were used for normally distributed data in three or more groups; Kruskal-Wallis test and Dunn’s test were used for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%), and quantitative data were expressed as mean, standard deviation, median, minimum and maximum values. Chi-square test was used to compare categorical variables according to age groups and dental status, and Chi-square, Fisher’s Exact test and Yates correction were used according to gender. Kruskal-Wallis and one-way analysis of variance were used to compare quantitative data according to age groups; Kruskal-Wallis, one-way analysis of variance and independent two-sample t-test according to dental status; Mann-Whitney U and independent two-sample t-test according to gender. A confidence level of 95% was assumed for all analyses, and the significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Results

In this study, CBCT images of 400 individuals were examined, with a gender distribution of 50.2% female and 49.8% male. The age distribution was 23.5% for ages 16–30, 60.8% for ages 31–60, and 16.7% for ages 61 and above. 77% of the participants had teeth, 15.2% were edentulous, and 7.8% had partial dentition. MLF was present in 99.8% of the patients, with the most common localizations being “above and below the genial tubercle” (59.1%) and “above the genial tubercle” (17.2%). The most frequent MLC types were G, B, and A. MLC predominantly progressed obliquely. 33.3% of participants had MPC, 26.3% had LaF, and for both canals, the majority had a diameter less than 1 mm. Discussion

Over the past decade, implant-supported treatments have become popular in modern dentistry, restoring function and aesthetics for the elderly, trauma victims, and individuals with congenital hypodontia. The mandibular symphysis region is ideal for patients with oral movement restrictions or TMJ disorders, but due to surgical risks, a detailed assessment is required before grafting [13]. Understanding the anatomy of the mandibular lingual blood vessels, particularly the submental and sublingual arteries, is critical to reduce the risk of hemorrhage, which is frequently observed in the interforaminal region [14].

Periapical and panoramic radiographs are cost-effective and accessible but insufficient for observing MLCs [19]. CT is effective in evaluating the mandibular region, but it has disadvantages such as high cost, radiation, and limited resolution [20]. On the other hand, CBCT is commonly used in treatment planning and is ideal for observing jaw anatomy with high resolution at a low radiation dose [15, 16].

In the literature, studies using CBCT typically report a high prevalence of at least one MLF. For instance, a meta-analysis reported that MLF was detected in 90.11% of 4,336 CBCT scans [17]. Other studies have reported prevalence rates ranging from 84.97 to 100% [18–22]. In our study, MLF was detected in 98.8% of patients, which is consistent with these findings. Variations in detection rates may result from differences in imaging techniques and equipment used [23]. The localization and number of MLFs vary among populations. Our results indicate the presence of MLFs on both sides of the genial tubercle, consistent with studies reporting multiple foramina [24–26]. Differences in findings may stem from sample sizes, imaging methods, and ethnic variations.

Regarding MLC types, in our study, type G was the most commonly detected, followed by type B, which differs from the findings of Abesi et al. [10], who reported type B as the most common. These differences may be due to population differences or sample characteristics. The number of canals we observed ranged from one to four, with two canals being the most common (48.6%). Some studies in the literature report that a single median canal is most common [18, 27], while others report the presence of multiple canals [3, 21, 28, 29]. It is noted that the location of the canal is more critical than the number in terms of surgical complications [9].

In our study, the most common canal trajectory was oblique at 64.1%, followed by horizontal and vertical paths. This finding aligns with some studies but contrasts with others that report the inferior direction as most common [18, 26, 30]. A study in the Turkish population reported that the most common trajectory was in the inferior direction at 48.5% [26]. This variability highlights the importance of individualized anatomical assessments during surgical planning.

Our measurements of MLC diameters and lengths were within ranges reported in other studies but showed some variations, possibly due to ethnic differences and imaging parameters [12, 18, 21, 22, 31–33]. The average lengths we observed were slightly longer than in previous research, which may be attributed to differences in sample size, methodology, and demographics. Distances from MLCs to anatomical landmarks like the superior alveolar crest and inferior mandibular margin are critical for implant planning [12, 22, 34]. In our study, these distances tended to decrease with age and tooth loss and were generally longer in males, consistent with the literature [26, 34, 35]. This underscores the importance of considering individual anatomical variations during surgical planning.

No significant difference was found in MLC diameters according to age groups (p > 0.05), consistent with Afandiyev [26] and Alerico et al. [35]. A significant difference existed in the diameter measured from the labial terminal according to dental status (p < 0.01), being greater in patients with partial dentition. Some studies did not find a significant gender difference in diameter [9, 36], while another did [11]. In our study, a significant difference was found between genders in the labial terminal diameter (p < 0.05). Regarding canal length, no significant difference was found between age groups, gender, and dental status (p > 0.05), consistent with some studies [30] but not others [28].

In implant surgery, the distance between the MLC and the superior alveolar crest is critical for implant size determination. Ideally, this distance should be 12 mm–10 mm for the implant and 2 mm safety margin [34]. Resorption of the alveolar crest in edentulous patients can reduce this distance. We found that this distance decreases with age and is longer in males, consistent with the literature [12, 26, 35]. Distances from MLC terminal points to the inferior mandibular margin were 13.91 mm and 10.02 mm, respectively. No significant difference was found in this distance according to age groups or dental status, but it was significantly larger in males (p < 0.05), consistent with Yildirim et al. [12]. The distance from the labial terminal point of the MLC to the labial cortical bone was larger in males (p < 0.05), with no significant difference based on age or dental status. This aligns with some studies [26, 35] but not others [37].

Statistical analyses revealed no significant relationships between canal presence or localization and age, dental status, or gender (p > 0.05), aligning with some previous studies [24, 26, 38, 39]. However, the presence of LaFs decreased with age and was more frequent in males (p < 0.05); this finding is not widely reported in the literature. While most canals were smaller than 1 mm, larger canals were more commonly found in males.

We detected MPCs in 33.3% of CBCT scans; this rate is higher than the 14.3–27.8% reported in some studies [40–45]. This difference may result from methodological variations or sample differences and emphasizes the need for detailed CBCT evaluations before surgical interventions. Most MPCs and LaFs had diameters less than 1 mm, suggesting that surgical risks may be low [11, 29, 31]. However, larger vessels can increase the risk of complications, including tumor invasion [46].

The studies reviewed exhibit several limitations that may impact the validity and generalizability of their findings. Firstly, many studies suffer from small sample sizes, which can limit the statistical power and may not adequately represent the broader population, as noted by Mehdizadeh et al. [47]. Additionally, geographical and cultural differences can influence results, making it challenging to apply findings universally, particularly in studies like those conducted by Hosseini et al. [48]. Furthermore, the methodologies employed, such as varying imaging techniques and processing algorithms, can lead to discrepancies in measurements, as highlighted by Kang et al. [49]. The reliance on cross-sectional designs in several studies restricts the ability to establish causal relationships or observe changes over time, which is a significant drawback in longitudinal assessments. Moreover, the lack of long-term follow-up data in many studies limits the understanding of the durability and effectiveness of treatments. Lastly, there is often insufficient focus on patient perspectives and experiences, which are crucial for comprehensive evaluations of treatment outcomes. However, the reference by Pi et al. [50] does not directly support the claim regarding patient perspectives and experiences, as it focuses on anatomical detection rather than treatment evaluations. Therefore, this citation has been removed. Addressing these limitations in future research is essential for enhancing the reliability and applicability of findings in clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study has illuminated the rich variations of the MLC and its nature that can manifest in different locations. It has also pointed out that the presence of MPC and LaF has a significant prevalence depending on variables such as age, gender, and dental status. Structures discovered in the mandible like the MLC, MPC, and LaF indicate that the interforaminal region is susceptible to potential complications, especially considering canal diameters over 1 mm and the ideal bone thickness for implant surgery. Therefore, detailed CBCT examinations before surgical interventions and careful identification of these anatomical structures are of vital importance for a successful and safe operation.
