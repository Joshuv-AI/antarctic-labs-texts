Perovskite/Copper Indium Gallium Selenide Tandem Solar Cells
Authors: Xin Chen, Lihua Hu, Chao Li, Chenglin Wang, Zuolin Zhang, Jian Shi, Hongjun Cui, Cong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Perovskite/Cu­(In, Ga)­Se2 (CIGS) tandems have emerged as a promising thin-film solution to combine high efficiency with lightweight, flexible, and scalable photovoltaics. This review summarizes advancements in wide-bandgap perovskite design, conformal growth on rough CIGS surfaces, and the challenges of contact formation. We examine the trade-offs between monolithic 2T and mechanically stacked 4T architectures, emphasizing industrial feasibility. Unlike prior reviews, we introduce a “performance–stability–cost” trimetric framework to evaluate technical routes, focusing on critical bottlenecks such as conformal deposition on textured CIGS, indium supply risks, and the mechanical reliability of flexible tandems. Recent progress in interface planarization, passivation, and stability optimization has led to efficiencies surpassing 27%, with considerable potential for flexible commercial applications. The review also explores the integration of these innovations for large-scale manufacturing, offering actionable insights into overcoming current limitations and advancing perovskite/CIGS tandems toward industrial readiness.

Introduction

In recent years, perovskite materials have become key research materials in the field of solar cells due to their excellent optoelectronic properties, tunable bandgaps, and low-cost manufacturing solutions. − Since their inception, perovskite solar cells (PSCs) have rapidly improved in efficiency, surpassing a certified conversion efficiency of 27%. − The tunable bandgap of perovskite materials allows them to combine with other photovoltaic materials, − such as silicon (Si) and copper indium gallium selenide (Cu­(In, Ga)­Se2, CIGS), to form ideal tandem structures. In these tandem solar cells (TSCs), perovskite materials not only provide efficient energy conversion but also lower overall production costs through their low-cost manufacturing processes. , Perovskite/Si tandem structures benefit from the well-established Si platform and show excellent performance, but face challenges related to manufacturing complexity and costs. ,− CIGS materials, known for their stability, optoelectronic performance, and flexibility, have been widely used in thin-film solar cells. , The inclusion of CIGS adds elasticity to the entire tandem device, enhancing its potential for use in real-world applications. Perovskite/CIGS TSCs, , as the next generation of high-efficiency photovoltaic technology, possess enormous market potential and application prospects due to their exceptional performance, lower production costs, and good stability. ,− With continuous technological advancements and progress in commercialization, perovskite/CIGS TSCs are expected to play an important role in the global energy transition and contribute significantly to sustainable development.

This article explores recent advances in perovskite/CIGS TSCs. Despite promising results, the long-term stability and environmental durability of perovskite/CIGS TSCs remain key challenges. ,,,− Perovskite materials are inherently sensitive to moisture, temperature fluctuations, and prolonged exposure to light, all of which contribute to degradation. Experimental results show that the combination of perovskite and CIGS achieves an ideal bandgap match between the two subcells, thereby enhancing PCE. Specifically, by optimizing the perovskite bandgap to 1.6–1.7 eV and maintaining the CIGS bandgap at 1.1 eV, researchers have successfully increased the efficiency of the 2T (two-terminal) configuration to over 27%, while the 4T (four-terminal) configuration has achieved efficiencies exceeding 30%. The tandem structure not only significantly improves the spectral utilization of the solar cell but also fully exploits the stability advantages of CIGS, enhancing the durability of perovskite materials in practical applications. By analyzing the optoelectronic performance and current matching issues in different configurations (such as 2T and 4T configurations), as well as the optimization of materials and production processes, this review will further explore the future development direction and commercialization potential of perovskite/CIGS TSCs.

Numerous reviews have summarized the research progress of perovskite/CIGS TSCs, but most focus on a single dimension: some emphasize fundamental material mechanisms (e.g., bandgap tuning of perovskite/CIGS) but lack discussion on industrialization feasibility; others compare 2T/4T device performance but ignore cost–stability trade-offs; a few highlight laboratory efficiency breakthroughs but rarely link them to practical application scenarios. In contrast, this review takes industrialization-oriented integration as the core thread, systematically integrating material modification, interface engineering, process scalability, and techno-economic analysis into a closed-loop framework. We prioritize key bottlenecks for commercialization (e.g., conformal deposition on rough commercial CIGS substrates, indium scarcity in CIGS) that are rarely addressed in previous reviews, and establish a “performance–stability–cost” tridimensional evaluation system for technical routes, providing actionable guidance for industrial technology selection.

Perovskite and CIGS Material Synergy

Advantages of Perovskite

PSCs have made significant strides in photovoltaic technology in recent years, thanks to their exceptional optoelectronic properties and tunable bandgap, making them a focal point of global research. ,,− Perovskite materials (Figure a), particularly metal halide perovskites, have been widely used in solar cells due to their excellent light absorption capabilities, high PCE, low-cost manufacturing process, and good bandgap tunability. Perovskite materials exhibit strong light absorption, which enables efficient energy conversion, even in thin-film form. Compared with traditional Si-based solar cells, perovskite materials have significantly better light absorption coefficients. This means that PSCs can capture more photons even with a thinner active layer, leading to an increase in PCE. Additionally, one of the most notable features of perovskites is their tunable bandgap. By adjusting the composition of the perovskite material (e.g., by changing the ions at the A, B, or X positions), their bandgap can be precisely controlled to meet different spectral requirements, as shown in Figure b. This tunable bandgap allows perovskites to optimize the absorption of high-energy photons under different lighting conditions, effectively enhancing the solar cell efficiency. PSCs are relatively low-cost to manufacture. Perovskite materials are typically processed using solution-based methods, which not only lower production costs but also improve scalability for large-scale production. This low-cost manufacturing process gives PSCs significant economic potential for the solar energy industry, particularly in developing countries or regions with limited resources. Over the years, the efficiency of single-junction PSCs has risen from an initial 3% to over 27%. This rapid improvement is primarily attributed to the outstanding performance of perovskite materials in light absorption and charge transport, as well as ongoing advancements in interface engineering and structural optimization.

Advantages of CIGS

CIGS solar cells, as a mature thin-film solar cell technology, have occupied an important position in the photovoltaic industry. The main advantages of CIGS solar cells are their high stability, excellent optoelectronic performance, and adaptability to flexible substrates, which, when combined with perovskite materials in TSCs, can further enhance the overall performance and durability. The CIGS crystal structure is shown in Figure c. The bandgap of CIGS material is tunable in a wide range of 1.0–1.7 eV, with a typical value of 1.1 eV for commercial CIGS films (Ga/(Ga+In) ≈ 0.3). The bandgap can be adjusted by modulating the Ga/(Ga+In) ratio: increasing the Ga content will enhance the lattice distortion and the valence band maximum splitting, thereby raising the bandgap from ∼1.0 eV (pure CuInSe2) to ∼1.7 eV (pure CuGaSe2). In addition, partial substitution of Se with S can further fine-tune the bandgap to meet the requirements for matching with perovskite subcells. This tunable characteristic enables CIGS to efficiently absorb low-energy photons (red and near-infrared light) in the optimized bandgap range and match with perovskite for spectral complementary utilization. This characteristic of the CIGS bandgap allows it to pair well with wide-bandgap (WBG) perovskite materials when forming TSCs, resulting in improved spectral matching and a higher overall efficiency.

Additionally, CIGS materials have a greater absorption depth, typically achieving high light absorption efficiency even with a thinner active layer, thus reducing the consumption of raw materials in the active layer. However, compared with crystalline silicon solar cells, CIGS still has higher large-scale production costs, which are mainly caused by the high investment in vacuum deposition equipment and the cost of rare metal raw materials such as indium and gallium. Another significant advantage of CIGS solar cells is their adaptability to flexible substrates. As CIGS solar cells are based on thin-film technology, their light-absorbing layer can be deposited on flexible substrates, enabling the production of lightweight, bendable solar cells. This characteristic gives CIGS solar cells tremendous potential in applications such as building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV), wearable devices, and aerospace. ,

Despite these advantages, CIGS materials face two critical challenges that limit their large-scale commercialization: (1) Indium is a rare metal with a crustal abundance of only ∼0.1 ppm, and its global production is highly concentrated in China, Peru, and Canada, leading to volatile market prices and supply chain instability. The CIGS absorber typically contains 10–15 at.% indium, and the high indium content significantly increases the raw material cost of CIGS modules. (2) Traditional CIGS thin-film deposition relies on high-cost vacuum coevaporation or sputtering equipment, which requires high investment and operating costs. In addition, the low utilization rate of raw materials in vacuum processes further increases the manufacturing costs of CIGS modules. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed feasible solutions; that is, reducing indium content by increasing the Ga/(Ga+In) ratio, developing indium-free alternatives (e.g., Cu2ZnSn­(S, Se)4, CZTSSe), and adopting nonvacuum solution-based deposition processes (e.g., inkjet printing, blade coating) to reduce equipment investment and improve material utilization.

Synergy of Perovskite and CIGS in Tandem Solar Cells

The combination of PSCs and CIGS solar cells forms high-efficiency, stable, and widely applicable TSCs. This tandem structure fully exploits the complementary advantages of both perovskite and CIGS materials, improving the PCE of solar cells and addressing the stability and performance limitations faced by single-junction solar cells. First, bandgap matching is the core of the complementarity between perovskite and CIGS TSCs. Perovskite materials have a wide bandgap (around 1.6 eV), enabling them to efficiently absorb high-energy photons from the solar spectrum (such as blue and green light), while CIGS materials have a narrower bandgap (around 1.1 eV), making them more suitable for absorbing low-energy photons (such as red and near-infrared light). This difference in bandgaps allows perovskite/CIGS TSCs to cover a broader portion of the solar spectrum, maximizing photon absorption and conversion, thus significantly improving solar cell efficiency, as shown in Figure d. By adjusting the Ga/(Ga+In) ratio of CIGS to tune its bandgap in the range of 1.0–1.7 eV, and matching it with the perovskite bandgap regulated by halide composition (1.2–2.3 eV), the tandem cell can achieve both optimal spectral utilization and current matching, which is the theoretical basis for efficiency breakthroughs of perovskite/CIGS tandems.

The manufacturing compatibility between perovskite and CIGS is another important aspect of their synergy. The solution-based processing method for PSCs not only reduces manufacturing costs but also facilitates the fabrication of thin-film solar cells on flexible substrates. CIGS thin-film technology has been widely used, and its high-efficiency production processes have matured, enabling the large-scale production of solar cells at lower costs. The combination of perovskite and CIGS allows for the enhancement of overall efficiency through the low-cost perovskite layer while leveraging the stability and scalability of the CIGS layer for large-scale production. As a result, perovskite/CIGS TSCs offer significant economic advantages during commercialization.

Thus, combining a perovskite and CIGS in TSCs greatly enhances the overall performance of solar cells through their complementary bandgaps, stability, and manufacturing processes. Perovskite provides high-efficiency light conversion and flexible bandgap tuning, while CIGS compensates for the stability issues of perovskite materials. The synergy of these two materials not only improves efficiency but also enhances the long-term reliability of the cells. This cooperation makes perovskite/CIGS TSCs a promising technology for achieving efficient, low-cost, and stable photovoltaic systems.

Tandem Structure and Device Configuration

Comparison of 2T and 4T Configurations

In perovskite/CIGS TSCs, the 2T and 4T architectures are the predominant designs, each with distinct trade-offs in efficiency and stability, as summarized in Figure a. The 2T configuration connects the perovskite top cell and CIGS bottom cell in series via a tunnel recombination junction, so both subcells share the same current path. This compact series stack is structurally simple and highly compatible with high-throughput manufacturing, which translates into reduced process complexity, lower cost, and favorable prospects for large-scale production. , By careful tunnel-junction engineering, 2T perovskite/CIGS tandems can deliver high power conversion efficiency at comparatively low cost, with a simple layer stack that is inherently scalable for large area, high-throughput manufacturing, and attractive for cost-sensitive applications. The main constraint, however, is strict current matching between the perovskite top cell and CIGS bottom cell, since both subcells operate in series. Their distinct optoelectronic properties and spectral responses make it difficult to simultaneously maximize photocurrent in both junctions, which can cap the attainable tandem efficiency. Consequently, substantial effort has focused on bandgap and thickness optimization, as well as spectral management, to maintain robust current matching under realistic illumination conditions.

In the 4T architecture, the perovskite top cell and CIGS bottom cell are optically coupled but electrically independent, each connected to its own electrode and operated at its own maximum power point. Decoupling the two junctions eliminates the strict current-matching constraint of 2T tandems and greatly relaxes device design, allowing independent optimization of bandgap, thickness, and interfaces in each subcell. As a result, both subcells can operate near their individual optima, which is particularly beneficial under realistic outdoor conditions with varying spectra and irradiance, and enables a more stable energy yield over time. , Furthermore, the flexibility of the 4T configuration allows for more precise adjustments to the spectral response and efficiency of each subcell during the design phase, facilitating more detailed optimization. Despite these benefits, 4T tandems suffer from higher structural and process complexity. The use of additional electrodes and interconnection layers raises materials and fabrication costs, and the more intricate device layout complicates module integration and scaling to large-area manufacturing. , Additionally, the connection and layout of four electrodes increase the complexity of the device, which can pose challenges in large-scale manufacturing.

Overall, 2T and 4T perovskite/CIGS tandems present complementary trade-offs. The 2T architecture, with its simple series-connected stack and low material usage, is intrinsically attractive for cost-sensitive, large-scale manufacturing, but its strict current-matching requirement constrains headroom for further efficiency gains. By contrast, 4T tandems lift this constraint and allow each subcell to operate at its own maximum power point, enabling higher efficiency and more robust long-term energy yield, albeit at the expense of added optical/electrical complexity and higher fabrication costs.

Current-Matching Issues in Different Configurations

Based on the bandgap complementarity mechanism, bandgap tuning serves as a core strategy for current-matching optimization in different tandem configurations. In perovskite/CIGS tandems, current matching between the perovskite top cell and the CIGS bottom cell is a central constraint on device design and efficiency. Unlike the previous simplification of fixed bandgaps, the dual tunability of perovskite (1.2–2.3 eV via halide composition regulation) and CIGS (1.0–1.7 eV via Ga/(Ga+In) ratio and S doping) provides a more flexible solution for current-matching optimization. For the 2T tandem configuration, adjusting the Ga content in CIGS to moderately increase its bandgap (e.g., from 1.1 to 1.2 eV) can better match the WBG perovskite (1.6–1.7 eV), reducing the spectral overlap loss and improving the consistency of photocurrent between the two subcells. In 2T architectures, both junctions are series-connected through a tunnel recombination layer, so the lower photocurrent subcell limits the tandem output. This requirement is particularly stringent because the two absorbers have distinct bandgaps and complementary spectral responses: perovskites harvest mainly the blue–green region, whereas CIGS is more efficient in the red and near-infrared. By contrast, 4T tandems decouple the junctions electrically, allowing each subcell to operate at its own maximum power point and largely removing the current-matching penalty. Based on the bandgap complementarity mechanism, bandgap tuning serves as a core strategy for current-matching optimization in different tandem configurations. For the 2T architecture with strict series current constraints, fine-tuning the bandgap of subcells can reduce the photocurrent difference between the perovskite top cell and the CIGS bottom cell. Additionally, since the performance of the cells can vary under different lighting conditions, designers need to optimize the bandgap combination dynamically to ensure effective current matching and optimal efficiency under various conditions.

In 4T perovskite/CIGS tandems, the two subcells are electrically decoupled, and each operates at its own maximum power point, so current mismatch no longer constrains the overall efficiency. This independence allows separate optimization of bandgap, thickness, and spectral response in each junction, and can be further enhanced by spectral-splitting optics (e.g., dichroic mirrors) that direct different wavelength bands to the appropriate subcell, thereby maximizing photocurrent extraction under realistic illumination (Figure b).

Optimizing current matching directly impacts the PCE of the TSCs. Under standard solar illumination, both 2T and 4T TSCs theoretically achieve maximum efficiencies exceeding 46%. However, in the 2T configuration, the need for current matching places stricter limitations on the top cell bandgap, with the optimal power conversion efficiency typically occurring in the 1.5 to 1.9 eV range. In contrast, the 4T configuration allows each subcell to operate at its maximum power point, offering a wider range of bandgap combinations and thus greater flexibility.

In 2T perovskite/CIGS tandems, bandgap and thickness tuning can partially alleviate current mismatch and yield meaningful efficiency gains, but the series-connection constraint ultimately limits the headroom for further improvement. By contrast, 4T devices decouple the subcells, offering far greater freedom to optimize the optoelectronic properties of each junction and to sustain high efficiency under variable illumination. As device and optical designs mature, 4T architectures, therefore, exhibit a clearer pathway to continued efficiency scaling than their cost-advantaged but current-limited 2T counterparts.

Efficiency and Stability Advancements

Perovskite/CIGS TSCs have made significant advancements in both efficiency and stability in recent years. With continuous improvements in materials, device designs, and manufacturing processes, the PCE and long-term stability of these TSCs have been significantly enhanced. This section explores the latest developments in the efficiency improvements and stability enhancements of perovskite/CIGS TSCs.

Efficiency Improvements

Interface Planarization for Conformal Perovskite Deposition

The PCE of PSCs has surpassed 27%, ,, making them a key technology in the field of solar cells. CIGS solar cells have also continued to improve, with the highest efficiency of single-junction CIGS solar cells reaching 23.64%. The rough surface of commercial CIGS films is a key bottleneck for perovskite top-cell deposition, as it causes film pinholes and shunting paths. Yang et al. proposed a chemical mechanical polishing (CMP) strategy for the ITO intermediate layer to address this issue. They used a commercially available SiO2 slurry to polish the ITO composite layer to make the ITO surface sufficiently smooth (Figure a). The polished thick ITO between perovskite and the rough CIGS alleviated the challenge of preparing WBG perovskite film on rough CIGS. This approach enabled spin coating of the perovskite and thin contact layers without significant losses caused by surface irregularities. The core mechanism of this method is to construct a smooth ITO buffer layer, which eliminates the surface topography of CIGS and provides a uniform substrate for perovskite spin-coating, thereby reducing shunting losses and improving film coverage. To further optimize charge extraction, they also employed heavily doped poly­[bis­(4-phenyl)­(2,4,6-trimethylphenyl)­amine] (PTAA) as the hole transport layer (HTL) to maintain the open-circuit voltage (V OC) while improving the fill factor (FF) and short-circuit current density (J SC). The efficiency of the 2T perovskite/CIGS TSCs with a 0.042 cm2 area achieved 22.43%. The device demonstrated a high V OC of 1.774 V. While the CMP strategy effectively solves the planarization problem, the thick ITO layer introduces two non-negligible limitations. First, the thick ITO enhances free carrier absorption in the near-infrared (NIR) region, which reduces the photon flux reaching the CIGS bottom cell and leads to a low J SC of 17.3 mA/cm2. Second, the CMP process adds extra steps to the manufacturing flow, significantly increasing the production cost and reducing the scalability for large-area module fabrication. This trade-off between efficiency improvement and process cost indicates that the CMP strategy is more suitable for laboratory-scale high-efficiency device fabrication than for industrial mass production. Jošt et al. proposed a more scalable alternative to CMP: conformal deposition of a 10 nm NiO X layer on rough CIGS surfaces via atomic layer deposition (ALD). The core mechanism here is that the ALD process enables atomic-level uniform coverage of the NiO X layer, which not only fills the valleys of the rough CIGS surface but also acts as a hole-selective contact to suppress shunting. To further optimize the interface, the authors constructed a NiO X /PTAA bilayer HTL, which reduces the energy level mismatch between NiO X and perovskite, thereby improving the hole extraction efficiency (Figure b). This strategy yielded a PCE of 21.6% for a 0.78 cm2 device, which is comparable to that of the CMP method but with better scalability. Compared with the CMP, the ALD NiO X strategy eliminates the complex polishing process and is compatible with large-area deposition. However, its PCE is slightly lower than that of the CMP-based device, which is mainly attributed to the higher interface resistance of the NiO X layer. In addition, the ALD process has a lower deposition rate than solution-based methods, which may increase the manufacturing cycle time. Overall, this strategy balances efficiency and scalability, showing greater potential for industrial applications.

Charge Transport Optimization and Interface Passivation

By improving the charge transport layers in PSCs, researchers have successfully increased the charge collection efficiency, further boosting the overall efficiency of perovskite/CIGS TSCs. ,, Albrecht et al. introduced organic small molecule materials based on carbazole bodies with phosphonic acid anchoring groups that can form self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) on various oxides. Besides minimal material consumption and parasitic absorption, the self-assembly process enabled conformal coverage of arbitrarily formed oxide surfaces with simple process control. The conformal coverage aimed to reduce nonradiative losses at the perovskite/HTL interface, allowing for the realization of a monolithic perovskite/CIGS tandem solar cell with an as-deposited, rough CIGS surface and certified efficiency of 23.26% on an active area of 1 cm2. The submicron-scale topographic irregularities were inherent to commercially available CIGS substrates, which induced spatial heterogeneity in the widely adopted SAMs, resulting in nonconformal perovskite deposition due to poor wettability. Peng et al. developed an N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF)-assisted hydrophilic-terminated SAM-reconstructed interface strategy, achieving conformal coverage of SAMs and perovskite. However, the conformal perovskite layer inherited substrate textures with rich defects, impeding homogeneous C60 evaporation and conformal capping. To address this, they combined 2,3,4,5,6-pentafluorobenzylphosphonic acid (F5BPA) and p-trifluoromethylphenyl ethylamine (p-CF3PEA) to spontaneously concentrate on textured perovskite surfaces to construct a uniform interfacial network that recast the perovskite surface and bridged C60 through enhanced interactions. The resulting 2T perovskite/CIGS TSCs exhibited a PCE of 26.14% (independently certified at 25.21%).

Given the challenges of constructing 2T perovskite/CIGS TSCs, mechanically stacking the WBG PSCs and CIGS solar cells to construct the 4T configuration tandem offers an attractive alternative. To construct efficient 4T perovskite/CIGS TSCs, an optical filler layer was introduced by Hou’s group to control and match the optical interference patterns with the AM1.5G spectrum, which efficiently improved the average near-infrared transmittance (Figure c). Then, the organic halide salt methylenediammonium diiodide (MDADI) was applied to further passivate the defects to enhance the V OC by 70 mV, and they demonstrated an efficient perovskite/CuInSe2 4T TSC with a high PCE of 29.9% (Figure d). The integrated J SC was then determined to be 20.9 and 18.9 mA/cm2, respectively, for the semitransparent perovskite front cell and the CuInSe2 rear cells from the EQE spectra (Figure e). Wang et al. demonstrated that doping A-site cations of WBG perovskite by bivalent state methylenediammonium cations (MDA2+) results in stronger interaction with the inorganic Pb–I framework to reduce formation of defects. They achieved a high PCE of 30.13% based on mechanically stacked perovskite/CIGS TSCs (Figure f, g). The fabricated flexible 2T perovskite/CIGS TSCs achieved an efficiency of 23.41% with MDACl2 inclusion (Figure h).

In the 4T configuration, each subcell operates independently, enabling more precise current matching and optimization, which results in a substantial increase in overall efficiency. The efficiency of 4T TSCs has now exceeded 30%. To further improve efficiency, researchers have employed spectral-splitting technologies, using spectral splitters (such as dichroic mirrors) to direct different wavelengths of light to their respective subcells. This reduces the current mismatch and enhances the overall efficiency of the solar cell.

Achieving optimal spectral matching with the CIGS bottom cell requires a WBG perovskite top absorber, typically realized through mixed-halide compositions with a high bromide content. Such compositions, however, are intrinsically prone to halide segregation and compositional inhomogeneity issues that are further exacerbated under scalable deposition conditions. These instabilities not only limit device efficiency but also undermine operational reliability, posing a major obstacle to the translation of laboratory-scale demonstrations into manufacturable flexible tandems. Recent progress has shown that rational control of perovskite crystallization chemistry can effectively address these limitations. By suppression of uncontrolled halide intermediate crystallization during film formation, homogeneous WBG perovskite layers can be achieved even under ambient, scalable coating conditions. Enabled by such advances, flexible monolithic two-terminal perovskite/CIGS TSCs have now reached efficiencies exceeding 27% while maintaining encouraging operational stability, marking an important step toward commercially viable, high-performance flexible tandem photovoltaics.

The above studies show that the efficiency improvement of perovskite/CIGS TSCs relies on the synergistic optimization of interface morphology, charge transport, and spectral matching. Interface planarization strategies (CMP, ALD NiO X ) focus on solving the shunting problem caused by rough CIGS surfaces, while interface passivation strategies (SAM reconstruction, defect passivation) aim to reduce nonradiative recombination. From a practical perspective, the SAM reconstruction strategy achieves the highest PCE (26.14%) by balancing planarization and passivation, but its complex multistep process still needs to be simplified for mass production. Future research should focus on developing low-cost, one-step strategies to simultaneously achieve interface planarization and passivation.

Stability Improvements

While perovskite/CIGS TSCs exhibit excellent efficiency improvements, their long-term stability remains a major challenge for large-scale commercial applications. CIGS has inherent stability, maintaining stable performance under high temperatures and humidity for extended periods. However, perovskite materials are highly sensitive to environmental factors, such as moisture, oxygen, and temperature, which can lead to degradation and performance loss of TSCs over time. Improving the intrinsic stability of WBG perovskites is a fundamental solution to address the core degradation issue of TSCs.

Perovskite Intrinsic Stability Modification

A core bottleneck limiting the stability of WBG perovskites (bandgap >1.6 eV) is light-induced halide phase segregation, which is triggered by the migration of iodide (I–) and bromide (Br–) ions under illumination or thermal stress. This phenomenon leads to the formation of iodide-rich low-bandgap domains and bromide-rich high-bandgap domains within the perovskite film, causing severe nonradiative recombination, V OC loss, and efficiency attenuation. For WBG perovskites with a high Br content, phase segregation can reduce the device efficiency after light soaking.

To address this challenge, researchers have developed three core strategies: (1) Interface passivation by using organic ammonium salts (e.g., p-CF3PEA+) to passivate the surface defects of WBG perovskites can reduce the defect-assisted ion migration path, thereby alleviating phase segregation. (2) 2D/3D heterojunction construction by coating a 2D perovskite layer on the surface of 3D WBG perovskites can form a physical barrier to block halide ion migration while reducing interface recombination. (3) Cation doping engineering with doping organic cations (e.g., MDA2+) or large-radius cations (e.g., Rb+, Cs+) into the perovskite lattice can enhance the binding energy between halide ions and the lattice, suppressing ion migration and phase segregation.

A representative study is the strain-induced Rb doping strategy proposed by Grätzel et al. (Figure a). By introducing Rb+ into the perovskite lattice, the researchers effectively suppressed the phase segregation of Rb–Cs-rich phases, achieved uniform Br/Cl distribution, and utilized lattice strain engineering to inhibit halide migration and nonradiative recombination. This strategy yielded WBG PSCs with a V OC of 1.30 V (93.5% of the radiative limit) and excellent operational stability: the unencapsulated device maintained 80% of its initial PCE after 750 h of continuous MPP tracking under 1-sun illumination at 45 °C in the N2 atmosphere (Figure b). This strategy represents a shift from passive doping to active strain regulation in intrinsic stability modification, which not only improves stability but also reduces photovoltage loss, achieving a balance between efficiency and stability that was difficult to achieve with traditional doping methods.

Interface Regulation and Defect Passivation

Interface recombination and poor contact caused by the rough CIGS surface are key factors limiting the stability of TSCs. The nature of the irregular rough morphology of commercial CIGS prevents tandem device performance. In addition, the minority carriers near the perovskite/C60 interface and the incompletely passivated trap states cause interface recombination. The technical route of interface regulation has evolved from “single-passivation” to “crystal growth modulation + interface field regulation” synergy. Peng et al. proposed a dual-regulation strategy combining d-hexyl lactate hydrochloride (d-HLH) and LiF. On one hand, d-HLH slowed down the crystal growth rate of perovskites and induced vertical orientation, enabling the perovskite film to fully cover the rough CIGS surface and reduce defect density (Figure c). On the other hand, LiF constructed an interface electric field to repel hole carriers away from the perovskite/C60 interface, suppressing nonradiative recombination. This synergistic strategy achieved a 2T tandem cell PCE of 24.6% (0.16 cm2). The unencapsulated device maintained 92.8% of its initial efficiency after 960 h of storage in nitrogen, and 84.4% in ambient air (30%–50% humidity) for the same period (Figure d). Compared with the early single SAM passivation strategy, this dual-regulation method simultaneously solves the problems of poor coverage and interface recombination, representing key progress in interface stability optimization for rough CIGS substrates.

Encapsulation Technology and Environmental Isolation

Environmental isolation via encapsulation is the last line of defense for tandem cell stability, with the technical route evolving from “physical barrier encapsulation” to “physical–chemical synergistic encapsulation”. Traditional encapsulation materials (e.g., glass, metal foil) can only block moisture and oxygen physically but are prone to failure under mechanical deformation (especially for flexible devices). Recent studies have focused on developing functional encapsulation materials with self-healing and chemical adsorption capabilities. For example, Lu et al. developed an encapsulant consisting of alkoxy polyvinylimidazole bis (trifluoromethanesulphonyl)­imide (EP) with damage perception and rapid self-healing properties. This material not only forms a physical barrier to block moisture and oxygen but also chemically adsorbs lead ions to prevent leakage (Figure e). Neutral water-soaking tests showed that damaged devices with this encapsulation maintained stable performance, while unencapsulated devices degraded completely within a short time (Figure f). The development of functional encapsulation materials has addressed the stability bottleneck of flexible TSCs, bridging the gap between laboratory stability and practical application requirements. Additionally, protective layers or encapsulation materials on the surface of the cells have also been explored, effectively preventing moisture and oxygen from reaching the perovskite layer, further improving the durability of the devices. −

Summary of Technical Evolution and Future Directions

The stability enhancement technology of perovskite/CIGS TSCs has undergone three stages of development: (1) focusing on individual aspects such as cation doping or simple interface passivation, with limited stability improvement; (2) combining material modification, interface regulation, and encapsulation technology, achieving significant improvement in both operational and environmental stability; and (3) developing low-cost, large-area compatible strategies (e.g., roll-to-roll compatible passivation and encapsulation processes).

Future research directions should focus on two core aspects: first, developing multistrategy synergistic optimization schemes to achieve intrinsic stability modification, interface passivation, and encapsulation integration; second, exploring stability enhancement strategies compatible with large-scale manufacturing, avoiding complex processes that increase production costs. In addition, establishing standardized stability testing protocols (e.g., unified testing conditions for flexible devices) is crucial for comparing and evaluating different strategies fairly.

Flexible and Lightweight Perovskite/CIGS TSCs

Flexible perovskite/CIGS TSCs combine the strengths of two highly complementary thin-film photovoltaic technologies, offering a compelling pathway toward lightweight, high-efficiency, and versatile energy-harvesting systems. Their most notable advantage is the excellent bandgap synergy. WBG perovskites efficiently harvest high-energy photons, while CIGS absorbs the near-infrared region with high stability. This complementary absorption enables higher PCE than either subcell alone, often surpassing 20%–24% in flexible formats and exceeding the performance of most other flexible PV technologies.

A defining advantage is their exceptional power-to-weight ratio, enabled by solution-processed perovskites and thin-film CIGS grown on lightweight polymer substrates. Such devices can weigh only a few grams per square meter while maintaining high electrical output, making them ideal for applications where weight is criticalsuch as aerospace, portable electronics, unmanned aerial platforms, and wearable devices. Their thin and bendable architecture supports integration onto curved or irregular surfaces, greatly expanding the design freedom for next-generation energy systems.

Mechanical Stability

Flexible perovskite/CIGS TSCs are widely used in scenarios requiring repeated mechanical deformation (e.g., wearable devices, curved BIPV), so uniform mechanical stability evaluation standards are crucial for performance comparison. However, existing studies adopt inconsistent test parameters, leading to difficulties in directly comparing stability levels. Based on international standards (e.g., ISOS-L-2 mechanical stability protocol) and industry research consensus, this section clarifies universal evaluation criteria and analyzes performance degradation laws under different test conditions.

Mechanical stability assessment of flexible perovskite/CIGS TSCs should explicitly account for both bending severity and accumulated mechanical fatigue. In practice, a complete and comparable bending test requires reporting the bending radius R, the number of bending cycles N, the bending mode (e.g., single- or double-sided), and the environmental conditions during testing. Omitting any of these parameters makes it difficult to benchmark results across different studies or to judge the relevance of the data for real-world applications.

For general application scenarios, a set of widely accepted and practically relevant test conditions can be adopted. A bending radius of R = 10 mm is commonly used as a baseline, with a typical range of 5–20 mm, where smaller radii impose more severe mechanical strain. The number of bending cycles is usually selected between 1000 and 10,000, covering both short-term mechanical tolerance and long-term durability. Single-sided bending is preferred, as it better reflects the dominant deformation mode encountered in most flexible device applications. Test environments are best controlled at 25 ± 2 °C and 30 ± 5% relative humidity to minimize interference from temperature- or humidity-induced degradation.

After bending, device reliability is primarily evaluated by the retention of efficiency (η/η0), together with the corresponding retention of V OC, J SC, and FF. In parallel, postbending morphological inspection is essential to confirm the structural integrity of the device stack, ensuring the absence of visible cracks or delamination in the functional layers.

Flexible Devices

Mechanical durability is another key strength. When deposited on flexible substrates such as polyimide, both perovskite and CIGS layers can withstand extensive bending, folding, and mechanical deformation while retaining stable electrical performance. This robustness makes them suitable for roll-to-roll manufacturing and long-term use in dynamic environments. Additionally, the low-temperature, solution-based processing of perovskites and the mature vacuum deposition techniques for CIGS provide a practical route toward scalable and cost-effective production. The thin-film nature of both layers minimizes material consumption, reducing manufacturing costs compared with those of crystalline silicon technologies. The compatibility of these tandems with large-area printing and flexible substrates further enhances their commercial potential. Zheng et al. targeted the poor coverage and shunting issues that occur when building monolithic perovskite/CIGS tandems on rough, flexible substrates. By using fully vacuum-deposited NiO x and C60/BCP charge-selective layers on conductive steel foil, they achieved uniform interfaces and reduced shunt losses, leading to an 18.1% efficiency (Figure a), which was a record for flexible monolithic perovskite/CIGS tandems at the time. Wang et al. addressed the chemical incompatibility between the AZO layer in flexible CIGS stacks and wide-bandgap perovskites, which can trigger rapid perovskite degradation and interdiffusion. Introducing a thin 20 nm IZO inert interlayer protected the perovskite (Figure b, c), enabling a 21.24% efficient flexible monolithic tandem with substantially improved thermal and operational stability. Jeong et al. focused on fabrication bottlenecks in flexible tandems, especially alkali–metal diffusion and mechanical/thermal stress that degrade CIGS quality and limit conformal perovskite coating (Figure d). A lift-off strategy with a polyimide interlayer regulated Na/K behavior, reduced deep defects, and alleviated stress, producing high-quality flexible CIGS and allowing smooth solution-processed perovskite top cells. The result was a certified 22.8% efficiency and 6.15 W g–1 specific power, demonstrating a more scalable route for high-performance flexible tandems.

The interfacial inhomogeneity arising from molecular aggregation in planar carbazole-based hole-selective contacts on flexible substrates contributes to the challenge of achieving high efficiency and mechanical durability in flexible perovskite/CIGS tandems. These devices demonstrated remarkable mechanical robustness, retaining their initial PCE after 10,000 bending cycles at a 10 mm radius.

Overall, flexible perovskite/CIGS TSCs offer a powerful combination of high efficiency, ultralightweight construction, mechanical flexibility, and scalable manufacturing, positioning them as one of the most promising candidates for next-generation mobile, wearable, and space-grade photovoltaics. Economic Viability and Commercialization Outlook

Perovskite/CIGS TSCs have demonstrated enormous potential due to their excellent optoelectronic performance and stability. However, beyond efficiency and stability, economic viability and commercialization are key factors driving the widespread application of this technology. As material costs continue to decline and manufacturing processes improve, the economic feasibility of perovskite/CIGS TSCs has gradually improved, and their commercialization outlook has become increasingly optimistic.

Reducing Material and Production Costs

Perovskites can be processed from solution at low temperatures using comparatively simple equipment, enabling large-area fabrication at much lower cost than conventional Si technologies, while their bandgap tunability makes them well suited as top cells in tandem with CIGS and other thin-film absorbers. CIGS, although more expensive than perovskite on a per-watt basis, benefits from a mature thin-film deposition infrastructure, good materials availability, and a tunable bandgap that complements perovskite in tandem stacks. It should be emphasized that the large-scale production cost of CIGS is higher than that of crystalline silicon solar cells due to the high cost of vacuum deposition processes and rare metal raw materials (indium, gallium). This cost gap makes perovskite/silicon tandems more competitive in the field of rigid, large-area ground-based photovoltaic power generation, while perovskite/CIGS tandems rely on their flexible and lightweight characteristics to occupy a niche in specific application scenarios.

The high manufacturing cost of CIGS modules is mainly attributed to two factors: the high price of indium raw materials and the high investment in vacuum deposition equipment. The vacuum coevaporation process, which is widely used for high-efficiency CIGS modules, has a material utilization rate of only ∼30%, resulting in significant waste of expensive indium. In contrast, nonvacuum solution-based processes (e.g., nanoparticle ink coating) can increase the material utilization rate to over 90%, reducing the raw material cost by ∼50%. However, the efficiency of CIGS modules prepared by nonvacuum processes is still lower than that of vacuum-deposited modules, which is a key bottleneck to be solved.

Regarding indium scarcity, reducing the indium content in CIGS absorbers by increasing the Ga ratio is a viable strategy. When the Ga/(Ga+In) ratio increases from 0.3 to 0.5, the indium content can be reduced by ∼40% while maintaining a suitable bandgap (∼1.2 eV) for tandem applications. In addition, developing indium-free CZTSSe materials is a long-term solution, but the efficiency of CZTSSe solar cells is still lower than 13%, which needs further improvement in material quality and interface engineering.

In perovskite/CIGS tandems, the efficiency gain and reduced perovskite thickness translate into lower overall material usage and a favorable techno-economic balance. As shown in Figure a, the manufacturing cost of single-junction PSCs is the lowest at $27.18/m2, followed by single-junction CIGS at $38.25/m2, and the 2T tandem configuration has a manufacturing cost of $45.17/m2. The manufacturing cost of perovskite/CIGS tandem modules is lower than the combined costs of perovskite and CIGS modules, indicating that upgrading to perovskite/CIGS TSCs from existing single-junction cells does not incur high additional costs.

Another economic advantage of perovskite/CIGS TSCs is the optimization of system costs. In solar photovoltaic systems, in addition to the solar cells themselves, balance-of-system (BOS) componentssuch as racks, inverters, wiring, and other accessoriesaccount for a significant portion of the cost. Compared with single-junction devices, tandems reduce balance-of-system costs and energy losses by boosting efficiency and shrinking the required module area, with 4T architectures particularly effective because each subcell can operate near its own optimum under fluctuating irradiance. From a techno-economic perspective, levelized cost of energy (LCOE), a ratio of the total lifetime cost to the total lifetime energy output, where total lifetime cost includes initial investment (module manufacturing, BOS components, installation), operation and maintenance (O&M) costs, and module replacement costs, and total lifetime energy output is determined by module efficiency, degradation rate, and annual irradiance, is far more sensitive to module efficiency and service lifetime than to absorber material cost, making efficiency and durability the primary levers for cost reduction, as illustrated in Figure b, c for CIGS and perovskite/CIGS tandem modules. The figures highlight the potential for a significant reduction in LCOE values for both types of modules while providing guidance on how improvements in each parameter can translate into LCOE reductions. Clearly, increasing module efficiency leads to a substantial reduction in LCOE. As long as the LCOE remains low, replacing modules during the photovoltaic system’s lifetime could be a viable solution at a lower cost.

To ensure the credibility of LCOE results, we clarify the core assumption parameters: equipment depreciation period, production capacity utilization rate, indium price fluctuation range, perovskite precursor price, annual O&M cost ratio (to initial investment), module degradation rate, and perovskite top-cell replacement cycle. To clarify the impact of parameter variations on LCOE results, we conducted quantitative sensitivity analysis by adjusting each core parameter within a ±20% range (industry-accepted fluctuation interval) while keeping other parameters constant. The results suggest three cost-reduction priorities for perovskite/CIGS tandems: (i) near-term (1–3 years), raise module efficiency (2T ≥ 28%, 4T ≥ 33%) via interface and bandgap optimization, cutting LCOE by ∼5%–8%; (ii) mid-term (3–5 years), push capacity utilization to ≥90% through roll-to-roll manufacturing and automation, lowering LCOE by ∼8%–10%; and (iii) long-term (5–10 years), develop low-indium CIGS and nonvacuum processing to reduce material exposure and capital intensity, bringing a further ∼10%–12% reduction.

Regarding large-scale production, the manufacturing technology for perovskite/CIGS TSCs has gradually matured. The solution-based processing method for PSCs and the thin-film deposition technology for CIGS solar cells can be effectively applied to large-scale production, giving perovskite/CIGS TSCs strong scalability. For example, by utilizing roll-to-roll (R2R) processing techniques, researchers have been able to enable large-scale manufacturing of perovskite/CIGS TSCs, which contributes significantly to reducing production costs and improving yield. Moreover, the flexibility of perovskite/CIGS TSCs allows for potential applications in wearables, BIPV, and other fields, further expanding the market scope.

Commercialization Outlook

As shown in Figure d, assuming a system lifetime of 30 years, the LCOE of perovskite and perovskite/CIGS photovoltaic systems is calculated with module replacements occurring at different times, based on the cost model and parameter assumptions. The core driving factor for module replacement lies in the service life mismatch between the two subcells: the perovskite top cell typically has a service life of 10–15 years due to environmental degradation, while the CIGS bottom cell can maintain stable operation for 25–30 years. This mismatch makes the tiered replacement strategy (only replacing the degraded perovskite top cell instead of the entire tandem module) economically viable. Figure d shows that, in a 30-year LCOE analysis with periodic replacement, the optimal “economic lifetime” of the perovskite top cell is ∼15 years. At this replacement cycle, the LCOE of the tandem system can be reduced by ∼18% compared with the overall module replacement scheme, mainly due to the savings in raw material costs for the CIGS bottom cell and the reduction in system downtime loss. By contrast, single-junction perovskite modules have lower initial costs but require more frequent replacements, leading to higher long-term LCOE in large-scale applications.

The replacement strategy varies with application scenarios: for large-scale ground-based photovoltaic power stations, tiered replacement is preferred because it can significantly reduce maintenance costs and extend the effective service life of the system; for flexible portable devices and aerospace applications, overall module replacement is more practical due to the low replacement difficulty and the need for integrated structural integrity. Even so, perovskite/CIGS tandems, which couple low-cost, high-efficiency perovskites with the stability of CIGS, remain attractive for BIPV, flexible and portable photovoltaics, and emerging space applications.

The economic improvement of perovskite/CIGS TSCs is reflected not only in cost reduction but also in significant environmental and social benefits. Compared to traditional Si-based solar cells, the manufacturing process of PSCs is more environmentally friendly, as it reduces reliance on rare metals and reduces energy consumption. Furthermore, the production of perovskite materials requires less energy and can utilize renewable resources, contributing to a reduction in the environmental burden of solar cell production.

Figure e shows the influence of PCE and lifetime assumptions on the normalized average environmental impacts of TSCs, illustrating how variations in lifetime and PCE would impact environmental effects per kWh. In both figures, the LCOE of the current lifetime and PCE of TSCs does not show significant differences. Compared to Figure f, for CIGS and perovskite/CIGS tandem configurations with a 15-year lifespan and 30% efficiency, this configuration achieves a neutral environmental preference. In contrast, at the same PCE and lifetime level, Si and perovskite tandem configurations remain among the least environmentally friendly options. As manufacturing technology continues to evolve, perovskite/CIGS TSCs will not only lower costs but also drive the sustainable development of the solar energy industry, contributing to the global energy transition.

The key parameter assumptions in the LCOE calculation for replacement scenarios are as follows: (1) the replacement cost of the perovskite top cell accounts for ∼35% of the cost of the entire tandem module; (2) the labor cost and downtime loss of tiered replacement are ∼50% lower than those of overall replacement; (3) the performance attenuation rate of the CIGS bottom cell is less than 0.5% per year within 30 years. These assumptions are derived from the actual operation data of CIGS modules and the cost accounting of perovskite thin-film deposition processes. Perovskite/CIGS TSCs showcase immense potential in both economic viability and the commercialization outlook. By optimization of materials and manufacturing processes, reduction of costs, and enhancement of efficiency, perovskite/CIGS TSCs are set to become an integral part of the future solar industry. As market demand grows and technological breakthroughs continue, perovskite/CIGS TSCs will find broader applications in the photovoltaic market and play a key role in the global energy transition and sustainable development.

In terms of commercialization positioning, perovskite/CIGS tandems do not compete with perovskite/silicon tandems in the cost-sensitive ground-based photovoltaic market. Instead, they target application scenarios where flexibility, lightweight, and high power-to-weight ratio are prioritized, such as curved BIPV, wearable electronic power supplies, and aerospace photovoltaic systems. In these scenarios, the performance advantages of perovskite/CIGS tandems can offset their cost disadvantages, thus showing unique application value.

Conclusion and Perspectives

Perovskite/CIGS TSCs are emerging as a promising high-efficiency technology, combining the low-cost, bandgap-tunable perovskite top cell with the proven stability and strong near-infrared absorption of CIGS. Their complementary spectral utilization enables substantial PCE gains, with recent progress in 2T and 4T configurations pushing certified efficiencies to 27.3% and 30.13%, respectively, underscoring their strong application potential. Despite these advances, several barriers analyzed in the main text still limit large-scale deployment, including the phase segregation of WBG perovskites, high manufacturing costs, indium scarcity in CIGS, and nonradiative recombination at the perovskite/CIGS interface. To address these bottlenecks, future research should focus on three hierarchical directions with clear technical support from the main text.

For WBG perovskites, the core task is to suppress light-induced halide migration and phase segregation. Building on the cation doping and interface passivation strategies, future work should explore synergistic schemes combining Rb/Cs cation doping, 2D/3D heterojunction construction, and bimolecular passivation (e.g., F5BPA + p-CF3PEA). For the perovskite/CIGS interface, rational interface engineering should be developed based on the ALD NiO X and SAM reconstruction technologies to reduce nonradiative recombination and improve V OC while maintaining conformal coverage on rough CIGS surfaces. To bridge the gap between laboratory devices and industrial production, it is critical to integrate solution-processed perovskites with low-cost CIGS fabrication routes. On one hand, replace high-cost vacuum coevaporation of CIGS with nonvacuum solution processes (e.g., nanoparticle ink coating) to reduce equipment investment and raw material waste. On the other hand, optimize the roll-to-roll (R2R) compatible processes for flexible tandem devices can be optimized and the thermal mismatch problem between perovskite and CIGS during large-area deposition.

Targeting the niche application scenarios of perovskite/CIGS tandems, future work should focus on two aspects: first, develop tiered replacement strategies for tandem modules based on the service life mismatch between perovskite (10–15 years) and CIGS (25–30 years) to reduce long-term LCOE; second, expand the application of flexible lightweight tandems in aerospace, wearable electronics, and BIPV, where their power-to-weight ratio advantages can offset the cost gap with silicon-based technologies.

In short, moving perovskite/CIGS tandems toward commercialization will hinge on three things: improving long-term stability, scaling up manufacturing processes that work on real (often rough) CIGS substrates, and choosing application scenarios where their lightweight and flexibility offer clear advantages. As progress continues on these fronts, perovskite/CIGS tandems are well positioned to move beyond lab demonstrations and become a competitive thin-film technology. This article discusses recent advances with industrial deployment in mind. We highlight bottlenecks that directly matter for scale-up, such as conformal processing on commercial CIGS and resource constraints related to indium. We also propose a practical way to compare technology routes using a performance–stability–cost perspective and outline how different device designs and processes can be matched to specific use cases (e.g., aerospace and curved BIPV) to define where perovskite/CIGS tandems can win first.
