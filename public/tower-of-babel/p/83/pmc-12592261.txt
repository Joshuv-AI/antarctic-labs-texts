Sarcopenic obesity and risk of falls: findings in Middle-aged and older Chinese population from CHARLS
Authors: Shenhan Yin, Jianhua Su, Jisheng Bao, Haibin Yu, Menglan Liu, Xiaolin Ji
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenic obesity (SO) is increasingly recognized as a significant health concern, particularly among older populations. Existing literature indicates that SO elevates the risk for various adverse health outcomes such as cardiovascular diseases, fractures, higher all-cause mortality. However, evidence regarding its impact on the risk of falls remains limited and inconclusive. Our study aimed to investigate the association between SO and fall incidents.MethodsA total of 10,905 participants were enrolled from the baseline survey of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) 2015 wave. Participants were categorized into four groups according to sarcopenia and obesity status, with the neither sarcopenia nor obesity group serving as the reference. Logistic regression was utilized to evaluate the cross-sectional association between SO and falls. Furthermore, we tracked fall incidents reported in follow-up surveys conducted in CHARLS 2018 and 2020 wave. Cox regression analysis was performed to explore how SO affected the risk of falls. Stratified Cox analyses by age (< 60 vs. ≥60 years) were also performed.ResultsIn the cross-sectional analysis (2015), the SO group [OR (95% CI): 1.84 (1.42 ~ 2.37), P < 0.01] showed a higher risk of falls compared to the reference group; however, this association was not statistically significant after adjusting for potential confounding factors. In the longitudinal analysis (2015–2020), the SO group [HR (95%CI): 2.78 (2.03 ~ 3.80), P < 0.01] had a significantly increased risk of falls. The results remained similar after adjusting for age, sex [HR (95%CI): 1.44 (1.02 ~ 2.04), P < 0.05], and additional covariates [HR (95%CI): 1.43 (1.00 ~ 2.03), P < 0.05]. Notably, stratified Cox models showed that SO was significantly associated with fall risk in both age groups, with a stronger effect observed in participants under 60 years [HR (95%CI): 2.85 (1.13 ~ 7.17), P < 0.05] than in those aged 60 and above [HR (95%CI): 1.64 (1.08 ~ 2.50), P < 0.05].ConclusionSarcopenic obesity is associated with an increased risk of falls among middle-aged and older adults, especially in longitudinal analyses. Age-stratified results suggest that the impact of SO on falls may be more pronounced in the middle-aged group. Our findings support the need for early identification and targeted interventions for individuals with SO to mitigate fall-related risks in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03215-0.

Introduction

The increasing number of falls has posed a huge challenge to public health, especially for the older adults, where falls lead to increased morbidity, disability and mortality [1, 2]. It is estimated that over 25% of the older adults fall down more than once a year, making falls one of the leading causes of death in this age group [3, 4]. In America, over 14 million older people are affected, resulting in substantial healthcare costs [3, 5]. Similarly, in China, fall-related mortality in individuals aged over 60 years has been rising steadily since 2013 [6].Beyond physical consequences [7], falls can lead to psychological distress, loss of confidence, activity restriction, and social isolation [8, 9]. Given the rapid aging of the global population, effective strategies for fall risk assessment, prevention, and intervention are critical [3, 10, 11].

Sarcopenic obesity (SO), characterized by the simultaneous presence of sarcopenia and obesity, has increasingly attracted clinical attention due to its association with multiple health complications such as cardiovascular disease, fractures and increased all-cause mortality [12–14]. Previous studies have demonstrated that both sarcopenia [15, 16] and obesity [17, 18] independently increases the risk of falls. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that their coexistence may further elevate fall risk. However, current evidence supporting the association between SO and falls remains limited and inconclusive [19, 20]. One major challenge is the absence of a universally accepted diagnostic criterion for SO [19, 21], resulting in inconsistencies across studies regarding definitions and measurement thresholds for sarcopenia and obesity. Furthermore, limited studies were mostly conducted in non-Asian populations whose body composition characteristics and metabolic risk profiles significantly differ from those of Asian populations [20, 22].

To address these limitations and fill the research gap in Chinese population, we utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) [23]. By applying diagnostic criteria suitable for Chinese individuals, we aimed to explore both cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between SO and fall risk among middle-aged and older Chinese adults.

Methods

Study population

Our data come from CHARLS [23], a survey launched by Peking University in 2011. The study targets individuals aged over 45 years and collects information on health status, cognitive function, financial well-being, and social relationships. Follow-up assessments are conducted every 2–3 years to capture changes over time.

In our study, we specifically use data from 2015 to 2020 to ensure temporal continuity and analytical robustness. In total, 21,801 people participated in the CHARLS 2015 wave. Individuals were excluded if they had missing information on age and sex (n = 4,118), obesity status (n = 4,128), or sarcopenia assessment (n = 1,968). Additionally, participants younger than 45 years (n = 667) and without fall information(n = 15) were excluded. Through this screening, 10,905 individuals were eligible for the baseline study. After further excluding those with a history of falls in 2015(n = 1,769) and without falls data from 2018 to 2020 (n = 1,627), 7,509 participants remained for the prospective cohort study (Fig. 1).

Grouping of sarcopenic obesity

The most commonly accepted diagnosis of sarcopenia in China follows the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 [24]. Low appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) combined with decreased muscle strength or impaired physical performance is diagnosed as sarcopenia. ASM was estimated using a validated anthropometric equation, as follows [25]: ASM = 0.193*weight(kg) + 0.107*height(cm) − 4.157*sex(male = 1,female = 2) − 0.037×age(years) − 2.631, which shows high agreement with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) [25]. In our study, we quantified muscle mass by height-adjusted muscle mass (ASM/Ht²), using the lowest quintile (20%) of the distribution in the study population as the cutoff value: <7.0 kg/m² for men and < 5.4 kg/m² for women. Muscle strength was assessed using handgrip strength, measured with a hand dynamometer, and the maximum value from either hand was used for analysis. Low muscle strength was defined as < 28 kg for men and < 18 kg for women. In addition, physical performance was assessed by five-time chair stand tests, with a time of more than 12 s was defined as low physical performance. Notably, in contrast to previous studies [12, 26], walking speed was not used as a screening criterion in our study because it may be affected by confounding factors such as osteoarthritis according to the latest consensus for SO [21].

Obesity was defined according to diagnostic criteria established for the Chinese population, which was defined as a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 28.0 kg/m², a waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) ≥ 0.5, or a waist circumference (WC) ≥ 85 cm for women and ≥ 90 cm for men [27]. All measurements of height, weight, and waist circumference were conducted by trained interviewers following uniform and standardized procedures.

Based on the above, we divided the participants into four groups according to their sarcopenia and obesity status: SO, only sarcopenia, only obesity and neither sarcopenia nor obesity.

Assessment of falls

Falls were assessed as the primary outcome variable in our study. Participants were asked the standardized question: “Have you fallen down?“, which asked whether the participant had a history of falling. This was recorded as yes or no based on the participants’ experiences.

Covariates

The included covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and their statistically significant differences among groups. Social characteristics included age, sex, education level and marriage (married and others). Personal history included previous smoking and drinking. Health-related factors included diastolic and systolic blood pressure, hypertension, diabetes, chronic lung disease, heart disease, kidney disease and digestive disease).

Statistical analysis

The required data were screened from the database using Stata software (version 15.0), and subsequent statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2). Violin plots and bar charts used to visualize group comparisons were generated using GraphPad Prism software (version 9). Continuous variables were presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) or median with interquartile range (IQR) according to their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Group comparisons were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. Post hoc pairwise comparisons for continuous variables were conducted using Bonferroni-adjusted Dunn’s test. For categorical variables, significant differences between groups were visualized using bar charts with letter-based annotations to indicate statistically distinct groups.

For the cross-sectional analysis, logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the association between SO and falls. The models were structured sequentially: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; Model 3 was further adjusted for sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, and comorbidities.

For longitudinal analyses, Cox proportional hazards models were utilized to assess the risk of incident falls associated with SO during follow-up. The same adjustment approach described above for logistic regression (Model 1, Model 2, and Model 3) was adopted. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p-value less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 10,905 people was divided into four groups: SO (n = 447[4.10%]), only sarcopenia (n = 519[4.76%]), only obesity(n = 8234[75.51%]), and neither sarcopenia nor obesity (n = 1705[15.64%]). The average age of the baseline population was 59.63 years (SD: 9.64), and 5366(49.21%) were male. Participants with SO were older (mean: 74.12 years, SD: 8.51), more likely to be female (77.18%), and had a higher illiteracy rate (18.35%) than those in other three groups (all P < 0.001). Except for stroke, liver disease, and cancer, the prevalence of chronic diseases significantly differed across groups, with the SO group exhibiting the highest rates of ever falls, hypertension, cardiovascular, and digestive disorders (all P < 0.001) (Table 1). To further visualize group differences, violin plots were used to illustrate the distribution of continuous variables, while bar charts with alphabetical annotations were employed to depict post hoc comparisons of categorical variables (Figs. 2 and 3). To enhance clarity regarding sex-specific patterns, anthropometric indicators and handgrip strength values were further stratified by sex (Table S1). In addition, we also summarize the baseline characteristics of 7,509 participants included in the longitudinal study(mean age: 58.82 ± 9.11 years, 50.39% women) (Table S2).

Cross-sectional associations between SO and falls in 2015

Logistic regression models were employed to estimate the odds ratio (OR) for falls among four groups at baseline, with the neither sarcopenia nor obesity group serving as the reference. Unadjusted analysis showed that both SO [OR(95%CI): 1.84 (1.42 ~ 2.37)]and only sarcopenia [OR(95%CI): 1.69 (1.31 ~ 2.16)] group had increased odds of falls (both P<0.01). However, further adjustments added the results were no longer significant (Table 2).

Longitudinal associations between SO and fall risk from 2015 to 2020

In the prospective cohort analysis, 7,509 participants(mean age 58.82 ± 9.11 years, 50.34% women) were screened spanning from 2015 to 2020. After 60 months of follow-up, 13.37% (1004/7509) of participants experienced falls. Significant differences in fall incidence were observed across the groups. The sarcopenic obesity group exhibited the highest fall incidence at 24.79% (58/234), followed by only sarcopenia group at 16.96% (49/289), only obesity group at 13.46% (776/5767), and neither sarcopenia nor obesity group at 9.93% (121/1219) (Fig. 4).

Table 3 Presents the hazard ratios (HRs) for fall risk across four groups. In the unadjusted model, all three groups exhibited a significantly elevated risk of falls compared to the reference group, with the SO group showing the highest risk [HR (95%CI): 2.78 (2.03 ~ 3.80), P < 0.01], followed by only sarcopenia group [HR (95%CI): 1.89 (1.35 ~ 2.63), P < 0.01], and only obesity group [HR (95%CI): 1.39 (1.15 ~ 1.68), P < 0.01]. After adjusting for age and sex, only the SO group had an increased risk of falling [HR (95%CI): 1.44 (1.02 ~ 2.04), P < 0.05], while the other two groups were no longer significant. Further adjustment for other covariates still maintained the same result [HR (95%CI): 1.43 (1.00 ~ 2.03), P < 0.05] (Table 3).

Stratified analysis by age

To further explore the potential heterogeneity in the association between sarcopenic obesity and fall risk across different age groups, we conducted stratified Cox proportional hazards regression analyses by age, categorizing participants into middle-aged (< 60 years) and older adults (≥ 60 years). As shown in Table 4, the results consistently demonstrated that sarcopenic obesity was significantly associated with an increased risk of falls in both middle-aged group [HR (95%CI): 3.52(1.42 ~ 8.73), P < 0.01]and older group [HR (95%CI): 2.11(1.43 ~ 3.09), P < 0.01]. After further adjusting for other covariates, only the SO group had an increased risk of falling in middle-aged group [HR (95%CI): 2.85 (1.13 ~ 7.17), P < 0.05]and older group [HR (95%CI): 1.64 (1.08 ~ 2.50), P < 0.05]. Interestingly, the magnitude of the association was more pronounced in the middle-aged group than in the older adult group.

Discussion

In our study, we investigated the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between SO and falls in Chinese population (age over 45) from the CHARLS. Our results showed that although the cross-sectional analysis did not reveal a statistically significant association, the longitudinal analysis clearly demonstrated that people with SO had a higher risk of falls than those without sarcopenia or obesity. The findings emphasize the long-term impact of SO on falls, reinforcing the need for early screening and targeted interventions.

Previous studies have extensively documented adverse health consequences associated with SO. A recent meta-analysis showed that the risk of all-cause mortality increased by 0.21 times in adults with SO [13]. Besides, Jiang et al. found that SO increases the risk of cardiovascular disease among Chinese population aged over 45 [12]. Similarly, Yu et al. revealed that SO was independently associated with cardiometabolic multimorbidity [28]. However, few studies have assessed the association between SO and fall risk, especially in Asian populations. Our findings provide strong evidence that SO significantly elevates the risk of falls, further complementing its adverse outcomes. Early identification and intervention of SO are therefore necessary.

It is worth noting that the cross-sectional analysis failed to find a significant association between SO and falls after adjusting for potential confounders. This may be due to that falls are influenced by cumulative effects of impaired physical performance. The cross-sectional study provides only a snapshot of SO and falls at a single time point and cannot capture these long-term effects, leading to a non-significant association. Also, the observed association may be influenced by reverse causality. People who have already fallen may reduce physical activity [29], leading to muscle loss and weight gain. The cohort study tracks fall events over time. Cox regression can account for both the occurrence and timing of falls, which strengthens causal inference and captures the progressive effects of SO on fall risk. Thus, while the cross-sectional study alone is insufficient to determine the role of SO in falls, the longitudinal findings provide stronger evidence for a causal relationship. This underscores the importance of long-term monitoring and early intervention in individuals with SO.

Our age-stratified Cox analysis further revealed that the association between SO and fall risk was consistent across both age groups (< 60 and ≥ 60 years). Interestingly, the hazard ratio for falls was even higher in the middle-aged group than in older adults. One possible explanation is that middle-aged adults with SO may have less exposure to fall prevention strategies that are more commonly implemented in older populations. In contrast, older adults may engage in more cautious behaviors or rely on assistive devices, which could partially offset their fall risk despite similar physiological impairments. These findings suggest that fall prevention strategies should not be limited to older adults but also consider middle-aged individuals with SO who are at elevated risk. The underlying mechanisms behind these differences warrant further investigation in future studies.

Our findings are consistent with previous research suggesting that SO contributes to functional decline and increased fall risk [22]. For example, Dowling et al. found a synergistic effect of obesity and low muscle strength on the risk of falls [30]. Recently, a meta-analysis demonstrated that older adults with SO are at a significantly higher risk of adverse musculoskeletal outcomes compared to those with either sarcopenia or obesity alone [14]. However, limited studies have specifically examined this association in Asian populations. By focusing on Chinese middle-aged and older adults and applying regionally appropriate diagnostic criteria, our study adds valuable epidemiological evidence to this field.

The relationship between SO and falls is complex and may be mediated by multiple pathophysiological mechanisms [31].SO represents an unfavorable combination of excess adiposity and diminished muscle quality, both of which contribute to impaired physical function. Adipose tissue promotes chronic systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, insulin resistance, and hormonal imbalances, which negatively impact muscle protein synthesis and mitochondrial function [32, 33]. Neuromuscular damage and decreased proprioception may further compromise balance and gait stability [34]. It is noteworthy that recent studies have revealed the potential role of dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of SO, suggesting a gut–muscle–fat axis that affects systemic metabolism and muscle integrity [35, 36]. At the molecular mechanism level, skeletal muscle is an important endocrine organ, and the myokines it secretes play a key role in maintaining metabolic balance. Dysfunction in myokine signaling may worsen obesity-related metabolic dysfunction, impair muscle regeneration, and accelerate the decline in muscle mass and strength, further increasing fall risk [31]. Future studies should focus on further clarifying the potential mechanisms of the relationship between SO and falls. In addition, demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors play an important role in the risk of falls and should be considered when evaluating SO-related outcomes [37]. On this basis, targeted interventions, including resistance training [8, 38], dietary optimization [39], and fall prevention programs [40, 41], can be implemented to mitigate the negative effects of SO on falls.

Our study has several strengths. Firstly, as far as we know, our study is the first to examine the relationship between SO and falls in a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, filling an important research gap. Secondly, our study followed the AWGS 2019 guidelines [24] for sarcopenia and China-specific obesity thresholds [27], ensuring ethnically appropriate diagnostic criteria. Unlike the cutoff value for obesity in Western population (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) [42], these criteria account for differences in body fat distribution and metabolic risk in Asian populations [43]. Thirdly, our study incorporated multiple obesity indicators instead of relying solely on BMI, which does not differentiate between fat mass and lean mass and may misclassify individuals with high BMI but significant muscle loss as non-obese [44]. Indeed, using BMI alone in our study resulted in a very low prevalence of SO, highlighting the necessity of incorporating WC and WHtR for a more comprehensive assessment of adiposity. What’s more, we did not include gait speed as a diagnostic criterion for SO, aligning with the joint consensus statement of the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) and the European Association for the Study of Obesity (EASO) because confounding factors may affect results [21]. By applying ethnically tailored criteria, incorporating comprehensive obesity assessments, and employing a longitudinal design, our study provides robust, generalizable evidence on the long-term impact of SO on fall risk in Chinese adults. Leveraging data from CHARLS, our research strengthens causal inferences and offers critical insights for fall prevention strategies in aging populations.

Despite its strengths, our study has several limitations. First, fall events were self-reported, which may lead to recall bias or underreporting. Second, although we adjusted for multiple confounders, we cannot exclude the effects of unmeasured factors such as medication use, cognitive impairment, or environmental hazards. Third, the classification of sarcopenia and obesity was based on baseline data and assumed to remain unchanged throughout the 5-year follow-up, which may lead to exposure misclassification, particularly in older adults whose body composition and muscle function are subject to change over time. Fourth, muscle mass in our study was estimated using an anthropometric equation rather than directly measured by DXA or bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Although this method is validated in Chinese population, it is not formally endorsed by AWGS and may affect diagnostic precision. Moreover, sarcopenia was defined using appendicular skeletal muscle mass adjusted by height squared (ASM/height²) according to AWGS 2019, which may underestimate sarcopenia in individuals with obesity [45]. Alternative adjustment methods for muscle mass warrant further investigation in future studies. Lastly, the study population consisted of Chinese adults, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other ethnic groups with different body composition and lifestyle factors.

Conclusion

Our study provides strong longitudinal evidence that SO is an independent risk factor for falls in both middle-aged and older adults. Age-stratified Cox analysis showed that the association was more pronounced in the middle-aged population. The findings support the need for early diagnosis and intervention of patients with SO to prevent falls and related adverse outcomes [46]. Given the rising prevalence of SO, addressing its impact on falls should be a priority in geriatric and metabolic healthcare to reduce disability, healthcare costs, and improve quality of life in aging populations.
