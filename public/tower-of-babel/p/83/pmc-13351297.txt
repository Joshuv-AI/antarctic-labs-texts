Contextualizing life expectancy losses during the COVID-19 pandemic in Brazil—authors’ reply
Authors: Luisa C Brant, Mariana S Felisbino-Mendes, Renato A Teixeira, Mohsen Naghavi, Antonio LP Ribeiro, Deborah C Malta
Published: 2026

We thank Parente and colleagues1 for their interest in our work and for the opportunity to clarify important aspects of our study, which aimed to describe temporal and geographic patterns in mortality, disability-adjusted life years, and life expectancy in Brazil and its federated units using estimates from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2023.2 At no point did we claim to demonstrate causal effects of specific political decisions, public policies, or societal phenomena. Rather, our interpretation was framed as a contextual discussion of findings observed during a period that has been extensively examined in the scientific literature. The article's conclusion reflects this approach: “This overall poor performance regarding health outcomes during the pandemic seems to partially reflect an uncoordinated national response, including delayed vaccination and lack of adherence to evidence-based measures.”

The statement highlighted by the authors was deliberately phrased as a hypothesis-generating interpretation (“suggest”), rather than as a causal conclusion. The observed associations were consistent across analyses, and the proposed relationship is biologically and socially plausible, as well as supported by previous studies.3, 4, 5 Indeed, the association between scientific denialism, fragmented public health responses, and adverse COVID-19 outcomes has been documented in multiple studies conducted in Brazil and elsewhere.3, 4, 5 Our discussion was informed by this broader body of evidence, which has consistently identified these factors as plausible contributors to heterogeneous pandemic outcomes.3, 4, 5

Importantly, we emphasize that all health information systems and modeling exercises contain uncertainty. For this reason, the limitations of GBD estimates were explicitly discussed in the original article. Methodological procedures such as correction for underreporting and redistribution of garbage codes are not additional sources of bias introduced by the GBD framework. They are internationally accepted approaches developed to improve comparability and address known limitations of mortality data. These methods have been extensively described, validated, and continuously refined across successive GBD iterations.

The determinants of excess mortality and life expectancy losses during the COVID-19 pandemic are complex and multifactorial. Our findings should therefore be interpreted as descriptive evidence that contributes to understanding these patterns, while recognizing the inherent limitations of observational population-level analyses for causal inference. Finally, we reaffirm the central role of science and evidence-based strategies in mitigating the effects of public health emergencies.
