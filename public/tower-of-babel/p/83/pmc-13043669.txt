Rapid urbanization reduces genetic diversity and increases genetic differentiation of a lynx spider Oxyopes sertatus in central Taiwan
Authors: Ying-Yuan Lo, Chi Wei, Wan-Jyun Chen, Chung-Ping Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urbanization is a dominant force driving destructive and irreversible changes of natural habitats in modern times. While the effects of urbanization on community composition and phenotypic responses are well-documented, its influence on genetic diversity and population structure remains understudied, particularly for invertebrates in subtropical regions. This study tested the hypothesis that urbanization reduces genetic diversity and increases population differentiation in the lynx spider Oxyopes sertatus, a common foliage-dwelling spider in Taiwan. We sampled 245 individuals from 17 sites distributed along an urban-rural gradient and quantified urbanization intensity using land-use composition at both landscape (4 km²) and local (0.25 km²) scales. Genetic diversity and population differentiation were assessed using mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase I (COI) and genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) generated via restriction site-associated DNA sequencing (RAD-seq). The results showed that genetic diversity declined with increasing urbanization intensity, with the strongest effects detected in genome-wide diversity indices. Urban populations also showed greater genetic differentiation compared to rural populations, indicating urban environments impose physical barriers to gene flow and elevate differentiation. However, STRUCTURE and principal component analysis (PCA) revealed no distinct genetic clusters, suggesting that dispersal may still occur. Our findings suggest that even a widespread and abundant spider species can undergo rapid genetic diversity loss in urban landscapes. These results highlight the importance of incorporating habitat connectivity into urban planning to maintain wildlife gene exchanges and support biodiversity in rapidly urbanizing regions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-40537-z.

Introduction

Urbanization causes one of the most destructive and irreversible change in global land use and land cover (LULC) in the past century1–3. The rate of urban expansion is much faster than the growth of human populations, even in regions where human populations have declined3–6. Although the global urban area occupies only between 0.5 and 3% of our planet’s surface7,8, urbanization greatly affects the ecology of natural habitats and created novel selection pressures for the inhabiting organisms9,10. The land use changes associated with urbanizing process are often accompanied by negative impacts on wildlife, such as the effects of urban heat island, artificial light at night, noise, and heavy metals pollution11–17. Although urban cities could potentially function as critical reserves for many wildlife18–22including threatened species23, these negative factors extensively modify various aspects of ecological processes from genetic variation of organisms to ecosystem dynamics4. Therefore, the urban expansion is considered one of the main causes for biodiversity loss24–26.

Urbanization is associated with the loss and fragmentation of natural habitats, coupled with an increase in artificial and impervious surface areas, such as buildings, bridges and roads. The negative impacts of habitat degradation on species diversity and community assembly due to urban expansion are well documented, such as decreased species richness and altered functional diversity27,28, whereas landscape genetics of urban wildlife remains poorly understood29,30. Organisms that live in or around urban areas are often restricted to small, isolated, and fragmented urban green spaces or areas adjacent to water resources. These green spaces act as urban “habitat islands”31, resulting in small populations, limited dispersal, reduced gene flow, and accelerating risk of local extinction. This phenomenon corresponds to the “urbanization fragmentation model” proposed by Miles et al.32, which hypothesizes that urban landscapes lead to increased genetic drift within the populations and reduced gene flow between populations. Numerous earlier studies support this model, such as bobcats (Lynx rufus), white-footed mice (Peromyscus leucopus), and Pallas’ long-tongued bat (Glossophaga soricina), where populations inhabiting highly fragmented city landscapes often show lower genetic diversity, stronger differentiation, or elevated inbreeding relative to nearby rural populations33–35.

In contrast, the “urbanization facilitation model” proposed by Miles et al.36as an alternative hypothesis, suggests that urban landscapes may reduce genetic drift within populations and enhance gene flow between populations, particularly for species highly adapted to anthropogenic environments or capable of dispersing via human-mediated pathways. Several studies supported this model, such as mosquito (Aedes albopictus), white clover (Trifolium repens), western black widow spider (Latrodectus hesperus)36–39. Beyond these two alternative models, some studies did not detect any significant effect of urbanization on genetic variation or differentiation40–42. Therefore, the responses to the impacts of urbanization may depend on studied organisms, their life history characteristics, and the historical context and trajectory of urban development32,39,43.

The subtropical island of Taiwan has experienced rapid urban development over the past century, with a high road density of 1.2 km/km244–46. Liu & Tung47 pointed out that Taiwan took only 40 years to achieve the same degree of urbanization (over 75% of the population resides in urban area) in the United States that required almost a century. Unfortunately, this trend of urbanization in Taiwan is likely to accelerate as in many other rapidly developing Asian and African countries at end of the 21st century48–50. Therefore, evaluating the impact of urbanization on biodiversity in Taiwan is crucial for planning mitigation strategies and conservation efforts. In Taiwan, many earlier studies have investigated how urban landscape affected species richness and composition51–55, life history strategies56, and human-wildlife conflict and survival risk of the organisms57–62. These studies indicated that urbanization in Taiwan caused diverse and complex impacts on wildlife. While some birds and mammals can adapt to urban environments, they also face anthropogenic threats, such as road kills, attacks by stray dogs, and limited food resources. However, the specific impact of anthropogenic environments on population genetic variation remains an area requiring systematic investigation. Sun et al.63characterized the demographic history of Taiwan’s leopard cat (Prionailurus bengalensis), and found that population genetic connectivity was influenced by highway systems according to resistance surfaces modeling. Furthermore, Peng et al.64 utilized microsatellite markers to investigate the colony structure of the invasive African big-headed ants (Pheidole megacephala) in central Taiwan. Their results indicated that urban populations exhibited higher genetic differentiation compared to peripheral forest populations, but the genetic diversity indices, such as allelic richness (Ar), expected heterozygosity (HE), or observed heterozygosity (HO), showed no significant difference. These studies indicated that genetic variation in different taxa may respond differently to urbanization pressures. Therefore, to comprehensively understand the impacts of urbanization on the population genetic structure of Taiwanese organisms, it is necessary to expand the scope of research to encompass a broader range of taxa.

Our aim in this study is to assess the effects of urbanization on genetic diversity of Oxyopes sertatus (Araneae, Oxyopidae), one of the most common spiders in urban green spaces and farmlands in Taiwan. We employed mitochondrial Cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI ) gene and genome-wide SNP data generated by restriction site-associated DNA sequencing (RAD-seq) to assess their genetic variation. The COI gene has been extensively sequenced across a wide range of animal taxa as a DNA barcoding marker. The nucleotide diversity (π) of COI can serve as a comparative framework across species and provides a valuable indicator for assessing population decline65–67. Compared with COI—which represents a single, maternally inherited, and non-recombining locus—genome-wide SNP data comprise a large number of loci inherited from both parents. Therefore, SNPs are more sensitive to recent gene flow and population differentiation and are particularly suitable for investigating the genetic consequences of recent urbanization42,68. We focused on O. sertatus because their migration among urban populations may be restricted by man-made barriers (e.g., buildings and roads) since the suitable habitats such as grassland and shrubs are generally limited and poorly connected. Samples were collected from central Taiwan along an urban-rural gradient, ranging from highly developed areas (city parks and campuses) to less developed regions (rural natural habitats in lowland mountains). We tested the predictions derived from the urbanization fragmentation model69: (1) the genetic diversity of O. sertatus populations decreases with increasing urbanization intensity; (2) the genetic differentiation of urban populations of O. sertatus is greater than that of rural populations; and (3) O. sertatus exhibits population genetic structures due to isolation along a urban-rural gradient.

Results

Pattern of land use and urbanization intensity

A total 245 Oxyopes sertatus were collected from 17 populations in central Taiwan along an urban-rural gradient (Fig. 1). To assess the urbanization intensity of each sampling site, we calculated the areas of eight land use types (Agriculture, Forest, Transport, Watercourse, Building, Park, Grassland, and Others; Table S2) within two spatial scales: a 2 × 2 km (landscape scale) and a 0.5 × 0.5 km (local scale) grid cell (see Methods for details). The agriculture, forest, and building were identified as three most dominant land use types across the 17 sampling sites at both landscape and local scales (Table S3–S4; Fig. 1). Based on the correlation matrix of land-use composition (Fig. S2–S3), building and transport infrastructure showed a significant positive correlation with each other. Both of these two land use types were, in turn, significant negatively correlated with forest and agricultural areas in both spatial scales. In addition, the watercourse was positively correlated with grassland in local scale. Some sampling sites contained mostly buildings with few forests (e.g., THU, NMNS, DFP, and FLP; Fig. 1, Tables S3–4), whereas others consisted of a majority of forests and agricultural lands with few buildings and transport infrastructure (e.g., BK, DK14, CLS, JJF, and WFP; Fig. 1, Tables S3–4). The sampling sites near the boundary between urban and natural habitats (e.g., XLC, DHP, BTP, PLS, DKP, DTZ, and DK78) had many buildings as well as forests and agricultural lands.

To examine variation of landscape properties of the sampling sites across the urban-rural gradient, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) at both scales using the area (km2) of these eight land use types as input variables. The PCA of landscape elements across sampling sites revealed that the PC1 explained 47.4% and 46.9% of land use variation at the landscape and local scale, respectively, and broadly represented a gradient in urban intensity (Fig. 2). Sampling sites with higher PC1 values were characterized by larger proportions of artificial structure (building, transport, and park), whereas sites with lower PC1 values contained higher proportions of natural land use types (forest, agriculture, watercourse, and grassland). Because PC1 represented the dominant axis of variation from natural to highly urbanized landscape, we subsequently used the PC1 values of each sampling site as a continuous “urbanization scores” to represent urbanization intensity. The urbanization scores of 17 sampling sites showed gradual changes of the intensity of urbanization: FLP having the highest urbanization intensity in both scales, and BK and DTZ having the lowest urbanization intensity in landscape and local scales, respectively (Table 1).

Genetic diversity

For the COI dataset, we assessed the effect of urbanization on population genetic diversity using nucleotide diversity and haplotype diversity (Hd) (Fig. 3). The results showed that nucleotide diversity did not differ with urbanization intensity at either the landscape scale (t = 0.18, p = 0.86; Fig. 3a) or the local scale (t = -0.40, p = 0.70; Fig. 3b) in linear regression models. Because the nucleotide diversity data contained outliers and deviated from a normal distribution, we additionally fitted robust regression models. In contrast to linear models, the robust regression models revealed a significant negative relationship between nucleotide diversity and urbanization intensity at both the landscape (t = -3.16, p = 0.006; Fig. 3a) and local scales (t = -3.55, p = 0.003; Fig. 3b). Highly urbanized populations (PC1 > 2, e.g., FLP, DFP and THU) had generally low nucleotide diversity, while site NMNS showed comparatively higher value (Table 1). The haplotype diversity also showed no significant relationship with urbanization (landscape: t = -1.42, p = 0.177; local: t = -1.59, p = 0.134; Fig. 3c–d).

For the RAD-seq dataset, we assessed population genetic diversity using expected heterozygosity (HE), observed heterozygosity (HO), and allelic richness (Ar) (Fig. 4). Expected heterozygosity was not significantly associated with urbanization (landscape: t = -1.87, p = 0.08; local: t = -0.84, p = 0.41). In contrast, observed heterozygosity decreased significantly at both the landscape scale (t = -4.00, p = 0.001) and the local scale (t = -2.22, p = 0.043). Allelic richness also declined significantly at the landscape scale (t = -3.32, p = 0.005), but not at the local scale (t = -1.81, p = 0.090). Overall, genome-wide diversity declined with urbanization, with reductions in HO and Ar accounting for most of the effect.

Effects of land use on each genetic diversity index were assessed using multiple linear regression models. At the landscape scale, the most-supported model for nucleotide diversity included forest, grassland, transport and others land use as predictor variables (F = 4.59, p = 0.018). In this model, nucleotide diversity was positively associated with forest, grassland and transport land uses (Table 2). At the local scale, no land use variables were significantly associated with nucleotide diversity. For the haplotype diversity, the most-supported model at the landscape scale included agriculture, forest and grassland as predictor variables (F = 6.94, p = 0.005). Haplotype diversity was significantly higher in populations associated with forest and grassland and lower in populations with greater agriculture land use. At the local scale, the most-supported model included only forest (F = 7.17, p = 0.017), which was positively associated with haplotype diversity (Table 2).

Genetic differentiation

To examine whether O. sertatus shows higher genetic differentiation among urban populations, we calculated pairwise FST among populations and compared mean pairwise FST values among three urbanization regions (urban, suburban, and non-urban; see Methods for detail). The pairwise FST values among O. sertatus populations ranged from 0 to 0.11 for the COI dataset and from 0 to 0.55 for the RAD-seq dataset (Table S5–S6). For the COI dataset, the results showed that the mean FST differed significantly among the three regions (Kruskal-Wallis: χ2 = 6.9, df = 2, p = 0.032; Fig. 5A). Dunn’s post hoc tests indicated that populations in the urban region had significantly higher mean FST than those in the non-urban region (z = -2.40, p = 0.049), while other pairwise comparisons were not significant (urban vs. suburban: z = -0.13, p = 1.00; suburban vs. non-urban: z = -2.29, p = 0.07; Fig. 5A). The RAD-seq dataset showed similar pattern as the COI, with mean FST differed significantly among regions (Kruskal-Wallis: χ2 = 20.2, df = 2, p < 0.001). Dunn’s post hoc tests showed that the mean FST was significantly lower in the non-urban region compared to both the urban (z = -4.44, p < 0.001) and suburban regions (z = -3.21, p = 0.004), whereas no difference was detected between urban and suburban regions (z = -1.37, p = 0.508; Fig. 5B). The Mantel test did not reveal isolation-by-distance (IBD) in either dataset, as pairwise FST values were not correlated with geographic distances (COI: r = -0.127, p = 0.825; RAD-seq: r = -0.149, p = 0.798).

Population structure

STRUCTURE analysis indicated an optimal K = 2, but assigned probabilities showed no clear subdivision, with all individuals clustering into a single genetic group (Fig. S4). A similar result was observed in PCA (Fig. 6), where PC1 and PC2 explained only 3.6% and 3.3% of the total variance, respectively. Most individuals clustered together, and only a small number of individuals were separated from the main cluster. However, discriminant analysis of principal components (DAPC) identified the BK population was a distinct genetic cluster different from all other populations along discriminant function 1 (DF1, 64.5% of variance; Fig. S5). Along DF2 (7.7% of variance), populations from lower-urbanization sites (e.g., JJF, DK14, DK78) were partly separated from those from higher-urbanization sites (e.g., NMNS, FLP), with some overlaps.

Discussion

Urbanization is increasingly recognized as a major driver of environmental change, with growing concern about its negative impacts on wildlife populations. In this study, we demonstrated that recent urban development can impose genetic consequences even on a common, non-web-building spider in a subtropical region. We found that O. sertatus populations in central Taiwan exhibited reduced genetic diversity and greater genetic differentiation with increasing urbanization intensity. Specifically, the observed heterozygosity and allelic richness from RAD-seq data declined significantly along the urbanization gradient. Similarly, nucleotide diversity from the COI dataset also showed a significant negative relationship with urbanization intensity in a robust regression model. Moreover, mean pairwise FST values were higher in urban region compared to non-urban region, consistently observed in both COI and RAD-seq datasets. These results support the “urbanization fragmentation model”32,36and indicate that the primary effect of urbanization on O. sertatus in central Taiwan is population isolation and fragmentation.

Patterns of decreasing genetic diversity and increasing population differentiation in response to urbanization have been reported in multiple taxa, including mammals34,70–72, birds10,73,74, amphibians19,75, and weevils76. Despite variation in dispersal ability and life history strategies, urbanization results in a similarly negative impact on their population connectivity. Natural habitats and green spaces in cities are typically small, isolated and fragmented by impervious surfaces such as buildings and roads. These habitat characteristics of cities not only lead to smaller wildlife populations but also impede their dispersal31,77,78. Consequently, greater genetic drift due to reduced population size, combined with limited gene flow, could result in loss of genetic diversity in urban organisms34,70,79.

The habitats of O. sertatus in Taichung City share these features, such as being small, fragmented, isolated, and surrounded by man-made barriers. Chuang80analyzed land use change in Taichung over the past 30 years, and pointed out that building expansion and grassland loss were major land-use changes. This indicates a severe decline in natural habitat for O. sertatus in the city. Although many human-mediated green spaces exist, they are often limited in size and heterogeneity, and may not provide the open grasslands and herbaceous vegetation preferred by O. sertatus. Therefore, this study suggests that O. sertatus populations have become fragmented and isolated by urban development in recent years, leading to reduced genetic diversity.

In contrast, the effects of urbanization on population genetic structure of O. sertatus were more complex. Although mean FST value was higher among urban populations than among non-urban populations, but not yet produced strong, discrete genetic clusters. Neither STRUCTURE nor PCA analyses revealed distinct genetic structure, indicating that O. sertatus from urban and non-urban population can be considered as single genetic cluster. Only DAPC showed some separation between urban and non-urban populations along DA2, with partial overlap in the suburban regions. Earlier studies of genetic diversity and differentiation have found no general pattern on how urbanization affects the genetic structure of wildlife30. Many studies have demonstrated that reduced genetic connectivity caused by urbanization leads to more genetically structured animal populations, including white-footed mouse in New York City, USA81, Western tiger snake (Notechis scutatus occidentalis) in Perth, Western Australia82, northern two-lined salamander (Eurycea bislineata) and northern dusky salamander (Desmognathus fuscus) around New York City, USA75,83. A classic study is the genetic structure of white-footed mouse in NYC, as the analysis revealed multiple genetic clusters from different parklands, even though the populations were separated by only a few kilometers81. In contrast, our study is in line with a few studies that revealed no or small effect of urbanization on genetic structure, such as song sparrows (Melospiza melodia) in Blacksburg of USA84and great tit (Parus major) in Montpellier of France10. Dispersal ability may be one of the primary factors determining the degree of effect of urbanization on genetic structure. For highly mobile species, like birds and flying insects, they can overcome the restrictions of movement among separated habitats by artificial facilities such as impervious roads and buildings. Furthermore, the age and intensity of city development, human-mediated dispersal, and the organism’s tolerance of urban habitat also affect how urbanization shape the genetic structure. For examples, Rodríguez-Bardía et al.85assessed the genetic diversity and population structure of two bird species with different urban tolerance in Costa Rica, and they found that higher genetic structure in white-eared ground-sparrows (Melozone leucotis, the urban avoider) than in house wrens (Troglodytes aedon, the urban dweller). Tseng86also revealed that yellow fever mosquito (Aedes aegypti) in Southern Taiwan exhibits weak population structure due to human transportation facilitating their dispersal among populations.

The dispersal ability of an organism, geographical scale, and age of urban development may explain the absence of significant population structuring of O. sertatus in central Taiwan. Currently, the dispersal ability of O. sertatus is not well understood, however the genus Oxyopes is known to exhibit ballooning behavior at immature stages, which enables spiderlings to disperse over long distances87. Therefore, the distances between urban green spaces in this study may not constitute a strong dispersal barrier for immature O. sertatus. Furthermore, rapid urban development in central Taiwan began only approximately 60 years ago45. Because genetic structure arises through the gradual accumulation of allele-frequency differences among populations, we suggested that a relatively short period of urbanization may be insufficient to generate pronounced genetic structures in these O. sertatus populations. Taken together, high dispersal potential and the recent onset of intensive urbanization likely contribute to the weak population genetic structure observed in this study.

Given that urbanization was associated with reduced genetic diversity and increased genetic differentiation, it is important to consider the implications of such erosion of genetic variation for population persistence and evolutionary potential. Genetic variation provides a key basis for evolutionary adaptation, fitness, and future viability of urban wildlife populations72,88–91. The rapid global urban development accompanied with climate changes exposes city wildlife to a continuously shifting environment and novel selective pressure. There are many studies that showed the phenotypes (e.g., morphological, behavioral, and physiological traits) of wildlife living in cities are different from those of populations in their natural habitats. Standing variation and new mutations are two pathways for evolutionary adaptation92. The rapid spread of wild type peppered moth (Biston betularia) in dark form during 19th century industrial age is an classic example of evolutionary adaptation through new mutations93,94. Nevertheless, some studies suggested that the standing variation plays a more important role in wildlife adaptation to environmental changes. The standing variation within populations can facilitate more rapid response to selection than waiting for the production of appropriate mutations34,39,92,95–98, particularly in the face of a rapidly changing world driven by anthropogenic pressures99. For an example, Winchell et al.100found that the phenotypic parallelism in response to similar urban environments in the Puerto Rican crested anole (Anolis cristatellus) across three cities is driven by parallel genomic adaptation. They also identified candidate loci associated with divergence in limb length and toepad morphology, as urban populations exhibit parallel increases in these phenotypic traits100. These phenotypic differences are related to locomotor performance, likely serving as targets of selection in urban populations that run on different surfaces compared to forest populations101. These findings point to the selection on standing genetic variation as a key process to rapid adaptation to novel pressures in cities102. However, Miles et al.32reviewed 167 published studies and found a signature of reduced genetic diversity within populations associated with urbanization. These studies suggested that wildlife populations in cities, particularly those with small population sizes, are more susceptible to genetic drift, leading to reduced genetic diversity and increased inbreeding probability. Consequently, this can limit evolutionary potential of wildlife and elevate risk of local extinction.

Although our results clearly show that urbanization is associated with reduced genetic diversity and increased differentiation in Oxyopes sertatus, it is important to acknowledge limitations of using COI and RAD‑seq SNP data to quantify genetic variation. In interpreting these patterns, we have followed the conventional practice in population genetics by treating mitochondrial COI and genome‑wide SNP markers as approximating neutral variation. However, many recent theoretical and empirical studies have challenged the classical neutral theory by showing that genome‑wide patterns of diversity cannot be fully explained by neutral processes alone, and that many seemingly neutral variants are influenced by selection, functional constraint, and antagonistic pleiotropy103–107. In this context, our interpretation of the relationship between genetic variation and urbanization should be considered as a provisional inference made under the widely used neutral (or nearly neutral) framework, rather than as an absolute or universally applicable rule.

Urbanization is considered an environmental filter that favors species preadapted to urban conditions108. These species survive and thrive in cities, becoming “urban exploiters” and exhibiting distinct genetic and morphological responses compared to those observed in O. sertatus in the present study. The western black widow spider has recently expanded across cities in western USA from its native habitat, exhibiting lower genetic differentiation36. Moreover, Lowe et al.109found that urban populations of Nephila plumipes, an orb-weaver spider in Australia, have larger body sizes (e.g., tibia length) and greater ovary weights. This advantage of body sizes and reproduction in cities may be attributed to higher temperature or increased prey availability because nocturnal flying preys are attracted by artificial lights thus enhancing the capture efficiency of N. plumipes. In contrast, the suitable habitats and prey resources for O. sertatus in central Taiwan are less abundant in urban green spaces due to a lack of vegetation heterogeneity and no benefits from increased prey59,110. Therefore, we suggest that O. sertatus likely acts as an “urban avoider” that exhibits a different response to the urban environment as compared to N. plumipes111.

Urban planning to minimize the loss of biodiversity is important to maintain urban ecosystem services (UES) and directly facilitate human well-being112–114. In contrast to the vertebrates, the invertebrates including spiders, are often overlooked in conservation planning of biodiversity in cities. However, the invertebrates play several crucial roles in ecological services such as pollination, pest suppression and trophic interactions in the city115. For examples, invertebrates serve as a primary food resource for many bird species in cities116, and their abundance may influence the reproductive success of these birds117,118. Koh et al.59,60showed that Taiwan barbets (Psilopogon nuchalis) in urban area face weaker predation pressure than those in wild habitats, but they have lower clutch sizes and higher nestling mortality. The reduced reproductive success of urban Taiwan barbets may be attributed to the notably inadequate availability of arthropod prey during brooding period in urban environments. Spiders, one of the most common invertebrates in cities and peri-urban regions, play an important role in ecological dynamic equilibrium as primary predators of various arthropods and natural pest controllers. It has been shown that Oxyopes species serve as pest control agents in agricultural ecosystems119. Preserving spider populations in cities contributes to the enhancement of urban ecosystem services. However, this study demonstrated that urbanization pressure might quickly alter genetic variation of spiders, even for common and abundant species like O. sertatus.

This study highlights that the conservation of urban ecosystem services and biodiversity should be considered in urban planning, design, and management. Given the continued expansion of cities, more studies are required to comprehensively elucidate the effects of urbanization on biodiversity, spanning from the genetic to the ecosystem level. The decline of genetic diversity is detrimental to the long-term survival of wildlife and eventually hampers the function of ecosystem services in cities. Therefore, studies focusing on both neutral and adaptive genetic variation across various taxa and cities are fundamental to predict how urbanization will affect city wildlife in the future. In practical implementation, the green (e.g., green buildings, parks, community gardens) and blue infrastructures (e.g., ponds, stream, wetlands) are demonstrated not only useful for providing hotspot habitats for urban wildlife but also for reducing urban heat island effect and air pollution113,120. It is also important to enhance connectivity among green spaces and natural habitats to reduce population isolation and limitation of dispersal77,121. When creating green spaces, a variety of native species and diverse flora, rather than introduced species, should be considered110,122. Several knowledge gaps remain in urban ecological conservation, including the necessary size of urban green spaces, the components of habitat heterogeneity, the design of ecological corridors, and strategies to avoid ecological traps78, requiring further research. These strategies of urban planning would facilitate to build environmental-friendly and resilient cities that not only benefit wildlife and biodiversity but also enhance physical and mental health of human.

Materials and methods

Sampling

The study was conducted in central Taiwan (Taichung City and Nantou County) from 2020 to 2022, along an urban-rural gradient ranging from city areas to less developed natural habitats (Fig. 1). A total of 245 Oxyopes sertatus from 17 populations were collected using the net-sweep method, in which an insect net is swept repeatedly through vegetation to capture active arthropods. This approach is widely used for sampling spiders and other arthropods in grassland and shrub habitats123. Specimens were preserved in 95% EtOH and stored at -20 °C. Each population included a minimum of seven spider samples (Table S1). All specimens are preserved in Taiwan Biodiversity Research Institute (TBRI).

Landscape characterization and urbanization intensity

The 17 sampling sites, defined by the central point where local spiders were collected, were arranged along an urban-rural gradient, from highly urban development region in the west to predominantly natural habitats in the east (Fig. 1). Accordingly, based on their geographic location along this gradient and overall level of urban development, the sampling sites were classified into three broad regions (urban, suburban, and non-urban). nevertheless, to qualify the finer measure of urbanization intensity, we calculated land use and land cover (LULC) areas within two spatial extents centered on each sampling site: a 2 × 2 km (landscape scale) and a 0.5 × 0.5 km (local scale) grid cell, following the methods of Lowe et al.124and Miles et al.69. These scales were chosen to capture both the broad regional context and the local habitats that may influence spider populations.

Land use was characterized using the Land Use Investigation Maps provided by the National Land Surveying and Mapping Center (NLSC), Ministry of Interior of Taiwan (https://www.nlsc.gov.tw/cl.aspx?n=13705). The original dataset contains a hierarchical system includes nine first-level categories, 48 s-level subcategories, and 93 third-level subcategories. For each sampling site, we calculated the area (km2) of second-level subcategories using QGIS 3.34125. Across all sites, nine first-level categories and 37 s-level subcategories were represented (Fig. S1). For subsequent landscape structure analysis, we reclassified the original complex land-use categories into eight major types based on their ecological relevance: (1) Agriculture; (2) Forest; (3) Transport; (4) Watercourse; (5) Building; (6) Park; (7) Grassland; (8) Others (Table S2; Fig. S1). This reclassification primarily followed the first-level categories of the NLSC system, with specific second-level sub-categories (e.g., parks and grasslands) extracted separately because they represent important habitats or refugia for O. sertatus. Additionally, all built structures were grouped into the “building” class to represent impervious urban cover.

To characterize the relationships among different land use types, we first constructed a correlation matrix based on the area proportion of eight land use categories across sampling sites using function ‘cor’, and visualized the matrix with the function ‘pairs.panels’ in the ‘psych’ R package126. To further examine variation of landscape properties of the sampling sites across the urban-rural gradient, we performed a principal component analysis (PCA) at both scales using the area of these eight land use types as input variables. The first principal component (PC1) captured 47.4% (landscape scale) and 46.9% (local scale) of the variation in land-use composition and was positively associated with artificial structures (building, transport, park and other), but negatively associated with natural environments (forest, agriculture, watercourse and grassland). Because PC1 therefore represented a gradient from natural to highly urbanized landscapes, we used the PC1 score of each site as a continuous urbanization intensity value84,127. Notably, the relative ranking of urbanization intensity across sampling sites may not be consistent between landscape and local spatial scales. The PCA was conducted using ‘PCA’ function in the ‘FactoMineR’ R package128.

DNA extraction and SNP calling

To assess genetic variation in Oxyopes sertatus populations, we utilized both mitochondrial COI sequences and genome-wide SNP data generated by RAD-seq. Genomic DNA was extracted from 1 to 2 legs of each specimen using the FavorPrep™ Tissue Genomic DNA Extraction Mini Kit (Favorgen Biotech, Ping-Tung, Taiwan). The partial COI gene was amplified using primers LCO-1490 and CR-2129,130. The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at 45 °C for 45 s, and elongation at 72 °C for 1 min; and a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Each 25 µL PCR reaction contained 9.5 µL of double-distilled H2O, 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 1 µL of DNA template, and 12.5 µL Taq DNA Polymerase 2× Master Mix RED (Ampliqon, Herlev, Denmark). PCR products were visualized through agarose gel electrophoresis (1% agarose) and sequenced by Genomics BioSci & Tech. Co., Ltd. (New Taipei City, Taiwan) using an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer.

For RAD-seq, 96 specimens (3–6 individuals per site) were randomly selected to be used in the library preparation. Genomic DNA was extracted from four legs of each specimen using the Maxwell® RSC Blood DNA Kit (Promega, USA). DNA concentrations were quantified with the QuantiFluor® dsDNA System (Promega, USA). The RAD-seq libraries were prepared following the Multiplexed Shotgun Genotyping protocol131. All DNA extractions were standardized to 50 ng DNA/sample. Double-stranded DNA was digested with the restriction enzyme MseI (a 4-base restriction enzyme: 5’ ... T^TAA ... 3’; 3’ ... AAT^T ... 5’) in a total reaction volume of 20 µl. DNA fragments between 300 and 450 bp were selected with Pippin Prep (Sage Science, USA) and enriched by 14 PCR cycles. The DNA library was sequenced in paired-end 150 bp reactions by NovaSeq 6000 System (Illumina, USA). Demultiplexing was performed based on barcodes with no mismatches allowed. Following quality control, which discarded reads containing more than five low-quality bases (Q < 20), loci identification and genotyping were conducted using the de novo assembly pipeline implemented in ipyrad v0.9.61132as no sufficiently closely related and high-quality reference genome was available. Reads were clustered at an 80% similarity threshold using VSEARCH133. A minimum read depth of 6x was applied for statistical base calling. To minimize missing data, loci were filtered by 25% of completeness per locus. Additionally, loci exhibiting more than 20% polymorphic sites or exceeding 8 indels were excluded to filter out potential paralogs. 14 samples were excluded due to low read numbers. The final RAD-seq dataset contained 82 samples, 2,422 loci and 29,353 SNPs.

Data analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R134. Genetic diversity within populations was separately estimated from the COI and RAD-seq datasets. For the COI dataset, nucleotide diversity (π) and haplotype diversity (Hd) were calculated for each population using the functions ‘nuc.div’ and ‘hap.div’in the R package ‘pegas’135. For the RAD-seq dataset, expected heterozygosity (HE) and observed heterozygosity (HO) were calculated from the ‘Hs’ and ‘Ho’ components of the output of the functions ‘basic.stats’ in the R package ‘hierfstat’136, and allelic richness (Ar) was calculated using the function ‘allelic.richness’ in the same package. Subsequently, the relationships between each genetic diversity index and urbanization intensity were evaluated using separate linear regression models. For each model, the visual inspection of the model residuals and Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to check for normality, as well as Bartlett’s test to evaluate heteroscedasticity. Given that the nucleotide diversity data contained outliers and did not fit a normal distribution, we additionally fitted robust regression models using the function ‘lmrob’ in the R package ‘robustbase’137and report these results alongside the ordinary least-squares estimates.

To assess the influence of land-use properties on genetic diversity, we fitted separate linear models for each genetic diversity index at both landscape and local scales. Response variables were five genetic diversity indices, and predictor variables were areas (km2) of the eight land use types. For each response variable and spatial scale, we used function ‘dredge’ in the R package ‘MuMIn’138to generate multiple candidate linear models that included all possible additive combinations of the eight land‑use predictors. This approach allowed us to compare alternative hypotheses about which land-use types were most strongly associated with each diversity index. Candidate models were ranked by corrected Akaike’s information criterion (AICc), and the model with the lowest AICc was selected as the best-supported model. After fitting the models, we evaluated model assumptions by examining diagnostic residual plots and assessing the normality and homoscedasticity of residuals using Shapiro-Wilk test and Bartlett’s test, respectively. To avoid problems of multicollinearity among predictors, we assessed variance inflation factor (VIF) using the function ‘vif’ in the R package ‘car’139and confirmed that all VIF values were below four.

Pairwise population differentiation was investigated via pairwise fixation index (FST). Pairwise FST values were estimated using the function ‘pairwise_Gst_Nei’ in the R package ‘mmod’140for the COI dataset and the function ‘Fst’ in the R package ‘genepop’141for the RAD-seq dataset. To compare the genetic differentiation across urbanization regions, the 17 populations were grouped into three regions (urban [n = 6], suburban [n = 6], non-urban5; Table 1; Fig. 1) based on locality and scores from the landscape PCA. Differences in mean pairwise FST values among regions were assessed using a Kruskal-Wallis test. A Dunn’s test with Bonferroni correction was subsequently performed as a post-hoc analysis to determine which regional pairs were significantly different. Finally, we examined whether pairwise FST values among populations were correlated with geographic distances (isolation-by-distance, IBD). Geographic distances were was calculated with the function ‘st_distance’ in the R package ‘sf’142,143. Mantel tests were conducted with 999 permutations using the function ‘mantel.rtest’ in the R package ‘ade4’144,145.

Population structure was investigated from RAD-seq dataset with Bayesian algorithm in program STRUCURE v.2.3.4146. Number of clusters (K) were simulated from one to ten with ten replicates for each K. A total of 100,000 iterations were simulated, and 50,000 iterations were set as burn-in. The results were summarized using CLUMPP147with CLUMPAK server148. We also performed Principal Component Analysis (PCA) using the function ‘dudi.pca’ in the R package ‘ade4’144,145. The result from PCA was visualized using R package ‘factoMineR’128. Finally, we performed Discriminant Analysis of Principal Components (DAPC) to detect subtle genetic structures. DAPC was conducted using the function ‘dapc’ in the R package ‘Adegenet’149with all 17 putative populations, and the first two principal components were selected to discriminate groups.
