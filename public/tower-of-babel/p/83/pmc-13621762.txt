Beyond semen analysis: in men with normal semen parameters telomere attrition and oxidative imbalance distinguish those fertile from those with infertility
Authors: Maria Santa Rocca, Andrea Di Nisio, Ilaria Cosci, Graziana Assalve, Giuseppe Grande, Andrea Graziani, Alessandra Ferramosca, Alberto Ferlin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStandard semen analysis has little prognostic value in distinguishing fertile from infertile men. Furthermore, in men with semen parameters within the reference ranges, it cannot distinguish fertile men from infertile men, i.e. partners of couples with idiopathic infertility. Oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, sperm telomere shortening, and DNA fragmentation have been proposed as contributors to impaired male fertility. However, these biomarkers have not been evaluated as a whole in subjects with normal semen parameters, partners of fertile and infertile couples.ObjectiveTo characterise a multidimensional panel of sperm biomarkers—including sperm telomere length (STL), mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNAcn), reactive oxygen species (ROS), lipid peroxidation (LP), sperm chromatin dispersion (SCD), and respiratory control ratio (RCR)—and to identify whether these parameters might discriminate, in men with normal semen parameters, infertile men from fertile controls.MethodsA total of 150 men with semen parameters within the normal ranges were enrolled: 47 male partners of couples with idiopathic infertility and 103 fertile controls. STL and mtDNAcn were quantified by qPCR; ROS were assessed using OxiSperm®II with semiquantitative imaging; LP was measured spectrophotometrically in seminal plasma; SCD was used to determine DNA fragmentation; and mitochondrial function was evaluated by oxygen consumption and RCR. Correlations between biomarkers and semen parameters were analysed using Pearson or Spearman coefficients, and intergroup comparisons were adjusted for age.ResultsSemen parameters did not differ significantly between male partners of fertile and infertile couples. compared to men of fertile couples, men of infertile couples exhibited significantly shorter STL (0.57 ± 0.59 vs 1.21 ± 1.13, p_adj = 0.001) and higher oxidative stress, with both ROS (8300.9 ± 4214.8 vs 6555.3 ± 3394.3, p_adj = 0.027) and LP (88.33 ± 55.50 vs 40.29 ± 46.98, p_adj < 0.001) markedly elevated. mtDNAcn, SCD, and RCR showed no difference between the groups. Across the entire cohort, STL correlated negatively with ROS and LP and positively with RCR. ROS correlated negatively with total sperm count, motility and RCR, and positively with LP. LP displayed the strongest pattern of associations, correlating negatively with concentration, total count, motility, STL and RCR, and positively with age and volume.ConclusionsAmong men with normal semen analysis, partners of couples with idiopathic infertility exhibit a distinct sperm molecular profile characterised by telomere shortening and oxidative imbalance. STL, ROS and LP emerged as age-independent biomarkers associated with infertility status and showed promising discriminatory ability in this cohort, supporting their integration as second-level tests to complement routine semen analysis in cases of normozoospermia.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Infertility is a complex disorder affecting approximately 8–12% of couples of reproductive age, with male factor accounting for about half of the cases [1–5].

Although the aetiology of male factor infertility (MFI) is a widely discussed topic and is the focus of many studies aiming to find new biomarkers for early and precise diagnosis, about 10–30% of male infertility (MI) cases remain unexplained (UMI) [6]. Genetic factors account for 10–15% of severe forms of MFI [7], suggesting that unidentified genetic defects could also be responsible for some milder or idiopathic cases. Consequently, molecular analyses uncovering new mechanisms that impair fertility have become increasingly important.

An essential prerequisite for a successful reproductive outcome is an intact sperm genome [8]. Recent studies have suggested that reduced sperm telomere length (STL) may lead to segregation errors and, consequently, genomic instability, which can result in male infertility [9]. Precisely, over the last few decades, several studies on infertile men have shown that telomere shortening correlates with impaired spermatogenesis, suggesting that telomere length may be a potential fertility biomarker [10–12]; however, it is not entirely clear whether the shortening of telomeres is the cause or the effect of reduced fertility [13].

Telomeres are tandem nucleotide repeats (TTAGG) complexed with Shelterin proteins, located at the ends of eukaryotic chromosomes. Their primary function is to maintain genomic stability by protecting genomic material from premature degradation during cell division. However, because of the inability of DNA polymerase to replicate the single-strand overhang [14], telomeres can be mistaken for double-strand breaks by the DNA repair machinery [15, 16]; therefore, the telomerase enzyme is essential for preserving telomere length by adding new telomeric repeats to the 3′ ends of each chromosome [17]. Nevertheless, telomerase activity gradually declines during cell differentiation; as a result, telomeres shorten until they reach the “crisis” point, after which the cell enters senescence [18].

Unlike somatic cells, germ cells have longer telomeres because the telomerase remains active longer [19], as a proper telomere length is required for transmission to offspring [20, 21]. Interestingly, mature spermatozoa display telomeres that are longer than those of their precursor cells, even though telomerase activity is silenced at this stage of maturation. One possible explanation is the activation of alternative lengthening (ALT) via telomere exchange, which helps preserve genomic stability [22].

Despite the complex architecture of telomeres, they are preferentially targeted by reactive oxygen species (ROS) due to their high guanine content, making them susceptible to oxidative damage [23, 24], which can affect genomic integrity [25].

ROS are usually generated by sperm metabolism, as they play a vital role in key processes by triggering redox reactions in proteins involved in sperm function, such as capacitation, hyperactivation, the acrosome reaction, and fertilisation [26]. Regardless of the source, an excess of ROS molecules can be toxic to cells. In fact, excessive ROS production due to an unhealthy lifestyle can also result from gut microbiota dysbiosis, leading to endotoxemia, which has been found to positively correlate with the formation of 8-OHdG in sperm DNA, suggesting a link between microbiota alteration, ROS, and sperm damage. [27–31].

Therefore, maintaining a balanced ratio between oxidant and antioxidant agents is essential for sperm health [32]. Mitochondria are a source of endogenous ROS [33]; these organelles are involved in numerous molecular mechanisms, making their role pivotal, particularly in cells with high-energy demands [34, 35].

As in other cells, mitochondria in sperm are crucial for maintaining cell homeostasis. Interestingly, sperm mitochondria’s shape is quite unusual; during spermatogenesis, these organelles undergo morphological changes, and at the end of sperm maturation, they elongate and are all wrapped around the axoneme in the sperm midpiece [36]. Moreover, during this process, the number of mitochondria is reduced through phagocytosis of removal of residual bodies by Sertoli cells [37]. The phagocytosis of sperm mitochondria is a fundamental process following fertilisation, as it ensures that a reduced number of sperm mitochondria enter the oocyte and are targeted for degradation, a necessary step to prevent the paternal mitochondria transmission to the embryo [37–39].

Although sperm mitochondria are not inherited by offspring, their functionality is a crucial prerequisite for successful fertilisation, as they ensure that the sperm cell can function correctly [40].

Impaired mitochondria produce excessive ROS [41], which can damage both nuclear DNA (nDNA) and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and sperm membranes by lipid peroxidation [42].

Recently, mtDNA copies (mtDNAcn) have been associated with impaired sperm quality and quantity [43–45]; however, it remains unclear whether altered mtDNA copies, alone or in combination with other pathological factors, may affect male fertility.

Routine semen analysis is the first diagnostic test in the evaluation of MFI [4, 46]. However, it cannot predict with accuracy the fertility potential of a man and cannot clearly distinguish between fertile and infertile men [47, 48]. Furthermore, no information can be derived on the fertility potential in men with semen parameters within the normal ranges. In fact, it is well known that “normal” semen parameters might be found in fertile men, but also in infertile couples, which are therefore classified as idiopathic when also female factors have been excluded.

To date, no study has simultaneously evaluated sperm telomere length (STL), sperm DNA integrity, and several key markers of mitochondrial function, including mtDNA copies (mtDNAcn), balanced ROS levels, low lipid peroxidation (LP), and respiratory control ratio (RCR) in men with semen parameters within the normal ranges comparing fertile men (controls) with infertile men (idiopathic couple infertility).

Material and methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University Hospital of Padova (PROT N. AOP2845), and written informed consent was obtained from each volunteer. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Subjects

The subjects were consecutively enrolled at the Unit of Andrology and Reproductive Medicine at the University Hospital of Padua between January 2024 and March 2025.

Inclusion criteria for controls (n = 103) and patients (n = 47) were: age between 18 and 50 years, semen parameters within the normal ranges (5th percentiles of the WHO guidelines), absence of risk factors for infertility [5], normal semen culture and absence of sperm autoantibodies. Controls were proven fertile (at least one pregnancy in the year before), whereas patients were partners of infertile couples, defined as no pregnancy after at least one year of regular, unprotected sexual intercourses.

Sperm preparation

Semen analysis was performed according to the World Health Organisation guidelines (WHO, 2021), after 2–7 days of sexual abstinence. Additionally, sperm were isolated using Percoll for molecular and biochemical assays.

STL and mtDNA copies measurement

Sperm DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Milan, Italy) with minor modifications [11]. DNA concentration and quality were assessed using NanoDrop1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Both STL and mtDNA copies were measured using a qPCR-based method as described previously (ScienCell AHDQ #8958) [49]. Briefly, mtDNA and Telomere primer sets were used to amplify a conserved mitochondrial region of mtDNA and telomeric sequence, respectively, while the single-copy reference (SCR) primer set was used as a reference gene. Both were run under the same conditions and performed on a StepOne Plus using the corresponding software. Samples were run concurrently in triplicate. The Delta cycle threshold (CT) method was applied to calculate the results, which were then compared with a pool of 10 normozoospermic fertile males used as the reference.

Seminal ROS evaluation

OxiSperm®II (Halotech DNA, Madrid, Spain) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After separation of seminal plasma from the sperm pellet by centrifugation, the sperm pellet is resuspended in 50 μl of PBS. Following a last spin, the sperm pellet is resuspended in 7.5 μl of sperm reactivity induction solution and left at room temperature for 5’. Five microlitres of each sample (neat, seminal plasma, and spermatozoa) is added to the membrane base and incubated at room temperature for 15’. The colour reactivity of the membrane gel for each semen component was compared with the colour of the scheme. Negative controls were run at each analysis by placing the same volume of PBS onto the control membrane. Semen samples were classified into LOW, MEDIUM or HIGH oxidative stress levels based on the colour developed after incubation with the reactive gel. A reference image is used to compare the colour of each sample to a standardised colour scale. ImageJ software was used exclusively for semiquantitative measurement of ROS on sperm.

Sperm chromatin dispersion (SCD) test

Sperm DNA fragmentation was measured using HaloSperm G2®(Halotech DNA, Madrid, Spain) as previously described by Fernandez et al. [50], and the degree of DNA dispersion was assessed by bright field microscopy. A minimum of 200 spermatozoa was evaluated and classified as having small, medium, or large halos, as well as degraded or absent halos. Sperm DNA fragmentation index (DFI) percentage was calculated with the following equation: [fragmented (small and absent halos) + degraded/200] × 100. A pooled control comprised of 10 randomly selected participant samples was stained and analysed at the time of each test to ensure consistency within the assay and reagents.

Lipoperoxides (LP) sperm test

The determination of LP was performed on seminal plasma samples. The analyses were performed by the LP Sperm Test Kit (Diacron International, Grosseto, Italy) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [51]. The test is based on the ability of peroxides to promote the oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+. The peroxidation product bound to the chromogen, forming a coloured complex, was measured spectrophotometrically at an OD of 505 nm using a Berthold luminometer. The increase in absorbance was directly proportional to the concentration of LP in the sample. A standard sample consisting of ferric chloride solution (1000 uEq/L) and a blank, in which the plasma is replaced by an equal amount of ddH2O, was analysed according to the procedure described above. The concentration of LP was expressed as µmol/L.

RCR

Mitochondrial respiration studies

Spermatozoa were isolated by centrifugation at 800 g for 10 minutes at room temperature and then resuspended in an isotonic salt solution containing 2 g/L bovine serum albumin, 113 mM KCl, 12.5 mM KH₂PO₄, 2.5 mM K₂HPO₄, 3 mM MgCl₂, 0.4 mM EDTA, and 20 mM Tris adjusted to pH 7.4 with HCl. After washing, the cells were subjected to hypotonic treatment to assess mitochondrial function. Oxygen consumption was measured at 36 °C using a Clark-type oxygen electrode (Hansatech Instruments, King’s Lynn, UK). During the measurements, 106 spermatozoa were provided with 10 mM pyruvate and 10 mM malate as respiratory substrates, along with 0.76 mM ADP to stimulate oxidative phosphorylation. The mitochondrial respiration rate (V) was expressed as nmol O₂ x ml− 1 x min− 1. To assess the efficiency of mitochondrial respiration, the respiratory control ratio (RCR) was calculated as the ratio between V₃ (oxygen uptake measured in the presence of substrates and ADP, representing state 3 respiration) and V₄ (oxygen uptake measured with substrates alone, corresponding to state 4 respiration) [52].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and as percentages for categorical variables. Prior to analysis, the distribution of all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test to determine normality.

For variables displaying a normal distribution, Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was used to evaluate the association between molecular/biochemical biomarkers (STL, mtDNAcn, ROS, LP, SCD, RCR) and semen parameters (volume, concentration, total sperm count, motility, vitality, morphology). For variables not normally distributed, Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ) was applied. Correlation results are reported with both p-values and their respective correlation coefficients.

Comparisons between fertile and infertile men were conducted using non-parametric tests (Mann–Whitney U test) because several biomarkers showed non-normal distributions. Variables that differed significantly between groups were subsequently analysed using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with age included as a covariate, to account for the effect of age on oxidative and telomere-related markers.

Associations between lifestyle variables (tobacco smoking, alcohol consumption, coffee intake, physical activity) and ROS levels were assessed using independent-samples t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, depending on variable normality. Categorical variables (e.g., BMI > 25 kg/m2, smoking yes/no) were compared using Pearson’s χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test when appropriate.

All tests were two-tailed, and the significance threshold was set at p < 0.05. No corrections for multiple testing were applied, as the study was exploratory in nature and aimed at identifying potential biomarkers for further validation.

To evaluate the discriminatory power of molecular and biochemical biomarkers in distinguishing infertile men from fertile controls, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analyses were performed for all parameters that showed significant differences between groups—namely STL, ROS, and LP. ROC curves were generated using a nonparametric approach, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated to quantify overall diagnostic accuracy. For biomarkers inversely associated with infertility, AUC values below 0.50 were interpreted as indicating inverse discrimination, meaning that lower values of the biomarker are associated with the infertile condition; in these cases, the complementary value (1 − AUC) was also considered for clinical interpretation.

For each biomarker, the optimal cut-off value was determined using the Youden Index (J = sensitivity + specificity − 1), which identifies the point on the ROC curve that maximises the combined sensitivity and specificity. For consistency across biomarkers, 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for AUC, sensitivity, and specificity.

Comparisons between AUC values were performed using the DeLong method, enabling pairwise evaluation of diagnostic performance among STL, ROS, and LP.

All ROC analyses were conducted using SPSS (v.26); significance was set at p < 0.05, and biomarkers with AUC ≥ 0.70 were considered to have acceptable discriminatory capacity.

ROS also demonstrated significant discriminatory performance, with an AUC of 0.684 (95% CI: 0.582–0.786; p = 0.001). The optimal cut-off for ROS was 7189, yielding a Youden Index of 0.332, consistent with moderate diagnostic utility with a sensitivity = 0.658 and specificity = 0.674. Although less accurate than LP, ROS still provides clinically meaningful discrimination and aligns with its central role in oxidative stress–related sperm dysfunction. In contrast, STL showed an AUC of 0.201 (95% CI: 0.107–0.294; p < 0.001), a value < 0.5 due to the inverse relationship between STL and infertility (shorter STL = higher infertility risk); because shorter STL was associated with infertility, this value reflects inverse discrimination rather than lack of diagnostic signal. Accordingly, the complementary AUC (0.799) indicates good discriminatory ability when lower STL values are interpreted as higher risk. Thus, the directionality of prediction must be reversed when interpreting STL; once inverted, STL aligns with its significant between-group reduction and remains biologically relevant, although less accurate than oxidative markers, with a calculated cut-off of 0.5 RQ (Relative Quantification), specificity = 0.767 and sensitivity = 0.723.

Comparison of AUC values confirmed that LP significantly outperformed both ROS and STL, supporting the predominance of lipid peroxidation as the best molecular discriminator of infertility in men with normal semen parameters. Overall, ROC analyses highlight oxidative stress–related parameters—especially LP—as the most reliable biomarkers for identifying infertility beyond standard semen analysis.

Discussion

This study provides the first integrated evaluation of biochemical and molecular sperm parameters in men with normal semen parameters, partners of fertile or infertile couples. We found that the biochemical and molecular profiles of sperm of infertile couples differed substantially from those of fertile controls, underscoring the limitations of routine semen analysis in capturing subclinical sperm dysfunction.

Over the last year, in fact, various markers have been proposed to provide more detailed information on sperm quality and function than standard semen analysis, which offers a general view of the reproductive tract’s health but has limited prognostic value for the couple’s reproductive potential.

Our study, therefore, not only confirms that normal semen parameters cannot predict fertility, but also shows that partners of infertile couples have sperm characteristics suggestive of worse sperm function, identifying which of the recently proposed sperm quality indicators might most accurately predict male infertility.

Sperm DNA quality is a paramount indicator of sperm reproductive health, as genomic integrity is essential for successful fertilisation and embryonic development, however, inflammation, environmental pollution and a poor lifestyle can significantly increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, which, in turn, can damage both sperm DNA and mitochondria [53].

Excessive ROS levels, in fact, along with alterations in mtDNA content (mtDNA cn) and sequence, and respiration defects, are typical hallmarks of mitochondrial dysfunction and may, therefore, contribute to male infertility [54].

Our study is the first to evaluate mtDNA cn in infertile men compared with healthy fertile men, unlike most studies that examined only healthy men as controls for whom no information on fertility status was available [55–59]. Although we found no difference in mtDNA cn between patients and controls, mtDNA cn was positively associated with semen volume and negatively associated with sperm concentration. The negative correlation between mtDNA cn and sperm concentration confirmed findings from earlier studies [57–62]. In contrast, no correlation with semen volume was reported, probably because no study has considered this macroscopic semen parameter in relation to mtDNA cn, except for Zhang et al. 2016, who, however, found no correlation. Since semen volume depends on the amount of seminal fluid produced by the accessory sex glands, and high seminal fluid can lower sperm concentration [63], the negative association that we found between mtDNA cn and sperm concentration might be due to increased semen volume. However, we are unable to explain why mtDNA cn is positively associated with semen volume.

Furthermore, although ROS can compromise mitochondrial functionality, no correlation between mtDNA cn and ROS levels was observed in our study. The latter data could be explained by the fact that excessive ROS might primarily cause mutations in mtDNA rather than increasing copy number.

Intriguingly, the most important finding of our study is that oxidative imbalance is a defining feature of infertility in men with normal semen parameters. ROS levels were, in fact, significantly higher in infertile subjects and were associated with reduced total sperm count, lower motility, and diminished mitochondrial respiratory efficiency.

A particularly interesting finding of this study is the significant negative correlation between ROS present in sperm pellet and RCR; however, no correlation between ROS in semen plasma and RCR was observed. This finding indeed suggests that high ROS levels generated by dysfunctional sperm mitochondria are more harmful to the mitochondria than ROS produced by leucocytes. Additionally, our results have also confirmed that tobacco smoking and alcohol consumption significantly increase ROS levels, emphasising that good lifestyle habits are crucial for reproductive health.

ROS are also responsible for lipid peroxidation (LP) of sperm membranes, given their high content of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs). The increase in LP observed in infertile men, together with its strong negative correlations with concentration, total count, motility, STL, and RCR, indeed suggests that lipid oxidative damage is a central downstream mediator of ROS-induced sperm dysfunction. Moreover, the positive correlation we observed between LP and semen volume is consistent with previous findings [64, 65] and may depend on sperm cell oxidative status. In fact, increased semen volume could be due to an inflammatory condition of the accessory glands, leading to more leukocytes, which are the main source of ROS. LP is a reaction particularly harmful to sperm membranes, as alterations in lipid membrane composition can affect not only sperm morphology but also functionality, reducing membrane fluidity and, consequently, the fertilisation rate [66]. Consistent with this evidence, we found that LP negatively correlated with most semen parameters, STL and RCR, whilst it correlated positively with age and ROS. The negative association between LP and RCR is notable, as it supports the hypothesis that mitochondrial membrane peroxidation may impair oxidative phosphorylation efficiency—a mechanism previously demonstrated in animal models but rarely documented in human sperm.

Our findings, hence, corroborate those of previous studies reporting higher LP percentages in infertile men and the association of LP with age, ROS and STL [64, 67–69]; interestingly, to our knowledge, the negative correlation between LP and RCR in idiopathic infertile men is a novel finding. In fact, the hypothesis that the inner mitochondrial membrane, where the enzymes of OXPHOS are located, may be damaged by LP, and its subsequent impact on respiration efficiency, has only been proposed in sperm of high-fat-fed rats [70].

Intriguingly, the ROC analysis allowed us to establish cut-off values for LP and ROS parameters predictive of infertility, suggesting that both could be markers with diagnostic properties. Among the two parameters, LP showed the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.812 and an optimal cut-off of 46.6 µEq/L. This value identifies a threshold above which infertility is substantially more likely, even in men with normal semen parameters. ROS also showed acceptable discriminative ability, with an AUC of 0.684 and an optimal cut-off of 7189 IntDen. This study thus provides the first reference values to guide the interpretation of oxidative stress tests in clinical practice.

Of note, this study is, to our knowledge, the first to propose a quantitative cut-off for ROS measured with OxiSperm®II using a semiquantitative image-based approach, thereby reducing operator dependency and enhancing the assay’s potential clinical utility [71].

Particular attention to ROS levels is essential because they provide insights into both mitochondrial health and sperm genome integrity. Excessive ROS levels can indeed damage telomeres, leading to telomere attrition through the formation of 8-oxoG [25]. STL is a biomarker that has been extensively studied over the last few decades and has been suggested as a predictive indicator of male fertility when it exceeds 1.0 RQ [72]. In our study, STL showed a negative correlation with LP and ROS, suggesting that telomeric sperm DNA can be affected under stress conditions.

Although several studies have hypothesised an effect of ROS on STL, only Mishra et al. have reported a significant negative correlation between ROS and STL in infertile men, concluding that chronic exposure to severe oxidative stress may cause telomere erosion, while mild ROS levels may preserve STL [72, 73]; however, ROS assessment in Mishra’s study, in contrast to ours, was limited to neat semen and, furthermore, about the 38% of their subjects had altered semen parameters.

Moreover, contrary to our expectations, no correlation between STL and SCD, and between ROS and SCD was observed. Although ROS-induced DNA damage is well established, the lack of correlation observed in our study may be due to the method used to detect DNA fragmentation. In fact, SCD is an indirect, operator-dependent method and therefore less sensitive [71]. Therefore, the absence of a link between ROS and SCD should be interpreted with caution. It may depend on limitations of the SCD assay in detecting chromatin damage induced by oxidative stress. Nevertheless, Patel. et al. (2025) reported that 6 of 13 studies included in the meta-analysis showed no correlation between STL and DNA fragmentation, and that this correlation was not observed, regardless of the tests, demonstrating that the findings are not always concordant.

Comparison between STL findings and previous studies shows that our results are inconsistent or not entirely comparable, and this may be due to the study’s design, the subjects analysed, and the methods used [9, 72]. In fact, this is a case-control study in which the reproductive health of all individuals is known, and the semen analysis was normal. Furthermore, semen evaluation was performed according to the WHO 2021 guidelines, and SCD, rather than the TUNEL assay, was used to assess DNA fragmentation.

Although ROC analysis was not primarily designed to define a telomere-length threshold and the directionality of the STL curve requires cautious interpretation, the consistent group difference and strong correlations with oxidative markers support the relevance of STL as a complementary indicator rather than a standalone diagnostic test.

The main strengths of this study, hence, include the well-characterised case–control design with clearly defined fertile and idiopathic infertile groups, all evaluated using WHO 2021 criteria, and the simultaneous assessment of multiple, mechanistically linked biomarkers—STL, mtDNA copy number, ROS, LP, SCD and RCR. Limitations of this study include a small sample size and a lack of data on mtDNA mutations. However, the smaller sample size is partly due to the recruitment of the infertile group based on strict inclusion criteria (normal parameters according to the WHO 2021 guidelines, but persistent infertility), which naturally limited the number of eligible participants during the study period. Therefore, the proposed biomarker thresholds should be considered preliminary and require external validation in larger, independent cohorts. Although the infertile group was relatively modest in size, post-hoc power estimates based on the observed effect sizes suggested that the available sample was sufficient to detect the main differences observed for STL and LP, while the ROS comparison appeared less stable and should be considered preliminary. Accordingly, external validation in larger cohorts remains necessary before these biomarkers can be considered for broader clinical use. Second, because the study was cross-sectional, it identifies associations rather than causal relationships. Accordingly, we cannot determine whether telomere shortening and oxidative imbalance are drivers of infertility or biological correlates of an already altered reproductive condition.

Due to the limited amount of biological material, we opted to perform a restricted number of tests that could still provide insights into both sperm mitochondrial and genomic health. The SCD assay, while widely used, is relatively insensitive to oxidative-specific DNA damage [71].

In conclusion, this is the first study to assess molecular and biochemical factors in a large cohort of men with normal semen parameters, identifying STL, ROS, and LP as the most suitable biomarkers for diagnosing infertility. Our results indeed showed that these three parameters differed significantly between infertile and fertile subjects, regardless of age.

Although we cannot definitively state whether telomere shortening is a primary cause of infertility or a secondary consequence of a heightened oxidative environment, to which the sperm cell is exposed, our findings suggest that the ROS level could likely be a good predictor of male reproductive status,. Indeed, despite the lack of mechanistic proofs, this study identified objective molecular markers in men who would be classified as fertile by standard WHO semen analysis. From a clinical standpoint, our findings support the concept that infertile men with normal semen parameters, commonly defined as idiopathic, harbour a distinct molecular and biochemical signature that routine semen analysis cannot reveal. The identification of LP and ROS thresholds associated with infertility provides practical reference values that could be used to flag patients with clinically relevant oxidative stress. In particular, LP > 46.6 µEq/L and ROS > 7189 IntDen, as observed in our cohort, may indicate a substantially increased probability of infertility, prompting clinicians to consider lifestyle counselling or antioxidant strategies. STL, while less suitable as a single diagnostic marker, complements this information by reflecting cumulative oxidative and mitochondrial stress at the genomic level. Nonetheless, future research should involve larger and more representative cohorts to validate our findings.

Overall, incorporating these second-level biomarkers into the diagnostic workup has the potential to improve risk stratification, personalise management and ultimately enhance reproductive outcomes for couples facing unexplained infertility.
