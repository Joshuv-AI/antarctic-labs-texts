Modulating amacrine cell–derived dopamine signaling promotes optic nerve regeneration and preserves visual function
Authors: Qi Zhang, Jingfei Xue, Jiahui Tang, Siting Wu, Zhe Liu, Caiqing Wu, Canying Liu, Yidan Liu, Jicheng Lin, Jiaxu Han, Liyan Liu, Yuze Chen, Jinpeng Yang, Zhidong Li, Ling Zhao, Yantao Wei, Yiqing Li, Yehong Zhuo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
As part of the central nervous system, the optic nerve, composed of axons from retinal ganglion cells (RGCs), generally fails to regenerate on its own when injured in adult mammals. An innovative approach to promoting optic nerve regeneration involves manipulating the interactions between amacrine cells (ACs) and RGCs. Here, we identified a unique AC subtype, dopaminergic ACs (DACs), that responded early after optic nerve crush by down-regulating neuronal activity and reducing retinal dopamine (DA) release. Activating DACs or augmenting DA release with levodopa demonstrated neuroprotective effects and modestly enhanced axon regeneration. Within this context, we pinpointed the DA receptor D1 (DRD1) as a critical mediator of DAC-derived DA and showed that RGC-specific Drd1 overexpression effectively overcame subtype-specific barriers to regeneration. This strategy markedly boosted RGC survival and axon regeneration after crush and preserved vision in a glaucoma model. This study unveils the crucial role of DAC-derived DA signaling in optic nerve regeneration, holding promise for therapeutic insights into neural repair.

INTRODUCTION

Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) serve as key nodes in the visual pathway, transmitting visually evoked electrical signals from the retina to distant brain targets via their axons. Because of the notable distance between the retina and the brain, the optic nerve, composed of RGC axons, becomes susceptible to various forms of injury, including traumatic and ischemic optic neuropathy, optic neuritis, and glaucoma (1, 2). Similar to other pathways in the mammalian central nervous system (CNS), the axons of adult RGCs have limited intrinsic regenerative potential (3, 4). Moreover, the surrounding glial environment inhibits axon growth (5, 6), resulting in permanent visual function deficits after injury.

Efforts to promote axon regeneration and preserve visual function have primarily focused on overcoming the limited regrowth capacity of adult RGCs (7–11) and transforming the inhibitory extrinsic environment into a permissive one (12–14). However, the crucial role of retinal interneurons, which have intimate connections with RGCs, following optic nerve injury remains largely unexplored. Initial research found that amacrine cells (ACs) inhibit the axon growth of neonatal RGCs when cocultured in direct contact (15). Subsequent studies reported that ACs respond to optic nerve injury and ultimately hinder RGC axon regeneration through the presynaptic release of mobile zinc (Zn2+) (16–18) or inhibitory neurotransmitters such as γ-aminobutyric acid and glycine (19). These findings suggest that ACs actively participate in axon regeneration after injury through various mechanisms (20, 21).

Dopamine (DA) is a vital neurotransmitter found throughout the CNS and plays a pivotal role in numerous physiological processes, including learning, memory, attention, and mood regulation (22, 23). It acts by binding to neuronal DA receptors, which can either excite or inhibit neuronal activity, depending on the receptor family subtypes involved (24, 25). Under pathological conditions, DA is implicated in the development of certain neurological and psychiatric disorders, such as Parkinson’s disease, addiction, and schizophrenia (26, 27). DA is synthesized and released by dopaminergic neurons, which express tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), the rate-limiting enzyme in DA synthesis (28). In the brain, dopaminergic neurons are primarily located in the substantia nigra, hypothalamus, and ventral tegmental areas (29). In the retina, DA is synthesized and released by a unique subtype of ACs, dopaminergic ACs (DACs) (30).

Retinal DA is considered a key factor in light-dark adaptation, circadian rhythm regulation, and myopia control (31, 32). Moreover, retinal DA levels increase during early developmental stages in various species and regulate the growth and maturation of other retinal cell types (33). Furthermore, a notable reduction in the number of DACs and their synaptic connections with other cells has been observed in the retinas of aging populations (34) and patients with Parkinson’s disease (35). However, the changing patterns of retinal DA and DACs following optic nerve injury, as well as the potential interactions between DACs and RGCs in neuroprotection and axon regeneration, have yet to be explored. Thus, in this study, we monitored the postinjury responses of DACs and explored therapeutic strategies for manipulating AC-derived DA signaling to achieve substantial optic nerve regeneration.

RESULTS

Optic nerve injury leads to retinal DA decrease and loss of neuronal activity in DACs

We first examined the responses of DACs after optic nerve injury by monitoring the expression of c-fos, a well-established marker for neuronal activity (36, 37). After dark adaptation, c-fos expression across all ACs [labeled by activating protein 2α (AP2α)] was significantly up-regulated 1 day following optic nerve crush (ONC) and remained at a higher level for at least 1 week (Fig. 1, A and C), consistent with a previous study (19). However, to our surprise, we observed a markedly decreased c-fos expression in DACs after the injury, which stands in stark contrast to the changes seen in other ACs (Fig. 1, B and C).

We then investigated whether the down-regulation of neuronal activity in DACs might be accompanied by a deficiency in DA synthesis and release. To explore this, we initially assessed the phosphorylation levels of TH after injury using retinal whole-mount immunostaining (fig. S1A). Compared to the uninjured retina, we observed a gradual decrease in both the immunostaining intensity of phosphorylated TH (pTH) and the TH phosphorylation level (the immunostaining intensity ratio of pTH/TH) at each time point (Fig. 1, D to F). This decrease suggests a sustained reduction in TH activity. The same pattern was consistent in retinal sections (fig. S1B). Notably, the number and morphology of DACs remained unchanged following optic nerve injury (fig. S1, C to E).

We next used liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC-MS)–based metabolomics to monitor retinal DA levels at 7 and 14 days after ONC (Fig. 1G). A heatmap displayed the changing patterns of all detected targets (fig. S1F). Violin plots demonstrated that the concentration of 3-hydroxytyramine, i.e., DA, and its primary metabolite, 3,4-dihydroxyphenylacetic acid, decreased at 7 days postcrush (dpc) and continued to remain at a lower level even at 14 dpc (Fig. 1, H and I). These findings are consistent with the ongoing loss of TH activity in DACs. Overall, these results indicate that optic nerve injury leads to a substantial and sustained decrease in the neuronal activity of DACs and their capacity for DA synthesis and release.

DA precursor l-DOPA protects RGCs and boosts axon regeneration by promoting DA release from DACs

Because retinal DA concentrations decreased following ONC, we investigated whether providing DA could offer neuroprotection and promote axon regeneration (Fig. 2A). Intravitreal injection of DA protected approximately 24% of the RGCs and stimulated the regeneration of a limited number of axons (Fig. 2, B to E). A similar effect was observed in the group treated with the nonselective DA receptor agonist 6-amino-5,6,7,8-tetrahydronaphthalene-2,3-diol hydrobromide (ADTN) (Fig. 2, B to E) (38). The limited therapeutic effect of DA may be attributed to its exceptionally short half-life and dynamic regulation, making it difficult to maintain a steady concentration.

Therefore, we explored the effectiveness of levodopa (l-DOPA), a medication commonly prescribed for Parkinson’s disease. l-DOPA is the primary product catalyzed by TH in the DA biosynthetic pathway. It is more stable but cannot directly bind to DA receptors. To fulfill its physiological function, it must be converted to DA by DOPA decarboxylase in DACs. Consequently, intravitreal injection of l-DOPA is expected to bypass TH inhibition and continuously supply DA in DACs (Fig. 2F). Following l-DOPA intravitreal injection, we observed a significant increase in the number of surviving RGCs and regenerating axons (Fig. 2, H to K). To further investigate how l-DOPA exerts its therapeutic effect, we simultaneously injected l-DOPA either with tetrabenazine (TBZ), a selective inhibitor of the vesicular monoamine transporter 2 (VMAT2) (39), or with α-methyl-l-tyrosine (AMPT), a TH inhibitor (Fig. 2G) (40). TBZ inhibits the VMAT2-mediated transport of DA into synaptic vesicles, while AMPT competitively binds to TH, thereby inhibiting tyrosine binding (Fig. 2F). In the presence of TBZ, the therapeutic effect of l-DOPA was significantly attenuated (Fig. 2, H to K). In contrast, AMPT did not affect the effectiveness of l-DOPA (Fig. 2, H to K). These findings suggest that l-DOPA bypasses the TH-mediated, rate-limited synthesis process to enhance retinal DA release.

Furthermore, we attempted to selectively ablate DACs by intravitreally injecting 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA). 6-OHDA is a selective neurotoxin that targets dopaminergic neurons and has been widely used to create models of Parkinson’s disease through direct injection into specific brain regions or systemic administration (41). To induce DAC depletion in the retina, we adapted a previous study’s protocol (42) and injected 6-OHDA into the vitreous chamber once a week for 2 weeks. DACs and other retinal neurons were examined at 7 and 14 days after the first injection. Results demonstrated a sustained and parallel decrease in DAC somata and neurite branches after 6-OHDA administration (fig. S2, A to C). Approximately 60% reduction in DACs was observed 14 days after the 6-OHDA injection, whereas the number of RGCs and other ACs remained unchanged (fig. S2, D and E), confirming the successful and specific lesioning of DACs. Subsequently, we administrated l-DOPA 14 days after the 6-OHDA injection to assess the impact of DAC damage on l-DOPA treatment (Fig. 2L). The neuroprotective and proregenerative effect of l-DOPA was significantly reduced when DACs had been damaged by 6-OHDA (Fig. 2, M to P), suggesting that the therapeutic effect of l-DOPA relies on an adequate number of intact DACs. In conclusion, these results demonstrate that the DA precursor l-DOPA primarily functions through DACs, bypassing the reduction of TH activity and directly enhancing DA release from DACs following optic nerve injury.

Restoring DAC neuronal activity enhances RGC survival and axon regeneration

Given the sustained down-regulation of neuronal activity in DACs following ONC, we used chemogenetic tools to directly manipulate DAC activity. We intravitreally injected adeno-associated virus 2/2 (AAV2/2)-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry and AAV2/2-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry into Th-Cre mice to Cre-dependently overexpress the engineered heterotrimeric GTP-binding protein (G protein)–coupled receptors hM3Dq and hM4Di specifically in DACs (fig. S2, F and I). After intraperitoneal injection of the selective ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) twice a day for 1 week, c-fos expression in DACs significantly increased at 7 dpc upon hM3Dq activation (fig. S2, G and H), while c-fos expression was notably down-regulated in the hM4Di-activated DACs in the uninjured retina (fig. S2, J and K). These results confirmed the successful manipulation of DAC neuronal activity.

We then examined the impact of DAC activation on RGC survival and axon regeneration. We injected AAV2/2-DIO-hM3Dq-mCherry 2 weeks before ONC and administrated CNO immediately after the crush twice a day for 2 weeks (Fig. 3, A and B). The activation of DACs with hM3Dq and CNO increased the number of surviving RGCs and regenerating axons at 14 dpc (Fig. 3, C to G). While we did not observe a synergistic enhancement of RGC survival (Fig. 3E), the proregenerative effect became more pronounced when l-DOPA treatment was combined with DAC activation (Fig. 3, F and G). In addition, we applied a similar hM4Di strategy as described above to investigate whether suppressing DAC activity would affect the therapeutic effect of l-DOPA (Fig. 3, H and I). In comparison to the green fluorescent protein (GFP)–transduced group, l-DOPA–induced neuroprotection and axon regeneration were diminished in the hM4Di-transduced group, where DAC activity was inhibited (Fig. 3, J to M). This limited protective effect of l-DOPA could be attributed to the reduced release of DA when DAC activity was suppressed. These findings suggest that restoring decreased neuronal activity in DACs increases DA release, thereby benefiting RGCs in terms of survival and axon regeneration following optic nerve damage.

Retinal DA specifically binds to DRD1 on RGCs to promote neuroprotection and axon regeneration

Next, our focus shifted to identifying the receptors that retinal DA binds to under neuroprotective conditions. DA receptors are classified as D1-like (D1 and D5) and D2-like (D2, D3, and D4) receptors, each triggering distinct downstream signaling upon activation (24, 25). We began by combining l-DOPA treatment with SCH23390, a selective antagonist of the DA receptor D1 (DRD1) (43), and sulpiride, a selective antagonist of the DA receptor D2 (DRD2) (Fig. 4A) (44). Neither SCH23390 nor sulpiride alone affected the survival rate of RGCs at 14 dpc (Fig. 4, B and C), whereas only SCH23390 diminished the neuroprotective and proregenerative effect of l-DOPA in combined treatment (Fig. 4, D to F). Furthermore, we directly evaluated the therapeutic effects of DRD1 and DRD2 activation by intravitreal injection of the selective DRD1 agonist SKF38393 (45) and the selective DRD2 agonist quinpirole (Fig. 4G) (46). Consistently, only SKF38393 promoted moderate RGC survival and axon regeneration after ONC (Fig. 4, H to K). These findings suggest that most of the neuroprotective and proregenerative effects of retinal DA may be activated by binding to DRD1 rather than DRD2.

We next explored whether retinal DA specifically binds to DRD1 located on RGCs. To investigate this, we leveraged our previous single-cell RNA sequencing data obtained from mouse retinal tissues to examine the expression patterns of DA receptors in various cell types (47). We observed that Drd1, Drd2, and Drd4 were expressed in bipolar cells and ACs. Drd1 exhibited high expression levels in horizontal cells, while Drd4 was notably enriched in photoreceptors and microglia. Drd3 transcripts were undetectable, and Drd5 showed scarce expression in the retina (Fig. 5, A to C). Notably, only a small proportion of RGCs expressed DA receptors, mostly Drd1 (Fig. 5B).

To verify our hypothesis that retinal DA specifically binds to DRD1 on RGCs for its therapeutic effect, we induced selective Drd1 deletion in the RGCs of Drd1-floxed (Drd1f/f) mice using intravitreal injection of AAV2/2-Cre driven by a mouse g-synuclein (mSncg) promoter, a recently identified RGC-specific promoter (48). Approximately 98 and 87% of GFP+ cells showed dual labeling with β tubulin III (TUJ1) and RNA binding protein with multiple splicing (RBPMS), respectively. Moreover, approximately 81% of TUJ1+ and 73% of RBPMS+ cells were double labeled with GFP 2 weeks after AAV2/2-mSncg-GFP injection (fig. S3, A to D). In addition, GFP expression was confined to the ganglion cell layer (GCL) (fig. S3, A and C), underscoring the robust transduction efficiency and specificity of AAV2/2 driven by mSncg in RGCs.

Following the intravitreal injection of AAV2/2-mSncg-Cre-GFP or AAV2/2-mSncg-GFP (negative control) into both wild-type and Drd1f/f mice, we conducted ONC combined with intravitreal l-DOPA injection (Fig. 5D). The neuroprotective and proregenerative effects of l-DOPA were entirely abrogated in Drd1f/f mice transduced with AAV2/2-mSncg-Cre-GFP, where Drd1 was selectively deleted in RGCs (Fig. 5, E to H). Furthermore, the deletion of Drd1 in RGCs did not affect the number of RGCs in uninjured retinas. However, there was a decrease in the number of surviving RGCs at 7 dpc compared to that in wild-type mice (fig. S3, E to G), indicating the necessity of DA signaling mediated by DRD1 for RGC survival under injured conditions. Together, retinal DA acts by binding to DRD1 on RGCs to promote their survival and axon regeneration.

Enhancing DA release combined with Drd1 overexpression offers robust RGC protection and extensive axon regeneration

Considering the limited expression of Drd1 in RGCs, as previously indicated by our whole-retina cell sequencing data, we explored whether Drd1 expression varies among RGC subtypes and whether these expression patterns correlate with the vulnerability and resilience of RGCs to injury. To investigate this, we used transcriptomic datasets from a recent single-cell sequencing study focused on RGCs (49). Consistent with our earlier findings, Drd1 was the predominant subtype expressed in RGCs within uninjured retinas (Fig. 6A). Specifically, among the RGC subtypes, Drd1 was most prominently expressed in alpha RGCs (αRGCs) and intrinsically photosensitive RGCs (ipRGCs) (Fig. 6A). Intriguingly, both αRGCs and ipRGCs are known for their resistance to axotomy or ONC (49–53). Our results further validated that αRGCs, labeled by secreted phosphoprotein-1 (SPP1), and ipRGCs, identified by melanopsin (OPN4), exhibited the highest survival rates following ONC (fig. S4, A to D). Their proportions increased among the total surviving RGC subtypes at 14 dpc (fig. S4, A to D). The relatively high survival rates of αRGCs and ipRGCs showed a significant correlation with their elevated Drd1 expression levels at 7 and 14 dpc (Fig. 6B). Moreover, Drd1 expression remained stable in ipRGCs and exhibited a gradual decline in αRGCs, which contrasted with the rapid decrease observed in other RGC subtypes at different time points after ONC (Fig. 6C). In addition, given that Drd1 expression was limited predominantly to αRGCs and ipRGCs, we further confirmed that mSncg-driven AAV2/2-Cre-GFP indeed transduced most of the αRGCs (~89%) and ipRGCs (~86%) to reliably delete Drd1 in our previous experiments (fig. S4, E and F).

Given the relatively high levels of Drd1 observed in the two injury-resistant RGC subtypes, we investigated whether their survival advantages were linked to this expression pattern by combining l-DOPA treatment with Drd1 overexpression (OE) across all RGC subtypes (Fig. 6D). Using mSncg-driven AAV2/2, we successfully overexpressed Drd1 in the majority of RGCs (~80%) (fig. S5, A to D). Our findings revealed that RGC-specific Drd1 OE alone increased the survival rate of RGCs, akin to the effects of l-DOPA treatment (Fig. 6, E and F). However, the combined approach of Drd1 OE with l-DOPA administration remarkably safeguarded around 45% of RGCs (Fig. 6, E and F). Unexpectedly, Drd1 OE alone induced robust axon regeneration at 14 dpc (Fig. 6, G and H). The combination of Drd1 OE with l-DOPA administration had a potent synergistic effect on regenerating axons, leading to extensive axon regeneration over long distances (Fig. 6, G and H). To assess the clinical potential of this promising therapy, we conducted postinjury treatment by injecting AAV2/2-mSncg-Drd1-GFP and l-DOPA immediately after ONC. Delayed Drd1 OE, in combination with l-DOPA, also provided RGC protection and axon regeneration. Furthermore, delayed Drd1 OE enhanced the regenerative effects of l-DOPA (fig. S6, A to E). In addition, we selectively lesioned DACs using 6-OHDA in Drd1 OE retinas to assess whether the therapeutic effect of Drd1 OE relies on DACs. The results demonstrated a significant decrease in the survival of RGCs and regenerating axons following 6-OHDA injection (fig. S6, F to J), indicating that the therapeutic effect of Drd1 OE largely depends on retinal DA from DACs.

In summary, these findings emphasize the synergistic potential of simultaneously enhancing DA release from DACs and inducing ectopic Drd1 expression across all RGCs to offer robust neuroprotection and extensive axon regeneration. Moreover, we have pinpointed the l-DOPA–DA–DRD1 axis as a crucial focus for manipulating DAC-derived DA signaling and as a promising target for therapeutic interventions following optic nerve injury.

Retinal DA leads to subtype-selective efficacy and Drd1 OE overcomes the subtype-specific barrier

Given the relatively high and sustained expression of Drd1 in αRGCs and ipRGCs after injury, we explored the possibility of a subtype-specific preference for the neuroprotective and proregenerative effects of DA-related treatments. To test this hypothesis, we initially used subtype markers SPP1 and OPN4 to directly label surviving RGCs in different treatment groups at 14 dpc (Fig. 7A). The survival rates of αRGCs and ipRGCs showed no significant differences among the three groups (l-DOPA, Drd1 OE, and l-DOPA + Drd1 OE) (Fig. 7, B, C, E, and F). However, compared to l-DOPA treatment alone, the survival rates of non-αRGCs and non-ipRGCs increased in the Drd1 OE group and were even higher in the combined therapy group (Fig. 7, D and G), indicating enhanced protection of more non-αRGCs and non-ipRGCs.

Subsequently, we explored subtype-specific preferences for axon regeneration, as many studies have reported these preferences (9, 50, 54). In our study, we adopted a retrograde labeling method, wherein we intraorbitally injected a small, fluorescently labeled dextran into the optic nerve, positioned ~0.5 mm distal to the crush site, 1 day before sacrifice to label regenerating axons (Fig. 7, H and I) (19, 48, 54). To verify the successful delivery of dextran into the optic nerves without leakage into the crush site, we used a dual-labeling approach by intravitreally injecting cholera toxin subunit B (CTB) 2 days before dextran injection for anterograde axon labeling. We found a complete overlap between dextran and CTB signals throughout uninjured optic nerves (fig. S7A). Approximately 76% of the RBPMS+ RGCs were labeled by dextran in the uninjured retinas (fig. S7, C to E). However, dextran was confined near the injection site with no overlap observed with CTB signals before the crush site at 14 dpc (fig. S7B). In addition, dextran did not label any RGCs at 14 dpc because regenerating axons were absent (fig. S7, C to E), confirming the specificity and efficacy of dextran retrograde labeling.

After the dextran injection, we costained the retinas with SPP1 to label the regenerating αRGCs, identified as dextran and SPP1 dual-labeled cells. Consistent with our earlier findings, the number of dextran+ cells increased in the Drd1 OE and combined treatment groups (Fig. 7, J and K), indicating an improved proregenerative effect. Similarly, the number of regenerating αRGCs remained consistent across the different treatments (Fig. 7L). The percentage of αRGCs among total regenerating RGCs significantly decreased from ~82% in the l-DOPA–injected group to ~66% in the Drd1 OE group and ~50% in the combined treatment group (Fig. 7M). In summary, these results highlight that while l-DOPA primarily protects αRGCs and ipRGCs by increasing DAC-derived DA, ectopic Drd1 OE successfully overcomes this subtype-specific barrier by extending Drd1 expression across all RGC subtypes, promoting a more robust level of RGC survival and axon regeneration.

DACs serve as the sole source of retinal DA that regulates neuroprotection and axon regeneration through both diffusion and synaptic transmission

In addition to the well-studied DACs, also known as type 1 DA cells, another subtype of catecholaminergic amacrine (CA) cells, known as type 2 CA cells, has also been identified in transgenic mouse retina labeled with Th-driven fluorescent protein (55, 56) (fig. S8, A and B). Type 2 CA cells lack TH immunoreactivity and are morphologically distinct from DACs, characterized by the S3 laminating plexus and some displaced somata in the GCL (fig. S8, A and B). Therefore, we used Th-GFP mice to investigate the postinjury responses of type 2 CA cells (identified as GFP positive and TH negative). Results revealed that the number and morphology of type 2 CA cells remained unchanged at 7 dpc (fig. S8, C to E). However, unlike DACs, c-fos expression was consistently low both before and after injury in type 2 CA cells (fig. S8, F and G), suggesting that these cells may not contribute to the DA-related pathogenesis of optic nerve injury.

Furthermore, a recent study identified RGC-derived DA, which regulates retinal angiogenesis during the early postnatal period (57). However, we observed that adult RGCs did not contain TH in the retina with or without ONC, as evidenced by the lack of overlap between RBPMS and either TH immunostaining or Th-GFP signals (including displaced cells in the GCL) (fig. S9, A to C). To further exclude the possibility of this cell-autonomous DA in our therapeutic settings, we analyzed the retinal vasculature in adult mice. Results indicated no significant difference in CD31+ vascular density among the uninjured, GFP control, and Drd1 OE groups at 7 dpc (fig. S9, D and E). In summary, these findings verify the absence of RGC-derived DA in adult mice and support the notion that the observed effects of DA-related treatment on optic nerve regeneration are not mediated by RGC-autonomous DA signaling. Together, we have confirmed DACs as the sole source of retinal DA in regulating neuroprotection and axon regeneration.

We were also curious about how the therapeutic impact of DAC-derived DA on RGCs operates—whether it is through diffusion or synaptic transmission. It is known that retinal DA mainly spreads throughout the retina from DACs via diffusion (58). However, it has also been revealed that ipRGCs receive modulatory inputs directly from DACs via synaptic connections (59, 60). We further confirmed the existence of synapses between DACs and ipRGCs through triple immunostaining of TH, melanopsin, and the postsynaptic marker postsynaptic density protein 95 (PSD95) (fig. S10A). To elucidate how DAC-derived DA signals are conveyed to RGCs, we compared the survival and regeneration of ipRGCs after specific presynaptic stimulation of DACs (Th-Cre/hM3Dq/CNO) with broad activation of DRD1 (SKF38393), which mimics the diffusive effect of DA (fig. S10B). Our previous findings have shown a similar effect on the survival and regeneration of total RGCs between these two manipulations (fig. S10, C and D). While there was no significant difference in ipRGC survival between these two manipulations (fig. S10, E to G), the proportion of regenerating ipRGCs increased after DAC activation (fig. S10, H to J), suggesting the existence of synaptic modulation between DACs and ipRGCs in DA-mediated protection. In conclusion, while the majority of DA signals are believed to operate through diffusion, our evidence suggests that synaptic transmission of DA between DACs and ipRGCs also exists in regulating postinjury neuroprotection and axon regeneration.

DA-related treatment promotes axon regeneration by directly activating RGCs and modulating canonical regenerative pathways

We first investigated whether the DA-related treatment modulates established neuroprotective and proregenerative signaling pathways in RGCs. Phosphorylation levels of adenosine 3′,5′-monophosphate (cAMP) response element–binding protein (CREB) and Ca2+/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) in RGCs tend to decrease following optic nerve injury but can be revitalized through various treatments, eventually promoting RGC survival and axon regeneration (61). Notably, the combined application of Drd1 OE and l-DOPA injection resulted in significant increases in the immunostaining intensities of phosphorylated CREB (pCREB) and phosphorylated CaMKII (pCaMKII) in RGCs at 3 dpc in comparison to the untreated group (Fig. 8, A to C). The dual leucine zipper kinase (DLK)/c-Jun pathway predominantly contributes to RGC death after optic nerve injury, with the level of phosphorylated c-Jun (p–c-Jun) in uninjured retinas experiencing a marked up-regulation after ONC (62). In line with previous studies, p–c-Jun level in RGCs significantly increased 3 days after ONC but was mitigated by the combined treatment of Drd1 OE and l-DOPA (Fig. 8D). The activation of the mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) and related pathways stands as a well-known mediator that fosters optic nerve regeneration and RGC survival (7, 8, 10). However, our study did not reveal any differences in the upstream or downstream targets of the mTOR pathway, including phosphorylated protein kinase B (pAKT), phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (pSTAT3), and phosphorylated ribosomal protein S6 (pS6) (fig. S11, A to D).

As a neurotransmitter, retinal DA could also directly excite RGCs and promote signal generation and transmission by activating DRD1 (63), which may benefit their survival and axon regeneration. To validate RGC activation after DA-related treatment, we monitored the RGC cAMP level, the direct downstream target of DRD1 activation, along with c-fos expression. Mice were dark adapted overnight before sacrifice to avoid the illumination which could stimulate endogenous DA release (64). Following l-DOPA treatment, there was a moderate increase in cAMP expression and the proportion of c-fos+ RGCs at 3 dpc compared to the vehicle control (Fig. 8, E to G). Notably, this effect was further amplified when combined with Drd1 OE (Fig. 8, E to G). Thus, l-DOPA treatment indeed activates RGCs after optic nerve injury, and ectopic Drd1 OE further enhances DA-induced RGC activation.

Previous studies have revealed that enhancing the neuronal activity of RGCs promotes axon regeneration (65). To investigate whether RGCs benefit from activation by DA, we intravitreally injected AAV2/2-DIO-hM4Di-mCherry into Vglut2-Cre mice to specifically express hM4Di in RGCs (fig. S11, E and F) (19). Activation of hM4Di by CNO suppressed the neuronal activity of RGCs, as evidenced by a decrease in the c-fos expressing among RGCs (fig. S11, G and H), ultimately diminishing the proregenerative effect of l-DOPA (fig. S11, I and J). Thus, in addition to the metabolomic-associated pathways, retinal DA can directly activate RGCs for neuroprotection and regeneration.

Moreover, RGCs have been observed to regenerate long-distance axons when neuronal activation is combined with the up-regulation of the mTOR pathway (65). In this study, l-DOPA and Drd1 OE treatment did not affect mTOR-related signaling targets, suggesting the possibility of a synergistic effect when combining DA-related therapy with mTOR activation to promote optic nerve regeneration. To investigate this further, we used AAV-driven shRNA to knock down the phosphatase and tensin homolog (Pten), a canonical and potent approach to promote axon regeneration by activating the mTOR pathway (8, 66), and combined it with DA-related manipulation (Fig. 8H). Approximately 83% of RGCs were transduced with AAV2/2-H1-shPTEN-GFP (fig. S11, K and L). The combined treatment of l-DOPA, Drd1 OE, and Pten knockdown (KD) significantly promoted greater axon regeneration than DA-related therapy or Pten KD alone (Fig. 8, I and J). Furthermore, cAMP plays a crucial role in enabling RGCs to respond to neurotrophic factors, and elevation of cAMP facilitates their proregenerative effects (13, 67). Consequently, in the context of increased cAMP induced by DA, we added recombinant ciliary neurotrophic factor (rCNTF), a potent proregenerative factor (68), into the aforementioned combinatory treatment (Fig. 8H). Encouragingly, this addition resulted in a significant boost to regeneration and induced full-length optic nerve regeneration extending into the optic chiasma at 28 dpc (Fig. 8, I and J).

In summary, the membrane-located G protein–coupled receptor DRD1 functions as a critical upstream node in response to DAC-derived DA, initiating some but not all signaling pathways associated with neuroprotection and regeneration. In addition, DA directly excites RGCs and facilitates axon regeneration through elevated neuronal activity. When combined with Pten KD and CNTF, DA-related treatment exhibits a strong additive and mechanistically synergistic effect, promoting extensive and long-distance optic nerve regeneration.

Drd1 OE protects RGC somata and axons in the chronic ocular hypertension–induced glaucoma model

As DRD1 activation functions as an upstream regulator influencing multiple signaling pathways, we investigated whether the neuroprotection and regeneration seen with DA-related therapy in the ONC model could also enhance neural repair and visual recovery in a more clinically relevant glaucoma model. To further validate this, we used our recently developed in situ–cross-linked hydrogel-induced chronic ocular hypertension (COH) model to stimulate a glaucoma-like condition. This model not only induces stable elevation of intraocular pressure (IOP) and characteristic neurodegeneration but also preserves the optical characteristics of the anterior segment due to the transparency of the hydrogel (69). To minimize the need for frequent eyeball penetration, which might disrupt IOP, we exclusively assessed the therapeutic effect of RGC-specific Drd1 OE by injecting AAV2/2-mSncg-Drd1-GFP and AAV2/2-mSncg-GFP 14 days before inducing the COH model (Fig. 9A). Subsequently, we administered an intracameral injection of the hydrogel, which rapidly solidified within minutes and obstructed the aqueous humor outflow pathway in the trabecular meshwork (Fig. 9, B and C). IOP was successfully elevated 7 days after hydrogel injection and remained at a high level for the following 3 weeks, in contrast to the sham group injected with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), where IOP remained unchanged (Fig. 9D).

We used optical coherence tomography (OCT) to measure the in vivo thickness of the retinal ganglion cell complex (GCC) as an indicator of RGC and optic nerve degeneration. Changes in GCC thickness in each mouse were compared to baseline values before COH induction to minimize individual differences. In vivo OCT revealed that GCC thickness decreased to ~67% in the GFP group 28 days after COH, while Drd1 OE preserved GCC thickness at ~84% (Fig. 9, E and F). This indicated that Drd1 OE significantly prevented GCC thickness decrease in COH eyes. In line with the OCT findings, Drd1 OE markedly increased the RGC survival rate from ~47% in the GFP group to ~81% at 28 days after COH (Fig. 9, G and H).

Next, we used CTB for anterograde tracing of RGC axon projections. In comparison to the sham group, the COH group displayed a ~58% reduction in CTB labeling intensity in the optic nerve after 1 month (Fig. 9, I and J). In contrast, a substantial portion of axons was protected following Drd1 OE, as evidenced by the recovery of CTB intensity to ~75% (Fig. 9, I and J). Because most RGC axons project to the contralateral side of the brain in mice (70), we assessed the CTB intensity in brain sections of the contralateral lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) and superior colliculus (SC), the two primary projection targets of RGC axons in the brain (71). A significant reduction in RGC axon projections was observed 28 days after COH, with ~50 and ~35% of the CTB intensity remaining in the LGN and SC, respectively (Fig. 9, K to M). In contrast, Drd1 OE significantly enhanced the nuclear projections of RGC axons to ~69% in the LGN and ~72% in the SC (Fig. 9, K to M). Together, Drd1 OE not only promotes RGC survival but also substantially preserves axons and maintains their projections to the target nuclei in the brain. This preservation constitutes the histological basis and a crucial prerequisite for functional vision recovery.

Drd1 OE preserves visual function

On the basis of the evidence presented above, we investigated whether Drd1 OE preserves visual function in the COH model. Initially, we assessed RGC function using a pattern electroretinogram (PERG), which measures the electrical activity of RGCs in response to patterned visual stimuli (72). Compared to the baseline before COH induction, the PERG response, represented by the P1-N2 amplitude, exhibited a lesser reduction in Drd1-overexpressing eyes 28 days after COH (Fig. 10, A and C). Furthermore, we examined the transmission capacity of the preserved RGCs by recording flash visually evoked potentials (FVEPs) in the primary visual cortices (73). Consistent with the PERG results, FVEP, represented by the N2-P2 amplitude, showed a slower decline after Drd1 OE compared to the GFP group 28 days after COH (Fig. 10, B and D). We also evaluated the optomotor response (OMR) to assess visual acuity in mice (74). Mice were placed on a platform, and the tracking of the grating stimulus of each eye was recorded and measured independently (Fig. 10E). The OMR test revealed that Drd1 OE restored nearly half of the visual acuity lost in COH eyes (Fig. 10F).

To further assess the functional recovery of vision, we conducted vision-based behavioral tests. First, we performed the visual cliff test to evaluate the ability to discriminate visual depth based on the innate preference of mice to avoid deep cliffs (75). Mice were released on the shallow side at the edge of the cliff, and the time spent on each side was recorded over 5 min (Fig. 10G). In the uninjured group, mice predominantly stayed on the shallow side (~80% of the time), while mice from the COH group spent more time on the deep side (~55%) of the cliff (Fig. 10H). In contrast, Drd1 OE significantly reduced the time spent on the deep side to ~30% in COH mice (Fig. 10H). We then assessed the rapid innate defense responses of mice to looming visual stimuli that imitate environmental threats (76). The programmed looming stimuli were displayed on a screen placed on top of the device. The responses of mice to looming stimuli, including freezing and fleeing to the shelter, were recorded using a camera (Fig. 10I). In the uninjured group, all 10 mice responded to the stimuli (Fig. 10J). One month after hydrogel injection, only one mouse in the GFP-transduced group responded to looming stimuli, whereas in the group treated with Drd1 OE, the number of responding mice increased to five (Fig. 10J). Collectively, our results provide in vivo evidence that Drd1 OE can effectively preserve visual function.

Retinal DA signaling is altered in spontaneous glaucoma macaques

Given the broad effect of DA-related treatment both observed in acute ONC and chronic glaucoma models in mice, we were then curious about whether retinal DA signaling was altered in nonhuman primates under pathological conditions. We analyzed our recent single-cell sequencing data of retinal samples from rhesus macaques with spontaneous primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) (Fig. 10, K and L). Demographic information for the normal control and POAG macaques, along with the details of POAG characteristics in the macaques (e.g., deeper anterior chamber, increased cup-to-disc ratio, and thinning retinal nerve fiber layer), has been reported in the previous study (77). Regarding the distribution of DA receptors in the macaque retina, DRD1 and DRD2 were the main expressed subtypes, predominantly found in the interneurons (Fig. 10M). When we specifically focused on RGCs, we were surprised to find that the expression levels of DRD1 and DRD2 drastically decreased in the spontaneous POAG macaque compared to the normal control (Fig. 10N). The decreased expression level of DRD1 in macaque RGCs after POAG is consistent with the earlier results obtained from sequencing data of mouse RGCs after ONC, suggesting that DA-related pathways may play a role in the pathogenesis of various optic nerve injury diseases in a clinically relevant situation. Targeting retinal DA signaling could potentially prevent vision loss in patients suffering neurodegenerative diseases.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we have reported the down-regulated neuronal activity in DACs, reduced retinal DA release, and decreased Drd1 expression in RGCs following optic nerve injury. These findings reveal a previously overlooked yet powerful therapeutic strategy involving DAC-derived DA signaling through the l-DOPA–DA–DRD1 axis for RGC protection and axon regeneration. The immediate decrease in neuronal activity and TH phosphorylation in DACs at 1 dpc persisted until 7 dpc, causing a sustained deficiency in retinal DA following ONC. Intravitreal injection of the DA precursor l-DOPA, or directly restoring DAC activity, compensated for DA reduction by increasing DA release, thus promoting RGC survival and axon regeneration. Through RGC-specific Drd1 deletion, we pinpointed DRD1 in RGCs as the sole receptor subtype responsible for mediating the therapeutic effects of DA. A notable enhancement was observed in both RGC survival and axon regeneration when ectopic Drd1 OE across all RGCs was combined with increased DA release. Furthermore, Drd1 OE not only provided postcrush protection and regeneration to RGCs but also preserved vision in a COH glaucoma mouse model. In addition, retinal DA signaling was altered in spontaneous glaucoma macaques, as DRD1 expression level decreased in the POAG macaque retina, suggesting a broad influence of DA signaling in various optic nerve injury diseases and highlighting the relevance of DA-related treatment for clinical applications. In summary, our findings unveil the crucial role of DAC-derived DA signaling between ACs and RGCs in promoting optic nerve regeneration and preserving visual function.

ACs in optic nerve regeneration

Apart from the established strategies for optic nerve regeneration by manipulating intrinsic growth-related factors and extrinsic environmental conditions, recent attention has been directed toward the pivotal role of interneurons in axon regeneration and related interventions. The involvement of AC-associated axon growth was initially found two decades ago (15). Subsequent studies further explored the mechanisms by which ACs influence RGC axon regeneration (16, 17, 19). Nevertheless, the mechanisms underlying AC-RGC interactions in optic nerve regeneration, although summarized in several theories (20, 21), remain elusive.

The first theory centers on the antagonist axon-dendrite interplay, positing that dendritic synapses of adult RGCs connected to ACs hinder axon regeneration. Zebrafish studies revealed that the spontaneous axon regeneration of RGCs commences only after dendrite shrinkage, and preserving dendrites and their synapses constrains axon regeneration following ONC (78). In the mouse ONC model, axon regeneration was associated with abnormal dendrite morphologies following intravitreal injection of rCNTF (79), and cutting RGC dendrites relieved antigrowth signals, facilitating axon regeneration, particularly in a DLK knockout context (80, 81). The second theory revolves around various neurotransmitters released by ACs, which may participate in the pathophysiological processes of optic nerve injury. Previous research has demonstrated that blocking receptors for inhibitory neurotransmitters from ACs enhances RGC responsiveness to growth factors and increases regenerating axons (19). Moreover, the modulation of neuronal activity in RGCs, governed by neurotransmitters from ACs, promotes long-distance axon regeneration and, in some cases, even full-length regeneration, resulting in partial recovery of visual function and vision-guided behaviors (65, 82). The third theory focuses on other synaptic vesicular components. Evidence suggests that Zn2+ accumulates in ACs immediately after ONC and is rapidly transferred to RGCs through synaptic vesicular release. Preventing Zn2+ transfer by genetically knocking out the Zn2+ transporter 3 or by pharmacologically chelating excess Zn2+ promotes optic nerve regeneration (16). In this study, we have unveiled a remarkable role for the DAC-derived DA and its related downstream target, DRD1, adding compelling evidence to the theory of AC-modulated optic nerve regeneration.

Overcoming the subtype-specific barrier to RGC survival and axon regeneration

The survival capacity of different RGC subtypes can greatly vary following optic nerve injury (49, 50). This makes exploring transcriptomic differences among these subtypes a promising approach to identifying potential therapeutic targets for neuroprotection.

In our study, retinal Drd1 was predominantly expressed in αRGCs and ipRGCs, two subtypes known for their resilience after optic nerve injury (49–53). Given this limited expression pattern of Drd1, increasing DA alone could only rescue a minority of RGCs. To address this, we developed an AAV that specifically overexpressed Drd1 across all RGC subtypes, transcending subtype-specific survival barriers and ultimately achieving a remarkably potent neuroprotective effect. After a single treatment with l-DOPA, most of the regenerating axons consistently originated from αRGCs. However, Drd1 OE in all RGC subtypes overcame this subtype-specific barrier, as indicated by the increasing number of total regenerating axons and the growing proportions of axons from non-αRGCs. Similar to findings in another study (54), our results suggest that achieving potent and long-distance axon regeneration may only be feasible when the manipulation affects a broad range of RGCs.

From axon regeneration to neuroprotection

The ONC model has been widely used in studies focusing on neuroprotection and axon regeneration because of its surgical accessibility and reproducibility. However, it is important to note that the ONC model replicates acute mechanical optic nerve injury outside the eyeball, which differs from glaucoma-induced injury. In glaucoma, elevated IOP is the primary cause of injury, and axons are not entirely severed. Consequently, therapeutic strategies identified in the ONC model may not necessarily yield similar results in glaucoma. Nevertheless, several studies have shown that genetic modulation promoting neuroprotection and proregeneration in the ONC model can lead to neural repair and safeguard visual function in the glaucoma model (48, 61, 74). This indicates an underlying connection between the pathological mechanisms of these two disease models. In our study, Drd1 OE, the therapy found in ONC model, also promoted neural repair and vision recovery in COH glaucoma model. The broad neuroprotective effect of Drd1 OE can be attributed to the activation of a wide range of downstream signaling cascades in response to DRD1 activation, although further research is needed to fully comprehend this mechanism.

The relationship between soma survival and axon regeneration has long been a complex and intricate puzzle. It is widely recognized that axon regeneration benefits from soma survival because the surviving soma provides a therapeutic window for axon regeneration. This has been substantiated by numerous studies illustrating that most proregenerative interventions also promote RGC survival (7–11). However, certain interventions have been found that enhance soma survival but simultaneously inhibit axon regeneration. For instance, brain-derived neurotrophic factor increased RGC survival after optic nerve injury (83) while attenuating the proregenerative effects induced by intraocular inflammation (84). Deletion of DLK suppressed RGC death but hindered axon regeneration in Pten-deficient mice (62). Recently, OE of CaMKIIα T286D, a constitutively active CaMKII mutant, robustly protected RGCs after ONC (61), while CaMKII activation has been reported to impede axon regeneration (85). In contrast, Sox11 OE has been reported to promote axon regeneration in non-αRGCs while killing αRGCs (9). In essence, the neuroprotective or proregenerative effects of each intervention depend on its unique signaling pathways. Therefore, a thorough examination of downstream intracellular signaling is crucial in understanding the intricate interaction between RGC soma survival and axon regeneration.

In summary, our study revealed that DACs, a unique subtype of ACs, immediately respond to optic nerve injury by reducing neuronal activity and DA release. Enhancing DA release from DACs, coupled with ectopic OE of Drd1 throughout all RGCs, substantially promotes RGC survival and axon regeneration following optic nerve injury and protects vision in the COH glaucoma model. Nonetheless, the precise mechanism underlying the prompt response of DACs to signals of RGC axon injury requires further investigation. In our forthcoming studies, high-throughput single-cell sequencing will help unravel the crucial intracellular signaling cascades downstream of DRD1 activation. Together, our findings highlight DAC-derived DA signaling as a dynamic regulator of RGC survival, optic nerve regeneration, and visual function preservation, underscoring its potential as a promising therapeutic target for clinical intervention in glaucoma and other optic neuropathies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental design

This study aimed to investigate changes in retinal DA signaling between DACs and RGCs after optic nerve injury and to identify related therapeutic strategies for promoting optic nerve regeneration and vision recovery. Neuronal activity and DA release both decreased in DACs after optic nerve injury, as evidenced by retinal immunostaining and LC-MS detection. l-DOPA was used to elevate DA release. Th-Cre mice were injected with AAV-DIO-hM3Dq or AAV-DIO-hM4Di to Cre-dependently manipulate DAC neuronal activity. Single-cell sequencing data were analyzed to identify the expression pattern of retinal DA receptors. Drd1f/f mice were injected with AAV-mSncg-Cre for RGC-selective Drd1 deletion. The neuroprotective and proregenerative effect of RGC-selective Drd1 OE mediated by AAV-mSncg-Drd1 was evaluated in an ONC model and a COH model. Mice were randomly allocated to each group. Investigators for measurement, quantification, and data collection were blinded to the treatment of the samples to avoid human bias. In general, 4 to 10 biological replicates in each group were used in this study. The exact numbers of biological replicates in different experiments are stated in the figure legends. Sample sizes were calculated on the basis of similar types of prior studies, where α = 0.05, (1-β) = 0.8, and effect size ≥ 2.

LC-MS–based metabolomics

To minimize the influence of circadian rhythm and light-dark adaptation, which can affect retinal DA and its associated metabolite levels (86), the collection of the retinas for LC-MS analysis was conducted precisely at 9:00 a.m. (1 hour into the light period), when retinal DA levels are reported to reach their daily peak (86). After freshly dissected retinas had been thawed and crushed, retina samples were mixed with 70% methanol, vortexed for 3 min at 2500 rpm, and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube and stored at −20°C for 30 min, followed by another centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was transferred for further LC-MS analysis using an LC–electrospray ionization–tandem MS system [ultraperformance LC (UPLC), ExionLC AD, https://sciex.com/; MS, QTRAP 6500+ System, https://sciex.com/]. The analytical parameters used in this study were as follows: a high-performance LC column [ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm by 100 mm); Waters, USA]; a solvent system consisting of water with 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (B); a gradient that began at 5% B (0 min), increased to 95% B (0 to 8.0 min), remained at 95% B (8.0 to 9.5 min), and ultimately returned to 5% B (9.5 to 12.0 min); a flow rate of 0.35 ml/min; a temperature of 40°C; and an injection volume of 2 μl. The AB Sciex QTRAP 6500 LC-MS/MS platform was used to detect the neurotransmitter contents. The mass spectrometer was set to positive electrospray ionization mode and used the multiple reaction monitoring mode for detecting retinal neurotransmitters at various mass-to-charge ratios. To quantify the analytes, a calibration curve was generated using linear regression, and Analyst software 1.6.2 (Sciex) was used for this purpose.

ONC model

ONC was performed in mice aged 8 weeks (average body weight, 20 to 25 g) under general anesthesia using ketamine/xylazine. The optic nerve was exposed intraorbitally and crushed with angled forceps for 5 s at approximately 0.5 mm behind the globe. An eye ointment containing tobramycin was applied to protect the cornea. The mice were placed on a heating pad in a quiet environment after surgery for recovery.

Intravitreal injection

Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of a mixture of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), and topical anesthesia of the cornea was administered with tetracaine hydrochloride. For intravitreal injection of various chemical compounds, a 30-G needle (305107; BD, USA) attached to a 10-μl Hamilton syringe (80001; Hamilton, USA) was inserted into the vitreous chamber just posterior to the limbus. Approximately 3 μl of the solution was injected into the vitreous chamber immediately after ONC, and the injection was performed again a week later. DA (100 μM; H8502; MilliporeSigma, USA), ADTN (100 μM; ab120150; Abcam, USA), SCH23390 (10 μM; D054; MilliporeSigma), sulpiride (10 μM; 0895; Tocris, USA), SKF38393 (100 μM; GC14048; GLPBIO, USA), quinpirole (100 μM; Q102; MilliporeSigma), l-DOPA (100 μM; GC15095; GLPBIO), and 6-OHDA (15 μg/μl; GC16267; GLPBIO) were dissolved freshly in a solution containing 0.1% (w/v) ascorbic acid (A92902; MilliporeSigma) in PBS and were used within 2 hours. TBZ (100 μM; T2952; MilliporeSigma), AMPT (100 μM; M8131; MilliporeSigma), and rCNTF (1 μg/ml; 450 to 50; Peprotech, USA) were prepared in PBS. For intravitreal injection of AAV, 2 μl of AAV was injected into the vitreous chamber 2 weeks before ONC to achieve stable target gene expression. For anterograde tracing of regenerating axons, 2 μl of Alexa Fluor 555 (1 mg/ml)–conjugated CTB (C22843; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and Alexa Fluor 647–conjugated CTB (C34778; Thermo Fisher Scientific) were intravitreally injected 2 days before sacrifice.

CNO application

CNO (GC10822; GLPBIO) was dissolved in PBS to a concentration of 1 mg/ml. Intraperitoneal injection of CNO (1.5 mg/kg) was applied twice a day (at 10:00 a.m. and 6:00 p.m.) for 2 weeks.

Retrograde labeling of regenerating RGCs

Intraorbital dextran injection to label regenerating axons was performed 1 day before sacrifice. After anesthesia, the optic nerve of a mouse was intraorbitally exposed ~1 to 2 mm distal to the eyeball without injuring the soft tissues, muscles, and retro-orbital sinus. The optic nerve was punctured ~0.5 mm distal to the crush site using a 32-G needle (S07262; SUNGSHIM, Korea). Approximately 1 μl of dextran–Texas Red (D3328; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and dextran–Alexa Fluor 647 (D22914; Thermo Fisher Scientific) was slowly injected into the optic nerve via the 32-G needle connected to a Hamilton syringe through the premade hole over 5 min. Dye leakage was cleared using tissue paper. The mice were then subjected to eye ointment treatment and placed on a heating pad for recovery.

Hydrogel-induced experimental glaucoma model

Elevation of IOP was induced by injection of in situ–cross-linking hydrogel into the anterior chamber of mouse eyes. The surgical methods have been previously described in more detail (69). Briefly, hyperbranched macromolecular poly(ethylene glycol) (HB-PEG) and thiolated hyaluronic acid (HA) (HA-SH) were fully dissolved separately in PBS at a concentration of 10 mg/ml for later use. The mice were anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine, and proparacaine hydrochloride eye drops were used before surgery to dilate the pupils. Using a 32-G needle attached to a Hamilton syringe, a 1-μl air bubble was injected into the anterior chamber from the limbus. Same volumes of HB-PEG and HA-SH solutions were mixed and immediately injected into the anterior chamber through the previous incision site. The needle was stabilized in the anterior chamber for 3 min to allow the HB-PEG/HA-SH hydrogel precursor to be completely cross-linked and reach a stable form. The mice were then treated with an antibiotic eye ointment and placed on a heating pad for recovery.

IOP measurement

IOP was measured using a TonoLab rebound tonometer (Icare, Finland) at 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days after hydrogel injection during a fixed period (10:00 a.m. to 12:00 a.m.). The mice were first anesthetized by inhalation of 2.5% isoflurane (R510-22, RWD, China), followed by maintenance with 1.5% isoflurane. The average of six measurements was automatically generated by the machine, and the mean of the six machine-generated readings was considered to represent the IOP.

Optical coherence tomography

OCT was performed using the Phoenix Micron IV image-guided OCT system (Phoenix, USA), as described previously (69). Briefly, after general anesthesia and pupil dilation, the eyes were moisturized with sodium hydroxypropyl methylcellulose gel. The mice were then placed on the imaging platform with the central cornea directly opposite the camera. The retina was scanned horizontally across the optic nerve head. The average GCC thickness of each retina was measured and calculated using Insight software (Phoenix Research Labs).

PERG recording

PERG was performed using the RETI-port system (Roland, Germany), according to the international standard steps described in our previous publications (18). In brief, mice were anesthetized using ketamine/xylazine and placed on the feedback-controlled heating stage with the body temperature maintained at 37°C under dim red light throughout the procedure. Sodium hydroxypropyl methylcellulose gel was used to prevent corneal opacities. The PERG electrode was placed on the cornea and positioned to encompass the pupil without obstructing the vision. Visual stimuli were generated from a screen 18 cm in front of the eyes that displayed a black-and-white reversing checkerboard pattern. In total, 100 contrast reversals of the PERG signals were repeated twice for each eye, and 200 cycles were segmented, averaged, and recorded. The average PERG signals were analyzed to evaluate the P1-N2 (peak-to-trough) amplitude.

FVEP measurement

FVEP was measured following the international standard steps that have been described in our previous study (87). Briefly, after anesthesia, the recording electrode was inserted between the two ears of the mouse and reached the occipital skull subcutaneously, whereas the reference and ground electrodes were placed subcutaneously in the forehead and tail, respectively. A drop of sodium hydroxypropyl methylcellulose gel was applied to the eyes to prevent corneal opacities. The RETI-port system was used to generate the stimuli and collect the FVEP signals. The FVEP signals at 1.0 Hz were recorded, and the superposition of 100 flash stimuli, repeated three times in each eye, was used as the final result.

OMR test

The spatial frequency of eyes was measured using the OMR test (Optodrum, Striatech, Germany) to evaluate the visual acuity of mice as described in our previous studies (87). Briefly, living mice were placed on a round platform located at the center of four monitors arranged in a quadrangle. Rotating vertical black-and-white strips were displayed on monitors surrounding the mice. A camera placed above the platform recorded the head movements of the mice. The movement of the head concordant with the direction of rotation of the stripes was considered positive tracking. Each eye was tested separately, on the basis of the direction of rotation. The rotation speed (12°/s) and contrast (100%) on the monitors were kept constant, and the spatial frequency started at 0.1 cycle/deg. The software continuously and automatically adjusted the spatial frequency to determine the visual threshold until no reflexes were triggered.

Visual cliff test

The visual cliff apparatus was adapted from a previous study (75). A transparent box (48 cm by 48 cm by 30 cm) was made of clear acrylic plates. Half of the box was placed on a table (shallow side), and the other half hung at a height of 60 cm over the floor (deep side). The same mosaic pattern was placed under the shallow side of the box on the table and beneath the deep side of the box on the floor. Mice received AAVs and hydrogel injections in both eyes for behavioral tests. The mice were released on the shallow side at the edge of the cliff and observed for 5 min. The times spent on the deep and shallow sides were recorded separately. Each mouse was subjected to one test. The box was thoroughly cleaned after each test.

Looming visual stimulus test

The looming visual stimulus test was adapted from a previous study (76). The test arena was an open-top box with dimensions of 48 cm by 48 cm by 30 cm made of frosted acrylic plates to prevent light reflection. A shelter (20 cm by 12 cm) in the shape of a triangular prism was placed in a corner of the box, while food pieces were placed in the opposite corner of the shelter to encourage the mice to stay outside the shelter. A monitor displaying the stimuli was placed on the top of the box. A camera on one side recorded the responses of the mice. The looming stimulus consisted of a black disc on a gray background with a diameter of 2° of the visual angle above the mouse head, expanded to 20° within 250 ms, and remained at this size for 250 ms. The stimulus was repeated 15 times and paused for 500 ms. A mouse was considered a responder to the stimulus if it demonstrated at least one of two behaviors: freezing or fleeing. Each mouse was subjected to one test. The box was thoroughly cleaned after each test.

Immunostaining of retinal cryosections and whole mounts

The mice were administered an overdose of anesthetic and were transcardially perfused with ice-cold PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosharp, China). After perfusion, the eyeballs were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA for 1 hour for later use. To prevent light-induced endogenous release of retinal DA, which could potentially affect c-fos expression in retinal neurons, mice undergoing c-fos experiments were all dark adapted overnight before sacrifice. Perfusion and dissection procedures were conducted under dim red-light conditions.

For retinal cryosection staining, eyeballs were first cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS overnight and then frozen in Optimal Cutting Temperature compound (4583, SAKURA, Japan) at −80°C. The frozen blocks containing tissue were sectioned at 14 μm (CM 1950, Leica, Germany). Sections were rinsed in PBS and blocked in 10% normal donkey serum (ASL050; Acmec, China), 5% bovine serum albumin (A1933; MilliporeSigma), and 0.1% Triton X-100 (T8200; Solarbio, China) for 1 hour. Sections were then incubated in a blocking solution containing primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. After washing three times with PBS, secondary antibodies and diluted DAPI (1:100; P0131; Beyotime, China) in blocking solution were added for 2 hours at room temperature. After three additional washing steps, the sections were mounted with an antifade mounting medium (S2100; Solarbio) for microscopy.

For retinal whole-mount staining, retinas were removed and blocked in 10% normal donkey serum, 5% bovine serum albumin, and 2% Triton X-100 for 1 hour. Retinas were then incubated in blocking solution containing primary antibodies overnight at 4°C and washed four times for 30 min each with PBS before another 2 hours of incubation with secondary antibodies at room temperature. The retinas were again washed four times for 30 min with PBS and mounted for microscopy.

Primary antibodies used were directed against c-fos (1:100; 2250 T; Cell Signaling Technology, USA), AP2α (1:50; 3B5; Developmental Studies Hybridoma Bank, USA), TH (1:100; MAB318; MilliporeSigma), pTH (1:100; 36-8600; Thermo Fisher Scientific), TUJ1 (1:500; 801202; Biolegend, USA), RBPMS (1:500; GTX118619; GeneTex, USA), SPP1 (1:100; AF808; R&D Systems, USA) OPN4 (1:500; PA1-780; Thermo Fisher Scientific), DRD1 (1:100; ab279713; Abcam), pCREB (1:100; ab32096; Abcam), p–c-Jun (1:100; 2361S; Cell Signaling Technology), pCaMKII (1:200; ab32678; Abcam), pAKT (1:100; 4060S; Cell Signaling Technology), pSTAT3 (1:100; 9145S; Cell Signaling Technology), pS6 (1:100; 2211S; Cell Signaling Technology), melanopsin (1:1000; AB-N39; Advanced Targeting Systems, USA), calretinin (1:1000; CG1; Swant, Switzerland), CD31 (1:300; AF3628; R&D Systems), PSD95 (1:50; ab12093; Abcam), and cAMP (1:100; ab76238; Abcam). Secondary antibodies included Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-mouse (1:500; ab150113; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti-rabbit (1:500; ab150077; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 488 donkey anti-goat (1:500; ab150129; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 555 goat anti-mouse (1:500; ab150114; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 555 donkey anti-rabbit (1:500; ab150062; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 594 donkey anti-goat (1:500; ab150132; Abcam), Alexa Fluor 647 goat anti-mouse (1:500; ab150115; Abcam), and Alexa Fluor 647 donkey anti-rabbit (1:500; ab150075; Abcam).

Retinal whole-mount microscopy and quantification

Images of retinal whole mounts were acquired using a Nikon confocal microscope (C2+; Nikon, Japan) and a Leica inverted fluorescence microscope (DMi8; Leica). For DAC counting and phosphorylation studies, four fields of 1260 μm by 1260 μm area were sampled from each retina (fig. S1A). For RGC and AC counting, RGC subtype identification, dextran labeling assessment, and vessel density quantification, eight fields of 538 μm by 538 μm area about 1.5 mm from the center of the retina were sampled from each retina.

Retinal section microscopy and quantification

Images of retinal sections were taken by the Nikon C2+ confocal microscope. To quantify the fluorescent intensity in RGCs and ACs, the mean immunostaining intensity of four representative fields was calculated as the intensity of each section, and the mean immunostaining intensity of three nonconsecutive sections was considered as the intensity of the retina. To quantify the fluorescent intensity in DACs, the mean immunostaining intensity of at least 30 DACs from 10 nonconsecutive sections was calculated to represent the whole retina.

Optic nerve clearance

The modified iDISCO (88) method with anterograde CTB labeling was used to visualize optic nerve clearance and axon regeneration. After transcardial perfusion with 4% PFA, the optic nerves were dissected and incubated in a series of 3, 60, and 90% methanol in PBS for 30 min at each concentration. The nerves were then switched to dichloromethane (DCM, 270997; MilliporeSigma)/methanol (2:1) for 20 min and 100% DCM for 20 min. Last, the nerves were transferred to dibenzyl ether (DBE, 33630; MilliporeSigma) and incubated overnight to achieve complete transparency. The cleared optic nerves were mounted using DBE for microscopy.

Evaluation of axon regeneration

CTB-prelabeled and cleared optic nerves were observed and captured by a Nikon confocal microscope using a Z-stack (10 μm per stack) and tile scan mode to acquire a full view of the nerves. We counted the regenerating axons that crossed the lines vertical to the optic nerve every 250 μm distal to the crush site until 2000 μm. Three representative Z-stacks at equal intervals for each nerve were selected to calculate the total number of axons. At each counting point in each stack, the number of axons (n), the width of the nerve (w), and the thickness of the optical section (t = 10 μm) were taken together to calculate the axon density, d=nw×t. The mean axon density of the three stacks was defined as D=d1+d2+d33. The total number of regenerating axons at each point was calculated as N = D × πr2, where r is the radius of the optic nerve.

Assessment of RGC projections to the LGN and SC

Three days after CTB injection, the mice were perfused with 4% PFA, and their brains were removed and postfixed in 4% PFA overnight. After fixation, the brains were cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS overnight and then frozen in the Optimal Cutting Temperature compound. Frozen brain tissues were sectioned coronally at 20 μm for the LGN and SC. The brain slices were rinsed in PBS, placed on a slide, and mounted with antifade mounting medium for microscopy. Three nonconsecutive sections of each LGN and SC were captured by the Nikon confocal microscope. The mean CTB intensity of LGN and SC in the three sections was considered as the intensity of the brain projecting targets. For the detection of surviving axons in the optic nerve, full views of the CTB-prelabeled and cleared optic nerves were captured by the Nikon confocal microscope using a Z-stack (10 μm per stack) and tile scan mode. The CTB intensity of the optic nerve was calculated and considered as the representation of surviving axons.

Single-cell sequencing data analysis

To analyze the gene expression of DA receptors, we used publicly available murine gene sets of RGCs (GSE137400) (49) and our previous single-cell sequencing data of retinal cells from mice (CRA006042) (47) and macaques (CRA026076). Downstream data analyses were performed using R (v4.1.3) with the R package Seurat (v4.1.1) (89).

Quantification

ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used for the quantification of retinal whole mount, retinal section, and cleared optic nerve.

Statistical analysis

Unpaired Student’s t test was used between the comparison of two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and two-way ANOVA with post hoc tests were used to compare multiple groups. Fisher’s exact test was used in the looming visual stimulus test to compare the responding behaviors in different groups. All data were presented as means ± SEM, and P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 9 (www.graphpad.com/scientific-software/prism/, GraphPad, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Details of the statistical analysis for each experiment were stated in the figure legends and data file S1.
