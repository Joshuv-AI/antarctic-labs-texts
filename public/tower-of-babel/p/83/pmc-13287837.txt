Clinical Validation of a Generative AI System for Diagnosing Ampullary Lesions: A Multicenter Study
Authors: Jang Ho Kwon, Ho Seung Lee, Seong Ji Choi, Kihwan Choi, Chang Mook Kang, Hoonsub So, Young Hoon Choi, Jae Min Lee, Kyoung Joo Lee, Jai Hoon Yoon, Dong‐Sup Jin, Hyo Jung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAccurate histologic classification of ampullary lesions is essential for guiding therapeutic decisions; however, conventional biopsy is limited by sampling error and false‐negative results. We evaluated the clinical utility of a generative artificial intelligence (AI)–based computer‐aided diagnosis (CAD) system that integrates real and synthetic endoscopic images to improve diagnostic performance.MethodsIn this retrospective multicenter study conducted across seven hospitals, duodenoscopic images were classified as Normal, Adenoma, or Cancer. A generative AI‐based CAD system using latent diffusion synthesized 500 images per class for data augmentation and was trained to predict histologic classes from endoscopic images. External validation assessed accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive values, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve. A reader study involving five expert and five trainee endoscopists compared diagnostic performance with and without CAD assistance.ResultsThe generative AI–based CAD system demonstrated high overall diagnostic accuracy (91.57%) and strong performance in identifying adenomas (accuracy, 88.76%). In the reader study, CAD assistance significantly increased adenoma sensitivity (63.47%–70.56%; p < 0.01), with corresponding improvements in predictive values. Both expert and trainee endoscopists benefited from CAD support, with reduced interobserver variability and consistent improvements across Normal, Adenoma, and Cancer classifications.ConclusionsA generative AI–based CAD system improved diagnostic accuracy and consistency in the evaluation of ampullary lesions. These findings support its potential as a clinically useful adjunct to routine duodenoscopy, particularly for improving adenoma recognition and supporting therapeutic decision‐making.Keywords: adenoma and adenocarcinoma, ampulla of vater, computer‐aided diagnosis, endoscopy, generative artificial intelligence

Introduction

Ampullary neoplasms range from benign adenomas to aggressive carcinomas [1, 2, 3, 4]. Premalignant ampullary adenomas are often curable by endoscopic papillectomy [5, 6]. Accurate pretreatment differentiation is therefore critical to avoid overtreatment, enable timely endoscopic resection, and ensure appropriate surgical referral [5, 7, 8].

Conventional diagnostic tools are limited: Endoscopic biopsy has up to 30% false negatives and substantial histologic discordance [9, 10]. Endoscopic ultrasound can provide complementary information but shows variable diagnostic accuracy [11].

Artificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated considerable potential in medical imaging, particularly in endoscopy [12, 13, 14]. However, applications of AI to the ampulla of Vater (AoV) remain limited.

In this study, we developed a generative AI–based CAD system to classify ampullary lesions as normal, adenomatous, or malignant. Using real‐world and synthetic images, we improved histopathologic prediction and evaluated clinical utility via external validation and a reader study.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This multicenter retrospective study reviewed duodenoscopic examinations performed between January 2013 and December 2023 at seven university‐affiliated hospitals in South Korea. Images from four centers were used for model training and internal validation, while those from three independent centers were used for external validation (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Eligible patients were adults aged 20–80 years who underwent duodenoscopy with available en face images of the AoV. Exclusion criteria included prior upper gastrointestinal surgery, previous therapeutic or diagnostic manipulation of the ampulla of Vater, inadequate image quality, non‐adenomatous and non‐malignant ampullary lesions, an insufficient number of analyzable images, and cases with insufficient follow‐up. The overall study workflow is shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1. Endoscopic images were retrospectively collected from each institution's picture archiving and communication systems (PACS). Images had been originally acquired during routine endoscopic procedures using standard side‐viewing duodenoscopes (JF‐260V, TJF‐260V, or TJF‐Q290V; Olympus Medical Systems, Tokyo, Japan) and endoscopic systems (EVIS LUCERA CV‐260SL/CLV‐260SL, EVIS LUCERA ELITE CV‐290/CLV‐290SL; Olympus Medical Systems).

Image Curation and Dataset Preparation

All images were standardized to 256 × 256 pixels and presented under identical conditions. Cases were categorized into three groups: Normal, Adenoma, and Cancer. For the Normal group, classification was based on endoscopic appearance and longitudinal follow‐up. In cases without biopsy, the AoV was considered normal if its appearance was unremarkable and remained stable for more than 1 year. In biopsy‐confirmed cases, classification as Normal required at least two negative biopsy results and morphologic stability for a minimum of 2 years. Final assignment to the Normal group required unanimous agreement among the three expert endoscopists based on endoscopic findings and follow‐up data.

Adenoma was defined by histologic confirmation of tubular or villous adenoma, including low‐ or high‐grade dysplasia, using specimens obtained through complete endoscopic or surgical resection. Cancer cases were defined by histologic confirmation of adenocarcinoma. Only images from the first diagnostic endoscopic session for each patient were included. For each Normal case, one representative high‐quality image was selected, whereas three representative images were selected for each Adenoma and Cancer cases. To minimize sampling bias and case overrepresentation, a limited number of representative images per case were selected, with class‐specific sampling to address imbalance.

The dataset was divided into training and validation sets derived from different institutions. The training set consisted of images collected from four hospitals and was used to develop the model using ten‐fold stratified cross‐validation. External validation was performed using images from three independent hospitals not involved in model development. To address limited sample size and class imbalance, synthetic image augmentation using a diffusion‐based generative model was applied, generating 500 synthetic images per class for the training set only. Synthetic images were excluded from internal and external validation, as well as from the reader study, to prevent data leakage and ensure unbiased performance assessment. For each synthetic image, the most similar real training image was identified based on cosine similarity in a deep feature space [15]. The Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM) and Learned Perceptual Image Patch Similarity (LPIPS) were subsequently computed for each pair [16, 17]

Expert Evaluation of Real and AI‐Generated Images

A perception test was conducted to assess whether expert endoscopists could distinguish synthetic images from real images. Five expert endoscopists, each with at least 5 years of independent clinical experience, independently reviewed a mixed dataset of 250 real and AI‐generated duodenoscopic images. Real images were randomly selected from the dataset, and synthetic images were randomly sampled from those generated by the diffusion model. Reviewers classified each image as “real” or “AI‐generated,” without time constraints or clinical information. Image order was randomized for each reviewer, and no feedback on accuracy was provided. For each reviewer, sensitivity, specificity, and overall accuracy were calculated from confusion matrices.

Development of the Generative CAD System

We developed a generative CAD system to predict the histologic categories using duodenoscopic images. The system comprised two components: image generation and histologic classification.

To address data scarcity and class imbalance, we implemented a latent diffusion model (LDM) to augment duodenoscopic image sets [18]. The LDM generated 500 synthetic images per class, which were combined with real images to form the training dataset. The overall workflow is illustrated in Figure 1.

Study workflow for evaluation of the generative CAD system for ampullary lesion classification. (a) Generative data augmentation: A latent diffusion–based generative AI model synthesizes images from imbalanced real‐world datasets to construct an augmented training dataset. (b) Model development: An ensemble classifier is trained using the augmented dataset to predict histologic categories. (c) Reader study and validation: The trained model provides diagnostic assistance to expert and novice endoscopists. Diagnostic performance is compared between unaided and CAD‐assisted interpretations using an external validation dataset.

The histologic classifier was based on the EfficientNet‐V2 architecture, pretrained on ImageNet, and fine‐tuned to classify ampullary lesions as Normal, Adenoma, or Cancer. For the final prediction, outputs from multiple classifiers were combined using an ensemble approach, with model parameters selected through internal cross‐validation (Supporting Information S1: Tables S2 and S3).

Evaluation of the CAD System

The CAD system was evaluated using an independent multicenter validation dataset consisting exclusively of real endoscopic images. For cases with multiple images, classification scores were averaged to derive a case‐level diagnosis. As an alternative strategy, the classifier randomly selected a single image per case and predicted the histologic category. This random sampling procedure was repeated 100 times, and the mean and standard deviation of performance metrics were reported. The conceptual workflow is shown in Figure 2.

Relationship between the histology prediction model and the image generation model in the proposed CAD framework. The nongenerative CAD system (left) relies exclusively on original images, resulting in limited feature separation among normal tissue, adenoma, and cancer. In contrast, the generative model (middle) learns domain‐specific representations and produces synthetic ampullary images for dataset augmentation. The generative CAD system (right) integrates original and generated images, yielding improved feature distributions and clearer class separation for histologic prediction. Filled, outlined, and circled shapes represent original, generated, and test images, respectively.

Diagnostic performance was assessed using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Confusion matrices were visualized as a flow diagram to illustrate transitions between true and predicted classes. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were also calculated.

Clinical Application in Endoscopists

To evaluate the clinical utility of the CAD system, five expert endoscopists (≥ 5 years of independent duodenoscopy experience) and five trainees (< 2 years of supervised experience) participated in a reader study using the external validation set. The reader study was conducted using only real images, with all cases included without additional selection and presented in randomized order. Individuals involved in image acquisition or affiliated with participating institutions were excluded to minimize bias.

The reader study was conducted in two sequential stages using all cases in the external validation set. Participants first classified images without CAD assistance, then reviewed the same images with CAD‐generated predictions and were allowed to revise their assessments. Diagnostic accuracy was calculated for each endoscopist under unaided and CAD‐assisted conditions.

Statistical Analysis

Diagnostic performance of the CAD system and human readers was evaluated using accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and AUC. ROC curves were generated by linearly interpolating binary reader decisions [19], and group‐level performance was summarized using vertically averaged ROC curves [20]. Confidence intervals (CIs) for AUCs were estimated using the DeLong method. For proportion‐based metrics, 95% CIs were calculated assuming a binomial distribution.

Paired binary outcomes were compared using McNemar's test. Comparisons between generative and non‐generative CAD systems and between unaided and CAD‐assisted readings were performed using generalized estimating equation (GEE) models with a logit link, accounting for within‐reader and within‐case correlations [21, 22]. An exchangeable working correlation structure with robust (sandwich) standard errors was applied. Odds ratios and absolute percentage differences (Δ%p) with 95% CIs were derived from GEE estimates to quantify CAD effects.

Given multiple endpoints and subgroup analyses, p values were interpreted descriptively, and no formal adjustment for multiplicity was applied. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R software.

Ethical Considerations and Data Anonymization

The study was approved by the institutional review boards of all participating centers, including Korea University Anam Hospital (IRB No. 2020AN0378), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design. All images were anonymized prior to analysis.

Results

Study Population

A total of 1273 patients and 2059 duodenoscopic images were included from seven centers. The training cohort comprised 917 patients (117 Cancer, 145 Adenoma, and 655 Normal cases) with 1441 images, whereas the external validation cohort included 356 patients (64 Cancer, 67 Adenoma, and 225 Normal cases) with 618 images (Supporting Information S1: Table S1). Evaluation of Generated Image Fidelity

Qualitative assessment of the latent diffusion–generated images demonstrated preservation of key anatomic features of the AoV, supporting adequate visual fidelity for training augmentation (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2). In the perception study, expert endoscopists were unable to reliably distinguish real images from AI‐generated images, achieving an overall accuracy of 50.8%, with PPV and NPV approximating chance‐level performance (Table 2). The measured cosine similarity, SSIM, and LPIPS between synthetic and real images were comparable to those observed within the real training set. No synthetic image reached SSIM > 0.95 or LPIPS < 0.05 against any real or synthetic image (Supporting Information S1: Table S4). Per‐class discrimination performance is presented in Supporting Information S1: Table S5.

Performance of Generative and Non‐Generative CAD Systems

The generative CAD system outperformed the non‐generative system, demonstrating higher accuracy (91.57% vs. 88.58%), sensitivity (85.18% vs. 80.39%), and specificity (94.56% vs. 92.86%) (Supporting Information S1: Table S6). PPV and NPV were also higher with the generative model (80.60% vs. 74.63% and 92.43% vs. 90.04%, respectively).

Classification outcomes are visualized using Sankey diagrams (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3). Correct classification rates increased from 91.0% to 93.3% for the Normal group, 84.3%–88.8% for the Adenoma group, and 90.4%–92.7% for the Cancer group. ROC curves comparing the two models are shown in Supporting Information S1: Figure S4. Across all classes, the generative model yielded higher AUC values, with narrower CIs, particularly for the Adenoma classification.

For normal lesions, CAD assistance significantly increased sensitivity (90.18% [95% CI, 88.98–91.42] vs. 91.78% [95% CI, 90.67–92.85]), specificity (89.33% [95% CI, 87.56–90.96] vs. 90.96% [95% CI, 89.41–92.52]), PPV (93.37% [95% CI, 92.36–94.43] vs. 94.42% [95% CI, 93.46–95.34]), and NPV (84.51% [95% CI, 82.55–86.41] vs. 86.91% [95% CI, 85.14–88.61]).

For adenomas, all diagnostic metrics were significantly improved with CAD assistance. Sensitivity increased from 63.47% (95% CI, 59.86–66.81) to 70.56% (95% CI, 67.08–73.89), specificity from 89.03% (95% CI, 87.85–90.21) to 90.56% (95% CI, 89.48–91.63), PPV from 59.12% (95% CI, 55.63–62.61) to 65.13% (95% CI, 61.67–68.33), and NPV from 90.70% (95% CI, 89.53–91.69) to 92.48% (95% CI, 91.45–93.44).

For cancers, CAD assistance significantly improved specificity (94.24% [95% CI, 93.37–95.05] vs. 95.35% [95% CI, 94.61–96.09]) and PPV (73.85% [95% CI, 70.34–77.06] vs. 78.20% [95% CI, 75.04–81.20]). Sensitivity (76.67% vs. 78.57%) and NPV (95.01% vs. 95.45%) increased numerically but did not reach statistical significance.

Across all lesion categories, overall diagnostic accuracy was significantly higher with CAD assistance (82.47% [95% CI, 81.22–83.69] vs. 85.22% [95% CI, 84.05–86.39]).

At the individual reader level, five of ten endoscopists (50%) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement with CAD assistance (p < 0.05). Although the remaining five did not reach statistical significance, most showed numerical improvements in accuracy. Notably, Physicians D and F exhibited substantial gains (p < 0.001). Reader‐specific changes in diagnostic accuracy are illustrated in Supporting Information S1: Figure S5.

Endoscopist Subgroup Performance With and Without CAD

In subgroup analyses, both expert and novice endoscopists demonstrated improved performance with CAD assistance (Supporting Information S1: Table S7). Among experts, overall accuracy increased significantly from 85.22% to 88.00%, whereas among novices, accuracy increased from 79.72% to 82.44%.

For normal lesions, CAD assistance significantly improved sensitivity and NPV for both experts and novices, whereas specificity and PPV improved significantly only among experts. Novices demonstrated significant gains in sensitivity and NPV, but changes in specificity and PPV were not statistically significant. For adenomas, all four diagnostic metrics improved significantly with CAD in both groups. For cancers, CAD assistance significantly improved specificity and PPV among both experts and novices. Sensitivity and NPV increased numerically in both groups but did not reach statistical significance.

With CAD assistance, the area under the ROC curve (AUROC) for adenomas increased significantly for both experts (0.790–0.830; p < 0.001) and novices (0.736–0.780; p < 0.001) (Figure 3). Significant improvements were also observed for normal lesions (p < 0.05). Although AUROC values increased for cancer classification, these changes were not statistically significant for either experts (p = 0.086) or novices (p = 0.202). In single‐image analysis, performance was modestly lower than that with multi‐image aggregation, with a consistent 1%–2% decrease across all metrics (Supporting Information S1: Table S8).

Discussion

Accurate diagnosis of ampullary lesions is essential because therapeutic decisions differ markedly among normal papillae, adenomas, and carcinomas [23]. However, reliable diagnostic accuracy during duodenoscopy is challenging owing to subtle visual differences and overlap among lesion categories. These limitations highlight the need for adjunctive tools.

In this multicenter study, we developed and validated a generative CAD system to classify ampullary lesions using endoscopic images. Across external validation cohorts, it showed high diagnostic accuracy, with the strongest performance for adenomas. In the reader study, CAD assistance increased adenoma sensitivity from 63.5% to 70.6% and overall accuracy from 82.5% to 85.2%. Improved adenoma recognition supports appropriate selection for endoscopic papillectomy and reduces unnecessary surgery and missed curative opportunities [24].

The role of AI in endoscopy has expanded rapidly [12, 13, 14, 25, 26]. Synthetic data generation has been explored in capsule endoscopy [27], generative adversarial network‐based esophageal cancer imaging [28], and LDM‐based polyp synthesis [29]. Our study extends this work by integrating generative augmentation into a multicenter diagnostic framework and evaluating its effect through external validation and a structured reader study, thereby strengthening its relevance to real‐world clinical practice.

This study demonstrates the clinical utility of CAD. Both expert and novice endoscopists showed improved diagnostic performance, particularly in adenoma detection, with higher sensitivity, predictive values, and overall accuracy. These findings are consistent with prior studies in endoscopic AI, which have demonstrated improved diagnostic consistency and reduced interobserver variability with CAD assistance [12, 13]. AUROC for adenoma classification also increased across experience levels, suggesting that CAD is a reliable adjunct that enhances interpretive consistency.

CAD support has clinical and educational value for both novice and expert endoscopists. It may narrow performance gaps by improving recognition of early neoplastic changes and reducing operator variability. For experts, CAD functions as a second reader, lowering cognitive load and highlighting subtle dysplastic features that might otherwise be missed.

Synthetic image fidelity was rigorously evaluated; in the perception study, AI‐generated images were indistinguishable from real images, aligning with prior endoscopic AI research [30]. These findings support the realism and clinical plausibility of the synthetic dataset for model training. LDM‐based generative augmentation improved classifier performance by adding diverse morphologic variations and addressing data scarcity and class imbalance, key challenges in ampullary AI research, thereby enhancing the CAD system's robustness across clinical settings. The inability of experts to distinguish synthetics from real images may reflect the high visual fidelity of the generated images. Subgroup analyses showed variable discrimination performance across lesion types, although overall specificity remained low and the findings should be interpreted with caution given the controlled study setting. To our knowledge, this is the first study to address the limited sample size and class imbalance in ampullary lesion diagnosis with AI‐generated images. Similar findings have been reported in recent endoscopic studies, where clinicians distinguished synthetic from real images only slightly above chance, with no differences by experience level, while AI‐generated images were considered clinically realistic in colonoscopy and wireless capsule endoscopy [31, 32].

Our findings have broad implications for clinical use. The CAD system maintained high diagnostic accuracy across various institutions and imaging environments, demonstrating robustness to differences in equipment and image quality. In ampullary evaluation, CAD‐assisted interpretation may standardize diagnostic performance and help reduce disparities between high‐ and low‐volume centers.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cohort study may inherently include potential selection biases. Benign non‐neoplastic conditions were not included, limiting generalizability across the full spectrum of ampullary abnormalities. Some normal cases lacked histologic confirmation, reflecting real‐world practice but introducing potential histological misclassification bias. Second, the retrospective multicenter design, modest sample size, and the limited number of still‐frame images may introduce bias and not fully capture real‐time assessment. Although externally validated, prospective multicenter studies incorporating both still images and video‐based analysis are warranted to confirm clinical impact and generalizability.

In conclusion, a generative AI–based CAD system improves diagnostic accuracy and consistency for ampullary lesions, enhancing adenoma and early cancer detection across experience levels. Prospective studies are needed to confirm its clinical impact.
