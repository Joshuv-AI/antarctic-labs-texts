Research note: Dietary resveratrol alleviates necrotic enteritis in broilers associated with in vitro inhibition of Clostridium perfringens and enhancement of host intestinal barrier function
Authors: Ziqiang Chen, Yiming Li, Xiaoyang Ma, Jian Meng, Nihar Ali, Zhe Wang, Yanchao Guo, Yuxin Li, Hongbin Si
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNecrotic enteritis (NE), primarily caused by Clostridium perfringens (CP), represents a significant threat to the poultry industry. Resveratrol (RES) possesses antimicrobial properties, yet its effects against CP and protective role in NE-challenged broilers remain incompletely understood. In this study, the in vitro antibacterial activity of RES against CP was evaluated, and its regulatory effects on bacterial gene expression were analyzed using prokaryotic RNA-seq. An NE infection model was established to investigate the effects of post-challenge dietary RES (125, 250, or 500 mg/kg) on growth performance, intestinal lesions, barrier function, and inflammatory responses. Resveratrol exhibited potent antibacterial activity against CP with a minimum inhibitory concentration of 0.16 mg/mL and dose-dependently inhibited bacterial sliding motility and biofilm formation. Transcriptome analysis revealed downregulation of peptidoglycan biosynthesis and energy metabolism pathways. In NE-challenged broilers, post-challenge dietary RES (250 mg/kg) improved average daily gain, reduced feed conversion ratio, attenuated intestinal lesions, decreased serum FITC-dextran concentrations, upregulated tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1) and mucin (MUC2), and suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6). These findings demonstrate that RES shows in vitro antibacterial activity against CP and is associated with improved intestinal barrier function and reduced inflammation in NE-challenged broilers, supporting its potential as a functional feed additive.Keywords: Clostridium perfringens, Intestinal barrier, Necrotic enteritis, Resveratrol, Transcriptome

Introduction

Necrotic enteritis (NE), caused by Clostridium perfringens (CP), is a major enteric disease that constrains sustainable poultry production, with annual economic losses exceeding USD 2 billion worldwide (Prescott et al., 2016). For decades, in-feed antibiotics have been used to control NE; however, antibiotic resistance and residue concerns have necessitated the development of safe, effective alternatives. Plant-derived polyphenols have attracted attention as potential antibiotic substitutes due to their antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory properties (Righi et al., 2021).

Resveratrol (trans-3,5,4′-trihydroxystilbene) is a naturally occurring polyphenol with documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antimicrobial activities against various pathogens. In poultry, dietary resveratrol (RES) has been reported to improve intestinal health under stress conditions (Ding et al., 2022). Despite these findings, the direct antibacterial activity of RES against CP—particularly its effects on bacterial metabolism and virulence-related phenotypes—remains poorly characterized. Moreover, integrative studies combining in vitro mechanistic insights with in vivo functional validation in NE-challenged broilers are lacking.

We hypothesized that RES may exert protective effects against NE through (i) direct inhibition of CP growth and virulence-related behaviors in vitro and (ii) improvement of host intestinal barrier integrity and immune homeostasis in vivo. To test this hypothesis, an integrative approach was employed: in vitro antibacterial assays and prokaryotic transcriptome sequencing were conducted to characterize RES-induced alterations in CP gene expression, followed by an in vivo NE challenge model to evaluate the effects of post-challenge dietary RES supplementation (days 24–28) on growth performance, intestinal lesions, barrier function, and inflammatory responses in broilers.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strain and reagents

CP type A CVCC2030 was obtained from the China Veterinary Culture Collection Center. Resveratrol (RES; purity ≥98%, CAS: 501-36-0) was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. A 100 mg/mL stock solution was prepared in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and stored at −20°C. Bacitracin methylene disalicylate (BMD) soluble powder was used as a positive control.

In vitro antibacterial assays

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of RES against CP was determined by the microbroth dilution method in Mueller-Hinton broth. RES was serially diluted (5 to 0.005 mg/mL), inoculated with 1 × 105 colony-forming units (CFU)/mL, and incubated anaerobically at 37°C for 16 h. DMSO (≤1%) and BMD served as solvent and positive controls, respectively. For sliding motility, log-phase bacteria were inoculated onto Brain Heart Infusion semi-solid medium (0.3% agar) containing 0, 0.08, 0.16, or 0.32 mg/mL RES; gliding zone diameters were measured after 72 h. Biofilm formation was quantified by crystal violet staining after 48 h incubation in 96-well plates with serial RES dilutions (0.078125–2.5 mg/mL).

Prokaryotic transcriptomic sequencing

CP was incubated to log phase and treated with 0.16 mg/mL RES (MIC) or DMSO for 3 h. Total RNA was extracted, and strand-specific libraries were constructed after rRNA depletion. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Clean reads were aligned to the CP CVCC2030 reference genome. Differential expression analysis was performed on raw counts using edgeR (|log2 fold change| > 1, false discovery rate < 0.05). Gene Ontology and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes enrichment analyses were performed using Goatools and KOBAS, respectively. Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed via STRING and visualized in Cytoscape; hub genes were identified using the Maximal Clique Centrality algorithm.

In vivo animal experiment

One-day-old male Cobb 500 broilers (n = 144) were obtained from Guangxi Dongshi Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. (Nanning, China) and randomly assigned to six groups (24 birds per group; 3 replicates of 8 birds each): uninfected untreated control (Con), infected untreated control (Mod), infected receiving BMD (200 mg/L in drinking water), and infected receiving low (LRes; 125 mg/kg), medium (MRes; 250 mg/kg), or high (HRes; 500 mg/kg) RES in the diet. Birds were housed in wire cages (0.8 m × 0.6 m × 0.45 m; 8 birds per cage; wire mesh floor, no litter) in a controlled-environment room with natural ventilation (temperature: 33°C at arrival, gradually reduced to 24°C by day 21; relative humidity: 60–70%; 24-h lighting program; ad libitum access to feed and water). The basal diet was a corn-soybean meal formulation (20% crude protein, 13.50 MJ/kg metabolizable energy) free of antibiotics, coccidiostats, probiotics, exogenous enzymes, and other gut-health additives. No vaccines were administered.

On day 14, all birds except those in the Con group were orally challenged with 1 mL of Eimeria maxima oocysts (1 × 105 oocysts/mL), whereas the Con group received 1 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). From days 17 to 23, challenged groups received 1 mL of CP culture (1 × 109 CFU/mL) daily, while the Con group received PBS. From days 24 to 28, the BMD group received 200 mg/L BMD in drinking water, and the LRes, MRes, and HRes groups received diets supplemented with 125, 250, and 500 mg/kg RES, respectively. Thus, RES and BMD were administered as post-challenge interventions during the recovery phase (days 24–28). The experiment was conducted at the Poultry Experimental Base of Guangxi University. All procedures were approved by the Guangxi University Animal Protection and Welfare Committee (approval no. GXU-2024-337).

Measurements and sampling

Growth performance was recorded by replicate (pen as the experimental unit, n = 6 birds per group, calculated as 3 replicates × 2 birds weighed per replicate). Broilers were weighed after a 12-h fast on days 14 and 29; feed intake was recorded throughout days 14–29 to calculate average daily feed intake (ADFI), average daily gain (ADG), and feed conversion ratio (FCR).

On day 29, three birds per replicate were euthanized by cervical dislocation for intestinal lesion scoring (0–4 scale; 0 = no lesions, 4 = severe diffuse necrosis) by three independent observers blinded to treatment. Intestinal permeability was determined following the method described by previous literature (Pham et al., 2022).Two birds per replicate were orally administered fluorescein isothiocyanate-dextran (FITC-d; 4.17 mg/kg body weight) 2 h before blood collection via jugular vein puncture. Serum FITC-d concentrations were measured to assess intestinal permeability. Blood samples were centrifuged (3,000 × g, 4°C, 15 min) for serum collection. Jejunal segments were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histomorphology (5-μm transverse sections stained with hematoxylin and eosin; blinded assessment) or snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for molecular analysis.Unless otherwise specified, all tissue and blood samples were collected from two birds per replicate (n = 6 birds per group)

Total RNA was extracted from jejunal tissues, and complementary DNA was synthesized. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was performed using SYBR Green Mix on a LightCycler 480II system, with β-actin as the internal control. Relative mRNA expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1), mucin (MUC2), and cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α) was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Serum catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities, and malondialdehyde (MDA) content, were measured using commercial kits. Jejunal concentrations of calprotectin, IgA, IgM, and IgY were determined by chicken-specific ELISA.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Growth performance, gene expression, antioxidant, and immunological data were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test, with replicate pen as the experimental unit. Intestinal lesion scores were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05 and a trend at 0.05 ≤ P < 0.10.

Results and discussion

In vitro antibacterial activity

It has been reported that RES possesses broad-spectrum antimicrobial activity against common pathogens (e.g., Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli) (Ruan et al., 2021). RES exhibited potent antibacterial activity against CP with an MIC of 0.16 mg/mL, comparable to bacitracin. The solvent control (DMSO, ≤1%) showed no inhibition. Resveratrol dose-dependently impaired sliding motility: gliding zone diameters decreased from 26.2 mm (control) to 15.2, 10.5, and 0 mm at 0.08, 0.16, and 0.32 mg/mL, respectively. Biofilm inhibition exceeded 50% at concentrations ≥0.31 mg/mL and reached >90% at ≥1.25 mg/mL (Fig. 1). These findings indicate that RES directly suppresses CP proliferation and virulence-related phenotypes in vitro.

Transcriptomic alterations

To preliminarily explore the molecular mechanisms underlying the observed in vitro anti-bacterial activity, RNA-seq of CP treated with RES (MIC, 3 h) was conducted, identifying 512 differentially expressed genes (252 upregulated, 260 downregulated). Downregulated genes were significantly enriched in peptidoglycan biosynthesis (e.g., murA, ddl) and teichoic acid biosynthesis (uppP), suggesting that RES severely disrupts bacterial cell wall integrity and energy metabolism (Ruan et al., 2021). However, these findings derive strictly from in vitro exposure and should be interpreted with caution. Because the in vivo study did not quantify intestinal CP load or toxin expression, direct modulation of CP metabolism in vivo remains speculative, and these in vitro pathways serve primarily as prospective antibacterial targets.

Growth performance

NE reduces growth performance and elevates FCR via CP-induced intestinal mucosal damage and malabsorption (Li et al., 2024). During the recovery phase (days 24–29), the NE challenge reduced ADG and increased FCR compared with the unchallenged control (Mod: 29.91 g and 2.34 vs. Con: 37.22 g and 2.05; P < 0.05). Dietary RES at 250 mg/kg (MRes) increased ADG by 3.38% and reduced FCR by 4.27% compared with the Mod group (30.92 g and 2.24; P < 0.05), with performance comparable to the BMD group (32.58 g and 2.18). No further improvement was observed at 500 mg/kg (HRes: 30.89 g and 2.29). These results indicate that RES partially improved growth performance during recovery but did not fully restore levels to the unchallenged control, suggesting a plateau effect at higher doses.

Intestinal lesions, permeability, and morphology

Broilers in the Mod group exhibited severe hemorrhage and necrotic plaques across the small intestine. The MRes group showed the mildest lesions, with score reductions of 58.3%, 62.5%, and 55.6% in the duodenum, jejunum, and ileum, respectively, compared with Mod (P < 0.05). Serum FITC-d concentrations were highest in the Mod group (2.8-fold increase vs. Con; P < 0.05) and decreased by 35.2% in the MRes group (P < 0.05), falling below the BMD level. Histologically, the Mod group exhibited severe villus rupture and shedding, whereas MRes restored the structure of villi and increased the ratio of villi height to recess depth. (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). These improvements suggest that post-challenge RES supplementation promotes intestinal structural repair during recovery (Wang et al., 2021).

Intestinal barrier function and inflammation

The NE challenge significantly downregulated jejunal mRNA expression of tight junction proteins (Occludin, ZO-1) and mucin (MUC2) in the Mod group (P < 0.05). Resveratrol treatment upregulated Occludin and MUC2 in all dose groups, with MRes restoring expression to >85% of Con levels; ZO-1 was also significantly increased in the MRes group. Concurrently, the Mod group exhibited elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α) and reduced IL-10 (P < 0.05). RES significantly suppressed IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α while increasing IL-10; in the MRes group, IL-6 decreased by 42.3% and IL-10 increased by 58.7% (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2). These findings demonstrate that RES enhances both physical and chemical intestinal barriers and rebalances inflammatory responses during the recovery phase.

Antioxidant capacity and mucosal immunity

Due to space constraints of the Research Note format, detailed antioxidant and immunological data are presented in the text without accompanying figures.The NE challenge reduced serum catalase (CAT) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activities and increased malondialdehyde (MDA) content (P < 0.05). Resveratrol dose-dependently restored antioxidant status: in the HRes group, SOD increased by 31.2% and MDA decreased by 28.6% compared with Mod (P < 0.05). Jejunal calprotectin, IgA, IgM, and IgY concentrations were elevated in the Mod group (P < 0.05). Resveratrol significantly reduced calprotectin and immunoglobulin levels compared with Mod, although they remained above Con values, indicating alleviation of excessive inflammation without immunosuppression.

Integration of in vitro and in vivo findings

The present data demonstrate that RES possesses direct in vitro antibacterial activity against CP and is associated with improved intestinal health outcomes in NE-challenged broilers. However, because intestinal CP colonization, toxin gene expression, and bacterial shedding were not quantified in vivo, the contribution of direct bacterial suppression to the in vivo protective effect remains speculative. The protective outcomes likely reflect a combination of potential direct antibacterial effects and host-mediated barrier restoration, anti-inflammatory activity, and antioxidant enhancement. The medium dose of 250 mg/kg was optimal for lesion repair, barrier restoration, and growth performance, whereas 500 mg/kg provided no additional benefit, suggesting that excessive polyphenol intake may interfere with nutrient absorption or microbiota balance.

In general, resveratrol showed potent in vitro antibacterial activity against CP and was associated with improved intestinal barrier function, reduced inflammation, enhanced antioxidant capacity, and partial growth performance recovery in broilers during the post-challenge phase. These findings support the potential of post-challenge dietary resveratrol (250 mg/kg) as a natural antibiotic alternative in poultry production. Further studies incorporating in vivo bacterial quantification and transcriptomic target validation are needed to clarify the relative contributions of direct antimicrobial versus host-mediated mechanisms.
