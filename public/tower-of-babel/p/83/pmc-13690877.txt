Adjunctive use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists after bariatric surgery: updated systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Sakshi Arora, Fnu Amna, Fatemeh Akbarpoor, Dhvanit Rajdeep, Muhammad Khalid, Gabriel Moura Machado, Niman Maalin, Asad Ur Rehman, Marconi Abreu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsWe meta-analyzed randomized trials to assess efficacy and safety of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) after bariatric surgery.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Central for randomized trials comparing GLP-1 RAs with placebo after bariatric surgery. Primary outcomes were change in body weight (kg) and HbA1c (%) at 6 and 12 months. We also assessed percent total body weight loss (%TBWL), quality of life, and adverse events. We pooled mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) using random effects and assessed heterogeneity with I² statistic. We also performed an exploratory subgroup analysis that compared very early (≤ 2 months) versus late (≥ 18 months) initiation after surgery.ResultsSeven RCTs (n = 460) met inclusion criteria; all evaluated liraglutide. GLP-1 RAs increased weight loss at 6 months (MD − 5.33 kg; 95% CI − 9.42 to − 1.23; p = 0.02) but not at 12 months (MD − 5.05 kg; 95% CI − 22.14 to 12.05; p = 0.33). GLP-1 RAs increased %TBWL at 6 months (MD 5.13%; 95% CI 2.14 to 8.11; p = 0.007) but not at 12 months (MD 3.96%; 95% CI − 3.33 to 11.26; p = 0.18). HbA1c did not differ at 6 or 12 months. Quality of life improved at 6 months in the trials reporting this outcome. Gastrointestinal adverse events were similar between groups. Subgroup analyses suggested differences by initiation timing.ConclusionGLP-1 RA therapy improved short-term weight outcomes after bariatric surgery, with no statistically significant between-group differences at 12 months. Timing of initiation may influence response and should be explored in trials with longer follow up.Trial registry: PROSPERO ID CRD420251251321.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00592-026-02730-4.

Introduction

Bariatric surgery is the standard of care for severe obesity and its metabolic complications [1]. However, about 30% of patients do not lose enough weight or regain weight within two to five years after surgery [2]. When this happens, the metabolic benefits can fade over time, with worsening glucose control and recurrence of obesity-related conditions such as dyslipidemia and hypertension.

After bariatric surgery, physiologic adaptation can increase appetite and reduce energy expenditure [3, 4]. Together, these physiologic responses can slow weight loss and contribute to a plateau or weight regain over time. In this population, GLP-1 RAs may counteract the drive to overeat by improving satiety, supporting further weight loss, helping preserve early surgical gains, and potentially lowering cardiometabolic risk [5, 6].

Most available data on GLP-1 RA use after bariatric surgery comes from small, single center observational studies that often lack a comparator group and vary widely in procedure type, time from surgery, dosing, and treatment duration. For example, clinicians may preferentially offer GLP-1 RAs to individuals with higher BMI and may choose more potent agents to maximize weight loss or avoid full dose titration to reduce adverse effects and discontinuation. More recently, randomized, placebo-controlled trials of liraglutide have shown benefit in some post bariatric populations, which strengthens causal inference [6–12]. Prior systematic reviews included mostly observational studies and focused on short term weight change, while clinically meaningful outcomes such as glycemic control, cardiometabolic risk factors, quality of life, and adverse effects were reported inconsistently [13–15]. As a result, key questions remain, including who benefits most, when to start therapy, and how GLP-1 RAs affect cardiometabolic outcomes, quality of life, and long-term safety in adequately powered randomized trials. Additionally, since those reviews were published, two additional RCTs have been reported [7, 10].

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis restricted to RCTs and assessed weight-related outcomes, cardiometabolic parameters, quality of life, and adverse events. We also performed a subgroup analysis to examine whether the timing of GLP-1 RA initiation after surgery influenced 6-month percent total body weight loss (%TBWL), comparing very early initiation (within 2 months) versus late initiation (18 months or more) after surgery. Our objective was to determine whether GLP-1 RA therapy improves weight loss and glycemic outcomes compared with placebo and whether these effects persist over time.

Methods

We conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported the findings according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [16, 17]. We prospectively registered the protocol in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; ID: CRD420251251321).

Eligibility criteria

We included studies that met all the following criteria: (1) randomized controlled trials (RCTs), (2) compared GLP-1 RAs with placebo, (3) enrolled adults who had undergone bariatric surgery, and (4) reported at least one outcome of interest. We excluded observational studies, case reports, commentaries, abstracts, editorials, letters, and reviews, as well as studies with missing information on the intervention or control, studies that did not include the population or outcomes of interest, and studies published as abstracts only or without a full manuscript.

Search strategy

Two authors (S.A. and G.M.) systematically searched PubMed, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, and Embase from inception to 11 December 2025. We used the following terms: “bariatric surgery,” “metabolic surgery,” “weight loss surgery,” “glucagon like peptide 1,” “GLP-1,” “liraglutide,” and “semaglutide.” The complete search strategy is provided in Supplementary Sect. 1. We also performed backward snowballing by screening the reference lists of included studies to identify additional trials. In our registered protocol, dual incretin agonists (e.g., tirzepatide) were not prespecified. In response to reviewer feedback, we re-ran the search including the term “tirzepatide” and “dual incretin/dual agonist” and did not identify any eligible randomized trials in the post-bariatric surgery setting.

Study selection and data extraction

Two authors (S.A. and G.M.) manually removed duplicates and independently screened titles and abstracts using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) [18]. They then reviewed full text studies based on predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with input from a third author (F.A.).

Two authors (S.A. and N.Q.) extracted baseline characteristics and outcome data. We extracted baseline data which included author, year of publication, sample size, mean demographic characteristics (e.g., age, sex, baseline BMI), type of GLP-1 RA, type of bariatric surgery, type 2 diabetes status, mean HbA1c and mean time since metabolic bariatric surgery. When studies reported values in different units, we converted them to uniform units as described in Supplementary Sect. 2.

Quality and certainty assessment

Two authors (S.A. and D.R.) independently assessed risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [19]. We rated each domain as low, some concerns, or high risk of bias and presented the results in a traffic light plot. The same authors assessed the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) framework [20]. Disagreements were resolved by consensus with input from the senior author (M.A.).

Outcomes

We assessed the following primary outcomes: change in body weight (kg) and HbA1c (%) at 6 and 12 months after starting GLP-1 RA or placebo in adults post bariatric surgery. We assessed the following secondary outcomes: change in BMI, fasting blood glucose (FBG), and percent total body weight loss (%TBWL) at 6 and 12 months; change in High density lipoprotein (HDL), Low density lipoprotein (LDL), and total cholesterol at 6 months; and change in systolic blood pressure (SBP) at 6 months. We assessed quality of life using the Impact of Weight on Quality of Life (IWQOL) total score at 6 months, a validated, obesity specific patient reported outcome measure that captures the effect of body weight on physical function, self-esteem, sexual life, public distress, and work-related activities, with higher scores indicating better quality of life. We also assessed safety outcomes (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache, decreased appetite). We collected 6 month outcome data between weeks 24 and 26.

Subgroup analysis

To assess whether timing of GLP-1 RA initiation after surgery influenced percent total body weight loss (%TBWL) at 6 months, we performed a subgroup analysis stratified by initiation timing. Studies were classified as very early initiation (GLP-1 RA started within 2 months after surgery) or late initiation (GLP-1 RA started more than 18 months after surgery), and we estimated the pooled 6-month change in % TBWL separately for each subgroup. This subgroup analysis was exploratory/hypothesis-generating, and we did not perform a formal test for interaction between subgroups.

Statistical analysis

We calculated dichotomous outcomes using relative risk (RR) and continuous outcomes using mean difference (MD), each with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We calculated effect estimates using the inverse variance method.

We calculated study weights using the Mantel Haenszel (MH) approach. We treated two-sided p values < 0.05 as statistically significant. We used a random effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) to account for methodological and demographic variability. We assessed heterogeneity using the Cochrane Q test and I2 statistic, with p > 0.10 and I²>25% considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. To address uncertainty in τ² estimation, we also applied the Hartung Knapp Sidik Jonkman (HKSJ) method to provide more robust confidence intervals in the random effects framework. For intention to treat analysis, we extracted outcome specific sample sizes based on the number of participants with available data at each follow up time point and analyzed participants according to the original randomization. We extracted per protocol data when intention to treat data were not available. When studies reported medians and interquartile ranges for outcomes of interest, we converted these to means and standard deviations using the Luo and Wan method [21]. We calculated mean change from baseline as the difference between follow up and baseline values and derived the corresponding standard deviation using the Cochrane formula, assuming a within person correlation coefficient (r) of 0.5 [22].

We conducted a leave one out sensitivity analysis to evaluate the influence of individual trials on the pooled effect estimates. We conducted all statistical analyses using R software (version 4.5.2) [23].

Results

Study selection and baseline characteristics

Figure 1 shows the study selection process. The initial search identified 1,530 records. After removing 408 duplicates, we excluded 1,102 records based on title and abstract screening. We assessed 20 full text articles for eligibility and included 7 RCTs (n = 460). Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics. All trials used liraglutide: 266 (57.8%) participants received liraglutide and 194 (42.1%) received placebo. Three trials initiated therapy within 2 months after surgery, whereas four initiated therapy 18 months or later. Across trials, participants underwent RYGB, VSG or LAGB. Mean age ranged from 37 to 57 years and the proportion of female participants ranged from 50% to 92%. The prevalence of type 2 diabetes, when reported, ranged from 6.5% to 100%. Follow up ranged from 6 to 12 months. Studies with 24 or 26 weeks follow up were categorized as 6 months.

Outcomes

Weight

Five studies reported change in body weight (kg) at 6 months and three at 12 months. Compared with placebo, GLP-1 RA reduced body weight at 6 months (MD = − 5.33 kg; 95% CI − 9.42 to − 1.23; p = 0.02; I² = 77.5%; Fig. 2A), but the difference was not statistically significant at 12 months (MD = − 5.05 kg; 95% CI − 22.14 to 12.05; p = 0.33; I² = 74.5%; Fig. 2B).

Change in body weight (kg) and HbA1c (%) between GLP-1 RA and placebo at 6 and 12 months

Body mass index (BMI)

Four studies reported BMI change (kg/m²) at 6 and 12 months. GLP-1 RA reduced BMI at 6 months (MD = − 1.03 kg/m²; 95% CI − 1.51 to − 0.55; p < 0.01; I² = 0%; Supplementary Fig. 2A), with no statistically significant difference at 12 months (MD = − 1.49 kg/m²; 95% CI − 4.74 to 1.75; p = 0.24; I² = 94.3%; Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Percentage total body weight loss (% TBWL)

Six studies reported % TBWL change at 6 months and four at 12 months. GLP-1 RA increased % TBWL at 6 months (MD = 5.13%; 95% CI 2.14 to 8.11; p < 0.01; I² = 95.6%; Supplementary Fig. 3A), but the difference was not statistically significant at 12 months (MD = 3.96%; 95% CI − 3.33 to 11.26; p = 0.18; I² = 91.8%; Supplementary Fig. 3B).

Impact of weight on quality of life (IWQOL)

Three studies reported IWQOL total score at 6 months. GLP-1 RA improved mean change in IWQOL total score from baseline at 6 months (MD = 5.83; 95% CI 3.12 to 8.54; p = 0.01; I² = 0; Supplementary Fig. 1).

Glycosylated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)

Five studies reported HbA1c change (%) at 6 months and three at 12 months. GLP-1 RA did not improve HbA1c at 6 months (MD = − 0.34%; 95% CI − 1.18 to 0.5; p = 0.32; I² = 89.5%; Fig. 2C) or 12 months (MD = 0.05%; 95% CI − 1.64 to 1.74; p = 0.91; I² = 75.3%; Fig. 2D).

Fasting blood glucose (FBG)

Four studies reported FBG change (mmol/L [mg/dL]) at 6 months and three at 12 months. GLP-1 RAs did not improve FBG at 6 months (MD = 0.00 mmol/L [0 mg/dl]; 95% CI − 0.93 to 0.94; p = 0.99; I² = 49.6%; Supplementary Fig. 4A) or 12 months (MD = − 0.06 mmol/L [−1.1 mg/dl]; 95% CI − 3.64 to 3.52; p = 0.95; I² = 80.6%; Supplementary Fig. 4B).

Systolic blood pressure (SBP)

Three studies reported SBP change (mm Hg) at 6 months. GLP-1 RAs did not change SBP (MD = 0.51 mm Hg; 95% CI − 2.76 to 3.77; p = 0.57; I² = 0%; Supplementary Fig. 5).

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL) and total cholesterol

Three studies reported changes in LDL (mmol/mol), HDL (mmol/mol), and total cholesterol (mmol/mol) at 6 months. We found no significant differences between groups for LDL (MD = − 0.13 mmol/mol; 95% CI − 0.55 to 0.29; p = 0.31; I² = 0%), HDL (MD = − 0.06 mmol/mol; 95% CI − 0.32 to 0.21; p = 0.47; I² = 28.1%), or total cholesterol (MD = − 0.22 mmol/mol; 95% CI − 1.27 to 0.83; p = 0.46; I² = 62.2%) (Supplementary Fig. 6A–C).

Sub-group analysis

Six studies reported % TBWL at 6 months. In this exploratory subgroup analysis, 2 studies started GLP-1 RA with in 2 months after surgery (very early initiation) and 4 studies started GLP-1 RA 18 months after surgery (late initiation). GLP-1 RA did not improve % TBWL for very early initiation subgroup (MD = 0.26%; 95% CI −0.18 to 0.70; p = 0.08; I² = 0%; Fig. 3A) but increased % TBWL for late initiation subgroup (MD = 5.54%; 95% CI 0.98 to 10.1; p = 0.03; I² = 97.3%; Fig. 3B).

Subgroup analysis: % TBWL at 6 months in groups with early (< 2 months) vs. late (> 18 months) initiation of GLP-1 RA post-surgery

Safety outcomes

Decreased appetite was more common with GLP-1 RAs than with placebo (RR = 3.46; 95% CI 1.59 to 7.51; p = 0.02; I² = 0%; Supplementary Fig. 7A). We found no differences in the risk of nausea, vomiting, diarrhea or headache (Supplementary Fig. 7B–E). We report decreased appetite as an adverse event because it was collected as a safety outcome in the included trials, although it may also reflect a pharmacologic effect.

Sensitivity analysis

Due to high heterogeneity, we performed a leave one out sensitivity analysis by removing one study at a time to confirm that the pooled results were not driven by a single trial. We present the results in Supplementary Fig. 8.

Risk of bias

We evaluated risk of bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for all seven included RCTs, with detailed results in Supplementary Fig. 9. The Lofton study raised concerns about outcome measurement because COVID-19 restrictions shifted assessments to remote, participant-reported measurements rather than direct measurements and led to missing outcomes that required in-person evaluation. The Brown study raised concerns about missing outcome data because prolonged COVID-19 lockdowns disrupted recruitment and follow up, and the trial ended early after the study medication became unavailable, leaving many participants without the planned 12-month assessments.

Certainty of evidence

We used GRADE framework to assess certainty of evidence for clinical outcomes based on risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. The evidence suggests moderate certainty in improvement in quality of life and weight loss at 6 months. Certainty was very low for body weight change at 12 months and for HbA1c change at 6 and 12 months. Certainty was low for % TBWL in subgroup of early initiators of liraglutide and very low for the late initiator group. We downgraded these outcomes mainly for imprecision and inconsistency. While short term weight benefits were evident, long term metabolic durability in this surgical population remains uncertain. We provide full GRADE assessment in Supplementary Fig. 10.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we evaluated the efficacy and safety of GLP-1 RAs in adults after metabolic bariatric surgery. All RCTs used liraglutide. Across seven RCTs (n = 460), GLP-1 RA therapy improved weight outcomes at 6 months, increased percent TBWL, and improved quality of life without increasing adverse events. At 12 months, between group differences were not statistically significant, and the combination of wide confidence intervals and heterogeneity limits inference about durability. Fewer trials contributed 12 month data, which further reduced precision. Several included trials also experienced COVID-related disruptions, and longer-term outcome data were limited. GLP-1 RAs did not improve HbA1c, FBG, lipids, or blood pressure at 6 or 12 months, and heterogeneity across studies was considerable. Subgroup analyses suggested that effects may differ by timing of initiation (≤ 2 months vs. ≥ 18 months after surgery), but these findings require cautious interpretation since this analysis was exploratory and not designed to formally test effect modification.

Bariatric surgery promotes weight loss through more than food restriction alone. The anatomical changes from surgery can speed nutrient delivery to the distal intestine, increasing L-cell stimulation and postprandial GLP-1 levels [24]. These anatomy driven hormonal changes may help explain why many individuals experience substantial and durable weight loss after bariatric surgery [25]. Early after surgery, this hormonal surge is often strongest and may be a major driver of weight loss. Individual differences in GLP-1 receptor sensitivity, gastric emptying, resting energy expenditure, vagal signaling, and genetic factors may contribute to a blunted response in some patients. Over months to years, changes in eating patterns, weight set point adaptation, and gut remodeling may further weaken this signal, contributing to weight regain or suboptimal weight loss.

Given this variability, pharmacologic GLP-1 receptor activation may be especially useful for patients with a weaker endogenous response. In the BARI-OPTIMISE trial [12], investigators enrolled participants with documented low GLP-1 responses and tested whether adding liraglutide could produce further weight loss. At 24 weeks, participants receiving liraglutide lost 8.82% of body weight versus 0.54% with placebo. These results suggest that pharmacologic GLP-1 receptor activation may help offset reduced endogenous GLP-1 signaling in selected participants.

Our subgroup analysis suggests that starting GLP-1 RA therapy very early after surgery may not add a clear short-term weight benefit. Early postoperative weight loss is largely driven by surgical changes, hormonal shifts, and caloric restriction [26], which may have limited additional benefit of GLP-1 RA therapy during this phase. In contrast, initiation later, once postoperative weight loss has slowed, may be more clinically relevant. At that stage, GLP-1 RA therapy may help with weight maintenance, mitigate weight regain, and support additional weight loss. However, heterogeneity remained substantial in the late-initiation subgroup, so these findings should be interpreted cautiously.

Several observational studies suggest that GLP-1 RA therapy after bariatric surgery can support additional weight loss in individuals with suboptimal response [27, 28]. However, observational estimates can overstate treatment effects because therapy is often initiated in patients with higher BMI or more pronounced weight regain, and dosing decisions may differ across patients without a standardized comparator. Our analysis helps address this limitation by restricting the evidence base to randomized trials. In this RCT-only meta-analysis, GLP-1 RAs improved weight outcomes at 6 months, while between-group differences were not statistically significant at 12 months. Importantly, all included RCTs evaluated liraglutide, which limits generalizability to contemporary agents. Post-bariatric observational data suggest greater weight-loss with semaglutide than with liraglutide, and a network meta-analysis also supports substantial weight and cardiometabolic effects with semaglutide in obesity populations [29, 30]. Because all included RCTs evaluated liraglutide, the effect sizes reported here may underestimate what could be achieved with contemporary incretin-based therapies.

In addition to the pooled estimates, individual trials provided several clinically relevant signals. In one trial, 76% of participants receiving a GLP-1 RA achieved at least 5% additional weight loss, nearly 25% below their prior postsurgical lowest weight [7]. In one study of participants with persistent type 2 diabetes, GLP-1 RA therapy reduced HbA1c by 1.22% (13.3 mmol/mol), suggesting potential benefits beyond weight loss in patients who did not achieve diabetes remission after surgery [6]. Taken together, these findings align with our pooled results: GLP-1 RAs improved short term weight outcomes versus placebo at 6 months. At 12 month between group differences were not statistically significant and estimates were imprecise.

Bariatric surgery can induce early diabetes remission, and glycemic improvement can precede maximal weight loss [31]. For that reason, the lack of a clear between group HbA1c difference in our pooled analysis was expected, since many participants had already normalized HbA1c after surgery. Bariatric surgery also improves cardiometabolic outcomes and is associated with lower mortality [32, 33], likely through modest improvements across multiple risk factors driven by sustained weight loss. Even so, many patients still carry residual cardiometabolic risk after surgery. In that setting, GLP-1 RAs may add value beyond incremental weight loss by further reducing major cardiovascular events, as shown in large outcome trials and meta-analyses [34]. GLP-1 RA therapy may also offer a pharmacologic option for patients with weight regain or suboptimal weight loss after bariatric surgery [35]. This approach may reduce the need for revisional procedures, which carry higher morbidity than primary surgery [36].

Across the included trials, GLP-1 RAs were generally well tolerated after bariatric surgery even in the studies initiating GLP-1RAs within 2 months of surgery. Even though gastrointestinal adverse events occurred in some participants they rarely led to treatment discontinuation. Most adverse events were mild and self-limiting, and no serious adverse events were attributed to the study drug during follow up.

In our pooled analysis, heterogeneity was high, especially at 12 months (I² = 93–98% for weight and glycemic outcomes), which reduces the confidence in the long-term estimates. This probably reflects genuine differences across the trials. Studies varied in procedure type (RYGB, VSG, LAGB), GLP-1 RA dose, patient populations (weight regain vs. insufficient weight loss), and when treatment started after surgery, and those design choices can shift the expected course of weight and glycemic change after surgery and the apparent response to therapy [6–12].

Our analysis has important limitations. First, only seven trials were included, which limited statistical power and prevented more detailed subgroup analyses. Second, heterogeneity was substantial across outcomes. Third, follow up was limited to 12 months, with many only including data up to 6 months, so we could not assess longer term efficacy or safety. Fourth, we cannot exclude publication bias given the small number of trials. Fifth, the internal validity of this meta-analysis may be limited by potential risk of bias in two trials where COVID-19 disruptions necessitated a shift to remote, self-reported assessments and led to substantial missing outcome data. Finally, our search identified randomized trials only for liraglutide. Because no eligible post-bariatric randomized trials were available for newer incretin-based therapies (e.g., semaglutide or dual incretin therapies such as tirzepatide), our results should not be interpreted as evidence about their effects. Additionally, we estimated values of standard deviations for change from baseline using an assumed within-person correlation coefficient (r = 0.5), which might affect the precision of the estimates. Accordingly, effect sizes in this meta-analysis should not be extrapolated to semaglutide or dual incretin agonists such as tirzepatide.

Future trials should enroll patients after surgical weight loss has stabilized, typically 12–18 months after surgery. They should include at least 12–18 months of treatment exposure to capture the full effect of incretin-based therapy and evaluate contemporary agents, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide.

Conclusion

In this meta-analysis of randomized trials, GLP-1 RA therapy was associated with greater weight loss, greater % TBWL, and improved quality of life at 6 months after bariatric surgery. Subgroup analyses suggested that effects may differ by timing of initiation (≤ 2 months vs. ≥ 18 months after surgery). At 12 months, between-group differences were not statistically significant. Gastrointestinal adverse events were generally mild and rarely led to treatment discontinuation. Because all included RCTs evaluated liraglutide, these findings may not generalize to newer incretin based therapies.
