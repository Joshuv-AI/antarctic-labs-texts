Barriers to implementation of discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients by junior nurses: a qualitative study
Authors: Xinyi Chen, Yujia Tang, Chunmei Wang, Cheng Liu, Wei Zhang, Haijuan Yuan, Fang Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the barriers experienced by junior nurses in providing discharge readiness services to elderly patients with cancer, with the aim of informing the optimization of clinical nursing practice protocols.DesignDescriptive qualitative research.MethodsBy purposive sampling, 15 junior nurses working in the general surgery department of a tertiary-level hospital in Yangzhou City were selected and interviewed in-depth one-on-one from February to March 2025.ResultsTwo themes and eight subthemes were distilled, including endogenous factors (insufficient interdisciplinary knowledge base of healthcare workers, professional attitudes and perceptions of healthcare workers, communication and coordination among healthcare workers and patients), and exogenous factors (insufficiently equipped human resources, lack of systematic and specialized training, lack of pluralism in social support, a single approach to discharge guidance, inadequate assessment and feedback mechanisms).ConclusionIn the practice of discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients, junior nurses face a series of barrier factors. Clinical junior nurses should emphasize the importance of patient’ discharge readiness services and adopt effective interventions to improve the level of patient discharge readiness and the quality of discharge instructions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Junior nurses, Elderly cancer patients, Discharge readiness services, Barrier factors, Qualitative study

Introduction

Cancer has become one of the major public health problems in the 21st century, and global cancer statistics show that in 2022, there will be as many as 19,976,000 new cancer cases worldwide [1]. According to the data released by China’s Cancer Center, the proportion of elderly patients among newly diagnosed cancer patients each year is 61.11%. And it is predicted that by 2050, the number of elderly cancer patients in China will increase to 9 million [2]. Elderly cancer patients represent one of the most vulnerable and clinically complex groups among surgical discharge populations. They often present with multiple comorbidities, complex medication regimens, high readmission risks, and unique physiological, psychological, and social needs [3]. However, the promotion and application of the Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) concept have significantly shortened their hospital stays [4]. While the reduction in hospitalization time improves healthcare efficiency. It also means that patients have less time to receive professional care in the hospital, thereby placing higher demands on their post-discharge rehabilitation management. Due to the compressed hospitalization period, patients and their families often face multiple post-discharge challenges, such as physical recovery, psychological adaptation, and social support, et al. To address this situation, the importance of discharge readiness services has become increasingly prominent. Discharge readiness serviceis a process initiated upon hospital admission. It involves a comprehensive assessment of the patient, followed by the development of a tailored post-discharge care plan by a multidisciplinary team. This ensures a seamless transition and continuous, integrated healthcare for the patient and their family after leaving the hospital [5]. Studies have shown [6] that Effective discharge readiness service is the cornerstone for preventing complications and the essential basis for delivering targeted, personalized home-based care. Healthcare professionals play a central role in treating and rehabilitating elderly cancer patients [7]. Therefore, ensuring that patients and their family members are adequately prepared for discharge has become an urgent and critical task for healthcare professionals, as well as an integral part of discharge planning. Currently, research on discharge preparation services focuses mainly on patients, with few studies from the perspective of healthcare professionals [8–9]. And notably, there is a lack of in-depth understanding regarding the specific experiences and challenges faced by nursing staff, particularly junior nurses, in providing this service.It is worth noting that the quality of discharge readiness services is highly dependent on the professional competence of nursing staff. In China, there is no unified standard for defining junior nurses. Some studies indicate that junior nurses typically refer to those with no more than five years of clinical nursing experience [10–11]. As emerging members of the nursing workforce, junior nurses demonstrate strong learning motivation and professional enthusiasm when providing discharge readiness services to elderly cancer patients. However, due to their limited clinical experience and lack of exposure to discharge education, they often face challenges in addressing the complex, multidimensional needs of these patients [12]. This includes perceived insufficiency in decision-making support and emergency response capabilities. Moreover, the psychological pressure arising from this process poses ongoing challenges to their professional role adaptation and emotional regulation.Therefore, this study employs a descriptive qualitative research approach to explore the experiences of junior nurses in providing discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients, with the aim of identifying barriers encountered during implementation. To identify these barriers, providing a foundation for optimizing the implementation pathways (e.g., multidisciplinary collaboration and resource allocation mechanisms) and operational procedures (e.g., assessment, care planning, patient education, and follow-up) of discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients. To improve service quality and ensure continuity of care.

Method

Design

This study was conducted from February to March 2025 using semi-structured in-depth interviews to investigate the barriers faced by junior nurses in providing discharge readiness services to elderly cancer patients. Furthermore, the research findings were reported in accordance with the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) [13]. This checklist aims to ensure the completeness and transparency of reporting in qualitative research across key dimensions, including the research team, study design, and analysis of results and findings.

Setting and participants

This study was conducted in a tertiary Grade A hospital (2,445 beds; 1,659 nurses) in Yangzhou City, Jiangsu Province, China. Participants were junior nurses with 1 ~ 5 years of work experience in the general surgery department (encompasses gastrointestinal surgery department, breast and thyroid surgery department, urology department, hepatobiliary and pancreatic surgery department), recruited between February and March 2025. Convenience sampling was adopted as the initial recruitment approach. The potential participants were recruited from hospital with which the research team maintained regular contact. Eligible individuals meeting the basic inclusion criteria and willing to complete face-to-face interviews were preliminarily recruited in this phase.After obtaining an initial pool of participants through convenience sampling, the research team transitioned to purposive sampling to refine sample composition and ensure sufficient diversity in key demographic characteristics, including gender, age, and educational attainment. Purposive sampling continued until theoretical saturation was achieved [14], thereby enriching the comprehensiveness of the data collected.

Inclusion criteria: (1) held valid nursing practitioner qualifications; (2) had accumulated 1 ~ 5 years of clinical work experience in the general surgery department; (3) had experience in providing standard discharge readiness services independently for elderly cancer patients; (4) voluntary participation.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) possessing over five years of work experience in the general surgery department (as the study aims to explore the barriers encountered by junior nurses); (2) being on long-term leave during the study period; (3) having resigned from the hospital. Finally,15 participants were interview.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. The interviews were conducted by two master of nursing students who had received systematic training in qualitative research and were proficient in its methodologies and interviewing techniques. One interviewer was responsible for facilitating the conversation, while the other observed and recorded the non-verbal cues of the participants. All interviews were conducted in a quiet conference room within the hospital, with doors closed to ensure privacy and minimize disruption. Prior to each interview, the researcher reaffirmed the study objectives to participants and obtained informed consent. During the interviews, the researcher followed a pre-designed interview guide while encouraging participants to freely share personal experiences and insights relevant to the topic. All interviews were fully recorded, with an average duration of 30 ~ 45 min. Following each interview, the recordings were simultaneously transcribed verbatim by two researchers, with all personally identifiable information removed to ensure anonymity. Since this study used a qualitative approach, the researcher collected data until saturation was reached. In other words, the researchers stopped collecting data when it no longer yielded new information or could not be coded further [15]. Ultimately, this study included 15 junior nurses.

The initial interview outline was developed based on the study objectives through a comprehensive literature review and discussions within the research team. To enhance its feasibility and relevance, a pilot interview was conducted with two junior nurse who met the inclusion criteria. Feedback primarily indicated that some terms were too academic, which hindered smooth communication. Consequently, we simplified the wording of several questions. The finalized interview outline is presented in Table 1.

Demographic information, including age, gender, years of working experience, and educational background, was collected from all participants. These demographic variables were analyzed using descriptive statistics (e.g., frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations) with SPSS 25.0.

Data analysis

Within 24 h of the interviews, the audio recordings were transcribed and imported into Nvivo 11 for coding and theme generation. The data were analyzed independently by two researchers. To ensure the credibility and objectivity of qualitative coding and reduce subjective bias, inter-coder reliability (ICR) was strictly assessed in this study. Cohen’s Kappa coefficient was adopted as the quantitative indicator to evaluate coding agreement between the two coders. After completing independent coding, we calculated the consistency value of all coded items, and the final Kappa value was 0.83. A Kappa value above 0.80 indicates excellent inter-coder agreement, demonstrating high reliability and stability of the coding results. A thematic analysis approach was used, following the phases described by Braun and Clarke [16].

The analysis comprised six stages: (1) familiarization with the data; (2) generating initial codes; (3) identifying themes; (4) reviewing themes; (5) defining and naming themes; and (6) producing the report.

In the first stage, the researchers carefully reviewed the interview transcripts to gain a comprehensive understanding of the depth and breadth of the data content. In the second stage, based on the research questions (What are the barriers that junior nurses face when implementing discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients?)and preliminary understanding of the data. The two researchers (XC and YT) coded the data by discussing initially identified patterns. In the third stage, the researchers categorized and integrated the initial codes, grouping related codes into potential themes, and reached consensus through discussion. In the fourth stage, the developed themes were repeatedly reviewed to ensure their close alignment with the original data, with iterative validation across different levels of abstraction. Finally stage, by refining the “essence” and logical structure of the core themes, an analytical narrative that aligns with the overall data and research questions was constructed, supported by typical quotations, culminating in a complete thematic analysis report.

Following thematic analysis, bilingual researchers translated relevant themes and illustrative quotations into English, which were then back-translated into Chinese to ensure semantic consistency with original transcripts [17]. Thematic saturation was determined to have been achieved after 15 interviews, as no new codes or themes emerged in subsequent analyses.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Yangzhou University Medical Ethics Committee (YZUHL20240013). Prior to the inclusion of the participants in this study, the researcher first explained to participants of the purpose of the study and the specific steps of its implementation, and proactively informed them of their right to withdraw from the study. Interviews were conducted after obtaining a signed informed consent form from each participant.

Trustworthiness

To ensure the rigor of the study, its credibility was systematically validated across four dimensions: credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability [18]. All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim. To enhance credibility, the researchers first analyzed the data independently and then reached a consensus through team discussions. Detailed documentation of each procedural step was maintained to ensure the research process could be examined, thereby strengthening dependability. To support confirmability, two researchers independently analyzed the data, and consistent conclusions were reached through team discussions. Notably, an independent external audit was not performed in this study’s trustworthiness evaluation. Instead, we established a full audit trail covering all raw data, coding drafts, codebook revisions and discussion records, and implemented dual-coder triangulation throughout data analysis. Finally, by providing comprehensive descriptions of the research context, participant characteristics, and thematic structure, the transferability of the findings was enhanced.

Theme categories

Theme 1: Endogenous factors

Inadequate interdisciplinary knowledge base

This primarily refers to the lack of clear understanding among junior nurses regarding the specific roles, scope of services, and referral criteria for disciplines such as rehabilitation therapy, nutritional support, and social services within discharge planning services. 12 interviewees indicated that they lacked adequate knowledge in other specialties relevant to discharge readiness services, which made it difficult for them to provide patients with scientific, comprehensive, and accurate discharge guidance and planning.

Honestly, I feel most powerless when patients ask questions outside my expertise. For example, when an elderly patient post-chemotherapy asks for dietary advice to boost white blood cells, I understand it’s crucial—but I simply don’t have the specialized knowledge to give a proper answer. (N2)

A typical scenario is a colorectal cancer patient who also has diabetes. The patient expresses a desire to eat foods like congee at home, but I know this could impact their blood glucose levels. I am unsure how to advise them to balance these needs. (N5)

Professional attitudes and perceptions

Lack of emphasis

Within the medical service system, some nursing staff did not fully recognize the critical significance and far-reaching value of patient discharge preparation services. Moreover, these services were often not given sufficient priority.

Since the hospital’s annual assessment focuses on things like surgical volume and cure rates and doesn’t evaluate discharge guidance, what’s the point in spending more energy on it? (N3)

I’m too busy every day to learn how to provide effective discharge guidance, and the hospital has no mandatory indicators for it. (N6)

Job burnout

Junior nurses reported burnout due to prolonged exposure to a high-intensity, high-stress, and repetitive work environment. This burnout, in turn, undermined their ability to carry out discharge preparation duties consistently and systematically.

The daily routine of facing many patients, answering the same questions countless times, and mechanically repeating discharge instructions has long since dulled my initial enthusiasm. (N9)

The constant stream of daily trivialities, including managing doctor-patient conflicts, leaves me physically and mentally depleted, with no energy left for meticulously educating patients about their discharge instructions. (N13)

Conventional thinking pattern

In this study, the general surgery department followed a conventional discharge process based on a standardized checklist. 9 junior nurses reported that their ingrained adherence to this task-oriented protocol limited their approach to discharge readiness services. Confined within this traditional framework, they tended to focus on completing standardized tasks, often overlooking the individual variability and specific psychosocial needs of elderly cancer patients.

In my view, our department’s discharge process is working very well, and we’ve never had any problems with it. Therefore, I see no reason to make any changes. (N7).

Most of our patients have similar needs, like what they can eat at home and their follow-up schedule. The department’s routine discharge program meets those needs just fine. (N11).

Communication and coordination among medical staff, nurses and patients

Low level of awareness among patients and their families

10 respondents in this study reported that the patient population in their department was predominantly elderly and had a low level of knowledge regarding disease prognosis. This lack of knowledge posed a major challenge to healthcare professionals in providing effective discharge preparation services.

The patients were told to come for regular review, but they felt that there was no need to be so frequent, and they felt that they had been getting better and that they could get better as long as they there is no discomfort they can do without multiple hospital visits. (N1)

Due to the special nature of the patients in the department, patients are more receptive to intuitive advice, such as other patients’ experiences or online information. What we, as professionals, say sometimes carries less weight. (N10)

Weak sense of multidisciplinary teamwork

Due to the multifaceted nature of discharge preparation services, all respondents in this study stated that no single discipline possesses all the necessary expertise, and therefore close collaboration across multiple disciplines is imperative.

We have a multidisciplinary team, ah, but usually it is only when we encounter difficult cases that we will discuss them together, and this discharge guidance mainly relies on the nurses in our department. (N4)

Last time, before a patient was discharged, he asked the doctor what he could eat when he went back, and the doctor said to continue to drink some soup and water. When I made ward rounds, the patient asked me again, and I replied that supplemental nutrition and food should be soft and gradual, and the patient didn’t know who to listen to. (N12).

For a special group like our ostomy patients, their post-discharge mental health is crucial. However, we don’t regularly include psychologists in their care; instead, the counseling falls to us nurses. Honestly, this isn’t ideal as it’s not our professional specialty. (N8)

Theme 2: Exogenous factors

Inadequate human resource provision

All nursing staff stated that, given the relative staff shortage, they were heavily occupied with daily clinical work, making it difficult to allocate sufficient time and energy to implementing patient discharge preparation services.

Our department performs over 900 operations annually. I’m busy all day and would like to spend more time communicating with patients or their families, but the time is simply unavailable.(N14)

Our department has 79 beds but fewer than 40 medical and nursing staff, a ratio far below international standards. This means each of us has to care for a large number of patients. (N11, noted with a sigh)

Lack of systematic and specialized training

The survey identified a critical gap in the hospital’s support system: the absence of systematic, specialized training for junior nurses on discharge planning. This deficit resulted in a lack of instruction on key competencies such as patient education, comprehensive care coordination, and effective handover communication. Consequently, nurses struggled to master the necessary knowledge and skills, which undermined the quality and effectiveness of discharge preparation services.

Each training is always about those contents, and many new concepts and methods are not involved, and nowadays the health concepts of the patients are increasingly improving, and relying on this knowledge is simply not enough. (N6)

The time for each training is too short, so many important contents are concentrated in a few hours, so we can’t learn anything at all, not to mention applying them to our practical work.“(N9)

Many patients ask what to take care of at home, but the discharge booklet is too vague and I don’t know much about certain elements (nutrition, exercise, etc.). (N10)

Lack of diversity in social support

Interviewees in this study unanimously stated that the current healthcare system lacked adequate patient information access channels and internet-based sharing platforms. This limitation severely constrained their ability to provide efficient discharge readiness guidance.

The department is full of older people who don’t use cell phones very much, but now a lot of health science information, which is electronic, can be read in the hospital, but when they go home this makes it difficult for them to access health knowledge, and the discharge guidance is not very effective. (N15)

If there is an information sharing platform, then the continuity of care after the patient is discharged from the hospital will be guaranteed, and the content of the discharge guidance will be much simpler. (N8)

Single approach to discharge guidance

Respondents in this study all agreed that the content covered in the current discharge preparation is lacking in breadth and depth and that the form of information sharing is relatively homogenous, making it difficult to fully meet the needs of patients.

Our current discharge instructions are a verbal talk and a paper copy, but some patients with a low level of literacy are illiterate and can’t understand the content at all. (N4)

When I was interned in a hospital in Nanjing before, I saw that people were making the content of the discharge instructions in the form of animation, and I think our department can learn from it. (N7)

Inadequate evaluation and feedback mechanisms

Nursing staff said that there was a lack of clear, quantifiable quality assessment criteria for discharge preparation services, which made it impossible to accurately measure the quality and effectiveness of discharge preparation services.

The fact that patients can be discharged safely and successfully means that the work has been done well, and sometimes I see nurses asking patients to fill out satisfaction surveys during my rounds, and I think that’s fine. (N2)

We also don’t know what we did well and what we still need to improve, in the clinic we mostly use patient satisfaction scores, but I feel this is too general and not specific. (N7)

Discussion

This study aimed to explore the barriers encountered by junior nurses in China during the clinical discharge preparation process. Through qualitative research, key endogenous and exogenous factors affecting discharge readiness services were identified. Most nurses lacked experience in structured discharge guidance, which serves as the cornerstone of effective discharge readiness services by translating knowledge into actionable patient education. This lack of experience contributed to significant gaps in their knowledge of various aspects of the discharge readiness services process. Secondly, hospital management had key deficiencies. Specifically, training was insufficient, and staffing was short. These problems directly caused the issues identified earlier. The lack of training caused junior nurses’ knowledge gaps in discharge readiness services. Chronic understaffing increased workloads, which prevented thorough discharge readiness services. Through this study, hospital administrators can understand the obstacles faced by junior nurses and adopt appropriate intervention measures to enhance patients’ satisfaction with discharge.

Young nurses are the backbone of providing discharge preparation services. The results of this study showed that most junior nurses had some knowledge gaps related to hospital discharge. Their feedback was caused by multiple factors, including a lack of systematic training and insufficient understanding of interdisciplinary knowledge. Related studies have shown [19] that standardized education and training, which follow established protocols and learning objectives, can significantly improve healthcare workers’ knowledge and professional competency in specialized fields. This competency encompasses the critical thinking and operational skills necessary for developing effective discharge readiness services. Therefore, it is recommended that hospitals should strengthen the training of healthcare workers in discharge preparation services.

To better support the psychological needs of elderly cancer patients, who are prone to postoperative anxiety and depression [20], training should strengthen healthcare workers’ competencies in psychological guidance. Through simulated cases and role-playing, staff can master effective communication and basic intervention techniques, helping patients build confidence and maintain a positive outlook. Additionally, training must include updates on relevant healthcare policies, such as medical insurance reimbursement and cross-regional healthcare protocols, enabling patients to understand their rights and reduce financial stress. Finally, providing clear referral pathways and specialty information from lower-level hospitals will facilitate continuity of care after discharge.

With the rapid development of electronic information technology [21]enables the adoption of a blended online-offline training model. Leveraging digital platforms, this model offers real-time live broadcasts and video replay functions. Such flexibility allows healthcare staff to adjust their learning schedules according to their shifts, preventing missed sessions due to time conflicts. Ultimately, it ensures that every junior nurse receives systematic and comprehensive training, thereby maximizing the overall effectiveness of the training program.

It was found [22] that healthcare workers in public hospitals nowadays have deficiencies such as weak communication skills in their daily work. It is recommended that daily training for nursing staff focus on advanced communication skills. Healthcare workers should be taught to explain discharge readiness services clearly and empathetically so as to enhance patient and family understanding and compliance.

This study found that burnout was prevalent among nursing staff due to workload and stress, consistent with the study by Wang Lizhi et al. [23]. This makes nursing staff often seem overwhelmed when implementing discharge preparation services. Therefore, it is recommended that hospitals should deeply integrate human resources at multiple levels.

It has been found [24] that discharge instructions have a profound impact on patients’ prognosis, quality of life, and readmission rates. Most of the respondents in this study reported a lack of a systematic discharge service process, making it difficult to provide quality discharge preparation services to patients. Relevant studies have pointed out [25] that patients need to receive pre-discharge assessment, discharge verification, and discharge instructions before discharge. A cyclical workload assessment mechanism can be established to provide monthly statistics on the departmental bed rotation rate and the amount of tasks directed by the discharge preparation service on a department-by-department basis, to allow managers to accurately grasp the dynamic changes in the workload of each department. Therefore, it is crucial to develop a comprehensive and standardized process for discharge preparation services. First, patient assessment for discharge readiness should be conducted throughout the medical care stage. It is currently recommended to use universal assessment scales, such as the Patient Learning Needs Scale (PLNS) [26]. However, these general scales lack disease-specific relevance. Therefore, future efforts should focus on developing specialized assessment tools tailored to corresponding clinical specialties. With the advent of the big data era, hospitals should strengthen the construction of information technology, which can be embedded in the discharge assessment scales required by patients to facilitate the transfer of information between healthcare professionals. Second, a standardized patient verification checklist should be developed [27]. A joint healthcare model can be used to verify the accessibility of patients’ disease-related information, out-of-hospital continuity of services/care, etc. At the same time, patients and their families are encouraged to actively participate in order to raise questions in a timely manner and ensure information symmetry between both parties. Finally, personalized guidance should be delivered through tailored content. Considering that elderly cancer patients often have lower education levels but respond well to subjective information, tools like instructional videos and mobile apps can be effectively used to explain rehabilitation knowledge and home care precautions. In addition, post-discharge follow-up is indispensable. It is suggested that nursing staff conduct follow-up via telephone and the “Internet + Nursing Service” model [21]. The model refers to a China-specific integrated care framework rolled out under national healthcare policies, which follows the “online application, offline service” mechanism. Through these dual-channel follow-up approaches, nurses can regularly track patients’ post-discharge rehabilitation progress, promptly resolve self-management difficulties, and deliver uninterrupted health support. This continuous care strategy helps optimize patients’ long-term prognosis, improve their overall quality of life, and reduce hospitnal readmission rates.

Studies have shown [28]that multidisciplinary collaborative management has a significant effect on improving the quality of patient discharge preparation services. It can effectively enhance the self-management ability of patients and then improve their overall quality of life. The discharge preparation service for elderly cancer patients is not a single dimension, but a healthcare management model that integrates multiple considerations. It is both comprehensive, multidimensional, and personalized. Therefore, it is recommended that nursing staff should fully assess and respect the individual differences of each patient, and develop a personalized discharge preparation service plan based on his or her condition, living habits, and family situation. In the context of the increasingly fine division of labor in the current healthcare system [29], the interviewees in this study unanimously indicated that, at present, the members involved in this management team are mainly healthcare personnel within the department, with a relatively single composition and a low level of participation from professionals of other disciplines, which makes it difficult to form comprehensive and integrated management of patients. The management system and division of labor within the team are vaguely defined, and there are problems such as mutual shirking and poor coordination, which impede the smooth promotion of the patient’s discharge guidance. Relevant expert consensus points out [30] that the management team of elderly patients’ discharge preparation should cover various professionals such as doctors, nurses, dietitians, rehabilitators, and psychological counselors. Therefore, it is recommended that a multidisciplinary team be constructed to combine management efforts and develop a clear plan for preparing elderly cancer patients for discharge management. It is also recommended that managers organize regular team meetings to share and exchange information promptly, to effectively coordinate and solve problems arising from the clinical work, to provide more accurate and comprehensive medical care services for elderly cancer patients, to satisfy their health needs, and to effectively improve the quality of their discharge.

Limitations

This study has the following limitations. The findings are derived from a relatively small sample (n = 15) of junior nurses recruited from the general surgery department of a single tertiary hospital. Although this sample represented approximately 48% (15/31) of the junior nurses employed in general surgery department, its limited size may affect the transferability of the results. Future research should aim to include a larger and more diverse sample of nurses from multiple clinical settings and healthcare institutions to enhance the generalizability of the findings and provide a more robust foundation for developing interventions.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identified key barriers to providing discharge readiness services for elderly cancer patients, as reported by junior nurses. To address these challenges, we recommend that hospitals implement targeted training programs, promote multidisciplinary collaboration in discharge planning, and effectively integrate the “Internet + Nursing Service”. Leveraging the advantages of this model, such as remote monitoring, timely communication, and accessible patient education. It can significantly enhance the efficiency and quality of discharge preparation.
