A Scoping Review of Solution‐Focused Measures
Authors: Jeeyeon Hong, Zach Cooper, Cynthia Franklin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSolution‐focused brief therapy (SFBT) is a resource‐based model that emphasizes solution‐building over problem‐solving. This scoping review identified solution‐focused (SF) measurement tools, examined their psychometric properties, and explored the conceptualization of a core SFBT construct, namely solution‐building. Using the PRISMA guidelines, we searched eight databases, supplemented with citation searching. Eligible studies included the development or validation of SF measures. Twenty‐four studies met the inclusion criteria, yielding 11 instruments (seven client‐focused and four provider‐focused). Measures were applied across diverse disciplines and cultural contexts. The Solution‐Building Inventory and Solution‐Focused Inventory had the most psychometric evidence. Mapping instruments to SFBT constructs revealed that “strengths and resources” and “hope and positive emotions” were most assessed, while “goal setting” and “using the client's language” were least represented. Only three measures demonstrated sensitivity to client change over time. Recommendations for future research and practice are discussed.

Solution‐focused brief therapy (SFBT) is a resource‐based, future‐oriented therapeutic approach developed by Insoo Kim Berg, Steve de Shazer, and their colleagues in the early 1980s (de Shazer 1985; Solution Focused Brief Therapy Association Research Committee 2025). Developed deductively through close observation of what therapeutic interactions most effectively facilitated change, SFBT has demonstrated efficacy in numerous randomized clinical trials (Franklin et al. 2023; Żak and Pękala 2025). According to developers, SFBT is distinctive from other therapies in its emphasis on solution‐building rather than problem‐solving (de Shazer et al. 1986). As a result, SFBT focuses on co‐constructing solutions with clients, which may not be related to the problem itself. Instead, solution‐building involves identifying what is already working, amplifying coping strategies, and enhancing client's sense of agency and hope, along with descriptions of realistic, actionable outcomes clients can envision in the present and future when the problem is solved (Berg and De Jong 1996, 2008; de Shazer et al. 1986; Franklin et al. 2016, 2025; Solution Focused Brief Therapy Association Research Committee 2025).

Research on Solution‐Building and Core Components of SFBT

Research has consistently supported the conceptual distinction between solution‐building and problem‐solving. Froerer and Connie (2016), in a Delphi study with 42 international SFBT experts, defined solution‐building as “a collaborative language process between the client and the therapist that develops a detailed description of the client's preferred future/goals and identifies exceptions and past successes” (Froerer and Connie 2016, 32). They identified key characteristics of solution‐building, such as using the client's language, maintaining a collaborative therapeutic alliance, and emphasizing the client's resources and expertise. Experimental studies have directly compared solution‐focused (SF) questions with problem‐focused (PF) questions, consistently demonstrating that an SF questioning approach significantly increases positive affect, self‐efficacy, goal‐attainment, and goal‐directed behaviors, while decreasing negative affect (Grant 2012; Grant and Gerrard 2019; Neipp et al. 2015). Koorankot et al. (2019) replicated these effects among clinical populations diagnosed with depression, showing that an SF questioning approach was associated with lower stress responses and greater increases in positive affect compared with PF questioning.

Building on the empirical foundation of solution‐building, current research has specified the core components of SFBT to operationalize how solution‐building functions in practice. A meta‐summary of SFBT change process research identified three overarching elements (Franklin et al. 2016): (1) co‐constructive language and the collaborative relationship, (2) strengths and resource building, and (3) development of the preferred future. Expanding this work, Franklin et al. (2025) synthesized findings from process research and underpinning theoretical frameworks to articulate five core components: (1) goal setting and development of the preferred future, (2) collaborative relationship/therapeutic alliance, (3) identification and activation of internal strengths and external resources, (4) enhancement of hope, positive emotions, and self‐competencies, and (5) behavioral activation through small, incremental steps towards solutions. Co‐construction of meaning was identified as central to all these processes, encompassing the use of SF questions (e.g., scaling and miracle questions) to advance the collaborative dialog and facilitate change. The core constructs outlined by Franklin et al. (2025) closely align with key elements of SFBT as described in the latest edition of the SFBT manual (Solution Focused Brief Therapy Association Research Committee 2025).

Measurement of SFBT

Several studies have sought to operationalize and measure SFBT constructs. For example, the Solution‐Building Inventory (SBI) assesses the concept of solution‐building Smock et al. 2010), the Solution‐Focused Inventory (SFI) measures SF thinking (Grant et al. 2012), and the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument was developed to evaluate the fidelity of SFBT interventions (Lehmann and Patton 2011). Despite these developments, SF measurement remains underutilized in research. A recent review of international SFBT studies found that only two out of 120 studies used an integrity checklist, while the majority relied on intervention manuals for fidelity (Pakrosnis and Żak 2024). To date, no existing reviews have systematically identified or critically appraised SF measures. The only related publication is a book chapter by Smock (2011), which briefly introduced four SF measures as early strength‐based instruments without any evaluation of psychometric properties. Furthermore, although the conceptualization of solution‐building has evolved and become more specified over time (Jerome et al. 2023), little is known about how SF measures conceptualize solution‐building or SFBT core constructs.

Study Aims

The purpose of this scoping review is to examine the current state of SF measures developed to assess SF constructs and SFBT processes and outcomes. The objectives are to (1) identify and describe characteristics of measures related to SFBT, (2) evaluate their psychometric properties (e.g., factor structure, validity, and reliability), and (3) examine how these tools conceptualize solution‐building. The third objective is particularly important because the way theoretical constructs are measured directly reflects how they are defined, interpreted, and empirically tested (Carey et al. 2019). Through visual mapping and comparison, this review seeks to clarify the extent to which existing measures align with core constructs articulated in the SFBT literature. These three research aims will help identify gaps to guide future directions for research and practice. For researchers, a more precise understanding of SF measures and their underlying constructs can enable more rigorous testing of an SFBT intervention, such as potential mediators (e.g., SFBT core constructs) that are associated with change in depression, anxiety, and other outcomes of interest (Carey et al. 2019). For clinicians, reliable and valid SF measures allow systematic monitoring of clients' progress in solution‐building, evaluating their adherence to the model, and refining practice accordingly (Franklin et al. 1997; Kort et al. 2021). Ultimately, this review aims to advance the evidence base for SFBT and promote greater alignment between theory, measurement, and clinical practice.

Methods

A scoping review methodology was selected for its suitability in exploring emerging or underexplored topics (Arksey and O'Malley 2005). To ensure transparency and rigor in reporting, this review adheres to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA‐ScR) checklist (Tricco et al. 2018).

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We included peer‐reviewed articles, book chapters, theses, and dissertations published globally, with full‐text articles available in English. The search included records published through June 30, 2025. Eligible studies included the development and/or psychometric properties of SF measurements. Specifically, studies should report validity, reliability, factor structure, measurement invariances, and/or change sensitivity. While we considered studies applying SF approaches across a variety of domains and contexts beyond mental health, only those reported to be explicitly grounded in the theoretical foundations of SFBT were included (Berg and De Jong 1996). In other words, instruments were retained if the papers explicitly stated that their item construction was derived from established SFBT theory, components, or assumptions.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

The search strategy was developed in collaboration with a research librarian at the affiliated institution. We conducted a search in eight databases, including Academic Search Complete, APA PsycINFO, CINAHL, Education Source, ERIC, MEDLINE, SocINDEX, and ProQuest. Search terms included combinations of keywords, such as “solution‐focused,” “solution‐building,” “therapy,” “approach,” “instrument,” “inventory,” “validation,” “reliability,” “construct,” and “factor analysis.” After removing duplicates, we screened reference lists and conducted forward citation searching. We also included measures that were introduced in Smock's (2011) book chapter. When both dissertation and journal article versions of a study were available, the latter was chosen to avoid duplication.

All retrieved citations were imported into Rayyan, a web‐based platform for systematic and scoping reviews. Two doctoral‐level reviewers with clinical licensure and experience utilizing SFBT independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by a full‐text review to determine final inclusion. Discrepancies were discussed and resolved through consensus, with a third reviewer consulted to address any disagreements. For data extraction, the first and second authors independently coded the studies using an Excel form. We recorded the basic study characteristics (e.g., publication year, type, instrument name, sample, country, and setting) as well as psychometric properties (e.g., factor structure, reliability, validity, and methodology). We used the categorization from Franklin et al. (2025), which are organized into four domains: (1) solution‐building tasks: goal/preferred future, solution‐building tasks/behavioral activation, and problem disengagement (PD); (2) cooperative relationship: emphasize alliance and using client's language, (3) strengths and resources: identify and activate internal and external strengths; (4) positive emotions: enhance hope or positive emotions, enhance self‐competencies. The two coders independently reviewed each instrument and recorded the item numbers corresponding to each core element, resolving any discrepancies through discussion.

Results

A total of 867 records were identified through database searches, including 677 from EBSCO and 190 from ProQuest. After removing duplicates and completing the title and abstract review, 22 records were assessed for full‐text review, with 14 meeting the final inclusion criteria. In parallel, 21 additional records were identified through citation searching, reference checking, and a top‐down search from a relevant book chapter. Among these, 10 records met the inclusion criteria. In total, 24 records were included in the final review. SF Measurements for Clients

A total of five measures across 16 studies were identified for client use. Of the 16 studies, five included clinical or subclinical populations, while others primarily sampled university students using a convenience sampling method. Most studies (n = 10) focused on the development, translation, or adaptation of either the SBI (Arslan et al. 2021; Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Jordan 2014; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2015, 2019) or the SFI (Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan 2025; Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). The SBI was developed to assess the concept of solution‐building, which is defined as (1) clearly identifying a solution, (2) recognizing exceptions to the problem, and (3) developing hope for the future (Smock et al. 2010). The SBI has been primarily tested among university samples, except for Jordan (2014), who examined outpatient clients. Originally developed in English, the SBI has been later translated and adapted for Japanese, Turkish, and Iranian populations (Arslan et al. 2021; Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2019).

The SFI measures a client's SF thinking in the context of coaching. SF thinking is conceptualized as (1) goal orientation (GO; identifying and moving toward preferred outcomes), (2) PD (reducing focus on problems), and (3) resource activation (RA; recognizing and using existing strengths/supports) (Grant 2011). The SFI was tested primarily with college students and/or working professionals (Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan 2025; Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). Initially developed in Australia, the SFI was adapted and validated for use in Chinese, Spanish, and Turkish populations (Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan 2025; Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015).

Other measures were developed for more specific populations and settings. For example, the Solution‐Focused Core Construct Scale (SFCC) measures improvements in SF constructs, such as hope, connection to important people, and confidence in problem‐solving (Cooper and Johnson 2024). It was administered to a clinical population experiencing depression and chronic illness in primary care. The Couple Resource Map Scale (CRMS) and the Couple Resource Scale (CRS: a shortened version of the CRMS) were created for couple therapy (Murray 2007; Pope et al. 2013). These measures aim to identify and assess personal, relational, and contextual resources within the couple relationship and were tested among clinical and subclinical populations. Lastly, the Solution‐Focused Recovery Scale (SFRS) evaluates coping capacities within adolescent survivors of sexual abuse (Kruczek and Vitanza 1999). CRMS, CRS, and SRFS were all initially developed as clinical tools, often read aloud during sessions to highlight clients' strengths and reinforce signs of change (Kruczek and Vitanza 1999; Murray 2007; Pope et al. 2013).

Psychometric Properties of Measurements for Clients

Reliability

The SBI demonstrated good to excellent reliability (α = 0.83–0.92) (Arslan et al. 2021; Jordan 2014; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2015, 2019), while none examined test–retest reliability. The SFI, in contrast, showed moderate to good values of test–retest reliability (r = 0.66–0.84) and showed good internal reliability (α = 0.70–0.84) (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). However, the RA subscale exhibited a poor to acceptable range of internal reliability (α = 0.57–0.72) (Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). The SFCC also examined test–retest reliability with a moderate correlation (r = 0.67) and found a good internal reliability across two waves (α = 0.87 and 0.88) (Cooper et al. 2024). Other scales (e.g., CRMS, CRS, and SFRS) demonstrated an acceptable to excellent range of internal reliability (Murray 2007; Murray and Forti 2009; Pope et al. 2013; Kruczek and Vitanza 1999; Kruczek and Ægisdóttir 2005).

Validity

All but two studies examined construct validity through convergent, discriminant validity tests or invariance testing. Regarding the SBI, convergent validity was shown through positive correlations with other psychologically related measures, such as hope, life orientation, and positive affect (Arslan et al. 2021; Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2015, 2019), with the range of r = 0.19–0.75. The discriminant validity was shown with a negative association with negative affect and clinical severity with SBI (r = −0.57 to −0.41) (Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Jordan 2014). Three SFI studies demonstrated evidence for both convergent and discriminant validity (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). Convergent validity was supported by positive correlations with broader psychosocial constructs, such as personal well‐being, resilience, satisfaction with life, and perspective‐taking (r = 0.33–0.76). Discriminant validity was established by negative association with self‐inferiority, hostility, depression, anxiety, and stress (r = −0.44 to −0.38). Lastly, Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan (2025) examined construct validity through metric, scalar, and strict invariance testing (i.e., measuring measurement consistency across time and between groups via factor analysis). The CRMS and CRS examined criterion validity via convergence testing with relationship satisfaction and self‐esteem scales (Murray 2007; Murray and Forti 2009; Pope et al. 2013). Similarly, the SFRS assessed convergent validity by correlating the scale with the contextual resource area scale, while demonstrating divergent validity using depression and anxiety screeners (Kruczek and Vitanza 1999; Kruczek and Ægisdóttir 2005). The SFCC scale utilized invariance testing to assess validity (Cooper et al. 2024).

Factor Analysis

The majority (13 out of 17) of articles utilized an exploratory factor analysis (EFA) or confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to identify and validate the underlying construct of solution‐building. For the SBI, inconsistencies were shown regarding the number of items and the factor structures (i.e., the number of constructs defined by the factor analysis). As opposed to its original design of three factors or constructs, Arslan et al. (2021) found two‐factor solutions, while all other four studies found a single factor (Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Jordan 2014; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2015, 2019). Moreover, the number of items varied, ranging from 6 to 14, with some items deleted due to low factor loadings or correlations (Arslan et al. 2021; Mollaei Mehneh et al. 2024; Smock et al. 2010; Takagi et al. 2015). Factor loadings ranged from 0.40 to 0.81 in these studies. Conversely, the SFI showed a consistent three‐factor structure with 12 items (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015), with factor loadings ranging from 0.52 to 0.95. Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan (2025) reported a low factor loading of 0.28 for the PD construct.

Measuring Change

Three studies tested the sensitivity of the scales to detect change. For example, SFI, SFCC, and SFRS all found significant increases in scores from pre‐ to posttreatment for those in the treatment group compared with the control group (Cooper and Johnson 2024; Grant et al. 2012; Kruczek and Vitanza 1999). SF Measurements for Providers

A total of six measures were found for the providers. Two instruments were designed for mental health providers: The Theoretical Orientation Scale for Counselors (TOSC) assessed clinicians' theoretical alignment with the SF approach, and the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument assessed adherence to SFBT key techniques (Lehmann and Patton 2011; Osborn 1997). The other measures were designed for clinical supervisors, nursing practitioners, school leaders, and corporate employees. Geographic diversity was shown, with several instruments developed in the Netherlands, Turkey, and Japan (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Sezer 2017; Theuns‐Boumans et al. 2022). Among measures for providers, the TOSC was most frequently studied, appearing in three studies (Osborn 1997; Osborn and Johanson 2001; Wolfe 1998).

Psychometric Properties of Measurements for Providers

Reliability

The internal reliability for TOSC ranged from α = 0.75 to 0.82 (Osborn 1997; Osborn and Johanson 2001; Wolfe 1998). The Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument had Cronbach's α of 0.89, showing good internal reliability (Lehmann and Patton 2011). While other measures fell into a similar range as the TOSC, the Supervisor Opinion Scale (SOS; Koob 1998) demonstrated poor overall reliability (α = 0.68) with particularly low internal reliability shown for multiple method (α = 0.15) and therapist‐focused (α = 0.34) subscales. None of the provider scales utilized test–retest methods to assess reliability.

Validity

Four of the eight studies examined construct validity via convergent validity, discriminant validity, or measurement invariance. For example, the SOS and Solution‐Focused versus Problem‐Focused Communication Scale (SFPF‐CS) demonstrated convergent validity with significant correlations shown with strengths, empowerment, positive affect, or self‐efficacy (r = 0.23–0.44) (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Koob 1998). One study testing the Solution‐Focused School Leadership Scale (SFSLS) repeated a CFA examining measurement invariance over time to assess the consistency of the measure across time (Sezer 2017).

Factor Analysis

Six out of eight studies performed factor analysis. The TOSC, SOS, SFSLS, and SFPF‐CS all found multiple factors (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Koob 1998; Osborn 1997; Osborn and Johanson 2001; Sezer 2017). However, the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument produced a single factor with factor loadings ranging from 0.27 to 0.95 (Lehmann and Patton 2011). The TOSC reported variation in factor structure and number of items due to low factor loadings and dropping of items in a follow‐up study Osborn and Johanson 2001). The SOS, SFBQ, and SFSLS reported a broad range of factor loadings from 0.27 to 0.95 (Koob 1998; Sezer 2017; Theuns‐Boumans et al. 2022).

Measuring Change

No studies examined change over time. Further details on psychometric properties for provider scales are presented in Supporting Information File 1.

Conceptualization of Solution‐Building

In response to study aim 3, we explored how solution‐building was conceptualized across the included measures, utilizing core constructs of SFBT as defined by Franklin et al. (2025). A visual summary of these conceptualizations is presented in Figure 2. Notably, the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument, SBI, SFI, TOSC, and SFPF‐CS included items covering the broadest SFBT domains, with six out of nine SF constructs represented in each measure. The most prominent theme across included measures was the identification and activation of strengths or resources, appearing in ten out of eleven instruments. “Identifying external strengths” was another common theme, typically operationalized through items assessing client's connections to important people in their lives. For instance, the SFCC specifically had an item asking feeling connected to important people (Cooper and Johnson 2024). Similarly, every item on CRMS and CRS focused on exploring clients' personal, relational, and contextual resources (Murray 2007; Murray and Forti 2009; Pope et al. 2013). The SFI also found RA as a factor in its factor analysis results (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017).

“Solution‐building tasks and behavioral activation” (defined as collaboratively developing solutions and encouraging clients to take achievable, goal‐directed actions) appeared in eight studies. It emerged as a construct with strong factor loadings in SBI, TOSC, and SOS (Koob 1998; Osborn and Johanson 2001; Smock et al. 2010). The SFSLS and Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument included items related to generating concrete actions (Lehmann and Patton 2011; Sezer 2017). “Hope and positive emotions” were represented in seven measures. For example, the SFCC specifically assessed clients' sense of hope (Cooper and Johnson 2024).

Both “Emphasize alliance” and “problem disengagement” were present in six instruments and appeared more frequently in provider measures. For instance, TOSC, SOS, SFBQ, and SFPF‐CS identified collaborative relationship and alliance as a key dimension of their scales (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Koob 1998; Osborn and Johanson 2001; Theuns‐Boumans et al. 2022). “Problem disengagement” was operationalized as shifting away from PF narratives and thinking. Both the SFI and TOSC specified nonpathological approach as a key dimension, which was confirmed by their factor analytic findings (Grant et al. 2012; Osborn and Johanson 2001). The SOS and SFPF‐CS had SF and PF approaches as contrasting factor structures (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Koob 1998). “Goal/Preferred future” was observed in only three measures. The SFI was the only instrument with GO as a distinct factor structure (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017). The TOSC and Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument had an item about collaborative goal setting (Lehmann and Patton 2011; Osborn 1997). “Using client's language” was absent across all measures.

Discussion

Our review aimed to identify SF measures, evaluate their psychometric studies, and map their conceptualization. To our knowledge, this is the first study to review SF instruments since the development of SFBT in the 1980s. In total, eleven SF measures were found from 24 studies, which encompassed important SFBT constructs, such as solution‐building tasks and behavioral activation, cooperative relationship, strengths and resources, and positive emotions. These SF measures were found within a diverse range of contexts, disciplines, and professions. Measures were used in professions of mental health therapy, coaching, clinical supervision, education, nursing, and organizational communication (Gingerich and Peterson 2013; Neipp and Beyebach 2022). Moreover, SFI, SBI, and most provider‐focused measures were developed outside Western countries. These patterns highlight the growing global interest in SF approaches and their ongoing adaptations across diverse professional and cultural contexts (Kitai and Shimada 2022; Sezer 2017; Theuns‐Boumans et al. 2022).

The SBI and SFI have garnered the strongest empirical support with multiple studies validating their psychometric properties. This trend aligns with the increasing number of studies on solution‐building and SF thinking, particularly in testing their relationships with other psychological constructs (Akça 2019; Avci and Ataç‐Öksüz 2023; Ciydem 2024; Karasu et al. 2023). In contrast, most other measures had one or two psychometric studies. For example, the CRMS, CRS, and SRFS were originally designed as initial assessment tools for clinical practice, with lengthy item numbers (36–63) (Kruczek and Vitanza 1999; Murray 2007; Pope et al. 2013). For clinical utility and evidence‐based assessment, further psychometric evaluation is necessary to develop shorter and more feasible versions (Hunsley and Mash 2007).

Most measures were tested with college students, confirming that SFBT is well‐suited for community samples and produces strong effects in these groups (Franklin et al. 2023; Vermeulen‐Oskam et al. 2024; Żak and Pękala 2025). However, this also suggests a reliance on convenience sampling, as college students are generally deemed relatively easy and cost‐effective to recruit, which underscores the need for future studies to use randomized sampling to enhance the external validity of SFBT measures across diverse populations (Wild et al. 2022). As some measures (e.g., SBI, SFI, SRFS, and CRMS) have already exhibited discriminant validity with depression and anxiety through differences in scores between clinical and subclinical populations (Grant et al. 2012; Kruczek and Ægisdóttir 2005; Murray and Forti 2009; Neipp et al. 2017; Jordan 2014; Yang and Hai 2015), further validation testing with clinical samples on these measures can better establish their clinical applicability.

The SBI and SFI captured the broadest array of SFBT constructs, reflecting their comprehensive focus on clients' solution‐building or SF thinking process (Grant 2011; Smock et al. 2010). Among provider measures, the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument and TOSC covered the most constructs, with the former assessing adherence to SFBT techniques and the latter evaluating clinicians' theoretical alignment with SF principles (Lehmann and Patton 2011; Osborn 1997). TOSC's inclusion of PD and therapeutic alliance shows that it extends beyond techniques to broader theoretical constructs. Clinicians should therefore select measures according to the specific purpose of assessments. The field would also benefit from integrated fidelity tools that combine both technique checklists with alignment with the SF approach, as both are critical components of SFBT (Blundo et al. 2014; Franklin et al. 2023).

Identifying internal and external strengths emerged as a prominent theme for client measures. This may be partially because context‐specific instruments (e.g., CRM, SFCC, and SFRS) targeted resources and strengths due to their aims. For provider measures, therapeutic alliance and collaborative approach appeared most often. This pattern aligns with findings from a meta‐summary of process research (Franklin et al. 2016) and a recent conceptual review (Jerome et al. 2023), where strengths identification and collaborative approach were dominant themes in SFBT studies. PD was also prevalent, consistent with early SFBT literature, which emphasizes shifting focus away from problems to future‐oriented goals and desired outcomes (Berg and De Jong 1996, 2008; de Shazer et al. 1986). A recent SFBT review also found that moving away from problems is a common theme (Jerome et al. 2023). However, inconsistent terminology was shown, as some instruments (e.g., SFLS, SFCC, and SFPF‐CS) conflate problem‐solving with solution‐building. To improve theoretical clarity, the SFBT field should adopt consistent terminology, highlighting solution‐building as a process of PD rather than problem‐solving (Corcoran and Pillai 2009).

We revealed a critical gap in that none of the measures assessed the use of clients' language. Co‐construction of meaning constitutes the core mechanism of change in SFBT (Berg and De Jong 1996, 2008; De Jong et al. 2013) and is foundational for building therapeutic alliance and collaborative relationships (De Jong et al. 2013; Franklin et al. 2016). While this omission may be due to the subtlety of this therapeutic technique, future instrument development could more explicitly include items on the use of client language. For example, fidelity measures could assess whether therapists reflect, mirror, or build on clients' own words during sessions. To evaluate theoretical alignment with the SF approach, items could ask clinicians to rate how closely they prioritize or adhere to the co‐construction of meaning in their practice.

Goal setting and the development of a preferred future were also largely missing, despite their fundamental role in solution‐building (de Shazer et al. 1986; Franklin et al. 2025). Our observations correspond with a recent conceptual review, which found that goal setting and use of language are mentioned less frequently than identification of client strengths across SFBT studies (Jerome et al. 2023). The developers of SFBT have consistently described collaborative goal setting and exploration of the client's desired future as an essence of the SFBT process (Berg and De Jong 2008; de Shazer 1985). Taken together, greater emphasis should be placed on goal setting and the use of client language when conceptualizing solution‐building.

More research should be conducted to strengthen the psychometric properties of SBI and SFI. Inconsistencies in the SBI's item number and factor structure across different samples can complicate decision‐making for researchers around which version to use for their samples. While these inconsistencies may partly reflect strong interitem correlations (Jordan 2014), additional validation studies are needed to achieve greater structural validity. For the SFI, particularly lower internal reliability for RA subscale have been reported across studies (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017; Yang and Hai 2015). One item, “Most people are more resilient than they realize,” showed weak correlations and factor loadings in English and Spanish studies (Grant et al. 2012; Neipp et al. 2017), but this finding was not replicated in Chinese and Turkish studies (Atılgan and Şanal‐Karahan 2025; Yang and Hai 2015). While Grant et al. (2012) recommended rewording this item into the first person to avoid general phrasing, this cross‐cultural difference suggests that translation and cultural interpretation may also be at play. Further examination of the item's cultural and linguistic relevance across diverse populations, coupled with efforts to rephrase the wording, is necessary to enhance internal reliability.

Surprisingly, only one study has examined the psychometric properties of the Solution‐Focused Fidelity Instrument (Lehmann and Patton 2011), despite its widespread use across numerous clinical trials (Cooper and Johnson 2024; Cooper et al. 2024; Vermeulen‐Oskam et al. 2024). Although the initial study reported high internal reliability (α = 0.89), it was based on a small pilot sample of 43 master's students, limiting external validity. Given that the instrument was developed over a decade ago and conceptualizations of SFBT have since evolved (Jerome et al. 2023), further efforts to refine and validate with a larger and contemporary sample are necessary. Future research can replicate factor analyses across diverse samples and note any variations in factor structure and psychometric properties for their respective samples.

Another limitation is the lack of testing on sensitivity to change, as most measures have been tested only in cross‐sectional samples. Only SFI, SFCC, and SRFS have demonstrated the ability to show significant score increases from pre‐ to posttreatment. Longitudinal studies are needed to assess whether SF measures effectively track client progress after intervention. While only the SBI found significant negative correlations between psychological distress and one's ability to build solutions (Jordan 2014), future research is needed on how solution‐building constructs mediate symptomology in SFBT outcome research. Research on mediation can advance understanding of which elements are most strongly associated with changes, as well as how these mediating effects vary across diverse populations.

Limitation

The study findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. First, as with most reviews, the search process is subject to human error. Because we primarily used “solution‐” as a keyword, measures not termed in this way may have been omitted. Lastly, restricting to English‐only publications may have excluded relevant studies published in other languages.

Conclusion

This review provides a comprehensive overview of SF measures. A total of 11 SF measurements were found across disciplines and national contexts. Among identified measures, SBI and SFI had the strongest empirical support, with the greatest number of psychometric studies shown. Identification of strengths and resources were the most common constructs, whereas goal setting and using the client's language were the least represented. Future research should build on by validating and refining these measures with diverse populations, particularly by testing with clinical samples, resolving inconsistencies in factor structures, and using longitudinal data to assess sensitivity to change.
