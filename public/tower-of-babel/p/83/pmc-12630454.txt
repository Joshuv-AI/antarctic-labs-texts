Foreperiod and Response Complexity Effects on Movement‐Related Potentials
Authors: Yuta Kimura, Steven A. Hackley, Hiroaki Masaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis study addressed a long‐standing controversy in the attention and action literature concerning whether speeding of a voluntary reaction by a neutral warning signal (WS) is due to faster, more efficient preparation and execution of the motor response. In contrast to most previous research, we found that manipulation of foreperiod duration (the interval from onset of the WS to onset of the reaction stimulus) did influence hand‐specific activity in the sensorimotor cortex, as measured with the lateralized readiness potential (LRP). We also confirmed that when cortical activity contralateral and ipsilateral to the hand of response is separately measured using localized current source density (surface Laplacian waves), speeding of late motoric processes can be observed. Certain alternative possibilities, such as distortion of the baseline‐correction procedure by the contingent negative variation (CNV) or misidentification of latency effects due to what we call the “aperture problem,” could not fully account for the findings. Facilitation of late motoric processes was most evident when the response involved a sequence of keypresses, comparable to a short phrase on the piano. Relative merits of conventional LRPs versus surface Laplacians are discussed.

Introduction

The author of the most influential book on event‐related potential (ERP) methodology (Luck 2005, 86) said that the lateralized readiness potential (LRP) was his favorite ERP component. He argued that it “may be the single most useful ERP component for addressing a broad spectrum of cognitive issues at this time.” Progress over the ensuing two decades has proven this enthusiasm well justified. With its well‐defined functional, neuroanatomical, and biophysical underpinnings, the LRP has indeed yielded many important contributions.

Further refinement of our understanding of the underpinnings of movement‐related potentials such as the LRP, though, has cast doubt on certain long‐standing conclusions in the literature. Consider the early, nonlateralized portion of the readiness potential that precedes self‐paced finger movements. Schurger et al.'s (2012, 2021) account of how an artifactually early onset of this component could be generated by subthreshold pink noise put an end to decades of controversy regarding Libet et al.'s (1983) famous study of free will. Whether Schurger's phenomenon poses a concern for research involving the LRP, with its more abrupt, stimulus‐controlled onset, remains to be determined.

Foreperiod Effects on LRP Latency

Of more immediate relevance are doubts focused on LRP findings regarding the phasic arousal and temporal expectancy elicited by warning signals (WSs). When reaction time is bifurcated into the interval from stimulus onset until LRP onset (S–LRP) versus from onset of the LRP until that of the response (LRP–R), these processes were found to mainly or only affect the earlier of the two (Hackley et al. 2007; Hackley and Valle‐Inclán 1998, 1999; Müller‐Gethmann et al. 2003; Seibold and Rolke 2014; Smulders 1993). This led to the conclusion that, for example, the effect of foreperiod (FP) duration on reaction time (RT) in a two‐choice‐RT task is due to facilitation of stimulus perception, response selection, or some other process that precedes hand‐specific activation in sensorimotor cortex (reviewed in Hackley and Valle‐Inclán 2003; Seibold et al. 2023).

Among these early LRP studies, that of Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) was particularly important because it involved parametric manipulation of the time from onset of the neutral WS until that of the imperative stimulus (IS). The FP ranged from 50 to 6400 ms, in eight steps. These durations were manipulated across blocks of trials, a procedure that results in slower responses at longer FPs (Woodrow 1914). Evidence indicates that this is because: (1) phasic arousal triggered by the WS gradually fades, (2) long time intervals are difficult to estimate, (3) it is effortful to sustain a high state of attention and preparation, and (4) reinforcement learning yields biased temporal expectations (Bertelson and Tisseyre 1969; Los and Van Den Heuvel 2001; Niemi and Näätänen 1981; Woodrow 1914).

In Experiment 1 of the Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) study, the WS was a light gray circle, and the IS was a tone of 800 or 1200 Hz. Sensory modalities of the WS and IS were reversed in Experiment 2. Following the method of Osman et al. (1995), FP effects were chronometrically localized by bisecting the RT into S‐LRP and LRP‐R intervals. The former was calculated from ERPs computed with EEG segments synchronized according to stimulus onset (S‐locked). The latter was calculated from ERPs time‐locked to keypress onset (R‐locked).

As expected from prior research, Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) found RTs to be shortest at FPs of 200–400 ms and to increase monotonically thereafter. The S‐LRP intervals of both experiments closely tracked RT means across FP conditions (Spearman's rho = 0.83 and 0.98 for Expts. 1 and 2, respectively) and yielded main effects of FP duration that were similar in size to those for RT. By contrast, FP effects on LRP‐R were numerically small in both experiments, albeit significant in the first one (visual WS, auditory IS). LRP‐R durations were not significantly correlated with RT (rho = −0.50 and −0.05 in Expts. 1 and 2, respectively). Taken as a whole, these data seem to imply that the speeding of voluntary reactions by a WS is not due to acceleration of hand‐specific response preparation or execution processes.

Difference Wave Problem vs. Aperture Problem

An acknowledged weakness of contra‐ipsilateral difference potentials (LRP, N2pc, Contralateral Delay Activity, etc.) is that, because they are based on the subtraction of waveforms recorded at sites overlying two different cortical regions, they are blind to the individual contributions of those sources. In the LRP studies discussed above, a genuine effect of FP duration at the contralateral sensorimotor cortex mediating correct‐hand responses might be obscured by an independent pattern of effects at the ipsilateral cortex, yielding a false negative. Using surface Laplacian methods (i.e., localized current source density, CSD) to separately measure activity on the two sides, Tandonnet et al. (2003) obtained evidence that this was indeed the case. The response‐locked, negative‐going deflection on the contralateral side began closer to response onset in the short FP (500 ms) than in the long‐FP (2500 ms) condition. This suggests that enhanced temporal preparation resulted in quicker, more efficient motor activity. The effect was large enough to fully account for the 20‐ms speeding of RT.

The positive‐going deflection ipsilateral to the responding hand exhibited no such facilitation. In fact, the grand‐average waveforms in their Figure 2 (Tandonnet et al. 2003) appear to show that the ipsilateral positivity reached a given level (say, 1 μV/cm2) closer in time to EMG onset in the long‐ than short‐FP condition. As noted in a replication study by the same group (Tandonnet et al. 2006, 161), this pattern might explain the observed null effects for R‐locked LRPs. The inverse chronometric patterns at ipsilateral and contralateral sensorimotor cortices might have canceled one another out in a counterbalancing fashion. These findings and those of later studies that also analyzed surface Laplacian waves (SLWs) (e.g., Jepma et al. 2009; Korolczuk et al. 2022, 2023) imply that null effects for the LRP‐R interval can be misleading.

We offer an alternative interpretation for consideration, one that focuses on the apparent reversal of the FP effect in the two hemispheres that appeared to be present in both studies by Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) and, as will be seen, in our replication of their results (see also Jepma et al. 2009, discussed in Section 4.5). Borrowing a term from vision science, we refer to this as the aperture problem: If only a portion of a linear contour is visible through a small window, the direction it moves can easily be misperceived.

Suppose an observer cannot see the striped cards in Figure 1A except through the circular window (aperture) of the opaque cover. When the upward‐sloping lines of the card shown on the left move downward, they might be mistakenly perceived as moving toward the bottom right of the gray cover or toward its right edge. Conversely, if the card in the right half of Figure 1A is pulled downward slightly, the observer might mistakenly think that those lines are moving either straight to the left or diagonally down and to the left.

Latency measurement and the aperture problem. Panel A illustrates the aperture problem in a general way (Wolfe et al. 2006). The viewer can see the striped card only through the hole in the opaque cover. As the card on the left is pulled down, they might perceive the stripes as moving down, to the right, or toward the lower right. As explained in Section 1.2, this is analogous to the upward‐sloping portion of the contralateral, response‐locked SLW, caricatured as the upper waveforms of Panel B. Conversely, the downward‐sloping stripes of the card on the right of Panel A are intended to be analogous to the downward‐sloping leading edge of the ipsilateral SLW. If viewed only through the holes in the card, an observer might mistakenly conclude that the dashed line in the upper aperture is shifted to the right relative to the solid line, whereas the dashed line in the lower aperture is shifted to the left. Note that the early, pre‐onset portion of the contralateral and ipsilateral SLWs for a given foreperiod condition is not separately rendered. This is to convey the idea that they overlap until SLW onset. Negative is graphed upward in Panel B, as in subsequent figures with real data.

The reader may have encountered this topic in the context of complex cells in the visual cortex (Hubel and Wiesel 1965). The cell's receptive field constitutes only a small window on the visual environment. If a segment somewhere in the middle of a moving edge is registered, the actual motion of that portion of the object is ambiguous. That ambiguity must be resolved at a higher level to achieve veridical perception.

In Figure 1B, we make a less direct analogy, one in which the aperture is the measurement window for the onset of an ERP component and latency is quantified as the point at which either a specified voltage or percentage of peak amplitude is first reached (as in the present study, but not Tandonnet et al. 2003, 2006). Suppose the upward‐pointing diagonals represent the onset slopes of negative, response‐locked, SLWs recorded over contralateral sensorimotor cortex, and the downward‐pointing diagonals represent the onset slope of positive SLWs recorded over ipsilateral cortex. The straight‐down “movement” is the difference in vertical position (μV/cm2) of these SLWs in the short‐FP condition (dashed lines) relative to the long‐FP condition (solid lines). This vertical displacement reflects a superimposed bilateral positivity, such as the offset of the contingent negative variation (CNV) that developed during the FP. If the amplitude of that offset were greater in the short‐ than long‐FP condition, an illusory impression of opposite latency effects in the two hemispheres could be produced due to the aperture problem.

Ambiguity due to the aperture problem can be resolved when the entire curve is considered. When one examines not just the pre‐response portion but the entire contra‐ and ipsilateral SLWs of Tandonnet et al. (2003 and, to a lesser extent, 2006), they do give the impression of simply being displaced downward in the short‐ relative to long‐FP condition. With genuine latency effects, the peak and trailing edge of the component are typically shifted by an amount similar to the leading edge, as in textbook examples of phase‐shifted sine waves.

In a study of accessory stimulus effects by Jepma et al. (2009), a task‐irrelevant tone was presented on some trials at about the same time as the visual IS. Although their results looked quite similar to those presented in the two Tandonnet papers, the authors reached a different conclusion. They inferred that a diffuse burst of arousal triggered by the loud tone imposed a bilateral voltage shift, and that there was no actual change in the speed of late, hand‐specific, motoric processes.

The Present Experiment

The main purpose of our experiment was to reexamine the null effect of FP duration on response‐locked LRPs. In addition to the R‐locked LRP, we also recorded contra‐ and ipsilateral SLWs. To examine possible contamination of these measures or their corresponding baselines by the CNV, we analyzed stimulus‐locked LRPs and SLWs, as well as CNVs, during the FP. These latter measures were not included in the Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) experiments.

To maximize the chance of observing a legitimate FP effect on the LRP‐R interval, we included blocks of trials in which a more complex motor response was required. Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) noted that prior RT studies reporting the interaction of FP duration with factors presumed to directly influence motoric processes (instructed muscle tension, Sanders 1980; similarity of the alternative responses, Spijkers 1990) had required responses more complex than a simple keypress. Specifically, they involved aiming or pointing movements. By factorially manipulating FP duration (300 vs. 1200 ms) and response complexity (sequential index‐index‐middle finger keypresses vs. index, only), we tested for such an interaction. Furthermore, because this type of response complexity manipulation is known to specifically influence the LRP‐R interval (Low et al. 2002; Schröter and Leuthold 2009; Smulders et al. 1995), the pattern of results for LRP and SLW might offer a useful comparison vis‐à‐vis the less consistent effects of FP duration.

Two other electrophysiological measures were used to help characterize the chronometric, anatomical, and functional characteristics of the FP effect. The N‐40 component, which has a narrow, midline frontal focus, is an established index of the response selection stage. This component is present in choice‐RT but not Go/NoGo tasks; it is larger for difficult than easy S‐R mappings, its onset and peak precede the LRP's onset and peak, and its apparent origin in the SMA (supplementary motor area) is consistent with hierarchical precedence over the late preparation and execution processes within sensorimotor cortex that are reflected in the LRP (Carbonnell et al. 2013; Vidal et al. 2003, 2011). Foreperiod modulation of the N‐40‐to‐keypress interval would constitute evidence in favor of the assumption that WSs can speed response selection, an assumption that is at odds with inferences drawn from diffusion models of human performance data (Jepma et al. 2012).

An additional, but only very approximate (Verleger 1997), index of the locus of the FP effect was peak latency of the P300 (P3b) in the S‐locked ERP. Under the assumption that one of the mechanisms by which warning speeds voluntary responses is facilitation of IS perception (Seibold and Rolke 2014), P300 latency should be shorter in the FP300 than the FP1200 condition.

Method

Participants

In order to reexamine the null effect of FP duration on R‐locked LRPs, we needed enough power to replicate the positive effect on contralateral SLW observed by Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006). In those two studies, the FP effect achieved significance with sample sizes of 12 and 10, respectively. The corresponding effect sizes were estimated at 0.592 and 0.526 (Cohen's f). Based on a G‐power analysis of the 2006 effect, a sample size of seven participants should be the minimum needed to achieve our goal with a power of 0.80. Other effects of interest, such as the FP × RC interaction predicted under the just discussed hypothesis of Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003), would require a larger—but difficult to estimate—number of subjects. With this in mind, we recruited double the number of volunteers who participated in the experiment of Tandonnet et al. (2003).

Twenty‐six young adults participated in this experiment. Two were excluded from the analysis because they exhibited no measurable response‐related lateralization of ERPs. Data from 24 participants (18 male; mean age and SD: 23.3 ± 2.1 years) were kept for further analysis. Participants were right‐handed except for one who was left‐handed, yielding a mean handedness score of 86.1 (Oldfield 1971). All had normal or corrected‐to‐normal vision. All participants were paid 1000 JPY per hour for their participation. This study was approved by the Waseda University Ethics Committee. Written informed consent was obtained before the experiment.

Trial Structure

As shown in Figure 2, each trial began with a white fixation cross, which served as the WS, presented on a black background. The size of the cross was 1.2 cm × 1.2 cm (visual angle of 1.2 degrees). It persisted until the IS, with an onset‐to‐onset interval of 300 or 1200 ms that defined the FP. A kanji character “右” (right) or “左” (left), white on a black background, 3.7 cm × 3.7 cm (a visual angle of 2.16 degrees), comprised the IS. One of these characters, randomly chosen, was presented at the center of the display and lasted until keypress onset.

Trial structure. A warning signal (WS) in the form of a plus symbol initiated each trial. It lasted either 300 or 1200 ms until the onset of an imperative stimulus (IS), the kanji character for left or right. Foreperiod (FP) duration was manipulated across blocks of trials, as was the complexity/length of the keypress response (index‐index‐middle or index only, for complex and simple conditions, respectively). ITI means intertrial interval. All time intervals are onset‐to‐onset.

In the Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) experiments, the IS was not presented at the center of the display. Instead, a red and a green light were simultaneously illuminated to the left and right of fixation. For half of the participants, the red light indicated that the hand on that side should respond; for the other half, the green light was the IS. Although the simultaneous display of the nontarget light contralateral to the cued hand should reduce contamination of the LRP by early visual ERPs, problems remain. A lateralized task‐relevant stimulus would be expected to capture attention, thereby triggering an N2pc (Luck and Hillyard 1994) that, as Valle‐Inclán (1996) pointed out, can contaminate the LRP. Furthermore, the stimulus–response compatibility of this configuration is known to directly modulate the amplitude of the early portion of the LRP and influence its latency (Valle‐Inclán 1996; reviewed by Leuthold 2011). It is acknowledged that surface Laplacian transforms should act as a spatial filter to reduce contamination via volume conduction from the parieto‐occipital generators of N2pc and, furthermore, that stimulus–response compatibility was equated across conditions in the Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) studies. In the interest of prudence, though, we chose to present all task‐relevant stimuli at central fixation.

Participants in the present study were instructed to respond to the IS by pressing the designated buttons on a custom‐made keyboard. In the complex response conditions, they responded with a sequence of button presses (i.e., index ➔ index ➔ middle finger presses, in this order). In the simple‐response condition, they pressed the designated key once with the index finger of the cued hand. Intertrial intervals (ITI) varied randomly among the values 2000, 2500, and 3000 ms.

The WS and IS were displayed on a computer monitor placed 100 cm in front of the participant. Presentation of these stimuli and recording of the RTs were controlled by a digital tachistoscope system (IS‐702, Iwatsu Isel Inc.).

Procedure

The experiment consisted of four different conditions created by the factorial combination of FP and response complexity (i.e., FP300 ms/simple, FP300 ms/complex, FP1200 ms/simple, and FP1200 ms/complex), presented in separate blocks. Participants performed 2 blocks of 64 trials in each condition. The order was counterbalanced across 24 participants and differed for each of them.

Before the start of the experiment session, participants performed a minimum of 20 complex responses to the experimenter's verbal cues. If the responding manner was still awkward, they were asked to continue practicing the response sequence until they could make smoother movements. Additionally, there were 10 warm‐up trials at the beginning of each block.

Recording

Electroencephalograms (EEGs) were recorded using Ag/AgCl electrodes at 128 scalp sites. Vertical electrooculograms (EOG) were obtained from 2 cm above and below the left eye, and horizontal EOGs from sites 2 cm left and right of the outer canthi. EEGs and EOGs were acquired at a bandwidth of DC to 205 Hz using a Biosemi Active Two system (Biosemi Inc.). The sampling rate was 1024 Hz.

Data Analysis

Trials in which the correct hand was used, and RT was between 200 and 800 ms were categorized as “correct” and retained for behavioral and electrophysiological analysis. Flawed execution of the keypress sequence (e.g., 2nd & 3rd keypresses in the wrong order) during a complex response was not considered an error; such trials were counted as “correct” if the cued hand was used. Finger‐thumb confusions were never observed.

EEG data were re‐referenced to the average of all scalp leads and then low‐pass filtered at a cutoff frequency of 30 Hz and a roll‐off of 12 dB/octave. Segmentation differed for the three main types of analyses: (1) For analysis of S‐LRP and S‐SLW intervals, epochs extended from −400 to 1000 ms relative to the onset of the IS. (2) For analysis of the LRP‐R and SLW‐R intervals, continuous EEG was segmented from −600 to 300 ms with respect to the first keypress onset. Note that this time‐locking point is about 90 ms later than in the Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) studies, which used EMG onset to synchronize epochs during signal averaging. Time locking to the keypress could impair comparison with the target articles because of variation in the EMG‐to‐keypress interval, but it allows findings to be more directly related to the cognitive psychology literature and to our own prior research. These same S‐ and R‐locked epochs were used to compute the N‐40 component. (3) Segmentation for analysis of the CNV yielded epochs extending from −1500 ms (which was 300 ms prior to WS onset in the FP1200 condition) until 800 ms after IS onset.

After segmentation, the EEG data were corrected for blink and ocular movement artifacts using the regression‐based algorithm described by Gratton et al. (1983) and then signal averaged per condition. Bipolar difference waves were computed from the resulting ERPs at C3′ and C4′, separately for left and right‐hand responses. (C3′ and C4′ designate D18 and B21 of the Biosemi system, which are about 1 cm superior to the conventional C3 and C4 sites.) These bipolar, contralateral‐minus‐ipsilateral difference waves were then averaged to obtain the LRP. The baseline correction employed during signal averaging to create S‐locked LRPs was based on mean voltage during the −100 ms interval preceding IS onset. The mean amplitude of this component was measured from 250 to 350 ms. For R‐locked LRPs, the baseline period was −500 to −400 ms before response onset, and amplitude was measured from −100 to 0 ms (IS onset). For CNVs, the baseline extended from −100 until the onset of the WS, and amplitude was measured during the final 100 ms of the FP.

To calculate SLWs, spherical spline interpolation was applied to the original ERPs, followed by surface Laplacian transformation with 3 as the degree of spline and a maximum of 15° for the Legendre polynomial (Perrin et al. 1987). The baseline period for calculating the stimulus‐locked surface Laplacian wave (i.e., S‐locked SLW) interval was the same interval as for the stimulus‐locked LRP. Baseline for the response‐locked surface Laplacian wave (i.e., R‐locked SLW) interval was from −450 to −200 ms before the first keypress. Onset of LRPs and SLWs was defined as the first time point at which 50% of peak amplitude was reached (FPL, for fractional peak latency). The S‐locked SLW amplitude was calculated within 200 to 300 ms after the IS presentation, and the R‐locked SLW interval amplitude was calculated from 100 to 0 ms before keypress onset.

Baseline for the R‐locked, N‐40 SLWs was −350 to −250 ms, peak amplitude was identified in the −200 to −50 ms range, and 50% FPL was calculated between −200 and 0 ms. Measurements were taken at Fz, the site at which amplitudes were largest in our data set. For S‐locked N‐40s, the corresponding values were −100 to 0 ms, 100 to 400 ms, and 100 to 400 ms. The term “SLW” is reserved for the contralateral and ipsilateral sensorimotor components discussed above; the frontocentral, midline component will be referred to as the “N‐40.” Baselines for the P300 were the same as for LRP and SLW. Peak latency at the Pz electrode was measured during the period from 300 ms to 500 ms after IS onset.

To help assess contamination of SLWs by the CNV, its amplitude at C3′ and C4′ was submitted to an ANOVA, with factors of FP, RC, and hemisphere. Repeated measures ANOVA (jackknife version, Ulrich and Miller 2001) with factors FP and RC was applied to LRPs and, with factors FP, RC, and hemisphere, to the surface Laplacians. ANOVA results were labeled with the subscript, F c, when appropriate to indicate the (n − 1) jackknife correction.

Results

Task Performance

A two‐way ANOVA confirmed expectations of longer mean RT for FP1200 than for FP300 (F (1, 23) = 4.52, p = 0.044, ηp2 = 0.16) and longer RT for complex than simple responses (F(1, 23) = 34.88, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.60). As shown in Table 1, the FP effect on RT averaged 7 ms in the simple condition and 12 ms in the complex condition. These numerical values are small, but comparable to those of the two experiments whose electrophysiological findings we seek to better understand (11 and 20 ms in Tandonnet et al. 2003, 2006, respectively). This FP main effect was significant and was judged large enough to support our goal of assessing modulation of the LRP‐R and SLW‐R intervals. No interaction between FP and RC was found (F (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.526, ηp2 = 0.02).

Figure 3B shows the R‐locked LRP waveforms. The R‐locked LRPs clearly indicated a faster progression from hand‐specific activation to overt response in the simple than complex response condition (Fc (1, 23) = 12.17, p = 0.002, ηp2 = 0.35). The main effect of FP was also significant (Fc (1, 23) = 7.68, p = 0.011, ηp2 = 0.25). Hand‐specific lateralization led more quickly to response onset in the FP300 condition (106 ms) than in the FP1200 condition (115.5 ms). No RC × FP interaction was found (Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.350, ηp2 = 0.04), but based on the suggestion of Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) noted earlier (Section 1.3), we analyzed the two types of responses separately. Student t‐tests did indeed indicate that the LRP‐R interval was significantly influenced by FP duration only during complex responses (tc (23) = 2.76, p = 0.011, a 14‐ms effect, Cohen's d = 0.56), not simple ones (tc (23) = 0.68, p = 0.500, 5 ms, Cohen's d = 0.14).

Mean R‐locked LRP amplitude during the 100 ms immediately preceding response onset was greater in the complex than the simple condition (Fc (1, 23) = 5.53, p = 0.028, ηp2 = 0.19). The prominent peak for the FP300‐complex condition that is visible in Figure 3B presumably contributed to the significant main effect for FP (Fc (1, 23) = 5.41, p = 0.029, ηp2 = 0.19) and to an almost significant RC × FP interaction (Fc (1, 23) = 3.17, p = 0.088, ηp2 = 0.12).

R‐Locked Surface Laplacian Waves

The general shape of the R‐locked waveforms shown in Figure 4B matches those of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006). Importantly, so does the pattern of FP effects on the leading edge of the contralateral negativity and ipsilateral positivity. Specifically, there was an apparent latency reversal across hemispheres such that the shorter, more effective FP reduced SLW‐R duration contralateral to the responding hand (Fc (1, 23) = 9.52, p = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.29), but tended to lengthen it on the ipsilateral side (Fc (1, 23) = 1.51, p = 0.231, ηp2 = 0.06). This reversal yielded a significant FP × hemisphere interaction for the SLW‐R interval (Fc (1, 23) = 17.54, p = 0.004, ηp2 = 0.43; see Table 2). We contend that the cross‐over aspect of this interaction is an artifact, an “illusion” brought about by the aperture problem. After about −200 ms, the FP300 waveforms of Figure 4B are bilaterally displaced downward relative to the FP1200 curves, as with the short‐FP condition of the simulated data shown in Figure 1B.

Yet there does seem to be a genuine latency effect in the contralateral hemisphere. Part of the evidence for this concerns waveshape. Consider the steep slope of the terminal CNV that can be seen in the FP300 but not the FP1200 condition (Figure 4A, −100 to 90 ms). Due to this difference, a change in the period used for baseline correction could alter the vertical displacement of the SLWs. Imagine that the solid and dashed lines for FP300 in Figure 4B were shifted upward by 3 μV/m2 so that the dashed lines (ipsilateral hemisphere) overlapped. The solid lines (contralateral) would not overlap. The leading edge, peak, and trailing edge of the FP300 curve would all lie to the right of (later in time than) those features of the FP1200 SLW. Thus, the morphological character of these data, coupled with the presence of a significant FP effect on the contralateral but not ipsilateral SLW‐R interval, supports the conclusion of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) that the speed of late motoric processes can be influenced by FP duration.

We turn now to response complexity (Figure 5). As expected, enhanced keypress complexity lengthened the SLW‐R interval (RC: Fc (1, 23) = 9.81, p = 0.005, ηp2 = 0.30; see Figure 5B). This is consistent with the assumption that three‐keypress sequences took longer to plan and initiate than single‐keypress responses (Schröter and Leuthold 2009). There was also a main effect of hemisphere (Fc (1, 23) = 7.70, p = 0.011, ηp2 = 0.25) such that ipsilateral positivity preceded keypress onset by a shorter interval than did contralateral negativity. No other main effect or interaction reached significance for the SLW‐R interval (FP: Fc (1, 23) = 3.42, p = 0.077, ηp2 = 0.13; FP × RC: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.330, ηp2 = 0.04; RC × hemisphere: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.806, ηp2 < 0.001; FP × RC × Hemisphere: Fc (1, 23) = 1.63, p = 0.215, ηp2 = 0.07).

There were also no significant effects for response‐locked amplitude within the −100 to 0 ms measurement interval (FP: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.873, ηp2 = 0.001; RC: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.498, ηp2 = 0.02; Hemisphere: Fc (1, 23) = 1.12, p = 0.301, ηp2 = 0.05; FP × RC: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.487, ηp2 = 0.02; FP × Hemisphere: Fc (1, 23) = 1.01, p = 0.326, ηp2 = 0.04; RC × Hemisphere: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.532, ηp2 = 0.02; FP × RC × Hemisphere interaction: Fc (1, 23) < 1, p = 0.719, ηp2 = 0.01).

Contingent Negative Variation

One of the goals of this study was to test an interpretation of the FP × Hemisphere interaction for the SLW‐R interval that we refer to as the aperture hypothesis (Section 1.2). The morphology of our R‐locked Laplacians was quite similar to that of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006). This includes the general impression of a bilateral displacement of short‐FP waveforms in the positive direction, a displacement that extends from −200 ms at least until the right edge of Figure 4B, in our study.

In the Introduction, we speculated that one source of this displacement might be the offset of the CNV. This conjecture is not testable because the offset of the various CNV generators is too poorly understood to allow their isolation from the R‐locked SLW. However, another possible source of the displacement should be easier to isolate—distortion by the CNV of the baseline employed in the analysis of the R‐locked SLWs. If mean baseline voltages were higher (more negative) in the FP300 condition due to differences in CNV amplitude, baseline correction would shift the SLWs downward (more positive) relative to the FP1200 condition.

We tested this by means of a 3‐way ANOVA (FP × RC × L/R Hemisphere) on mean S‐locked CNV voltage from −83 to 167 relative to IS onset (see Figure 9). This measurement window roughly corresponds to the SLW baseline in the R‐locked analysis, −450 to −200 ms prior to the first keypress, given that RT across the four conditions averaged 367 ms. However, neither the FP main effect nor the FP × Hemisphere interaction achieved significance (F (1, 23) = 1.69, p = 0.206, ηp2 = 0.07; and F (1, 23) = 2.71, p = 0.113, ηp2 = 0.11, respectively). So, if the aperture hypothesis is correct, the bilateral displacement must take place during the R‐locked SLWs rather than during the period used for baseline correction.

Discussion

Recap

This study was intended to address a long‐standing controversy in the field of action and attention as to whether the speeding of voluntary responses by a neutral WS, temporal cue, or accessory stimulus involves acceleration of late motoric processes (reviewed in Hackley and Valle‐Inclán 2003; Nobre et al. 2007; Sanders 1980; Seibold et al. 2023). The strongest arguments against a late motoric locus are based on absent or very small effects for the LRP‐R interval or a poor correspondence between modulation of this interval and the RT (e.g., Hackley et al. 2007; Smulders 1993; Müller‐Gethmann et al. 2003). However, as Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) point out, LRP‐R results can be misleading because a genuine facilitation of activity on the contralateral side that controls the movement could be obscured by a null or reversed effect on the ipsilateral side. Their R‐locked SLW recordings in a choice‐RT task with short and long FPs appeared to demonstrate this phenomenon.

As did ours. The single‐keypress, R‐locked SLWs in Figure 6B closely match those of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006). To better understand those important earlier findings, the present study incorporated four changes: (1) Imperative stimuli were presented at fixation to avoid contamination by lateralized attention‐related components such as the N2pc; (2) S‐locked averages for LRPs and SLWs supplemented R‐locked averages so as to assess pre‐motoric effects; (3) CNVs during short and long FPs and their brief continuation into the RT interval were examined for evidence of an influence on the SLWs or their baseline; and (4) the manipulation of FP duration was factorially combined with one of response complexity, which is known to influence both RT (Henry and Rogers 1960; Sternberg et al. 1978) and the LRP‐R interval (Low et al. 2002; Osman et al. 1995; Schröter and Leuthold 2009; Xu et al. 2015).

To supplement recordings from sensorimotor cortex, we also assessed FP effects on two components that arise in other brain regions. The first, P300/P3b, mainly reflects activity in parietal cortex and the second, N‐40, likely originates in the supplementary motor area (SMA). The latency of the P300 was speeded in the short‐ relative to the long‐FP condition with S‐locked averaging as was N‐40 with R‐locked averaging. Consistent with the widely held view that a WS can influence multiple points along the S‐R pathway (e.g., Seibold et al. 2023), these P300 and N‐40 effects are likely to signify an influence on perceptual discrimination of the IS and the decision regarding which response to make.

Response Complexity

As expected, the RT, LRP‐R, and SLW‐R were lengthened during trial blocks in which participants executed three keypresses rather than one, and there were no significant changes in either the S‐LRP or S‐SLW interval (Tables 1 and 2). This pattern suggests that delayed keypress onset in the complex condition was specifically due to changes in a late motoric stage, initially manifest at around the time of the first asymmetry in the activity of contralateral and ipsilateral sensorimotor cortex (Figures 3, 5, and 6). Because RC was manipulated across blocks in our choice‐RT paradigm, the participant might in principle have been able to carry out some of the extra programming needed for the index‐index‐middle finger sequence during the FP or S‐LRP interval. This seems not to have been the case. Consistent with prior LRP findings (Low et al. 2002; Xu et al. 2015) and the just‐in‐time conception of motor programming (Klapp and Maslovat 2020), the extra steps needed to create the patterned, 3‐keypress sequence became evident less than a quarter second before overt movement.

The main reason a manipulation of RC was included in this experiment was to follow‐up on the comment by Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003) that “Future research is needed to assess whether temporal preparation may speed up motor processing if the response requires the activation of more complex motor patterns like aiming movements.” They suggested this because early, purely behavioral studies had found that manipulations of temporal uncertainty interact with motoric variables such as tonic muscle tension (Sanders 1980) and response specificity (Spijkers 1990) when the latency of targeted reaching movements was the dependent measure. Our findings support this hypothesis in that an effect of FP on the LRP‐R interval was significant for complex but not simple responses (Table 2 & Figure 6). The N‐40‐to‐R interval was similarly shortened on complex‐ but not simple‐response trials. The FP × RC interaction failed to reach significance for either LRP‐R or N‐40‐to‐R, but the potential for Müller‐Gethmann and colleagues' hypothesis to reconcile discrepancies in the literature encourages further research.

Aperture Hypothesis

The odd reversal between hemispheres for FP effects on the SLW‐R interval in the data of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) might constitute another clue toward reconciling discrepancies in the literature. As pointed out in their 2006 paper, the “difference observed over the sensorimotor cortex involved in the incorrect response, although non‐statistically significant in the present data, could mask the effect observed over the contralateral cortex by some kind of counterbalancing effect.” Prolongation rather than abbreviation of the ipsilateral LRP‐R interval on trials with the more effective, shorter foreperiod (i.e., FP300) was also nonsignificant in our data, but the FP × Hemi interaction did achieve significance (p = 0.005).

We argued earlier (Section 1.2) that at least a portion of this interaction might indicate a bilaterally symmetrical amplitude effect (as in Jepma et al. 2009) rather than latency modulation that was of opposite direction in the two hemispheres. By analogy with the aperture problem of vision science (Figure 1), restricting consideration to the onset portion of the SLW can lead one to mistake a global amplitude shift for a latency effect. When one examines the SLW as a whole in their data or in ours (see −140 to 100 ms in Figure 4B), there does appear to be a bi‐hemispheric positive shift in the short‐ relative to the long‐FP condition.

One possible source of this bilateral shift could be the offset of the CNV. Unfortunately, this would be difficult to test because the offsets of the various generators of the CNV and related components are not well enough understood to distinguish them from the SLW, per se. The CNV cortical sources that underlie temporal expectation, anticipatory visual attention, and response choice might terminate prior to the R‐locked SLW; those with a role in response preparation might simply transition to execution mode; and those involved with error correction or reinforcement learning might continue until the outcome of the response is fully processed.

Another source of the bilateral amplitude shift could be the distortion of the period used in SLW baseline correction by differences in CNV amplitude across conditions. Although our test of this did not return significant results (Section 3.6), it is clear from Figures 4A and 9A that there was a dramatic difference between FP conditions during this general time period with respect to CNV amplitude, slope, and shape. Small differences in temporal position or width of the baseline interval could, in principle, have an appreciable effect on either the stimulus‐ or response‐locked SLW. Baseline correction plays a vital role in event‐related electrophysiological analyses, but correction for large, complex, and systematic differences across conditions is not trustworthy. In such cases, baseline‐free techniques for quantifying latency (e.g., slope analysis, Vidal et al. 2003) should be considered.

Relative Advantages of LRP & SLW

So long as such confounding differences in amplitude, slope, or shape are not specific to the hand of response, they do not pose a problem for the LRP. The two‐step computational procedure (subtracting ipsilateral waveforms from contralateral, averaging left and right responses) will remove such confounding differences (Gratton et al. 1988). Compare the flat, clean, pre‐response period of the S‐locked LRPs in Figure 3A with the corresponding period for the SLWs in Figure 4A.

While this is a distinct advantage for the LRP, Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) are undeniably correct that LRP results can be misleading, given that different waves are blind to the distinct contributions of the two underlying cortical areas. Indeed, our results support their conclusion that shorter, more effective FPs yield faster processing within the sensorimotor cortex contralateral to the responding hand (Figure 4B), an effect that is usually missed or underestimated in LRP experiments. In the present study, the main effect of FP duration seems to have reached statistical significance for the LRP‐R only because trial blocks with complex, 3‐keypress responses were included (Section 3.2).

From a heuristic perspective, it is useful to contrast the LRP and SLW by describing the former as a difference wave. In fact, both are. When a surface Laplacian is computed, mean voltage within the surround is subtracted from that of its center. If activity within part of the surround were to be systematically influenced by a manipulated variable but not its center, the resulting SLW would vary accordingly. Depending on the vagaries of volume conduction and source geometry, an LRP could in principle yield a more faithful representation of sensorimotor cortex activity than its corresponding pair of SLWs. Supplementing SLWs at C3′ and C4′ with full topographical CSDs for critical time points could provide clarity in such cases.

The safest interpretation of an LRP latency effect is usually something along the lines of “At least as early as such‐and‐such point in time, there is a difference in processing as a function of the selected hand of response.” Drawing inferences, instead, from differences across conditions with respect to amplitude, polarity, or waveshape of the LRP can be tricky. There is the temptation to assume that this component provides a continuous index of a unidimensional construct, the relative degree of activation for one response versus the other. Some patterns of results do seem to conform to this assumption, such as polarity reversal as the participant switches his or her initial decision (the Gratton dip; Gratton et al. 1988), abrupt cessation of the LRP when response preparation is aborted (the No Go LRP; Miller and Hackley 1992), or scaling of LRP amplitude with the gradual accumulation of perceptual evidence (Van Vugt et al. 2014; but cf. Rogge et al. 2022). By contrast, SLW research indicates that in some situations, ipsilateral positivity indexes a process that is qualitatively different than contralateral negativity. It can reflect active inhibition of the non‐selected response that is deployed strategically in a manner distinct from contralateral activation of the chosen one (Burle et al. 2004; Korolczuk et al. 2022).

Both LRP and SLW have their respective advantages and disadvantages. For most relevant research situations, therefore, the cognitive electrophysiologist may find it useful to analyze them in tandem.

Comparison With the Accessory Stimulus Effect

As noted earlier (Section 1.1), psychologists have identified multiple mechanisms that contribute to the ability of a WS to speed voluntary reactions, each of which can be studied with methods other than manipulation of FP duration (Hackley 2009; Nobre et al. 2007). The present experiment was not designed to isolate the effect of, say, phasic arousal or temporal expectation on the LRP‐R, but there is relevant prior research. An fMRI study showed these two processes to be associated with different patterns of cortical activity (Hackley 2009), and previous EEG research has documented null effects for both on the LRP‐R: Comparing variable versus fixed ITIs, Smulders (1993) found that temporal expectancy shortened the S‐LRP but not the LRP‐R interval. Hackley and Valle‐Inclán (1998, 1999) examined phasic arousal using accessory stimulation and found, similarly, that the accessory shortened S‐LRP but not LRP‐R.

The just‐cited studies preceded Tandonnet et al.'s (2003) seminal paper, but Jepma et al. have more recently used SLWs and diffusion modeling of behavioral data (Jepma et al. 2009) to reexamine the null effect of accessory stimulation on the LRP‐R. In a close replication of Hackley and Valle‐Inclán (1998), their participants responded to one of four possible visual imperatives in which letter identity (S or T) signified which hand to use if a response were required, and letter size (small or large) indicated whether or not to respond. Letter onset was accompanied on a random half of the trials by a task‐irrelevant tone (80 dB, 150 ms long, 30 ms lead time). Responses were speeded on trials with the auditory accessory, and by an amount that could be fully attributed to abbreviation of the S‐LRP interval. The LRP‐R interval was not influenced by accessory presence.

Laplacian analyses by Jepma et al. (2009) yielded an important new finding: R‐locked SLWs were displaced vertically by accessory stimulation on the contralateral and ipsilateral sides, apparently by a bilateral, superimposed negativity. Based on this pattern and diffusion modeling, the authors concluded that responses were speeded by nonspecific, phasic arousal that accelerated some process other than decision making. The pattern of bilateral SLW displacement looked similar to that of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) and the present study (Figure 4B). However, it was in the opposite direction in the sense that the condition with faster responding (accessory present) was more, rather than less, negative. The origin of this discrepancy is unclear. We wish to point out, though, that it might involve differences not in the hand areas of the sensorimotor cortex but, rather, in some nearby region within the Laplacian surrounds.

Conclusions

In this reassessment of FP duration effects on movement‐related potentials, Laplacian analyses confirmed the contention of Tandonnet et al. (2003, 2006) that the interval extending from the onset of the contralateral, R‐locked SLW until that of the response is speeded by a neutral WS in an amount that varies with FP duration. Congruent with the suggestion of Müller‐Gethmann et al. (2003), we also obtained a reliable FP effect on the LRP‐R interval that was specific to the condition for which complex, 3‐keypress responses were required. These two findings help reconcile some long‐standing discrepancies in the literature (Sections 4.1 and 4.2).

However, the inference that FP modulation of late motoric processes is greater for complex than simple responses must be considered tentative because no FP × RC interaction was obtained. The positive results from simple‐effects analysis might be specific to our use of a very short‐FP duration, 300 ms, that encroaches on the boundary between the phasic arousal and temporal preparation, 200–400 ms. In parametric studies of FP duration, there is often a discontinuity at around that time, indicating a stronger influence of WS intensity, duration or modality on decision (viz, speed‐accuracy tradeoffs), or motor (e.g., keypress force, probe reflex amplitude) measures at brief FPs (reviewed in Hackley and Valle‐Inclán 2003; Posner 1978). This is presumably because exogenously triggered arousal begins and ends quickly, whereas endogenously controlled temporal preparation takes time to engage. We are examining the relationship between FP and RC in a follow‐up study (Kimura et al. unpublished manuscript) that uses a broader and therefore more effective FP range, 600–3000 ms, while avoiding the boundary between phasic arousal and temporal preparation.

Patterns in the data highlight some limitations of the LRP and SLW methodologies. Because LRP is a contra‐ipsilateral difference wave, false negatives can occur when effects within one hemisphere are obscured by inconsistent activity within the other. The fact that activity at ipsilateral sites in this and other SLW studies often fails to mirror what is observed at contralateral sites (Burle et al. 2004, 2010; Korolczuk et al. 2022, 2023) raises theoretical concerns about aggregating the two to create an LRP. The LRP cannot be assumed to index a unidimensional construct of relative correct versus incorrect‐hand activation.

So long as this is kept in mind, the LRP not only retains its utility in cognitive neuroscience; it offers some advantages over Laplacian methods. Importantly, the subtraction algorithm by which LRP is computed makes it largely impervious to contamination by activity that is not specifically related to the chosen response. This includes bilateral amplitude modulation, as noted by Jepma et al. (2009) in their study of nonspecific arousal triggered by a task‐irrelevant accessory stimulus. We argued (Sections 1.2, 3.4, and 4.3) that focusing too narrowly on the onset portion of the SLW can lead one to confuse bilateral amplitude modulation with a latency effect, due to the aperture problem. In chronometric and other research contexts for which response‐specific activation of sensorimotor cortex is important, the safest approach for now would be to analyze both LRP and SLW.
