A novel inhalable tanshinone IIA lipid nanocrystal formulation for the enhanced treatment of idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis
Authors: Qin Wang, Yun Zhao, Yuanhu Mao, Xiaobo Zhang, Lei Huang, Lei Tang, Yi Chen, Zipeng Gong, Jianqing Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a progressive, fatal interstitial lung disease marked by relentless deterioration of pulmonary function. Clinically, oral pharmacotherapy for IPF is hampered by two major limitations: inefficient drug delivery to the lung parenchyma and systemic exposure leading to unavoidable off-target side effects. In this study, we developed an inhalable tanshinone IIA (TA) formulation based on lipid nanocrystals to address these challenges. Leveraging the high drug loading capacity and structural stability of lipid nanocrystals, this platform overcomes the intrinsic hydrophobicity of TA, thereby markedly improving its aqueous dispersibility, aerosolization performance, and regional deposition in the deep lung. In vitro, TA lipid nanocrystals significantly enhanced cellular uptake in both pulmonary epithelial cells and lung fibroblasts. In vivo, they achieved substantially high and sustained pulmonary drug exposure. In a bleomycin-induced mouse model of IPF, inhaled TA lipid nanocrystals attenuated histopathological fibrosis, preserved lung function, and suppressed chronic inflammation via modulating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the coagulation-fibrinolysis system. Collectively, this study establishes TA as a promising inhaled therapeutic agent for IPF and introduces lipid nanocrystals as a versatile, scalable, and clinically translatable platform for targeted pulmonary delivery in fibrotic and other chronic respiratory diseases.Graphical Abstract Inhalable TA lipid nanocrystals for IPF treatments Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12951-026-04786-4.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) is a chronic progressive interstitial lung disease caused by a variety of lung injuries, including toxic, autoimmune, infectious, traumatic, and drug-induced causes [1–3]. Accumulating evidence indicates that persistent alveolar epithelial cell injury, activation, and dysregulated repair processes are key contributors to the onset and progression of IPF [4]. Persistent alveolar epithelial cell injury leads to aberrant activation, resulting in the dysregulated production of multiple pro-inflammatory and profibrotic mediators, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), chemokine C-C motif ligand 2 (CCL2), and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) [5]. These cytokines promote fibroblast proliferation, migration, and differentiation into myofibroblasts. Activated fibroblasts and myofibroblasts subsequently secrete excessive extracellular matrix (ECM) components, including fibronectin, collagen, and matrix metalloproteinases. Excessive deposition of ECM disrupts normal lung architecture, leading to alveolar destruction, reduced pulmonary compliance, and progressive impairment of gas exchange [6, 7]. As a result, patients with IPF experience an irreversible decline in lung function, leading to symptoms such as dyspnea that can ultimately be life-threatening. Its average life expectancy after diagnosis is only 3 ~ 5 years, and the five-year survival rate is less than 30% [8, 9]. Although lung transplantation remains the most effective treatment for IPF, only a small proportion of patients are able to undergo this intervention due to economic constraints, postoperative complications and age-related eligibility criteria [10, 11]. Current evidence-based guidelines for the treatment of IPF recommend the use of pirfenidone and nintedanib. These two drugs can delay the progression of IPF to a certain extent and reduce the mortality rate of IPF. However, the oral administration of high doses of these drugs may lead to adverse effects in the gastrointestinal tract and liver, imposing significant psychological and physiological burdens on patients [11–13]. Recently, PDE4B inhibitors nerandomilast has been approved for the treatment of pulmonary fibrosis. Meanwhile, new anti-fibrotic drugs such as Lysophosphatidic acids (LPA) antagonist BMS-986020 and novel Autotaxin (ATX) inhibitor BBT-877 are under development [14, 15]. Obviously, the development of more effective, reliable, and cost-effective therapeutic regimens for IPF remains a key focus in clinical research.

Tanshinone ⅡA (TA) is a hydrophobic diterpenoid compound extracted from the traditional Chinese medicinal herb Salvia miltiorrhiza. Previous studies have shown that TA exhibits anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative stress, anti-apoptotic, and anti-angiogenic effects, and has attracted widespread attention in the treatment of fibrosis in organs such as the lungs, heart, liver and kidneys [16]. Inhalation administration enables direct delivery of drugs to the pulmonary disease site without systemic circulation, offering advantages such as targeted and localized treatment, rapid clinical response, improved drug utilization, and enhanced patient convenience, which also reduces adverse reactions associated with oral or intravenous administration, making it a promising drug delivery approach for the treatment of IPF [17, 18]. However, drugs delivered to the respiratory tract following inhalation must overcome multiple pulmonary physiological barriers, such as mucociliary clearance, alveolar macrophage uptake, and epithelial permeability limitations, before reaching the target lung tissue to exert their therapeutic effects. Specifically, drug particles deposited in the conducting airways may interact with mucus and subsequently be cleared via mucociliary transport. Inhaled particles that reach the respiratory zone may encounter pulmonary surfactants, which can promote the formation of a “protein corona” and increase their recognition and uptake by alveolar macrophages, leading to phagocytosis and clearance [17, 19, 20]. Therefore, developing an inhalable formulation of TA capable of overcoming these physiological barriers is essential for achieving efficient drug delivery to the lungs, a critical step toward improving the therapeutic outcomes of IPF.

Nanocrystal, as a classic nano-formulation, have been extensively validated and successfully commercialized to address the solubility challenges of poorly water-soluble drugs across oral, parenteral, and other drug delivery routes. Nanocrystal technology reduces drug particle size to the nanoscale, thereby increasing the surface area of the particles and significantly enhancing the drug’s saturation solubility and dissolution rate. Compared with other nano drug delivery systems, nanocrystal formulations consist primarily of drug with stabilizers, offering the distinct advantage of high drug loading efficiency, which is suitable for the development of inhalable formulations. However, as thermodynamically unstable systems, drug nanocrystals face significant stability challenges. Studies have demonstrated that drug nanocrystals are prone to undergo Ostwald ripening during storage, leading to crystal growth and aggregation. To enhance the stability of drug nanocrystals, researchers have proposed a “liposomalization” strategy [21, 22]. Briefly, drug nanocrystals were encapsulated by liposomes via film dispersion method (FDM) to improve stability and high biocompatibility. However, the separate preparation of liposomes and nanocrystals, followed by the physical encapsulation of nanocrystals within liposomes, is still associated with critical drawbacks. Therefore, we proposed incorporating phospholipids as part of the surface stabilizer during the nanocrystal preparation process and developing TA lipid nanocrystals via the “bottom-up” method to enhance the physicochemical properties. The structure of TA lipid nanocrystals is relatively simple with the core of TA nanocrystals (NC@TA) covered by stabilizers and phospholipids. This delivery system not only retains the high drug loading capacity inherent to nanocrystals but also achieves enhanced colloidal stability through the incorporation of phospholipids as surface stabilizers, effectively mitigating Ostwald ripening in traditional nanocrystals during storage.

In this study, we designed an inhalable formulation of TA lipid nanocrystals for the treatment of IPF. Phospholipids including DPPC, DPPG and DPPE were used to prepare TA lipid nanocrystals. Using particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), zeta potential and in vitro drug release behavior as core evaluation indicators, we optimized the prescription and preparation process of TA lipid nanocrystals with excellent physicochemical stability and respiratory delivery adaptability to ensure their structural stability during the atomization process. The mucus penetration ability of TA lipid nanocrystals was evaluated using in vitro mucus penetration models and further analyzed at the molecular level by isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC). Moreover, the in vivo biodistribution of TA lipid nanocrystals following inhalation has been systematically investigated using fluorescence tracing and TA concentration detection. Finally, an IPF mouse model was established to investigate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of TA lipid nanocrystals and to further explore their potential mechanism in regulating the progression of IPF (Fig. 1). It is suggested that the inhalable lipid nanocrystals provide a safe and efficient new paradigm for chronic lung diseases, which overcome the limitations of pharmacological properties and enhance clinical treatment benefits.

Preparation and optimization of TA nanocrystals

Screening of stabilizer

According to the prescription in Table S1, the following methods were used to prepare NC@TA to examine the effects of the type, dosage, and preparation method of stabilizers on the physical and chemical properties of NC@TA. “Top-down” method (TD): Prepare the stabilizer solution of the corresponding concentration as per Table S1, then add TA to the above-mentioned stabilizer solution, vortex and water bath ultrasonic to disperse it properly, and finally use an ultrasonic cell disrupter (500 W, operating time 1 min, pulse 3 s, interval 3 s) to obtain NC@TA. “Bottom-up” method (BU): Prepare stabilizer solutions of corresponding concentrations according to Table S1 and set aside. TA was dissolved in acetone to obtain a TA solution. The stabilizer solution was added to the TA acetone solution to initiate precipitation of NC@TA. Ultrasonication was then performed to uniformly disperse the NC@TA. The organic solvent was then removed by rotary evaporation under reduced pressure. Finally, the NC@TA were sonicated using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (500 W, operating time 1 min, pulse 3 s, interval 3 s) to obtain NC@TA.

Optimization of drug-lipid ratio

According to the formulation in Table S2, TA lipid nanocrystals were prepared using the following methods to investigate the effects of different phospholipids, phospholipid-to-drug ratios, and the preparation methods on the physicochemical properties of the TA lipid nanocrystals. Film dispersion method (FDM): The prescribed amount of phospholipid was weighed into an eggplant-shaped flask and dissolved in chloroform. The chloroform was removed using a rotary evaporator, forming a white film on the flask wall. Then, NC@TA prepared using the optimal formulation and process determined in “Screening of stabilizer” were added to the flask and incubated at 50℃ for 30 min to form phospholipid-coated lipid nanocrystals. Subsequently, the membranes were further removed by water bath sonication for 1 ~ 2 min. Finally, the lipid nanocrystals were dispersed using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (500 W, 1 min, on for 3 s, off for 3 s) to obtain TA lipid nanocrystals. “Bottom-up” method (BU): Similar to the method in “ Screening of stabilizer”, first, dissolve the specified amounts of TA and phospholipids in acetone and ethanol respectively, then mix them evenly. The mixed solution of TA solution and phospholipid is added to the stabilizer solution to precipitate nanocrystals, which are then uniformly dispersed by ultrasound; and the organic solvent is subsequently removed by rotary vacuum evaporation. Finally, the nanocrystals were sonicated using an ultrasonic cell disruptor (500 W, 1 min, on for 3 s, off for 3 s) to obtain TA lipid nanocrystals.

Screening of phospholipids ratio

Lipid nanocrystals prepared using mixed phospholipids and those prepared using a single phospholipid may have different physicochemical properties. In this study, the total amount of TA and mixed phospholipids was fixed to explore the effects of different mass ratios of DPPC/DPPG or DPPC/DPPE on the physicochemical properties of lipid nanocrystals.

Through the exploration of the prescription process, it was determined that the preparation process of TA lipid nanocrystals is the “bottom-up” method. The best prescription uses P407 as the stabilizer, and simultaneously adds DPPC, mixed phospholipids of DPPC and DPPG, and mixed phospholipids of DPPC and DPPE. The mass ratio of phospholipids to TA is 3 : 1, and the mass ratios of DPPC to DPPG and DPPE are all 9 : 6. Then, TA lipid nanocrystals were prepared by the “bottom-up” method as described in “Optimization of drug-lipid ratio”. Besides, the preparation methods of IR-780-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals (NC@TA/IR-780) and NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals (NC@TA/NR) are similar. IR-780 or NR was dissolved in methanol and then thoroughly mixed with the phospholipid solution. The subsequent operations were consistent with the preparation method of TA lipid nanocrystals.

Release of TA from lipid nanocrystals

The in vitro release of TA lipid nanocrystals was determined by dialysis method and slightly modified. In order to meet the sink conditions for TA release and avoid aggregation and precipitation due to low solubility in water during the release process, PBS containing 40% methanol was used as the release medium for TA lipid nanocrystals in this study [23]. First, 400 µL of TA lipid nanocrystals were precisely measured and placed in a dialysis bag (MWCO 14,000 Da) and immersed in 20 mL of PBS containing 40% methanol. Then, the mixture was placed in an air bath constant temperature shaker and shaken at 37℃ and 50 rpm. At predetermined time points (1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), samples were taken from the release medium, and an equal volume of fresh release medium was added after sampling. TA was determined according to the above HPLC method, and the cumulative release rate of TA lipid nanocrystals was calculated.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC, Nano ITC, Waters Corporation, USA) was used to study the interaction mechanism between TA lipid nanocrystals (TA concentration was 0.5 mg/mL) and mucin at the molecular level. In vitro cellular uptake and transmembrane transport of TA lipid nanocrystals

In vitro cellular uptake of TA lipid nanocrystals

A549, RAW264.7, and MRC-5 cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and cultured for 24 h. After removing the original culture medium, 1 mL of culture medium containing NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals was added. The equivalent doses of TA were 2, 0.5, and 1 µM, respectively, and the equivalent dose of NR was 30, 7.5, 15 ng/mL. After incubation at 37℃ for 4 h, the culture medium was discarded, and the cells were washed twice with PBS. Then, 100 µL of RIPA lysis buffer (RIPA lysis buffer : PMSF = 100 : 1) was added to each well. After shaking on ice for 30 min, the lysate was removed and centrifuged (10,000 × g, 15 min). An appropriate amount of the supernatant was used for BCA protein quantification. The remaining sample was added to methanol and sonicated for 15 min. The sample was then air-dried and dissolved in 800 µL of methanol, then TA content was determined using HPLC.

At the same time, flow cytometry was used to determine the cell uptake of NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals. After the cells were incubated with NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals for 4 h, the culture medium was discarded, the cells were washed twice with PBS, and the cells were collected by trypsin digestion and centrifuged (3000 × g, 5 min). The supernatant was discarded, 1 mL of PBS was added and mixed, and the average fluorescence intensity was measured using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCantoⅡ, Becton, Dickinson and Company).

In vitro transmembrane transport of TA lipid nanocrystals

In order to understand the in vitro transport behavior of TA lipid nanocrystals, a Calu-3 cell model was established. The transepithelial electric resistance (TEER) value was measured. The specific method is as follows: Calu-3 cells were seeded on transwell chambers (6.5 mm diameter, 0.4 μm pore size, polyester membrane) at a density of 1.5 × 105 cells/well and cultured in an incubator at 37℃ and 5% CO2. After 21 days of culture, the cells formed a monolayer structure with tight junctions. The TEER value without cell layer was measured as a blank control, and culture medium containing TA lipid nanocrystals was added to the upper chamber of the transwell. The electrical resistance of the cell monolayer was measured at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, and 24 h of incubation, and the TEER value was calculated. At the same time, after 4 h co-incubation, TA lipid nanocrystals were removed from the upper chamber and replaced with blank culture medium.

In vitro anti-fibrosis study of TA lipid nanocrystals

Scratch test

A549 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well. When the cells grew to 70 ~ 80% confluence, they were cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h. Then, culture medium containing TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) was added for stimulation for 24 h, and then scratches were made perpendicular to the cell plane with a pipette tip. After scratching, the cells were washed three times with PBS. The cells were incubated with TA lipid nanocrystals, free TA and PFD for 24 h (the equivalent doses of TA and PFD were 2 µM). The model and control groups were treated with equal amounts of culture medium. Images were captured using an optical microscope at 0 and 24 h, respectively, and the cell migration rate was calculated using Image J software.

Epithelial-mesenchymal transition process

A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well, incubated overnight. When the cells grew to 70 ~ 80% confluence, and then cultured in serum-free medium for 24 h. The cells were induced in culture medium containing TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) for 24 h, and then washed twice with PBS solution. The cells were co-incubated with TA lipid nanocrystals or free TA and PFD for 24 h (the equivalent doses of TA and PFD were 2 µM), and the model and control groups were treated with equal amounts of culture medium. The cells were collected and the levels of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and vimentin in the cells were determined using ELISA kits. All steps were performed according to the kit instructions.

Immunofluorescence

MRC-5 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and induced with TGF-β1 (10 ng/mL) for 48 h to simulate the in vitro fibrotic environment. The culture medium was then removed, and the cells were incubated with TA lipid nanocrystals or free TA and PFD for 24 h (the equivalent doses of TA and PFD were 1 µM). The model and control groups were treated with equal amounts of culture medium. The cells were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed three times with PBS buffer for 5 min each time. Immunostaining permeabilization solution (0.1% Triton X-100, Cat no. T8200, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added for permeabilization at room temperature for 10 min, and then washed with PBS buffer three times, each time for 5 min. Immunostaining blocking solution (1% BSA, Cat no. A8020, Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) was added and blocked at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were incubated with primary antibodies against α-SMA rabbit mAb (Cat no. A17910, ABclonal, Wuhan, China) and collagen-I rabbit mAb (Cat no. A24112, ABclonal, Ltd, Wuhan, China) respectively at 4℃ for 12 h, respectively. The primary antibody was then discarded, and the cells were incubated with CoraLite488- conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (H + L) (Cat no. SA00013-2, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Then, cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (Cat no. C1002, Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Finally, add the anti-fluorescence quencher and seal the plateand observed under a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, LSM 9000, Carl Zeiss AG).

In vivo biological studies of TA lipid nanocrystals

C57BL/6J mice were randomly divided into P407-NC@TA/IR-780 group, DPPC-NC@TA/IR-780 group, DPPG-NC@TA/IR-780 group and DPPE-NC@TA/IR-780 group. Subsequently, IR-780-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals (IR-780 equivalent dose of 200 µg/kg, TA equivalent dose of 10 mg/kg) were administered to mice via a small animal oronasal exposure system via a nebulizer (Aerogen® pro, Galway, Ireland). Then, mice were anesthetized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging at 1 h and 8 h using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS Lumina III, PerkinElmer, USA). The excitation/emission wavelengths of IR-780 were set at 780/845 nm. Finally, tissue fluorescence signal intensity was quantified using Living Image® software.

NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals were administered to KM mice via a small animal oronasal exposure system via a nebulizer (NR equivalent dose of 1 mg/kg, TA equivalent dose of 10 mg/kg). After 10 min, the mice were anesthetized and killed, and the lung tissue was quickly collected and fixed. Fluorescence sections were prepared to observe the retention of lipid nanocrystals in the lung tissue. The excitation/emission wavelengths of NR were set to 560/635 nm.

Pharmacokinetics and biodistribution of TA lipid nanocrystals

KM mice were randomly divided into P407-NC@TA group, DPPC-NC@TA group, DPPG-NC@TA group and DPPE-NC@TA group. TA lipid nanocrystals were administered to mice at an equivalent dose of TA at 10 mg/kg using a small animal oronasal exposure system via a nebulizer (Aerogen® pro, Galway, Ireland). At predetermined time points (0.05, 0.083, 0.17, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, and 8 h), mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and blood was collected and centrifuged (1,160 × g, 10 min) to obtain plasma. After blood collection, the lungs were immediately lavaged with PBS to obtain BALF. The BALF was centrifuged at high speed (10,000 × g, 10 min) to separate the supernatant and sediment for later use, the lungs and other tissues were collected and homogenated. Taking cryptotanshinone as the internal standard,then methanol was added to extract TA, and the TA content in each biological sample was determined by HPLC.

In vivo anti-fibrotic efficacy of TA lipid nanocrystals

The IPF mouse model was established using BLM. BLM was administered to mice at a dose of 2 U/kg by tracheal instillation. The mice were then placed upright for 1 ~ 2 min, rotated slightly left and right, and the chests were gently massaged to ensure that BLM was evenly distributed in the lungs. Then the mice were randomly divided into model group, PFD group, P407-NC@TA group, DPPC-NC@TA group, DPPG-NC@TA group, DPPE-NC@TA, blank P407 group, blank DPPC group, blank DPPG group and blank DPPE group, and the mice not treated with BLM served as the control group.

Starting from the second day after BLM treatment, mice were given P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, DPPE-NC@TA, PFD solutions and blank vehicle solutions P407, DPPC, DPPG and DPPE through the small animal oronasal exposure system via a nebulizer at a dosing frequency of once every two days. The dosages of TA and PFD were 10 mg/kg and 20 mg/kg, respectively. The control group and the model group were treated with equal amounts of PBS in the same manner, and the living conditions and body weight changes of the mice were monitored during the treatment. On the 22nd day following treatment, the key lung function parameters of mice, including forced vital capacity (FVC), residual volume (IC), and dynamic compliance (Cdyn), were measured in real time using animal lung function analysis system (AniRes2005 V3.5, Yuyan Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China). The mice were then killed by anesthetized with isoflurane, and the lung tissues were collected and weighed to calculate the corresponding lung coefficients. Some mice underwent bronchoalveolar lavage with 1 mL of pre-chilled PBS, and the BALF supernatant was collected and stored at -80℃ for the determination of the expression levels of TGF-β1, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. The left lungs of some mice that did not undergo bronchoalveolar lavage were removed, washed with an appropriate amount of PBS, dried with filter paper, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological analysis, including hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson staining, along with immunohistochemical analysis of α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ. The remaining lungs were stored at -80℃ for the determination of hydroxyproline (HYP).

In addition, the IPF mice were then randomly divided into the control group, model group, and P407-NC@TA group, and administer the drug according to the above-mentioned method and frequency. On the 22nd day following treatment, lung tissues were collected and subjected to transcriptomic analysis, and potential pathways were verified by WB, q-PCR and Elisa methods.

Safety evaluation

KM mice were randomly divided into control group, P407-NC@TA group, DPPC-NC@TA group, DPPG-NC@TA group and DPPE-NC@TA group, and drug administration was carried out according to the method and frequency of “In vivo anti-fibrotic efficacy of TA lipid nanocrystals”. After the treatment, whole blood of mice was collected for routine blood analysis, and blood biochemical analysis was conducted on the serum samples of each group of mice, including liver function indicators aspartate transaminase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), as well as renal function indicators creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN) and uric acid (UA). At the same time, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney were harvested and stained with HE for histopathological analysis.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data are expressed as mean ± SD. The t-test was used to test the significance of the difference between the two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test the significance of the difference between the multiple groups. Significance criteria: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Molecular dynamics simulation of TA and stabilizer molecules

Both small molecule surfactants and polymer surfactants have been used as the stabilizers of nanocrystals. Above all, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed to systematically investigate the interactions between TA and stabilizers, with the aim of identifying potential candidates suitable for the formulation of NC@TA (Figure S1, Supporting information). The root mean square deviation (RMSD) during the simulation was calculated, and the results showed that all systems reached equilibrium approximately 20,000 ps after the start of the simulation. Before the start of each system, TA and stabilizer molecules were randomly distributed in the water box. The radius of gyration (RG) indicates the root mean square distance of all atoms in a molecule relative to its center of mass, and its fluctuation reflects the stability of the binding between small molecules during the simulation [25]. The RG and total energy interactions between TA and small molecule stabilizers were calculated throughout the simulation (Fig. 2A-B). The results revealed that the RG profiles of all small molecule stabilizers exhibited significant fluctuations, with SDS inducing the most pronounced energetic effects. Among macromolecular stabilizers, P407 displayed a relatively stable RG trajectory over the simulation period and generated the strongest energy interaction, suggesting the most robust association between P407 and TA (Fig. 2C-D). Furthermore, representative conformations illustrating the interaction modes between each stabilizer and TA during the simulation are presented (Fig. 2E). Collectively, the “dry chemical” experimental findings indicate that all tested stabilizers possess the capacity to bind strongly with TA, which may facilitate the formation of NC@TA.

Preparation and characterization of TA lipid nanocrystals

Various conditions decide the key physical and chemical parameters such as the solubility and dissolution rate of nanocrystals, including the nanocrystal preparation methods, the type and concentration of stabilizers. To optimize the characteristics of NC@TA, we employed comprehensive indicators including particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and in vitro drug release performance. We investigated the effects of small molecule stabilizers (Fig. 2F-I) and polymer stabilizers (Fig. 2J-M) on the characteristics of TA nanocrystals. Regardless of the small molecular or polymer stabilizers, although the nanocrystals prepared via the top-down approach exhibited the smallest average particle size at a 2% (w/v) concentration, owing to the potential of high stabilizer concentrations to perturb the osmotic homeostasis of artificial mucus, a concentration of 0.2% (w/v) was adopted as the optimized stabilizer level. For small-molecule stabilizers, both SDS-NC@TA prepared by top-down approach and Tween 80-NC@TA prepared by bottom-up approach achieved comparably minimal particle sizes (Fig. 2F-H). Given SDS’s susceptibility to precipitate at low temperature and the consequent compromise in formulation stability, Tween 80-NC@TA prepared via the bottom-up approach was the optimal strategy. For polymeric stabilizers, P407-NC@TA prepared via the bottom-up approach exhibited the smallest size (Fig. 2J-L). However, Tween 80-NC@TA and P407-NC@TA still faced the challenge of low drug release (Fig. 2I&M). In summary, although the particle sizes of Tween80-NC@TA and P407-NC@TA fall within an acceptable range, both formulations exhibit suboptimal colloidal stability that evidenced by relatively large hydrodynamic diameters, poor dispersion homogeneity, and insufficient drug release under physiological dissolution conditions.

To improve the physicochemical properties of NC@TA, synthetic phospholipids (DPPC, DPPG, and DPPE) were added as co-stabilizers. The effects of different phospholipid-to-drug mass ratios and preparation methods on the physicochemical properties of TA lipid nanocrystals were investigated (Figure S2-3, Supporting information). When the “bottom-up” method was used and the phospholipid : drug mass ratio was 3 : 1, the drug release rate of TA lipid nanocrystals was significantly improved, but the particle size distribution was not significantly improved. Based on the results, the ratio of TA and mixed phospholipids was fixed at 3 : 1 (w/w) and the “bottom-up” method was selected as the preparation method. To further improve the characteristics, DPPC/DPPG or DPPC/DPPE at different mass ratios were used to prepare TA lipid nanocrystals. In terms of NC@TA prepared by small molecule surfactants, the mass ratio of DPPC/DPPG or DPPC/DPPE has no significant effect on their particle size (Figure S4, Supporting information). However, the particle size of NC@TA prepared by P407 has been significantly reduced by mixed phospholipid at the DPPC/DPPG or DPPC/DPPE mass ratio of 9 : 6 with PDI < 0.3 (Figure S5, Supporting information). The zeta potential of all TA lipid nanocrystals is negative or close to neutral. Therefore, the “bottom-up” method is used for the preparation of TA lipid nanocrystals, the mass ratio of phospholipid to drug is 3 : 1, for which the ratio of mixed phospholipids is DPPC : DPPG (or DPPE) = 9 : 6. For the sake of clarity, DPPG-NC@TA and DPPE-NC@TA will be used in the following sections, and lipid nanocrystals using only DPPC will be labeled as DPPC-NC@TA.

Characterization of TA lipid nanocrystals

Above all, DPPG-NC@TA and DPPE-NC@TA have smaller particle sizes than DPPC-NC@TA and P407-NC@TA (Fig. 3A). All TA lipid nanocrystals have good dispersibility and negative or close to neutral zeta potential (Fig. 3B-C). The LE of all TA lipid nanocrystals tends to be 100%, and the DL is approximately 16% to 34% (Figure S6A, Supporting information). In addition, we also monitored the placement stability and atomization stability of TA lipid nanocrystals from the aspects of particle size and PDI. The placement stability results show that TA lipid nanocrystals have high stability for at least one week at 4 °C (Figure S6B-C, Supporting information). After atomizing with a nebulizer for different times, we measured the particle size of TA lipid nanocrystals. The results show that after 7 atomization processes, the TA lipid nanocrystals have relatively small changes in particle size and PDI, and possess good atomization stability (Figure S6D-E, Supporting information). The cumulative release of TA from DPPG-NC@TA and DPPE-NC@TA was significantly improved than that of DPPC-NC@TA and P407-NC@TA (Fig. 3D). Although 40% methanol PBS medium differs from the physiological environment, it can ensure that TA remains in a leaky state throughout the release process, thereby distinguishing the release differences of nanocrystals prepared by different prescriptions. The morphology of TA lipid nanocrystals was observed using TEM (Fig. 3E). P407-NC@TA showed an irregular sphere, and this remains the case even after being wrapped by DPPC and DPPG. Only DPPE-NC@TA exhibits a regular sphere indicating that the evenly distribution of phospholipids on the surface of the nanocrystals. In addition, a self-made TSI was used to study the characteristics and deposition of TA lipid nanocrystals in various stages of the simulated respiratory tract after nebulization (Table 1 and Figure S7, Supporting information). The particle size of TA lipid nanocrystals slightly increased at S0 and S1 following nebulization, while it was slightly reduced or remained nearly unchanged compared to S1 at S2. No significant changes in PDI and zeta potential were observed at all stages after nebulization. All particles are most concentrated at S2, and the distribution of TA lipid nanocrystals at S2 is significantly higher than that of P407-NC@TA, especially for DPPE-NC@TA.

Furthermore, TA lipid nanocrystals were analyzed using X-ray diffractometer (Fig. 3F). The TA has characteristic diffraction peaks at 7.26°. The P407 and phospholipids have characteristic diffraction peaks in the range of 10° ~ 30°. The diffraction peaks of the physical mixture were similar to those of TA and surfactants, indicating that physical mixing did not change the crystal form of TA. However, for all TA lipid nanocrystals, the characteristic diffraction peak of TA at 7.26° disappeared and new peaks appeared in the range of 30° ~ 90°, indicating the formation of a new crystal form of TA. The thermal behavior of TA lipid nanocrystals was further studied using differential calorimetry (Fig. 3G). The endothermic peak of TA, P407 and phospholipids disappeared in all TA lipid nanocrystals. Interestingly, the endothermic peaks shift and weaken in physical mixtures, which may be attributed to physical interactions between the components and TA. Meanwhile, the thermal stability of TA lipid nanocrystals was probed through thermogravimetric analysis as the temperature ascended steadily toward 600℃, unveiling its resilient structural integrity under intense thermal stress (Fig. 3H). TA begins to decompose at around 250℃. All TA lipid nanocrystals start to decompose as the temperature rises, but the mass loss is less than that of TA, except for DPPC-NC@TA. Although all the physical mixtures start to decompose as the temperature increases, the final mass loss is much higher than that of TA indicating the formation of a new crystal form during the preparation of TA lipid nanocrystals.

Interactions between TA lipid nanocrystals and mucin

To systematically evaluate the penetration efficiency of TA lipid nanocrystals in respiratory mucus, both a gelatin-mucus model and a transwell model were used. The results from the gelatin-mucus model demonstrated that P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, and DPPE-NC@TA were capable of penetrating the artificial mucus and being retained within the gelatin layer, accounting for approximately 58.88%, 84.88%, 58.99%, and 16.62% of the total administered dose at 8 h, respectively (Fig. 4A-B). In the transwell model, the cumulative amounts of P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, and DPPE-NC@TA that penetrated the polycarbonate membrane with mucus were 24.48%, 22.90%, 21.88%, and 1.90% of the total administered dose at 8 h, respectively, with corresponding Papp values of 5.13 × 10− 6, 4.91 × 10− 6, 2.71 × 10− 6, and 0.65 × 10− 6 cm/s, respectively (Fig. 4C-E). Interestingly, we found that the permeability of TA lipid nanocrystals in the two models was different. The primary limitation arises from the transwell model architecture. Following mucus penetration, TA lipid nanocrystals must traverse the polycarbonate membrane (0.4 μm pore size) to reach the basolateral compartment, introducing an artificial diffusion barrier that reduces apparent permeability. However, it is not difficult for us to find that the permeability trends of TA lipid nanocrystals on the two models tend to be the same.

To further understand the interactions of TA lipid nanocrystals with mucin at the molecular level, ITC was used to analyze the interactions. When titrating P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, and DPPE-NC@TA with mucin solutions, a 300 s titration interval was sufficient to allow the thermal signal to return to baseline between titrations. And the raw thermogram was recorded in µJ/s, and the enthalpy change was precisely calculated for each injection, revealing subtle thermodynamic interactions between the TA lipid nanocrystals and mucin (Fig. 4F-J). The thermodynamic parameters of the interactions between nanocrystals and mucin were calculated (Fig. 4K). The results indicated that the interaction between TA lipid nanocrystals and mucin were an exothermic spontaneous reaction accompanied by increasing entropy. The binding constant Ka of DPPE-NC@TA is significantly higher than those of other lipid nanocrystals, being 2.67-, 1.99-, and 1.83-fold greater than that of P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, and DPPG-NC@TA, respectively. According to Ross theory, a negative ΔH and a positive ΔS indicate an enthalpy-driven process governed by non-covalent interactions, with electrostatic interactions playing a dominant role [26, 27]. Therefore, this result at the molecular level confirms that the poor mucus permeability of DPPE-NC@TA is mainly due to the formation of strong non-covalent bonds with mucin.

In vivo biodistribution of TA lipid nanocrystals after aerosol inhalation

IR-780-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals were prepared and characterized to investigate the biodistribution of nanocrystals after aerosol inhalation (Figure S8, Supporting information). Then, mice were anesthetized, and the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys were collected for ex vivo fluorescence imaging at 1 h and 8 h using IVIS (Fig. 5A-C). The results showed that TA lipid nanocrystals were mainly distributed in the lung and liver after aerosol inhalation. TA lipid nanocrystals demonstrated significant lung distribution following administration, as evidenced by significantly higher fluorescence intensity in the lungs compared with the heart, liver, spleen, and kidneys. At 8 h post-administration, pulmonary fluorescence signals declined across all groups to varying extents. Notably, the DPPE-NC@TA/IR-780 group retained the highest pulmonary fluorescence intensity at this time point, suggesting superior pulmonary retention. In contrast, the DPPC-NC@TA/IR-780 group exhibited the lowest lung signal intensity at 8 h, consistent with accelerated pulmonary clearance. However, the distribution of TA lipid nanocrystals in the local lung tissues and respiratory bronchioles cannot be directly observed using the IVIS. Therefore, NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals were prepared to further investigate the pulmonary retention of nanocrystals after aerosol inhalation via fluorescent sections (Figure S9, Supporting information). Fluorescent sections of the lung tissue and bronchioles were collected 10 min after aerosol inhalation (Fig. 5D). All the NC@TA/NR showed accumulation at both bronchioles and lung tissues indicated by the fluorescence of NR, especially for NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals.

The BALF was divided into supernatant and sediment. Since alveolar macrophages account for 95% of the total BALF cells [28, 29], TA in the BALF sediment represents the fraction that has been internalized by alveolar macrophages and not yet eliminated, whereas TA in the BALF supernatant can be regarded as the portion retained within the mucus layer (Fig. 5E-G). Correspondingly, TA in lavaged lung represents the amount of TA accumulated in the lung tissue and the TA in whole lung includes TA in BALF and lavaged lung (Fig. 5H-I). The Cmax and AUC of DPPE-NC@TA in BALF supernatant, BALF sediment and whole BALF were higher than those of P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, and DPPG-NC@TA. The AUCall of DPPE-NC@TA in the whole BALF were 1.87, 1.47 and 1.84 times that of P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA and DPPG-NC@TA, respectively. While the AUCall of DPPC-NC@TA in lavaged lung and plasma were higher than those of P407-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, and DPPE-NC@TA, indicating that DPPC-NC@TA exhibited higher retention in the lavaged lung and the circulation after inhalation.

In vitro anti-fibrotic studies of TA lipid nanocrystals

Above all, the cytotoxicity of free TA and TA lipid nanocrystals on A549, MRC-5, RAW264.7 and Calu-3 cells were evaluated (Figure S10-13, Supporting information). Then, the cellular uptake of NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals was monitored by flow cytometry. Compared with free NR, TA lipid nanocrystals significantly increased the cellular uptake of NR (Fig. 6A-B). Meanwhile, the TA content was detected by HPLC to indicate the cellular uptake of TA lipid nanocrystals (Fig. 6C-E). Unlike the results obtained by flow cytometry, the cellular uptake indicated by TA content measurement showed that P407-NC@TA did not enhance the cellular uptake of TA, and only DPPE-NC@TA increased the cellular uptake of TA in A549, MRC-5 and RAW264.7 cells. The reason for this difference might be related to the different detection methods and target substances of flow cytometry and HPLC. Flow cytometry was used to detect the fluorescence amount after cells took up NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals. Since the labeled positions of the fluorescent molecules in the nanocrystals may be the inner nuclei of the nanocrystals or the lipid layer. Therefore, when cells come into contact with lipid nanocrystals, they can adsorb and combine fluorescent molecules, thereby causing all NR-labeled TA lipid nanocrystals to exhibit strong fluorescence signals. However, HPLC detects the intracellular TA content after the cells take up TA lipid nanocrystals. Therefore, only TA lipid nanocrystals that are completely taken up by cells and remain intracellular can be detected by HPLC after cell lysis. We also evaluated the intercellular transport ability by monitoring the TEER of bronchial epithelial cells. After successfully constructing a Calu-3 monolayer cell model using a transwell chamber (TEER > 300 Ω·cm2), TA lipid nanocrystals were treated (Figure S14, Supporting information). The results showed that TEER was decreased due to TA lipid nanocrystals, and TEER gradually increased within 24 h after removal TA lipid nanocrystals, indicating that TA lipid nanocrystals could open tight junctions and penetrate through the intercellular pathways.

The progression of IPF is related to different types of cell interactions, such as the interaction between fibroblasts and epithelial cells, involving processes such as epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), activation of fibroblasts and differentiation into myofibroblasts. Subsequently, we further studied the in vitro anti-fibrotic effect of TA lipid nanocrystals. The wound healing experiment of A549 epithelial cells showed that the cell migration rates in the TA lipid nanocrystal groups were significantly lower than that in the model group (Fig. 6F-G). Meanwhile, the protein expression levels of N-cadherin and vimentin were significantly upregulated, whereas E-cadherin expression was markedly downregulated in A549 cells stimulated by TGF-β1 (Fig. 6H-J). In vivo anti-fibrosis study of TA lipid nanocrystals

A BLM-induced IPF mouse model was established to investigate the therapeutic efficacy and underlying mechanisms of TA lipid nanocrystals. First, the in vivo efficacy study was conducted in accordance with the established modeling and dosing regimen (Fig. 7A). The day of BLM administration was designated as day 0. Starting from day 1, the survival status and body weight of the mice were monitored, with body weight recorded every two days. On the 22nd day, mice were euthanized under gas anesthesia, and lung tissues were harvested for determination of the lung coefficient. The body weight of the control group gradually increased during the treatment period, while the body weight of the model group and each blank vehicle solution group continued to decrease from the 5th day and stabilized until the 11th day, with a body weight loss rate of approximately 20% (Fig. 7B and Figure S15A, Supporting information). In the PFD, P407-NC@TA, DPPC-NC@TA, DPPG-NC@TA, and DPPE-NC@TA groups, mouse body weight decreased slightly by approximately 5% during the first 7 days and subsequently stabilized.

FVC is the gold standard for assessing lung function and response to therapeutic interventions in IPF [30]. On the 22nd day, key lung function parameters, including FVC, IC, and Cdyn, were measured in real time using animal lung function analysis system to characterize the therapeutic effect of TA lipid nanocrystals (Fig. 7C-E and Figure S15B-D, Supporting information). The FVC, IC, and Cdyn values of the model and blank vehicle solution groups were significantly decreased compared to those of the control group, indicating a significant decline in lung function after IPF model establishment. The FVC, IC, and Cdyn values of the PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals groups were significantly higher than those of the model group, indicating that PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals possess a protective effect in IPF mouse model and can significantly alleviate the decline in lung function.

Furthermore, lung coefficients in each group were calculated (Fig. 7F and Figure S15E, Supporting information). The lung coefficient of the model group and the blank carrier group was significantly higher than that of the control group, whereas treatment with PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals resulted in reduced lung coefficients. To evaluate the anti-fibrotic effects of TA lipid nanocrystals, pathological changes in lung tissues were assessed using HE and Masson staining, along with immunohistochemical analysis of α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ (Fig. 7G-K and Figure S15F-J, Supporting information). Compared with the control group, the model group and the blank carrier group exhibited marked pathological alterations, including alveolar cavity collapse, disruption of alveolar architecture, pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration, and extensive deposition of blue-stained collagen fibers. The expression levels of α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ were significantly upregulated. Following treatment with PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals via aerosol inhalation, pulmonary inflammatory infiltration was attenuated, the area of collagen fibrosis was markedly reduced, and the expression levels of α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ were downregulated.

Given that HYP has long been used as the “gold standard” for collagen content evaluation, its concentration serves as a key indicator of collagen metabolism [31]. TGF-β1 plays a pivotal role in promoting fibroblast-to-myofibroblast differentiation, EMT, and subsequent collagen accumulation [32]. Therefore, HYP and TGF-β1 were quantified to assess the anti-fibrotic efficacy of TA lipid nanocrystals (Fig. 7L-M). The levels of HYP and TGF-β1 were determined in lung tissues and BALF, respectively. Compared with the control group, HYP and TGF-β1 levels were significantly elevated in the model group. Following treatment with PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals, the expression of both markers was significantly reduced, indicating attenuation of pulmonary fibrosis. Since IPF is characterized by excessive inflammatory responses, the levels of key pro-inflammatory cytokines in BALF, including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, were quantified following treatment (Fig. 7N-P). Compared with the control group, the expression of these cytokines was markedly elevated in the model group, indicating a robust inflammatory state. Notably, treatment with PFD and TA lipid nanocrystals significantly reduced their expression levels, demonstrating a potent anti-inflammatory effect. In addition, the safety profile of TA lipid nanocrystals following aerosol inhalation was systematically evaluated. The results demonstrated that TA lipid nanocrystals exhibited favorable biocompatibility. After inhalation, no significant alterations in hematological parameters were detected in mice (Table S4, Supporting information) and histopathological examination of major organs revealed no notable abnormalities (Figure S16A, Supporting information). Moreover, biochemical analysis revealed that compared with the control group, there were no significant increases in serum ALT, AST, BUN, Cr and UA after treatment of TA lipid nanocrystals (Figure S16B-F, Supporting information), further demonstrating the safety of nanocrystals. Therefore, the therapeutic effect of TA lipid nanocrystals is close to that of the first-line drug PFD and has good safety, exhibiting great clinical translation potential.

Anti-fibrotic mechanism of TA lipid nanocrystals

To explore the potential mechanisms of TA in IPF treatment, we conducted transcriptomic studies on the tissue samples of the treated mice. Compared with the control group, a total of 1579 differentially expressed genes were identified in the model group, including 862 up-regulated genes and 717 down-regulated genes. Compared with the model group, a total of 520 differentially expressed genes were identified in the TA treatment group, including 255 up-regulated genes and 265 down-regulated genes. Venn analysis was subsequently performed to identify common differentially expressed genes shared between the model group versus the control group and the TA treatment group versus the model group, resulting in the identification of 212 overlapping differentially expressed genes (Fig. 8A, Figure S17-18, Supporting information). To investigate the biological processes affected by TA during the treatment of IPF, gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes identified. The top 15 significantly enriched terms in gene function were selected from the three categories based on ascending p-value, including biological process (BP), molecular function (MF), and cellular component (CC). The BP analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were mainly enriched in biological processes such as regulation of fibroblast proliferation, positive regulation of fibroblast proliferation, positive regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, and cell-substrate adhesion. The CC analysis found that most of the genes were located in collagen-containing extracellular matrix, aggresome, presynaptic actin cytoskeleton, and integrin alpha5-beta1 complex. The MF analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes had molecular functions such as regulating transcription corepressor activity, thrombopoietin receptor activity, P-type transmembrane transporter activity, and epidermal growth factor receptor binding (Fig. 8B). Besides, KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on the differentially expressed genes, revealing 17 significantly enriched pathways with p values less than 0.05. These pathways were highly associated with the complement and coagulation cascades, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, JAK-STAT signaling pathway, and Wnt signaling pathway (Fig. 8C). It is suggested that these pathways may be involved in the anti-pulmonary fibrosis effect of TA.

Discussion

Nanocrystals have the advantages of simple preparation process and high drug loading capacity. The surface stabilizer is a critical determinant of nanocrystal morphology and stability and must fulfill three key requirements: strong adsorption onto the drug crystal surface, high surface coverage, and a robust barrier effect [33]. Above all, we used MD simulations to predict the interactions between stabilizers and TA. The results indicate that all stabilizers possess the capacity to bind strongly with TA, suggesting a potentially significant stabilizing effect on TA. We further confirmed the prescription of NC@TA through “wet chemical experiments”. In terms of small molecule stabilizers, compared with SDS and Rha, the nanocrystals prepared by Tween 80 are more stable and have a smaller particle size, making them more suitable for use as stabilizers. It may be attributed to the low critical micelle concentration of Tween 80, which enables a more pronounced emulsifying effect even at low concentrations. This property enhances the adsorption of Tween 80 onto the surface of drug crystals, promoting the formation of a stable protective layer and thereby improving nanocrystal stabilization [34, 35]. For macromolecular stabilizers, the particle size of NC@TA prepared by P407 is smaller than that prepared by HPMC and TPGS. It is suggested that P407 adsorbs onto the surface of poorly soluble drug primarily through the PPO segment, and the hydrophilic PEO segment provides strong steric hindrance [36], thereby stabilizing the NC@TA. HPMC and TPGS are mainly adsorbed on the surface of drug crystals through hydrogen bonding [37]. However, due to the absence of hydrogen bond donor groups, TA cannot be effectively stabilized by HPMC and TPGS, leading to an increased particle size.

Improving the dissolution of poorly water-soluble drugs is a primary objective in the development of nanocrystals. Although NC@TA prepared with Tween 80 and P407 exhibit acceptable particle size and narrow size distribution, their limited in vitro drug release may compromise the therapeutic efficacy of TA on IPF. We proposed the incorporation of DPPC, DPPG, and DPPE as components of the surface stabilizer system to further enhance the physicochemical properties of NC@TA. The results showed that, when the mass ratio of phospholipid to drug was held constant, varying the composition of the mixed phospholipids had no significant effect on the particle size of lipid nanocrystals stabilized with Tween 80. However, when the mass ratio of DPPC to DPPG (or DPPE) was 9 : 6, the particle size of TA lipid nanocrystals prepared with P407 was significantly reduced, and drug release was markedly improved. Previous studies have demonstrated that P407 can exhibit a strong interaction with phospholipids and actively participate in, promote, and stabilize liposomes and lipid-based nanodrug vesicles [38–40]. Therefore, the optimal surface stabilizer combination of P407 and mixed phospholipids for the preparation of TA lipid nanocrystals is closely attributed to their interactions. It is suggested that hydrophobic PPO segments of P407 can insert into the interstitial spaces within the phospholipid layers, while the hydrophilic PEO chains of P407 extend outward to form a stable hydration layer, thereby generating pronounced steric hindrance. Together with phospholipids, they constitute a stable “micelle”-like architecture that enables effective encapsulation of the nanocrystal drug and prevents particle aggregation. TA lipid nanocrystals exhibited no significant changes in particle size, PDI after aerosolization, indicating good aerosol stability. The primary site of deposition following aerosolization was at S2, representing the lower respiratory tract. DPPG-NC@TA and DPPE-NC@TA showed higher deposition rates at S2 compared to that of P407-NC@TA. This may be attributed to their smaller particle sizes and better stability, which facilitates distal airway diffusion, thereby potentially enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of TA.

Respiratory mucus serves as the primary physiological and pathological barrier to the airway transport of inhaled particles, with mucin as its principal structural component. The viscoelastic network formed by cross-linked protein fibers endows the mucus barrier with hydrogel-like properties [41]. Secretory mucins determine the viscosity of respiratory mucus and establish the structural foundation of complex gel networks. These mucins primarily undergo polymerization through disulfide bonds formed between their cysteine-rich domains, as well as via non-glycosylated regions and non-covalent intermolecular interactions, thereby forming a stable and dense physical network [41–43]. Due to the abundance of carboxyl groups and DNA-derived phosphate residues in mucus, its internal environment carries a net negative charge [44]. These unique properties enable respiratory mucus to defend against the invasion of foreign pathogens through multiple mechanisms while simultaneously influencing nanomedicine delivery. Interactions between nanomedicines and mucins, such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, may lead to nanoparticle retention within the mucus layer, thereby affecting their in vivo fate and therapeutic efficacy [45]. In this study, the in vitro mucus penetration of TA lipid nanocrystals was evaluated using a gelatin-mucus model and a transwell system. The interactions between TA lipid nanocrystals and mucin were analyzed at the molecular level using ITC. DPPE-NC@TA exhibited the strongest interaction with mucin and was prone to retention in the mucus layer. In contrast, DPPC-NC@TA exhibited weaker interactions with mucin and enhanced mucus penetration capability, enabling rapid delivery to the alveolar epithelium. Since both TA lipid nanocrystals exhibit excellent anti-fibrotic therapeutic effects, lipid nanocrystals are highly promising as inhaled drug carriers, regardless of their behavior as either mucus adhesion or mucus penetration.

The in vivo fate of TA after inhalation is closely linked to its therapeutic efficacy against IPF. We investigated the distribution of TA lipid nanocrystals in the lungs, plasma, and other major organs of mice following inhalation. The results demonstrated that lipid nanocrystals significantly enhance pulmonary retention of TA. Concentration-time profiles and pharmacokinetic parameters in BALF and lung tissues revealed that DPPE-NC@TA exhibited superior lung retention. In contrast, plasma concentration-time curves showed that DPPC-NC@TA rapidly entered the systemic circulation, resulting in reduced pulmonary retention. Combined with the analysis of interactions between TA lipid nanocrystals and mucin, the underlying mechanism may be attributed to the strong interaction between DPPE-NC@TA and mucin in respiratory mucus, which promotes mucus adhesion and the formation of a drug reservoir within the mucus layer, enabling sustained drug release and thereby contributing to the high pulmonary exposure of TA in DPPE-NC@TA group. A BLM-induced IPF mouse model was established to investigate the effects of TA lipid nanocrystals. Mice in the model group and the blank vehicle group showed significant weight loss and impaired lung function. Following treatment with TA lipid nanocrystals, mice exhibited marked improvement in overall health, with only a slight reduction in lung function, approaching levels observed in the control group. Histopathological examination of lung tissue revealed marked pathological alterations in the model group, including alveolar collapse and extensive deposition of collagen fibers. These abnormalities were significantly ameliorated in mice treated with TA lipid nanocrystals. In addition, TA lipid nanocrystals reduced the levels of TGF-β1, HYP, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, indicating therapeutic effects through promotion of collagen fiber degradation and suppression of inflammation. However, the IPF mice in each group that received TA lipid nanocrystal treatment showed similar improvement effects in terms of lung function, degree of pulmonary fibrosis and inflammatory level. DPPE-NC@TA did not show superior therapeutic effects than the other TA lipid nanocrystals. This indicates that both the rapid penetration of mucus and the retention in the mucus layer for a certain period of time can effectively exert the therapeutic effect of TA against IPF. Although P407-NC@TA demonstrates favorable therapeutic efficacy in the treatment of IPF as well, its insufficient stability renders it more susceptible to aggregation and sedimentation during storage compared to TA lipid nanocrystals. Since NC@TA was prepared immediately prior to each administration, no significant differences in therapeutic efficacy were observed as a result of reduced stability.

To deeply explore the molecular mechanism of anti-pulmonary fibrosis, we conducted transcriptome analysis. It is revealed that the anti-inflammatory effect of TA against IPF is mediated through modulation of the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway. Moreover, we discovered that the therapeutic effect of TA against IPF is closely related to the coagulation cascade reaction. Current evidence indicates that the coagulation cascade, platelet activation, and local secretion of soluble mediators are closely associated with the progression of early lung tissue injury in IPF [46–50]. Since salvianolic acid B has been reported to regulate the coagulation-fibrinolysis system in the treatment of IPF [24, 51], modulation of this system was hypothesized as a potential mechanism underlying the coagulation cascade-related effects of TA in IPF. It has been demonstrated that TA lipid nanocrystals downregulated the expression levels of FXa, FIIa, FDP, and PAI-1, while upregulating the expression of uPA and tPA, key factors closely associated with the coagulation-fibrinolysis system. Collectively, the anti-IPF mechanisms of TA are closely linked to its anti-inflammatory effects and its regulatory effects on the coagulation-fibrinolysis system.

Although mixed phospholipids including DPPC, DPPG and DPPE have been optimized in this work, the particle size of TA lipid nanocrystals is still relatively large, which is unsatisfactory for inhalation. The structure-activity relationship between the phospholipid type, fatty acid chain length and saturation, and the physicochemical characterization, mucus retention, lung tissue exposure and pharmacodynamic effects of TA lipid nanocrystals is not yet clear. Preliminary transcriptomic analyses indicate that TA may exert its therapeutic effects through regulating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the coagulation cascade. However, the downstream molecular targets and associated regulatory networks involved in IPF remain poorly defined. Future efforts should focus on optimizing TA nanocarriers and elucidating the pharmacological mechanisms underlying therapeutic effects of TA against IPF, to support the clinical translation of TA-based inhalable formulations.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a bottom-up formulation strategy utilizing P407 as an interfacial stabilizer to fabricate TA lipid nanocrystals incorporating mixed phospholipids, including DPPC, DPPG and DPPE, for inhaled therapy of IPF. The resulting DPPG-NC@TA and DPPE-NC@TA exhibited hydrodynamic diameters < 400 nm and zeta potentials in the near-neutral to moderately negative range (-5 to -15 mV), whereas DPPC-NC@TA displayed a similarly small size but a more neutral surface charge. After inhalation, DPPE-NC@TA demonstrated sustained retention in BALF, attributable to strong interactions with mucin. In contrast, DPPC-NC@TA achieved rapid mucus penetration, consistent with its weak mucin affinity. In vivo pharmacodynamic evaluation in a bleomycin-induced IPF mouse model revealed that inhaled TA lipid nanocrystals significantly improved pulmonary function, reduced HYP content in lung tissue, and suppressed levels of TGF-β1 and pro-inflammatory cytokines (including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) in BALF. Histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses further confirmed that TA lipid nanocrystals markedly attenuated collagen deposition and significantly downregulated expression of α-SMA and collagen-Ⅰ in lung parenchyma, indicating inhibited myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix accumulation, with no statistically significant differences observed among the three phospholipid-based formulations. The transcriptomics study revealed that TA exerted its therapeutic effect on IPF by regulating the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway and the coagulation-fibrinolysis system. Collectively, this work establishes a rationally designed, mixed-phospholipid-based lipid nanocrystal platform for pulmonary delivery of TA, offering a clinically viable, lung-targeted strategy that enhances drug retention, bioavailability, and therapeutic efficacy in IPF.
