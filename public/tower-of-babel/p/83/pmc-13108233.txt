The Relevance of Crystal Clear and Unclear Days to Evaluate Residual Interictal Burden in Patients With Migraine Treated With CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies
Authors: Marcello Silvestro, Ilaria Orologio, Raffaele Ornello, Francesca Trojsi, Alessandro Tessitore, Simona Sacco, Antonio Russo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundAlthough treatment goals in migraine prevention have moved beyond the benchmark of a 50% reduction in monthly attacks, residual disease burden is still evaluated based on residual headache frequency. However, migraine‐related symptoms can persist despite headache freedom, leading to so‐called unclear days that may meaningfully contribute to interictal burden.MethodsIn this prospective, real‐world study, patients with chronic or high‐frequency episodic migraine treated with CGRP‐monoclonal antibodies (CGRP‐mAbs) were followed for six months. Interictal burden was assessed using the Migraine Interictal Burden Scale (MIBS‐4) alongside the monthly unclear and crystal clear days. Patients achieving optimal (< 4 monthly attacks) or modest (4–6 monthly attacks) migraine control were stratified according to the presence of “residual interictal burden” (MIBS‐4 ≥ 3).ResultsTwo hundred patients were included. CGRP‐mAbs treatment was associated with a reduction in MIBS‐4 scores and an increase in crystal clear days (p < 0.001). Despite optimal or modest migraine control, 45.1% of patients at month 3 and 27.2% at month 6 continued to exhibit a substantial “residual interictal burden”. Patients with “residual interictal burden” showed significantly more unclear days and fewer crystal clear days compared with those without residual interictal migraine burden, while no differences were observed in residual headache days, concomitant preventive treatments, or cephalalgiophobia.DiscussionEven when migraine attacks are adequately controlled, residual interictal burden may remain and seems to be predominantly associated with unclear days rather than with residual attacks. Assessing crystal clear and unclear days provides a complementary, patient‐centered perspective on treatment response and interictal recovery in migraine.Keywords: CGRP monoclonal antibodies, crystal clear days, interictal burden, MIBS‐4, unclear days

Introduction

Migraine is a neurological disorder characterized by attacks lasting up to 72 h, typically presenting with moderate to severe headache accompanied by neurovegetative symptoms, sensory hypersensitivity, and intolerance to physical exertion [1, 2]. These features, either alone or in combination with a broad spectrum of additional ictal manifestations (e.g., vestibular or cognitive symptoms), as well as prodromic and postdromic symptoms, substantially contribute to the disability and functional impairment experienced during migraine attacks [3, 4, 5]. However, migraine‐related disability extends beyond the ictal phase, affecting everyday functioning and overall disease impact through what is commonly referred to as interictal burden [6, 7]. Despite its clinical relevance, the determinants of interictal burden remain insufficiently characterized. Psychological and cognitive factors related to attack recurrence (such as anticipatory anxiety, cephalalgophobia, and migraine‐related stigma) are likely contributors [8]. Nonetheless, converging evidence suggests that migraine does not fully remit between attacks, persisting as a “background” cranial discomfort as well as a range of non‐headache symptoms (e.g., nausea, photophobia, phonophobia, fatigue, and impaired concentration) [9]. Therefore, patients may experience headache‐free days that are nonetheless burdened by residual migraine‐related symptoms (“unclear days”), rather than days of complete symptom resolution (“crystal clear days”) [10, 11].

In recent years, there has been increasing recognition that preventive treatment goals should extend beyond the conventional benchmark of a 50% reduction in headache days to encompass residual disease burden [12, 13]. However, clinical outcomes are still predominantly evaluated based on headache occurrence, implicitly assuming that headache‐free days represent true recovery, an assumption that may overlook persistent interictal disability. While the efficacy of calcitonin gene–related peptide monoclonal antibodies (CGRP‐mAbs) in reducing monthly migraine and headache days and attack‐related burden is well established, their effects on interictal burden remain only partially explored [14, 15].

In the present study, we aimed to assess the impact of CGRP‐mAbs on interictal burden by evaluating their ability to reduce the number of unclear days and increase the number of crystal clear days. Furthermore, to clarify the clinical relevance of these constructs, we examined whether patients achieving optimal (< 4 residual migraine attacks per month) or modest (4–6 residual migraine attacks per month) migraine control, according to the International Headache Society (IHS) position paper, may nonetheless continue to experience substantial interictal disability and whether the latter could be primarily driven by a persistently high number of unclear days or low number of crystal clear days.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This is an observational, prospective, non‐randomized, single centre, open‐label real world study focusing on the effectiveness of CGRP‐mAbs on the interictal migraine burden beyond headache attacks frequency. We analyzed clinical data from consecutive patients with chronic migraine (according to the third IHS Classification of Headache Disorders) [16] or with (high frequency) episodic migraine (≥ 8 and ≤ 14 migraine days/month for more than 3 months) [17] followed up for at least 6 months in the Headache Center of the Department of Neurology at the University of Campania “Luigi Vanvitelli” between February 2024 and August 2025. All patients were aged between 18 and 75 years and had failed at least three oral preventive medications classes (propranolol or metoprolol, topiramate or valproate, amitriptyline) or onabotulinumtoxin‐A due to lack of efficacy, intolerable side effects, or contraindications. Lack of efficacy was defined as no meaningful improvement (< 50% of reduction in headache days/month) in the frequency of migraine and headaches (the latter in patients with chronic migraine) after at least 3 months of treatments as recommended by the European Headache Federation guidelines [18]. Tolerability failure was defined as documented discontinuation due to adverse events at any time of the treatment. All patients received subcutaneous (s.c.) injection of monthly galcanezumab 120 mg (with the first loading dose of 240 mg), monthly fremanezumab 225 mg or erenumab 140 mg (every 28 days). Other standard of care preventive oral medications (alone or in combination) if with a stable dose for at least 3 months before starting CGRP‐mAbs preventive treatment (as per clinical practice) were allowed. The migraine and headache frequency (defined as the monthly mean of migraine and headaches) as well as the migraine and headache intensity and response to painkiller were evaluated by paper standardized headache diaries at baseline (i.e., the 3 months before starting the treatment with CGRP‐mAbs) and during the treatment (i.e., the third and the sixth months of treatment with CGRP‐mAbs). Specifically, all patients filled‐in a headache diary consisting of a table with the days of the month (up to 31 days) on the abscissa and the hours of the day (up to 24 h) on the frames. Patients were required to classify each day of the month as one of the following categories: migraine day, headache day, unclear day, or crystal clear day. A “migraine day” was defined as any day with a headache exhibiting migraine characteristics or responding to triptans' administration. A “headache day” was defined as any day on which the patient reported a headache, including those with tension‐type features. Finally, patients were instructed to identify “crystal clear days” and “unclear days”. An “unclear day” was defined as a headache‐free day characterized by residual migraine‐related symptoms while a “crystal clear day” was defined as a day without both headache and migraine‐related symptoms. More specifically, “crystal clear days” were defined as days of complete well‐being with respect to the migraine condition, regardless of the presence of non–migraine‐related symptoms.

The patient should mark the onset and the end of every headache episode and the time of pain‐killer intake to allow the calculation of both the attacks duration and the response to the symptomatic drugs, and also in order to evaluate the painkiller intake/month. Moreover, the patient inserted the intensity of each attack (according to the 10‐point NRS scale).

AI‐based language tool (ChatGPT, OpenAI) was used to support English language revision (grammar, syntax, and phrasing). No AI tool was used to generate, analyze, or interpret study data. All changes were reviewed by the authors, who take full responsibility for the final manuscript.

The protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethical Committee of the University of Campania “Luigi Vanvitelli”. The study conforms with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki published on the website of the Journal of American Medical Association. Each patient gave a free, written informed consent for participation in the study and the analysis and publication of the protocol data.

Collected Variables

At baseline (T0) data aimed at assessing demographics such as (i) gender (ii) age and (iii) BMI as well as clinical information of migraine history such as (a) disease history (years), (b) psychiatric comorbidities (e.g., anxiety and depression) (assessed by Structured Clinical Interview for DSM‐V Axis I Disorders) and (c) previous treatment failures among topiramate, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, candesartan or lisinopril, beta‐blockers, flunarizine, and onabotulinum Toxin‐A (BoNT‐A). Furthermore, at baseline (T0) and at the end of the third (T1) and the sixth month (T2) of CGRP‐mAbs preventive treatment, the following parameters were collected (i) headache frequency, considering both monthly migraine days (MMDs) and monthly headache days (MHDs), (ii) unclear days frequency (iii) crystal clear days frequency, (iv) headache pain intensity (assessed by numerical rating scale [NRS]), (v) monthly days with acute medication intake (MAMI), (vi) concomitant migraine preventive treatment among topiramate, amitriptyline, venlafaxine, candesartan or lisinopril, beta‐blockers, flunarizine, BoNT‐A, (vii) interictal burden by Migraine Interictal Burden Scale (MIBS‐4) [19]. Finally, by means of a semi‐structured interview we explored the presence of cephalalgiophobia (yes/no). During the 6 months of CGRP‐mAbs preventive treatment, all treatment emergent adverse events (TEAEs) were recorded and used as safety outcome.

In a secondary analytic step, patients who achieved an optimal (< 4 attacks per month) or modest (4–6 attacks per month) migraine control (according with IHS position paper), were divided based on the MIBS‐4 in patients with high “residual interictal burden” (≥ 3 points on MIBS‐4) and patients with low/absent “residual interictal burden” (> 3 points on MIBS‐4) in order to compare the parameters of disease severity (see Figure 1).

Flow chart illustrating patient inclusion and longitudinal stratification. A total of 200 patients completed the 3‐month (T1) and 6‐month (T2) follow‐up and were included in the final analysis. At each time point, patients with modest or optimal migraine control were further stratified according to residual interictal burden (MIBS‐4 > 3).

The dataset was complete, and no missing data were observed during data collection or analysis.

Statistical Analysis

All demographic and clinical data were checked for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). The assumed effect size was conservatively set at dᶻ = 0.35, derived from previous randomized evidence evaluating changes in interictal burden following treatment with CGRP monoclonal antibodies. In particular, a phase III randomized controlled study assessing galcanezumab efficacy showed a mean reduction in MIBS‐4 of approximately 1.9 points at Month 3, corresponding to a medium within‐subject effect size; however, considering the higher variability expected in real‐world settings, a more conservative estimate was adopted. To detect a difference between two dependent means with an alpha error of 0.05 (two‐sided) and a statistical power of 0.95, a minimum sample size of 107 evaluable patients was required. Continuous variables conforming to normal distribution are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) while continuous data not conforming to normal distribution and categorical variables are expressed as median ± interquartile range; rate values are reported as subject counts and percentage. We used a one‐way t‐test to compare continuous variables conforming to normal distribution, Pearson's chi‐square test to compare categorical variables, while the non‐parametric Kruskal‐Wallis test was used for data not conforming to normal distribution. Hypothesis testing was 2‐tailed and results have been considered statistically significant if p < 0.05. Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons was applied. All analyses were performed using STATA version 16 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

Discussion

In the present study, we reported a significant decrease of unclear days and an increase of crystal clear days along with the decrease of interictal burden evaluated by MIBS‐4 in patients with migraine treated with CGRP‐mAbs. Furthermore, we demonstrated that a substantial proportion of patients, despite achieving optimal or modest migraine control according to the IHS position paper, still experience a limited number of crystal clear days, resulting in a high residual interictal burden, as opposed to patients with a higher number of crystal clear days who exhibit a low residual interictal burden.

In recent years, it has been increasingly suggested that migraine prevention should move beyond the traditional goal of at least 50% reduction in headache attack frequency, embracing a broader concept of residual migraine burden [12, 20]. Nevertheless, within this evolving framework, the assessment of treatment success still largely relies on the residual number of migraine or headache days, implicitly assuming that days without headache closely correspond to a state of recovery and restored well‐being. However, interictal burden in migraine is common, multidimensional, and poorly explained by attack frequency alone [21, 22]. Consistent with this notion, in the present study, a significant proportion of patients achieving ≤ 6 monthly headache days—thus meeting criteria for modest or optimal migraine control according to the IHS position paper—continue to report high MIBS‐4 scores (≥ 3 score), reflecting a persistent “residual interictal burden” at the end of the third and/or the sixth month of CGRP‐mAbs treatment.

Previous observation has shown that a critical interictal burden persists in patients starting CGRP‐mAbs treatment, as reflected by moderate‐to‐severe MIBS‐4 scores in the majority of treated patients with migraine [14]. However, the determinants of this interictal burden are still unclear, the study not being specifically designed to assess residual burden among clinical responders.

Traditionally, interictal burden has been mainly attributed to psychological and social factors, such as anticipatory anxiety regarding future attacks and the social, familial, and self‐directed stigma often associated with migraine [21, 22, 23, 24]. Notably, in the present study, the prevalence of cephalalgiaphobia, defined as anticipatory anxiety related to migraine attacks, did not differ—among patients with optimal or moderate migraine control—between patients without and with residual migraine interictal burden.

Being the residual migraine interictal burden not correlated to the presence of cephalagiophobia, the alternative and increasingly supported perspective suggesting that the interictal phase should not be regarded as a truly asymptomatic state deserve to be taken in consideration [9, 25]. Indeed, it is noteworthy that interictal phase is frequently characterized by a range of headache‐free migraine‐related symptoms, allowing the distinction between days marked by residual migraine symptomatology despite the absence of headache (“unclear days”) and days of complete well‐being (“crystal clear days”) [10, 11]. Consequently, it can be argued that a reduction in headache frequency, not translating into a parallel increase in crystal clear days, may fail to yield a meaningful improvement in interictal burden, in turns supporting that residual interictal disability, as captured by the MIBS‐4, is not primarily driven by residual attacks. In line with this hypothesis, in our cohort of patients we found that “residual migraine burden” was strongly associated with a higher number of unclear days and a lower number of crystal clear days compared with patients exhibiting low interictal burden. This observation supports the notion that unclear days may represent a key driver of “interictal” burden since they affect quality of life, emotional well‐being, work and relationships contributing to the overall impact, personal and socio‐economic costs characterizing also the interictal period in patients with migraine. Interestingly, no differences were observed between the two groups (e.g., patients with migraine with and without “residual interictal migraine burden”) in the proportion of patients receiving concomitant standard of care preventive treatments, disavowing the hypothesis that additional treatments targeting central nervous system pathways are needed to improve residual interictal non‐painful symptoms characterizing “unclear days”, potentially reflecting non‐peripheral mechanisms origin [26].

Taken together, these findings highlight the importance of systematically assessing residual interictal burden in patients with migraine, since such dimension of disease burden may persist despite apparently satisfactory control of migraine in terms of headache frequency and substantially impairing the quality of life of these patients.

The present study is not exempt from limitations. First of all, the concepts of unclear days and crystal clear days, while clinically intuitive and grounded in existing literature on interictal symptoms [10], are not based on formally validated patient‐reported outcome measures and rely on patient self‐report. In addition, the sample size, although adequate for the primary longitudinal analyses, did not allow reliable stratification according to individual CGRP monoclonal antibodies or according to migraine diagnosis (chronic versus episodic), precluding drug‐specific or phenotype‐specific conclusions. Despite these limitations, the large real‐world sample and the longitudinal design provide a robust characterization of interictal outcomes beyond headache frequency.

Our results suggest that focusing exclusively on attack‐related outcomes risks overlooking a clinically meaningful component of ongoing disability. In this context, the consideration of crystal clear days as well as unclear days emerges as a valuable complement to traditional migraine metrics, offering a more nuanced and patient‐centered measure of a true interictal recovery. Incorporating crystal clear days and unclear days investigation into the routine clinical assessment may therefore help identify patients with unmet needs and guide more comprehensive and effective migraine management strategies.
