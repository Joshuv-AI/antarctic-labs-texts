Hydrophobins RolA and HypB from Aspergillus oryzae function on the surface of conidia and aerial hyphae, respectively
Authors: Yuki Terauchi, Kenya Tsuji, Hirotaka Kobayashi, Moriyuki Kawauchi, Chihiro Tanaka, Yoichi Honda, Akira Yoshimi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrophobins are low-molecular-weight amphiphilic proteins found in filamentous fungi. In this study, we focused on two Aspergillus oryzae hydrophobins, whose biological functions were unclear. We found that hydrophobins RolA and HypB are highly expressed. We created strains with disruption of one of the respective genes, ΔrolA and ΔhypB, and examined their phenotypes under various stress conditions. The two strains had different phenotypes under stress conditions that affected the cell wall surface function. Self-assembled structures on the conidial surface were absent in the ΔrolA strain, whereas specific structures on the surface of aerial hyphae were absent in the ΔhypB strain. Colony hydrophobicity was lower in the ΔhypB strain than in the control CNT strain, whereas conidial surface hydrophobicity was lower in the ΔrolA strain than in the control CNT strain. These findings indicate that RolA and HypB share similar functions on the cell surface, but RolA functions mainly on the conidial surface, whereas HypB functions mainly on the surface of aerial hyphae.Keywords: conidial surface, hydrophobicity, hyphal surface, rodlet

1. Introduction

Hydrophobins are small secreted amphiphilic proteins widely conserved in filamentous fungi, including ascomycetes and basidiomycetes. Hydrophobins self-assemble and form an amphiphilic membrane (de Vocht et al., 1998; Hakanpaa et al., 2004). Their primary structure is characterized by the abundance of hydrophobic amino acid residues and eight conserved cysteine residues (Schuren & Wessels, 1990; Wösten & de Vocht, 2000). These cysteines form four intramolecular disulfide bonds, and hydrophobic amino acid residues between the cysteines cause self-assembly of hydrophobins at air-liquid or solid-liquid interfaces (Valsecchi et al., 2019; Wösten & de Vocht, 2000). Hydrophobins are grouped into different classes based on the number of amino acid residues between cysteines, hydropathy plots, the solubility of self-assembled structures, and the shape of the membranes formed by these structures (Kershaw & Talbot, 1998; Kwan et al., 2006; Quarantin et al., 2019; Valsecchi et al., 2019; Wessels et al., 1991; Wösten, 2001; Wösten et al., 1994). Class I hydrophobins are highly hydrophobic and form extremely robust amyloid-like self-assembled structures called rodlets (Ball et al., 2020; Morris et al., 2012; Wösten & de Vocht, 2000). Class II hydrophobins form aggregates distinct from rodlets and somewhat less robust (Wösten & de Vocht, 2000). An intermediate class, named class III, has the characteristics of both classes I and II (Jensen et al., 2010).

Class I hydrophobins confer hydrophobicity to mycelia, thereby promoting the development of aerial hyphae (Bell-Pedersen et al., 1992; Grunbacher et al., 2014; Wessels et al., 1991; Wösten et al., 1993; Zhang et al., 2011). Rodlet structures cover conidia, facilitating their aerial dispersal (Cai et al., 2020; Stringer et al., 1991). In several filamentous fungi, including model fungi Aspergillus nidulans, Neurospora crassa, and plant pathogen Pyricularia oryzae, rodlets have been observed on conidial surfaces (Lauter et al., 1992; Stringer et al., 1991; Talbot et al., 1996). When hyphae are in a liquid environment, class I hydrophobins disperse into the liquid and concentrate at the air-liquid interface, reducing surface tension and aiding in hyphal penetration through the interface (Ball et al., 2020; Wösten et al., 1993, 1994). Class I hydrophobins also have species-specific functions. In P. oryzae, MPG1 is involved in the formation of appressoria (Talbot et al., 1993). In Aspergillus fumigatus, hydrophobins that cover conidia shield them from recognition by the host immune system (Aimanianda et al., 2009; Paris et al., 2003). In the edible oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ostreatus), hydrophobins are required for efficient decomposition of lignin (Han et al., 2023b) and also protect mycelia from environmental stresses (Han et al., 2023a, 2025). Aspergillus species, including A. nidulans and A. fumigatus, have class I and III hydrophobins but not class II (Tanaka et al., 2022), except A. terreus, which has a class II hydrophobin (Jensen et al., 2010). In vegetative hyphae of Aspergillus spp., the expression levels of class III hydrophobins are lower than those of class I hydrophobins (Tanaka et al., 2022), and class I may be used primarily during colonization.

Aspergillus oryzae is a filamentous fungus found in soil and air and is used in the traditional Japanese fermentation industry to produce sake (rice wine), shoyu (soy sauce), and miso (soybean paste). When A. oryzae is cultured with polybutylene succinate-co-adipate (PBSA) as a carbon source, its class I hydrophobin RolA promotes PBSA degradation by forming ionic interactions with the PBSA-degrading esterase CutL1 (Takahashi et al., 2005, 2015; Tanaka et al., 2017; Terauchi et al., 2017). RolA forms rodlets in vitro (Terauchi et al., 2020). In addition to RolA, Kamiya et al. (2012) identified HypB and HypC, and reported that RolA, but not HypB or HypC, contributes to the conidia formation. However, the role of RolA in biological processes has not been investigated by using the gene disruption approach.

Here, to elucidate the biological function(s) of hydrophobins in A. oryzae, we first obtained the amino acid sequences of the RolA, HypB, HypD, and HypE hydrophobins from the NCBI and CaoGDX databases. On the basis of their hydropathy plots and expression analysis of their genes, we speculated that RolA and HypB, which belong to class I, function as the primary hydrophobins during vegetative growth and conidiation in this fungus. We generated strains with the disrupted rolA or hypB gene and analyzed their phenotypes (mycelial growth, conidiation, stress tolerance, and surface hydrophobicity of colonies and conidia). We also examined the surface structures of hyphae and conidia by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). On the basis of these analyses, we discuss the biological functions of RolA and HypB in A. oryzae.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Strains and growth media

Strains used in this study are listed in Table 1. An auxotrophic adeA mutant of A. oryzae (ΔadeA) (Zhang et al., 2017) was used as the host strain for all genetic manipulations. The A. oryzae CNT strain (Yoshimi et al., 2018) was used as a control for gene expression and phenotypic analyses. CDE medium (Mizutani et al., 2008), in which sodium nitrate in Czapek-Dox medium (Miyazawa et al., 2016) is replaced with 70 mM sodium hydrogen L(+)-glutamate monohydrate as the nitrogen source, or potato dextrose (PD) medium (Shimadzu Diagnostics, Japan) were used as standard culture media. CDE medium containing 2% agar was used to collect conidia and to assess growth on plates. ME liquid culture (9% malt extract, 0.5% yeast extract) and ME agar were used for gene expression analysis.

2.2. Hydropathy plot analysis

The amino acid sequences of A. oryzae RolA (XP_001824881.1), HypB (XP_001727202.1), and HypD (BAR73404.1) were obtained from the NCBI database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/), and that of HypE (RIB40_02005915) from the CaoGDX database (https://nribf21.nrib.go.jp/CAoGDX/index.cgi?prj=01949&lang=en). ProtScale (https://web.expasy.org/protscale/) was used to assign a hydropathy index based on the Kyte-Doolittle scale (Gasteiger et al., 2005; Kyte & Doolittle, 1982).

2.3. Molecular phylogenetic analysis of Aspergillus hydrophobins

All hydrophobin amino acid sequences from other Aspergillus species were obtained from NCBI (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). Sequences were aligned using the MUSCLE algorithm in MEGA v11.0.13 (Tamura et al., 2021). Phylogenetic analysis was performed using the maximum likelihood method in IQ-TREE v3.0.1 (Minh et al., 2020). The selected amino acid substitution model was JTT+G (Jones et al., 1992). Branch support was assessed using the Ultrafast Bootstrap approximation with 1,000 replicates (Hoang et al., 2017). The phylogenetic tree was visualized in FigTree v1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018).

2.4. Expression analysis of hydrophobin genes

For liquid culture, 106 conidia/mL were inoculated in Erlenmeyer flasks, which were rotated at 120 rpm at 30 °C for 1 or 2 d. For solid-state culture, 106 conidia were inoculated on a membrane filter with a pore size of 1.0 µm lying on an ME agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 2 or 3 d. Total RNA was extracted from mycelia in liquid cultures or from mycelial colonies on filters by freezing in liquid N2 and triturating, and was purified using the RNeasy mini kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany). cDNA was synthesized using Superscript II Reverse Transcriptase (Invitrogen-Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, United States). Real-time PCR was performed using Fast SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems-Thermo Fisher Scientific) with primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. Each primer was designed to include an intron in the amplified PCR fragment. The housekeeping gene encoding histone H2B was used as a control. The amplification rate of the primers was 90%-110%, and the comparative method was used for calculation of the expression level of each hydrophobin gene (Yoshida & Tanaka, 2019).

2.5. Construction of strains with disruption of hydrophobin genes

The sequences of all primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Fragments containing the 5′ and 3′ non-coding regions of rolA (amplicons 1 and 2, respectively) and hypB (amplicons 3 and 4, respectively) derived from A. oryzae genomic DNA and the adeA gene (amplicon 5) from the TOPO-2.1-adeA plasmid (Miyazawa et al., 2016) were amplified by PCR. Amplicon 1 was amplified with the primers AOrolA-top-f and AOrolA-ANadeA-top-r2, amplicon 2 with AOrolA-bottom-r and AOrolA-ANadeA-bot-f2, amplicon 3 with AOhypB-pZErO2-if-top-f and AOhypB-top-r, amplicon 4 with AOhypB-bottom-f and AOhypB-pZErO2-if-bottom-r, and amplicon 5 with ANadeA-f and ANadeA-r. The PCR products were gel-purified and used as substrates for the second round of PCR, in which AOrolA-top-f and AOrolA-bottom-r were used to fuse amplicons 1, 2, and 5 for the rolA disruption cassette, and AOhypB-pZErO2-if-top-f and AOhypB-pZErO2-if-bottom-r were used to fuse amplicons 3, 4, and 5 for the hypB disruption cassette (Supplementary Fig. S1A). The resulting PCR products were cloned into the EcoRV-linearized pZErO2 plasmid using an In-Fusion HD Cloning Kit (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Japan). The disruption cassettes were amplified from the plasmid or excised from it and used for transformation of the A. oryzae ΔadeA strain (Mizutani et al., 2008). Transformation was performed as described previously (Fujioka et al., 2007). Correct integration of the disruption cassettes was confirmed by PCR (Supplementary Fig. S1). For both rolA and hypB, three independent disruption strains were established and analyzed.

2.6. Inhibition of growth and conidiation by stress-inducing compounds

Sensitivity of the CNT, ΔrolA, and ΔhypB strains to various stress-inducing compounds was evaluated as described previously (Yoshimi et al., 2013), with a minor modification. Briefly, conidial suspensions of each strain (104 cells) were spotted on the center of a 9-cm plate containing PD or CDE agar medium with one of the compounds (final concentrations: 1.6 M NaCl, 1.6 M KCl, 3.2 M sorbitol, 50 μg/mL Calcofluor White [CFW], 100 μg/mL Congo Red [CR], 0.01% SDS, 20% glycerol, 0.05% H2O2) and incubated at 30 °C. To observe the colonies before they reached the edge of the plate, the incubation period was set to 5 d on PD agar and 6 d on CDE agar. Each experiment was performed in quadruplicate. The above concentrations were chosen on the basis of preliminary experiments, in which the dose-response was determined by comparing the mean diameters of the colonies at different concentrations, and the concentration that produced a significant difference from the control (medium without any stresses) was determined and used in further experiments. The reduction rate of colony growth was defined as the differences in diameter between stressed and unstressed colonies, expressed as a percentage of the diameter of the unstressed colonies.

To assess the inhibitory effect of the compounds on conidiation, A. oryzae strains were grown on PD agar medium until the colony had reached the edge of the plate (6 d) to ensure sufficient conidia were obtained. The conidia were suspended in PBS with 0.1% Tween-20 and counted in a hemocytometer. Conidial density was calculated from the number of conidia and the colony area, and that of the mutant strains was evaluated relative to that of the CNT strain.

2.7. SEM

Observation of conidial surface: Thin ca. 1 cm2 sections of PD agar were cut from an agar plate and sandwiched between sterilized glass slides. An inoculum of spore suspension (104/mL; 1 μL) was applied to the side of PD agar. The samples were then cultured at 30 °C for 6 d. The upper glass slide was removed, and the samples were gently immersed in fixative solution (2.5% glutaraldehyde + 2% paraformaldehyde), dehydrated in acetone, dried by critical point drying, coated with osmium using an osmium plasma coater (Neoc-Pro/P, Meiwafosis), and observed by SEM (Regulus 8220, Hitachi High-Tech). The secondary-electron-image resolution was 0.7 nm at 1.0 kV.

Observation of aerial hyphal surface: A. oryzae was cultured on PD agar for 6 d, the hyphae that had grown were immersed in fixative solution and treated and observed by SEM as above.

2.8. Colony wettability test

The wettability of colonies was tested as described previously (Grunbacher et al., 2014), with a minor modification. Conidial suspension of each strain (104 cells) was spotted on the center of a PD agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 6 d. Solution containing 50 mM EDTA and 0.2% SDS (10 μL) was dropped at 8 points at equal intervals between the center and the edge of the colony, incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and the droplets were observed and photographed.

2.9. Microbial adhesion to hydrocarbons (MATH) assay

Conidial hydrophobicity was assessed using the MATH assay as described previously (Smith et al., 1998), with a minor modification. Briefly, a conidial suspension of each strain (104 cells) was spotted on the center of an PD agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 6 d. The conidia were collected in phosphate-urea-magnesium buffer (1 L: 22.2 g K2HPO4, 7.26 g KH2PO4, 1.8 g urea, 0.2 g MgSO4·7H2O, final pH 7.1) (Holder et al., 2007), and density of the conidial suspension was adjusted to an absorbance at 470 nm (A470) of 0.4 (Genesys 10UV, Central Scientific Trading Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Hexadecane (300 µL) was added to 3 mL of the suspension, which was then vortexed and left standing for 15 min at room temperature. 3. Results

3.1. Sequence alignments and hydropathy plots of A. oryzae hydrophobins

We compared four A. oryzae hydrophobins with A. nidulans RodA, a class I hydrophobin (Valsecchi et al., 2019; Wösten & de Vocht, 2000) used as a reference. An alignment of the deduced amino acid sequences is shown in Fig. 1A; the eight cysteine residues characteristic of fungal hydrophobins were conserved in all four sequences. The hydropathy pattern of A. nidulans RodA revealed three distinct hydrophobic regions located between cysteine residues: Cys3-4 (residues 67-105), Cys4-5 (107-124), and Cys7-8 (133-149) (Fig. 1B). The hydropathy patterns of RolA and HypB, but not HypD or HypE, were similar to those of RodA (Fig. 1B), suggesting that RolA and HypB are class I hydrophobins. The molecular phylogenetic tree of Aspergillus hydrophobin proteins is shown in Supplementary Fig. S2. It revealed that HypE is an ortholog of the class III hydrophobin XP_001216429.1 (Jensen et al., 2010) from Aspergillus terreus, suggesting that HypE belongs to class III. Although HypD is phylogenetically grouped with A. nidulans DewA (class I), it has a unique long hydrophilic C-terminal extension (Fig. 1) that is absent in typical class I members.

Fig. 1. Hydrophobins of Aspergillus oryzae. A: Alignment of hydrophobins RolA, HypB, HypD, and HypE of A. oryzae and RodA of A. nidulans. The eight cysteine residues that form the conserved disulfide bonds are shown in dashed boxes. Hydrophobic regions A. nidulans RodA are shaded in gray. B: Hydropathy plots of hydrophobins. Solid lines, the indicated hydrophobins of A. oryzae; dashed line, RodA of A. nidulans as a reference; shaded areas, the hydrophobic regions of A. nidulans RodA.

3.2. Expression analysis of hydrophobin genes in liquid and solid cultures

We observed that conidia had started to form by day 2 of solid cultures (data not shown). Expression levels of A. oryzae hydrophobin genes in 2-d cultures are shown in Fig. 2. Both rolA and hypB were expressed in both liquid and solid cultures, but the expression levels of both genes were markedly higher in solid cultures; this is consistent with the fact that A. oryzae normally forms conidia and aerial hyphae on solid cultures. The expression of the hypD and hypE genes was not detected under any conditions, suggesting that, at least under the conditions tested, hypD and hypE were not involved in hyphal hydrophobicity.

Fig. 2. Expression analyses of hydrophobin genes in Aspergillus oryzae. Mycelia were grown in ME (liquid) or ME agar (solid) medium for 2 d, and RNA was extracted and used for qRT-PCR. Error bars, standard deviation (n ≥ 3).

3.3. Growth and conidiation of the rolA and hypB disruptants in A. oryzae

To investigate the function of RolA and HypB in A. oryzae, we generated the disruption strains of rolA (ΔrolA) and hypB (ΔhypB) (Supplementary Fig. S1). At day 5 on PD agar medium, colony size of these disruptants appeared to be similar, but all ΔrolA colonies were slightly darker (Fig. 3A). To compare the conidiation ability of these disruptants, we calculated their conidial density relative to that of CNT. Conidial density did not differ significantly between all disruptant strains and CNT (Fig. 3B).

Fig. 3. Phenotypes of hydrophobin disruption strains of Aspergillus oryzae. A: Colonies of the CNT, three ΔrolA strains and three ΔhypB strains on PD agar. The photographs were taken at day 5. B: Conidial density. Conidia were collected from 6-d-old colonies on PD agar and counted (n = 4). Significant difference from CNT in Dunnett's test is shown (*P < 0.05).

3.4. Sensitivity test with stress compounds

The sensitivity of the disruptants to various extracellular compounds that cause environmental stress is shown in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Figs. S3 and S4. In comparison with the colony diameter of CNT on CDE medium at day 6, that of ΔrolA and ΔhypB was slightly, but significantly suppressed (Supplementary Fig. S4A). In comparison with the growth reduction rate of CNT, that of the mutants was significantly greater in the presence of KCl (Supplementary Fig. S4C) and significantly smaller in the presence of NaCl (Supplementary Fig. S4B) or CR (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Interestingly, the growth of the ΔrolA but not ΔhypB strains was completely inhibited under H2O2 stress (Supplementary Fig. S4G). In the presence of SDS, the growth reduction rate was significantly and consistently lower in the ΔhypB strains than in CNT (Supplementary Fig. S4F). In the presence of CFW, glycerol, or sorbitol, no consistent significant differences in the reduction rate were found; any differences appeared to depend on individual strain rather than to reflect the genetic backgrounds (Supplementary Figs. S4D, H, I).

Fig. 4. Mycelial growth of each strain under stress conditions. Colonies on CDE agar were photographed at day 6. CFW, Calcofluor White; CR, Congo Red.

3.5. Observation of hyphal and conidial surfaces by SEM: hydrophobicity of colonies and conidia

To evaluate the effects of hydrophobins on the cell surface in A. oryzae, we observed the hyphal and conidial surfaces by SEM (Fig. 5). Some form of decoration was present on the hyphal surface in all strains, but fewer structures were observed in the ΔhypB strain, resulting in a smoother surface (Fig. 5, upper panels). This suggests that HypB influences the structure of the hyphal surface.

Fig. 5. Observations of each strain by scanning electron microscopy.

Certain class I hydrophobins form rodlet structures on the conidial surface (Lauter et al., 1992; Stringer et al., 1991; Talbot et al., 1996), and such structures were present on the conidial surface of CNT and ΔhypB but not ΔrolA (Fig. 5, lower panels), suggesting that RolA affects the structure of the conidial surface.

As RolA affected the surface structure of conidia and HypB affected that of hyphae, we evaluated whether these hydrophobins affect colony hydrophobicity. Both CNT and ΔrolA repelled water in droplets placed between the center and the edge of the colony. In ΔrolA, slight variation in the degree of water repellency was observed among individuals, but overall the disruptants showed almost no difference compared with CNT (Fig. 6A). In contrast, in ΔhypB the droplets were barely visible after 1h and colony hydrophobicity gradually decreased from the center toward the edge (Fig. 6A). In MATH assay, the ΔhypB strain showed nearly the same values as the CNT strain, whereas the ΔrolA strain exhibited a significant decrease compared to the CNT strain (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that HypB contributes only marginally, if at all, to conidial hydrophobicity in A. oryzae, and that RolA plays a more dominant role in this property. The observations that HypB deficiency altered hyphal surface structure and that the ΔhypB colonies became wettable suggest that HypB primarily contributes to hyphal rather than conidial hydrophobicity in A. oryzae.

Fig. 6. Hydrophobicity tests. A: Colony hydrophobicity. Conidial suspension of each strain (104 cells) was inoculated on the center of a PD agar plate and incubated at 30 °C for 6 d. Solution containing 0.2% SDS and 50 mM EDTA (10 μL) was dropped onto the colony surface as shown, incubated at room temperature for 1 h, and the droplets were photographed. B: MATH assay of conidial hydrophobicity. Measurements were repeated four times for each strain (n = 4). Significant differences from CNT in Dunnett's test are shown (**P < 0.01, *** P < 0.005).

4. Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the hydropathy plots and expression levels of four hydrophobins in A. oryzae and found that RolA and HypB belong to class I (Fig. 1). The functions and characteristics of A. oryzae hydrophobins are thought to be closely related to those of hydrophobins from A. nidulans and A. fumigatus (Tanaka et al., 2023). Hydrophobins of the latter two species have been well studied (Grunbacher et al., 2014; Stringer et al., 1991; Stringer & Timberlake, 1995; Valsecchi et al., 2017). Aspergillus nidulans has six hydrophobins: RodA (AnRodA) and DewA-DewE (AnDewA-AnDewE) (Grunbacher et al., 2014; Stringer et al., 1991; Stringer & Timberlake, 1995) (Supplementary Fig. S1). AnRodA, AnDewA, and AnDewB belong to class I, whereas AnDewC, AnDewD, and AnDewE are considered intermediate (Grunbacher et al., 2014) or class III (Tanaka et al., 2022; Supplementary Fig. S2). AnRodA is expressed mainly on the conidial surface, where it forms rodlets and makes the conidia and the colony hydrophobic (Girardin et al., 1999; Grunbacher et al., 2014; Stringer et al., 1991). Aspergillus fumigatus has seven hydrophobins, RodA-RodG (AfRodA-AfRodG) (Valsecchi et al., 2017; Supplementary Fig. S2). AfRodA is an ortholog of AnRodA and has similar biological functions (Girardin et al., 1999; Paris et al., 2003; Valsecchi et al., 2017). RolA of A. oryzae is an ortholog of AnRodA and AfRodA, and its function may be similar; it is expressed during conidiation (Fig. 2), confers hydrophobicity to conidia (Fig. 6B), and participates in rodlet formation on the conidial surface (Fig. 5). Our data indicate that the contribution of RolA-type hydrophobins to colony hydrophobicity is limited or negligible, and that they play a highly conserved role among Aspergillus species, particularly in conferring hydrophobicity to conidia.

Among other class I hydrophobins in A. nidulans, AnDewA is present on the conidial surface and appears to confer hydrophobicity to the colony to a greater extent than AnRodA does, whereas AnDewB has a weaker effect on colony hydrophobicity (Grunbacher et al., 2014). In A. fumigatus, AfRodB is highly expressed during biofilm formation but not during biofilm growth (Valsecchi et al., 2017), and its function remains unclear (Gibbons et al., 2012; Valsecchi et al., 2017). In A. oryzae, HypB is an ortholog of AnDewB and AfRodB, but its ability to confer hydrophobicity to the colony (Fig. 6A) is more similar to that of AnDewA than to that of AnDewB. Furthermore, on the 2 days of cultivation on ME agar medium, which corresponds to the stage of hyphal growth and early conidiation, the expression level of hypB was higher than that of rolA. Therefore, unlike the function of RolA-type hydrophobins, that of HypB orthologs appears to have diverged to some extent across Aspergillus species.

Interestingly, the ΔrolA and ΔhypB strains responded similarly to most stressors, except H2O2 (Fig. 4; Supplementary Figs. S3, S4). The ΔrolA strain lacked the rodlet layer on the conidial surface (Fig. 5), and the absence of this protective barrier may have increased cell wall permeability to H2O2, leading to the loss of conidial viability and failure of germination. In contrast, the disruption of all hydrophobins in A. fumigatus does not alter its sensitivity to oxidative stress induced by H2O2 (Valsecchi et al., 2017). Although RolA is an ortholog of AfRodA, their effects on the structural integrity of the cell surface membrane and cell wall permeability may differ. In A. oryzae lacking RolA or HypB, sensitivity to KCl increased significantly, that to CR and NaCl markedly decreased, and that to glycerol and sorbitol was less pronounced than that to KCl, NaCl, and CR (Supplementary Figs. S3, S4). The observed phenotype appears to be a response to high K+ concentrations rather than osmotic stress. This suggests that the rolA or hypB disruption might alter cell wall permeability to specific ions; it is also possible that cell growth is affected more by high concentrations of K+ than Na+. In A. fumigatus, CR resistance is markedly higher in the ΔAfrodA mutant than in the ΔAfrodB-G mutant and the wild type, which do not differ significantly from each other (Valsecchi et al., 2017). Although disruption of class I hydrophobins does not lead to an observable phenotype (Grunbacher et al., 2014; Valsecchi et al., 2017), our results show that RolA and HypB play major roles in the cell wall surface function in A. oryzae.

HypD and HypE were not expressed at all during vegetative growth and conidiation (Fig. 2). Class III hydrophobins are defined by their intermediate characteristics between classes I and II (Jensen et al., 2010; Tanaka et al., 2022). Phylogenetic analysis revealed that HypE belongs to class III, whereas HypD is structurally similar to class I members but possesses a unique hydrophilic C-terminal extension that is absent in typical class I hydrophobins. This hydrophilic domain is thought to play a supportive role at water-deprived environment in A. oryzae (Nakajima et al., unpublished but described in the database entry of NCBI). This structural divergence suggests that HypD is an atypical hydrophobin, potentially belonging to class III. The function of this class remains largely unknown. For example, in A. fumigatus, the genes for AfRodC and AfRodG are less transcribed than those for AfRodA and AfRodB, and their biological roles are unknown (Valsecchi et al., 2017). In A. nidulans, AnDewB-AnDewE might affect colony wettability, but their contribution is very minor in comparison with that of AnRodA and AnDewA (Stringer & Timberlake, 1995). In the plant pathogen Verticillium dahliae, the class II hydrophobin gene VDH1 is important for microsclerotial formation and is expressed in solid cultures rather than in liquid cultures (Klimes & Dobinson, 2006). Constitutive expression of VDH1 delays disease symptom development (Klimes et al., 2008). Fungal species in different ecological niches may have hydrophobins that function only in limited situations depending on the niche.

In conclusion, we found that both RolA and HypB of A. oryzae are localized to the cell surface, but RolA functions on the conidial surface, whereas HypB functions on the surface of aerial hyphae. RolA is essential for the formation of rodlets, whereas HypB is necessary for the formation of specific structures on the respective surfaces. Both hydrophobin genes are expressed in both liquid and solid cultures, suggesting that these hydrophobins function in both environments, although their localization, function, and expression timing likely differ. In general, filamentous fungi possess multiple hydrophobins, and this study has provided insights into some of the variation in their biological roles.
