No advantage for patient-specific UKA in comparison with standard UKA regarding clinical and functional results at short-term follow-up
Authors: Rolf Haaker, Adham Moussa, Damyan Sabev
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeUnicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKA) is a less invasive alternative to total knee arthroplasty (TKA) for patients with isolated unicondylar knee osteoarthritis and obtains good functional and clinical results. Still, the revision and survival rates of UKA are inferior compared to TKA. Therefore, patient-specific implants were introduced to obtain more reliable results. This retrospective study compares standard UKA and patient-specific UKA after short-term follow-up.MethodsOne study group consisted of 50 patients with a standard UKA implant, implanted using navigation technology. The other group consisted of 25 patients with a patient-specific implant, implanted using respective patient-specific instruments.Results2 years postoperatively, functional and clinical scores were similar or better in the standard UKA group. The postoperative range of motion and axial alignment were comparable in both groups. The number of complications was low, without any case of aseptic loosening.ConclusionStandard UKA and patient-specific UKA provide good short-term clinical and functional results. No benefit was obtained using patient-specific implants, which would justify the high cost and effort of this technique for “standard” patients with medial osteoarthritis.Trial registration numberNCT03201172 (clinicaltrials.gov).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00590-025-04467-2.

Introduction

Unicondylar knee arthroplasty (UKA) was first described in the 1950s and is a well-accepted treatment for patients with isolated medial osteoarthritis. UKA may provide benefits for the patients in comparison with total knee arthroplasty (TKA). The procedure is less invasive than TKA, including a shorter hospital stay, lower intraoperative complication rate, lower mortality and faster recovery [1]. Clinical and functional outcome parameters for UKA have shown superior or similar results compared to TKA [2–6]. In UKA procedures, less bone is resected than in TKA, leaving more bone substance for potential revision surgeries. In contrast to these advantages, revision rates for UKA are mostly inferior to those of TKA. This is most obvious in the reports of national joint registries [7–10], providing a high number of cases for sound statistical analysis.

Different reasons for this higher revision rate of UKA in comparison with TKA are discussed, including poor patient selection, younger and more active patients, the experience of the surgeon, a demanding surgical technique regarding implant positioning, progressing osteoarthritis, and the supposedly relative ease of revision [11–15].

Patient-specific instrumentation and implants were developed to improve implantation accuracy and, thus, may lead to better results following UKA. Patient-specific instrumentation uses three-dimensional imaging technologies to manufacture patient-specific cutting guides for the resection of the femur and tibia and is combined with off-the shelf implants. Patient-specific implants are also manufactured based on imaging scans and theoretically provide optimally fitting implants [16]. The accuracy and bone coverage of UKA implanted with patient-specific instrumentation or patient-specific implants is very high [17, 18]. Still, superiority of these applications in contrast to standard UKA on a functional level or regarding complications and survival is not proven [19, 20] and postoperative results of patient-specific implants in UKA are published rarely [21]. Additionally, these applications are very cost-intensive and time-consuming and not yet standard of care. Therefore, the value and benefit for the patient of this technique has yet to be proven.

The aim of this study was to compare clinical outcomes of patients receiving standard off-the-shelf UKA and patient-specific UKA. Our hypothesis was that patients of both groups reveal similar functional and radiological results after a follow-up of 2 years.

Material and methods

Patients

This study was performed as a retrospective observational monocenter comparative follow-up study based on two previously treated patient groups.

The standard UKA group consists of a consecutive series of 50 patients treated between December 2015 and May 2017 with the medial unicondylar knee prosthesis Univation XF (B. Braun Aesculap, Tuttlingen, Germany). Follow-up of these patients was performed prospectively as part of the study after written informed consent.

The patient-specific UKA group consists of 25 patients treated between April 2011 and January 2016 with the patient-specific unicondylar implant iUni (ConforMIS, Burlington, USA). Follow-up of these patients was performed in clinical routine. Written informed consent was obtained retrospectively for inclusion of these retrospective follow-up data into the study.

Patient inclusion flowchart

Inclusion criteria for this study were implantation with the respective implants approximately 2 years before the examination and written informed consent of the patients. Indication for implantation of Univation XF or iUni were similar, including medial osteoarthritis and Ahlbaeck’s disease. Patients below the age of 18 years and pregnant women were excluded from the study.

Surgical technique

In the standard UKA group, all implantations were performed using a navigation system (OrthoPilot, B. Braun Aesculap, Tuttlingen, Germany) with the option to choose either the navigation of the tibial component only or the femoral and tibial component with definition of the resulting leg axis as described previously [22]. In this group plain long leg X-rays, lateral view X-rays and tangential patellar X-rays were performed preoperatively. All patients in the current study received a fixed bearing medial implant. The implant is made from cobalt–chromium and is also available in a coated AS version, which comprises a 7-layer coating suitable for patients with suspected or confirmed metal hypersensitivity. Two patients in the current study received an AS coated implant due to known hypersensitivity against the implant material.

Each patient in the patient-specific UKA group underwent a CT-scan to plan the individual, custom-made implant as described by Beckmann et al. [23]. 6 weeks later, the custom-made implant and respective instruments were received. The single use instruments included cutting blocks for the femoral and tibial cuts, which had to be fixed in a special position in the front of proximal tibia and further on to the surface of the medial femur. There were also three polyethylene inlays in different heights from 6 to 8 mm included in the package. The iUni implant comprises a fixed bearing meniscus component. All these unicondylar knee arthroplasties could be performed according to the preoperative planning.

Preoperatively, at the day of the surgery, all patients received two doses of Cefazolin for infection prevention. Surgeries were performed under general anesthesia, unless patients insisted on spinal anesthesia, which was then performed exceptionally. The surgery was performed using a motorized leg holder (Maquet, Rastatt, Germany). After minimal invasive incision through a midvastus section, the surgery was performed as intended by the manufacturer of the implant with mechanical alignment. Regarding the surgical technique it can be noted that the femur component of the iUni implant does not require distal or posterior cutting but only cartilage removal, which is in contrast to the Univation XF implant, for which a distal and posterior femur cut has to be performed. Postoperative routine included early mobilization, continuous passive motion and physiotherapy and was comparable in both groups.

Measurements

Primary endpoint of the study was the knee joint function of the operated knee 2 years after surgery assessed by the Knee Society Score (KSS). Secondary outcome measures were Knee Injury Osteoarthritis and Outcome Score (KOOS), complications, and radiological results regarding the mechanical axis, tibial slope and radiolucent lines.

For radiological analysis, plain long leg X-ray, lateral view X-ray and tangential patellar X-rays were acquired from all patients preoperatively, directly postoperatively and at the last follow-up. Radiological implant position control was performed by a radiological institute (Raylytic, Graz, Austria). Based on the X-Rays, the mechanical hip knee ankle angle (HKA), the lateral femoral mechanical angle (LFMA) and the medial tibial mechanical angle (MTMA) were calculated. HKA is defined as the medial angle between the mechanical femoral axis (center of the femoral head—knee center) and the mechanical tibial axis (knee center—ankle center). LFMA is defined as the lateral angle between the mechanical femoral axis and the line connecting the joint surface of the femoral condyles. MTMA is defined as the medial angle between the tibial plateau and the mechanical tibial axis. Furthermore, femoral and tibial radiolucencies were evaluated qualitatively.

Statistical methods

Sample size calculation was not performed in this study. Rather, all available, eligible and willing patients were included in this study. At the time of study planning the number of eligible and willing patients was not known. Therefore, a priori power analysis was not feasible.

Continuous variables like knee scores, etc., were compared by conducting a t test. In case of unequal variances (tested with F-test) between treatment groups a correction of degrees of freedom was applied using the method of Satterthwaite. Statistical analysis was performed after closure of the database by statistical software SAS 9.4.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained by the ethics committee of the Universitätsmedizin Göttingen (Number: 34/7/17). The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03201172) before inclusion of the first patients. A written informed consent was obtained from all patients of both groups prior to inclusion into the study.

In total 75 patients could be followed-up in the study, 50 in the standard UKA group and 25 in the patient-specific UKA group. Primary diagnosis was medial gonarthrosis in all patients.

Radiological results

Radiolucent lines

AP and lateral X-rays were screened for signs of radiographic loosening of the implant components. In the standard UKA group, for one patient, the respective X-rays were not available and in one case, tibia X-rays were not assessable. Therefore, 48 tibia and 49 femur components of the standard UKA group were analyzed, as well as all 25 tibia and femur components of the patient-specific UKA group.

Radiolucent lines at the femoral component were observed in 4 of 49 cases (8.2%) for the standard UKA group, and in 1 of 25 cases (4.0%) of the patient-specific UKA group. At the tibial component, radiolucent lines were observed in 10 of 48 standard UKA (20.8%) and none of 25 patient-specific UKA (0.0%) patients (p = 0.0108).

In total, no signs of radiographic loosening (tibia or femur component) were observed in 36 of 48 patients of the standard UKA group (75.0%), and 24 of 25 patients in the patient-specific UKA group (96.0%).

Complications

In total, 6 complications were documented in the course of the study. Complications occurred in two patients in the standard UKA group (4.0%) and in three patients in the patient-specific UKA group (12%).

Two patients in the standard UKA group (one patient had osteoporosis), had a postoperative tibia head fracture and were treated with plate osteosynthesis. One of those subsequently experienced migration and aseptic loosening of the tibial component, which required a revision surgery with a TKA.

One patient in the patient-specific UKA group underwent postoperative arthroscopy due to a hemarthrosis. Another patient had to be mobilized under anesthesia due to inadequate postoperative flexion. A third patient had a postoperative wound healing disorder, requiring a reoperation of the wound. All three complications were resolved without consequences for the patients.

Discussion

In this study standard UKA and patient-specific UKA revealed comparable functional and radiological results. Significantly better results were obtained for the total KSS and KSS function as well as the KOOS subscores for pain and symptoms in the standard UKA group. The difference of KOOS pain is not only statistically significantly higher in the standard UKA group but also exceeded the minimal clinically important difference [24]. The other subscores of the KSS and KOOS as well as the range of motion were comparable in both groups. In general, KSS and KOOS scores in this study are in the range of other studies on UKA after short-term follow-up [2, 4, 6]. The mean operative time was reduced more than 20 min in the standard UKA group compared to the patient-specific UKA group. The number of complications was low in both groups, mostly being general complications, which may occur after UKA surgeries [2, 7, 9, 10]. Although the rate of patients with a deviation of more than 3° from the mechanical axis of the HKA angle (180°) was more than 20% higher in the standard UKA group preoperatively, this rate was comparable directly postoperatively and 2 years after surgery in both groups. Of note, in the patient-specific UKA group 2 years after surgery the rate of patients with more than 3° deviation from the mechanical HKA angle was as high as preoperatively, whereas in the standard UKA group this rate was still more than 24% lower 2 years postoperatively compared with the preoperative rate. Recurrence of (mostly varus) deformity may be attributed to muscular and ligament-related aspects, resorption of bone at the cement–bone interface and to polyethylene wear and was reported after UKA previously [25].

Data from arthroplasty registries and other studies indicate that progression of osteoarthritis and aseptic loosening are the main causes for revision of UKA [7, 9, 10, 12, 26]. Notably, in the current study, no cases of aseptic loosening occurred with either implant at a mean follow-up of about 2 years. This is in contrast to previously reported high rates of 17.7% to 23% of aseptic loosening in patients with both implants used in this study [27–29]. Regarding aseptic loosening, a correct and careful cementation technique, the tibial slope and the correct positioning of the tibial component are critical factors from our experience. A horizontal or even negative slope expectably induces high compressive loads on the posterior aspect of the tibial component, which may cause a tilting mechanism, resulting in aseptic loosening of this component. Further, any asymmetric load on the tibial component induces edge loading of the femoral component on the condyle, which may also result in early aseptic loosening and should therefore be avoided.

Clinical data on patient-specific medial UKA are published rarely and mostly short-term results (up to 3 years) are available. These studies showed significant improvements of patient-reported outcome measures comparing pre- and postoperative state [16, 30, 31] and satisfactory alignment [30, 32, 33]. In a study comparing standard UKA (Oxford, Biomet Zimmer) and patient-specific UKA, the latter was associated with significantly decreased surgical time and length of hospital stay [32]. As previously discussed, Talmo et al. published a case series of iUni with a high early failure rate [28], whereas Weber et al. present promising long-term survival rates of the same implant [34]. In a systematic review Demey et al. conclude, that there is not enough data to prove superiority of patient-specific UKA in comparison with standard UKA. Besides effects on the patients, these results also provide important input on the discussion regarding the cost-effectiveness of different types of implants and approaches. Patient-specific UKA is very time- and cost-intensive, and therefore should provide superior results for justification of their use in “standard” patients. This could be shown neither in the previously published scientific literature nor in the current study. Still, patients with specific demands due to abnormal anatomy or the like, could profit from patient-specific UKA.

This study has several limitations, which should be considered when interpreting the results. The first limitation concerns the heterogenous study design. Follow-up data of the patient-specific UKA group was collected retrospectively, whereas for the standard UKA group follow-up data collection was performed prospectively. Still, data acquisition and analysis were performed in the same way for both groups. Second, the number of patients included in this study is limited, especially in the patient-specific UKA group. Furthermore, sample size calculation and a priori power analysis could not be performed. The number of patients in UKA studies is typically rather low, which is owed to the generally lower number of UKA patients in comparison with TKA patients. Still, the available data allow statistical analysis and is based on a sound basis. Third, surgeries in both groups were not performed in the same time period but consecutively, i.e., patients in the patient-specific group were treated between April 2011 and January 2016, whereas patients in the standard group were treated between December 2015 and May 2017. Still, surgeries in patients included in this study were performed after the learning curve of the respective system and surgical approach. Fourth, the follow-up of the patients is limited to 2 years.

In conclusion, satisfactory results were observed with both, standard and patient-specific unicondylar implant designs after a 2-year follow-up.
