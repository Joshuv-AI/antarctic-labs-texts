Three-dimensional numerical simulation and experimental validation of airborne ground-penetrating radar based on CNCS-FDTD method under undulating terrain conditions
Authors: Yuelong Chi, Lifeng Mao, Xuben Wang, Su Pang, Yi Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD) approaches face challenges with increased computational demands and errors as terrain complexity and flight altitude rising. This study introduces the Crank-Nicolson-Cycle-Sweep-FDTD (CNCS-FDTD) method, enhancing airborne ground penetrating radar (GPR) simulations over undulating terrains. CNCS-FDTD, as an unconditionally stable implicit algorithm, overcomes these by allowing larger time steps without the constraints of the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition. Our research aims to assess how CNCS-FDTD can improve computational efficiency and accuracy in modeling airborne GPR responses across varied terrains. Initial simulations using a three-dimensional graben model indicate that CNCS-FDTD can maintain calculation stability with significantly larger time steps, reducing computational time by 42%. To further verify the reliability of the numerical simulation results, the paper also presents experimental tests of the undulating terrain model. By comparing the numerical and physical simulation results of models under different flight altitudes and terrain conditions, the accuracy of the simulation results is validated.Subject terms: Solid Earth sciences, Geophysics

Introduction

Airborne Ground Penetrating Radar (GPR) has emerged as an efficacious and non-intrusive tool for subsurface exploration, demonstrating significant utility in geographically challenging environments such as mountainous terrains, polar regions, and minefields, where human accessibility is severely limited1,2. Early applications, as documented by Cook3 among others, leveraged the low conductivity loss characteristics of ice to facilitate glacier detection via airborne GPR. Nonetheless, the operational efficacy of airborne GPR datasets is often circumscribed by the maneuverability constraints of aircraft, which are subject to minimum flight speeds, require extensive areas for turning, and are dependent on runways for takeoff and landing. Consequently, the deployment of fixed-wing aircraft for GPR surveys has predominantly been confined to expansive and relatively flat terrains, such as those found in Greenland or the Antarctic continent4–7.

The advent of small Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs) equipped with GPR systems has significantly broadened the applicability of airborne GPR methodologies8,9. Recent scholarly endeavors have focused on the development and empirical assessment of UAV-based airborne GPR systems. For instance, Church et al.10, employed GPR imaging to evaluate variations in basal terrains and ice thickness, thereby shedding light on the formation dynamics of pre-glacial lakes. Santin et al.11, utilized GPR data spanning different temporal intervals to analyze glacier surface and bed characteristics, elucidating patterns of change and inferring glacial dynamics. Gianluca et al.12, introduced an airborne GPR system with a central frequency of 165 MHz, conducting flight tests over the Moroccan desert to provide preliminary system validation. Grathwohl et al.13, exploited the efficiency of UAV-mounted GPR for time-critical avalanche victim rescue operations. Ruols et al.14, and García-Fernández et al.15, further extended the application of UAV-mounted GPR systems to glacier detection and the investigation of array-based configurations, respectively, acquiring three-dimensional datasets and conducting physical experiments in real-world scenarios.

Forward modeling of airborne GPR simulates the diffusion and propagation of radar waves, facilitating a nuanced understanding of wave propagation laws, target response characteristics, and data interpretation. Fu et al.16, and Feng et al.17, further contributed to this body of knowledge by simulating airborne GPR profiles of subterranean cavities and conducting forward modeling on undulating terrain electrical models, respectively, thereby enhancing our understanding of the influence of various factors on GPR detection efficacy.

Despite the complex challenges posed by surface environmental conditions, the deployment of a robust three-dimensional forward modeling algorithm to simulate terrain effects remains imperative. However, the literature on three-dimensional numerical modeling in this context is scant. This paper endeavors to bridge this gap by evaluating the detection effectiveness of airborne GPR through the lens of three-dimensional numerical modeling and experimental validation.

The Finite Difference Time Domain (FDTD) method, a cornerstone in the domain of three-dimensional forward modeling for GPR, has seen significant scholarly engagement18–20. Traditional FDTD methods, governed by the Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition21, often resort to a regular stair-step approach for simulating undulating terrain, necessitating fine grid partitioning and consequently, an expanded computational domain. This, coupled with the aerial placement of the GPR field source, escalates computational resource consumption, thereby diminishing computational efficiency22. In recent years, various improvements to the FDTD method have been proposed to enhance its stability and efficiency. Crank and Nicolson23 proposed the Crank-Nicolson FDTD (CN-FDTD) algorithm, which is unconditionally stable and can overcome the limitations of the CFL condition. To overcome the limitations of the CFL condition, Zheng et al.24 and Namiki et al.25 proposed the use of the ADI-FDTD method to solve Maxwell’s equations. This method is unconditionally stable and can be considered an approximate solution of the CN-FDTD method. Several scholars have proposed improved ADI-FDTD methods to enhance the accuracy and stability of solving Maxwell’s equations. These improvements primarily focus on reducing splitting errors and optimizing the numerical stability of the algorithm, thereby significantly improving the accuracy of the computational results while maintaining computational efficiency26,27. Compared to the ADI-FDTD method, the CN unconditionally stable FDTD method offers higher numerical accuracy and lower numerical velocity anisotropy28–30. However, CN-FDTD requires solving large sparse matrices in each iteration, resulting in high computational costs, making it challenging to apply to the three-dimensional Maxwell’s Eqs30–33, and making it difficult to implement in three-dimensional airborne GPR simulations.

To address this issue, scholars have proposed various fast approximation algorithms for CN-FDTD. For instance, Sun et al.30, introduced the CNDG-FDTD method, which can be subdivided into two sub-steps, each requiring the solution of a large tridiagonal matrix for one field component, which is inefficient for practical problems. Sun et al.31, proposed the CNAFS-FDTD algorithm, which, in solving the time-domain three-dimensional Maxwell’s equations, only necessitates the successive solution of tridiagonal matrices, eliminating the need to solve large sparse matrices and thereby saving computational time and memory compared to the direct implementation of CN-FDTD. Sun et al.32,33, developed the CNCS-FDTD algorithm, which integrates CN-FDTD, iterative solving methods (Cycle Sweep), and uniform grid numerical techniques to solve partial differential equations accurately. This method decomposes the generalized CN matrix into two simple matrices in two sub-steps, significantly improving the solving speed while maintaining accuracy. Feng et al.34, utilized the CNCS-FDTD algorithm to solve three-dimensional low-frequency underground electromagnetic sensing problems, maintaining accuracy with a central frequency of 25 Hz and a time step that could be 1000 times larger than that of conventional FDTD, validated through three-dimensional numerical simulations of airborne transient electromagnetics (ATEM). Sun et al.35 applied the CNCS-FDTD technique to transient electromagnetic 3D forward modeling. The forward modeling results showed good agreement with analytical solutions and linear digital filter solutions.

This paper introduces the CNCS-FDTD method into the realm of three-dimensional forward modeling for airborne GPR, establishing a three-dimensional graben structure model to compare single-point responses at varying time steps with those obtained from conventional FDTD methods. By ensuring both stability and efficiency, appropriate time steps are selected for experimental numerical simulations of typical terrain models. Concurrently, physical models that align with the numerical simulations are constructed, taking into account experimental conditions such as terrain and instrumentation specifics. The radar response data at different flight altitudes were collected to verify the accuracy of the numerical simulation results. Additionally, it explores the response characteristics of targets influenced by terrain changes, analyzing the travel time and amplitude variations of radar waves as they reach the targets, and assessing the impact of changes in flying height under different scenarios. This comprehensive approach not only enhances the understanding of airborne GPR dynamics in complex environments but also contributes to the refinement of methodologies for more accurate subsurface exploration using advanced three-dimensional numerical modeling techniques.

CNCS-FDTD method

Based on the CN algorithm, the FDTD method still utilizes the Yee grid discretization method21. In this approach, the electric field components are defined on the edges of the rectangular grid cells, while the magnetic field components are defined at the center of the cell faces. Experiment

The experimental section begins by numerically simulating the response of a three-dimensional graben model using the CNCS-FDTD algorithm. The results obtained with different time steps are compared with those obtained using conventional FDTD methods to verify the accuracy of the proposed method. The stability of the results obtained with different time steps is analyzed, and the largest time step that ensures stability in the simulation results is selected to achieve optimal computational efficiency.

Subsequently, a three-dimensional model featuring a water-containing slab-shaped inclined target body within a two-layer background medium is constructed. Numerical simulations and experimental validations are performed under three distinct surface morphologies: convex terrain, flat terrain, and concave terrain. The focus is on analyzing the superposition and divergence of radar wave energy due to terrain changes, variations in propagation paths, as well as the response characteristics, travel time, and amplitude variations of the reflected waves from the target body. On this basis, a three-dimensional model of sloped terrain was constructed, featuring a 5 cm thick horizontal aquifer within the overlying strata. The radar wave response characteristics were further analyzed under two flight modes: horizontal flight and fixed altitude relative to the ground surface. Finally, by varying the flight altitude (the distance from the antenna to the ground, hereafter the same), the impact of flight altitude on the radar wave response characteristics was examined.

Verification of correctness and selection of CFLN parameters

Employing the CNCS-FDTD algorithm, numerical simulations of the airborne ground penetrating radar response of a three-dimensional graben model are conducted. The results are compared with those obtained using traditional FDTD algorithms, implemented by gprMax, to validate the accuracy of the proposed method. The geological model of the graben is depicted in Fig. 2., consisting of a two-layer medium with a subsurface depression structure measuring 4.2 m × 4.2 m × 1.2 m. The bedrock is buried at a depth of 2.4 m, and the relative permittivity of the overburden layer and bedrock is 7 and 4, respectively, with conductivity values of 1 mS/m and 2 mS/m, respectively. The transmitting and receiving antennas are positioned in the air at a z-coordinate of − 2 m, with a separation distance of 0.6 m, and excited with an x-directional current signal at a main frequency of 67.6 MHz.

Numerical and physical simulation experiments of three typical terrain models

The impact of terrain on the reception signal of airborne GPR is investigated through the construction of a three-dimensional model of a water-bearing sloping target body within a two-layer background medium. Numerical simulations and experimental validations are conducted for convex, flat, and concave terrains to understand the influence of terrain on radar signal propagation.

Due to experimental hardware constraints, we selected the following parameters for numerical simulation and experimental validation: Antenna center frequency: 1200 MHz; Flight altitude: 60 cm.

The flat terrain model has dimensions of 3 m in length and 2 m in width, with an overlying rock layer thickness of 0.8 m and a downward extension of 0.4 m for the underlying bedrock. The target body is a inclined plate-like structure, with dimensions of 0.15 m in thickness, 0.4 m in width, and 0.47 m in length, inclined at an angle of approximately 30 degrees. Positioned within the overlying strata, the target body’s upper-left corner is located 0.3 m below the surface, while its lower-right corner is 0.6 m below the surface (Fig. 6b). To analyze the impact of terrain undulations on subsurface structure detection, a convex and concave hemispherical terrain feature, 20 cm in height and 60 cm in width, is added to the center of the model surface (Fig. 6a,c). The model comprises four components: air, surrounding rock, bedrock, and target body. The relative permittivity and conductivity of each part: the relative dielectric constant of the surrounding rock is 4, with a conductivity of 2 mS/m; the bedrock has a relative dielectric constant of 9 and a conductivity of 5 mS/m; and the target body has a relative dielectric constant of 16 and a conductivity of 10 mS/m. The excitation is centered at a frequency of 1200 MHz, with a transmitted pulse of a Ricker wavelet. The transmit-receive distance is 8 cm, and the line connecting the transmit and receive antennas is perpendicular to the survey line. The time window is set to 32 ns, with a flight altitude of 60 cm. The grid spacing is , resulting in a total of 1500 × 1000 × 1050 grid cells.

Three typical terrain models for ground-penetrating radar. (a) Convex terrain; (b) Flat terrain; (c) Concave terrain.

Physical models corresponding to the numerical simulations are constructed in a controlled environment, with the experimental setup designed to replicate the numerical model’s conditions as closely as possible. An air-coupled antenna with a center frequency of 1200 MHz was utilized. The antenna was mounted on a DJI M300 unmanned aerial vehicle, with a maximum payload capacity of 2.7 kg, and the stability of this drone series has been validated in field experiments14,42,43.

Figure 7 presents the three-dimensional numerical simulation results for three representative terrain models. The simulations qualitatively reveal the stratigraphic distribution and illustrate the impact of concave and convex terrains on target identification. Specifically, convex terrains cause energy convergence, which overlaps with the reflected waves from the target, thereby forming the primary anomaly associated with convex terrain targets. In contrast, flat and concave terrains do not exhibit radar wave energy characteristics induced by the terrain.

At 5 ns, the phase axes of the reflected waves generated by convex and concave terrains both exhibit downward-opening curves resembling one branch of a hyperbola. Specifically, the reflected waves caused by convex terrains precede those of flat terrains, while those caused by concave terrains follow them. Additionally, as the measurement points move away from the midpoint of the undulating terrain, the energy of the reflected waves from convex and concave terrains gradually diminishes. For the flat terrain model (as depicted in Figs. 8b and 9b), between 5 ns and 15 ns, the phase axes of the reflected waves from each vertex of the target form downward-opening hyperbolic curves. Energy accumulation occurs on the right branches of the hyperbolas, allowing for a preliminary determination of the position and shape of the target based on the vertex positions of the hyperbolas. The radar reflection waves at the interface below the target exhibit a discontinuous state, while in other regions, they exhibit a horizontal linear distribution. Overall, the results of numerical simulations and physical experiments demonstrate good consistency. The phase axes of the terrain, underground target, and bedrock surface are clearly presented, further validating the reliability of numerical simulations and physical experiments.

Radar records of targets at the same location (x = 1.5 m) in various terrains were extracted and compared (refer to Fig. 10) to analyze the amplitude and arrival time differences. Convex terrains result in energy accumulation, leading to an increase in the amplitude of the reflected waves. Conversely, concave terrains cause energy dispersion, resulting in a decrease in amplitude, while flat terrains exhibit no such effects, with amplitudes falling between the former two cases. The radar waves in concave terrains reach the target earliest, followed by flat terrains, with convex terrains exhibiting the latest arrivals.

Slope model experiment

In regions with complex terrain, horizontal flight paths can result in excessively long propagation distances for radar waves in the air, adversely affecting the detection capabilities of airborne ground-penetrating radar. Therefore, in practical applications, flight paths that maintain a consistent altitude relative to the ground are typically employed to ensure the accuracy of detection results. The simulation experiments were conducted on a slope model, where the flight transect maintained a constant height above the ground.

A three-dimensional model of a sloped terrain was constructed (see Fig. 10). The model measures 3 m in length and 2 m in width, featuring a 5 cm thick aquifer within the overlying rock strata. The distances from the surface to the top of the water-bearing layer are 0.6 m on one side and 0.2 m on the other, with the bottom of the water-bearing layer being 0.4 m above the bedrock surface. The transect runs along the x-direction. The parameters for the excitation source and numerical simulation are consistent with previous setups, and the physical experiment model parameters match those used in the numerical simulations. The water-bearing layer was simulated by transporting moist soil and laying it horizontally. The vertical distance between the flight transect and the ground surface was fixed at 60 cm.

Both numerical and physical simulation results clearly displayed the reflection wave co-phase axes at the top and bottom surfaces of the water-bearing clay layer (see Fig. 11). As the flight progressed along the slope, the overlying strata became thinner, reducing the travel time of radar waves to reach the interface between the water-bearing clay layer and the bedrock, thereby gradually elevating the reflection wave co-phase axes at this interface. Due to secondary reflections from the water layer, reflection wave co-phase axes similar in appearance to those at the bedrock interface were observed beneath the water layer, increasing the difficulty in distinguishing the bedrock interface. Theoretical calculations of radar wave propagation times and comparisons with profile results can be used to differentiate between reflection waves from the bedrock interface and secondary reflections from the water layer.

Impact of different antenna heights on detection results

Radar reflection profiles are often influenced by both antenna height and terrain. To discuss the impact of antenna height, the experiment utilized the convex terrain model (Fig. 6a), concave terrain model (Fig. 6c), and sloped terrain model (Fig. 12), all simulated under horizontal flight conditions.

Comparison of simulation results of different flight heights for convex and concave terrain models

From Figs. 13, 14, 15 and 16, it can be observed that the curvature of the hyperbolas formed by the in-phase axis of the reflected waves caused by convex and concave terrains gradually decreases with increasing flight altitude. The energy of the reflected waves diminishes with increasing horizontal distance from the center of the convex or concave terrain, and as the flight altitude increases, the rate of energy attenuation slows down, extending the range of influence. Due to the energy superposition caused by the convex terrain, a continuous curve is observed on the right side of the target body, and the range of influence of the superimposed energy increases with flight altitude. The energy of the reflected waves from the target body decreases with increasing flight altitude, resulting in smaller amplitudes and blurred in-phase axes, which reduces the resolution of deeper strata. Additionally, the distance between the target body and the air in the concave terrain model is smaller than in the convex terrain model, leading to a slower rate of energy attenuation with increasing flight altitude. The physical simulation results are consistent with the numerical simulations, indicating the effectiveness of the proposed numerical simulation method.

Comparison of simulation results at different flight altitudes for the sloped terrain model

By comparing the numerical (Fig. 18) and physical (Fig. 19) simulation results of the sloped terrain model at flight altitudes of 30 cm, 60 cm, 90 cm, and 120 cm from the starting point of the flight line, it was observed that the two sets of simulation results were largely consistent. As the antenna height above the ground varied from the start to the end of the flight line, the aquifer exhibited distortions in the time profile. With the increase of flight height, the change of the reflected wave curve of the aquifer is gradually smoothed. Additionally, four diffraction wave anomalies were observed on the surface in the physical simulation profile, which gradually disappeared as the distance between the surface and the antenna increased. This indicates that with increasing flight altitude, the radar signal is more significantly affected by the volumetric effect of anomalies, enhancing the suppression capability of local anomalies and resulting in smoother in-phase axes of the strata reflection waves. During physical simulation, the energy of the reflected waves from the aquifer decreased with increasing flight altitude, and the in-phase axis became blurred, reducing the resolution of deeper strata.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates the application of the CNCS-FDTD method for 3D forward modeling of airborne GPR, highlighting its unconditional stability and efficient iterative solution capabilities. The method enables the use of larger time steps, significantly reducing computation time while maintaining accuracy. The influence of terrain on airborne GPR responses is substantial, underscoring the necessity of incorporating terrain effects in detection analyses. The CNCS-FDTD method’s ability to simulate these effects accurately provides a valuable tool for enhancing the understanding and interpretation of airborne GPR data in complex environments. Compared to horizontal flight, the fixed altitude flight mode relative to the ground surface can avoid interpretation difficulties caused by the deformation of the in-phase axis of surface reflected waves, facilitating the identification of the interface between the aquifer and the bedrock. As the flight altitude increases, the radar transmitting antenna’s coverage area on the ground surface expands, thereby enhancing the volumetric effect of anomalies on the radar signal. This improves the suppression capability of local anomalies, resulting in smoother reflected waves from the surface morphology and anomalies, effectively highlighting the regional background structural information.
