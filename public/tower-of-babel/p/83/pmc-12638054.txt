Bacterial Dysbiosis Observed in the Terminal Ileum of Ulcerative Colitis
Authors: Jordan S Mar, Allen Nguyen, Alexis Scherl, May Wittke, Mary Doona, Christopher A Lamb, Mary E Keir
Published: 2025

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) describes a collection of conditions characterized by chronic gastrointestinal (GI) inflammation. Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) represent the two predominant manifestations of IBD. Though CD and UC share many features, a primary difference between these two forms of IBD is the location of GI inflammation. CD typically manifests as patches of inflammation occurring throughout the GI tract.1 Conversely, UC-associated inflammation is restricted to the colon with the upper GI tract, including the terminal ileum (TI), going unaffected except in cases of “backwash” terminal ileitis.1,2 While the etiology of IBD is unclear, a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility, microbial dysbiosis, immune dysregulation, and additional environmental factors is hypothesized to drive IBD onset.3 Microbial dysbiosis, a shift in the GI microbiota composition compared with healthy subjects, has been repeatedly described in both CD and UC.3 Notably, studies characterizing IBD-associated dysbiosis routinely sample either stool or mucosal biopsies taken near the site of disease (ie, the colon in UC) for microbiome characterization. Given that TI inflammation can be subclinical in UC despite the presence of immune dysregulation in the colon, we sought to determine whether microbial dysbiosis is found in the TI of adults with UC.

Methods

Prior to scheduled clinically indicated endoscopy visits, adults with UC, adults with CD, and healthy volunteers were identified for potential study enrollment. After written informed consent in accordance with research and ethics committee approval (Newcastle and North Tyneside 1 REC 10/H0906/41), 20 healthy volunteers, 40 adults with CD, and 15 adults with UC were enrolled and biopsies were collected from endoscopi­cally uninflamed regions of both the TI and colon of each participant for histology and microbiota profiling. All healthy volunteers were either symptomatic or colonic polyp surveillance patients who were found to have normal endoscopic appearances at endoscopy. Disease activity (Simple Endoscopic Score for CD or Mayo Clinic Score [MCS] for UC) was recorded for adults with CD and adults with UC but was not considered for participation in the study (Table 1). Biopsies collected for microbiota profiling were preserved in RNAlater (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Biopsies collected for histology were fixed in formalin prior to paraffin embedding. Baseline demographics (including age, sex, disease severity, and concomitant medications) are summarized in Table 1. Histological analysis was performed on TI biopsies collected from 12 of the 13 adults with UC and confirmed a lack of inflammation in all cases, consistent with endoscopic observations at the site of biopsy collection. Bacterial microbiota profiling was performed on all biopsies. Briefly, DNA was extracted from biopsies using the QIAGEN AllPrep DNA/RNA 96 Kit (Cat. No. #80311) and subjected to bacterial 16S-V4 rRNA gene sequencing as previously described.4 QIIMEv2019.7 was used to process 16S-V4 rRNA gene sequence data as previously described.4,5 Taxonomy was assigned based on the V4 region of the Genome Taxonomy Database 16S rRNA gene sequence database (r214.1).6 Resulting abundance tables were rarefied to 8,000 reads per sample. Samples with <8,000 reads were excluded from downstream analysis. Of the 134 total collected biopsies, 63 out of 64 TI (20 of 20 healthy volunteers, 30 of 31 adults with CD, 13 of 13 adults with UC) and 69 out of 70 colonic (20 of 20 healthy volunteers, 38 of 38 adults with CD, 11 of 12 adults with UC) biopsies yielded 16S-V4 rRNA gene sequence data of sufficient quality and sequencing depth. α- and β-diversity measures were calculated using QIIMEv2019.7. All statistical analyses were conducted in the R statistical environment (R version 4.5.0, R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The Wilcoxon rank sum test was performed to compare α-diversity between study groups. β-diversity distance matrices were visualized via nonmetric multidimensional scaling and permutational multivariate analysis of variance was used to determine relationships between volunteer health states and gross bacterial microbiota composition. To identify significantly enriched or depleted bacterial genera, the DESeq2 R package was used as previously described.4 Significant genera were defined as those having a raw P value <.05 and a Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted P value ≤.3.7 Only genera with at least 10 reads detected in at least 20% of samples were considered for differential abundance testing. This study was funded by Genentech Inc.

Results

We first examined ɑ-diversity of mucosa associated bacterial communities in TI and colonic biopsies collected from healthy volunteers, adults with CD, and adults with UC (Table 1). Consistent with previous reports, colonic ɑ-diversity, as measured by Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, was significantly reduced in both forms of IBD compared with healthy volunteers (Figure 1A).3,8 Interestingly, ɑ-diversity in the TI was also reduced in adults with UC compared with healthy volunteers despite a histologically confirmed lack of TI inflammation in these participants (Figure 1B). Furthermore, TI ɑ-diversity was reduced in UC volunteers to a comparable level to that of CD volunteers with confirmed ileal involvement (Figure 1C). While reduced ɑ-diversity was also observed in the TI of CD volunteers with colon restricted disease, this reduction was not statistically significant (Figure 1C). ɑ-Diversity was not significantly associated with disease activity (as measured by MCS for UC and Simple Endoscopic Score for CD for CD), regardless of diagnosis or tissue (CD colon: Spearman’s ρ = 0.01, P = .93; UC colon: Spearman’s ρ = 0.30, P = .37; CD TI: Spearman’s ρ = 0.07, P = .72; UC TI: Spearman’s ρ = −0.28, P = .36). Similar observations were made when comparing bacterial community richness (not shown), indicating that these observations were not an artifact of the chosen ɑ-diversity metric. Consistent with ɑ-diversity observations, analysis of pairwise unweighted UniFrac distances between bacterial community profiles revealed a significant shift in the bacterial microbiota composition of both CD and UC volunteers compared with healthy volunteers in both the colon and TI (Figure 1D, E). Utilizing weighted UniFrac and Jaccard distances also yielded consistent results (not shown), indicating the observed UC-associated shift in TI microbiota composition was not an artifact of the selected distance metric. Combined, these observations indicate microbial dysbiosis may exist in the TI of adults with UC despite a lack of observable disease activity or overt inflammation at that site.

To further characterize this UC-associated shift in TI microbiota composition and identify discrete bacterial genera driving these changes, we performed differential abundance testing utilizing DESeq2. Lachnospira was significantly enriched while Akkermansia, Parasutterella, Fimivicinus, and Alistipes were significantly depleted in the TI of adults with UC compared with healthy volunteers (Figure 1F). Significant Parasutterella and Alistipes depletion was also observed in the TI of adults with CD with ileal involvement but not in those with colon-restricted disease activity (Figure 1G). Additionally, nominally significant Fimivicinus and Akkermansia depletion was observed in the colon of adults with UC compared with healthy volunteers, suggesting that selection against these taxa exists throughout the GI tract of adults with UC (Figure 1H). Conversely, Lachnospira, the only significantly enriched bacterial genus detected in the TI of adults with UC, was not significantly enriched in the colon of adults with UC. None of the genera associated with UC TI dysbiosis were differentially abundant in the colon of adults with CD, regardless of disease location (Figure 1H). Together, this suggests discrete features of UC-­associated TI dysbiosis overlap with both CD-associated TI dysbiosis and UC-associated colonic dysbiosis.

Discussion

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first characterization of microbial dysbiosis in the TI of adult UC patients despite the absence of TI inflammation. Reduced ɑ-diversity coupled with Akkermansia, Parasutterella, Fimivicinus, and Alistipes depletion was observed in the TI mucosa–associated bacterial communities from UC volunteers. Similar observations were previously made in a small cohort of early-onset pediatric UC patients, suggesting that TI dysbiosis in the absence of TI inflammation is a common feature of UC.9 Reduced ɑ-diversity is broadly associated with increased intestinal inflammation and is a consistent feature of colonic dysbiosis in both UC and CD.3,8 Indeed, this was confirmed in our study despite the small sample size. Akkermansia and Alistipes depletion is also a frequently observed feature of colonic dysbiosis in IBD, with both genera hypothesized to be beneficial members of the GI microbiome.8 In support of Akkermansia and Alistipes being beneficial in the context of GI inflammation, supplementation with either genus has independently demonstrated protective effects in murine models of colitis.8,10,11 Combined, this suggests similar selective pressures shaping colonic dysbiosis in UC may exist in the TI and drive development of overlapping features of microbial dysbiosis throughout the GI tract that can contribute to loss of immune homeostasis.

Given the relatively small size of our study, larger studies describing the TI microbiome in UC in relation to host features are necessary to confirm our observations and identify potential causes and implications of UC-associated TI dysbiosis. For example, in our study, features of TI dysbiosis in UC did not associate with available measures of disease severity (MCS). Additionally, we observed enrichment of Lachnospira, a butyrate-producing genus associated with increased fiber intake,12 in the TI of UC patients. While loss of butyrate-producing taxa is typically associated with colonic or fecal UC dysbiosis,13 excess butyrate levels were reported to impair intestinal epithelial barrier function in both in vitro and in vivo models.14,15 Lachnospira expansion has also been described in UC, particularly those with active disease.16–18 Furthermore, the observed reduction in TI microbiota diversity of CD patients with colon-restricted disease was not statistically significant. Future efforts to profile the TI microbiome in larger cohorts of IBD patients with a more diverse range of disease severity are necessary to ensure these observations are due to true biology and are not an artifact of our preliminary study size. Last, we observed microbial dysbiosis in the absence of histological inflammation in the TI of UC patients. Whether or not loss of homeostasis at the level of epithelial gene expression exists in the TI of UC patients is an important future question to address.

In sum, these preliminary findings indicate GI dysbiosis is not necessarily restricted to the site of disease activity in UC. This may reflect loss of homeostasis throughout the GI tract or could be due to the effects of treatment and/or lifestyle changes associated with UC. Deeper characterization of the TI environment in UC will aid in our understanding of the role the TI plays in UC and its potential contribution to patient outcomes.
