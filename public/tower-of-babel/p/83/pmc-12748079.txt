Bleeding Risk With Combining Antiplatelets and Anticoagulants for Secondary Stroke Prevention: A Propensity Score–Matched Analysis
Authors: Salia Farrokh, Krishna Nalleballe, Sanjeeva Onteddu, Jose I. Suarez, Julian Bösel, Vishank A. Shah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLimited data exist on the safety of combining antiplatelets and anticoagulants for secondary stroke prevention in acute ischemic stroke (AIS). We sought to examine the hemorrhage risk with combining single (SAPT) or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with anticoagulants in patients with AIS with concomitant atrial fibrillation and acute myocardial infarction.MethodsThis retrospective cross‐sectional cohort study used TriNetX to collect data from 76 hospitals in the United States. Adult patients with diagnoses of AIS, atrial fibrillation, and acute myocardial infarction were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes. Propensity score–matched analysis, compared the odds for acute spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage and gastrointestinal bleeding at 3 months, 12 months, and throughout follow‐up within TriNetX, among 3 matched subcohorts: anticoagulant alone, anticoagulant plus SAPT, and anticoagulant plus DAPT.ResultsAmong 144 434 patients with AIS, atrial fibrillation, and myocardial infarction (mean age, 71.9 years; 43.3% women), 8772 (6.1%) patients received anticoagulants alone, 88 430 (61.2%) received anticoagulant plus SAPT, and 47 232 (32.7%) received anticoagulants plus DAPT. After propensity score matching, 8706 patients were included in each subcohort. Compared with anticoagulant alone, anticoagulant plus SAPT and anticoagulant plus DAPT showed no significant increase in intracerebral hemorrhage risk at 3 and 12 months but increased long‐term risk (odds ratio for anticoagulant plus SAPT, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.11–1.44], P<0.001; for anticoagulant plus DAPT, odds ratio, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.18–1.53]; P<0.001). Gastrointestinal bleeding risk was elevated at all time points with combination therapies.ConclusionsCombining antiplatelets with anticoagulants after AIS may be associated an increased risk of intracerebral hemorrhage in the long term, particularly beyond 12 months, but with an increased risk of acute gastrointestinal bleeding in the short and long term. Larger prospective studies are warranted to confirm our findings.

Nonvalvular atrial fibrillation (AF) is a major risk factor for acute ischemic strokes (AIS), is associated with a higher risk of disability and death than patients with AIS without AF, 1 and requires lifelong anticoagulation for secondary stroke prevention. Patients with AIS and AF often have a comorbid history of coronary artery disease (CAD) in up to 25% to 35% of cases, 2 , 3 and may present with concomitant acute myocardial infarction (MI) in up to 1% to 2% of cases. 4 CAD, particularly in the setting of acute MI, warrants single (SAPT) or dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with low‐dose aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor agent such as clopidogrel, prasugrel, or ticagrelor. 5 Moreover, according to the 2021 American Heart Association guidelines for CAD, patients require DAPT for 1 to 6 months following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) before deescalation to SAPT with a P2Y12 inhibitor. 6 This highlights the clinical dilemma of which therapeutic strategy to pursue for patients who require anticoagulants for secondary stroke prevention for AF‐related cardioembolic strokes, but may also require antiplatelet therapy for immediate coronary artery thrombosis prevention.

Often, SAPT is combined with anticoagulation in patients with AF and CAD, particularly in the setting of acute MI. For example, the American College of Cardiology guidelines recommend combining a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) and a P2Y12 inhibitor for the first 6 to 12 months after PCI followed by anticoagulant monotherapy thereafter. 7 Additionally, the American College of Cardiology guidelines provide 4 clinical scenarios in which the use of triple antithrombotic therapy may be warranted: (1) patients receiving an anticoagulant for AF who undergo PCI and require DAPT; (2) patients who are already receiving DAPT for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease with newly diagnosed AF who require anticoagulants for stroke prevention; (3) patients who are receiving anticoagulants for venous thromboembolic disease and need PCI and therefore require addition of DAPT and; (4) patients on DAPT for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease and newly diagnosed venous thromboembolic disease who require anticoagulation. 7 According to these guidelines, the duration of triple therapy should be limited to 30 days and likely needs to be individualized on the basis of individual patient risk factors. 7 Prior reports show that triple therapy significantly increases the risk of bleeding; the reported bleeding risk when adding SAPT to anticoagulation therapy is about 20% to 60% compared with adding DAPT to oral anticoagulants, which is estimated to be 2‐ to 3‐fold. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Of note, compared with vitamin K antagonists, DOACs have a similar or better efficacy profile, with reduced risk of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), while the risk of extracranial bleeding (eg, gastrointestinal bleeding) may vary depending on the type of DOAC, and there are limited data on comparisons of the safety of combining SAPT/DAPT with different types of anticoagulation therapies. Nonetheless, more recently, analysis from the ENGAGE AF‐TIMI (Effective Anticoagulation With Factor Xa Next Generation in Atrial Fibrillation–Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction) 48 trial showed that concomitant use of SAPT and anticoagulants resulted in similar incidence of ischemic events but was associated with higher risk of life‐threatening systemic and intracranial bleeding. 15 Of note, the edoxaban group exhibited similar relative efficacy but reduced bleeding compared with warfarin with or without SAPT. Despite these data, patients are often prescribed long‐term combination therapy, with 12% to 26% receiving a lifelong combination of an antiplatelet agent with an anticoagulant. 16 , 17

This clinical conundrum is further exaggerated in AIS survivors, where the risk of ICH in the acute setting and long term may be 15 times higher than patients without an AIS. 18 However, there are limited to no data on the safety or efficacy of combining antiplatelets with anticoagulants for secondary stroke prevention in AIS survivors, given that most studies examining combination antithrombotic regimens in AF and acute MI have traditionally excluded patients with a diagnosis of AIS. Likewise, there are limited data on the usage of such combination therapies in patients with AIS in current clinical practice.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to describe the frequency of combination antiplatelet and anticoagulant prescriptions in the United States and to describe the short‐ and long‐term risk of ICH and acute gastrointestinal bleeding when combining antiplatelets and anticoagulation therapy in patients with AIS and AF and concomitant acute MI. We hypothesized that DAPT or SAPT in combination with anticoagulants would be associated with a higher risk of ICH and gastrointestinal bleeding in both the short and long term after AIS.

METHODS

Study Design, Data Source, and Availability

We conducted a retrospective cross‐sectional cohort study on deidentified patient data included in the TriNetX database. TriNetX is a global federated health research network recording electronic health records data in real time from a large network of participating academic and community health care organizations located mainly in the United States and US territories. 19 Data included in the TriNetX platform include patient demographics, diagnoses classified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes (Table S1), as well as medication data through their corresponding World Health Organization Anatomic Therapeutic Chemical codes. 19 While TriNetX collects data from electronic health records in real time, leading to no lag between hospital admission and data entry, the deidentification of the data limits access to details regarding hospital site name, size, hospital characteristics, or precise location. However, due to the availability of large volumes of patient‐level data linked to billing codes, TriNetX provides valuable data for comparative effectiveness analyses and has been previously used in several other clinical studies. 20 , 21 , 22 The Institutional Review Board at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences deemed the study as exempt given the use of deidentified data from a health care analytics platform that is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act and US federal law that protects the privacy and security of health care data. We adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines established by the Enhancing the Quality and Transparency of Health Research network for reporting of this study. Data sets can be accessed through queries performed on the TriNetX platform. 19 Data outputs, analytic methods, and study materials for the above study are available upon written request.

Study Cohorts

For this analysis, cross‐sectional cohorts were queried by 1 physician (K.N.) on March 31, 2023, using browser and real‐time features on a TriNetX network called “Research,” which included 76 participating health care organizations across the United States and US territories, of which 52 centers responded with patients who met inclusion criteria for this study. Inclusion criteria were (1) adult patients (aged ≥18 years); (2) primary discharge diagnosis of AIS or cerebral infarction (ICD‐10‐CM: I63); (3) underlying diagnostic code for AF or atrial flutter (ICD‐10‐CM: I48); and (4) a simultaneous new diagnostic code for acute MI (ICD‐10‐CM: I21). We did not include a separate code for PCI to maximize patient retention in the cohorts given that patients with acute MI often receive antiplatelet therapy even in the absence of PCI. 23 To further maximize patient inclusion, we did not limit our search to a specific time window; however, none of the patients included had an index event >20 years before the query date (ie, March 2003), an approach that has been used previously. 20

Patients were categorized into 3 subcohorts based on medication exposure: (1) Group 1: anticoagulant alone (medication code, National Library of Medicine: VA:BL110; ICD‐10‐CM code, Z79.01); (2) group 2: anticoagulant plus SAPT (above code plus medication [code] for aspirin [National Library of Medicine: RXNORM:1191] or clopidogrel [National Library of Medicine: RXNORM:32968] or ticagrelor [National Library of Medicine: RXNORM:1116632]); (3) group 3: anticoagulant and DAPT (medication codes for anticoagulant plus any 2 antiplatelet agent medication codes). Comparisons were made between patients with anticoagulant alone versus anticoagulant plus SAPT and anticoagulant alone versus anticoagulant plus DAPT.

Outcomes

The primary outcome for the study was risk of nontraumatic spontaneous ICH (ICD‐10‐CM code: I61), and the secondary outcome was the risk of acute gastrointestinal bleeding (ICD‐10‐CM: K92.2). Outcomes were measured at 3 time points: (1) within 90 days; (2) within 12 months; and (3) at any time point after the index event until the query date.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted using the TriNetX research analytics platform that uses R package 2.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Baseline characteristics, including demographics and medical comorbidities were compared using the independent sample's t test for continuous variables and χ 2 test for categorical variables. To generate balanced cohorts, the TriNetX analytics platform was used to generate individual propensity scores using logistic regression, after which 1:1 propensity score matching was performed using “greedy nearest neighbor” algorithms with a prespecified caliper width of 0.1 pooled SDs, methodology that has been used previously in multiple studies. 20 , 22 , 24 Propensity score density graphs were examined, and an SMD of <0.1 between cohorts was considered as well balanced. Cohorts were matched using the following patient characteristics: age, race, sex, ethnicity, hypertension, type 2 diabetes, heart failure, CAD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, smoking, chronic kidney disease, obesity, and hyperlipidemia, classified using ICD‐10‐CM codes (Table S1). These characteristics were chosen given their clinical relevance to cardiovascular disease burden, short‐ and long‐term mortality and morbidity risk after AIS. Logistic regression was used to compare propensity‐matched cohorts and generate odds ratios for risk of ICH and acute gastrointestinal bleeding at the prespecified time points after the index event.

RESULTS

Cohort Characteristics

Within TriNetX, 144 434 patients were identified with an index event of AIS and concomitant acute MI as well as AF using ICD‐10‐CM diagnostic codes. The mean±SD age of the entire cohort was 71.9±12.3 years; 62 521 (43.3%) were women; and 110 537 (76.5%) were White individuals, 17 921 (12.4%) Black individuals, 1700 (1.2%) Asian individuals, and 5906 (4.1%) of Hispanic ethnicity. Intracerebral Hemorrhage

After 1:1 propensity score matching, 3.7% with an anticoagulant alone, 4.1% with anticoagulant plus SAPT and 3.8% with anticoagulant plus DAPT had a new diagnosis of ICH by 3 months after the index event. By 12 months after the index event, 4.4%, 4.9% and 4.9% had a new diagnosis of ICH in anticoagulant‐alone, anticoagulant plus SAPT, and anticoagulant plus DAPT groups, respectively. Throughout the entire follow‐up period until the query date, 4.9% with anticoagulant alone, 6.2% with anticoagulant plus SAPT, and 6.6% with anticoagulant plus DAPT had a new diagnostic code for ICH (P<0.0001; Figure 2).

In logistic regression after propensity score matching, anticoagulant plus SAPT (odds ratio [OR], 1.09 [95% CI, 0.94–1.28]; P=0.26) or anticoagulant plus DAPT (OR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.87–1.18]; P=0.87) was not associated with increased odds for ICH at 3 months compared with anticoagulant alone. Similarly, at 12 months after the index event, odds for ICH with anticoagulant plus SAPT (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.98–1.31]; P=0.08) or anticoagulant plus DAPT (OR, 1.13 [95% CI, 0.98–1.30]; P=0.1) was not increased when compared with anticoagulant alone. However, the lifetime odds (through the entire follow‐up period after the index event until the query date) for ICH was significantly higher with anticoagulant plus SAPT (OR, 1.26 [95% CI, 1.11–1.44]; P<0.001) and anticoagulant plus DAPT (OR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.18–1.53]; P<0.001), when compared with anticoagulant alone (Table 3).

Acute Gastrointestinal Bleeding

In contrast with ICH, proportions of patients with acute gastrointestinal bleeding at all time points were proportionately higher after combination of SAPT or DAPT with anticoagulant compared with anticoagulant alone (all P≤0.005; Figure 2). Odds for acute gastrointestinal bleeding were significantly increased with combination therapy when compared with anticoagulant alone at 3 months (anticoagulant plus SAPT: OR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.08–1.42]; P=0.002; anticoagulant plus DAPT: OR, 1.47 [95% CI, 1.29–1.68]; P<0.001). Similarly, at 12 months, the odds for acute gastrointestinal bleeding were significantly higher with anticoagulant plus SAPT (OR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.05–1.32]; P=0.005) and anticoagulant plus DAPT (OR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.28–1.59]; P<0.001) compared with anticoagulant alone. Finally, the lifetime odds of acute gastrointestinal bleeding with anticoagulant plus SAPT (OR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.10–1.35]; P<0.001) and anticoagulant plus DAPT (OR, 1.45 [95% CI, 1.31–1.60]; P<0.001) were also significantly higher compared with anticoagulant monotherapy (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this large multiyear, multicenter retrospective cross‐sectional observational study using the TriNetX health care analytics platform on a cohort of >140 000 patients with AIS with concomitant diagnoses of acute MI and AF, 94% received combination therapy with antiplatelets and anticoagulant, and >30% were prescribed triple antithrombotic therapy. Combining SAPT or DAPT with anticoagulant was not associated with increased risk of nontraumatic ICH in the first 3 to 12 months but was associated with a significantly higher lifetime risk of ICH for the entire follow‐up duration available within the TriNetX database, beyond 12 months. On the contrary, risk of acute gastrointestinal bleeding was significantly higher at 3 months, 12 months, and throughout follow‐up with combining 1 or 2 antiplatelets with anticoagulant. These findings suggest that while combining antiplatelets with anticoagulation in patients with cardioembolic ischemic stroke may be relatively safe in the short term, the duration of such combination therapy should be minimized to reduce the risk of ICH and gastrointestinal bleeding. Preventative strategies, such as H2‐receptor blockers or proton pump inhibitors, should also be considered to mitigate the elevated short‐term risk of gastrointestinal bleeding.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include data from the largest cohort to date of patients with AIS with comorbid AF and acute MI from >50 US academic and affiliated community health centers included in the TriNetX database. TriNetX, a federal health care analytics platform, has been validated for comparative effectiveness research and provides the benefit of large volumes of patient data, including diagnostic and medication codes, by collating data from electronic health records across a large number of US centers in real time. Moreover, our study also provides the largest propensity score–matched analysis of patients with AIS in the contemporary literature that were prescribed antiplatelets in combination with anticoagulant, particularly triple antithrombotic therapy, providing important insights into prescription practices for this important clinical conundrum. Finally, our study analyzed bleeding outcomes at multiple longitudinal time points, elucidating potential safety or harm of combination antiplatelet and anticoagulation therapy after AIS in the short and long term.

Important limitations of this study need to be highlighted. First, adjudication of cases and outcomes were done using ICD‐10‐CM codes, which may be prone to misclassification biases as they rely on coders and clinicians to assign appropriate diagnostic codes. For example, MI in the past may be coded as acute MI upon admission. However, separate ICD‐10‐CM codes exist for acute versus chronic MI, acute versus chronic strokes, traumatic versus nontraumatic ICH, and only specific ICD‐10‐CM codes for acute disease processes (Methods and Supplement) were used in this study. Moreover, our very large sample size may, at least in part, mitigate the risk of this bias. Second, while we used propensity score matching to balance cohorts for multiple vascular risk factors and demographic characteristics that are known to influence bleeding outcomes, we were unable to account for important known confounders due to lack of availability of these data in TriNetX, such as admission stroke severity, imaging characteristics such as stroke size, cerebral microbleeds, small‐vessel disease, intra‐ or extracranial atherosclerosis, and left atrial or ventricular thrombi, all of which may influence the use of combination antithrombotic therapy, their duration, and the risk of long‐term bleeding. Similarly, the retrospective nature of this study also limits the ability to adjust for all possible unknown confounders, limiting causal interpretation. Third, an important limitation of our analysis is the lack of details regarding the type of anticoagulant (DOAC versus vitamin K antagonist), duration, and dosing of anticoagulant and antiplatelet agents. While all patients received anticoagulant plus antiplatelets when entering the data set, data on discontinuation of anticoagulant or antiplatelet agents in the future were not available; thus, we were unable to delineate how long these combination therapies were prescribed, limiting determination of a temporal relationship and, hence, assigning a causal interpretation to our findings. We therefore intentionally did not compare the bleeding risk between anticoagulant plus SAPT versus anticoagulant plus DAPT groups as many patients could have transitioned from DAPT to SAPT at some point in the course of therapy, making the assessment between the 2 groups complex and unreliable. Fourth, we did not examine the association with short‐ and long‐term ischemic outcomes due to limited ability to identify new diagnoses of recurrent stroke and the lack of neuroimaging data within TriNetX. This combined with the absence of data regarding the indications for prescribing triple therapy or SAPT plus anticoagulant makes it difficult to evaluate whether the clinical benefits outweigh the bleeding risks observed. Fifth, another important limitation is the potential for undocumented aspirin use in our study, as it can be obtained over the counter, possibly leading to misclassification of some patients categorized as receiving anticoagulation alone. Sixth, given the relatively small number of ICH events occurring within the 12‐month follow‐up period, the study is likely underpowered to detect meaningful differences between treatment groups. Furthermore, the lack of granular clinical data such as blood pressure measurements, platelet counts, alcohol consumption patterns, and fall risk assessments limits the ability to adequately match groups for these important predictors of bleeding risk. Finally, TriNetX data set does not easily distinguish between new and historical bleeding events, potentially confounding our analysis, although and acute and chronic ICH has different ICD codes. Nonetheless, we were unable to reliably determine whether observed ICH events were new occurrences or recurrences, nor could we adequately account for prior history of gastrointestinal or other major bleeding events before the index AIS event. These limitations ultimately prohibit us from performing a time‐to‐event analysis or longitudinal modeling and we cannot exclude a potential immortal time bias. Finally, TriNetX does not allow for downloading individual patient data, and the built‐in statistical method for propensity score matching uses the greedy nearest neighbor approach. Other approaches, such as overlap propensity score or inverse probability of treatment weighting, may yield different outcomes, which we were unable to assess. Nonetheless, the large number of patients in our cohort, use of propensity score matching, and availability of long‐term data provide important insights into this specific patient population, particularly given the limited availability of data for this clinical conundrum. However, given the several limitations, confirmation of our findings in prospective studies and randomized controlled clinical trials is necessary.

Implications of the Study

Several clinical trials have examined the risk of bleeding when combining anticoagulant with SAPT or DAPT in the cardiac patient population. The PIONEER AF‐PCI (An Open‐Label, Randomized, Controlled, Multicenter Study Exploring Two Treatment Strategies of Rivaroxaban and a Dose‐Adjusted Oral Vitamin K Antagonist Treatment Strategy in Subjects With Atrial Fibrillation Who Undergo Percutaneous Coronary Intervention) trial randomized 2124 patients with nonvalvular AF who needed PCI with stent placement for ACS or stable ischemic heart disease, to receive rivaroxaban in combination with clopidogrel, low‐dose rivaroxaban with clopidogrel and aspirin or standard therapy including warfarin (international normalized ratio, 2–3), clopidogrel, and aspirin for 12 months. 25 The primary outcome of clinically significant bleeding over the treatment period of 12 months was lower in the groups receiving rivaroxaban and clopidogrel compared with triple therapy with warfarin, clopidogrel, and aspirin. RE‐DUAL PCI (Evaluation of Dual Therapy With Dabigatran Versus Triple Therapy With Warfarin in Patients With AF That Undergo a PCI With Stenting), a multicenter noninferiority trial, randomized 2725 patients with AF and recent PCI for ACS or stable ischemic heart disease, to receive dabigatran plus clopidogrel versus triple therapy with warfarin, clopidogrel, and aspirin. 26 Major bleeding or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding, was overall lower in anticoagulant and SAPT (15.4%) compared with triple therapy (26.9%) (hazard ratio, 0.72 [95% CI, 0.58–0.88]; P<0.001). Similarly, the ENTRUST‐AF PCI (Edoxaban‐Based Versus Vitamin K Antagonist–Based Antithrombotic Regimen After Successful Coronary Stenting in Patients With Atrial Fibrillation) noninferiority trial randomized 1506 patients with AF receiving PCI for ACS or stable ischemic heart disease to receive edoxaban plus clopidogrel versus triple therapy with warfarin, clopidogrel, and aspirin. 14 Edoxaban plus clopidogrel was noninferior to triple therapy with respect to clinically significant bleeding (17% versus 20%) without an increase in ischemic events. Finally, the AUGUSTUS (Antithrombotic Therapy After Acute Coronary Syndrome or PCI in Atrial Fibrillation) trial randomized 4614 patients with AF and recent ACS or PCI to 4 groups: apixaban plus clopidogrel, apixaban plus aspirin and clopidogrel, warfarin and clopidogrel or warfarin, and aspirin and clopidogrel. 10 Primary outcome was defined as major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding. Apixaban when compared with warfarin was associated with lower rates of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding regardless of prior history of stroke. This study concluded that anticoagulation with a DOAC and a P2Y12 is likely appropriate in the majority of patients, and triple therapy with aspirin may be contributing to bleeding risks. More importantly, the AUGUSTUS trial highlighted that avoiding aspirin resulted in a 47% lower risk of bleeding than adding aspirin without a significantly higher incidence of coronary ischemic events. Collectively, these trials demonstrate that SAPT and DOAC may be safer with respect to bleeding outcomes when compared with triple antithrombotic therapy in patients with AF and CAD. Unfortunately, with the exception of the AUGUSTUS trial, all the remaining trials intentionally excluded patients with a recent diagnosis of AIS. Moreover, in the AUGUSTUS trial, only 14% of the participants had a history of stroke or transient ischemic attack, which were not necessarily acute events, making it challenging to extrapolate these study findings for the purpose of secondary stroke prevention in patients presenting with a diagnosis of AIS. Our study thus provides novel insight into current prescribing practices involving the combination of antiplatelets with anticoagulant in patients with AIS with AF and ACS, and it delineates the short‐ and long‐term risks of bleeding in this specific patient population, which is largely unknown in the existing literature.

In a recent study of 93 patients receiving triple antithrombotic therapy, the readmission rate due to bleeding was 2.2% at 14 days and 6.5% at 30 days. 27 Importantly, the indication for antiplatelet therapy in this study was not limited to ACS, and ≈15% received it for a diagnosis of AIS. Additionally, the indication for anticoagulation was AF in 40% of the patients, but venous thromboembolic disease and other indications were also included. Within 30 days of initiation of triple therapy, 4.3% of patients endured major bleeding as defined by the International Society on Thrombosis and Hemostasis. These proportions appear to be similar to our study, which also showed an average risk of ICH and acute gastrointestinal bleeding of 4% and 6%, respectively, in patients receiving SAPT or DAPT and anticoagulant after AIS.

CONCLUSIONS

In this large retrospective, multicenter, cross‐sectional observational cohort study across the United States, combining antiplatelets with anticoagulant was commonplace for patients with AIS with concomitant AF and acute MI. While no increased short‐term risk of ICH was observed, long‐term risk rose by 25% with addition of SAPT and 35% with addition of DAPT over anticoagulant alone. Acute gastrointestinal bleeding risk increased both short and long term, by 20% with combining 1 and 45% with 2 antiplatelet agents over anticoagulant alone. These findings underscore the need for individualized risk–benefit assessments when considering combination antithrombotic therapy for secondary stroke prevention, with early deescalation as soon as feasible and consideration of preventative strategies to mitigate the risk of acute gastrointestinal bleeding such as proton pump inhibitors or H2‐receptor blockers. Prospective clinical trials are necessary to further evaluate the safety and efficacy of combining antiplatelets with anticoagulant for secondary stroke prevention in patients with AIS.
