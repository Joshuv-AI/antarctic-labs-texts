Gender-based differences in the job titles and lifestyles in the cataract and refractive surgery society in Japan
Authors: Naoko Kato, Takashi Kojima, Masayuki Ouchi, Tomoaki Nakamura, Yoshihiro Tokuda, Tadayuki Yakushiji, Kazuo Ichikawa
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
To investigate sex differences in the titles and lifestyles of Japanese ophthalmologists, we evaluated work places and private lives. Retrospective cross-sectional study. The study included 1721 members (1344 males and 377 females) of the Japanese Society of Cataract and Refractive Surgery. An online, anonymized questionnaire was distributed to the society members. The questionnaire included 40 questions to collect data on profiles, lifestyles, job title, families, spouses, children, household chores, child-rearing, and work satisfaction. In total, 219 members (144 males and 75 females; 53.4 ± 1.0 and 51.3 ± 9.9 years old, respectively) completed the questionnaire. The job title, working time, annual income, marriage rate, and the number of children significantly differed between male and female respondents. Female respondents had greater responsibilities toward house chores, child care, and nursing, whereas several male doctors had spouses who did not work or worked for shorter times, earned a lower income, and contributed greater toward family responsibilities. Female respondents changed their job titles after having children more frequently than male respondents. Both males and females had limited time available for community activities and volunteer work. There were no significant differences in daily sleep duration. Both sexes were equally satisfied with their career choice of ophthalmology; however, fewer females recommended ophthalmology as a career for students and children compared to males. There are significant sex differences among ophthalmologists in Japan in terms of family responsibilities; this topic has received insufficient attention.

1. Introduction

Male doctors have traditionally outnumbered female doctors worldwide, particularly until the end of the twentieth century.[1,2] However, the proportion of female doctors has increased in the past 2 decades, reaching 50% in several countries, particularly in Eastern Europe, South America, and Africa. By contrast, the proportion of female doctors has remained low in Eastern Asian countries. South Korea and Japan rank at the bottom of 22 countries belong to the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development in terms of the proportion of female doctors (24% and 22%, respectively).[3]

Even in Western countries, where the proportion of female doctors has reached 50%, gender discrimination against female doctors in terms of promotion, leadership positions, working time, annual income, and private life has been reported.[4–12] On the other hand, fewer studies have evaluated sex discrimination against female doctors in Japan. Kawase et al[13,14] evaluated the sex differences among surgeons in terms of their job title and personal life. Kishibe et al[15] reported significantly fewer female than male doctors at academic positions in the field of dermatology. Yamazaki et al[16,17] found that female doctors often have greater burdens compared to male doctors and attempt to balance their professional and private lives. Nishida et al[18] also found that physician-mothers but not physician-fathers had to balance their family and clinical responsibilities during the coronavirus disease-19 pandemic in Japan.

Several studies from the field of ophthalmology conducted in countries other than Japan have reported similar trends.[19–24] Furthermore, some studies have also evaluated possible solutions for such inequalities.[23,24] However, no studies have examined the job titles and lifestyles of Japanese ophthalmologists according to sex. Therefore, we used a questionnaire to evaluate this among members of the Japanese society of cataract and refractive surgery (JSCRS). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first survey of Japanese ophthalmologists.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

An online questionnaire was sent to the member of the JSCRS via e-mail. The website link for information related to the questionnaire created by Google Forms was sent to 1721 ophthalmologists and researchers (1344 males and 377 females). The data were collected over 1 month, from September 29, 2022 to October 31, 2022. First, we requested to all members to answer the questionnaire by e-mail and sent reminders on October 13, 2022 and October 24, 2022, respectively. The survey was conducted anonymously, and no personal identifying information was collected.

2.2. Questionnaire

The questionnaire included 40 questions designed to collect data on personal profile, lifestyle, job title, families, spouse, children, household chores, child-rearing, and work-related satisfaction (Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J786).

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP 13.2.0 (SAS Institute Inc). After the normality test, Student t test was used to evaluate the differences between male and female respondents in terms of their age and graduation year. Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to analyze differences in terms of age at first marriage, age at the birth of the first child, and number of children. The chi-square test was used to evaluate differences in terms of academic title, job title, working time, annual income, time for household chores, child care, nursing care, sleeping, marital status, duration of parental leave, frequency of change in job title, satisfaction from work, and private lives, and recommendation to medical students and their own children regarding pursuing an ophthalmology career. P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

3.1. Background information of respondents

Of the 1721 members, 219 completed the questionnaire, including 144 males and 75 females. All respondents answered to questions 1 to 12, 33, and 35 to 40. Among them, 134 males and 53 females, who were married, answered questions 13 to 20. The questions about children, questions 21 to 32, 122 males and 55 females who had child(ren) answered. The question about the nursing care, question 34, 19 males and 21 females with family member(s) who needed nursing care answered.

The mean age of the respondents was 53.4 ± 1.0 and 51.3 ± 9.9 years in males and females, respectively. The graduation year was not significantly different between the sexes. In total, 102 (70.8%) and 51 (68.0%) of male and female respondents had an Medical Doctor, respectively, whereas 42 (29.2%) and 24 (32.0%) had both an Medical Doctor and Doctor of Philosophy, respectively (Table 1).

3.2. Job title, pay, and hours

The job title was significantly different between the sexes. The most common job title for both sexes was practitioner (clinic director), which accounted for 65% and 36% of male and female respondents, followed by full-time doctor in a hospital, 17 % and 27 % of the male and female respondents, respectively. Other job titles were relatively uncommon among male respondents, although female respondents reported several additional job titles. Part-time doctors accounted for 9% of the female respondents and only 1% of male respondents. One female (1%) and 2 male respondents (1%) answered that they were currently not working as medical doctors. One female (1%) and no male respondents answered that she/he worked as a researcher (Fig. 1A). Regarding annual income, the largest income group in both sexes earned > ¥21 million, with 64% and 26% of males and females earning > ¥21 million, respectively. However, annual income was significantly higher in males (Fig. 1B). Regarding working hours, 96% of males worked > 8 hours per day and 26% worked > 15 hours per day. Among female respondents, 81% worked > 8 hours per day and 13% worked > 15 hours per day. Working hours were significantly longer in males (Fig. 1C).

3.3. Private life

In total, 54% and 64% of male and female respondents were involved in community or volunteer work, and 31% and 27% spent < 1 hour per day on it, respectively (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J788). Approximately 14%, 47%, 23%, and 5% of male doctors spent no time, < 1 hour per day, 1 to 2 hours per day, and more than 3 hours per day on house chores, child care, and nursing care, respectively. By contrast, the values were 1%, 9%, 31%, and 39% for females (Fig. 2). Time spent on hobbies or relaxation varied from 0 to > 6 hours per week, with no significant differences between the sexes (Figure S1B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J788). Sleep time varied from 3 to > 8 hours, and was most commonly 6 to 7 hours for both sexes with no significant differences between the sexes (Figure S1C, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J788).

Private lives of Japanese ophthalmologists. Female respondents spent a significantly longer time on house chores, child care, and nursing care.

3.4. Marital status

Among all respondents, 134 (93.1%) male and 53 (71.3%) female respondents were married, whereas among those aged > 50 years, 86 (95.6%) male and 36 (66.7%) female respondents were married. The age at first marriage was 30.3 ± 5.0 and 30.3 ± 4.5 years in male and female respondents, respectively. The age at the time of first child birth was 33.3 ± 5.0 and 31.3 ± 4.3 years in males and females, respectively (Table 2).

3.5. Spouses

The spouses of respondents had various occupations. The spouses of 49% of male doctors were employed but were not doctors, whereas those of 33% were not employed; 18% were married to a medical doctor. By comparison, 99% of the spouses of female doctors were employed and 62% were medical doctors (Figure S2A, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J790). The occupations of nondoctor spouses of male respondents included self-employment (42%), part-time work (33%), telework/work from home (19%), and full-time work (6%). On the other hand, the occupations of nondoctor spouses of female respondents included full-time work (58%), telework/work from home (21%), self-employment (16%), and part-time work (5%; Figure S2B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J790). The working time of spouses was ≤ 4 hours for 68% of male respondents, whereas fewer spouses worked for more hours. On the other hand, the working time of the spouses of female doctors was 12 to 15 hours for 36%, 9 to 10 hours for 17%, and ≤ 4 hours for 6% (Fig. 3A). Time spent on house chores, child care, and nursing care by spouses was > 6 hours for 37%, 2 to 3 hours for 17%, and 3 to 4 hours for 17% of male respondents. By contrast, the values were < 1 hour for 52%, 1 to 2 hours for 21%, and 2 to 3 hours for 12% of female respondents (Figure S2C, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J790). House chores were performed entirely by the spouse, mostly by the spouse, by the spouse and respondent, mostly by the respondent, and entirely by the respondent in 36%, 53%, 8%, 2%, and 2% of male respondents, and 2%, 4%, 14%, 63%, and 18% of female respondents, respectively (Fig. 3B). Finally, annual income of the spouse was ¥3.0 to 5.99 million for 44% of the male respondents but ¥9.00 to 11.99 million in 27% and > ¥21 million in 24% of female respondents. The differences were significant (Fig. 3C).

3.6. Child care and lifestyle

Male and female respondents had an average of 1.8 ± 1.1 and 1.3 ± 1.0 children, respectively, a significant difference (Table 2). For male respondents, the spouses cared for the children in 80% of cases where daycare/after-school childcare was used in 15%. For female respondents, daycare/after-school childcare was used in 64% of cases, parents cared in 13%, baby sitter/maid in 2%, and 18 % of the female respondents cared their children by themselves in 18% where the spouse cared in 0% (Figure S3A, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J792). The responsibility for picking children up from school in case of sickness was of the spouse for 93% of males and the 7% of females (Fig. 4A). The spouses cared for the children at night or during holidays for 85% of males but none of females (Fig. 4B).

The age at the time of the birth of the first child was significantly higher among male respondents (Table 2). Overall, 94% of males did not receive child care leave, whereas all female respondents did. The duration of child care leave varied from several weeks to a year. In addition, 5% of females quit or changed their job and 2% enrolled in graduate programs after child birth, whereas no males left or changed jobs (Fig. 4C). Approximately 38% of the spouses of male respondents did not receive child care leave, indicating that they were probably not employed, and 26% quit or changed their jobs. In addition, 93% of the spouses of female respondents received no childcare leave (Figure S3B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J792). The male respondents stated that their spouses provided all or most of the childcare, whereas female respondents stated that they provided all or most of the childcare (Figure S3C, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J792).

3.7. Change in work schedule after having children

The work schedule changed after having children for 32% and 71% of males and females, respectively. This was the case for the spouses of 56% of male respondents and 4% of female respondents (Fig. 5A). Before marriage, most male and female respondents worked full-time (90% and 83%, respectively), and only a few respondents worked part-time (2% and 0%, respectively) or as practitioners (0% and 2%, respectively; Figure S4A, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J794). After marriage, the work schedule differed between the sexes even before the birth of the first child (Figure S4B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J794). However, the difference between the sexes increased further after this time point. Among male and female respondents, 61% and 38% had their own practices, respectively, whereas 35% and 38% worked full-time, respectively (Figure S4C, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J794). Compared to female respondents without children, female respondents with children more commonly worked as clinic directors and less commonly worked as full-time doctors in hospitals. The work schedule did not differ between male respondents with and without children (Fig. 5B).

3.8. Nursing care

The numbers of family members who required nursing care were 2, 1, and 0 in 1%, 12%, and 87% of male respondents, and in 3%, 24%, and 73% of female respondents, respectively (Figure S5A, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J797). The primary caregivers were public service, private service, family members, and the respondent for 33%, 28%, 28%, and 11% of male respondents and for 19%, 29%, 14%, and 38% of female respondents, respectively (Figure S5B, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/J797).

3.9. Satisfaction related to career and private life

Both male and female respondents had similar satisfaction related to their career and private lives (Figure S6A, B Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K481). As problems faced in the job, the both male and female respondents stated work time at the first place and content of work/ability/skills at the second. The house chores and nursing care and for family members were recognized as problems by 23% and 11% of the female respondents but only by 8% and 3% of male respondents, respectively (Fig. 6). When asked whether they would select ophthalmology as a career again, if given the opportunity, 68% and 57% of males and females answered affirmatively (Figure S6C, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K481). In total, 28% and 30% of the male respondents “strongly recommended” and “recommended” ophthalmology as a career to their male students or sons, compared to 9% and 26% of the female respondents, respectively (Figure S6D, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K481). In comparison, 31% and 22% of male respondents and 14% and 32% of female respondents “strongly recommended” and “recommended” ophthalmology as a career to their female students or daughters, respectively (Figure S6E, Supplemental Digital Content, http://links.lww.com/MD/K481).

The house chores (23%) and nursing care and for family members were recognized as problems by 23% and 11% of the female respondents and by 8% and 3% of male respondents, respectively.

4. Discussion

We found significant sex differences in the work environment and private lives of Japanese ophthalmologists. The proportion of respondents with a Doctor of Philosophy was not significantly different between the sexes, indicating an equivalent educational level between them. However, there were significant differences between the sexes in working schedule, annual income, marriage rate, number of children, and responsibilities toward house chore, child care, and nursing.

The work schedule differed significantly between male and female respondents after, but not before, marriage. Male respondents worked for longer hours than female respondents after marriage (62% of males worked > 10 hours and 26% > 12 hours). Although females worked fewer hours, 44% of them worked > 10 hours and 13% worked > 12 hours. Full-time doctors in Japan usually perform night duties every 2 to 3 weeks without a compensatory day off. Due to their long working hours, most male respondents were unable to engage in activities other than work, such as household chores, child care, nursing care, and community activities. This tendency was not affected by the number of children, possibly due to the limited number of respondents in the present study. On the other hand, female doctors may have worked fewer hours to be able to attend to family responsibilities.

Doctors require support in their private lives because of their long working hours, which limits the occupational activities of their partners and spouses. The marriage rate was higher among male ophthalmologists (93.1% in all respondents and 95.6% among those aged > 50 years) than female ophthalmologists (71.6% and 66.7%, respectively). In addition, the spouses of 49% and 33% male respondents were not medical doctors and were not working, whereas the values for female respondents were 36% and 2%, respectively. These data may suggest that female respondents were unable to balance their work and family obligations, which in turn affected their work schedule, including changing form full-time to part-time work.

Responsibilities toward children and need for parental leave are possible obstacles in the career development of Japanese ophthalmologists. Most female respondents who bear children receive maternity and childcare leave of variable lengths and may even quit or change their job. By comparison, 94% of the male respondents did not receive childcare leave and none changed their job.

Although we did not evaluate job titles at hospitals, previous studies have found that fewer female doctors are promoted to leadership positions compared to male doctors.[6,7,21] A similar trend may also be seen for Japanese ophthalmologists, as supported by the greater proportion of male respondents working full-time and as clinic directors, and greater proportion of female respondents working part-time.

The difference in work schedule between the sexes also leads to a difference in their annual income. Part-time workers in Japan generally work fewer hours and have less social security, authority in the work place, and opportunities for promotion compared to full-time workers.

Male and female respondents had no significant differences in terms of their satisfaction with their work and personal life or selection of career if given the opportunity. However, fewer female respondents recommended an ophthalmology career to medical students and their children compared to male respondents, probably because the former considered that an ophthalmology career is insufficiently rewarded compared to the hard work required.

Japanese society is aging rapidly and nursing care for the elderly is a major problem.[25,26] Females are commonly responsible for the nursing care of family members. Unexpectedly, significantly fewer family members of male respondents required nursing care compared to female respondents, suggesting that the former do not spend sufficient time with their family members to recognize that nursing care is required. Despite the availability of public and private nursing services, female doctors have greater responsibilities toward nursing care of family members compared to male doctors.

These sex differences among Japanese ophthalmologists may be explained by the long-lasting culture, and influence of Confucian education. Traditional customs persist among families, communities, and societies. As a result, female doctors often struggle to balance their professional and family responsibilities, whereas male doctors typically assume the financial responsibility to provide for their family.

During the past several decades, sex equality and women rights have received significant attention, leading to an increasing number of female students being enrolled in Japanese medical schools. The current differences between the sexes may lead to resignation of female doctors and exhaustion of male doctors due to overwork. Furthermore, harsh work environments may lead to poor workplace relationships, sexual and moral harassment,[27,28] and medical malpractice.[29,30] Achieving sex equality will support female doctors and prevent doctor shortages, which is expected in the near future.

The major limitation of the present study was that it only included only limited members of the JSCRS, who are cataract and refractive surgeons, and may not be representative of all Japanese ophthalmologists. To raise the response rate to the questionnaire, we sent reminders twice to all of the members, and the response rate increased each time. However, other members who did not answer might overly be cautious about the questionnaires received through internet or have less interest in the sex differences among ophthalmologists. In addition, the findings may vary among younger individuals who may have different ideas and preferences regarding their careers and personal lives compared to older individuals. Future studies should include a nationwide sample to verify the generalizability of our results.

5. Conclusion

There are significant sex differences among Japanese ophthalmologists, which may originate from conservative ideas of the expected roles of each sex and an unsupportive work environment for Japanese medical professionals. To reduce these differences, the work environment and ideas regarding roles in the work place and family life should be improved, for instance by shortening working hours of full-time doctors on both sexes and/or introducing quota system.
