Obstructive sleep apnea in patients with acute myocardial infarction: one-year follow-up study
Authors: Justyna Tyfel-Paluszek, Aleksandra Mikunda, Aleksandra Kułaga, Łukasz Pominkiewicz, Jacek Łach, Robert Skomro, Wojciech Płazak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aim, materials and methodsObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) leads to organ ischemia, including that of the heart. The aim of this study was to assess the frequency of OSA by polygraphic examination in patients admitted with acute coronary syndrome, to evaluate the influence of OSA on echocardiographic findings and myocardial infarction markers and to describe the influence of CPAP treatment on OSA parameters and echocardiographic indices after myocardial infarction. We included 25 patients treated with primary coronary angioplasty, aged 60.3 ± 19.4 years, with a BMI of 32.2 ± 3.8.ResultsPolygraphy showed OSA in 18 (72%) patients, and in 12 (48%) severe OSA (AHI > 30) was diagnosed. OSA predominantly affected obese patients (BMI < 30, n = 13: AHI 23.2 ± 17.4, BMI ≥ 30, n = 12: AHI 55.5 ± 23.6, p < 0.000001). In patients with normal RV structure and function AHI was significantly lower compared to those with RV dysfunction. In patients without OSA, CKMB and troponin levels were significantly lower as compared to those with OSA: AHI < 15: CKMB 41.7 ± 40.7, Tn 0.84 ± 1.0; AHI≥15: CKMB 169.2 ± 112.4, Tn 4.77 ± 4.43; p = 0.04. CPAP therapy was started only in 9 (50%) patients with OSA due to low patient compliance. In patients treated with CPAP, the therapy resulted in a significant decrease in OSA indices (AHI dropped from 52.0 ± 18.6 to 5.0 ± 1.0; p = 0.001). The function of the RV, as measured by TAPSE, improved only in patients treated with CPAP (CPAP: from 20.5 ± 3.3 to 22.3 ± 2.3, p = 0.04; no CPAP: 22.3 ± 4.7 to 23.6 ± 1.8; p–ns).ConclusionsWe demonstrate a high prevalence of OSA in patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome. OSA influences RV enlargement and dysfunction, as well as CKMB and troponin levels during myocardial infarction. After myocardial infarction, RV function improves only in patients treated with CPAP.

Objectives and aim of the study

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) has been shown to affect 14% of men and 5% of women in developed countries, in population-based studies utilizing an AHI cutoff of ≥ 5 events/h (hypopneas associated with 4% oxygen desaturations) combined with clinical symptoms [1]. In certain groups, the occurrence of OSA is much higher than the general estimate. For instance, it affects about 70% to 80% of individuals being assessed for bariatric surgery [2], and roughly 60% to 70% of those who have experienced a transient ischemic attack or stroke [3]. Other clinical populations with elevated rates of OSA include patients with coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, cardiac arrhythmias, resistant hypertension, type 2 diabetes, and polycystic ovary syndrome [1]. OSA leads to blood desaturation, organ ischemia including the heart, blood pressure profile disturbances with lack of its night physiological decrease („non-dippers patients”), heart rhythm and conduction abnormalities – bradycardia, AV blocks, atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmias [1].

Our previous studies [4–6] and many others have proved that in patients without other comorbidities, OSA leads to right ventricular enlargement and dysfunction, together with endothelial dysfunction, increasing cardiovascular risk. The published data [7, 8] suggest, that in patients after myocardial infarction, the frequency of OSA is extremely high, reaching 40–50%, but most of the collected data are based on questionnaires. In very high-risk patients with acute coronary syndrome, early detection of OSA may be crucial.

The aims of the present study were as follows:1/ to assess the prevalence of OSA using polygraphic examination in patients admitted to the hospital with acute coronary syndrome,2/ to evaluate structural and functional disturbances of the heart by echocardiography in acute coronary syndrome patients with vs. without OSA during myocardial infarction and in 1-year follow-up,3/ to evaluate the levels of myocardial infarction markers in patients with vs. without OSA,4/ to evaluate the influence of CPAP treatment administered after myocardial infarction on sleep apnea parameters and echocardiographic results in OSA patients [4–6].

Materials and methods

We included 25 patients who were admitted to hospital urgently due to acute coronary syndrome, in whom myocardial infarction was treated with primary coronary angioplasty, and in whom such a procedure was the definitive invasive treatment, meaning that no further stages of coronary revascularization were needed [1]. We excluded patients with hemodynamic or respiratory instability for more than 24 h, congenital or severe valvular heart diseases, a history of pulmonary embolism, severe COPD or asthma, prior treatment for OSA or a life expectancy of less than 1 year [1].

Because the study aimed to assess the impact of hypoxemia and oxygen desaturation associated with obstructive sleep apnea, patients with conditions known to cause reduced oxygen saturation were excluded.

Eligibility was determined based on medical documentation provided by the patients and data available in the hospital electronic medical records [1].

Due to the acute cardiological status of the participants, functional diagnostic testing was not performed.

For the purposes of the study, in all patients echocardiography and polygraphy were performed on the 3rd to 5th day after admission. Patients diagnosed with moderate (AHI ≥ 15) or severe (AHI ≥ 30) OSA were referred to the ambulatory pulmonology department one week after discharge for standard CPAP treatment implementation.

Inclusion into the study did not result in disruption of standard cardiovascular therapy, which was determined solely by the attending physician. The attending physician was blinded to the results of the polygraphy.

All patients on CPAP treatment were followed after the discharge by the pulmonologist via telemetric monitoring of CPAP devices. A control echocardiographic examination was recorded in all patients after 1 year of observation [4–6].

In the present study, the analysis deliberately focused on objective monitoring of respiratory parameters, without the use of screening questionnaires. According to the current position of the American Heart Association, symptom-based tools such as the Epworth Sleepiness Scale and the STOP-Bang questionnaire do not replace objective sleep diagnostics and have limited predictive value in patients with cardiovascular disease [9, 10].

Moreover, in patients with acute myocardial infarction, obstructive sleep apnea often occurs without the typical symptom of excessive daytime sleepiness, further limiting the usefulness of history-based screening approaches [10, 11].

Polygraphic examination and CPAP adjusting

The overnight recordings were performed using a sleep-monitoring system (NoxT3, Nox Medical, type III according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, AASM) in all subjects [1]. Standard criteria for sleep event scoring were used. Obstructive apnea (OA) was scored when there was an absence or reduction to less than 10% of the baseline airflow with continued respiratory effort, lasting 10 s or longer. Central apnea (CA) was scored as an absence or reduction to less than 10% of baseline airflow without continued respiratory effort, lasting 10 s or longer. Hypopnea (H) was scored as a decrease in airflow of at least 30% of baseline for 10 s or more accompanied by a 3% or more decrease in oxygen saturation. When an event met apnea criteria and was associated with absent inspiratory effort in the initial part of the event, followed by resumption of inspiratory effort in the second part of the event, it was scored as a mixed apnea (MIX). The AHI was expressed as the sum of the number of apneas plus the number of hypopneas that occurred, on average, per hour of recording.

Recent studies increasingly highlight the importance of adequate sleep in cardiovascular disease [12]. Sleep disturbances exert multidirectional effects, and the individual mechanisms underlying these associations are difficult to investigate and quantify separately [13].

In the present study, polygraphy was selected as the diagnostic method because the primary aim was the quantitative assessment of respiratory events and intermittent hypoxemia rather than detailed analysis of sleep architecture. For the identification of clinically significant obstructive sleep apnea and estimation of the burden of apneas and hypopneas, polygraphy is considered an appropriate and validated diagnostic tool.

Early polygraphic assessment was performed to characterize sleep-disordered breathing during the acute phase of myocardial injury, when sympathetic activation, intermittent hypoxia, and hemodynamic stress may exert the greatest impact on ventricular remodeling [4].

We acknowledge that this approach is associated with certain limitations, including less precise estimation of central apneas and the inability to assess arousals. At the same time, the acute clinical condition itself may substantially influence parameters related to sleep quality, sleep depth, and fragmentation of sleep stages, which would also affect measurements obtained using polysomnography.

Prior to initiation of CPAP treatment, a mask-fitting test was performed for each patient. The in-hospital titration protocol included two consecutive nights during which effective therapeutic pressures were adjusted. Initial pressure settings were usually 7–13 cm H₂O, and expiratory pressure relief was set to 3 cm H₂O. In all cases, we enabled the “ramp” setting, which initiates airflow at a low level and slowly increases the pressure to the set level in order to make titration more comfortable for the patient. Two types of devices were used: RESMED AutoSet S9 (ResMed, Bella Vista, Australia) and REMstar Auto (Philips-Respironics, Murraysville, PA, USA) [4]. Initial therapeutic pressure level, respiratory relief level, and therapeutic pressure rise time were adjusted individually based on a patient’s comfort or clinical response.

Echocardiography

We performed two-dimensional (2-D) echocardiographic imaging in standard views, including conventional 2-D, Doppler, Color-Doppler, and Tissue-Doppler images (TDI), using a Philips IE33 device (transducer X5-1; 1.3 to 4.2 MHz) [2]. The examination was performed in the left-lateral decubitus and supine positions. For right heart assessment, the RV-focused 4-chamber view was used. The tricuspid regurgitant jet was imaged from different views and the maximal velocity was recorded. The pulmonary artery pressure was calculated using the modified Bernoulli equation [3]. The M-mode technique was used to assess the maximum systolic excursion of the lateral tricuspid annulus in an apical 4-chamber view. Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) was performed to obtain peak systolic (S’) velocity and peak early (E’) diastolic myocardial annular velocity. Once the optimal 2-D image was obtained, acquisition was made in the form of 5 heart-beats, enabling us to choose the most reliable measurements of dimensions and Doppler waveforms.

Echocardiographic analyses were performed by two independent, experienced, certified investigators who were blinded to the patients’ OSA status [4–6].

Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) demonstrated very good inter-observer agreement, with measurement differences below 5%.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean and standard deviations (SD). The Shapiro-Wilk test was used to determine the normality of variable distribution. The Student’s t-test for continuous variables was conducted to assess differences between the groups. The accepted statistical significance threshold was established as a p-value of < 0.05 [4–6].

Study limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the moderate sample size may have limited the statistical power of the multivariable analyses. Residual confounding cannot be excluded, and confirmation of our findings in larger cohorts is warranted.

Second, the lack of assessment of sleep quality prior to hospitalization may limit a comprehensive clinical characterization of obstructive sleep apnea in the studied population. However, the use of objective respiratory monitoring in all consecutive patients minimized potential selection bias and allowed the analysis to focus on actual oxygen desaturation events during the acute phase.

Third, allocation of patients to CPAP therapy was not randomized and was based on patient preference and treatment adherence. This approach reflects real-world clinical practice, in which the decision to initiate CPAP therapy often depends on the patient. Consequently, our observations illustrate how CPAP use was associated with the evaluated cardiological parameters in patients who chose to undergo therapy, although causal interpretation remains limited.

Finally, a difference in hypoxic burden was observed between patients with STEMI and NSTEMI. However, this aspect was not subjected to formal statistical analysis because of the limited sample size.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the local Ethics Committee (approval number 1072.6120.29.2022). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Influence of OSA on echocardiographic findings at inclusion

Echocardiography revealed the influence of OSA on right ventricle structure and function. In patients with normal RV dimension (≤ 30 mm) AHI was 20.8 ± 19.2, in those with RV enlargement (> 30 mm) AHI was 44,1 ± 28.9, p = 0.01. In patients with normal RV function (TAPSE ≥ 24 mm) AHI was 17.7 ± 11.3, while if RV function was decreased (TAPSE < 24 mm), AHI was 43.9 ± 25.7, p = 0.019.

Influence of OSA on myocardial infarction markers

A significant relationship between OSA parameters and myocardial injury markers was found. In patients without OSA, CKMB and troponin levels were significantly lower compared to those with OSA: AHI < 15: CKMB 41.7 ± 40.7, Tn 0.84 ± 1.0; AHI ≥ 15: CKMB 169.2 ± 112.4, Tn 4.77 ± 4.43; p = 0.04.

Clinical analysis (STEMI vs. NSTEMI) was performed in accordance with the Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction, as established by the European Society of Cardiology (ESC), American College of Cardiology (ACC), and American Heart Association (AHA). This classification is based on the underlying mechanism of myocardial injury. Within this framework, NSTEMI can be considered a less advanced, earlier stage of ischemic heart disease, which—if left untreated—may progress to STEMI. The relationship between OSA and myocardial infarction can be classified as Type 2 MI, secondary to an imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand. The primary mechanism involves increased myocardial oxygen demand relative to supply, and treatment focuses on correcting the underlying cause of oxygen deficiency.

All patients in the study underwent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) as part of standard care. Interventions involved the following vessels: left anterior descending artery (LAD) in 7 patients, circumflex artery (Cx) in 2 patients, right coronary artery (RCA) in 4 patients, and multivessel PCI in 12 patients. Standard pharmacological therapy included dual antiplatelet therapy. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy was considered an adjunctive treatment to prevent hypoxemia.

Our results demonstrated a significant correlation between OSA and both biomarker levels and ventricular functional parameters. Specifically, MB ranged from 25 to 435 ng/mL (mean 181.4 ± 115.0), and troponin (Tn) ranged from 0.05 to 15.5 ng/mL (mean 4.19 ± 4.25). These findings suggest that OSA may modulate the course of myocardial infarction. However, given the current study limitations, causal interpretation remains preliminary and warrants further investigation.

Moreover, all the patients included in the study with STEMI manifestation of acute coronary syndrome had severe OSA.( Fig. 5 ) In patients with NSTEMI without OSA, CKMB (41.7 ± 40.7) and troponin (0.84 ± 1.0) levels were lower compared to those with OSA (CKMB 87.2 ± 101.4, Tn 1.33 ± 1.09).

In the analyzed cohort, 100% of patients with STEMI exhibited severe OSA. However, due to the small subgroup size, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Wide confidence intervals reflect the uncertainty of the estimate, yet this observation highlights a potentially strong association between severe OSA and the occurrence of STEMI. These results are consistent with accumulating evidence supporting the significant role of severe OSA in the pathogenesis of acute coronary syndromes [14].

CPAP therapy

Out of 18 patients in whom OSA was diagnosed and who were referred to ambulatory pulmonology department during one week from admission, CPAP therapy was started in only 9 (50%) patients. In the remaining 9 (50%) patients, who were evaluated by the pulmonologist and qualified for CPAP treatment, CPAP therapy was not started, mainly due to their own belief that the diagnosed OSA is of little importance.

In patients treated with CPAP, the therapy resulted in significant decrease of OSA indices (AHI dropped in this group from 52.0 ± 18.6 to 5.0 ± 1.0; p = 0.001).

Influence of OSA and CPAP therapy on echocardiographic findings at 1-year follow-up

Echocardiography undertaken one year after myocardial infarction showed significant increase of LV ejection fraction (54.5 ± 7.0 vs. 49.6 ± 8.3 at inclusion to the study, p = 0.008).

In the whole observed group the parameters of RV structure and function did not change (RV diastolic diameter from 32.3 ± 5.6 to 32.4 ± 4.4 mm; TAPSE from 22.4 ± 4.2 to 23.2 ± 2.1 mm). However, in OSA patients who refused CPAP treatment slight further enlargement of RV diameter was seen (31.0 ± 5.2 to 33.1 ± 4.2, p – ns), while in those treated with CPAP, RV diameter slightly decreased (34.4 ± 6.1 to 33.6 ± 3.7, p – ns). The function of RV as measured by TAPSE improved only in patients treated with CPAP (CPAP: from 20.5 ± 3.3 to 22.3 ± 2.3, p = 0.04; no CPAP: 22.3 ± 4.7 to 23.6 ± 1.8; p – ns).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows: 1/ the high prevalence of OSA in patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome, 2/ the influence of OSA on right ventricle enlargement and dysfunction, 3/ the relationship between sleep apnea parameters and myocardial injury markers, 4/ low patient compliance with CPAP therapy, 5/ further post-myocardial RV enlargement in OSA patients who refused CPAP treatment, but the decrease of RV diameter in those treated with CPAP [1–5].

We have found OSA in 72% of the studied cohort, including 12 patients (48%) with severe sleep apnea. This aligns with previous data reporting a high comorbidity of OSA and cardiovascular disease [7], as well as OSA and acute coronary syndromes [8, 15]. OSA has been linked to proinflammatory and prothrombotic factors associated with the development of atherosclerosis [16], as well as enhanced platelet activity and aggregation [17]. There is evidence that OSA is associated with an increased risk of adverse events after ACS [8]. Furthermore, animal trials demonstrated that exposure to chronic intermittent hypoxia (CIH) after myocardial infarction exacerbates pathophysiological remodeling and heart failure as compared to animals in a normoxic environment [18, 19]. This emphasizes the importance of routine screening for OSA in cardiac patients to help reduce cardiovascular risk and the negative consequences of cardiovascular events. On the other hand we still lack controlled trials investigating the effectiveness of OSA treatment in primary and secondary cardiovascular prevention.

We believe that our finding that patients with OSA demonstrate higher myocardial injury markers during an acute coronary event is of great interest and may have significant prognostic impact. This suggests that OSA may aggravate ischaemic injury. This statement is supported by earlier studies showing that patients with higher AHI have higher troponin release during ACS [20] and have a larger infarct size on cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging [21]. Notably, all patients with STEMI in our cohort had severe OSA, implying a possible association between OSA severity and the extent of myocardial injury. It has been confirmed in mice and rats that exposure to intermittent hypoxia increases myocardial sensitivity to ischemia [22, 23]. This is consistent with studies linking OSA to oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and a pro-thrombotic state which may increase the severity of ischemic episodes [4].

The association between OSA and elevated CK-MB and troponin levels is biologically plausible, given the intermittent hypoxia, oxidative stress, sympathetic activation, and enhanced inflammatory response characteristic of OSA. However, causal inference cannot be drawn from the present data.

The magnitude of myocardial necrosis biomarker release is also influenced by other clinical factors, including infarct size, infarct location and culprit vessel, time from symptom onset to reperfusion (door-to-balloon time), presence of multivessel disease, and renal function. These variables may act as potential confounders and should be considered when interpreting the results.

In our study patients with OSA presented with an enlarged right ventricle and mild reduction in its systolic function, as indicated by reduced TAPSE. These results are consistent with the pathophysiological understanding of OSA leading to increased pulmonary artery pressure, RV overload, and eventually RV dysfunction [6]. However, although previous research has shown the beneficial effect of CPAP therapy [24], our study did not demonstrate a significant influence of CPAP therapy on RV enlargement and function. This may be due to poor CPAP adherence. Poor CPAP compliance is common among patients and has been demonstrated in many prior studies [25]. Some previous papers have shown that MACE risk reduction in OSA depends on good long-term adherence. Furthermore, younger patients, patients with severe OSA, with a higher hypoxemic burden, and patients without overt cardiovascular end-organ disease seem to benefit more [26].

In patients with newly diagnosed OSA, sleep apnea–specific hypoxic burden, as determined from sleep recordings, has been identified as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events and mortality, with potential clinical applicability. In contrast to observations from population-based cohorts, symptom-based OSA clinical subtypes have not demonstrated independent predictive value in clinical trials.

Accumulating evidence further suggests that hypoxic burden, rather than the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) alone, correlates more strongly with the risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes [5, 27]. Focusing the analysis on desaturation parameters during the acute phase of myocardial infarction thus allowed evaluation of a pathophysiologically relevant component.

Notably, in our study, patients who declined CPAP treatment showed further RV enlargement and worsening of RV function – which was not observed in CPAP users, where a slight (statistically significant) improvement in systolic RV function was found.

The main limitations of this study are the small patient group and the observational design. Furthermore, echocardiographic assessments and AHI measurements were conducted at fixed intervals, which may have resulted in temporal variability being overlooked. The small number of patients willing to start CPAP treatment, as well as their poor adherence to it, indicates a need for enhanced patient education about OSA and its clinical significance. This also suggests the urgent need to find effective treatment options for this disorder.

Our study did not address the broader impact of sleep on cardiovascular disease. This complex relationship reflects multifactorial interactions between the cardiovascular system and sleep disturbances, encompassing psychological factors, clinical symptoms, and cardiometabolic aspects [12]. Since the American Heart Association (AHA) incorporated sleep into its “Life’s Essential 8” as one of eight fundamental elements defining optimal cardiovascular health, numerous reports have highlighted the importance of this relationship [28]. Sleep represents an important domain that warrants further investigation in future studies.

Overall, our findings highlight the importance of screening for sleep apnea in ACS patients, particularly given its high prevalence in ACS and the potential therapeutic benefits of early detection and treatment of OSA [1–5]. Future randomized trials with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the impact of OSA and CPAP treatment on cardiovascular outcomes and on cardiovascular prevention.
