Permeable void-free interface for all-solid-state alkali-ion polymer batteries
Authors: Wang Lyu, Hongwei Fu, Apparao M. Rao, Zhiyu Lu, Xinzhi Yu, Yue Lin, Jiang Zhou, Bingan Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
All-solid-state batteries suffer from a loss of contact between the electrode and electrolyte particles, leading to poor cyclability. Here, a void-free ion-permeable interface between the solid-state polymer electrolyte and electrode is constructed in situ during cycling using charge/discharge voltage as the stimulus. During the charge-discharge, the permeation phase fills the voids at the interface and penetrates the electrode, forming strong bonds with the cathode and effectively mitigating the contact problem. Our all-solid-state potassium ion polymer batteries maintain high Coulombic efficiency more than 2000 cycles at a high operating voltage of 4.5 volt and stably cycle more than 500 cycles even at 4.6 volt. Our rational design for mitigating the contact problem is versatile, as demonstrated by the scalability of all-solid-state graphite-based polymer potassium-ion pouch cells and all-solid-state lithium-ion polymer batteries.

INTRODUCTION

All-solid-state batteries are garnering considerable interest in energy storage applications due to their low cost, potentially high energy density, and good safety (1, 2). However, simultaneously achieving high ionic conductivity, high electrochemical voltage, and stable cycling remains challenging for all-solid-state electrolytes (SSEs) (3, 4). Meanwhile, one of the main challenges in attaining stable extended cycling of all solid state batteries arises from the inadequate SSE-electrode contact interfacial (5, 6). The loss of contact of SSEs-electrodes leads to insufficient ion transport and degradation of battery performance (7–9). Furthermore, interfacial instability, inhomogeneous space charge layers caused by incompatibility, and electrochemical decomposition at high voltages are currently the main problems at the cathode-SSEs interface (10, 11). In regard to the application of SSEs for all-solid-state batteries, the design of an suitable interface to achieve intimate contact between the electrode and the solid-state-electrolyte is an imperative criterion (12). Therefore, simultaneously improving the compatibility of the cathode-electrolyte contact interface, constructing cathode-side efficient ion transport channels, and addressing the stability at high voltage (>4.5 V) are very important for a battery.

Recently, several studies have reported that stimulus-responsive polymers can undergo changes leading to a shift in chain conformation, molecular structure, or phase transformation, giving them unique forms (polymer gels or solid phases) and functionalities (13, 14). To enhance the cycling performance of all-solid-state polymer batteries at high voltages, it is desirable to seek a suitable all-solid-state polymer electrolyte design strategy, focusing on the interfacial ion transport properties related to cathode compatibility or permeability. Thus, stimulus-induced phase transitions in polymers may affect the interfacial structure, creating an effective permeability that modulates ion transport and controls electrode and electrolyte decomposition. Here, we leveraged this unique property to develop a stimulus-responsive pregrafted solid polymer electrolyte (PGSPE) by blending polyvinylpyrrolidone iodine (PVPI) and polyvinyl oxide (PEO). Specifically, the PGSPE undergoes a phase transition in situ to generate a solid permeation phase (SPP) with ion-transporting channels when stimulated by voltage during charge/discharge cycling, forming a void-free SSE-electrode interface and effective permeable ion transporting networks inside the electrode (Fig. 1). Meanwhile, the electrochemical stabilization voltage of PGSPE improved because of the interaction of the grafted functional additive with PEO. The proposed mechanism of SPP ionic networks and high voltage stability is supported by fluorescence visualization and microscopic studies. The high voltage, high performance, and stable operation of PGSPE-based solid-state alkali-ion polymer batteries validate our strategy for void-free interfacial contact.

Conventional solid-state batteries exhibit voids at the cathode-SSE interface and the loss of contact between the electrode particles and SSE during cycling, making it difficult or impossible for ions to migrate.

A permeable 3D SSE with excellent bonding with the cathode is constructed by creating an SPP in situ from a PGSPE to fill the voids for a void-free interfacial contact.

RESULTS

PGSPE fabrication and electrochemical performance

We combined solution-induced phase separation with hybrid grafted polymer formation. The PGSPE contains PEO, PVPI, and bis(fluorosulfonyl)imide potassium salt (KFSI), which was constructed by the conventional solution casting volatilization method (fig. S1). The effect of different PEO/KFSI molar ratio (EO/K; EO/K ratio = 20, 16, 10, 5) on the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte was also investigated, where an EO/K ratio of 10:1 was determined to be optimal (fig. S2 and table S1). Both PVPI and PEO have excellent solubility in acetonitrile (ACN), which makes phase separation and cografting feasible. The ultraviolet (UV)–visible spectrum, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) were used to verify the successful fabrication of PGSPE. Specifically, the iodide ions (I− and I3−) shown in the UV-visible absorption spectrum (fig. S3) are derived from the release of iodine in PVPI. Iodine ions are immobilized in PGSPE during its formation, which is beneficial for improving ion conductivity (15, 16). The vibrational modes of characteristic bands coexistence of the C─O─C and C═O of the PGSPE confirm the cohybridization of PEO and PVP (fig. S4). Meanwhile, the C═O group in PVP provides favorable complexation sites for metal cations and promotes smooth ionic conduction (17). The PEO and PVP phases have interconnected network structures (grafted phase; fig. S5), which is attributed to their compatibility during cohybridization. This grafted phase enhances the PEO amorphous structural domains, possibly forming more efficient ionic conducting pathways in PGSPE (18, 19). These results indicate that the I−, I3−, and PVP are successfully grafted onto the PEO to generate PGSPE with functional groups. The addition of PVPI effectively increased the ion conductivity of pure PEO-KFSI electrolyte through this method, with PGSPE–10% PVPI exhibiting the best ion conductivity (Fig. 2A and fig. S6). SSEs with high ion conductivity are beneficial for achieving stable ion transport and ensuring the normal operation of batteries. The temperature dependence of ionic conductivities for PEO-KFSI and PGSPE (10% PVPI) showed that PGSPE has higher ion conductivity (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, the ion transfer number is important for the polymer electrolyte to achieve stable ion transport, and PGSPE has enhanced ion transference numbers relative to PEO-KFSI (figs. S7 and S8).

The cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of the corresponding matched Prussian blue (PB) cathode materials and potassium metal show that the PEO-KFSI electrolyte cannot achieve normal ion transport. In contrast, PGSPE exhibits a complete redox peak, indicating that it is capable of normal ion migration and good matching with PB (Fig. 2C). Meanwhile, potassium symmetric batteries using PGSPE exhibit good reversible plating/stripping for potassium metals, maintaining stable cycling and low polarization (Fig. 2D). This is attributed to the inhibition of potassium dendrites by I3− ions, as demonstrated in our previous work using an iodinated solid polymer electrolyte (ISPE) (16). Specifically, PVPI can form I3− ions in PGSPE (fig. S3), and the unique reaction of I3− with alkali metals can inhibit dendrites (fig. S9). The scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of potassium metal surfaces cycled with PGSPE electrolytes show no evidence for potassium dendrites. However, obvious potassium dendrites were formed on the surface of potassium metal using the PEO-KFSI electrolyte (fig. S10). Benefiting from the reaction of I3− ions with alkali metals, an iodide interface is formed at the interface of PGSPE and potassium metal, which can inhibit dendrite formation. The formation of the iodinated interface was verified by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) and x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy on the potassium metal surface (figs. S11 and S12), while the formation of KI was verified by transmission electron microscope (TEM; fig. S13), illustrating the potassium dendrites inhibition by I3− ions, playing a key role in stabilizing the anodic interface. On the contrary, the potassium symmetric batteries using PEO-KFSI electrolyte have an extremely short lifetime and large polarization, even short-circuiting early during cycling or being unable to cycle. In addition, the cycling performances of potassium symmetric cells with PEO-KFSI polymer electrolytes (EO/K ratio = 20, 16, 10, 5) are also provided (fig. S14). Unfortunately, PEO-KFSI polymer electrolytes with different EO/K ratios exhibit poor potassium-symmetric cycling performance, with voltage oscillations and short circuits leading to failure early in during cycling. PGSPE has higher ionic conductivity and stability to potassium metals relative to pure PEO-KFSI, which enables it to be used in potassium ion (metal) batteries. Maintaining stability over long periods while operating at high voltages is critical to battery stability. The constant voltage polarization curve demonstrates that PGSPE remains stable at 4.2 V, providing enhanced voltage stability compared to the pure PEO-KFSI, which exhibits an unstable leakage current at only 3.6 V (fig. S15).

PB analogs as cathode materials exhibit excellent performance and are widely used in batteries. Therefore, we chose this classic material as the cathode for polymer electrolytes (20–23). The all-solid-state potassium ion batteries using PB as the cathode and potassium metal as the anode were fabricated to examine the electrochemical performance of PEO-KFSI and PGSPE electrolytes. The voltage profiles showed that the cells with PEO-KFSI electrolyte suffered obvious, sustained voltage oscillations, and high polarization was observed (Fig. 2E and fig. S16). These results suggest the continuous decomposition of PEO-KFSI leading to overcharging and micro short-circuit failures (the cutoff voltage is only 4.2 V) and produced a high interfacial resistance with the electrode material; it also had a low and extremely unstable coulombic efficiency (fig. S17A). The in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) data showed that the PGSPE cells exhibited lower and more stable impedance than the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte (fig. S18). Meanwhile, the battery with PGSPE exhibits a perfect voltage distribution curve (cutoff voltages at 4.2, 4.5, and 4.6 V) and still maintains a low polarization at high voltage. Encouraged by this high voltage stability, the cycle recovery performance of PGSPE was verified by setting different rates (charge cutoff voltage of 4.5 V). Specifically, the voltage profile of the cell shows a perfect voltage distribution curve at different rates while having a low polarization, indicating that the PGSPE cells have excellent cycling performance and low cell resistance at different rates (fig. S17B). The cell exhibits an excellent rate capability and high capacity when cycled at 0.05, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, and 1 C (2.4 to 4.5 V), delivering high specific capacities of 143.6, 126.5, 115.7, 101.5, and 84.5 mAh g−1, respectively, along with good rate cycling recovery (fig. S17C).

The PEO-KFSI cell under long cycling exhibits a low and markedly decreasing capacity and ultimately ineffective charge/discharge (virtually no capacity, cutoff voltage of only 4.2 V; fig. S19). In contrast, the PGSPE cell exhibits a stable cycling capability at a high charge cutoff voltage of 4.5 V (fig. S20) with a high initial capacity of 113.28 mAh g−1. It maintains a stable high Coulombic efficiency for more than 200 cycles at 0.2 C (84% capacity retention). Meanwhile, the high voltage and long cycle stability have also been validated at 4.5 V. The cell using PGSPE achieved 2000 stable cycles at 0.5 C while maintaining high Coulombic efficiency and capacity retention (70%), with a decay rate of only 0.015% per cycle (Fig. 2F). Particularly, PGSPE exhibits excellent stability at a high voltage of 4.6 V, as evidenced in the high capacity retention of 73% more than 500 cycles with high Coulombic efficiency (Fig. 2G and fig. S21A). The minimal decay of the median voltage throughout cycling also demonstrates excellent stability at high voltages. The charge-discharge curves exhibit low polarization and imply that PGSPE is more conducive to promoting excellent interfacial behavior (fig. S21B). This high stability at high voltages enables PB to deliver initial capacities up to 134.3 mAh g−1 at high cutoff voltages.

A polymer pouch cell was assembled using PB and potassium metal foil with the PGSPE (2.5 to 4.5 V) to verify the scalability. There was no obvious capacity fading more than 120 cycles at 0.5 C, and it maintains a high and stable Coulombic efficiency (Fig. 2H). The pouch cell using organic liquid electrolyte suffered a severe thermal runaway by continuously exposing air, along with intense combustion without direct contact with an ignition source (Fig. 2I). No obvious thermal runaway behavior occurred in the solid-state cell with continued exposure to air. Because of the immobility and nonvolatility of the solid-state polymer electrolyte, the cell did not burn without direct contact with an ignition source (Fig. 2J). Therefore, PGSPE’s permeable SPP strategy has been shown to be effective in increasing the charge voltage and extending the battery’s efficient and stable cycle life.

In situ 3D permeable SPP network validation

In our design strategy, PGSPE is used as a molecular repository and SPP as a releasing molecule to realize the release of SPPs in the source layer through voltage change during charge/discharge. The continuous voltage change stimulates a macroscopic phase transition (manifested as phase separation) of PGSPE, ultimately in situ forming a transporting network by the SPP that effectively improves the electrolyte-electrode interface. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) is a good example of its compatibility, solubility, and film-forming properties. Carbonyl groups in the PVP structure provide excellent grafting matrices for complex functional components. Work has been reported on the ability of PEO and PVP to form crosslinked polymers (19, 24–26). Our work identified a unique ability of PEO-PVP crosslinked polymers to release SPP under voltage (fig. S22). We illustrate the behavior of PGSPE voltage-stimulated release of SPP by observing changes in the surface micromorphology of PGSPE after constant voltage polarization (fig. S23A). Specifically, an effective voltage stimulus could not be formed at voltages lower than 3 V, and the PGSPE surface showed no noticeable changes relative to the original PGSPE (fig. S23, B and C). When the voltage reached 3 V, noticeable holes appeared on the PGSPE surface due to the release of SPP (fig. S24, A to C). In addition, the PGSPE surface showed a three-dimensional (3D) SPP polymer network under sustained high voltage (fig. S24, D and E). Therefore, stimulation at voltages higher than 3 V induced the formation of SPP and 3D interconnected polymer networks in PGSPE. A finite element modeling was used to elucidate the influence of SPP penetration on the ion transport at the cathode side. First, the SPP effectively penetrated between the cathode particles, filling the interfacial voids (fig. S25A). Meanwhile, the evolution of the ion transport behavior inside the PB cathode particles was investigated.The presence of voids results in inhomogeneous ion transport at the interface with efficient ion transport paths only at the cathode-electrolyte contact areas (fig. S25B). On the contrary, the infiltrated SPP constructs ion transporting paths between the cathode particles for efficient and uniform ion transport (fig. S25C). Further, density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed that the SPP has enhanced ionic interactions with the cathode than PEO, allowing the SPP’s stronger bonding with the cathode to favoring the formation of a void-free interface. Meanwhile, the K+ ion transport paths and corresponding diffusion energy barriers in the interface indicate that K+ ion transport is more readily available at the SPP-cathode direct-contact interface relative to the interface with void (fig. S26).

Laser confocal microscopy is capable of imaging fluorescence in three dimensions as an effective imaging tool. Rhodamine B is a common fluorescent staining reagent that has been widely used in biology, materials, and medicine (27–29). We used a simple and effective fluorescent labeling method to visualize the 3D formation of the permeable SPP ion network in situ (rhodamine B–labeled PGSPE; fig. S27). Specifically, the fluorescence of rhodamine B on the cathode surface was detected after charge/discharge using rhodamine B–labeled PGSPE assembly batteries (Fig. 3A and fig. S28). The 3D fluorescence response in different cross-sectional regions also demonstrates the ability of the SPP network to fill voids at the electrolyte-electrode interface (Fig. 3B). The variation of fluorescence intensity reveals the 3D properties of the SPP network to some extent. For example, in fluorescence intensity analysis of the selected 3D cross sections (Fig. 3, C and D), the strong fluorescence intensity is attributed to the SPP network in the filled voids, indicating that the SPP network tends to aggregate at the voids in the cathode effectively. Next, we investigated the cathode surface and cross section after cycling by SEM. The SEM and corresponding 3D reconstruction display that SPP fills the voids on the cathode surface in situ and forms an interconnected permeable network under a sustained voltage (Fig. 3, E and F). The gradient distribution of EDS components in the contact area between SPP and cathode indicates the effectiveness of this permeation network (fig. S29). Meanwhile, cross-sectional SEM and corresponding 3D reconstruction showed that SPP (spherical polymer) infiltrated from the PGSPE side to the cathode surface, effectively filling the voids and exhibiting a uniform and smooth distribution at the interface (Fig. 3, G and H, and fig. S30). Compared to the pristine cathode with a sparse and porous inhomogeneous surface (Fig. 3I and fig. S31), the cycled cathode surface using PGSPE exhibits a smooth and continuous planar polymer structure attributed to the formation of the SPP. However, the cycled cathode surface using the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte still showed a structure similar to the pristine cathode surface. It is noteworthy that the phenomenon of SPP penetration into the cathode occurs similarly in cells with graphite as the anode. Using graphite negative electrode can effectively avoid the effect of potassium dendrites and highlight the protective role of SPP in filling voids and maintaining good interfacial contact on the cathode side (fig. S32). Meanwhile, we calculated the porosity of the cathode by ImageJ (30–32) after cycling with PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte and PGSPE. The porosity of the cathodes after cycling with the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte was 27.3%. Benefiting from the unique permeability, SPP effectively fills the cathode voids, exhibiting a reduced porosity of 5.9% after cycling with PGSPE (fig. S33).

Constructing a tight cathode-solid-state polymer electrolytes interface plays an important role in the stable cycling of batteries. As discussed, a permeable 3D solid-state polymer electrolytes with excellent bonding with the cathode is constructed by creating SPP in situ from the PGSPE to fill the voids for a void-free interfacial contact. Cross-sectional SEM analyses of the cathode-SSE interfaces after cycling show that the cross section formed with PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte contains voids between PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte and PB. In contrast, the SPP effectively improved the interfacial voids and penetrated the cathode, establishing a direct permeable contact between the cathode and solid-state polymer electrolyte. In other words, a void-free contact interface was formed (Fig. 3J and fig. S34). In particular, when the cathode exhibits an uneven structure (which creates voids at the interface), the SPP forms a complementary structure in close contact with the cathode, effectively eliminating voids. Notably, this phenomenon also exists between the cathode particles, which form a continuous permeable ion-transporting network. The ion transporting network formation at the cathode accompanies the evolution of the SPP during cycling.

The TEM imaging and EDS chromatography (Fe is the cathode’s characteristic element, and S is SPP’s characteristic element) of the cathode-SSE contact interface after cycling provided vivid descriptions of the direct permeable contact between the cathode and solid-state polymer electrolyte (Fig. 3K). The results show that SPP permeated the cathodes to form interconnected, tightly connected structures, and K element is uniformly present in the SPP, cathode, and cathode-SPP interface, implying efficient ion transport network behavior. The spatial correlation between the SPP network and the cathode was described by 3D TEM imaging and EDS chromatography (Fig. 3L and fig. S35). The results show that the cathode and the SPP are interconnected in 3D, forming an interactive network, and they demonstrate the spatial correlation between the ionic network of the solid permeable phase and the cathode. Further cross-sectional segmentation analysis of the 3D chemical composition shows the interpenetration state of the characteristic elements, providing a more intuitive illustration of an effective SPP ion network (fig. S36). In conclusion, the in situ constructed SPP ion transporting network suppresses the cathode-electrolyte interface instability that may be caused by contact degradation, which is important for long, stable cycling of solid-state batteries.

Study of PGSPE high stability voltage

Electrochemical stabilization voltage has been a key factor limiting polymer electrolytes’ use in high-voltage battery systems. It is very important to measure the electrochemical stabilization voltage when investigating the characteristics of the electrolyte in high-voltage battery systems. Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves show that PGSPE has an electrochemical stabilization voltage of up to 5 V, whereas the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte has only 3.6 V, with a large current change starting at 3.1 V (Fig. 4A). Considering the sluggish kinetics of the polymer electrolyte, only a very small amount of current is yielded. We amplify the redox current due to electrolyte decomposition by mixing the electrolyte with an electronically conductive material (33, 34). The redox current was obviously amplified in this way, and the PEO-KFSI electrolyte suffered an obvious current fluctuation around 3.1 V. Notably, PGSPE did not show obvious current fluctuations, indicating its enhanced electrochemical stability (fig. S37).

The surface potentials of different electrolytes were compared according to kelvin probe force microscope (KPFM; fig. S38, A and B), PGSPE has a lower and more uniform surface potential distribution. The surface potential of the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte has a high surface potential distribution range (70 to 125 mV), which is much higher than that of the PGSPE (20 to 70 mV). The low surface potential facilitates the construction of a stable interface by reducing the interfacial potential and promoting ion migration (35–37). Meanwhile, the surface potential distribution in different regions is more uniform for PGSPE, and the PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte showed an inhomogeneous potential distribution (Fig. 4B). Therefore, we deduce that a lower and more uniform surface potential might be one of the causes of PGSPE’s high stability voltage. Further analysis suggests that the inhomogeneous potential distribution of the PEO-KFSI electrolyte may be attributed to the surface defects (agglomerated KFSI crystalline particles), which results in a obviously local higher surface potential than that in other areas (fig. S38, C and D). This local high potential may also affect the space charge layer distribution at the interface (35, 38). The inhomogeneous surface potential may lead to an inhomogeneous distribution of the space charge layer at the interface, while localized high-potential regions may induce the formation of high electric field regions (fig. S38E). This behavior may increase electric field strength at the defects, forming partial discharges and leading to electrolyte decomposition or even overcharging breakdown and short circuits (39, 40). In short, PGSPE has a more uniform charge distribution and electric field, promoting a uniform voltage distribution, which means better interfaces and excellent high voltage stability during charge/discharge.

An oscilloscope system is constructed to measure the voltage response of a symmetric cell to illustrate the space charge layer’s distribution by the voltage change (Fig. 4C). The PEO-KFSI polymer electrolyte had sustained voltage oscillations throughout the process and large voltage differences (48 and 68 mV) relative to the PGSPE (8 and 12 mV) (Fig. 4D). This phenomenon may be attributed to the voltage response triggered by the defects (KFSI crystalline particles) on the PEO-KFSI surface (41). This suggested that the PEO-KFSI surface has an inhomogeneous charge layer, which may be detrimental to the high voltage stability and form an ion transport interfacial barrier (11, 42–44). Furthermore, the release behavior of the space charge layer was investigated. During the charge release process, PGSPE has a high charge release efficiency of 93.3%, obviously higher than that of PEO-KFSI with only 53.8% (fig. S39). Therefore, these results indicate that PGSPE not only has a stable and uniform space charge layer but also has a good charge release capability. This may also be responsible for the high voltage and ion transport stability of PGSPE, enabling it to promote stable interfacial compatibility and inhibit the decomposition of the cathode and cathode-solid state polymer electrolyte interfaces at high voltages.

By analyzing the surface potential distribution for the original and cycled cathodes (Fig. 4E and fig. S40), the voids cause the surface of the original cathode to show an obvious inhomogeneous distribution of surface potential. The cycled cathode has a lower and uniform surface potential distribution due to the SPP filling the voids. These results indicate that the SPP builds a stable interfacial contact with the cathode, favoring ion migration and achieving high voltage stability (45, 46), which can effectively suppress the decomposition of the SSE, cathode, and the electrolyte-cathode interface at high voltage. In addition, the stress due to volumetric deformation of the electrode during cycling is also a factor that affects the stability of the solid-state polymer electrolyte-electrode interface (47, 48). The nanomechanical property tests (Fig. 4, F to H) verified that the cycled cathode has superior viscoelastic, viscoplastic, and creep properties compared to the original cathode. This maintains good interfacial contact between solid-state polymer electrolyte and the electrode during cycling to preserve the battery cycling performance (49). This indicates that the SPP has sufficient compliance and ductility to accommodate the volume expansion of the cathode during charging and discharging, which is conducive to alleviating the stresses and strains induced by volume changes (50).

Scalability of all-solid-state polymer pouch cells

Graphite is considered to be one of the most promising anode materials. However, its use in batteries is still challenging due to active ion depletion, leading to severe energy density decay and low Coulombic efficiency. Hence, we developed a simple, efficient, and scalable pre-potassiation method. The complete system consists of an atomization system, a conveyor system, and a drying system (Fig. 5, A and B). The prepared solid-state potassium ion polymer pouch cells demonstrated stable cycling capability. The detailed all-solid-state polymer pouch cell preparation process as well as key materials and equipment are detailed in figs. S41 to S43. The graphite-based pouch cells (single layer, double layer, and three layer) exhibit almost perfect voltage curves and excellent stability, long cycle life, high capacity retention rate, and high stable Coulombic efficiency (Fig. 5, C and D). In addition, a graphite-based all-solid-state potassium-ion battery using PGSPE achieved stable cycling with a PB cathode loading of 6 mg/cm2 (fig. S44). However, achieving normal cycling of all-solid-state Li-ion battery for highly loaded cathodes based on SSEs remains challenging. Benefiting from PVPI additives enhances ionic conductivity and electrochemical stability of PEO-based polymer electrolytes. In addition, the unique SPP permeability builds ion channels at the cathode, which is important for achieving stable cycling at highly loaded cathodes in all-solid-state Li-ion battery. Therefore, to explore the feasibility of this strategy in all-solid-state Li-ion batteries, we introduced PVPI into the widely studied PEO-based polymer electrolyte–constructed Li-PGSPE (good ionic conductivity to ensure the normal working of the battery; fig. S45) for Li-ion batteries. The all-solid-state battery matched with a high loading LiFePO4 cathode was able to achieve a capacity of 17 mAh at 0.5 C (Fig. 5E), verifying that this unique permeability of PGSPE facilitates the use of highly loaded cathodes in solid-state battery systems. Meanwhile, the all-solid-state pouch cell’s safety was verified by testing in air. The cell remained operational even without an aluminum-plastic film to isolate it from air (Fig. 5F).

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we designed and developed an in situ SPP-based electrolyte with an ion transporting network on the cathode side, which successfully mitigates the conventional cathode-electrolyte interfacial issues with poor connectivity. It also has high electrochemical stability voltage and can achieve stable cycling at 4.5 and 4.6 V. Last, a scaled-up manufacturing design for polymer pouch cells based on the pre-potassiation strategy is presented, which validated the high performance and stable operation of PGSPE-based all-solid-state potassium-ion polymer pouch cells. We hope this rational SSE design strategy can be used widely as an interfacial modification strategy for various solid-state polymer batteries because of its unique permeability and high voltage stability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Preparation of electrolytes

The electrolyte was prepared in an argon gas-filled glove box. A certain proportion of PEO (average Mv ~600,000, powder; Aladdin) and KFSI (99.0%; SongJing) was weighed and dissolved in ACN solution and stirred for 24 hours at 60°C obtain PEO-KFSI solutions. The preparation of PEO-KFSI-PVPI solutions took the same approach and was obtained by adding different mass fractions of PVPI [weight % (wt.% = 5, 10, 15, and 20)] to the PEO-KFSI solution. Next, the PEO-KFSI and PEO-KFSI-PVPI solutions were doctor blade-casted on polytetrafluoroethylene mold in the glove box and left overnight to dry naturally. Last, the residual solvent was removed by evaporation in a vacuum oven (60°C for 12 hours) to obtain PEO-KFSI SPE and PGSPE. Li-PGSPE was used for lithium-ion battery preparation. The same methods were used to prepare PEO18-LiTFSI (EO/Li = 18:1) and PGSPE where PEO-LiTFSI was used as substrate and 10% PVPI was added as additive.

Preparation of PB cathode material

First, obtain 160 ml of K4Fe(CN)6·3H2O (99%; Aladdin) and 40 ml of FeCl3 (99.9%; Aladdin) (molar ratio 1:2) solution and stir continuously for 2 hours. Then, slowly drop the FeCl3 solution into the K4Fe (CN)6 solution through a separating funnel, maintain high-speed stirring throughout the process, continue stirring for 2 hours, and then age for 24 hours. The precipitate was centrifuged three times with ionized water and anhydrous ethanol and dried under vacuum at 80°C for 24 hours. Last, PB is obtained by grinding cathode material powder and placed in a glove box for preservation.

Preparation of electrodes

Active material, Super P as a conductive additive, and polyvinylidene fluoride as a binder were dried under a vacuum. They were weighed in a weight ratio of 6:2:2 and mixed with N-methylpyrrolidone solvent for ball milling for 6 hours to obtain a homogeneous PB cathode mixture slurry. Then, the slurry is coated on the current collector Al@C foil through a coating machine and dried at 120°C. Last, the cathode is dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 hours and placed in a glove box for later use (the loading density of the PB cathodes were 1.2 to 1.5 mg cm−2). The preparation of graphite anode adopts the same method using graphite, Super P as a conductive additive, and carboxymethyl cellulose as a binder; weighed in a weight ratio of 8:1:1; mixed with a solvent of ethanol and water; and ball milled for 6 hours to obtain a uniform graphite anode mixture slurry. Then, the slurry is coated onto the current collector copper foil using a coating machine and dried at 120°C. Last, the anode is dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 hours and placed in a glove box for future use (the loading density of the graphite anodes were 1.5 to 1.8 mg cm−2). The specifications of the battery material used in the experiment are mainly divided into coin cells and pouch cells. The cathode and potassium anode for coin cells are circular coupons with 14 and 16 mm in diameter, respectively. For pouch cells, the specifications of cathode plates, potassium anode plates, and graphite anode plates are 40 mm by 50 mm, 40 mm by 50 mm, and 45 mm by 55 mm, respectively.

Preparation of LiFePO4 cathode

The LiFePO4 material was purchased from Guangdong Candlelight New Energy Technology Co. Active material, Super P as a conductive additive, and polyvinylidene fluoride as a binder were dried under a vacuum. They were weighed in a weight ratio of 8:1:1 and mixed with N-methylpyrrolidone solvent for ball milling for 6 hours to obtain a homogeneous LiFePO4 cathode mixture slurry. Then, the slurry is coated on the current collector Al@C foil through a coating machine and dried at 120°C. Last, the cathode is dried in a vacuum oven at 80°C for 24 hours and placed in a glove box for later use (the loading density of the LiFePO4 cathodes were ~10 mg cm−2).

Preparation of potassiated graphite anode

Conventional 1 M KFSI@EC/DEC electrolyte was used as the pre-potassiation solution, and the solution was uniformly sprayed on the graphite anode by a spray system. Then, the fresh potassium foil was flatly laminated on the surface of the graphite anode and left for 4 hours to complete the pre-potassiation process. Last, the potassiated graphite anode was transferred to a drying system to remove the residual solution.

Preparation of pouch cells

The cathode sheet is cut into 40 mm by 50 mm and welded with Al-tab. The K metal sheet and graphite (K thickness of ~230 μm, graphite thickness of ~23 μm) is prepared in a glove box, cut to 40 mm by 50 mm, and welded with Ni-tab. The PGSPE is 60 mm by 70 mm (thickness of ~100 μm) ensure that the cell does not make contact and cause a short circuit. The cathode, electrolyte, and K metal (or graphite) form a sandwich structure and are encapsulated in an aluminum plastic film (80 mm by 90 mm). Using double-sided coated electrodes (PB cathode and potassiated graphite anode), multilayer pouch cells were prepared using a stacking process. A holding pressure mold was used to obtain the appropriate pressure to ensure that the ASSB pouch cell has stable interfacial contact.

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical properties of the electrolyte were tested using CHI600E systems and the battery performance testing using the Neware battery test system (BTSCT-3008-TC 5.X. Shenzhen. China). All tests were performed by maintaining a constant temperature (50°C). The ionic conductivity of the electrolytes was calculated from the EIS data obtained using EIS with SS||electrolyte||SS symmetric cells at the desired temperatures. The corresponding EIS was measured in the 105 to 0.1 Hz range with an amplitude of 5 mV. Electrochemical stability voltage was measured using K||SPE||SS cells by LSV from 2 to 6 V at a scan rate of 0.1 mV s−1. The K||SPE||K symmetric cells were used to measure galvanostatic voltage profiles and stability to K metal. The PB||SPE||K cells were used to measure the stability under high voltage in electrochemical floating experiments. For the rate cycling performance, PB||PGSPE||K cells were cycled between 2.4 and 4.5 V (PB||PEO-KFSI||K cells were cycled between 2.4 and 4.2 V) with rates ranging from 0.05 to 1 C. The long cycle performance was tested at 0.2 and 0.5 C and measured at different voltage intervals (2.4 to 4.5 V, 2.5 to 4.5 V, and 2.5 to 4.6 V). For pouch cell charge/discharge tests, PB/PGSPE/potassium graphite cells were cycled between 2 and 4.2 V at 0.5 C.

Material characterization

Atomic force microscopy (AFM, Bruker, Dimension Icon) characterized the surface morphology of materials, and the corresponding KPFM mode characterized the surface potential of materials. The FTIR spectra of samples were obtained by a VERTEX 70 spectrometer. The UV-visible spectrum (RAYLEIGH, UV2100) was used to record the I3− and I− signals. Under ambient conditions, the SPE was placed between two glass slides for solid UV spectroscopy (UV-2600 SHIMADZU). SEM and corresponding EDX analysis (SEM, JEOL JSM 6380LV FESEM) were used to characterize the morphologies and microstructures of samples. A standard lift-off procedure was used to prepare the samples, and the samples were cut using a scalpel and fixed on a vacuum transfer sample stage in a glove box to avoid exposure to water and oxygen. The sample’s fluorescence was detected using a laser scanning confocal microscope (ZEISS, LSM980) and analyzed using corresponding data processing software (ZEISS ZEN 3.9).

DFT calculations and finite element simulations

The Gaussian 16 C.01 software was used to perform quantum chemistry calculations. Low spin Fe2+ complexes’ binding energy differences were calculated using hybrid functional TPSSh and def2-TZVP basis sets (51, 52), with the basis set superposition error being corrected using the counterpoise approach. Hirshfeld partitioning technique [Independent Gradient Model based on Hirshfeld partition (IGMH) method] and Multiwfn (53) were used in independent gradient model computations (54). Visual molecular dynamics was used to visualize surfaces (55). Calculations of potassium ion transport were performed using the climbing image-nudged elastic band technique (56) and the Vienna ab initio simulation package (57). The ionic cores were described using the projector augmented wave pseudopotential, and the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) exchange-correlation function with a 520-eV kinetic energy cutoff was described using the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof formulation (58). The Cl-NEB computations used gamma-point sampling. On the basis of previously obtained data, the GGA + U technique was used to address the localization of the d-orbital in the high spin Fe and low spin Fe, with Ueff values of 7.0 and 3.0 eV, respectively (59). With a width of 0.05 eV, the Gaussian smearing method was used, and the convergence criterion for force and energy were 0.03 eV/Å and 10−6 eV per cell, respectively. The impact of van der Waals interactions was examined using Grimme’s DFT-D4 method (60, 61).

The finite element simulation uses commercial COMSOL software. The infiltration process used built-in module phase field and electrostatic field modules. The upper boundary of the electric field is grounded, and a voltage of 3 V is applied to the lower boundary. The PVP particles are initially spherical in the phase field, wherein the velocity field is associated with the electric field. The ion flux adopts built-in modules for dilute material transfer and electrostatic field modules. The concentration boundary of the dilute substance is set with a lower boundary inflow of 1 mol/m3, an upper boundary outlet, and an electric field with an upper boundary grounded. A voltage of 3 V is applied to the lower boundary, and the velocity field of the dilute substance is associated with the electric field.
