Structural behaviour of geopolymer concrete beams reinforced with bamboo bars
Authors: Masakazu Terai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal warming has emerged as a critical environmental issue. The primary cause is carbon dioxide (CO2), and the construction industry is responsible for significant CO2 emissions during the production of cement and steel. In recent years, there has been an increasing trend toward manufacturing methods that do not utilize steel or cement. In this study, we investigate the structural performance of “bamboo-reinforced concrete,” which employs bamboo reinforcement in place of steel bars and combines it with geopolymer concrete that does not require cement. Although this technology is based on the use of natural and rustic materials, it is anticipated that such nature-based solutions may be recognized as leading-edge technologies in the future. To explore the structural performance of bamboo-reinforced concrete, beam-shaped specimens were fabricated and subjected to flexural tests. The experimental results revealed the following: (1) Geopolymer concrete was trial-produced using glass powder as a binder. The results confirmed that it is possible to produce geopolymer concrete with a setting time exceeding 2 h and a 28-day compressive strength of 15 N/mm2 or more. (2) Due to the lower bond strength of bamboo reinforcement, the failure mode of bamboo-reinforced concrete beams was found to differ from that of steel-reinforced concrete beams. (3) The strength of bamboo-reinforced concrete beam members could be accurately evaluated using values calculated from existing steel-reinforced concrete design equations. However, it was also confirmed that strength evaluation considering bond strength is necessary for bamboo-reinforced concrete beams.Keywords: Geopolymer concrete, Bamboo, Beam, Flexural load test, Waste glass powder, BondSubject terms: Civil engineering, Composites

Introduction

Recently, environmental issues have attracted increasing amounts of attention. There is no doubt that a major cause of global warming is CO2, which has been rapidly emitted by humans since the Industrial Revolution. Since a large amount of fossil fuels are used in the production of steel, production may be restricted in the future for the reduction in carbon dioxide emissions due to environmental issues. Furthermore, the cement used in concrete is known to emit a large amount of CO2 during manufacturing. Although the demand for cement in developed countries has plateaued, the increase in cement production and usage in emerging countries in recent years shows that it will be necessary to develop alternative materials if CO2 emissions during cement production or the use of cement itself cannot be reduced,. For these reasons, although reinforced concrete is a composite material of steel and concrete, reinforced concrete structures are not good for the environment because large amounts of CO2 are emitted during the manufacturing of steel and cement.

Bamboo-reinforced concrete (BRC)

Concrete is prone to cracking under external forces, such as heat, dryness, and earthquakes, due to its low tensile strength. Thus, concrete structures are usually reinforced by steel or other materials to reduce cracks (reinforced concrete structures, fibre-reinforced concrete, etc.). Because steel prices are soaring, there are many areas in the world where concrete structures that originally required reinforcement have not been sufficiently reinforced. Therefore, the development of structures that do not use steel materials is meaningful for the future. Reinforced concrete structures without steel bars are being developed. Other materials for reinforcing bars include stainless steel1 and fibre-reinforced polymers2–4, but although these materials have high strength and corrosion resistance, they are expensive. In recent years, natural materials such as bamboo have attracted attention as reinforcing materials5–7 because they are inexpensive, easy to obtain, and environmentally friendly.

Published accounts have indicated that the use of bamboo to reinforce concrete structures dates back a century in Southeast Asia. Early experimental studies of BRC were conducted at MIT in 19148, in Germany9 in the 1930s, and in the United States following the Second World War (WW2)10,11. In Japan, research on BRC was also actively conducted during this period, and two books were published12,13. Furthermore, under wartime conditions in Japan, concrete structures reinforced with bamboo were constructed as a substitute for reinforcing steel due to a shortage of steel, and parts of these structures still exist in various places. BRC structures were no longer built in Japan after the end of WW2, but BRC research has been reconsidered in response to the rapid development of infrastructure in Southeast Asia and other countries, and to the recent rise in steel prices and environmental issues.

In recent years, bamboo-reinforced concrete has attracted attention as an environmentally friendly material, but the following structural problems have been noted14–16. (1) Low adhesion, (2) variation in strength, (3) deterioration in alkaline environments, (4) effects of water absorption and expansion, (5) insufficient strength, (6) difficulty in processing, and (7) the need for corrosion prevention measures. To solve these problems, research is being conducted on the development of surface modification technology, standardization of bamboo materials, and improvement of mechanical fixing methods, but at the current stage, application is limited to small-scale civil engineering structures. For example, paved roads and bridges, or secondary concrete products such as curbs and drainage pipes.

There is a large variation in strength of bamboo because it is a natural and unstable material. Furthermore, the Young’s modulus of bamboo is lower than that of steel, so the deflection is large, localized cracks are likely to occur in the concrete; thus, an improvement in structural performance cannot be expected. Therefore, among the above problems, (1) low adhesion and (4) the effects of water absorption and expansion are serious problems that affect the structural performance of bamboo-reinforced concrete.

Low adhesion stress

Previous studies have noted that bamboo has a smooth surface and low adhesive strength17–19. Bamboo has nodes, so it has more of a snag than does round steel; however, it has an adhesive strength of only approximately 0.4 to 0.6 N/mm2 if it is not processed. Many processing methods have been proposed to improve the adhesive strength of bamboo. Cutting20, attaching metal fittings (hose clamps)21, wrapping wires22 have been proposed, and the adhesive strength of untreated bamboo has improved from 0.8 to approximately 1.3–3.0 by surface treatment. The author of23 also conducted research to improve the adhesive strength between bamboo bars and concrete by drilling holes in round bamboo and inserting multiple bamboo skewers to create an uneven surface. As a result, it was confirmed that bamboo skewer processing can result in high adhesive performance, so this processing method was also used in this study.

However, surface treatment may damage the bamboo surface or cause new weaknesses due to cross-sectional defects. Moreover, regardless of how much surface treatment is applied, the adhesive strength between the bamboo and the concrete is much lower than that between deformed rebar and concrete (1.4–2.0 N/mm2).

Expansion due to water and heat

During the pouring and curing of concrete, bamboo bars absorb water and expand. The bamboo expands and pushes the concrete aside. The bamboo then loses water and shrinks to almost its original dimensions at the end of the curing period, leaving a void around it. Additionally, the difference in the thermal expansion of bamboo relative to that of concrete may cause cracks in the concrete during use. The expansion and contraction of bamboo in concrete pose serious limitations to the use of bamboo as a steel substitute in concrete. Effective waterproofing treatment is required to improve the adhesion between the bamboo bars and concrete.

To address this issue, methods such as applying white paint, white lead24, epoxy resin25, rubber-based paint14, asphalt paint, and coal tar to the bamboo surface have been used. These surface treatments suppress water absorption, prevent expansion and relaxation, and contribute to improving adhesion to a certain extent. However, attention must be given to the environmental impact and health safety of the treatment agents applied. In addition, such treatments are expensive in terms of material costs and labour-intensive application, which can lead to high manufacturing costs.

Geopolymers

Geopolymers are solidified materials produced by the condensation polymerization reaction between an industrial byproduct containing aluminium silicate, which is called an active filler, and an alkaline solution; these materials were developed in the 1970s. Since they are relatively new materials still under research in many countries, various raw materials are being used for geopolymers, and as a result, the reaction products constituting cured samples greatly differ. Therefore, universal formulation design and strength management methods have not been established. However, since geopolymers do not use cement, they are being investigated as alternative materials to conventional cement concrete. Structures built with geopolymers in place of cement can reduce CO2 emissions by approximately 80%. If this figure is reliable, geopolymers should be considered as construction materials in the future26.

As mentioned above, there is a limit to improving the adhesive strength of bamboo by surface treatment. In contrast, attention has been focused on improving the irreducible strength by changing the concrete. Several researchers27–29 have confirmed that, compared with cement concrete, geopolymer concrete forms a dense interfacial transition zone (ITZ) around the reinforcement surface, which improves the bond strength. However, these experiments were performed on SD rods and FRP rods30–34, and only two studies on bamboo rods have been reported in the literature35,36. Awalluddin35 conducted pull-out tests to confirm the bond strength between bamboo bars and fly ash-based geopolymers. The bond strength of untreated bamboo was 0.80 for ordinary Portland cement (OPC) and 0.87 for geopolymer concrete (GPC). In addition, this study presented the bond strength of bamboo bars with and without surface treatments, such as nodes, notches, and wire wrapping, and showed that the use of bamboo strips in fly ash geopolymer concrete is feasible. Mahantesh36 reported the efficacy of binding wire-wrapped bamboo splints as reinforcements along with bamboo fibres in alkali-activated geopolymer concrete. The flexural behaviour of steel wire-wrapped bamboo splints as reinforcements in GPC beams provided valuable feedback on the use of bamboo as reinforcement and fibre. However, no comparison has been made with cement concrete, and the reinforcing effect of GPC bamboo bars is not clear. Luhar and Khandenwal37 confirmed that GPC has lower water absorption than does OPC and revealed that the occurrence of microcracks is suppressed because bamboo material, which has high water absorption, does not expand or loosen due to water absorption. Several studies have investigated GPCs with natural fibres as reinforcing materials38–42. There are many cases where GPC has improved strength compared with OPC, and although the mechanism of strength improvement is not clear, the combination of bamboo and GPC was found to be effective.

Purpose

Geopolymer concrete, a type of concrete that does not use cement, is being researched as an alternative material to conventional cement concrete. Bamboo-reinforced concrete, which uses bamboo, a natural material, is expected to become more widespread in the future as an environmentally friendly structure. However, many problems remain to be solved, and little research has been done on bamboo reinforcement and geopolymer concrete. Therefore, we herein conducted a basic study on the structural performance of geopolymer-reinforced concrete (RC) members with bamboo as the main reinforcing bar. Six beam samples were fabricated with the type of concrete and the type of reinforcing bar as parameters, and basic beam bending experiments were carried out. In this work, we verify the structural performance and failure mode of geopolymer concrete beams with bamboo reinforcing bars and consider the applicability of existing RC evaluation formulas to strength evaluation methods.

Experimental program

Specimens

Developing bamboo reinforcement with sticks and surface treatment

Previous research has shown that bamboo has a smooth surface and low bond stress. The author conducted research to improve the adhesion between bamboo bars and concrete by drilling holes in round bamboo and inserting multiple bamboo skewers to create an uneven surface43. As a result, it was confirmed that bamboo skewer processing can result in high adhesion performance, so this processing method was also adopted in this study. As shown in Fig. 1, bamboo strips with a diameter of 3 mm were inserted into holes drilled at a constant pitch. However, since cracks will occur along the bamboo strips if holes are drilled at the same pitch, the bamboo strips were rotated 90° for the test pieces with a 50 mm pitch (Fig. 1 right). For all test pieces, the length of the bamboo strips protruding from the bamboo surface was approximately 4 mm.

Bamboo reinforcement processing with bamboo sticks.

Because bamboo is a wood material, it repeatedly absorbs water and relaxes in concrete. Gaps are generated at the interface between the bamboo and the concrete, which reduces the bond stress. Therefore, as shown in Fig. 2, some of the bamboo main bars were treated with rubber waterproofing, which was confirmed to be effective for adhesion. In the past, we conducted adhesion tests to confirm the effects of the rubber coating and the bamboo skewer processing23. The rubber coating is a thin coating of about 60 to 100 μm, so the interface will not be weakened. It has been confirmed that the rubber coating provides sufficient waterproofing and increases adhesion strength by about 50%.

The solution–powder ratio of the geopolymer mortar was set to 0.6, and 20% of the total powder was replaced with ground blast furnace slag powder (BFS). Many studies have confirmed that substitution with BFS is effective for curing at room temperature. BFS has the advantages of being able to cure at room temperature and high strength, but has the disadvantage of a short curing time due to its high viscosity. The alkaline solution was prepared by mixing sodium silicate and 10 mol of sodium hydroxide in a 2:1 ratio and then water was added to adjust the viscosity. After several trials, we obtained a curing time of more than 2 h and a strength of 17.5 N/mm2 after 4 weeks, so this mixture was used for the beam test samples. The cement concrete was mixed with a water–cement ratio of 67% so that the compressive strength was 18 N/mm2, matching the strength of the prototype geopolymer concrete.

Geopolymers using glass powder

Glass is indispensable to our daily life today, and it is used in many everyday applications, such as windows, bottles, automobiles, solar panels, and heat-resistant containers. Most of this glass is recycled as raw material for construction glass, bottles, road coating materials, glass wool, lightweight foam, and other civil engineering materials. However, materials that cannot be recycled for these uses are disposed of in landfills. It is necessary to consider alternative uses for glass to extend the lifespan of landfill sites.

Geopolymers use industrial byproducts such as fly ash and blast furnace slag as raw materials, so they can be considered effective resources. In recent years, from the perspective of effectively utilizing waste glass, there has been an increase in the number of studies using glass powder as a raw material for geopolymers44–46. Therefore, in this study, we considered the use of a large amount of glass powder as an active filler in geopolymer concrete. Generally, geopolymers develop strength through heat curing, but it is necessary to develop strength at room temperature curing for construction applications. Many studies have confirmed that replacing the active filler with blast furnace slag (BFS) is effective for room-temperature hardening47,48. BFS has the advantage of curing at room temperature but has the disadvantage of high viscosity and a short curing time, so mixing (substituting) a large amount of BFS will impair workability; thus, it is not practical. In this study, we also use a geopolymer with glass powder as an active filler (binder). This material development was prototyped at the beginning of 2021, and the results were published in a report49,50. Through this measure, we determined the mixing conditions that would allow us to obtain a GPC with a setting time of more than 2 h and a compressive strength of more than 15 N/mm2 at a material age of 28 days.

Table 4 shows the results of the component analysis of various powders using fluorescence X-ray analysis (XRF). The glass powder used in this experiment was pulverized from a mixture of recycled household glass and coloured glass, with Si as the main component, followed by Ca. Compared with BFS and FA, which are active fillers for general GPC, glass powder also contains some aluminium, which contributes to polymerization. Therefore, if it can be dissolved with a strong alkali, it can function as an active filler. The materials used in this experiment, along with the descriptions, density, and actual measured values of the specific surface area of the powder are shown in Table 5. Glass powder is roughly crushed and has a smaller specific surface area than BFS or cement. Therefore, as shown in Table 7, the compressive strength was not very high.

Specimen preparation

The concrete was manufactured using a concrete mixer in our laboratory. The mixing procedure was as follows: (1) cement (binder) and sand were added and mixed for approximately 1 min; (2) water (alkaline activator solution) was added and mixed for 1 min; and (3) gravel was added and mixed for 3 min. After the mixing process, the slump, temperature, and air volume of the fresh concrete were measured. After curing in the laboratory for a few days, the samples were demoulded and cured at a room temperature of 5–10 °C and a humidity of approximately 45–55% until the testing age of 28 days. Several test pieces for strength test management were also cast. Figure 5 shows how the beam samples were prepared.

Specimen preparation.

Loading and measurement

All the samples were subjected to four-point flexure in a simple beam format, as shown in Fig. 6. The samples were tested for failure under static monotonic loading via a 1000 kN universal testing machine. The load was applied to the centre of the span via the loading beam. During the tests, the occurrence of cracks was observed, and the service load (P) was measured by a load cell. The displacements (δ) of the samples were externally measured by three displacement transducers instrumented at the bottom of the samples, as shown in Fig. 6. In addition, a displacement transducer was placed on both ends of the main bars extracted from the end surface of the beam, and the slip from the end surface was measured, as shown in Fig. 6. One pair of strain gauges was attached to the tensile main bar and stirrup. The attachment locations are indicated by “●” symbols in Fig. 6.

Cracking pattern and failure mode

The final cracking patterns of each sample at the end of loading are shown in Fig. 8. In the SD sample, the tensile main reinforcement yielded after flexural cracks occurred, and then the concrete on the compression side collapsed, resulting in flexural compression. Shear cracks were observed in the shear force-generating zone between the support points outside the test section and the loading points. The shear cracks did not widen because there were sufficient stirrups. There was no clear difference in the occurrence of cracks regardless of the type of concrete. In the bamboo sample, only flexural cracking occurred, and one crack widened significantly as it deformed. In the Ba_N(GPC) sample, bond splitting cracks also formed along the main tensile and compressive reinforcements. Figure 11, which shows the amount of slippage of the main tensile reinforcement, shows that the main reinforcement had slipped out, except for the Ba_St (OPC) sample. The load suddenly dropped after slipping a few millimetres. Therefore, it was determined that the BRC sample failed by bond failure. Load‒deflection relationship

The load‒deflection relationship for each sample is shown in Fig. 9. The deflection δ on the horizontal axis is the average deformation at three locations: two points below the loading point and one point at the centre of the sample, as shown in Fig. 6. The flexural crack point is defined as the point at which the slope of the flexural load–deflection relationship clearly decreases from the elastic stiffness in the initial stage of loading. A flexural crack occurs at a load of approximately 10 kN for the OPC sample and 4 kN for the GPC sample, after which the sample undergoes flexural deformation, but the bonds of the main bars gradually break, and the flexural rigidity decreases. In the SD sample, bond failure did not occur, and flexural yielding occurred because the main reinforcement yielded at 55 to –60 kN. In the Ba_N (GPC) sample, the stiffness suddenly decreased because bond failure occurred in the main reinforcement at the same time that flexural cracks formed. In the Ba_St (OPC) sample, the bamboo fractured before bond slip occurred, resulting in a brittle decrease in yield strength. Therefore, as shown in Table 8, the final failure mode was concrete compressive failure after flexural yielding in the SD sample; and flexural failure, in which the tensile reinforcement broke after flexural cracking occurred in the bamboo sample. However, since the main reinforcement of the bamboo sample exhibited bond failure, it exhibited complicated behaviour depending on when failure occurred.

Comparison of load against deflection for all beam samples.

Slip of the tensile main bar

As shown in Fig. 10, the ends of the main tensile bars were extended, rather than anchored, from the concrete surface by approximately 50 mm, and the amount of slip was measured as the relative displacement between this end and the concrete surface. Figure 11 shows the amount of slippage of the main tensile reinforcement with red and blue lines on both sides of the bar. The OPC sample did not slip at all, but the GPC sample slipped by approximately 0.1 mm and reached its maximum yield strength. Judging from the strain conditions of the main reinforcement, the GPC sample began to slip at approximately R = 1/100 after flexural cracks occurred, and along with deformation, the attachment broke along the entire length of the main reinforcement, confirming that the ends were slipping out. In the Ba_N (GPC) sample, bond slipping occurred at the same time as flexural cracks occurred, and the tensile bar had slipped by more than 10 mm at the maximum force. In addition, in the Ba_St (OPC) sample, the main reinforcing bars fractured before the bond slip spread to the ends, causing the load to drop suddenly.

Slip measurement of main bar from end surface.

The change in the strain distribution of the main tensile reinforcement and stirrup reinforcement is shown in Fig. 12. The strain was extracted at member angles R (= δ/a) of 1/200 rad, 1/100 rad, and 1/50 rad. The tensile strain is expressed as ‘ + ’, and the compressive strain is expressed as ‘-’. Here, δ is the average value of the three displacement meters installed on the bottom surface of the test sample, and a is the shear span (= 600 mm). The strain values in Fig. 12 are the average strain values of two gauges attached as a pair at the locations marked with “●” in Fig. 6.

Strain transition: (left) main bar and (right) stirrup.

The main reinforcement in the SD sample began to yield at R = 1/50 rad. However, it was confirmed that only the central strain of the beam was significantly distorted and that the outer strain was not significantly distorted by the end of the experiment. No difference in strain was observed depending on the type of concrete. None of the stirrups in any of the test samples yielded until a large deformation of R = 1/20 rad. Additionally, similar to the main reinforcement, the stirrups near the stress point were greatly distorted, but the stirrups on the outside of the beam were not as distorted. Although shear cracks were observed within the shear span, the cracks did not widen, suggesting that the stirrups were effective.

The bamboo samples began to become significantly distorted at R = 1/200 rad, and the strain spread to both ends of the beam. This is thought to be because the bonds of the main reinforcing bars broke with flexural deformation, and the main bars began to slip. The breaking strain of the bamboo was approximately 8000 μ, but the Ba_N (OPC) sample exceeded this value by R = 1/50 rad. No shear cracks occurred in any of the bamboo samples, and there was almost no increase in strain except for the stirrups near the flexural cracks.

Conclusions

This study aimed to determine the structural performance of geopolymer reinforced concrete (RC) members using bamboo as the main reinforcement material. Six RC beam test samples were manufactured with the concrete type and reinforcement type as parameters, and basic beam flexural experiments were conducted. The feasibility of using bamboo as a reinforcement for concrete members was examined by comparing the structural performance and failure mode of geopolymer concrete beams with those of bamboo bars under a four-point flexural test setup. The findings are described below.

Geopolymer concrete was trial-produced using glass powder as a binder. The results confirmed that it is possible to produce geopolymer concrete with a setting time exceeding 2 h and a 28-day compressive strength of 15 N/mm2 or more.

Geopolymer concrete was trial-produced using glass powder as a binder. The results confirmed that it is possible to produce geopolymer concrete with a setting time exceeding 2 h and a 28-day compressive strength of 15 N/mm2 or more.

Bamboo-reinforced concrete beams exhibited early bond slippage, and cracks did not distribute uniformly. As a result, bending failure occurred. In contrast, in the case of ordinary concrete specimens, bond slippage did not propagate to the ends of the members, and thus the load-bearing capacity increased until bamboo reinforcement rupture.

The reasons for the low bond strength between geopolymer concrete and bamboo reinforcement are considered to be as follows: (1) the concrete strength was 30–50% lower than that of ordinary concrete, and (2) geopolymer concrete exhibited greater drying shrinkage than cement concrete, resulting in the formation of microcracks on the interior surfaces of the members and around the coarse aggregate.
