The High Removal Efficiency of H2S by MDEA Solution in High-Jets Oscillating Purifier
Authors: Liang Ma, Yuting Zhang, Anlin Liu, Zhisheng Zhao, Liwang Wang, Yulong Chang, Xiaoxu Duan, Peng Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is an extremely noxious impurity generated from industrial processes, but conventional absorption devices applied to remove H2S are often constrained by low mass transfer coefficients and large equipment volume. To overcome these limitations, we propose a novel high-jet oscillating purifier (HOP) and systematically investigate the key operational parameters that influence desulfurization performance when HOP is combined with a methyl diethanolamine (MDEA) solution. Experimental results demonstrated that H2S removal efficiency and mass transfer coefficients increased with absorption flow rate (0.06–0.12 m3/h), methyl diethanolamine concentration (0.8–3.2 wt %), and inlet H2S concentration (20–160 mg/m3), but decreased with methyl diethanolamine solution temperature (25–85 °C). Intriguingly, inlet gas flow rates (5 and 17 m3/h) improved removal efficiency but reduced mass transfer coefficients. Radial injection (α = 0°) of absorption liquid achieved optimal performance, yielding peak efficiency at 98.7%, surpassing nonradial injection (α = ±45°) by 12.3–15.8%. The mass transfer coefficients (K G a) at 0.0715 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1, are about ten times higher than conventional devices. Semiempiric equation (R 2 = 0.99, p < 0.001) was derived to predict K G a across operational conditions. The industrial application indicated that HOP achieved an average removal efficiency higher than 95.1% with only 1/8 of the device volume compared to conventional scrubbers. The study indicates that HOP combined with MDEA solution has great potential for application in removing H2S for industrial plants.

Introduction

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) is a colorless, corrosive, flammable, and highly toxic gas, commonly encountered in industrial processes. Its presence not only leads to corrosion of downstream equipment but also compromises product quality. , Even at low concentrations, as low as 5 ppm, H2S can cause severe harm to the human respiratory system and eyes when released into the atmosphere. , Furthermore, H2S readily dissolves in atmospheric moisture, contributing to acid rain formation and resulting in significant environmental damage. Thus, the separation and reduction of H2S emissions are critical for enhancing industrial production and mitigating environmental pollution.

To mitigate H2S emissions, various advanced removal technologies have been developed, including membrane separation, biological methods, advanced oxidation processes, adsorption, and absorption. Among these, the absorption method is widely utilized due to its ease of maintenance and reliable performance. − The selection of the absorbent is a critical factor influencing removal efficiency. Common absorbents include physical solvents, ionic liquids, deep eutectic solvents (DESs), and alkanol amines. Physical solvents interact with polar H2S molecules through charged portions, operating effectively under low temperatures and high partial pressures. However, heavy hydrocarbons present in flue gas can reduce the selective absorption efficiency. Ionic liquids, despite their promising properties, face challenges such as high production costs, complex synthesis, and toxicity, which limit their large-scale commercial applications. , In contrast, DESs offer advantages such as high thermal stability, low toxicity, and ease of preparation. While they have demonstrated potential in capturing CO2, SO2, and NH3, their application for H2S capture remains relatively underexplored. , Alkanolamines are currently the most widely applied absorbents due to their unique absorption–desorption characteristics. Among these, methyl diethanolamine (MDEA) has garnered significant attention for its high selectivity, low solvent vapor pressure, and efficient energy utilization. − During the absorption process, H2S is initially transferred from the gas phase to the liquid phase via a gas–liquid mass transfer, followed by a chemical reaction with the absorbent. Thus, enhancing gas–liquid mass transfer is essential for improving removal performance. However, traditional gas–liquid reactors, such as spray towers, packed towers, and bubble towers, often exhibit low mass transfer coefficients, large volumes, and high investment costs. − Therefore, it is crucial to develop innovative technologies that enhance gas–liquid mass transfer and improve the H2S removal efficiency.

Common equipment for enhancing mass transfer includes impinging flow towers, vortex flow scrubbers, rotating packed beds (RPB), bubble columns, and high-jet oscillating purifiers (HOP). Impinging flow towers and vortex flow scrubbers are static devices with simple structures, where flue gas and absorbent collide and flow at high velocities, generating significant turbulence. This turbulence can increase the mass transfer coefficient by 18–33% compared to conventional scrubbers. , RPB represents a typical dynamic process intensification reactor. In which the absorbent is broken into fine droplets, and the cyclone field accelerates the surface renewal rate of these droplets, resulting in a 10- to 100-fold increase in the mass transfer coefficient. However, RPBs are often associated with high energy consumption. Bubble columns enhance gas–liquid contacting by inducing strong disturbances and increasing residence time, thereby improving H2S removal efficiency by 58.4 ± 1.0%. Despite these advancements, developing equipment that combines a high mass transfer coefficient with a simple structure remains a significant challenge for effective H2S removal.

In recent years, HOP has attracted significant attention due to its simple structure, high mass transfer coefficient, and low energy consumption. When flue gas enters the HOP, it impinges on the injected liquid absorbent, breaking it into fine droplets and increasing the gas–liquid contact area by several orders of magnitude. , Simultaneously, the droplets rotate at high speed within the HOP under the influence of a cyclonic field, creating a high-gravity environment that enhances surface renewal and gas–liquid mass transfer. , The HOP performs the complex processes of gas–liquid impingement, reaction, and separation simultaneously, making this static high-gravity system less susceptible to scaling and clogging. , It has been successfully applied for the removal of various pollutants, including CS2, CO2, NH3, HCl, SO X , and NO X , from flue gas. ,− Compared to traditional spray towers, the HOP demonstrates a 10-fold increase in mass transfer coefficient, an 80% reduction in equipment volume, and up to 90% savings in investment costs. , Despite these advantages, the performance of the HOP in H2S removal remains uncertain. Moreover, the angle of the injection holes, which determines the formation of columnar liquid jets and significantly impacts removal efficiency, has not been systematically investigated, posing a major obstacle to its industrial application.

Here, the operating conditions influencing H2S removal performance in the HOP combined with a MDEA solution were investigated for the first time. Key parameters included gas and absorbent flow rates, H2S and MDEA concentrations, the absorption temperature, and injection hole angles. Semiempiric equations were subsequently developed to predict the mass transfer coefficient of the HOP for H2S removal. Finally, the H2S removal performance of HOP was evaluated under industrial application conditions.

Detailed information is provided in Table . Before the experiment, 30 L of absorption solution were placed in the circulation tank and stirred for 20 min to ensure uniform mixing. During the experiment, filtered air was introduced into the mixing tank via a fan, where it was mixed with H2S to simulate the industrial gas. Subsequently, the MDEA (C5H13NO2) absorption solution was pumped into the jacket of the HOP by using circulating pumps. The absorption solution passed through holes in the column sidewall, forming liquid jets due to pressure. Simulated flue gas entered the HOP tangentially, cutting the absorption solution into droplets. To maintain a liquid seal, the bottom liquid valve was adjusted, allowing the flue gas to be discharged solely from the central pipe. The absorption solution, having absorbed H2S, flowed out from the bottom of the HOP and into the circulation tank. Each experimental run lasted 10 min under specified operating conditions, with corresponding parameters measured when the experimental system was stable. Each condition was repeated three times, and the results were averaged. The reagents used in the experiments were MDEA (≥99%, Aladdin) and distilled water (≥18.2 MΩ·cm, Fast-X1).

Results and Discussion

The Influence of Inlet Gas Flow Rate

Figure exhibits the influence of the inlet gas flow rate (Q G, m3/h) on H2S removal efficiency and K G a. As Q G increased from 5 to 17 m3/h, H2S removal efficiency in the HOP decreased from 96.8 to 81.7%, while K G a increased from 0.0112 to 0.0518 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. Elevated Q G intensified the high-gravity field and turbulent vortex formation within the HOP, enhancing droplet fragmentation from absorption jets and thereby improving gas–liquid mass transfer. However, higher Q G reduced the gas residence time, limiting H2S diffusion into the liquid phase and lowering the removal efficiency. The adverse residence time effect dominated removal efficiency trends, whereas the beneficial mass transfer enhancement. At a fixed Q G, the radial injection angle (α = 0°) achieved optimal performance, with maximum removal efficiency at 0° exceeding α = +45° and α = −45° by 5.0 and 6.3%, respectively. Similarly, K G a at α = 0° (0.0183–0.0416 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1) surpassed α = +45° and α = −45° by 21.4 and 35.8%. Thus, while higher Q G reduced H2S removal efficiency, it elevated K G a, particularly at α = 0°, highlighting the trade-off between K G a and removal efficiency.

The Influence of Absorption Flow Rate

Figure illustrates the relationship among absorption flow rate (Q L, m3/h), H2S removal efficiency, and K G a. As Q L increased from 0.06 to 0.12 m3/h, both removal efficiency and K G a in the HOP rose steadily, with efficiency ranging from 77.8 to 94.3% and K G a from 0.0272 to 0.0518 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. Higher Q L enhanced droplet formation by supplying more absorbent to the HOP, which expanded the gas–liquid interfacial area and intensified H2S-absorbent interactions. However, beyond 0.12 m3/h, the efficiency plateaued as the gas became insufficient to fully atomize the excess liquid into fine droplets. At all Q L levels, the 0° injection hole angle achieved optimal performance; removal efficiency (84.2–94.2%) surpassed 45° (77.8–87.6%) and −45° (78.9–86.8%) by up to 6.7 and 7.5%, respectively. Similarly, K G a peaked at 0° (0.0333–0.0518 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1), exceeding values for +45° (0.0272–0.0377 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1) and −45° (0.0281–0.0365 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1) by 37.2 and 41.6% at maximum. These results confirmed that increasing Q L enhanced H2S removal and K G a, with the 0° hole delivering superior performance.

The Influence of H2S Inlet Concentration

Figure demonstrates that increasing the inlet H2S concentration enhanced both removal efficiency and the K G a. When the H2S concentration increased from 20 to 160 mg/m3, removal efficiency improved from 79.6 to 91.3%, while K G a rose from 0.0288 to 0.0440 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. This trend was attributed to intensified gas–liquid interactions; higher H2S concentrations amplified the mass transfer driving force, reaction kinetics, and collision frequency between H2S and absorbent droplets. At lower concentrations (e.g., 20 mg/m3), an insufficient driving force limited H2S diffusion into the liquid phase, reducing reaction rates and absorption capacity. As concentrations increased, however, simultaneous improvements in diffusion and reaction rates elevated performance. Notably, the incremental gains in efficiency and K G a diminished at higher H2S levels (>160 mg/m3), constrained by gas–liquid mass transfer saturation. The 0°-hole angle consistently achieved peak performance, with removal efficiency ranging from 89.9 to 91.3%, which were 4.5 and 8.4% higher than values for +45 and −45°, respectively. Similarly, K G a at 0° increased from 0.0414 to 0.0440 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1 across the tested concentration range, surpassing +45° and −45° angles by 20.2 and 37.9%. These results confirmed that H2S concentration positively governed desulfurization performance, with optimal outcomes at a 0°-hole angle.

Effect of H2S concentration and hole angles on H2S removal performance.

The Influence of MDEA Absorption Concentration

Figure shows the influence of MDEA concentration (C MDEA, wt %) on H2S removal efficiency and K G a. As C MDEA increased from 0.8 to 3.2 wt %, both H2S removal efficiency and K G a rose significantly. This trend was attributed to enhanced gas–liquid absorption kinetics, higher C MDEA increases the availability of reactive absorbent, promoting more frequent H2S-MDEA interactions and improving mass transfer efficiency. Beyond 3.2 wt %, however, removal efficiency and K G a stabilized due to droplet saturation and elevated surface tension in the MDEA solution, which hindered droplet atomization within the HOP. Under identical C MDEA conditions, the 0°-hole angle achieved peak performance, with removal efficiency ranging from 92.8 to 98.7%, surpassing results for +45° (67.8–83.3%) and −45° (55.8–73.7%). Similarly, K G a reached its maximum at 0°, varying from 0.0475 to 0.0715 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. The 0° configuration outperformed +45° and −45° angles, yielding 42.0 and 52.4% higher K G a, respectively. These findings demonstrated a strong positive correlation between CMDEA and desulfurization performance with optimal outcomes achieved at a 0°-hole angle.

The Influence of Absorption Temperature

As shown in Figure , both the H2S removal efficiency and K G a decreased with increasing absorption temperature (T L, °C). When the MDEA solution temperature rose from 25 to 85 °C, the removal efficiency declined from 90.0 to 55.8%, while K G a dropped from 0.0414 to 0.0147 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. This trend reflected the dual role of the temperature in gas–liquid reactions. Elevated temperatures accelerated H2S molecular diffusion across the liquid film due to enhanced thermal motion. However, they also reduced the equilibrium-driven absorption capacity of MDEA and shifted the reaction equilibrium unfavorably, ultimately dominating the performance outcomes. At constant T L, the 0°-hole angle achieved the highest removal efficiency (80.8–90.0%), followed by +45° (67.8–83.3%) and −45° (55.8–73.7%). Similarly, K G a peaked at the 0° configuration, decreasing from 0.0414 to 0.0297 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1 with rising temperature. The maximum K G a at 0° exceeded those at +45° and −45° by 45.6 and 102.1%, respectively. These results confirmed that the absorption temperature inversely governs both H2S removal efficiency and K G a.

The Analysis of Injection Hole Angles

As illustrated in Figure , the motion direction can be spatially decomposed into radial and vertical (Z-axis) components. Optimal atomization efficacy and H2S removal performance were achieved at α = 0°, where the absorption liquid interacted fully with the gas in both the radial and vertical directions. In contrast, at α = +45°, reduced atomization efficiency was observed due to the gas flow colliding with limited absorption liquid along the positive Z-axis, yielding larger droplets under gravitational effects. At α = −45°, the downward flow of the absorption liquid restricted the radial interaction with the gas, resulting in the poorest atomization and H2S removal. Furthermore, at nonzero angles (α = ±45°), a portion of the absorption liquid was displaced near the intake or below the center tube, preventing it from entering the annular gap between the center tube and the inner cylinder wall. This displacement caused unreacted liquid to bypass the flue gas, diminishing the H2S removal efficiency. So, the removal efficiency followed the trend η (α = 0°) > η (α = +45°) > η (α = −45°).

Industrial Application

In an industrial case study at Panjin Pengchili Petrochemical Co., Ltd. (China), a high-efficiency H2S removal system was implemented alongside the production of 300,000 tons/year of aromatics (benzene, toluene, and mixed xylene). As illustrated in Figure , process gas discharged from the absorption tower contained 140–170 ppm of H2S. This gas underwent primary purification via HOP-A before entering the reactor. Postreactor gas, with residual H2S concentrations of 30–50 ppm, was further treated by HOP-B. Both HOP-A and HOP-B utilized a 27 wt % MDEA solution and operated at identical gas flow rates of 3000 m3/h. Steady-state H2S concentrations at the inlets and outlets of the HOPs were measured using a Testo 350 gas analyzer, and removal efficiencies were calculated (Figure ). HOP-A achieved 95.7–99.4% efficiency, while HOP-B demonstrated inferior performance at 90.0–97.9%, attributed to its lower inlet H2S concentration. This elevated concentration enhanced the diffusion-driving force and infiltration into MDEA droplets, increasing the mass transfer coefficient and average removal efficiency (Section ). Notably, both systems consistently exceeded 95.1% average efficiency, underscoring the HOP’s robust potential for industrial H2S abatement in high-throughput flue gas applications.

As shown in Figure , the HOP device was used to replace the conventional scrubber when treated exhaust gas contained H2S with a flow rate of 6000 m3/h. Under the comparable removal efficiency at 97%, the conventional absorption device had a volume of Φ3.8 × 32 m, while the HOP device had only a volume of Φ2.0 × 8 m, about 1/8 of the conventional scrubber. This is mainly due to the K G a in the HOP device being ten times higher than conventional scrubber. Smaller volume means a much smaller investment, including manufacturing, maintenance, and supporting facilities. Furthermore, the HOP maintained robust efficiency even at gas flow rates significantly exceeding the operational limits of traditional equipment, enabling larger volumes of flue gas to be treated per unit time. Furthermore, the high-speed swirling flow of the flue gas in the HOP generates a strong shear force, which washes away the deposits adhering to the inner wall. This can cut off the corrosive effect of the deposited substances and avoid abnormal structural stress, thereby significantly extending the service life of the device. Therefore, the HOP has great potential application in removing H2S from industrial exhaust.

Conclusions

This study evaluated the performance of H2S removal using MDEA absorption in HOP under varied operational parameters. Results demonstrate that H2S removal efficiency and the K G a of H2S increased with absorption flow rates, MDEA concentrations, and inlet H2S concentrations. Conversely, both removal efficiency and mass transfer coefficient declined as MDEA absorption temperature increases. Notably, the increase in gas flow rate contributed to higher removal efficiency but lower K G a. Regarding injection hole angles, radial injection (α = 0°) of absorption liquid outperformed nonradial injection (α = +45° or −45°), achieving up to 98.7% efficiency and K G a values of 0.0715 kmol·m–3·kPa–1·s–1. This enhancement stems from radial injection that maximizes multidirectional collisions between absorbent droplets and H2S-containing gas. The semiempiric equation was derived to predict HOP mass transfer performance, and an industrial application demonstrates the average H2S removal efficiencies higher than 95.1%. Compared to conventional scrubbers, the HOP-MDEA system offers ten times higher K G a and 1/8 of the device volume, positioning HOP as a promising technology for industrial flue gas desulfurization.
