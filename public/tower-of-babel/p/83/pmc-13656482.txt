Surfactin-like lipopeptides contribute to interference competition of Bacillus velezensis against the marine Flavobacteriaceae in phycosphere
Authors: Runlin Cai, Hao Feng, Yang Liu, Shuyi Liang, Danhong Wen, Zhimu Xie, Yongliang Gan, Guanjing Cai, Hui Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe phycosphere is a nutrient-rich microenvironment surrounding phytoplankton cells and serves as a hotspot for microbial interactions by releasing phytoplankton-derived organic molecules to dynamically attract and support the colonization of heterotrophic bacteria. Although the taxonomic composition, metabolic profiles, and host-microbe interactions of phycosphere bacterial communities have been extensively characterized, the underlying mechanisms driving competition among these co-existing bacterial taxa remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we demonstrate that Bacillus velezensis SPE2, a low-abundance isolate from the phycosphere of dinoflagellate, exhibits a wide degree of antagonistic activity against multiple marine Flavobacteriaceae strains, a dominant taxonomic group across the phycosphere of diverse phytoplankton species. Through an integrative approach combining genetics and metabolomics, we show that the antagonistic behavior of strain SPE2 is primarily mediated by the production of bioactive secondary metabolites. Activity-guided purification further leads to the identification of two antibacterial surfactin-like lipopeptides as key exometabolites responsible for these inhibitory effects. Mechanistically, these lipopeptides exert their antibacterial activity against Flavobacteriaceae species by disrupting the integrity of bacterial cell membranes.ConclusionsOur findings reveal surfactin-like lipopeptides as key molecular mediators of bacterial interference competition, conferring a competitive strategy for Bacillus species to secure persistence in the phycosphere. Moreover, this work underscores phycosphere as a largely untapped ecological niche for discovering novel bioactive compounds with potential applications in pharmaceutical and biotechnological fields.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40793-026-00937-0.Keywords: Phycosphere, Bacillus velezensis, Flavobacteriaceae, Surfactin, Interference competition

Introduction

As a microscale ecological niche surrounding phytoplankton cells, the phycosphere is dynamically shaped by the continuous release of phytoplankton-derived organic molecules [1]. This microenvironment operates as a critical hotspot for microbial interactions, harboring dense communities of heterotrophic bacteria that mediate essential biogeochemical processes, such as carbon remineralization and energy transfer within marine microbial networks [1–3]. These processes ultimately influence global carbon cycling and ocean productivity [4]. Global microbial surveys have demonstrated distinct taxonomic selectivity in phycosphere bacterial communities, which are frequently dominated by a handful of lineages, such as Rhodobacteraceae, Flavobacteraceae, and Alteromonadaceae [1, 5]. These consistent ecological patterns suggest that certain bacterial lineages have evolved specialized lifestyles that are fundamentally adapted to their interactions with phytoplankton hosts [6]. Although the archetypal phytoplankton-associated taxa dominate the phycosphere, recent taxonomic studies have uncovered a significantly broader microbial diversity in this niche than previously recognized [7, 8]. Some previously overlooked lineages, such as members of the family Bacillaceae, though ubiquitously detected across marine and terrestrial ecosystems [9], consistently maintain low relative abundance (< 1.0%) in the phycosphere as part of the rare biosphere [8, 10]. The ecological significance of these low-abundance microbes within the community has gained increasing attention [10]. Functional analysis reveals that the metabolic potential of these microbial members is disproportionate to their abundance [11]. Mounting evidence indicates that these microbial minority populations serve as keystone players in shaping the microbial composition and performing specific functions within the host [10, 12]. For instance, some Bacillus strains (family Bacillaceae) have been shown to form mutualistic relationships with dinoflagellates or enhance the interspecific competitiveness for their hosts [13, 14], underscoring their functional indispensability in phytoplankton-microbe interactions. Given their persistence and ecological impact, elucidating the mechanisms by which these low-abundance taxa maintain stable colonization in the phycosphere is essential to understand microbial community assembly and host-microbe interactions.

Interspecies competition is widespread in bacterial communities and pivotal in regulating the composition and dynamics of microbial communities, thereby maintaining biodiversity and ecosystem stability [15, 16]. Broadly, bacterial interspecies competition can be categorized into two distinct patterns [17]. The first represents a passive competition mode primarily driven by resource limitation, wherein microbial populations compete for essential survival resources, including nutrients, physical space, and other necessities [18, 19]. The second constitutes an active interference competition strategy, characterized by direct antagonistic interactions in which one species actively inhibits or eliminates competitors through secreted antimicrobial agents or contact-dependent killing mechanisms [19, 20]. In interference competition, the production of antibacterial metabolites is a key determinant of population dynamics and microbial colonization success in diverse environments [21]. The structural and functional diversity of these metabolites underlies the high complexity of interference competition among phylogenetically distinct microorganisms [20, 22]. A representative case involves plant-associated Bacillus strains that dynamically activate secondary metabolite biosynthesis upon detecting siderophores produced by a Pseudomonas competitor [23]. This metabolite response represents a multimodal defense mechanism as it includes polyketides and bacteriocin that provide broad-spectrum antibiotic activity, as well as surfactin lipopeptides that facilitate biofilm formation and promote cellular motility [23]. However, as a low-abundance member in the phycosphere, our knowledge about such ecological relevance of secondary metabolites for Bacillus to colonize this ecological niche and coexist with other bacteria is still limited.

To address these gaps, a low-abundance bacterium, Bacillus velezensis SPE2, was successfully isolated from the phycosphere of the dinoflagellate Symbiodinium pseudominutum CCMP3450 and found to be capable of antagonism against Flavobacteriaceae strains, which consistently dominate these niches due to their exceptional metabolic capacity in degrading phytoplankton-derived organic matter [24]. Specifically, we elucidated the biosynthetic potential of strain SPE2 for antibacterial metabolites and characterized key exometabolites that mediate interference competition against Flavobacteriaceae strains, along with their inhibitory mechanisms. The results of this study could help us to understand how low-abundance colonizers like Bacillus, utilize exometabolites to establish ecological persistence with dominant taxa in phycosphere.

Methods

Bacterial isolation and identification

Bacterial strains in the phycosphere of the dinoflagellate S. pseudominutum CCMP3450 were isolated from the exponentially growing dinoflagellate cultures. After being serially diluted, aliquots (100 µL) of each dilution (10−3, 10−4, 10−5) were spread onto 2216E marine agar plates. All plates were incubated at 25 °C for seven days and colonies were purified by sub-cultivation on 2216E marine agar plate. After growth on 2216E marine agar plate for 72 h, morphologically different colonies were selected for further identification. Nine bacterial strains were successfully isolated (Table S1). Genomic DNA was then extracted using the Ultra-Clean microbial DNA isolation kit (Mo Bio Laboratories, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 16S rRNA gene sequences were amplified using the universal primers 27F/1492R [25]. Both 16S rRNA genes and draft genomes of all tested strains were sequenced by Beijing Genomics Institute (BGI, China). The obtained 16S rRNA gene sequences were analyzed through the EZBioCloud (http://www.ezbiocloud.net). Phylogenetic tree was constructed in MEGA version 7.0 software by using the maximum-likelihood (ML) and visualized by tvBOT [26, 27]. The draft genomes of all tested strains were analyzed using our previously described method [28]. The average nucleotide identity (ANI) values were calculated using an online ANI calculator with OrthoANI algorithm (www.ezbiocloud.net/tools/ani). These strains were preserved in cryotubes as suspensions in 2216E marine broth containing sterile 20% (v/v) glycerol at −80 °C in our laboratory. For subsequent experiments, these strains were cultured on 2216E marine agar plates at 28 °C.

Antagonistic activity

Pairwise antagonism assay was performed to systematically assess inhibitory interactions between the nine strains as the method described previously (Fig. S1) [20, 21]. First, all strains were grown in 2216E marine broth at 28 °C for 24 h. Broth cultures of the target strains were adjusted to an OD600 of 0.3, and 100 µL aliquots of each culture were then spread onto circular 2216E plates (90 mm in diameter) to create bacterial lawns. Next, the broth culture of the producer strains was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.3 and 5 µL of this culture was spotted onto each of the bacterial lawn plates. Results of the antagonistic activity were evaluated after incubation at 28 °C for 48 h. The radius of inhibitory zone was estimated using the following formula: inhibition zone radius = radius of the transparent zone – radius of the bacterial colony.

Given that strain SPE2 exhibited selective inhibitory activity, particularly with significant antagonistic effects against Flavobacteriaceae members (Table S2), we expanded the assay scope to include six additional Flavobacteriaceae strains (Robiginitalea biformata PM2, Maribacter cobaltidurans PR1, Maribacter flavus PR66, Flagellimonas amphidinii AC10, Flagellimonas taeanensis SK9, and Zeaxanthinibacter enoshimensis PT1), previously isolated from different dinoflagellate species (Table S1) [29]. These strains were included in this study to further characterize the specificity of strain SPE2.

To evaluate the antagonistic activity of strain SPE2 against Flavobacteriaceae strains in liquid culture, R. biformata PM2 was used as the indicator strain. Strains SPE2 and PM2 were each resuspended in 2216E marine broth to 2 × 107 cells/mL. Equal volumes of the two cell suspensions were combined and the mixture was inoculated at 1% (v/v) into 10 mL of 2216E marine broth, followed by aerobic co-culture at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm. Then, the colony-forming units (CFU) of each strain were determined at 24 h intervals throughout the co-culture period. Samples were serially diluted (10-fold) in sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 10 mM, pH 7.2 to 7.4), and appropriate dilutions were spread onto selective agar plates. Individual cultures of strains SPE2 and PM2 in 2216E marine broth served as controls. All experiments were performed in three biological replicates.

Prediction of secondary metabolite biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs)

The secondary metabolite BGCs of strain SPE2 and eight Flavobacteriaceae strains were identified using antiSMASH version 8.0 with default parameters and BGCs with a total length of greater than 10 kb were retained to reduce the risk of fragmentation [30, 31]. The BGCs of strain SPE2 were further compared with those in Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) protein database (https://www.genome.jp/tools/blast/) using “blastp” with an E-value cutoff of 1 × 10−10. The identified BGCs were grouped and visualized using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/).

Antibacterial activity of crude extracts from strain SPE2

To obtain sufficient crude extracts for isolating and identifying antibacterial compounds from strain SPE2, we compared the yields and antibacterial activity of crude extracts generated through bacterial fermentation using different culture medium including 2216E marine broth and tryptic soy broth (TSB). Strain SPE2 was cultured overnight at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm in 50 mL Erlenmeyer flasks containing 20 mL of marine 2216E broth medium. Then, 2 mL of this seed culture was transferred into separate 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask, each containing 200 mL of either marine 2216E or TSB broth. After incubation for 72 h at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm, the bacterial cells were removed by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 10 min. The pH of the supernatants was adjusted to 2.0 using 6 M HCl, and the solutions were kept at 4 °C overnight. Then, the precipitates were collected by centrifugation and extracted three times with 20 mL of MeOH. The MeOH extracts were evaporated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator to obtain the crude extracts, which were then weighed to compare the yields between the two media.

The antibacterial activity of the two crude extracts from strain SPE2 against eight Flavobacteriaceae strains was tested using a drop plate assay [32]. Briefly, both crude extracts were dissolved in MeOH. Each Flavobacteriaceae strain was grown in marine 2216E broth at 28 °C for 48 h with shaking at 180 rpm, and then was adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 using 10 mM PBS. The freshly cultured cells of each Flavobacteriaceae strain were performed in series of dilution with a ratio of 1:10 and then treated with the two crude extracts at a final concentration of 200 µg/mL, respectively. Equivalent amounts of PBS and MeOH served as negative controls. After incubation at 28 °C for 30 min, the bacterial cells (5 µL of each diluted suspension) were spotted onto marine 2216E agar plates and incubated at 28 °C for 96 h before being photographed.

The chemical diversity of strain SPE2 based on molecular networking analysis

Both crude extracts were dissolved in LC-MS grade MeOH on a final concentration of 1 mg/mL and filtrated through 0.22 μm syringe filters to evaluate by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography electrospray ionization high-resolution tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS) analysis. The analysis was performed on a Thermo Scientific Ultimate 3000 UHPLC system coupled to an LTQ Orbitrap Elite mass-spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm particle size) with a mobile phase flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of (A) water with 0.1% formic acid and (B) acetonitrile. A gradient elution program was run from 10% to 100% B over 15 min. The injection volume was 2 µL, and the column and sample temperatures were maintained at 40 °C and 10 °C, respectively. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out in positive ion mode with the following parameters: capillary voltage, 3.5 kV; source vaporizer temperature, 250 °C; capillary temperature, 275 °C; sheath gas flow rate, 35 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 10 Arb. Full-scan MS data were acquired over the m/z range of 100–1200 Da, and automated MS/MS fragmentation was performed at a collision energy of 35 eV.

The raw MS/MS data files were converted to mzXML format using msConvert (version 3.0.18165, ProteoWizard) [33]. The converted files were then analyzed using the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) pipeline (http://gnps.ucsd.edu) with the following parameters: precursor ion mass tolerance, 0.02 Da; fragment ion mass tolerance, 0.02 Da; minimum pairs cosine score, 0.5; minimum matched fragment ions, 4; network topK, 10; minimum cluster size, 2; maximum connected component size, 100 [34]. The “Search Analogs” function was enabled. A solvent blank sample was included in the analysis to filter out contaminants and background noise. The resulting molecular network (accessible at https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/status.jsp?task=4e36fd84ed114ae38835b060ef81594c) was visualized using Cytoscape [35]. For in silico dereplication, the GNPS libraries and dereplication tools (https://gnps.ucsd.edu/ProteoSAFe/static/gnpstheoretical.jsp), Dereplicator+, and SIRIUS 4 were employed to reanalyze the constructed molecular network and confirm the known nodes [36, 37]. All resulting files were consolidated and uploaded to ClassyFire (http://classyfire.wishartlab.com/) for compound family annotations.

Isolation, purification and structure elucidation of antibacterial compounds from strain SPE2

A bioassay-guided purification process was conducted to isolate the antibacterial compounds from strain SPE2. The crude extract was subjected to ODS chromatography (YMC*Gel ODS-A-HG, 50 μm) using a gradient elution of MeOH/H2O (50% to 100%, v/v) to obtain six fractions (A1 − F1). Robiginitalea biformata SP8 was selected as the indicator strain for the bioassay-guided purification. All fractions were dissolved in MeOH and evaluated for their antibacterial activity against strain SP8 using the drop plate assay as described above on the final concentrations of 10, 50, and 100 µg/mL, respectively. The antibacterial fractions (B1 and C1) were applied to Sephadex LH-20 eluting with 50% MeOH/CH2Cl2 to afford seven subfractions (A2 − G2). The antibacterial subfractions (C2, D2, and E2) were further purified by reversed-phase high performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) using an Artchrom WP Phenyl-Hexyl column (5 μm, 10 × 250 mm) on a Waters 2695 HPLC system (Waters, USA) with ultraviolet detection at λ = 203 nm. The mobile phase consisted of 73% CH3CN/H2O containing 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), delivered at a flow rate of 3.0 mL/min. This process afforded two pure compounds as 1 (312.3 mg, tR = 14.5 min) and 2 (1042.7 mg, tR = 16.5 min).

The molecular weight data of two pure compounds were analyzed by ESI-MS and ESI-HRMS/MS using the TSQ Endura triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and MaXis Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany), respectively. Data from ESI-MS were obtained in positive ion mode under the following conditions: capillary voltage, 4.0 kV; sheath gas flow rate, 20 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 5 Arb; ion transfer tube temperature, 300 °C; vaporizer temperature, 300 °C; scan range (m/z), 300–1500 Da. ESI-HRMS/MS data were obtained in positive ion mode under the following conditions: capillary voltage, 4.5 kV; dry gas, 4.0 I/min; dry temperature, 180 °C; scan range (m/z), 50–1500 Da. One-dimensional nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (1H and 13C NMR) of two pure compounds with DMSO-d6 (δH 2.50 and δC 39.5 ppm) as the solvent, was recorded using an AV600 NMR spectrometer (Bruker, Switzerland) at 600 and 150 MHz for 1H and 13C NMR, respectively.

Expression of the surfactin biosynthesis gene in strain SPE2 in response to phycosphere-derived metabolites

The expression of the srfAA gene responsible for biosynthesis of surfactin was detected by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) under different cultivation conditions. Strain SPE2 was incubated at 28 °C with shaking at 180 rpm in 2216E marine broth and in TSB, both supplemented with 10% (v/v) filter-sterilized algal spent medium. The algal spent medium was obtained from exponentially growing cultures of the dinoflagellate S. pseudominutum CCMP3450, filtered through a 0.22 μm polyethersulfone membrane to remove algal and bacterial cells. Strain SPE2 was cultivated in 2216E marine broth, TSB, or algal spent medium alone, which served as the controls. The cell density of strain SPE2 under different cultivation conditions was monitored every 12 h by flow cytometer (Challenbio, China). At each time point, 1 mL of bacterial culture were harvested by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 4 min at 4 °C. Total RNA was extracted with the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TRAN, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using the StarScript™ Ⅲ All-in-one RT Mix with gDNA Remover (GeneStar, China). The qPCR reaction was performed on the qTOWER³ G system (Analytik Jena, Germany). The relative expression level of srfAA was quantified based on the comparative threshold cycle method (2−ΔΔCT) [38]. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Table S3 [39].

Antibacterial activity and time-killing kinetics assay of pure compounds

To evaluate the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of pure compounds, eight Flavobacteriaceae strains were grown in marine 2216E broth at 28 °C for 48 h with shaking at 180 rpm. Their cultures were then adjusted to an OD600 of 0.1 using 10 mM PBS and subjected to a series of dilution with a ratio of 1:10. Each pure compound was serially diluted using a two-fold dilution. Serially diluted pure compounds and Flavobacteriaceae strain were added to the wells of a 96-well plate. The mixtures were incubated for 30 min at 28 °C, and then 5 µL of each dilution was dropped on marine 2216E agar plates. For time-kill kinetics analysis, the mixtures were sampled at 10 min intervals during incubation. All plates were incubated at 28 °C for 96 h to demonstrate the inhibitory activity and bactericidal dynamics of the pure compounds at various concentrations.

Cell viability and cellular integrity of Flavobacteriaceae strain

The cell viability and cellular integrity of R. biformata PM2 after pure compounds treatment were assessed using the LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Viability Kit (Beyotime, China), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, strain PM2 was grown in marine 2216E broth at 28 °C for 48 h with shaking at 180 rpm and then was adjusted in PBS to an OD600 of 0.1. Subsequently, 2 µL aliquots of each pure compound, dissolved in MeOH, were added to 198 µL of the cell suspensions to achieve a final concentration of 6.25 µM. The treated cultures were then incubated at 28 °C for 30 min. Equivalent amounts of PBS and MeOH were selected as controls. The collected bacterial cells were stained with the LIVE/DEAD Bacterial Viability Kit with DMAO and PI at 28 °C for 30 min in the dark. Thereafter, the bacterial cells were transferred to slides for microscopic observation under a ICX41 fluorescence microscope (Ningbo Sunny, China).

Statistical analyses

All activity assays were performed in triplicate. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Differences between groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test for pairwise comparisons, or by two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction test for multiple comparisons. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

B. velezensis SPE2 exhibits a wide degree of antagonistic activity against eight Flavobacteriaceae strains

PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene and sequencing revealed that strain SPE2 had highest similarity (99.93%) with the type strain B. velezensis CR-502T (Fig. S2, Table S1). Due to the limited resolution of 16S rRNA gene sequence in distinguishing Bacillus species (Fig. S3), strain SPE2 was further phylogenetically classified based on its draft genome (Fig. S4). Phylogenomic analysis of the genome showed that strain SPE2 exhibited genetic identity with B. velezensis FZB42T (ANI value > 99.9955%) (Fig. S5, Table S4). Collectively, strain SPE2 could be identified as B. velezensis.

Intriguingly, in the pairwise antagonism assay (Fig. S1) [20, 21], we assessed antagonistic interactions among nine bacterial strains isolated from the dinoflagellate S. pseudominutum CCMP3450 (Table S1). In total, 72 pairwise combinations were tested. Notably, strain SPE2 demonstrated selective antagonism, particularly showing significant growth inhibition against R. biformata SP8 and Flagellimonas alvinocaridis SP22, two dominant Flavobacteriaceae strains from the same habitat (Fig. 1A, Table S2). This antagonism was further verified against six additional Flavobacteriaceae strains obtained from diverse dinoflagellate hosts (Fig. 1A, Table S1) [29]. Strain SPE2 showed broad antagonistic effects against all tested Flavobacteriaceae strains with inhibition zones ranging from 1.65 ± 0.17 (F. alvinocaridis SP22) to 9.88 ± 0.52 mm (R. biformata PM2) (Fig. 1B). These findings indicated the considerable variability in antagonism between B. velezensis and marine Flavobacteriaceae. To further validate this antagonism in liquid culture, we examined the interaction between strain SPE2 and R. biformata PM2, a representative Flavobacteriaceae strain, in 2216E marine broth. Co-cultivation of the two strains resulted in a time-dependent decline in the viable counts of strain PM2 after 24 h, whereas the population of strain SPE2 remained at higher levels than that in the alone cultured control, indicating that strain SPE2 can effectively suppress the growth of strain PM2 (Fig. 1C).

Genomic capacity for production of antibacterial metabolites in B. velezensis SPE2 explains its antagonism

.

The BGCs of strain SPE2 and eight Flavobacteriaceae strains in this study were predicted by using antiSMASH version 8.0 [30]. Across all strains, 39 BGCs were identified and separated into nine distinct secondary metabolite subclasses, including arylpolyene, azole-containing ribosomally synthesized and post-translationally modified peptide (azole-containing RiPP), cytokinin, non-ribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS), polyketide synthase-like (PKS-like), type III polyketide synthase (T3PKS), terpene, trans-acyltransferase polyketide synthase (trans-AT PKS), and one other type (Fig. 2A, Tables S5 and S6). It was worth noting that strain SPE2 harbored 15 BGCs, in stark contrast to the Flavobacteriaceae strains which possessed only three BGCs per genome on average. This disparity underscores the genomic potential for secondary metabolite biosynthesis in strain SPE2 (Table S6).

Biosynthetic potential of strain SPE2 and eight Flavobacteriaceae isolates. A Identification of BGCs across genomes of all the analyzed bacterial isolates. Left: Bar plot showing the total number of BGCs per isolate. Right: Heat-map displaying the abundance of each BGC types per isolate. Heat-map colours correspond to number of BGCs detected. B Graphic representation of the gene clusters related to the production of secondary metabolites with antimicrobial action in strain SPE2

Several BGC types, such as the azole-containing RiPP, NRPS, PKS-like, trans-AT PKS, and one BGC of other type, were exclusively identified in strain SPE2 (Fig. 2A). The central genes of these BGCs were predicted to be involved in the biosynthesis of lipopeptides (fengycin and surfactin), dipeptides (bacilysin), ribosomally synthesized peptides (plantazolicin), macrolides (macrolactin H and difficidin), siderophores (bacillibactin), and other type (bacillaene) (Fig. 2B, Table S7). As key members of natural product classes, these specialized metabolites have been reported to exhibit broad-spectrum antimicrobial properties [40]. The unique possession of these BGCs in strain SPE2 implies its potential capacity to suppress Flavobacteriaceae strains via the production of specialized metabolites.

Molecular network and compound family annotation highlight lipopeptide metabolites from B. velezensis SPE2

Given that inhibitory interactions can be influence by cultivation conditions or other environmental factors [20], two culture media, including 2216E marine broth and TSB which is a standard medium widely used for high efficiently producing secondary metabolite [41], were applied to evaluate the chemical diversity of secondary metabolites produced by strain SPE2. Following cultivation in both media, metabolite extraction revealed a fivefold greater yield from TSB culture compared to 2216E culture (Fig. S6). Drop plate assay showed that crude extracts derived from both culture media completely inhibited growth of six Flavobacteriaceae strains, including R. biformata PM2, R. biformata SP8, M. cobaltidurans PR1, M. flavus PR66, F. amphidinii AC10, and Z. enoshimensis PT1, while exhibited weaker inhibitory effects for F. taeanensis SK9 and F. alvinocaridis SP22 (Fig. S7). Although the inhibitory effects were largely consistent across both extracts, the substantially greater crude extract yield achieved in TSB culture underscores the role of cultivation conditions in modulating biosynthesis of bioactive metabolites.

Additionally, to compare the metabolic profiles of the two extracts, we conducted UHPLC-ESI-HRMS/MS analysis and processed the subsequent data using the GNPS platform [34]. This analysis generated 1735 mass spectra, which were subjected to molecular network to reveal 228 nodes representing 129 distinct molecular families (each containing at least two structurally related analogs) based on fragmentation pattern similarities (Fig. 3A). The molecular networking analysis demonstrated that strain SPE2 produced a substantial number of shared metabolites (83 nodes, accounting for 36.4%) in both culture media, as indicated by the dual-colored node pie charts in the molecular network (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, 81 nodes (35.5%) were exclusively detected in the TSB culture, while another 64 nodes (28.1%) were uniquely produced in the 2216E culture (Fig. 3B). We further processed the obtained molecular network to in silico dereplication and the resulting was then uploaded to ClassyFire (http://classyfire.wishartlab.com/) for compound family annotation. Seven compound families were annotated and the top-ranked compound families included depsipeptides, as well as amino acids, peptides and analogues (Fig. 3C, Table S8). Subsequently, we focused on the information of these metabolite identities within the molecular network. The analysis revealed three distinct clusters (Fig. 3A), which were annotated as lipopeptide derivatives, such as agrastatin A, surfactin B, and iso-C15 bacillomycin D (Fig. 3D). In cluster 1, agrastatin A was annotated with a molecular ion peak at m/z 1449.79 [M + H]+ corresponding to its molecular formula of C71H108N12O20 (Fig. 3D). This compound, originally found in B. subtilis AQ713, has been reported to display a broad fungicidal spectrum [42]. Cluster 2 was annotated as surfactin analogues, including surfactins B (m/z 1040.69 [M+NH4]+) and C (m/z 1054.70 [M+NH4]+) (Fig. 3D), which were renowned for their potent antibacterial activities [43]. Cluster 3 was annotated as members of the bacillomycin family, such as iso-C15 bacillomycin D and iso-C17 bacillomycin D. These annotations were supported by matching their fragmentation spectra at m/z 1045.56 [M + H]+ and m/z 1073.59 [M + H]+ with those in databases (Fig. 3D). These compounds were shown to exhibit significant antifungal activity, particularly filamentous fungi [44]. Given that the partial overlap of lipopeptide metabolites in both extracts and their superior antibacterial efficacy (Fig. 3D), these lipopeptides are likely to play a major role in mediating the interbacterial competition of strain SPE2 with marine Flavobacteriaceae strains.

Surfactin-like lipopeptides contribute to the antagonism of B. velezensis SPE2 against Flavobacteriaceae strains

Based on the fivefold higher extraction yield in TSB versus 2216E medium, we performed a large-scale cultivation (10 L) of strain SPE2 in TSB medium to obtain sufficient crude extract (15.59 g) for downstream analysis (Fig. 4A). Following activity-guided purification, two pure compounds (1 and 2) that exhibited strong inhibitory effects against the target strain R. biformata SP8 were successfully isolated (Figs. 4A – D, and S8). These pure compounds were subjected to ESI-MS and ESI-HRMS/MS experiments in positive ion mode. Protonated parent ion peaks in ESI-MS data were observed at m/z 1022.50 [M + H]+ for fraction 1 (Fig. S9) and m/z 1036.52 [M + H]+ for fraction 2 (Fig. S10). Both compounds displayed identical fragmentation patterns with the daughter ion peaks of [M + H-Leu]+, [M + H-2Leu]+, [M + H-2Leu-Asp]+, [M + H-2Leu-Asp-Val]+, [M + H-3Leu-Asp-Val]+, and [M + H-4Leu-Asp-Val]+ in ESI-HRMS/MS data (Figs. 4E, S11 and S12). These fragmentation profiles confirmed the presence of conserved peptide moieties in both molecules. The observed 14 Da mass difference (equivalent to a -CH₂- unit) between these compounds suggested that they were homologues with differing in fatty acid chain lengths (Fig. 4E). Subsequent NMR analysis (Figs. S13 – S16, Table S9) combined with the ESI-HRMS/MS data (Fig. 4E) led to the identification of compounds 1 and 2 as n-C14 surfactin and anteiso-C15 surfactin (Fig. 4F), according to the same ESI-HRMS/MS and NMR data as previously reported [45].

Considering the phycosphere as a chemical marketplace where metabolites are exchanged between the host alga and associated bacteria [1], we investigated whether surfactin biosynthesis in strain SPE2 is modulated by metabolites secreted by the host or by co-occurring bacterial species. The srfAA gene, which is responsible for the biosynthesis of surfactin, was found in strain SPE2 (Fig. 2B). Therefore, we examined the expression of srfAA under different cultivation conditions. Notably, although the addition of algal spent medium had no significant effect on the growth of strain SPE2 (Fig. 5A), the expression of srfAA was induced by phycosphere-derived metabolites. Specifically, the elevated srfAA expression in the presence of algal spent medium suggests that surfactin is actively produced within the phycosphere (Fig. 5B). The expression of srfAA in 2216E marine broth supplemented with 10% algal spent medium at 48 h showed a more than 2-fold increase compared to that in 2216E marine broth alone (Fig. 5C). A comparable induction pattern was also observed in TSB medium at 48 h, with significantly higher srfAA expression in TSB supplemented with 10% algal spent medium than in TSB alone (Fig. 5C). These results demonstrate that the biosynthesis of surfactin in strain SPE2 is also influenced by phycosphere-derived metabolites.

To evaluate the antibacterial activity of n-C14 surfactin and anteiso-C15 surfactin, susceptibility testing was performed against eight Flavobacteriaceae strains at different concentrations (Fig. 6A). Both compounds demonstrated potent antibacterial activity against R. biformata PM2, R. biformata SP8, M. cobaltidurans PR1, M. flavus PR66, F. amphidinii AC10, and Z. enoshimensis PT1 with the MIC values ranging from 3.13 to 12.5 µM (Fig. 6B). In contrast, F. taeanensis SK9 showed weak susceptibility (MIC > 200 µM) to both compounds, which is consistent with the antagonistic assay results (Fig. 1B). The time-kill kinetic assay demonstrated distinct bactericidal manner between the two surfactin derivatives against R. biformata PM2 (Fig. S17). n-C14 surfactin elicited rapid killing with complete eradication after 10 min of exposure. In contrast, anteiso-C15 surfactin exhibited time-dependent activity, wherein 10 min of exposure induced growth inhibition and extended 20 min of treatment achieved full bactericidal action (Fig. S17). Notably, n-C14 surfactin, which contains a linear 14-carbon fatty acid chain, exhibited moderate antibacterial activity (MIC = 50 µM) against F. alvinocaridis SP22, whereas anteiso-C15 surfactin, featuring a branched 15-carbon fatty acid chain, displayed weaker activity (MIC > 200 µM) (Fig. 6B). These results suggest that their antibacterial activity is influenced by both the length and structure of the fatty acid chain. Overall, both compounds displayed varying degrees of inhibition against eight Flavobacteriaceae strains, indicating their synergistic contribution to the antagonistic effects of B. velezensis SPE2 against marine Flavobacteriaceae.

Surfactin-like lipopeptides induce cell death in Flavobacteriaceae strains by damaging the cell membrane

To evaluate the potential mechanism inducing cell death of the two lipopeptides, the cell viability and cellular integrity of the sensitive R. biformata PM2 were assessed after being treated with both compounds. The permeability of the bacterial membrane was evaluated using dual fluorescent staining with N, N-dimethylaniline N-oxide (DMAO) and propidium iodide (PI) after incubation. The results showed that untreated cells of strain PM2 exhibited green fluorescence, whereas those being treated with either lipopeptide exhibited intensified red fluorescence (Fig. 7). These results indicated that the permeability of the cell membrane increased following surfactin-like lipopeptides treatment, ultimately leading to cell death in Flavobacteriaceae strains.

Discussion

Microbial interactions are pivotal drivers of bacterial community dynamics in phycosphere, and their inherent complexity is increasingly recognized [6, 7, 46]. However, research on phycosphere microbiomes has disproportionately focused on numerically dominant taxa (e.g., Flavobacteriaceae) [6], systematically overlooking low-abundance members despite their potential functional significance. Here, we explored the interference competition between Bacillaceae and Flavobacteriaceae, two bacterial families with contrasting abundance patterns in phycosphere (Fig. 1A). Intriguingly, although the Bacillaceae strain exhibited lower relative abundance, it effectively suppressed the growth of marine Flavobacteriaceae strains, the dominant family in this niche (Fig. 1A and C). This finding suggests that microbial community dynamics are not dictated solely by numerical dominance [47], but are also shaped by antagonistic interactions that contribute to ecological equilibria.

The ecological success of Flavobacteriaceae in phycosphere is traditionally ascribed to their dual metabolic specialization, including degradation of phytoplankton-derived macromolecules into bioavailable substrates and synthesis of host-protective metabolites [6, 24]. Our recent findings expand this understanding by revealing distinct taxonomic composition between dinoflagellate- and diatom-associated microbiota, underscoring the importance of host-specific selection pressures [8]. For instance, dinoflagellate-associated bacterial communities showed enrichment in motility related genes and sulfur compound utilization pathways (exemplified by Roseibium), which aligns with their host’s production of sulfur-containing metabolites and the requirement for chemotactic navigation in dynamic environments. Conversely, diatom-associated bacteria exhibited specialized adaptations for glycan utilization (as seen in Polaribacter), corresponding to their host’s predominant release of polysaccharide exudates. Beyond these specialized taxa, we also identified ubiquitous “foundation” genera (e.g., Marivita and Marinobacter) that persist across various phycosphere due to their superior environmental adaptability. This trait appears linked to their enriched Type VI secretion systems (T6SS), a conserved feature among phycosphere bacteria globally. In contrast, low-abundance members, such as those belonging to the family Bacillaceae, generally lacked T6SS and TonB-dependent transporters SusCD glycan uptake systems [8]. These adaptive patterns collectively explain why optimized families like Rhodobacteraceae, Flavobacteraceae, and Alteromonadaceae consistently dominate phycosphere communities through host-specific resource exploitation and niche specialization, while Bacillaceae remain relatively scarce despite possessing competitive traits. Notably, our results demonstrate that these intrinsic advantages do not preclude competitive suppression by low-abundance antagonists, revealing an ecological scenario where competitors coexist within shared niche. This interpretation is further supported by established roles of surfactin in facilitating colonization of B. subtilis within synthetic communities [48]. Some Bacillaceae are known for mutualistic interactions with phytoplankton [13, 14], their roles as competitors have been neglected. Using B. velezensis SPE2 as a model organism for Bacillaceae species [9, 47], we found that its suppression for competitively superior Flavobacteriaceae represents a multifaceted survival strategy, enabling persistence in the phycosphere despite low-abundance. Numerous Flavobacteriaceae members, such as Flagellimonas, Robiginitalea, and Maribacter, were predominant in both diatom- and dinoflagellate-associated communities (with a relative abundance > 1% at the genus level) [8], indicating that the antagonistic interactions between Bacillus and these bacterial genera may be a general phenomenon across different phycosphere. Such interspecific competition may act as a stabilizing force, preventing the over dominance of any single group and thereby enhancing microbiome resilience.

Building on the evidence of antagonistic interactions shaping phycosphere dynamics (even involving low-abundance taxa), it becomes clear that resident microorganisms endure intense competition for nutrients concurrent with significant environmental challenges, including ultraviolet radiation, oxidative stress, and rapid fluctuations in physicochemical parameters [1, 49]. This confluence of biotic and abiotic stressors creates a potent selective pressure favoring traits that enhance competitive fitness and stress resilience. Consequently, the specialized metabolite production represents a key adaptation strategy, enabling bacteria to antagonize competitors while surviving adverse conditions. A prime illustration is Phaeobacter inhibens whose dominance in the phycosphere is significantly attributed to the secretion of tropodithietic acid (TDA), a sulfur-containing antibacterial compound [50, 51]. Crucially, beyond its ecological role in microbial warfare with niches like the phycosphere, TDA exhibits potent broad-spectrum antibiotic activity against clinically relevant pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus and Vibrio anguillarum [52]. Its unique dithiete scaffold positions it as a promising structural template for developing novel anti-infective agents, especially given the urgent need to combat escalating antibiotic resistance [52]. These results significantly advance our understanding of marine chemical ecology, demonstrating how competitive interactions in marine micro-environments like the phycosphere drive the evolution of potent bioactive molecules. Furthermore, the remarkable bacterial diversity inherent in this niche, coupled with the intense selection pressures, underscores the immense potential of phycosphere as a critically underexplored reservoir for discovering specialized metabolites with significant pharmaceutical relevance.

As an illustration of such chemically mediated competition, the present study reveals that B. velezensis SPE2 secretes surfactin-type lipopeptides in response to antagonism against marine Flavobacteriaceae. Structurally, surfactin is an amphiphilic molecule comprising a hydrophobic fatty acid chain and a hydrophilic cyclic peptide moiety [53], which confers its potent surface active properties. Antibacterial assays revealed distinct susceptibility patterns among Flavobacteriaceae strains to n-C14 surfactin and anteiso-C15 surfactin (Fig. 6A). Six strains (R. biformata PM2/SP8, M. cobaltidurans PR1, M. flavus PR66, F. amphidinii AC10, and Z. enoshimensis PT1) exhibited high sensitivity (MIC = 3.13 − 12.5 µM), whereas F. taeanensis SK9 demonstrated unexpected resistance (MIC > 200 µM), suggesting intrinsic surfactin tolerance in some Flavobacteriaceae members (Fig. 6B). Comparative analysis of membrane lipid profiles across the eight strains discovered that F. taeanensis exhibited the predominance of iso-C15:0 and iso-C15:1 fatty acids and higher fatty acid diversity (Table S10), suggesting that intrinsic resistance may stem from strain-specific variations in main fatty acid composition and membrane architecture. This observation aligns with previous findings in B. subtilis, where the modifications both in the polar head group and the fatty acid region confer self-tolerance to surfactin, supporting membrane architecture as a determinant of surfactin resistance [54]. Notably, molecular networking analysis revealed that, besides the two major surfactins (n-C14 and anteiso-C15) in cluster 2, a series of low-abundance surfactin analogues and compounds belonging to other structural classes were present (Fig. 3A). These findings do not exclude the possibility that these minor constituents participate in the antagonism through synergistic interactions. Indeed, the co-occurrence of multiple metabolite classes suggests that the observed antagonism likely arises from the combined action of the dominant surfactins and the accompanying low-abundance metabolites. For instance, plant-associated B. velezensis has been shown to mobilize a substantial portion of its secondary metabolites, including polyketides and surfactin lipopeptides, in response to rhizosphere competitors [23], illustrating how interspecies interactions trigger multiple classes of antibacterial metabolites that act in concert.

The biosynthesis of surfactin in strain SPE2 is also influenced by phycosphere-derived metabolites (Fig. 5C). It has been reported that Bacillus cells will upregulate surfactin production to coordinate several adaptive responses, when environmental nutrients become limited [55]. This phenomenon indicates that host-derived exudates can alter the nutritional microenvironment of co-occurring Bacillus populations, thereby modulating the regulatory networks governing surfactin biosynthesis. Beyond its role in interbacterial competition, surfactin has been shown to exhibit a concentration-dependent algicidal effect against the toxic dinoflagellate Karlodinium veneficum [56]. This suggests that, in the phycosphere, surfactin may also mediate interactions between bacteria and their hosts. Our experimental evidence demonstrates that n-C14 surfactin and anteiso-C15 surfactin, at the MIC value of 6.25 µM, trigger membrane damage in R. biformata PM2 (Fig. 7). This membrane-targeting mechanism underpins its antibacterial activity and likely represents a broader ecological strategy employed by Bacillus to suppress competitors in complex polymicrobial niches. Comparative studies highlight the multifunctional role of surfactin in biofilm formation [57], phylloplane persistence [58], and rhizosphere colonization [59]. Collectively, these findings position surfactin as a key metabolite enabling Bacillus to counteract its numerical disadvantage in phycosphere. This competitive strategy, evidenced across diverse environments, from plant rhizospheres to marine phycosphere, suggests a conserved ecological principle.

Conclusion

We propose that the lipopeptide metabolites produced by B. velezensis play an important role in mounting multifaceted defensive strategy to enhance its fitness and persistence in competitive environment. This research advances our understanding of the chemical ecology governing microbial interactions in marine environments, particularly the understudied dynamics between rare and dominant taxa in phytoplankton-associated microbiomes. Furthermore, this ecology-driven approach underscores that the phycosphere holds considerable potential for yielding novel bioactive compounds, representing an underexplored frontier in the search for new pharmaceutical and biotechnological resources.
