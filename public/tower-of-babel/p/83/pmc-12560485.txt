The negative responses and acclimation mechanisms of Neopyropia yezoensis conchocelis filaments to short- and long-term ocean acidification
Authors: Danrui Li, Li Liu, Ruowen Ding, Xianghai Tang, Yuanyuan Wang, Yan Zhao, Xuexi Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOcean acidification (OA) significantly alters the carbonate chemistry of seawater, leading to a decrease of seawater pH to impact the physiological and biochemical processes of those intertidal macroalgae. Previous studies have focused on the response of macroalgae to OA at thallus stage, while the effects at filamentous stage remain insufficiently explored.ResultsThis study investigated the physiological-biochemical and molecular mechanisms of the filamentous conchocelis stage (the diploid sporophyte) of Neopyropia yezoensis responding to short- (5 days) and long-term (20 days) OA (2000 ppm CO2, pH 7.53). The results showed that short-term OA rapidly inhibited the growth and photosynthesis, suppressed chlorophyll synthesis and nitrogen assimilation, and down-regulated genes associated with photosynthesis, Calvin cycle, and carbohydrate metabolism of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments. However, N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments showed acclimation strategies under long-term OA, in terms of metabolic reorganization, prioritizing stress tolerance over growth. Further weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) based on the metabolomic and transcriptomic results under long-term OA showed that the strategy was manifested by the accumulation of soluble sugars as osmolytes, lipid β-oxidation compensating for energy deficits, and H+ extrusion mediated via ABC transporters.ConclusionsThis study suggested time-depended responses of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments to OA, proving the pronounced negative effects of OA on N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, revealing N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments could acclimate to long-term OA by resource reallocation. These findings provide new insight into the survival of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under OA, and facilitate the development of technologies and breeding strategies for improved acidification tolerance in N. yezoensis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07432-7.Keywords: Ocean acidification, Neopyropia yezoensis, Filamentous stage, Resource relocation, Acclimation mechanism

Background

Since the Industrial Revolution, atmospheric CO2 concentrations have surged from 278 to 422 ppm (a 51.8% increase; Keeling Curve, 2024) due to human activities [1], and model simulations predicted that atmospheric CO2 levels could even reach 2000 ppm by 2300 [2]. The ocean has absorbed approximately 30% of atmospheric CO2, triggering cascading disruptions in marine carbon chemistry, further leading to ocean acidification (OA) [3]. This chemical restructuring of carbon in the ocean poses a direct threat to marine primary producers relied on stable carbon chemistry, including pH decline, increase in pCO2, and shifts in dissolved inorganic carbon (DIC, including CO2, HCO3⁻, and CO32⁻) speciation [4].

As key contributors of marine primary productivity, intertidal macroalgae play pivotal roles in the biological CO2 pump and ecosystem stability [2, 5]. The ecological significance of intertidal macroalgae is particularly pronounced in intertidal zones where they experience naturally fluctuating pH conditions, making them potentially more vulnerable to OA than subtidal species [6]. Previous studies revealed the complex responses of marine macroalgae to OA. For example, Chen et al. [7] demonstrated that OA enhanced the photosynthesis and growth of Neoporphyra haitanensis thalli, whereas OA resulted in a decrease in growth of Neopyropia yezoensis thalli at low light intensity [2]. These contrasting responses highlighted that intrinsic factors (like species and life cycle stages) and extrinsic factors (like temperature and light intensity) could impact the responding and acclimation strategies of marine macroalgae to OA [8–10].

N. yezoensis is an important intertidal economic macroalgae, inhabits in the intertidal zone and periodically affected by various stresses, compelling it to evolve unique mechanisms for acclimating flexibly to environmental fluctuations [11, 12]. Notably, it has a biphasic life cycle, consisting of filamentous stage (conchocelis phase, diploid) and thallus stage (blade phase, haploid), each occupying distinct ecological niches [13]. The entire life cycle of N. yezoensis alters between filamentous stage and thallus stage, which of these two stages inhabit under distinctly different environmental conditions [2]. The conchocelis filaments typically develop inside shells or other calcareous substrates with limited exposure to sunlight. In contrast, the thalli adhere to intertidal rocks, where they are regularly submerged in seawater and subjected to direct sunlight [14]. The conchocelis filaments cultivated in seawater or liquid medium are referred to as free-living conchocelis. Due to the limited availability of natural shells and the complexity of traditional methods, free-living conchocelis is now being utilized to innovate seedling cultivation techniques [2]. Obviously, these two stages of N. yezoensis life cycle exhibit significantly difference in morphology, physiological metabolism, and stress tolerance [15].

While current research has primarily focused on the responses of thallus stage rather than filamentous stage to multiple environmental stressors, including OA [16], low temperature [17], drought [18], and UV-B radiation [19] among others. Emerging studies revealed differences in stress response mechanisms between filamentous and thallus stages of Neoporphyra and Neopyropia. For example, Wang et al. [20] found that N. haitanensis conchocelis filaments exhibited greater sensitivity to increased CO2 concentrations than thalli. Ma et al. [2] demonstrated that elevated CO2 enhanced N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments growth but reduced the growth of thalli at low light intensity. Weng et al. [21] observed that salt and oligoagar stresses significant changed the floridoside (a photochemical compound) in N. haitanensis, and the thalli displayed more pronounced changes in floridoside compared to the conchocelis filaments under high temperature condition. These results indicated that the response characteristics and mechanisms of the filamentous stage to OA might fundamentally differ from the thallus stage. However, the study focused on the response of the filamentous stage to OA remained less understood, particularly from a multi-omics perspective.

Therefore, this study firstly compared the short- (5 days) and long-term (20 days) OA responses of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments by integrating physiological, metabolomic, and transcriptomic analysis. The main objectives of this study were to: (1) elucidate the physiological inhibition and stress responses under short-term OA; (2) investigate the metabolic reprogramming and potential acclimation mechanisms under long-term OA of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments. This study focuses on elucidating the intrinsic physiological and molecular response mechanisms of the filamentous stage to OA, which provides the foundational knowledge necessary to later understand its performance in complex natural settings. Understanding these stage-specific responses could provide important data basis for accurately predict future changes in intertidal community structure, as OA might alter the competitive schemes among inertial macroalgae communities. Besides, for the nori aquaculture industry, identifying this vulnerable life stage is essential for developing targeted mitigation and breeding strategies that could resistant future global changes.

Methods

Culture of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments were kindly given by MoE Key Laboratory of Marine Genetics and Breeding, Ocean University of China. They were cultured in the CO2 incubator (Fig. S1). N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments was cultured in Provasoli Enriched Seawater (PES) medium under 50 µmol m−2 s−1, with the 12 h:12 h light: dark cycle at 20 °C [22, 23]. The OA experiment lasted for 20 days, and the sterilized seawater (SSW) pre-saturated with CO2 for 24 h was replaced every 2 d. To maintain the constant CO2 concentration, the CO2 partial pressure was regulated by inflating CO2 to the culture system at the rate of 280–310 mL min−1. Two CO2 concentrations were established: 400 ppm for the control group and 2000 ppm for the OA group. The pH values in the control group were maintained between 8.13 and 8.19, while those in the OA group ranged from 7.49 to 7.55 (Fig. S2), which was a level relevant to intertidal zone acidification conditions and necessary to cause robust physiological and molecular mechanism responses [24–26]. Each group was set up as triplicates. The pH value of the culture medium was monitored using a pH meter (Sartorius, PB-10, Germany). The wet weight (WT, g) and physiological-biochemical parameters were sampled on day 0, 5 and 20, referring to control, short- and long-term OA samples. Physiological-biochemical parameters determination

The physiological-biochemical parameters included photosystem II (PSII) photosynthetic parameters, pigments composition, cellular carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur contents, cellular soluble sugar, total protein, lipids content, and Rubisco activity.

Imaging-PAM (Heinz Walz, Effeltrich, Germany) was used for measuring of PSII photosynthetic parameters. N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments in the two treatments were placed in the dark for 20 min before measurements. The minimum fluorescence yield (Fo), maximum fluorescence yield (Fm), and maximum fluorescence value (F’m) were recorded to calculate the maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (Fv/Fm) and actual quantum yield (Y(II)).

For the determination of pigments contents, the N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples weighing 0.1 g were ground and mixed with 10 mL of 80% acetone solution (V/V). After extraction at 4 °C for 24 h in dark, samples were centrifuged (10000 g, 10 min) at 4 °C, and the supernatants were analyzed using a spectrophotometer (UV-8000, Metash, Shanghai, China) at wavelengths of 480, 510, 630, 647, and 664 nm. For the cellular element composition analysis, the N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples were dried at 105 °C for 30 min, followed by drying at 70 °C until constant weight. The dried samples were ground into a fine powder, and 2 mg of each sample was placed in a tin boat for carbon, nitrogen, and sulfur content determination using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, Elementar, Germany).

Nutrient contents and metabolomic profiles of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments were assessed at day 20 to characterize the metabolic steady state achieved after long-term acclimation to OA. For the measurements of soluble sugar, total protein, lipids content, and Rubisco activity, commercial assay kits from Solarbio and Nanjing Jiancheng were used (soluble sugar content: A145-1-1, total protein content: A045-2-2, triglyceride content: BC0625, Rubisco activity: BC0440). The assays were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Metabolomic analysis and data processing

N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples in the control and OA group were collected on day 0 and 20 for metabolomic analysis, and each group had six replicates. 100 mg N. yezoensis filament sample was weighed into a centrifuge tube, and thoroughly mixed with 1 mL of cold methanol acetonitrile (v/v, 1:1) using the ultrasonic cleaner (SB25-12 DTD, SCIENTZ, Ningbo, China). The samples were centrifuged for 20 min (14000 g, 4℃), and the supernatants were collected for the further liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis.

The P. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples were placed in the autosampler and use UPLC-ESI-Q-Orbitrap-MS system (UHPLC, Shimadzu Nexera X2 LC-30AD, Shimadzu, Japan) coupled with Q-Exactive Plus (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, USA) for subsequent analysis. To monitor analytical stability and ensure data quality, a pooled quality control (QC) sample was prepared by combining equal aliquots of all experimental samples and was analyzed repeatedly throughout the acquisition sequence. Chromatographic separation was achieved on a reversed-phase column (ACQUITY UPLC® HSS T3) with a water/acetonitrile gradient containing 0.1% formic acid. Mass spectrometry data were acquired in both positive and negative ionization modes over a mass range of 70−1050 Da at high resolution.

Raw data were processed using MS-DIAL software for peak picking, alignment, and annotation. Metabolites were identified by matching exact mass (mass tolerance < 10 ppm) and MS/MS spectra against public databases and an in-house standard library. Only metabolic features demonstrating good reproducibility in the QC samples (relative standard deviation, RSD < 30%) were retained for subsequent statistical analysis, ensuring the reliability of the dataset.

Principal component analysis (PCA) was used for unsupervised pattern recognition and to visualize overall metabolic variation between groups, and orthogonal projections to latent structures-discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) were subsequently used as a supervised method to maximize separation between treatment and control groups and identify the most significant variables contributing to that separation [29]. Metabolites with variable importance on projection (VIP) >1 and p < 0.05 were considered as differential metabolites (DMs) between the control and OA groups. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis was performed on DMs, with statistically significant enrichment at p < 0.05. For comprehensive details on instrument parameters, chromatographic gradients, and full data processing workflows, please refer to the supplementary methods.

Transcriptomic analysis and data processing

N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples were collected on day 0, 5 and 20 for the transcriptomic analysis, and each group had three replicates. 0.5 g N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments samples were weighed into a centrifuge tube and thoroughly grounded for further experiment. Total RNA was extracted from N. yezoensis filament samples using Trizol kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). RNA integrity and quality were verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Sequencing libraries were prepared from poly(A)-enriched mRNA using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq6000 platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were subjected to stringent quality control. Adapters and low-quality reads were filtered out using fastp to obtain high-quality clean reads, and were aligned to the assembly genome of N. yezoensis using HISAT2 [30]. To quantify the expression abundance and variations of each gene, fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) values was used.

Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using the DESeq2 package in R. Genes with an adjusted p-value (false discovery rate, FDR) < 0.05 and |log2FC| ≥ 1.5 were considered statistically significant. Principal component analysis (PCA) was employed to assess overall sample relationships and batch effects. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes was performed based on the KEGG database, with pathways considered significantly enriched at FDR ≤ 0.05. For detailed information on sequencing depth, assembly statistics, and full analysis parameters, please refer to the supplementary materials.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

WGCNA R package (v1.73) was applied to the transcriptomic data based on the trimmed mean of M-values of normalized fragments per kilobase of exons per million mapped fragments (FPKM) values. A soft-thresholding power (β) was selected based on scale-free topology (R² >0.8) to construct a signed network. Hierarchical clustering with dynamic tree cutting (minModuleSize = 100, mergeCutHeight = 0.25) identified gene module and labeled by colors. Module eigengenes (MEs) were calculated as the first principal component of each module. Selected DMs levels were correlated with MEs using Pearson correlation. Module memberships (MM, correlation between gene expression and ME) were calculated, and hub genes (top 10% MM, p < 0.05) were extracted. Hub genes were annotated via KEGG pathways (clusterProfiler, v4.16.0) and mapped to correlated metabolites to identify key pathways. The enrichplot R package (v1.28.2) was used to visualize subnetworks of hub genes.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and the figures were generated using OriginPro 2023 (OriginLab, Northampton, USA). Means and standard deviation (S. D.) for triplicates of each group were calculated and presented in the figures and tables. The least significant difference (LSD), as part of a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), was employed to assess the statistical significance between samples in the control and OA groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. PCA and OPLS-DA models were conducted using the R package gmodels for the RNA sequencing dataset.

Results

Effects of short- and long-term OA on growth and photosynthesis in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

The RGR of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments were 72.7% and 64.0% of the controls in the short- and long-term OA group, respectively (Fig. 1A). The Fv/Fm and Y(II) values of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments decreased by 12.6% and 23.5% in the short- and long-term OA group compared to the controls, respectively (p < 0.05), and the values did not change significantly until long-term OA (Fig. 1B and C). The chl a and car contents were 30.0% and 36.0% lower than the controls under short-term OA, respectively, and were kept declining to 59.3% and 50.2% lower than the controls under long-term OA, respectively (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D and E).

Effects of short- and long-term OA on cellular element contents, biochemical composition and Rubisco activity in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

Compared to controls, the cellular carbon content in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments decreased by 6.6% under short-term OA (p < 0.05), and remained until long-term OA (Fig. 2A). Cellular nitrogen content showed significant decreases of 17.4% and 48.0% under short- and long-term OA, respectively (p < 0.05, Fig. 2B). No significant changes were observed in cellular sulfur content of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under OA (p > 0.05, Fig. 2C). The C/N ratio rose significantly by 17.5% and 89.5% under short- and long-term OA, respectively (p < 0.05; Fig. 2D). Soluble sugar content increased to 2.53 times under long-term OA compared to the controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 2E). Conversely, total protein and lipid contents fell by 69.4% and 20.2% under long-term OA compared to controls (p < 0.05; Fig. 2E). Rubisco activity exhibited no significant change under long-term OA (p > 0.05, Fig. 2F).

Effects of long-term OA on metabolome of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

The PCA results indicated a clear separation between samples from the control and long-term OA group (Fig. S3A). The OPLS-DA model was fitted, yielding the main parameters R2Y of 0.98 (Fig. S3B). The closer the R2Y values are to 1, the more reliable the OPLS-DA model is, suggesting that the model was effective in distinguishing metabolomic results between the control and OA groups [31]. A total of 663 DMs were identified in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under long-term OA compared to the controls, with 187 up-regulated and 476 down-regulated DMs (Fig. S3C and S3D). These DMs were mainly annotated into categories of sugars, lipids and amino acids (Fig. 3A and B). Notably, these DMs were significantly enriched in carbon metabolism pathways, such as TCA cycle, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, glyoxylate and CoA biosynthesis, and biosynthesis of amino acid pathways, including valine, leucine, and isoleucine metabolism, arginine biosynthesis, beta-arginine biosynthesis, and cysteine and methionine metabolism (Fig. 3C). The accumulation of soluble sugars and amino acids, coupled with the changes in lipid content, suggested a metabolic reprogramming towards osmoprotection and membrane remodeling of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under long-term OA.

Effects of short- and long-term OA on transcriptome of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

The PCA results showed distinct aggregation distributions among the control, short- and long- term OA groups (Fig. S4A). The correlation coefficients for each group were close to 1, indicating that the transcriptomic results were reliable and the sample selection was appropriate (Fig. S4B). The short-term OA group exhibited a greater number of down-regulated DEGs (1117) than up-regulated DEGs (772; Fig. S4C and S4D). Conversely, the long-term OA group displayed more up-regulated DEGs of 1070 than down-regulated DEGs of 758 (Fig. S4C and S4D).

To further investigate the expression patterns of genes at different acidification time, KEGG pathways enrichment analysis was conducted of all DEGs in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under short- and long-term OA (Fig. 4). The results showed that the pathways related in photosynthesis and carbon metabolism, including photosynthesis-antenna protein, photosynthesis, carbon fixation in photosynthetic organisms, carbon metabolism, and glycolysis pathways were enriched in both short- and long-term OA groups, with most of DEGs down-regulating under OA in these pathways (Fig. 4A and D). The widespread down-regulation of these genes pointed to a fundamental repression of energy acquisition as a primary response of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments to OA. Besides, significant enrichment was observed in the short-term OA group for the ribosome biogenesis in eukaryotes, biosynthesis of amino acid, and nitrogen metabolism pathways, most of which were down-regulated (Fig. 4A and B). In the long-term OA group, the DEGs enriched in the fatty acid metabolism and biosynthesis were down-regulated, and those enriched in the phenylalanine metabolism and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis were up-regulated (Fig. 4C and D). Notably, compared to the short-term OA, DEGs enriched in the pentose phosphate pathway, ABC transporters, glutathione metabolism, and cyanoamino acid metabolism pathways were up-regulated in the long-term OA group (Fig. 4E and F).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) results

To elucidate the regulatory networks between genes and metabolites and uncover potential molecular mechanisms underlying the response of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments to long-term OA, WGCNA was conducted based on 10,726 DEGs. The DMs involved in key metabolic pathways (sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, and TCA cycle) were used as phenotypic traits to construct a co-expression module-trait relationship framework under both control and long-term OA treatments (Fig. 5). Hierarchical clustering identified eight distinct gene modules (Fig. 5A). Among these, the MEbrown module, comprising 5954 genes, exhibited strong positive correlations with fatty acids and amino acids and negative correlations with soluble sugars and organic acids (R² >0.9, p < 0.05; Fig. 5B). In contrast, the MEblue module, consisting of 1413 genes, displayed the opposite correlation patterns relative to the MEbrown module (R² >0.8, p < 0.05; Fig. 5B). The expression profiles of hub genes within these two key modules were visualized using heatmaps (Fig. 5C and D), and subsequent KEGG network analysis was performed to elucidate the functional pathways potentially regulated by these hub genes (Fig. 5E and F). The KEGG enrichment showed that hub genes in the MEbrown module were mainly enriched in the photosynthesis, carbohydrate metabolism, antioxidant metabolism and fatty acid degradation pathways (Fig. 5E), while those in the MEblue module were primarily enriched in the biosynthesis of amino acids, lipid metabolism, energy metabolism and secondary metabolism pathways (Fig. 5F).

Discussion

Short-term OA inhibited the photosynthesis and energy metabolism of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

Given the fundamental differences between the filamentous stage (conchocelis phase, diploid) and thallus stage (blade phase, haploid), our study focused on the latter and found that short-term OA rapidly inhibited the physiological performance of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments. While the CO2 concentration used in our experiment was high (2000 ppm), it resulted in a stable and ecologically relevant pH (7.5) and was primarily employed as an effective tool to investigate the core response mechanisms of N. yezoensis to OA [26]. We observed a significant reduction in RGR (Fig. 1A) accompanied by a decrease in both Fv/Fm and Y(II) values (Fig. 1B and C), suggesting that elevated CO2 concentrations imposed low-pH stress under short-term OA, inhibiting the photosynthetic function [32]. Similar rapid photosynthetic inhibition under low pH conditions had been reported in Pyropia linearis by Israel et al. [33] and in Prionitis cornea by Kim et al. [34], suggesting this phenomenon may represent a common stress response among red algae. Such inhibition of growth could be associated with lowered photosynthetic activity and acidification of the medium [35]. However, previous researches indicated that N. haitanensis showed an increase in growth and photosynthesis under OA, which might be attributed to its greater physiological flexibility in carbon utilization and energy-efficient modulation of its carbon concentrating mechanism [35, 36]. This different interspecific response suggested that OA may alter the competitive balance between species, allowing more resilient species like N. haitanensis to gain dominance in future intertidal environments, thereby leading to the evolution of foundational species composition in intertidal communities [37].

Meanwhile, the cellular chl a and car contents (Fig. 1D and E), as well as the cellular nitrogen content were decreased under short-term OA (Fig. 2B). Since photosynthetic pigments are nitrogen-dependent [38], the decrease of photosynthetic pigment and nitrogen content could exacerbate photosynthetic inhibition by inhibiting the light capture capacity [39]. Previous research indicated that enhanced carbon fixation capacity could alleviate excessive CO2 accumulation under high CO2 concentration [40]. While in our study, short-term OA did not enhance the Rubisco activity or carbon fixation rates despite increased CO2 availability (Fig. 2F), suggesting that the detrimental effects of low pH stress likely suppressed the potential beneficial effects of increased CO2 availability. Our results indicated N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments were more vulnerable compared to the thalli. Studies of Chen et al. [16], Bao et al. [41], and Zhang et al. [42] indicated that the RGR, net photosynthetic rate and pigments contents of N. yezoensis thalli showed significant increase under similar OA conditions with our study.

On the molecular level, our transcriptomic analysis revealed down-regulation of genes encoding three critical energy metabolism pathways, including the photosynthesis, carbon metabolism and carbohydrate metabolism. The photosynthesis pathway included photosynthetic pigments, photosystems, electron transport system, and carbon dioxide reduction [17]. Light-harvesting complex proteins (LHC) consist of the major light-harvesting antenna including LHCI and LHCII, which absorb light energy and transfer it to the photosynthetic reactor center [43]. After short-term OA treatment, the inhibition of photosynthesis occurred through coordinated down-regulation of photosynthesis and photosynthesis-antenna proteins genes. Specifically, genes related to PSII components, like PsbM, PsbO and PsbP (log2FC=−1.50, −3.79, and − 1.44), and photosynthetic electron transport chain components, like PetC and PetH (log2FC=−1.55 and − 1.01), showed significantly down-regulation, directly correlating with observed Fv/Fm and photosynthetic pigments decrease (Fig. 6, Fig. S5 and Table S1).

After short-term OA, the genes enriched in Calvin cycle were down-regulated, with the most pronounced down-regulation observed in the genes encoding phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK), phosphoribulokinase (PRK), and ribose-5-phosphate isomerase (PRI) (Fig. 6 and Table S1). These genes encoding key enzymes involved in the regeneration of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), thus influencing the CO2 fixation process [44]. PRI catalyzes the conversion of ribulose-5-phosphate (Ru5P), and RPA (encoding PRI) exhibited down-regulation under short- and long-term OA (log2FC=−2.13) compared to the controls (Fig. 6 and Table S1). Similarly, PRK (encoding PRK), which phosphorylates Ru5P to form RuBP, also showed down-regulation under short-term OA (log2FC=−2.02) compared to the controls (Fig. 6 and Table S1). These down-regulation of genes impaired RuBP regeneration, explaining the contradictory phenomenon of decreased cellular carbon content in the presence of elevated CO2 concentration (Fig. 2A).

Concurrently, energy metabolism of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments undergoes restructuring under short-term OA. The inhibited activities of key enzymes in the glycolysis, such as phosphofructokinase (PFK) and pyruvate kinase (PK), were expected to hinder the glycolytic process [45]. PFK, PK and PGK were down-regulated (log2FC=−2.00, −1.07 and − 2.44) under short-term OA compared to the controls (Fig. 6 and Table S1). The genes responsible for fatty acid biosynthesis enzymes, including FabF (encoding 3-oxoacyl-[acyl-carrier-protein] synthase II), FabG (encoding 3-oxoacyl-[acyl-carrier protein] reductase), FabZ (encoding 3-hydroxyacyl-[acyl-carrier protein] dehydratase), and FabD (encoding [acyl-carrier-protein] S-malonyl transferase), were also down-regulated under short-term OA (log2FC=−1.37, −1.21, −0.91, and − 1.28) compared to the controls (Fig. 6 and Table S1), indicating that both glycolysis and fatty acid biosynthesis were inhibited (Fig. 6 and Table S1). Many studies have demonstrated that fatty acid accumulation occurred under high CO2 treatment, as enhanced carbon metabolism provides the necessary backbone for fatty acid synthesis [46, 47]. However, our results showed that both carbon fixation and carbon metabolism were inhibited after OA, resulting in an insufficient carbon skeleton and reduced capacity for fatty acid synthesis, which might explain the decreased lipid content in our research (Fig. 2C). In conclusion, short-term OA rapidly inhibited the growth and photosynthesis of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments by suppressing photosynthetic pigment synthesis, down-regulating key genes encoding light capture, electron transport, and carbon fixation, and disrupting energy metabolism.

Long-term OA triggered carbon allocation shifts from growth to stress protection in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments

Long-term OA exacerbated the inhibitory trends of RGR, chlorophyll pigments and nitrogen content (Fig. 1A, D and E). However, Fv/Fm, Y(II) values and carbon contents showed inhibition levels similar to those under short-term OA, suggesting that N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments might have developed the capacity to acclimate long-term OA stress.

The metabolomic analysis revealed that N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments exhibited a metabolic reorganization characterized by a strategic shift toward stress mitigation at the expense of growth-related processes under long-term OA (Fig. 7). The most significant alteration was the accumulation of soluble sugars, with galactose, trehalose, stachyose, maltose, melibiose, and gentiobiose showing significantly increases (log2FC = 1.91, 3.68, 7.06, 2.92, 2.27, and 2.78) after long-term OA (Fig. 7 and Table S2). Osmoregulation serves as a critical response mechanism of plants under abiotic stress, contributing to the maintenance of cellular osmotic stability to alleviate the detrimental impacts imposed by environmental stressors [48, 49]. The elevated soluble sugar contents could decrease the osmotic potential of cells, thereby helping to preserve the stability of the cellular structure and function [50]. Previous studies also reported that several macroalgae, including Ulva rigida [51], Hypnea spinella [52], and Saccharina latissima [53], exhibited the increase in soluble sugars contents under elevated CO2 concentration. Meantime, the amino acids contents, including critical amino acids glutamine, alanine, and histidine, were significantly decreased (log2FC=−2.35, −4.17, and − 2.26) after long-term OA (Fig. 7 and Table S2). The OA treatment led to reduced cellular nitrogen contents and down-regulation of genes encoding nitrate reductase (Table S1), thereby hindering the biosynthesis of amino acids. Wu et al. [54] also confirmed that increased CO2 concentration led to a reduction in the activity of key enzymes and gene expression involved in nitrogen metabolism in Pinus massoniana, resulting in decreased levels of total nitrogen, ammonium nitrogen, nitrite, and specific amino acids.

Changes in metabolite profiles of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under long-term OA. A Heatmap visualization of DMs. B Pathway-based analysis showing reorganization of metabolic pathways in response to OA

Besides, our study also found that four DMs related to the TCA cycle, including citric acid, isocitric acid, cis-aconitate, and fumaric acid, showed increases (log2FC = 2.19, 1.22, 3.32, 3.35) under long-term OA compared to the controls (Fig. 7 and Table S2), indicating potential bottlenecks in carbon processing despite elevated CO₂ availability. At the same time, essential fatty acids such as docosahexaenoic acid and icosapentaenoic acid (log2FC=−1.95 and − 4.26) were decreased under long-term OA (Fig. 7 and Table S2), revealing constraints on lipid biosynthesis even under carbon-enriched conditions. These changes showed that for N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, carbon was preferentially flowed toward immediate stress-protective compounds (like soluble sugars and organic acids) rather than growth-critical biomolecules (like proteins and membrane lipids), showing the biochemical trade-offs of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under long-term OA.

WGCNA explored the regulatory networks between DEGs and DMs

The WGCNA further elucidated the regulatory networks between DEGs and DMs of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments responding to long-term OA. The WGCNA results showed that the MEbrown module exhibited a significant down-regulation of photosynthesis-related genes (carbon fixation and light-harvesting complexes), alongside down-regulation of genes associated with carbohydrate metabolism pathways (glycolysis and fructose and mannose metabolism; Fig. 5D and Table S1). Such transcriptional profile suggests a substantial impairment in photo-assimilate production and carbohydrate metabolism under long-term OA, likely driven by OA-induced disruption of chloroplast function and energy depletion [55]. To compensate for this energy deficit, genes associated with fatty acid degradation were up-regulated (ACX3 and MFP, log2FC = 3.7 and 1.3, Table S1), indicating that N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments mobilized lipid as an alternative carbon source via β-oxidation. These shifts might exacerbate oxidative stress due to ROS generation, so N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments up-regulated genes related to ascorbate synthesis (UGD1 and LGALDH, log2FC = 1.2 and 1.1, Table S1) to scavenge ROS synthesis in both impaired photosynthesis and fatty acid degradation [56]. These changes indicated that under long-term OA, the energy applies shifted from carbohydrates to lipids in N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, while antioxidant capacity was enhanced to mitigate oxidative stress. The up-regulation of ascorbate synthesis is particularly significant, as it may help maintain redox homeostasis in chloroplasts affected by OA to preserve cellular integrity [57].

The MEblue module exhibited a down-regulation of genes related to the amino acid biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 5F), including histidine metabolism, which is critical for intracellular pH buffering capacity [58]. This suppression likely due to the nitrogen assimilation limitation under OA treatment, compounded by reduced carbon skeleton availability due to inhibition of photosynthesis (Fig. 1B and C, and Fig. 2A and B). Conversely, genes associated with ABC transporters were up-regulated (Abcd3, ABCB4 and ABCG14, log2FC = 1.8, 1.6 and 1.7, Table S1), likely to expel excess H⁺ to alleviate the damage caused by OA. The antagonistic regulation between MEbrown (energy generation via fatty acids) and MEblue (ion homeostasis via transporters) revealed a strategy of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments as sacrificing growth to prioritize stress mitigation under long-term OA. However, long-term OA might exceed the compensatory capacity of these responses, leading to growth suppression and oxidative damage (Fig. 1A).

Ecological implications, limitations, and future directions

Our study provided a mechanistic framework for understanding how short- and long-term OA might impact N. yezoensis at filamentous stage. While our controlled laboratory experiment could not directly predict for complex natural environment, it still allowed us to raise critical hypotheses about potential ecological and economic consequences based on our results. The observed trade-off of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, where long-term OA enhanced cellular stress tolerance at the cost of growth, suggested a potential constraint in conchocelis production under future OA conditions. This inhibition could reduce the production and release of conchospores and lead to a decrease in the edible thallus stage, thereby ultimately affecting overall aquaculture production [59]. Ecologically, if it translates to the field, as a foundational intertidal species, the changes in energy allocation strategy of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments could lead to reduced biomass, altering its competitive interactions and potentially disrupting local community structure and ecological function [37]. In summary, our study provided a mechanistic framework that OA might act as a selective stressor to reshape intertidal communities by altering the energy allocation strategies of key foundational species.

While this study provided valuable mechanistic insights into the physiological and molecular responses of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments to short- and long-term OA, extrapolating these implications to natural systems required caution due to several experimental limitations. Firstly, the controlled laboratory environment necessarily lacked key biotic and abiotic interactions present in nature, such as competition, predation, and the presence of a calcareous substrate for boring [2]. Secondly, the study focused on a single life cycle stage, and the response at the population level will depend on the integrated sensitivity of all stages in its life cycle. Finally, while the sample size of three replicates is standard for physiological and omics analysis, it inherently limited us to detect small-effect changes. Therefore, our ecological implications were focused on the most robust effects and revealed the intrinsic physiological and molecular response of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments under controlled conditions, rather than providing direct predictions for real marine ecosystem. Future studies employing larger sample sizes, multi-stressor conditions, and mesocosm or field experiments that include natural substrates are necessary to validate these mechanistic insights in a more ecological context and to investigate more subtle physiological and molecular responses.

Conclusion

The multi-omics approach in this study revealed a fundamental change in the strategy of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, from acute physiological inhibition under short-term OA to a metabolic reallocation that enhanced stress tolerance under long-term OA (Fig. 8). The results indicated that short-term OA rapidly inhibited photosynthesis and energy metabolism of N. yezoensis conchocelis filaments, while long-term OA induced metabolic reorganization, prioritizing stress tolerance over growth. Further WGCNA based on the metabolomic and transcriptomic results showed that this compensatory strategy, achieved at the cost of suppressed growth, underscores a critical trade-off between resilience and productivity under long-term OA. Our findings provide a mechanistic understanding of how this critical life history stage may persist in a future ocean, with potential consequences for its ecological and economic yield.
