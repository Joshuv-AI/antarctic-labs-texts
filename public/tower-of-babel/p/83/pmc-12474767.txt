Sacubitril/Valsartan-Induced Hypoglycemia After Gastric Bypass: A Case Report with Documented Endogenous Hyperinsulinemia
Authors: Mona Guetlin, Michael Joubert, Julia Morera
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Post-bariatric hypoglycemia (PBH) is a frequent yet complex complication following Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, typically related to exaggerated insulin responses after rapid glucose absorption. Identifying alternative or contributing mechanisms is particularly challenging in this population due to altered anatomy and limited access to standard diagnostic tools. We describe the case of a 65-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiac sarcoidosis, who achieved diabetes remission after gastric bypass. Several months later, he developed frequent postprandial and nocturnal hypoglycemic episodes despite strict dietary adjustments. Continuous glucose monitoring showed 38% time below range. A 72-h fasting test revealed inappropriately high proinsulin and C-peptide levels, indicating endogenous hyperinsulinemia. The patient was receiving sacubitril/valsartan for heart failure. Upon discontinuation of this treatment due to worsening renal function, hypoglycemic episodes resolved completely, and a repeat fasting test was normal. This is, to our knowledge, the first case report describing sacubitril/valsartan-associated hypoglycemia in a patient post-gastric bypass surgery, and the first to document inappropriate insulin secretion under treatment using a fasting test. Preclinical data suggest that neprilysin inhibition may enhance insulin secretion, possibly via increased GLP-1 bioavailability. While sacubitril/valsartan has demonstrated cardiovascular benefit, its metabolic effects remain underrecognized. Given the growing number of patients who have undergone bariatric surgery and the widespread use of this medication, clinicians should consider its potential role in refractory hypoglycemia. Early identification may avoid unnecessary investigations and support appropriate therapeutic adjustments.

Introduction

The links between hypoglycemia and gastric bypass are well documented. Hypoglycemia prevalence is high in this context, reaching 88% when detected through a test meal [1] or 75% with continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) [2]. According to the 2020 International consensus on the diagnosis and management of dumping syndrome, post-bypass hypoglycemia (PBH) are mainly postprandial hypoglycemia linked to an insulin secretion peak following a glycemic peak due to the accelerated absorption of carbohydrates in the intestines. These hypoglycemia episodes may or may not be associated with pancreatic nesidioblastosis (diffuse hyperplasia and hypertrophy of pancreatic islet cells) [3]. In rare cases, hypoglycemia may be due to malnutrition, adrenal insufficiency, insulinoma, non-pancreatic cell tumors [4], or factitious hypoglycemia [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to perform a complete etiological assessment and rule out alternative causes to guide the treatment.

Sacubitril/valsartan is recommended as a first-line treatment for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [6]. Hypoglycemia is described as a potential adverse effect of this treatment.

In this article, we report the case of a patient hospitalized for hypoglycemia in the context of a gastric bypass, suggesting a potential role of sacubitril/valsartan in the occurrence of hypoglycemia.

Clinical Presentation

The patient was a 65-year-old man with a complex medical history, including class III obesity (maximum BMI of 39 kg/m2), insulin-requiring type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, severe obstructive sleep apnea syndrome, peripheral artery disease requiring femoral stenting, multiple ischemic strokes, advanced chronic kidney disease, and both diabetic retinopathy and neuropathy. In 2015, he was diagnosed with cardiac sarcoidosis, presenting as dilated cardiomyopathy and arrhythmogenic heart disease with a left ventricular ejection fraction reduced to 25–30%, for which a triple-chamber implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted. In December 2021, he underwent metabolic surgery with a Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, which resulted in a 27-kg weight loss within 3 months (corresponding to a 27% total body weight reduction and 60% excess weight loss). This led to diabetes remission, allowing discontinuation of insulin glargine U100 and liraglutide, as well as improved blood pressure control. His cardiological treatment regimen included sacubitril/valsartan 24/26 mg (initiated in 2016), bisoprolol 5 mg twice daily, furosemide 60 mg, amiodarone, azathioprine 50 mg, and simvastatin.

In September 2022, during a nephrology admission for acute kidney injury (KDIGO stage III) secondary to infectious colitis, the patient experienced frequent late postprandial and nocturnal hypoglycemic episodes, which were relieved by oral glucose intake. Despite strict dietary supervision and adherence to international recommendations used to prevent postprandial hypoglycemia after bariatric surgery, including meal fractionation, reduced carbohydrate intake, and preference for low-glycemic-index foods, hypoglycemia persisted. He also reported irregular bowel habits and abdominal discomfort. The Glucose Management Indicator (GMI) was 5.7%, and he reported 10 to 15 hypoglycemic episodes per week, although continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) data were not available at that time.

The patient was subsequently admitted to the endocrinology department in October 2022 for an etiological evaluation of his hypoglycemic episodes, with a particular focus on assessing for dumping syndrome. An oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) with 75 g of glucose showed a fasting plasma glucose of 76 mg/dl, which rose to 182 mg/dl at 60 min and declined to 110 mg/dl at 120 min, consistent with preserved glucose tolerance. However, a 10 beats per minute increase in heart rate at 30 min suggested early dumping syndrome. A 72-h fasting test revealed hypoglycemia (glucose < 72 mg/dl) with non-elevated insulin levels (N < 3 mIU/l), in contrast with elevated proinsulin and C-peptide concentrations (> 5 pmol/l and > 0.2 nmol/l, respectively), indicating endogenous hyperinsulinism (Table 1). Beta-hydroxybutyrate levels at the end of the fast were low (2.37 mmol/l), supporting inappropriate insulin secretion, as insulin suppresses ketogenesis (expected value ≤ 2.7 mmol/l in hyperinsulinemic states). Given the evidence of inappropriate insulin secretion, diazoxide was initiated at a total daily dose of 300 mg (100 mg administered three times daily) to suppress insulin secretion and improve glycemic control. Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) while the patient was on combined sacubitril/valsartan and diazoxide therapy demonstrated a marked reduction in time below range (TBR) to 12%, accompanied by an increase in time in range (TIR) to 79% (Fig. 1, Period B).

In light of suspected insulinoma, imaging studies were undertaken to identify a potential pancreatic lesion. Abdominal MRI could not be performed due to the presence of an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator. A contrast-enhanced pancreatic CT scan revealed no detectable mass. Endoscopic ultrasound showed no abnormalities in the isthmus, body, or tail of the pancreas; however, the pancreatic head could not be visualized due to the altered anatomy from gastric bypass surgery. An 18F-FDG PET scan showed no hypermetabolic foci suggestive of a neuroendocrine tumor. A subsequent 68Ga-DOPA PET scan identified a questionable area of increased uptake in the body–tail region of the pancreas. As imaging findings remained inconclusive, medical management with diazoxide was continued.

Sacubitril/valsartan was eventually discontinued in May 2023 in anticipation of dialysis initiation due to worsening renal function. At the same time, diuretic doses were increased in the context of a presumed cardiorenal syndrome. No alternative heart failure therapy was introduced immediately after discontinuation. In particular, no medication known to induce hyperglycemia—such as non-cardioselective beta-blockers or sympathomimetic agents—was initiated.

Two months later, the patient presented to the emergency department with persistent hyperglycemia, with blood glucose levels exceeding 200 mg/dl (2 g/l) for the preceding three weeks while on diazoxide monotherapy. Glycemic excursions peaked at 300 mg/dl (3 g/l) and were accompanied by a 10-kg weight loss. CGM data revealed a Glucose Management Indicator (GMI) of 10%, with an average glucose level of 278 mg/dl and a coefficient of variation of 32.5%. Time in range (TIR) was only 13%, while time above range (TAR) reached 87% (Fig. 1, Period C), prompting discontinuation of diazoxide therapy.

A new hospitalization was scheduled in November 2023 to reassess the patient's glycemic profile following the discontinuation of both sacubitril/valsartan and diazoxide. Although the patient continued to report hypoglycemic episodes, their frequency had markedly decreased. Upon admission, continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) revealed a Glucose Management Indicator (GMI) of 6.5%, with an average glucose level of 133 mg/dl and a coefficient of variation of 51.4%. Time in range (TIR) was 77%, time above range (TAR) 21%, and time below range (TBR) only 2% (Fig. 1, Period D).

A diagnostic workup similar to that conducted in October 2022 was performed. This time, the 72-h fasting test was normal, with no episodes of hypoglycemia below 81 mg/dl. Discussion

This case highlights a potential role for sacubitril/valsartan in the development of hypoglycemia, likely through inappropriate insulin secretion, as demonstrated by a 72-h fasting test performed during treatment.

While the cardiovascular benefits of sacubitril/valsartan are well established, its glycemic effects remain underrecognized. However, post hoc analyses of the landmark PARADIGM-HF and PARAGON-HF trials have reported modest but consistent effects on glycemic control.

In the PARADIGM-HF trial, which included patients with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction, post hoc analysis revealed a significantly greater reduction in mean HbA1c in the group treated with sacubitril/valsartan compared to enalapril. This difference emerged during the first year of follow-up and was sustained for up to three years. Moreover, patients not treated with insulin at baseline were less likely to initiate insulin therapy in the sacubitril/valsartan arm. Notably, however, the incidence of reported hypoglycemia was similar between the two groups [6].

The PARAGON-HF trial, involving patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction, also demonstrated a significant reduction in mean HbA1c with sacubitril/valsartan, but in contrast, reported a higher incidence of hypoglycemia in the treatment group compared to controls. Still, the rate of insulin initiation remained comparable across both groups [7].

A pooled analysis of both trials confirmed these findings, suggesting a modest but consistent glucose-lowering effect of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with heart failure [8].

To date, only three clinical cases of sacubitril/valsartan-associated hypoglycemia have been published. In the first, a 62-year-old man with type 2 diabetes, treated with continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion and ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF 30%), experienced a 22% reduction in daily insulin requirements after initiating sacubitril/valsartan 97/103 mg. The reduction was more pronounced for prandial insulin than for basal insulin (− 28% vs. − 14%). Frequent postprandial hypoglycemic episodes were reported, and while CGM data showed improved time in range (from 48% to 63% over 4 months), time below range was not documented. HbA1c decreased by 0.4%, and mean glucose levels declined by 34 mg/dl [9]. The second case involved a 51-year-old woman with type 1 diabetes using an insulin pump for 13 years and LVEF between 30% and 35%. She experienced two episodes of severe hypoglycemia shortly after initiating sacubitril/valsartan 49/51 mg, which resolved after reducing insulin doses by approximately 10% [10]. The third case described an 84-year-old female patient without diabetes (BMI 29.6 kg/m2) with heart failure and reduced ejection fraction who was treated with sacubitril/valsartan 24/26 mg. She developed repeated fasting hypoglycemic episodes with blood glucose levels between 72 and 121 mg/dl. HbA1c levels over the previous four years had remained stable (5.2–5.4%). Adrenal insufficiency was ruled out. A 72-h fast was not performed, but discontinuation of sacubitril/valsartan and substitution with candesartan resolved the hypoglycemia [11].

Sacubitril, one of the components of sacubitril/valsartan, is a neprilysin inhibitor that prevents the degradation of natriuretic peptides, notably brain natriuretic peptide (BNP). This leads to beneficial cardiovascular effects, including vasodilation, natriuresis, and reduced blood pressure. Valsartan, an angiotensin II receptor blocker, contributes additional antihypertensive and cardioprotective effects.

Preclinical studies have shown that pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of neprilysin in obese or diabetic mice increases BNP levels and reduces blood glucose concentrations in both fasting and fed states. These effects were associated with improved glucose tolerance, increased insulin secretion, and enhanced insulin sensitivity [12]. Furthermore, in vitro studies using mouse pancreatic islets demonstrated that neprilysin degrades glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1), an incretin hormone that stimulates insulin release. Thus, neprilysin inhibition may enhance GLP-1 activity and promote insulin secretion [13].

In the present case, fasting hypoglycemia occurred while the patient was on sacubitril/valsartan, and was associated with elevated proinsulin and C-peptide levels, indicating endogenous hyperinsulinemia. These findings are consistent with the insulinotropic effects observed in preclinical models of neprilysin inhibition. However, the GLP-1 pathway alone does not fully explain the presence of fasting hypoglycemia in this context, suggesting that additional mechanisms may be involved.

Conclusions

This case report provides novel insights into the metabolic consequences of sacubitril/valsartan in the post-bariatric setting. To our knowledge, it represents the first documented case of hypoglycemia related to sacubitril/valsartan use in a patient with prior Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, and the first to establish endogenous hyperinsulinemia through a 72-h fasting test under treatment. These findings strengthen the hypothesis, previously suggested in preclinical models, that neprilysin inhibition may stimulate insulin secretion and contribute to hypoglycemia.

The evaluation of recurrent hypoglycemia in patients with a history of bariatric surgery is particularly complex due to altered gastrointestinal physiology, accelerated glucose absorption, and limited access to complete pancreatic imaging. These factors complicate the diagnostic distinction between dumping syndrome, insulinoma, and drug-induced hyperinsulinemic hypoglycemia.

Given the increasing prevalence of bariatric surgery worldwide and the widespread use of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with heart failure, clinicians should be aware of the potential glycemic effects of this drug, particularly in vulnerable populations such as post-bypass individuals. Recognizing this possible iatrogenic contribution is essential to ensure appropriate glycemic surveillance and to consider therapeutic adaptations, such as dose adjustment or the introduction of glucose-raising agents when necessary.
