Integrated multi-omics identifies plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis as key pathways in kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis var. deliciosa) resistance to Botryosphaeria Dothidea infection
Authors: Lixia Ye, Xuan Luo, Zhi Wang, Qiong Huang, Lei Zhang, Lei Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoft rot, a devastating fungal disease, severely impacts kiwifruit during postharvest storage, causing substantial fruit damage and rendering the fruit inedible. Botryosphaeria dothidea (B. dothidea) is the primary pathogen causing kiwifruit soft rot, but its pathogenic mechanism remains unclear.ResultsIn this study, morphological observation, transcriptome sequencing, and untargeted metabolomics were employed to analyze the changes in ‘Jinkui’ kiwifruit after artificial inoculation with B. dothidea. The results showed that obvious lesions began to appear on the fruit surface at 72 h post-inoculation (hpi) with B. dothidea, and the peel rot initiated at 120 hpi. Transcriptome sequencing revealed a significantly higher number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at 72 and 120 hpi than at 12 and 24 hpi. KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that DEGs at 72 and 120 hpi were significantly enriched in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway. A total of 403 metabolites were identified via untargeted metabolomics, among which the number of differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) was highest at 72 hpi (46 DAMs). These DAMs predominantly belong to the lipid and organic acid classes and are associated with pathways such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis and secondary metabolite biosynthesis. Co-enrichment analysis revealed significant enrichment of DEGs and DAMs in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. The correlation between DEGs and DAMs was assessed using the O2PLS model and the Pearson correlation coefficient. Two key DEGs were identified: a MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) and a ChlH-like gene (Acc00854), both of which potentially play crucial regulatory roles in the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot.ConclusionsSignificant changes in kiwifruit tissue morphology, gene expression, and metabolite profiles were observed after 72 h of B. dothidea infection. The plant hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathways are closely associated with the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot. The MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) and the ChlH-like gene (Acc00854) are likely to be the key regulators. This study identifies the core regulatory pathways and genes involved in kiwifruit soft rot, provides new insights into the pathogenic mechanism of B. dothidea, and offers theoretical support for developing effective strategies to control kiwifruit soft rot.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07480-z.Keywords: Kiwifruit, Soft rot, B. dothidea, Transcriptome, Metabolome

Background

Kiwifruit (Actinidia chinensis Planch.) is a fruit native to China that has gained popularity due to its economic, nutritional, and health benefits. However, kiwifruit is highly susceptible to fruit rot during cultivation, storage, and transportation, which significantly impacts its yield and quality [1, 2]. Fruit ripe rot, also known as soft rot, is characterized by its hidden onset, rapid progression, long duration, and difficulty in management [3, 4]. Current management strategies for kiwifruit soft rot include cultivating resistant varieties, implementing agricultural practices, applying biological control agents, and using chemical fungicides [5]. Preharvest application of chemical fungicides is an economical and effective method to prevent kiwifruit soft rot [6, 7]. However, the long-term application of chemical fungicides can lead to pathogen resistance, environmental pollution, and human health risks associated with pesticide residues [1]. Thus, investigating the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot is crucial for developing new control strategies.

Soft rot is caused primarily by the pathogens Botryosphaeria dothidea (B. dothidea), Botrytis cinerea, Phomopsis lithocarpus, and Diaporthe phaseolorum. Among these, B. dothidea is the predominant pathogen, responsible for over 80% of soft rot cases [8]. It is also a widespread plant pathogenic fungus that poses a significant threat to various economically important crops [9, 10]. After being infected by B. dothidea, plants exhibit a range of symptoms, with fruit soft rot being the most common [9]. In kiwifruit, B. dothidea invades fruit tissues mainly through wounds, fissures, lenticels, and calyx sinuses [11]. Subsequently, it will grow and reproduce in the intercellular space or cells of kiwifruit and secrete extracellular enzymes (cellulase and pectinase) and toxins. These enzymes will decompose plant cell walls, cell membranes, and intercellular substances, separate cells, soften and rot fruit tissues, and then cause typical symptoms of soft rot [12].

Revealing the pathogenic mechanism is the key to controlling the disease caused by B. dothidea. Currently, numerous genes and metabolic pathways closely linked to B. dothidea resistance have been identified. For example, the ERF14 transcription factor can activate the apple PR gene promoter and increase its expression, thereby enhancing the defense ability against B. dothidea [13]. Similarly, MdWRKY15 improves the resistance of apple to B. dothidea via the salicylic acid-mediated pathway by directly binding the MdICS1 promoter [14]. On the contrary, the MdSYP121 gene can reduce the tolerance of transgenic apple callus to B. dothidea infection by regulating the transcription of salicylic acid-related genes [15]. In Chinese hickory, WRKY2 enhances the ability of plants to prevent B. dothidea invasion by increasing the expression of the pathogenesis-related gene PR1 and activating antioxidant defenses and salicylic acid pathways [16]. It is worth noting that phenylpropanoid pathway genes and metabolites are closely related to plant resistance to B. dothidea. For example, melatonin activates the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway and improves the disease resistance of pears to B. dothidea [17]. Lignin and flavonoids can strengthen cell walls and potentially contribute to resistance against B. dothidea infection [18, 19]. Furthermore, the glyoxalase gene family member PbrGLYI-28 was notably upregulated in response to B. dothidea infection, and its silencing increases pear sensitivity to B. dothidea, elevates methylglyoxal levels, and reduces glyceraldehyde activity [20]. PbrCHIA can decompose B. dothidea mycelia in vitro and bind to PbrLYK1B2 to inhibit ROS accumulation, thus positively regulating pear resistance to B. dothidea [21]. However, there is still a lack of reports on B. dothidea resistance-related pathways and genes in kiwifruit.

B. dothidea can infect a variety of woody plant species, leading to diverse diseases such as fruit rot, wilt, stem canker, and tree death [22, 23]. Here, the fruit of ‘Jinkui’ kiwifruit was inoculated with the B. dothidea to establish soft rot. After incubation for 12, 24, 72, and 120 h, the lesion area was measured, and transcriptome and untargeted metabolomics were performed. The integrated omics analysis initially revealed a strong association between the hormone signal transduction and phenylpropanoid metabolic pathways with the progression of kiwifruit soft rot. The MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) and the ChlH-like gene (Acc00854) are likely key regulators in the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot. This study provides a basis for understanding the resistance mechanism of kiwifruit to B. dothidea and offers theoretical guidance for preventing and controlling kiwifruit soft rot.

Materials and methods

A. chinensis and pathogenic bacterial materials

‘Jinkui’ kiwifruit, A. chinensis var. deliciosa cv., was obtained from the National Kiwifruit Germplasm Resource Garden of China (30°29’ N, 114°16’ E). It generally takes 7–15 days to soften and ripen at room temperature (15–25 °C) after harvest, and it is not resistant to kiwifruit soft rot. When the soluble solids content was greater than 6.5%, six 5-year-old trees were randomly selected, and a total of 200 fruits were collected for subsequent experiments. The strain of (B) dothidea (no. JZG1 and GenBank no. KU360149), isolated from kiwifruit with soft rot, was cultured on a potato dextrose agar (PDA) plate at 25 °C for seven days. Mycelial plugs of B. dothidea were then prepared using a 5 mm sterile hole puncher, and sterile medium was used as a control.

Inoculation of A. chinensis fruits with B. dothidea

Fruits of ‘Jinkui’ with a uniform shape and no scar were selected, washed, dried, and wiped twice with 75% ethanol. The fruit was subsequently placed on an ultraclean bench, and a sterile needle was used to create a wound with an area of approximately 5 mm at the midpoint. The wounds of the fruits in the treatment group were covered with a B. dothidea mycelium block, whereas those in the control group were inoculated with sterile PDA medium. The fruits were sprayed daily with sterile water and kept in a constant temperature incubator set at 25℃ with a humidity of at least 90% under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. After 12, 24, 72, and 120 h of culture, the incidence of the inoculation site in the inoculated group (T) and the control group (C) was observed, and the size of the lesion was measured. The peel tissue, collected within 0.5 to 1.0 cm of the wound, was promptly frozen for later sequencing and metabolomics analyses. In addition, the fruit peel and flesh around the lesion were collected at 120 hpi, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histopathological examination, and fixed in FAA for toluidine blue staining observation.

Toluidine blue staining

Toluidine blue staining was performed according to a previously reported method [24]. Briefly, the samples were dehydrated via a graded ethanol series (50% twice, followed by 60%, 70%, 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% twice) and were then cleaned with xylene for 50 min. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in paraffin, cut into 8 to 10 μm-thick slides, and stained with 2% toluidine blue aqueous solution for 10 min. After being washed and sealed with neutral gum, the slides were observed and photographed under light microscopy (Nikon 90i, Japan).

RNA sequencing and analysis

For RNA sequencing, fruit peel and flesh collected at 12, 24, 72, and 120 h in both the control (C12, C24, C72, and C120) and treatment (T12, T24, T72, and T120) groups were sent to Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). There were three biological replicates at each time point, and each replicate contained three fruits. The peel and pulp of three fruits in each replicate were collected and mixed into one sample, and a total of 24 samples were obtained. Total RNA was extracted from each sample via a Trizol reagent kit (Invitrogen, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions. To obtain cDNA, the mRNA was first enriched with oligo (dT) beads, then fragmented into short segments, and finally reverse transcribed using random primers. The ends of the obtained double-stranded DNA were subsequently repaired, poly (A) was added, the sequencing adapter was connected, and an Illumina HiSeq2500 was used for sequencing.

The raw data were filtered via fastp (version 0.18.0) to remove unqualified data. The clean data were then mapped to the reference genome A. chinensis Red 5 (http://kiwifruitgenome.org) [25] using HISAT2.2.4 software. The normalized FPKM values of the annotated genes were calculated using Stringtie software. DEGs between C12 vs. T12, C24 vs. T24, C72 vs. T72, or C120 vs. T120 were screened with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05, and |log2 fold change (FC)|≥1. KEGG pathway analyses were performed, and the threshold for significantly enriched pathways was set as P < 0.05. Heatmaps of the DEGs were visualized by the HeatMap Illustrator tool of TBtools [26].

Metabolite isolation and analysis

The fruit tissue was crushed with liquid nitrogen, and 100 mg of the obtained powder was dissolved in 1.0 mL of 70% methanol aqueous solution. Lidocaine (0.1 mg/L) was added as the internal standard and incubated at 4 °C overnight. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 10 min, the supernatant was collected and filtered through a 0.22 μm pore size filter membrane for liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry/mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) analysis.

The LC-MS/MS analysis utilized an LC-ESI-MS/MS system featuring a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 C18 column and a 6500Q TRAP triple quadrupole-linear ion trap mass spectrometer [27]. The injection volume was 2 µL, and the flow rate was 0.4 mL/min. The mobile phases consisted of 0.04% acidified water (A) and 0.04% acidified acetonitrile (B). The elution gradient was programmed as follows: 95:5 A/B at 0 min, transitioning to 5:95 A/B from 11 to 12 min, reverting to 95:5 A/B at 12.1 min, and maintained until 15 min. The QE MS operated with a spray voltage of 5.5 kV, an ESI source temperature of 500 °C, a curtain gas pressure of 25 psi, and collision-activated dissociation at its maximum setting. A full scan was conducted at a resolution of 70,000 over a range of 50–1000 m/z.

Data filtering, peak detection, alignment, and calculations were performed using Analyst software (version 1.6.1). The processed data matrix was compared with the public database to identify metabolites [28, 29]. DAMs between the two groups were identified using thresholds of P < 0.05 and variable importance in the projection (VIP) ≥ 1. The metabolic pathways associated with these DAMs were further analyzed using the KEGG database. The average relative content of DAMs in each group was standardized using z-scores, followed by K-means clustering analysis.

Integrated analysis of RNA sequencing and metabolomics data

The common KEGG pathways enriched by all DEGs and DAMs in the four groups (C12 vs. T12, C24 vs. T24, C72 vs. T72, and C120 vs. T120) were analyzed. The correlation between the identified DEGs and DAMs was evaluated by the bidirectional orthogonal projections to latent structures (O2PLS) model and the Pearson correlation coefficient. O2PLS analysis was carried out using the OmicsPLS package [30]. The cross-validation method was used for multiple pre-modeling, and the model with the smallest prediction error was selected. R2 was used to represent the contribution, and the loading plots of different omics data were drawn for the variables of the joint part. The top 25 DEGs and DAMs, identified by the sum of squares of their loading values, were selected to create a loading map highlighting the strongest associations. Pearson coefficients were calculated using the expression of all DEGs and the abundance of all DAMs. DEGs and DAMs with absolute correlation coefficients exceeding 0.5 were selected to construct the network diagram [31, 32].

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences were calculated in IBM SPSS Statistics software (v26.0). One-way ANOVA and the Duncan test (p < 0.05) were used to assess the significant differences.

Results

Inoculation of B. dothidea in kiwifruit induced soft rot

To investigate the pathogenic mechanisms underlying soft rot in kiwifruit, an artificial inoculation assay was performed on ‘Jinkui’ fruit using the pathogen B. dothidea. At 12 and 24 hpi, no visible lesions were observed on the surface of the inoculation site (Fig. 1A). However, by 72 hpi, obvious lesions began to emerge, characterized by brown water-soaked areas on the exterior and discoloration of the internal tissue (Fig. 1A). By 120 hpi, the lesions had expanded further, and the mycelia of B. dothidea had invaded the flesh and continued to grow inside. Furthermore, the flesh decayed, and the diseased tissue expanded in a conical shape into the deep flesh to form a cavity (Fig. 1A). The measurements revealed that the lesion diameter was 10.85 mm at 72 hpi and increased to 18.27 mm at 120 hpi (Fig. 1C), indicating accelerated disease progression during this period. In addition, histological examination with toluidine blue staining confirmed fungal hyphal penetration beyond the fruit surface into subepidermal flesh cells (Fig. 1B). These findings suggest that B. dothidea invades the fruit cell tissue via the mycelium, thereby destroying the cell structure and causing the fruit to soften and rot.

Statistics and KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes in the transcriptome

To investigate the transcriptional changes associated with B. dothidea infection in kiwifruit, RNA-seq analysis was performed on inoculated fruit tissues collected at 12, 24, 72, and 120 hpi. The total number of reads per sample ranged from 39,809,382 to 64,239,260. Each library presented a Q30 value exceeding 93%, with an average GC content between 48.12% and 51.61% (Table S1). A total of 35,935 genes were annotated using multiple databases (Table S2). Based on FDR < 0.05 and |log2FC|≥1, a total of 106 DEGs (80 up-regulated, 26 down-regulated) at 12 hpi (C12 vs. T12), 79 DEGs (28 up-regulated, 51 down-regulated) at 24 hpi (C24 vs. T24), 2780 DEGs (914 up-regulated, 1866 down-regulated) at 72 hpi (C72 vs. T72), and 5377 DEGs (2268 up-regulated, 3109 down-regulated) at 120 hpi (C120 vs. T120) were identified, respectively (Fig. 2A, Table S3). Venn diagram analysis revealed that only one common DEG was shared across all four stages, whereas 1,160 common DEGs were identified between 72 and 120 hpi (Fig. 2B). The number of DEGs clearly increased progressively with the duration of infection, with significantly more DEGs observed at 72 and 120 hpi than at 12 and 24 hpi (Fig. 2A), indicating that significant alterations in gene expression mainly occur after 72 hpi, which is consistent with the phenotypic observations that distinct symptoms of soft rots begin to appear at 72 hpi (Fig. 1A).

To further elucidate the functional roles of the DEGs identified at 72 and 120 hpi, KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. These analyses revealed that the DEGs at both 72 and 120 hpi were most significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction pathways (Fig. 2C), suggesting that plant hormones likely play a pivotal role in the response to infection by B. dothidea. Moreover, the DEGs at 72 hpi were also significantly enriched in several additional pathways, including protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum and starch and sucrose metabolism. The DEGs at 120 hpi were notably enriched in pathways including plant-pathogen interactions and MAPK signaling pathway in plants (Fig. 2C). The enriched pathways at 72 hpi were related mainly to metabolism and the stress response, whereas those at 120 hpi were transformed into defense signaling.

Expression profiles of differentially expressed genes related to the plant hormone signal transduction pathway

Given the significant enrichment of DEGs in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway, we conducted an expression heatmap analysis focusing on hormone-related DEGs. The DEGs were associated with all eight major plant hormone signaling pathways: auxin, cytokinin, gibberellin, abscisic acid, ethylene, brassinolide, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid. Notably, the auxin pathway contained the greatest number of DEGs with 38 genes (Fig. 3, Table S4). Overall, the majority of DEGs were notably down-regulated at 72 and 120 hpi, such as the CRE1, AHP, B-ARR, and A-ARR genes in the cytokinin signaling pathway; the GID1, GID2 and TF genes in the gibberellin signaling pathway, the ETR, EIN2, EBF1/2, and EIN3 genes in the ethylene signaling pathway; and the BRI1, BSK and BZR1/2 genes in the brassinosteroid signaling pathway (Fig. 3). This observation strongly suggests that the endogenous levels of various hormones in the fruit undergo significant alterations after 72 hpi with B. dothidea, which likely promotes the development of soft rot. In addition, some hormone-related DEGs, such as AUX1, SIMKK, and ERF1/2, were up-regulated in the four stages (Fig. 3). Overall, these hormone pathway-related genes directly respond to B. dothidea infection and are likely closely associated with the resistance of kiwifruit to soft rot, especially genes related to the ethylene, jasmonic acid, and salicylic acid pathways.

Statistics and expression heat map analysis of differentially accumulated metabolites in the metabolome

To investigate the metabolic alterations induced by B. dothidea infection in kiwifruit, we conducted untargeted metabolomic analysis. A total of 403 metabolites in 10 categories were identified, with flavonoids accounting for the largest proportion (23.57%), followed by lipids (15.88%), amino acids and their derivatives (13.40%), and phenolic acids (12.90%) (Fig. 4A, Table S5). Based on the criteria of P <0.05 and VIP ≥ 1, the DAMs were identified as follows: 8 DAMs (5 up-, 3 down-regulated) at 12 hpi, 18 DAMs (13 up-, 5 down-regulated) at 24 hpi, 46 DAMs (30 up-, 16 down-regulated) at 72 hpi, and 37 DAMs (28 up-, 9 down-regulated) at 120 hpi. The quantity of up-regulated DAMs exceeded that of down-regulated DAMs at each of the four stages (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the number of DAMs at 72 hpi and 120 hpi was greater than that at 12 hpi and 24 hpi, which was consistent with the trend in the number of DEGs from the transcriptome. These results further confirmed that the gene expression level and metabolite content in kiwifruit began to change significantly 72 h after B. dothidea infection. Venn diagram analysis showed that only one common DAM across the four stages: MAG (18:3) isomer 3 (Fig. 4C). To further analyze the accumulation patterns of DAMs, we performed a heatmap analysis focusing on DAMs with FC > 1.5 or FC < 0.67. A total of 3, 13, 32, and 24 DAMs were identified at 12, 24, 72, and 120 hpi, respectively. The majority of these DAMs were upregulated, and predominantly belonged to the classes of lipids, organic acids, and amino acids and their derivatives (Fig. 4D-F). These findings indicated that B. dothidea infection in kiwifruit mainly caused the changes of lipids, organic acids, and amino acids and their derivatives, which may directly lead to the decay of kiwifruit.

K-means clustering analysis and KEGG enrichment analysis of differentially accumulated metabolites

To analyze the metabolite change trends across different groups, the average relative content of DAMs was standardized using z-scores, followed by K-means clustering analysis. A total of 85 DAMs were classified into nine subclasses, with subclass 6 containing the highest number of DAMs (17), and subclass 1 containing the fewest (4) (Table S6). The metabolite expression levels in subclasses 6, 7, and 8 at T72 and T120 were significantly higher than those at other time points (Fig. 5), which aligns with the trends observed in the DEGs. Notably, the metabolites in subclasses 6, 7, and 8 were predominantly organic acids and lipids, indicating a significant increase in these compounds in kiwifruit flesh at 72 h post-inoculation with B. dothidea.

K-means clustering analysis of differentially accumulated metabolites

To further explore the functions of the DAMs, KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. At 12 hpi, the DAMs were enriched predominantly in pathways such as metabolic pathways, starch and sucrose metabolism, and biosynthesis of antibiotics (Fig. S1A). At 24 hpi, the DAMs were primarily concentrated in pathways including phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and biosynthesis of secondary metabolites (Fig. S1B). At 72 hpi, the key enriched pathways for DAMs included antibiotic biosynthesis, phenylalanine metabolism, and flavone and flavonol biosynthesis (Fig. S1C). At 120 hpi, the DAMs were predominantly associated with pathways such as secondary metabolite biosynthesis, microbial metabolism in various environments, and aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis (Fig. S1D). Notably, pathways such as biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, phenylalanine metabolism, and biosynthesis of antibiotics were enriched at multiple time points. These pathway-related DAMs may regulate resistance to kiwifruit soft rot.

Expression profiles of differentially expressed genes and differentially accumulated metabolites in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway

To further analyze the metabolic pathways associated with kiwifruit soft rot, we examined the co-enrichment pathways of DEGs and DAMs. The most significantly co-enriched pathways were metabolic pathways, biosynthesis of secondary metabolites, and carbon metabolism. Notably, a substantial number of DEGs and DAMs were enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway (Fig. S1), which has been reported to be related to plant disease resistance [17]. Therefore, the DEGs and DAMs in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway were further analyzed by an expression heatmap. The DEGs include mainly PER (8), ACT (7), PAL (4), CYP (4), 4CL (3), and CAD (3). Notably, in the control group, many DEGs were highly expressed at C72; in contrast, in the B. dothidea inoculation group, most DEGs were highly expressed at T120, such as the phenylalanine synthesis-related gene PAL; enzyme 2.3.1.133-related genes ACT and ASAT3; lignin synthase-related genes PNC1, PER5 and GSVIVT (Fig. 6). Among the 7 DAMs, coumarinate, spermidine, caffeoyl quinic acid, ferulic acid and scopoletin were significantly down-regulated at 72 and 120 hpi (Fig. 6). These results suggest that B. dothidea is likely induces kiwifruit soft rot by inhibiting the early response of certain genes in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, thereby suppressing the synthesis of coumarinate, lignin and other substances.

The correlation between differentially expressed genes and differentially accumulated metabolites

To further screen the key regulatory genes of kiwifruit soft rot, an O2PLS model was constructed using the expression levels of all the DEGs and the metabolite abundance data of the DAMs in the four stages for correlation prediction. Based on the element loading values, the top 25 DEGs and DAMs with the highest correlations were selected for detailed analysis (Table S7). The metabolites were mainly organic acids (7), lipids (5), and amino acids and their derivatives (4). Among them, aldehydo-D-galacturonate and 2-furanoic acid (mws0177) was the most significantly up-regulated, with log2FC of 3.09 and 3.21 at 72 hpi and 120 hpi, respectively. The top 25 DEGs with the strongest correlations were only differentially expressed at 72 hpi and 120 hpi, and most of them were down-regulated. Several of the selected genes are potentially related to disease resistance, such as the outer membrane usher protein-like gene (Acc22392), the cell wall integrity and stress response component 4-like gene (Acc25187), and the cyclin-D3-1-like gene (Acc12063) (Fig. 7A).

Additionally, the Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated, and the DEGs and DAMs with absolute values of correlation coefficients greater than 0.5 were selected to construct a correlation network. A total of 15 DAMs and 124 DEGs were identified (Table S7, Fig. 7B). The classification analysis revealed that the DAMs were mainly organic acids (7) and phenolic acids (4), and most of them were significantly up-regulated. These DAMs were associated primarily with two modules. Module 1 included 10 DAMs and 89 DEGs, while Module 2 contained 2 DAMs and 30 DEGs. All the DAMs in Module 1 were up-regulated, while all the DAMs in Module 2 were down-regulated at 120 hpi. Similarly, most of the 124 DEGs were up-regulated. At 72 hpi and 120 hpi, the number of up-regulated DEGs was 12.5-fold and 3-fold that of down-regulated DEGs, respectively. Further, these DEGs included plant hormone-related genes (auxin: SAUR36/Acc09025, ILL3/Acc03231, PIN1A/Acc08104, ethylene: ERF5/Acc15852, ABA: CAR4/Acc05911), transcription factors (NAC22/Acc07174, WRKY53/Acc19793, BZIP18/Acc00311), and disease resistance-related gene (LRR-RLK/MSTRG.1147) (Table S7).

Integration of the O2PLS model and Pearson correlation coefficient identified eight common DAMs, which were 4 organic acids, 2 phenolic acids, 1 coumarin, and 1 other. Seven of the eight DAMs were significantly up-regulated at 72 hpi and 120 hpi, among which phenyllactate, 3-(4-hydroxyphenyl)-propionic acid, L-(-)−3-phenyllactic acid, and 2-furanoic acid were the most significantly up-regulated. These findings further confirmed that the acid substances are closely associated with the progression of kiwifruit soft rot. Only two common DEGs were identified: a MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) and a ChlH-like gene (Acc00854) (Fig. 7B). The MYB transcription factor was induced by B. dothidea infection, whereas the ChlH-like gene was inhibited (Table S7). These two genes potentially act as central regulators in the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot.

Discussion

Kiwifruit soft rot is a postharvest disease characterized by small depressions on the fruit surface and flesh soft rot, which leads to a complete loss of its edible value [33]. B. dothidea is a major pathogen responsible for postharvest soft rot in kiwifruit [34]. In this study, obvious lesions began to appear at 72 hpi, and the fruit began to rot at 120 hpi. Similarly, transcriptomic and untargeted metabolomics analyses revealed a significantly greater number of DEGs and DAMs at 72 and 120 hpi compared to 12 and 24 hpi. This pattern aligns with previous findings in ‘Jinyan’ kiwifruit, where more DEGs were identified at 72 hpi than at 24 hpi [35], supporting the view that B. dothidea undergoes a latent period exceeding 24 h before symptom development in artificially inoculated kiwifruit. In addition, paraffin sections showed that the hyphae of B. dothidea invaded fruit cells after 72 hpi. B. dothidea likely degrades the fruit cell tissues through mycelial secretions, resulting in cell lysis and ultimately causing the softening and rotting of the fruit.

When plants are infected with B. dothidea, a series of complex physiological and biochemical changes occur. For example, fruit firmness, titratable acid, and ascorbic acid decreased, while lipid peroxidation and malondialdehyde, which destroyed cell membrane integrity, increased [36]. To resist the invasion of pathogens, the activities of defense substances such as lignin, antioxidant enzymes, chitinase, and β−1,3-glucanase are increased, thereby limiting the spread of B. dothidea [37, 38]. In terms of metabolites, lipids and organic acids are closely related to plant disease resistance. It has been reported that the cuticle and wax layers of the plant epidermis contain abundant lipid substances that can effectively prevent the invasion of pathogens [39]. Phosphatidic acid can activate downstream signaling pathways, thereby inducing defense responses in plants [40]. Lipids also serve as precursors for the synthesis of phytoalexins, which are antimicrobial compounds in plants [41]. It has also been reported that some organic acids exhibit antibacterial activity. For example, benzoic acid and salicylic acid effectively inhibit pathogen growth and reproduction [42, 43]. Jasmonic acid and its methyl ester enhance plant disease resistance by activating defense-related gene expression [44]. The level of SA in kiwifruit tissues changed significantly after B. dothidea infection [10]. In this study, untargeted metabolomics showed that most of the DAMs in the four stages were lipids and organic acids, with all lipids and the majority of organic acids being upregulated. Multiple glycerol esters, free fatty acids, lysophosphatidylcholine, and organic acids were significantly up-regulated after B. dothidea infection, but the involved regulatory mechanism requires further investigation.

Plant hormones can directly or indirectly inhibit the growth of pathogens by regulating plant defense mechanisms and physiological metabolic processes. In kiwifruit, methyl jasmonate (MeJA) has been shown to restrict lesion diameter expansion caused by B. dothidea in ‘Hongyang’ and increase the activities of PAL and C4H, thereby strengthening the resistance of kiwifruit to soft rot [38]. Similarly, exogenous application of MeJA to ‘Jinqiu’ kiwifruit significantly reduces lesion diameter, enhances the activities of catalase and peroxidase, and increases the accumulation of total phenolic content [45]. In this study, the DEGs at 72 and 120 hpi were most significantly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction pathways. Further analysis showed that the DEGs were associated with all eight major plant hormone signaling pathways, with the auxin pathway having the greatest number of DEGs. It can be speculated that other hormones, such as auxin and ABA, may also be related to the resistance of kiwifruit to soft rot.

Furthermore, a large number of DEGs and DAMs were significantly co-enriched in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway. The phenylpropanoid pathway can produce antibacterial compounds, such as lignin, lignans, and coumarins, which can increase the strength and disease resistance of plant cell walls. In addition, this pathway also contributes to the synthesis of signaling molecules such as salicylic acid, which are crucial for plant defense responses [46, 47]. The up-regulated expression of the PAL and C3H genes can increase lignin content and enhance the lignification of the cell wall, thereby improving the disease resistance of Chinese hickory [10]. In rice, viral infection significantly activates the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway, resulting in increases in ferulic acid and cinnamic acid contents. These metabolites inhibit viral replication and promote lignin deposition in cell walls, creating a physical barrier to prevent viral spread [48]. In this study, phenylpropanoid pathway-related genes PAL, ACT, ASAT3, PNC1, PER5, and GSVIVT were significantly up-regulated, and the metabolites coumarinate, spermidine, caffeoyl quinic acid, ferulic acid, and scopoletin were significantly down-regulated at 72 and 120 hpi. These results were similar to those of previous experiments conducted on Actinidia chinensis cv. ‘Hongyang’ [38]. Therefore, it can be inferred that the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway is significantly associated with kiwifruit soft rot resistance.

To date, the number of reported genes associated with B. dothidea resistance in kiwifruit is still limited. WRKY, GATA, and PLATZ transcription factors were significantly induced in resistant kiwifruit varieties following inoculation with B. dothidea [35]. MeJA enhances kiwifruit disease resistance by modulating gene expression in the phenylpropane (AcPAL, AcC4H, Ac4CL, and AcCAD) and jasmonic acid (AcLOX, AcAOS, AcAOC, AcOPR3, AcJAR1, AcCOI1, and AcMYC2) pathways [38]. Here, multiple candidate genes were identified through correlation analysis of transcriptomics and metabolomics, which were significantly upregulated at 72 and 120 h post-inoculation with B. dothidea. These genes include plant hormone-related genes (SAUR36, ILL3, ERF5, and CAR4) and transcription factors (NAC022, WRKY53, and BZIP18). However, several pathogenesis-related genes, such as the outer membrane usher protein-like gene (Acc22392), the cell wall integrity and stress response component 4-like gene (Acc25187), and the cyclin-D3-1-like gene (Acc12063), were downregulated at 120 hpi. Integration of the O2PLS model analysis and Pearson correlation coefficient analysis identified two core regulatory factors: a MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) that was upregulated and an ChlH-like gene (Acc00854) that was downregulated. These two genes may play a key regulatory role in the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot, and their functions and regulatory mechanisms require further elucidation.

Overall, B. dothidea may cause kiwifruit soft rot through multiple mechanisms after invading the fruit. On the one hand, B. dothidea secretes a variety of extracellular enzymes (such as cellulase and pectinase) and toxins to degrade the cell walls and intercellular substances of kiwifruit cells, resulting in cell separation and tissue softening. On the other hand, B. dothidea inhibits the MAPK signaling pathway by secreting effector proteins into kiwifruit cells, thereby preventing the activation of defense genes in kiwifruit and consequently reducing its disease resistance. In addition, B. dothidea may promote the production of ethylene in plants, accelerating fruit ripening and senescence, thus increasing fruit susceptibility to pathogen attack. Moreover, it may also inhibit the signal transduction of defense-related hormones such as salicylic acid and jasmonic acid, thereby weakening the defense response of kiwifruit. The differential expression of these pathway-related genes and metabolites collectively regulates the resistance of kiwifruit to B. dothidea.

Conclusions

In this study, an integrated analysis of transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses was employed to identify the key genes and metabolites involved in the pathogenesis of kiwifruit soft rot. The results revealed that the plant hormone signal transduction pathway and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway were closely associated with the pathology of kiwifruit soft rot. Specifically, the MYB transcription factor (Acc32263) and the ChlH-like gene (Acc00854) are likely to play crucial regulatory roles in this pathogenic process. These results lay a foundation for elucidating the molecular mechanism underlying kiwifruit soft rot and provide valuable insights for the development of efficient prevention and control strategies.
