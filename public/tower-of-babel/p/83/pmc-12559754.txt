Predicting older adults smart healthcare adoption using an extended TAM
Authors: Jinfang Zhang, Yi Ma, Hongchao Zhang, Yuan Chen, Jiani Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study utilizes the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) as its theoretical foundation to examine factors influencing the adoption of smart healthcare among community-dwelling older adults. Focusing on elderly residents in Shaoxing, Zhejiang Province, China, the “Smart Healthcare Usage Intention Scale” was developed based on TAM constructs and validated through reliability and validity testing. A total of 403 participants were recruited via convenience sampling between August 2024 and January 2025. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, t-tests, Pearson correlation analyses in SPSS 27.0, and structural equation modeling (SEM) in Amos 28.0 for path and mediation analyses. The results indicated a behavioral intention (BI) score of 10.00 ± 3.26. The model exhibited good fit (CMIN/DF = 2.713), revealing that personal tendency, social support, and perceived value had significant positive effects on both perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU). Furthermore, PU and PEOU were found to positively influence BI, and PEOU also had a significant positive effect on PU. Mediation analysis identified six parallel and three serial mediating pathways, underscoring the essential mediating roles of PU and PEOU. These findings provide both theoretical and practical implications for promoting smart healthcare adoption in older adult populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21419-2.Keywords: TAM, Older adults, Smart healthcare, Mediation analysis, Behavioral intentionSubject terms: Geriatrics, Risk factors

Introduction

The global population is aging at an unprecedented rate, presenting formidable challenges to healthcare systems worldwide1,2. This trend is particularly pronounced in China, which is undergoing a rapid transition into a super-aged society3,4. This demographic shift intensifies pressure on existing healthcare infrastructure, which is already strained by resource limitations and inefficiencies in community-based care models. In response to the escalating healthcare demands of its aging population, the Chinese government has implemented a comprehensive strategy designed to enhance the accessibility and quality of medical services5. This approach integrates family, community, and institutional care to address the diverse needs of older adults and ensure appropriate care delivery in suitable environments6. Nevertheless, although these policies aim to promote equity, they have not yet substantially improved the overall quality of healthcare services for older adults. Further adjustments and optimizations of the healthcare system are urgently needed to effectively address the challenges associated with population aging7.

Smart healthcare represents a patient-centered approach that incorporates technologies such as the Internet of Things (IoT), artificial intelligence (AI), and big data analytics to support predictive, preventive, and personalized medicine, thereby improving the efficiency and outcomes of healthcare delivery8. This approach employs remote monitoring and assistive technologies to track vital signs and promote independence in daily activities among older adults9,10. Integrated AI and data analytics facilitate tailored healthcare solutions for this population11, and devices such as smart wristbands allow real-time transmission of health data to enable prompt interventions. Additionally, telemedicine services enhance accessibility to medical expertise and help reduce the burden on healthcare infrastructure through remote consultations12. These advancements not only increase the convenience of healthcare services but also mitigate demands on medical resources. Nevertheless, the full implementation of smart healthcare is constrained by a pronounced digital divide, as older adults encounter specific obstacles to technology adoption—including technophobia, age-related physical and cognitive constraints, and insufficient social support or training opportunities13,14.

In this context, the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) offers a robust theoretical framework for investigating the acceptance of smart healthcare among older adults15. Initially proposed by Davis in 1989 and rooted in the Theory of Reasoned Action, TAM serves as a foundational model for explaining and predicting individual acceptance of new technologies16. It has since become one of the most influential and widely validated theories in technology adoption research17. Due to its conceptual parsimony and strong predictive validity, TAM has been extensively applied across diverse domains, including e-commerce, e-learning, mobile services, and healthcare18–20. Within the Chinese context, the core constructs of TAM have been empirically supported. For example, Liu Yuxiao showed that perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) significantly predict the adoption of smart monitoring devices among older adults21. Consistently, empirical studies conducted in China have confirmed PU and PEOU as the primary determinants when examining elderly individuals’ acceptance of emerging technologies or interventions22. Moreover, PEOU exhibits a direct influence on PU. The external variables, such as system design, user characteristics, and task characteristics, also impact PU and PEOU, indirectly shaping technology acceptance23. In the context of smart healthcare, PU reflects the extent to which older adults believe smart healthcare can provide practical benefits, while PEOU reflects their subjective perception of the ease of operating smart healthcare systems. These two factors effectively explain older adults’ acceptance and willingness to use smart healthcare, offering theoretical support for optimizing its design and services.

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) provides a foundational yet limited framework for understanding technology adoption among older adults, as its emphasis on perceived usefulness (PU) and perceived ease of use (PEOU) overlooks critical age-specific contextual and psychosocial factors. To address this gap, we integrate insights from extended technology acceptance theories and life-course perspectives. From TAM2 and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), we incorporate social influence—operationalized as social support—and contextual enabling factors24,25. Drawing on Socioemotional Selectivity Theory (SST), we introduce perceived value and personal disposition as direct antecedents of behavioral intention, reflecting older adults’ heightened focus on emotionally meaningful and present-oriented goals rather than solely future-related utility26. This synthesized model preserves the core mechanisms of TAM while extending it into a more holistic and contextually nuanced framework tailored specifically to predicting smart healthcare adoption among older adults in China.

Theoretical background and hypotheses

While the core TAM establishes the fundamental mechanism of technology acceptance, its parsimony necessitates the incorporation of context-specific external variables to fully capture the adoption dynamics among older adults. This study introduces three key constructs—personal disposition, social support, and perceived value—each justified by a distinct theoretical lineage. First, drawing from personality psychology and technology readiness literature, personal disposition is introduced as a stable internal trait that predisposes individuals to form certain perceptions about technology27. Individuals high in openness and optimism (key facets of personal disposition) are likely to perceive new technologies as more useful and easier to use28. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

H1

Personal disposition positively influences PU.

H2

Personal disposition positively influences PEOU.

Second, the construct of social support is explicitly conceptualized as an operationalization of the social influence factor29. For older adults, particularly in a collectivist culture like China’s, influence from family, friends, and healthcare providers (a form of subjective norm) is a critical facilitator of adoption. This social support can enhance the perceived usefulness and reduce the perceived complexity of technology. Thus, we propose that:

H3

Social support positively influences PU.

H4

Social support positively influences PEOU.

Third, and most critically, we integrate the perceived value construct, grounded in the Socioemotional Selectivity Theory (SST)30. SST argues that with a limited time horizon, older adults prioritize emotionally meaningful goals and present-day satisfaction over future-oriented knowledge acquisition. Consequently, their acceptance of smart healthcare is not driven solely by utilitarian usefulness (PU) but also by the perceived value it brings—such as enhanced social connectedness, peace of mind, and a sense of autonomy. This value perception is a direct antecedent to their assessment of a technology’s usefulness. This leads to our hypotheses:

H5

Perceived value positively influences PU.

H6

Perceived value positively influences PEOU.

Building upon the foundational relationships of TAM, we also hypothesize:

H7

PU positively influences BI.

H8

PEOU positively influences BI.

H9

PEOU positively influences PU.

Methods

Study design and participants

This population-based cross-sectional study recruited community-dwelling older adults from Shaoxing, Zhejiang Province, China. The target population consisted of individuals who regularly accessed healthcare services, including community health centers, general hospitals, and rehabilitation facilities. Participants were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) being 60 years old or older; (2) able to understand and comprehend Chinese; (3) capable of expressing ideas accurately in speech. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe cognitive impairment or disorders; (2) any terminal illness. The investigation was conducted between August 2024 and January 2025. All methods were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Xinchang Hospital of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2025-LW-001). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants after comprehensive explanation of study objectives and procedures. The sample size for this study was determined according to a commonly applied rule of thumb in SEM, which suggests a minimum sample size of 10 observations per measured variable31. Given that our questionnaire comprised 21 measurement items, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 210. The final sample included 403 participants, substantially exceeding this threshold, thereby ensuring robust model estimation, adequate statistical power, and enhanced stability of the results.

Questionnaire design

Drawing upon validated measurement instruments from prior studies, we developed a preliminary “Survey Questionnaire on Elderly Adults’ Willingness to Adopt Smart Healthcare Services.” The questionnaire was adapted from Davis ‘s seminal TAM scale, with supplementary dimensions derived from Galit Nimrod’s and Cong Cao’s studies on personal disposition, social support, and perceived value32,33. The questionnaire consists of two parts. The first part collects information on the respondents’ socio-demographic characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, education level, and monthly income. The second part of the research is based on an extended TAM framework. This is achieved by employing six theoretical constructs that are measured using a set of 21 validated items.The conceptual model encompasses six dimensions: personal disposition (4 items), social support (4 items), perceived value (4 items), PU(3 items), PEOU(3 items), and BI (3 items). All constructs were measured using a 5-point Likert scale anchored at 1 (strongly disagree) and 5 (strongly agree). Participants were explicitly instructed to select the response option that most accurately reflected their subjective assessment for each questionnaire item. The total score for each dimension was calculated by summing the responses of all items within that dimension. The complete set of measurement items for each construct is provided in Appendix 1. The measurement model employed six constructs with the following score ranges and theoretical interpretations:

Personal Disposition(4–20): This construct captured fundamental demographic and socio-economic factors that form the baseline context for technology adoption. It was designed to measure respondents’ self-assessment of how their economic income, educational attainment, age, and geographic location (urban vs. rural) would influence their decisions regarding smart healthcare. This directly accounts for key potential confounders like SES and the digital divide.

Social Support(4–20): This dimension assessed the perceived availability of external support systems, including support from family members, the mitigating role of health insurance policies, and the facilitating role of community initiatives (e.g., promotional activities and a generally supportive atmosphere) in adopting smart healthcare.

Perceived Value(4–20): This construct evaluated the individual’s cost-benefit analysis based on their personal circumstances. It measured the recognition of a future need for smart healthcare due to one’s health status, the process of evaluating its potential benefits, and the subsequent consideration of use.

PEOU(3–15): Adapted from the classic TAM, this construct gauged the user’s subjective assessment of the effortlessness associated with interacting with smart healthcare systems. It measured facets such as the ease of learning, the simplicity of operation, and the ability to accomplish tasks without undue difficulty.

PU(3–15): Also adapted from the TAM, this construct measured the degree to which a user believes that smart healthcare would enhance the efficiency and outcomes of their healthcare management. It focused on the perceived utility for medical needs, the improvement in efficiency and convenience, and the overall enhancement of care quality.

BI(3–15): This construct measured the active willingness and planned future use of smart healthcare services. It captured the respondent’s openness to use, their willingness to learn how to use it, and their intention to engage with it frequently.

Data collection

Before data collection, informed consent was obtained from all participants. Data were collected through questionnaires and oral examinations conducted in the participants’ communities. A questionnaire survey was used in this investigation. The Scientific Research Department of Xinchang Hospital of Chinese Medicine organized a training session for the research team before the survey commenced. The trained researchers collected data using face-to-face interview techniques. The questionnaire was designed for self-administration by participants; however, if participants encountered difficulties, researchers assisted by recording their responses. The questionnaires were reviewed on-site, and all items were carefully checked. If obvious errors or omissions were identified, corrections and additions were made immediately. In this study, a total of 420 questionnaires were distributed, and 403 valid questionnaires were collected, resulting in an effective response rate of 95.95%.

Data analyis

The statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 27.0 software. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables. The mean and standard deviation (SD) were used to present continuous variables. Structural equation modeling (SEM) was performed using AMOS 28.0 to test the hypothesized model. The values of the comparative fit index (CFI), the ratio of chi-square to degrees of freedom (CMIN/DF), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were used to assess the fit of the hypothetical model.

Content validity

Content validity was evaluated through rigorous assessment by an expert panel comprising seven specialists, including physicians, nursing professionals, and public health practitioners. All panel members possessed substantial professional experience, having worked extensively in either hospital settings or regional community health service institutions. The content validity index (CVI), ranging from 0.91 to 0.95 after consultation with the seven experts, indicates that the questionnaire items have good content validity34. All items were determined to be scientifically robust and conceptually appropriate, requiring no further modifications.

Reliability and validity analysis

To ensure the reliability and validity of the questionnaire, a pilot study was conducted prior to its formal administration. A total of 100 questionnaires were collected in this pre-survey. The data were analyzed for reliability and validity using SPSS 27.0 Cronbach’s alpha is the most commonly used reliability test index. According to established standards, reliability coefficients are interpreted as follows: 0.6–0.7 indicates acceptable reliability, 0.7–0.8 suggests good reliability, 0.8–0.9 demonstrates excellent reliability, and 0.9-1.0 represents outstanding reliability35. In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the six dimensions were 0.867, 0.821, 0.854, 0.820, 0.867, and 0.886. The overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the questionnaire was 0.938, which meets the reliability test requirements. These results indicate that the scale used in this study has good internal consistency and reliability. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) and Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity were conducted on all measurement indicators. The results showed that the KMO value for the structural validity of each variable was 0.907, and the significance level (p-value) of Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity was less than 0.001. Exploratory factor analysis yielded a cumulative variance explanation rate of 71.725%, with standardized factor loadings of the items ranging from 0.583 to 0.817. According to the evaluation criteria, the KMO value ≥ 0.5 and a significant Bartlett’s Test of Sphericity indicate good validity36. These results confirm the appropriateness of the measurement indicators and support the validity of the scale. The satisfactory reliability and validity indices of the sample data suggest strong intercorrelations among variables, thereby justifying the application of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) for subsequent data analysis.

Discussion

This study developed an extended TAM to explore the factors influencing the adoption of smart healthcare among community-dwelling older adults in China. The main findings demonstrate that personal disposition, social support, and perceived value significantly strengthen older adults’ BI to adopt smart healthcare, primarily through both parallel and serial mediation effects mediated by PU and PEOU. The model revealed six significant parallel mediation pathways and three important chain mediation pathways, highlighting the complex psychological mechanisms that drive technology adoption in this demographic.

In this study, the willingness score of the older adult group to adopt smart healthcare was 10.00 ± 3.26, which is consistent with previous reports38,39. This suggests a moderately positive attitude toward smart healthcare adoption among older adults in the Shaoxing community. The observed positive effects of PU and PEOU on BI align with core TAM propositions, yet can be further elucidated through gerontological theories40,41. From the viewpoint of SST, older adults tend to prioritize emotionally meaningful goals and present-oriented satisfaction42. PU corresponds to this preference by delivering tangible immediate benefits—such as reassurance derived from health monitoring and improved social connectedness—that offer emotional gratification43. Meanwhile, PEOU helps reduce the emotional cost associated with learning new technology, thereby alleviating anxiety and frustration. Additionally, self-efficacy theory offers a complementary explanation: a system perceived as easy to use (high PEOU) enhances older adults’ confidence in their operational capability (technology self-efficacy). This increased self-efficacy subsequently reinforces their perception of the system’s usefulness (PU) and fortifies their intention for sustained use44. Thus, the relationships between PU-BI and PEOU-BI extend beyond cognitive evaluations and are deeply embedded in age-related motivational and emotional processes.

The mediation analysis uncovers nuanced pathways through which external variables affect BI. The six identified parallel mediation routes (e.g., Personal Disposition → PU → BI; Social Support → PEOU → BI) indicate that each external factor can independently promote adoption intention by either elevating perceived utility or simplifying perceived usability. More notably, the three significant chain mediation pathways (e.g., Social Support → PEOU → PU → BI) reveal a sequential cognitive mechanism. Specifically, this chain suggests that social support initially mitigates perceptions of technical difficulty (increasing PEOU), which in turn enables users to better appreciate the system’s practical benefits (enhancing PU), thereby strengthening their intention to adopt. This underscores that, for older adults, overcoming usability barriers is often an essential prerequisite for recognizing the true value of smart healthcare.

These findings provide practical implications for the design of interventions and the optimization of healthcare systems. First, interventions should be multi-dimensional. In addition to technical training aimed at improving PEOU, programs should clearly demonstrate how smart healthcare addresses everyday health concerns—thus enhancing PU—in ways that align with SST by highlighting emotional security and autonomy45. Second, at the clinical level, incorporating data from smart devices into electronic health records can support proactive healthcare46. The identified chain mediation effect indicates that when clinicians recommend user-friendly devices, a positive feedback loop can be initiated: ease of use encourages continued engagement, which in turn generates actionable health data for personalized medicine and early intervention47. Third, system designs must follow gerontechnology principles by incorporating intuitive interfaces—such as voice commands and large icons—to reduce cognitive demand13. Finally, policy measures should promote the development of technology-support networks within communities, training family members or volunteers to offer ongoing social support, which our model confirms directly strengthens both PEOU and PU.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The use of a convenience sample recruited from community centers in Shaoxing may restrict the generalizability of the findings, as participants were predominantly from low income and educational backgrounds, which may lead to overestimation of adoption intention among the wider population of older adults. Moreover, the cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences to be drawn; therefore, the identified pathways should be interpreted as associative rather than causative. Additionally, the dependence on self-reported measures may introduce social desirability bias and common method variance. Finally, the lack of data regarding non-respondents hinders the evaluation of potential selection bias, as those who participated may have had a more favorable attitude toward technology than those who declined.

In summary, this study confirms an extended TAM that clarifies the multi-faceted factors influencing smart healthcare adoption among older adults. Moving beyond conventional simplified models, the framework incorporates personal disposition, social support, and perceived value, while systematically elucidating both parallel and sequential mediation effects through PU and PEOU. The results emphasize that fostering technology adoption requires integrated strategies that target not only cognitive perceptions but also socio-emotional factors relevant to older adults. This study offers a theoretical basis and actionable insights to support the integration of smart healthcare into healthy aging policies and practices.
