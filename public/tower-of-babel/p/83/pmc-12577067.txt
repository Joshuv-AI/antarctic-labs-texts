Exploratory analysis of lamivudine legacy and clonal selection of HBs truncation mutants in HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yu-De Chu, Pei-Huan Ho, Chao-Wei Hsu, Chih-Yung Chiou, Ming-Wei Lai, Chau-Ting Yeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hepatitis B virus (HBV) integration and persistent covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) are key drivers of hepatocarcinogenesis and remain uncured by current nucleos(t)ide analog (NA) therapy. While high-barrier NAs like entecavir effectively suppress viral replication, they do not eliminate transcriptionally active HBV integrants. In this exploratory study, we analyzed HBV surface (HBs) gene truncation mutations in four patients with HBV-related hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), including two with prior lamivudine exposure. Next-generation sequencing of DNA and RNA from serum, tumor, and para-neoplastic liver tissues revealed truncation variants, such as sW172* and sW182* were, exclusively in the two lamivudine-experienced patients. These variants were predominantly enriched in tumor RNA, raising the possibility of clonal selection during hepatocarcinogenesis. No truncation mutations were observed in the two patients treated solely with entecavir, despite detectable intrahepatic cccDNA. These findings support the hypothesis that transcriptionally active HBV integrants, possibly shaped by prior lamivudine treatment, may contribute to HCC via truncated HBs protein expression. The selective presence of certain mutants in tumor tissue suggests a potential biological role, though further validation is needed. Given the small sample size and limited number of lamivudine-experienced cases, our observations should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, the results highlight the relevance of antiviral treatment history in shaping HBV mutational profiles and raise the possibility that integrant-derived HBs truncation variants could serve as biomarkers for HCC risk stratification. Early detection of such variants may help refine surveillance strategies in virally suppressed patients and inform future therapeutic approaches.

Introduction

Hepatitis B, caused by hepatitis B virus (HBV), manifests as either acute or chronic hepatitis B (CHB) [1]. While most individuals with acute HBV infection recover spontaneously within six months, a proportion progress to CHB, which remains a significant global health burden, particularly in endemic areas such as Taiwan [2]. CHB substantially contributes to liver-related morbidity and mortality, primarily through progression to liver failure, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [3–6].

In Taiwan, CHB is commonly managed with nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs), which suppress HBV replication, normalize alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels, and improve histologic and clinical outcomes [7, 8]. Nevertheless, complete viral eradication remains elusive due to the persistence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), a stable episomal reservoir that continues to transcribe viral RNA even under long-term antiviral treatment [9].

Lamivudine, one of the first approved NAs, is now rarely used due to its low genetic barrier to resistance. However, many patients currently receiving entecavir or tenofovir had initially been treated with lamivudine [10, 11]. This earlier exposure may have favored the emergence of HBV variants, including chromosomally integrated sequences encoding truncated hepatitis B surface (HBs) proteins with oncogenic potential [12]. These integrants can remain transcriptionally active long after lamivudine withdrawal and may not be silenced by newer NAs such as entecavir.

Owing to the overlap between the HBV polymerase and HBs genes, polymerase mutations selected by antiviral therapy often result in co-occurring mutations in the HBs gene. In some cases, these mutations generate premature stop codons that produce truncated surface proteins, such as rtA181T/sW172* and rtM204I/sW196* [13–15]. Experimental models have demonstrated that certain truncated HBs variants can induce endoplasmic reticulum stress, impair antigen secretion, and promote cellular transformation [16, 17]. Nevertheless, the distribution, origin, and selection dynamics of these oncogenic mutants in clinical specimens remain poorly defined.

In this study, we investigated the presence and tissue compartmentalization of HBs truncation mutations in four patients with HBV-related HCC who were currently or previously treated with entecavir, including two with prior lamivudine exposure. Using next-generation sequencing (NGS), we analyzed HBs gene sequences from serum, cancerous liver tissue, and para-neoplastic liver tissue. By comparing viral DNA and RNA profiles across compartments, we aimed to determine whether specific truncation mutants were clonally enriched in tumor tissue and assess whether their emergence might reflect a legacy effect of earlier lamivudine therapy. This study provides new insight into the long-term oncogenic consequences of HBV integration and may help identify circulating or tissue biomarkers of HCC risk in virally suppressed CHB patients.

Materials and methods

Patients and sample collection

This study was conducted with approval from the Institutional Review Board of Chang Gung Memorial Hospital (IRB No. 201900261B0). A total of four HCC patients who had received entecavir therapy were retrospectively enrolled. Among them, two patients had prior exposure to lamivudine. Paired tissue and serum specimens collected at the time of surgical resection were obtained from the Biobank of Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital. Longitudinal clinical information, including treatment history, virological markers, and pathological findings, was retrospectively reviewed and documented for subsequent analysis.

DNA isolation and cccDNA quantification

Serum-derived DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany; Cat. No. 51306) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. For liver tissues, extrachromosomal DNA was isolated using the Hirt method, which is optimized for the enrichment of episomal DNA, including HBV covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), as previously described [18]. Quantification of intrahepatic HBV cccDNA was performed using real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) with peptide nucleic acid (PNA) clamping to ensure specificity for cccDNA, following established protocols [19].

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissue specimens using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN; Cat. No. 52904). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription using Moloney murine leukemia virus (MMLV) reverse transcriptase (Biotools, New Taipei City, Taiwan; Cat. No. TTF-MLV01), following the manufacturer’s recommendations. The resulting cDNA was used for downstream amplification of HBV transcripts.

Deep sequencing of the HBs gene

HBs gene sequences were amplified by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) from three sources: serum-derived DNA, extrachromosomal DNA from liver tissues, and cDNA synthesized from tissue-derived RNA. PCR amplification was performed using HBs-specific primers (S-F: CCTGCTGGTGGCTCCAGTT and S-R: CGTCAGCAAACACTTGGC). The resulting amplicons were subjected to NGS for comprehensive analysis of HBs gene variants, as previously reported [20]. Sequence data were analyzed to identify HBs mutations, with particular attention to truncation variants and their distribution across tissue compartments and circulation.

Results

Distinct patterns of HBs truncation mutations in patient-1 with prior lamivudine exposure

To investigate the presence, origin, and tissue distribution of HBs gene truncation mutations in HCC, we analyzed serum and liver-derived HBV DNA and RNA from four HCC patients (designated patient-1 through patient-4), all of whom had received entecavir treatment, with two patients having prior exposure to lamivudine. A summary of treatment histories and sample collection timepoints is provided in Fig. 1. NGS was performed on DNA and RNA from serum, tumor, and adjacent non-tumorous liver tissues to characterize the mutational landscape of the HBs gene and to assess the compartmentalization and potential clonal enrichment of truncation mutations.

Patient-1 had received entecavir for 1.5 years, with HCC diagnosed 4 months after cessation of therapy (Fig. 1, top left panel). Notably, this patient had previously been treated with lamivudine for two years, approximately 12 years prior to the diagnosis. Quantification of liver tissue HBV DNA revealed a total viral DNA load of 9.9 × 105 copies/g, of which 9.6 × 105 copies/g was cccDNA, indicating that cccDNA comprised the vast majority of intrahepatic viral DNA, with negligible relaxed circular DNA (rcDNA) detected. Serum HBV DNA at the time of HCC diagnosis was low (356 copies/mL), suggesting suppressed but not entirely eliminated viremia.

NGS analysis of the HBs gene revealed the presence of two truncation mutations, sW36* (1.02%) and sW196* (3.60%), in serum-derived HBV DNA. Remarkably, identical mutations were also detected in the cccDNA extracted from para-neoplastic liver tissue at similar frequencies (sW36*: 1.0%; sW196*: 3.10%), suggesting that these variants likely originated from intrahepatic reservoirs and were not newly emergent in circulation. Analysis of HBV RNA extracted from para-neoplastic tissue revealed a broader spectrum of truncation mutants, including sC69* (5.95%), sW172* (4.71%), sW182* (2.29%), sW191* (3.66%), sW199* (4.94%), and sW223* (3.42%). This diversity indicates transcriptional activity from multiple mutant HBV templates within the non-tumorous hepatic environment.

In sharp contrast, HBV RNA from the cancerous tissue in patient-1 was overwhelmingly dominated by a single truncation variant, sW182*, which accounted for 99.5% of the total HBV RNA pool. No detectable cccDNA or HBV DNA was found in the tumor tissue itself, suggesting that the observed HBV RNA was transcribed from persistent episomal templates rather than from active replication intermediates. The selective enrichment of the sW182* mutant in tumor tissue implies a potential clonal expansion or transcriptional advantage, consistent with a possible role in tumorigenesis (Fig. 2).

Tumor-specific enrichment of sW172* mutant despite viral suppression in patient-2

Patient-2 had also previously received lamivudine and was under continuous entecavir therapy at the time of HCC diagnosis (Fig. 1, top right panel). A one-year treatment gap had occurred between the two regimens. Total liver tissue HBV DNA was measured at 6.1 × 104 copies/g, with cccDNA accounting for nearly all of it (6.0 × 104 copies/g), again indicating that the intrahepatic HBV reservoir was dominated by stable episomal DNA. Serum HBV DNA was undetectable, consistent with effective viral suppression. No truncation mutations were detected in the cccDNA of para-neoplastic tissue. However, HBV RNA extracted from this tissue harbored two truncation mutants: sW172* (7.5%) and sW196* (34.9%). Interestingly, in the corresponding tumor tissue, only sW172* was present in HBV RNA, and at a much higher frequency (55.0%). This selective enrichment suggests that the sW172* mutant may have undergone clonal expansion within the tumor, supporting a potential role in hepatocarcinogenesis. The absence of sW196* in tumor-derived RNA, despite its high prevalence in adjacent tissue, raises the possibility that sW196* may not confer a growth advantage or was selectively excluded from the tumor clone (Fig. 2).

Absence of truncation mutations in patient-3 treated exclusively with entecavir

Patient-3, who had been treated exclusively with entecavir, developed HCC during ongoing therapy (Fig. 1, bottom left panel). Quantitative analysis revealed total intrahepatic HBV DNA at 3.4 × 104 copies/g, with cccDNA comprising 3.3 × 104 copies/g, indicating almost exclusive presence of the stable viral form. Serum HBV DNA was undetectable. Importantly, no HBs truncation mutations were identified in any of the analyzed samples from this patient, including serum, tumor, and para-neoplastic liver tissues. This case provides a comparative reference, illustrating that HCC can occur even in the absence of detectable truncation mutants, and suggesting that alternative oncogenic mechanisms unrelated to HBs truncation may also be operative (Fig. 2).

HCC without evidence of truncation mutants in entecavir-treated patient-4

Patient-4 developed HCC approximately one year after initiating entecavir therapy (Fig. 1, bottom right panel). Tissue HBV DNA was measured at 4.8 × 104 copies/g, with cccDNA comprising 4.7 × 104 copies/g. Serum HBV DNA was undetectable at the time of HCC diagnosis. Consistent with patient-3, no truncation mutations were detected in any of the biological compartments analyzed. Notably, this patient later transitioned to tenofovir for reasons not documented in the medical record and subsequently experienced virological flares after treatment withdrawal. Intermittent elevations in transaminase levels were recorded during the treatment course and were attributed to episodic alcohol consumption. While these factors may have contributed to hepatic inflammation, there was no molecular evidence of HBV-related truncation variants in this case (Fig. 2).

Summary and implications: clonal enrichment of truncation mutants in tumors with historical lamivudine exposure

Taken together, the data reveal several key insights into the dynamics of HBs truncation mutations in the context of antiviral therapy and HCC development. First, in patients with a history of lamivudine exposure (patients 1 and 2), truncation mutants were more prevalent and exhibited selective enrichment in tumor tissues, suggesting a possible legacy effect of early-generation NAs with low genetic barriers to resistance. Second, in both patients where truncation variants were detected, tumor tissues harbored a narrower and more dominant spectrum of mutations compared to adjacent non-tumorous tissue, indicating a possible clonal selection process. Specifically, sW182* and sW172* showed strong dominance in the tumor microenvironment, consistent with prior reports of their oncogenic potential. Third, the absence of truncation mutations in patients treated exclusively with entecavir (patients-3 and − 4) supports the hypothesis that high-barrier NAs may limit the emergence or maintenance of such mutations over time.

Overall, these findings suggest that HBs truncation mutants may be transcriptionally active and selectively enriched in HCC tissues under specific treatment histories, particularly following exposure to older NAs. Their presence in para-neoplastic and serum compartments further supports their potential as circulating biomarkers for HCC risk. The data also underscore the importance of longitudinal virological surveillance, even in patients under effective viral suppression, and highlight the need for mechanistic studies to further elucidate the oncogenic roles of individual HBs truncation variants.

Discussion

This study provides mechanistic insight into the origin, distribution, and selection dynamics of HBs gene truncation mutations in HBV-related HCC under antiviral therapy. Four patients with HCC who were either undergoing continuous entecavir treatment (patients-2, -3, and − 4) or had recently discontinued entecavir (patient-1) were analyzed (Fig. 1). In all cases, serum and intrahepatic HBV replication was profoundly suppressed, minimizing interference from replicative intermediates such as rcDNA, linear, or single-stranded HBV DNA [21]. This suppression allowed for high-confidence interpretation of HBs gene sequencing results across DNA and RNA compartments.

Two distinct sources of oncogenic HBs truncation mutants were observed: one derived from episomal cccDNA and the other from chromosomally integrated HBV DNA [22, 23]. When detectable, serum HBV DNA mirrored the mutational spectrum of intrahepatic cccDNA, as seen in patient-1 (Fig. 2, top and bottom bars). This alignment suggests that viral RNAs transcribed from cccDNA retained the capacity to be packaged, reverse-transcribed, and secreted as mature virions, thereby contributing to the circulating viral pool [24]. In contrast, HBV RNA extracted from para-neoplastic liver tissue displayed a markedly different truncation profile, enriched for mutations such as sW172*, sW199*, and sC69*, which were absent in serum and cccDNA. These variants are presumed to originate from integrated HBV DNA rather than episomal templates [23, 25] (Fig. 2, second and third bars).

Importantly, HBs gene transcripts derived from cccDNA rarely reached a mutation frequency above the 1% detection threshold in para-neoplastic tissue and thus were not represented in the figure (Fig. 2, patients-1 and − 2, third and bottom bars). This observation suggests that integrant-derived transcripts likely constitute the majority of the intrahepatic HBs mRNA pool in non-cancerous tissues [26], effectively diluting the representation of cccDNA-derived transcripts. However, unlike cccDNA-derived genomic RNA, these integrant-derived transcripts lacked the structural completeness necessary for packaging and secretion, consistent with their absence in serum HBV DNA [27, 28]. These findings collectively support a model in which only cccDNA-sourced viral RNA contributes to active virion production, whereas integrated HBV DNA serves primarily as a source of mutant mRNA with potential functional or oncogenic consequences [29].

A key and consistent observation was the dramatic enrichment of specific truncation mutants, sW182* in patient-1 and sW172* in patient-2, exclusively in tumor tissues. In both patients, prior exposure to lamivudine was documented, implicating early-generation NA therapy as a potential contributor to the genesis of integrated HBV DNA harboring oncogenic truncation mutations [30] (Fig. 3). It is plausible that lamivudine treatment exerted selective pressure on HBV, promoting the emergence of replication-defective but potentially oncogenic mutants [31]. Subsequent viral integration events may have preserved these variants within the host genome. Upon cessation of lamivudine and transition to entecavir, wild-type cccDNA likely re-emerged as the dominant transcriptional template. However, the HBV-integrant-derived transcripts carrying mutant HBs sequences remained transcriptionally active, particularly in non-tumorous hepatic tissues [23].

In patient-2, where cccDNA activity was effectively suppressed by entecavir (Fig. 1), no serum HBV DNA was detected, further supporting the conclusion that replication-incompetent HBV integrants were the primary source of intrahepatic HBs transcripts [24]. Despite the broader spectrum of truncation mutations present in non-tumorous liver tissue, only a single variant, sW172*, was found in tumor-derived HBV RNA, enriched from 7.5% in para-neoplastic tissue to 55.0% in tumor tissue. A similar clonal dominance was observed for sW182* in patient-1, rising from 2.29% to 99.5% in tumor RNA. These findings point to a stringent selection process during tumor evolution, favoring specific truncation mutants with potential oncogenic properties [32].

Interestingly, patients-3 and − 4, who had no prior exposure to lamivudine and were treated exclusively with entecavir, exhibited no detectable HBs truncation mutations in any compartment, despite the presence of cccDNA in liver tissues and confirmed HCC diagnosis. This contrast supports the hypothesis that the emergence and persistence of oncogenic truncation variants may be significantly influenced by the mutagenic legacy of earlier therapies with low resistance barriers, such as lamivudine [31]. The absence of these variants in entecavir-only patients further highlights the potential preventive benefit of initiating treatment with high-barrier agents [33].

Collectively, our findings support a model in which integrated HBV DNA, particularly that formed during early lamivudine therapy, acts as a long-term source of aberrant, replication-defective transcripts encoding oncogenic HBs truncation proteins. These variants are not secreted but may contribute to tumor development through intracellular mechanisms such as ER stress, immune modulation, or interference with normal surface antigen processing [34]. These truncated proteins may promote hepatocarcinogenesis through multiple mechanisms, including induction of endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, dysregulation of antigen presentation, immune escape, or modulation of oncogenic signaling pathways such as HIF1A and TGF-β [35]. The tumor-specific clonal enrichment of select variants underscores their potential biological significance and justifies further functional validation. Nevertheless, given the small sample size (n = 4), the current study should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate the findings and generalize their clinical significance.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the persistent oncogenic potential of HBs truncation mutants derived from integrated HBV DNA, particularly in patients with prior exposure to lamivudine. These variants remain transcriptionally active during subsequent entecavir treatment, especially in non-cancerous hepatic tissues, and may undergo selective clonal enrichment within tumor regions. The dominant presence of specific mutants, such as sW172* and sW182*, in HCC tissue underscores that only a subset of truncation mutations likely confers a growth or survival advantage during hepatocarcinogenesis. In contrast, such mutations were absent in patients treated exclusively with entecavir, suggesting that high-barrier antiviral agents may mitigate the generation or long-term persistence of these oncogenic variants. These exploratory findings suggest a potential impact of antiviral treatment history when interpreting HBV mutational landscapes in HCC. Moreover, they support the potential utility of integrant-derived HBs truncation mutations as biomarkers for HCC risk stratification and as candidate molecular targets for future therapeutic intervention. Validation in larger patient cohorts and mechanistic dissection of the oncogenic properties of individual truncation variants will be essential to further elucidate their clinical and biological significance. This exploratory study provides preliminary evidence for the oncogenic potential of specific HBs truncation variants, which warrants further investigation in larger patient cohorts.
