Rethinking extubation failure as risk calibration in ventilator liberation
Authors: Jin Sun Cho
Published: 2026

Liberation from mechanical ventilation is widely regarded as a major milestone in recovery from critical illness. Nevertheless, extubation failure remains common and is consistently associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer hospital stays, and increased mortality [1]. Consequently, extubation failure has traditionally been viewed as an adverse event that should be minimized. However, decisions regarding extubation inherently involve uncertainty and competing risks because delaying extubation prolongs exposure to the well-recognized harms of invasive mechanical ventilation [2,3]. In this context, pursuing the lowest possible extubation failure rate may not represent optimal care and may instead harm patients through unnecessary prolongation of ventilation. Viewed from this perspective, extubation failure may represent not simply a preventable complication, but rather an inevitable consequence of balancing the competing risks of premature and delayed liberation.

In this issue, Gur et al. [4] examine the relationship between intensive care unit (ICU)-level extubation failure rates and patient outcomes. Their principal finding was a U-shaped association between unit-level extubation failure rates and both 30-day mortality and ventilator-free days, with poorer outcomes observed at both extremes. Although extubation failure at the individual patient level remains a marker of physiological vulnerability [5], analyses at the unit level suggest that the overall failure rate may reflect how cautiously or aggressively an ICU approaches ventilator liberation. This observation is consistent with prior work by Kapnadak et al. [6], who demonstrated increased ventilator-free days in ICUs operating within an intermediate extubation failure-rate range. Collectively, these findings challenge the deeply ingrained assumption in critical care that lower extubation failure rates necessarily indicate higher-quality care [7]. Extremely low failure rates may reflect systematic delays in extubation, whereas excessively high rates likely indicate premature liberation before adequate physiological readiness has been achieved. Together, these observations frame ventilator liberation as a risk-benefit trade-off, analogous to blood transfusion thresholds, fluid resuscitation targets, and glycemic control strategies [8-10], in which optimal outcomes depend on balancing competing risks rather than eliminating adverse events altogether.

The tendency toward excessive caution in extubation decisions has been well documented across diverse clinical settings. Data from the Weaning from mechanical ventilation in intensive care units across 50 countries (WEAN SAFE) study [11], a large international point-prevalence study of weaning practices, demonstrated that initiation of weaning was delayed in 22.4% of patients globally, suggesting that conservative practice is not an isolated phenomenon but rather a systemic feature of critical care culture. Taran et al. [12] further reported that decisions to withhold extubation often did not correspond to objective physiological risk, with 38% of patients not extubated within 24 hours after their first successful spontaneous breathing trial. Clinicians consistently overestimated the likelihood of extubation failure even among patients who met standard readiness criteria, a bias that may result in thousands of unnecessary ventilator-days annually [13]. The harms associated with these additional ventilator-days are well established. Diaphragmatic atrophy begins within days of controlled ventilation [14], ventilator-associated pneumonia carries a case-fatality rate of up to 40% [15], and ICU delirium is independently associated with long-term cognitive impairment [16]. Consequently, a failure-averse culture may unnecessarily prolong ventilation in physiologically ready patients. Very low unit-level extubation failure rates, particularly when standard weaning readiness criteria have already been satisfied, do not necessarily indicate superior care.

By demonstrating a U-shaped association between extubation failure and outcomes, the study by Gur et al. [4] makes several important methodological contributions. First, the unit-level analytic approach reframes extubation failure as a systems-level performance indicator rather than solely an individual patient event. The authors therefore propose a potential tool for ICU quality assessment that reflects a unit’s risk-calibration culture rather than simply quantifying adverse events. Second, unlike previous studies that matched patients according to severity at ICU admission [6], the authors matched patients using variables measured at the time of extubation, thereby creating comparator groups with similar physiological status at the moment the liberation decision was made. However, variables measured at extubation are downstream consequences of the preceding clinical trajectory, which complicates causal interpretation. Consequently, the observed associations may reflect both the extubation event itself and antecedent illness severity, and residual confounding cannot be excluded given the retrospective study design. In addition, the single-center retrospective design limits generalizability. Notably, the observed optimal extubation failure rate for ventilator-free days (22.8%) was substantially higher than the previously reported range of 7%–15% [6]. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in methodology, case mix, institutional practices, and definitions of extubation failure. Accordingly, the reported threshold should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating observation that requires validation across diverse clinical settings. The appropriate extubation failure rate for a given ICU will likely depend on its patient population, staffing and monitoring resources, and availability of noninvasive respiratory support after extubation.

Unlike catheter-associated infection, pressure ulcers, or unplanned extubation, for which lower rates unambiguously reflect higher-quality care, extubation failure appears to show a bidirectional relationship with outcomes in which both unusually low and unusually high rates may indicate suboptimal practice. This distinction has important implications for the design of weaning protocols. Rather than pursuing maximal reduction in extubation failure, future protocols should acknowledge the existence of an acceptable performance range, recognizing that complete elimination of extubation failure is neither feasible nor desirable. Prediction models integrating physiological readiness, weaning indices, and unit-level contextual factors may help define optimal extubation failure thresholds and individualize extubation timing. Emerging predictive analytics and real-time physiological monitoring may further improve individualized extubation timing and facilitate identification of patients at risk of either premature or delayed liberation [17,18]. Finally, multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate this U-shaped relationship across diverse ICU settings and to determine whether unit-level extubation failure rates can serve as practical quality metrics rather than universal benchmarks. In this regard, the threshold identified in this single-center retrospective study should not be adopted as a universal target because its applicability to individual extubation decisions and cross-institutional benchmarking remains unvalidated.

The findings of Gur et al. [4]challenge the long-held assumption that extubation failure is necessarily a marker of clinical misjudgment that careful practice should eliminate. By demonstrating a U-shaped relationship between ICU-level extubation failure rates and patient outcomes, the authors suggest that successful ventilator liberation should be judged not by the failure rate itself, but by whether that rate reflects a deliberate and appropriately calibrated balance between competing risks. A unit that achieves an extremely low failure rate through systematic excessive caution is not necessarily providing optimal care; rather, it may be exchanging one set of harms for another that is less immediately visible. Recognizing this trade-off represents an essential step toward a more rational and patient-centered approach to weaning. In modern critical care practice, the safest path may not be the one associated with the fewest failures.
