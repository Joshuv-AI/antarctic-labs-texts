High Accuracy, Questionable References: Large Language Models’ Performance and Citation Reliability in Foot and Ankle Surgery Examinations
Authors: Benjamin Nieves-Lopez, Andrea Fabregas, Gonzalo F. Del Rio Montesinos, José A. Rosario-González, Jean L. Santos-Agrait, Edward T. Haupt, Keith T. Aziz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Artificial intelligence large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used for medical education and decision support in orthopaedics, yet their performance on foot and ankle surgery board-style questions remains underexplored. Additionally, the capability of LLMs to provide reliable references and source material for education has not been evaluated. Therefore, this study aimed to compare the accuracy and citation reliability of ChatGPT‑5.4, Gemini‑3, and Copilot on foot and ankle surgery Self-Assessment Examination (SAE) questions.Methods:A total of 192 multiple-choice questions (64 text-only, 128 image-based) from OrthoBullets Foot and Ankle SAE forms D and E were entered into ChatGPT‑5.4, Gemini‑3, and Copilot; video-based items were excluded because of Copilot limitations. Accuracy was compared overall and by question type and imaging modality. References volunteered or prompted were classified as real, partially fabricated, or completely fabricated and compared across models and by answer correctness. Statistical significance was set at a P value <.05.Results:ChatGPT‑5.4 achieved the highest overall accuracy (89.6%) vs Gemini‑3 (76.6%) and Copilot (74.0%). ChatGPT‑5.4 and Gemini‑3 showed accuracy that did not differ significantly on text-only vs image-based questions, whereas Copilot performed significantly worse on image-based items (P = .028). With the numbers available, no significant difference in accuracy could be detected by imaging modality for any model. ChatGPT‑5.4, Gemini‑3, and Copilot fabricated 22.9%, 16.1%, and 2.8% of all references, respectively; most ChatGPT‑5.4 and Gemini‑3 fabrications were partially fabricated, whereas all Copilot fabrications were completely fabricated. ChatGPT‑5.4 fabricated significantly more references when it answered incorrectly (P = .013).Conclusion:ChatGPT‑5.4 showed the highest accuracy on foot and ankle SAE questions, with diminished image-related performance gaps relative to prior generations, but also the highest rate of reference fabrication. All models produced fabricated citations, underscoring the need to verify AI-generated references and to use LLMs as adjunct rather than primary educational tools in foot and ankle surgery.

Introduction

The use of artificial intelligence (AI) in medicine has been exponentially expanding over the past decade, especially in orthopaedics. 1 Some examples of large language models (LLMs) include ChatGPT, Copilot, Gemini, among others. All of these work to enable the analysis of large data, identification of patterns, and generation of predictions with minimal input. LLMs are based on algorithms that uses data from internet-based sources and information from previous interactions, generating conversational human-like responses. This increase in AI application has largely been driven by its common use by medical students and residents as a supplementary learning resource, particularly for board-style question preparation.2,3

LLMs have demonstrated utility in medical education, generating concrete explanations and summarized concepts for board-style questions and hypothetical clinical cases. 4 Additionally, it improves patient’s comprehension and engagement by simplifying explanations to orthopaedic conditions, procedures, and management. 5 Although AI is proficient in providing standardized orthopaedic assessments, it still underperforms in cases that require imaging interpretation.6-8 Certainly, this represents a significant challenge in AI’s integration to orthopaedic surgery where imaging studies are of utmost importance for clinical and surgical decision making.

The use of AI in foot and ankle surgery remains an emerging field, with the most common applications mainly focused on diagnostic support and clinical decision making. AI models have demonstrated performance comparable to specialists in radiographic interpretation, fracture classification, and deformity measurements, improving both accuracy and efficiency. 9 In Achilles tendon pathology, AI has additionally shown to improve accuracy and efficiency in preoperative planning and rehabilitation monitoring through imaging and wearable data integration. 10 However, its role in knowledge-based assessment is less defined. Some studies evaluating AI models on standardized orthopaedic questions report variable performance, especially due to difficulties with image-based questions.6,11 Thus, although AI is increasingly effective in interpreting patient data and supporting clinical decisions, its performance in subspecialty-specific knowledge assessment remains unclear.

Despite AI’s improvement, one of the major concerns on AI’s integration is the phenomenon of AI hallucination. This term is given for the information generated by AI models that might appear plausible but is partially or entirely fabricated. 12 This includes the generation of nonexistent references, the use of incorrect citations, or misleading clinical explanations. Previous literature has shown that AI models tend to produce fabricated citations, altering author names, study titles, and journal details on medical essay-driven prompts. 13 AI hallucination has been documented in multiple medical settings, representing a crucial concern for its use in research and its integration as a support tool for clinical decision making.13-15 Despite the growing evidence of AI in both its potential and limitations in orthopaedics, AI’s integration in foot and ankle surgery remains less well investigated.11,16

The role of AI as an educational resource in foot and ankle surgery remains unclear, particularly for questions with visual elements, such as those included in the foot and ankle surgery Self-Assessment Examination (SAE). In addition, the reliability of AI in providing accurate and verifiable academic references in response to these questions remains unexplored. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the performance of ChatGPT-5.4, Gemini-3, and Copilot on answering the foot and ankle surgery SAE and its capability to accurately provide real references used to answer these questions.

Methods

Data Set

This study used foot and ankle surgery questions from the OrthoBullets Foot and Ankle SAE forms D and E (www.orthobullets.com), to evaluate the accuracy rates of the LLMs ChatGPT-5.4, Gemini-3, and Copilot. From a total of 200 questions, 64 were text-only, 128 were image-based, and 8 contained a clinical video as part of the question. Because of Copilot’s inability to interpret videos, the 8 questions containing a clinical video were excluded from the study. Consequently, a total of 192 questions were eligible for analysis. All the questions, each accompanied by multiple-choice answers, were copied and pasted into the LLMs’ text box. Accompanying visual elements such as radiographs, clinical pictures, computed tomography (CT) scans, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies, and charts were copied directly from the web page and pasted into the LLMs’ text box in the original JPG format, to prevent pixelation or changes in resolution, without any associated description or caption of the images. To eliminate the potential of learning bias from previous interactions, prior conversations were deleted before independently inserting each question, and a new chat session for each question was initiated. After presenting each question and its choices, all the LLMs automatically selected one of the provided choices as the answer.

To explore the LLMs’ capabilities to provide references, their response was evaluated to determine if the LLMs voluntarily cited a reference as part of their answer to the question. If the LLMs did not provided a reference, it was prompted to “Provide the references of the sources used to answer this question.” The total amount of references per question was quantified and, then, were evaluated to determine if they were real or not. A real reference consisted of accurately naming all parts of an existent reference, including the name of the authors, title, journal information, publication year, and the website link. If every component of the reference provided by the LLMs were non-existent, it was categorized as a complete fabricated reference. If some of the components of the reference were incorrect, non-compatible, or non-existent, it was categorized as a partial fabricated reference. The methodology is summarized in Figure 1.

Statistical Analysis

The overall performances of ChatGPT-5.4, Gemini-3, and Copilot were independently analyzed using χ2 tests. Additionally, separate analyses were performed to evaluate the LLMs accuracy based on the presence of visual elements in the questions, as opposed to text-only. Further stratification of the visual elements was performed to assess LLMs variation in performance. LLMs tendency to provide references voluntarily were independently evaluated based on the correctness of their answer and the presence of visual elements using χ2 and, where sample sizes were less than 5, Fisher-Freeman-Halton exact tests. Additionally, the total amount of references provided, further stratified by the real and fabricated references, was evaluated and compared based on the accuracy of the LLMs using an independent sample t test. The LLMs’ rate of reference fabrication relative to the total amount of references was evaluated. Independent comparisons of reference fabrication, stratifying by complete or partial, were performed using a paired sample t test. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 29.0, with a threshold for statistical significance set at a P value <.05. All analyses were exploratory, and results should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

This study used deidentified, standardized questions and did not involve human or animal subjects; therefore, institutional review board approval was not required.

Results

LLMs’ Performance on the Foot and Ankle Surgery SAE

Of the 192 questions, ChatGPT-5.4 obtained a significantly higher accuracy rate of 89.6% (172/192) compared with Gemini-3’s 76.6% (147/192) and Copilot’s 74.0% (142/192) (P < .001, P < .001; respectively). ChatGPT-5.4 performed significantly better than Gemini-3 and Copilot in both text-only (P < .001, P = .004, respectively) and image-based questions (P < .001, P = .002; respectively). ChatGPT-5.4 exhibited an accuracy rate of 92.2% (59/64) in text-only questions compared with 88.3% (113/128) in image-based questions, with no significant difference (P = .404). Gemini-3 correctly answered 81.3% (52/64) of the text-only questions compared with 74.2% (95/128) in image-based questions, with no significant difference (P = .278). Copilot performed significantly better in text-only questions with a correctness rate of 82.8% (53/64) compared with 68.8% (88/128) in image-based questions (P = .028), as shown in Table 1.

LLMs’ Capability to Provide the References Used for Their Response

ChatGPT-5.4 voluntarily provided the references used in 71.9% (138/192) of the questions. When it answered correctly, it volunteered significantly more (75.6%, 130/172) compared with that (40.0%, 8/20) when it answered incorrectly (P < .001). No significant differences were observed when stratified by the presence of images, providing references in 78.1% (50/64) of the text-only questions and 68.8% (88/128) of the image-based questions, with no significant difference (P = .173).

Gemini-3 voluntarily provided the references used to answer in 3.6% (7/192) of the questions. It volunteered in 2.7% (4/147) of the questions that were accurately answered compared with 6.7% (3/45) when answered incorrectly. No significant differences were observed when stratified by the presence of images, providing references in 4.7% (3/64) of the text-only questions and 3.1% (4/128) of the image-based questions, with no significant difference (P = .586).

Copilot voluntarily provided the references used to answer in 48.4% (93/192) of the questions. When it answered correctly, it volunteered significantly more (53.5%, 76/142) compared with 34.0% (17/50) when it answered incorrectly (P = .018). When stratified by the presence of images, Copilot volunteered significantly more in the text-only questions (62.5%, 40/64) compared with the image-based questions (41.4%, 53/128; P = .006), as shown in Table 3.

Reference Fabrication Rates

ChatGPT-5.4 provided 703 references in total with a mean of 3.7 (SD = 1.3) and 3.8 (SD = 1.4) references per question answered correctly or incorrectly, respectively. With the numbers available, no significant difference in the total amount of references could be detected based on accuracy (P = .210). From these references, 542 (77.1%) were real and 161 (22.9%) were fabricated. With the numbers available, no significant difference in the amount of real (P = .516) and fabricated (P = .983) references could be detected based on accuracy. ChatGPT-5.4 provided significantly more fabricated references when it answered incorrectly, with a mean of 1.2 (SD = 1.3) compared with 0.8 (SD = 0.9) when it answered accurately (P = .013). From the 161 fabricated references, 80.1% (129/161) were partially fabricated and 19.9% (32/161) were completely fabricated (P < .001).

Gemini-3 provided 796 references in total, providing significantly more references when it answered correctly (mean of 4.2, SD = 1.5) compared to incorrect answers (mean of 4.1, SD = 1.0; P = .005). From these references, 668 (83.9%) were real and 128 (16.1%) were fabricated. A mean of 3.5 (SD = 1.6) real references was provided when answered correctly compared to a mean of 3.5 (SD = 1.3) when incorrect. A mean of 0.7 (SD = 0.9) fabricated references was provided when answered correctly compared with a mean of 0.6 (SD = 0.8) when it answered incorrectly. No significant differences in the amount of real and fabricated references were observed based on accuracy. From the 128 fabricated references, 91.4% (117/128) were partially fabricated and 8.6% (11/128) were completely fabricated (P < .001).

Copilot provided 929 references in total, with a mean of 4.7 (SD = 1.9) and 5.4 (SD = 2.4) references per question answered correctly or incorrectly, respectively. From these references, 903 (97.2%) were real and 26 (2.8%) were fabricated. A mean of 4.5 (SD = 1.9) real references was provided when answered correctly compared with a mean of 5.2 (SD = 2.3) when incorrect. A mean of 0.1 (SD = 0.5) fabricated references were provided when answered correctly compared with a mean of 0.1 (SD = 0.4) when it answered incorrectly. No significant differences in the amount of real and fabricated references were observed based on accuracy. From the 26 fabricated references, 0.0% (0/26) were partially fabricated and 100.0% (26/26) were completely fabricated (P < .001).

Overall, ChatGPT-5.4 fabricated significantly more references compared with Copilot but not Gemini-3 (P < .001, P = .059; respectively). These results are detailed in Tables 4 and 5.

Discussion

The use of AI in medicine has experienced a considerable expansion over the past decade, with its applications in orthopaedic surgery ranging from clinical decision support to medical education.1,2 Prior literature has examined the performance of AI on orthopaedic standardized examinations as well as its utility in responding to frequently asked patient questions, and although meaningful improvements have been documented across successive AI generations, a persistent limitation has been identified with image-incorporating questions.3,5,7 Despite the increase in literature, the role of AI in foot and ankle surgery, especially in standardized knowledge assessment, remains limited. This gap is particularly relevant because foot and ankle surgery often relies on nuanced clinical reasoning and individualized decision making rather than rigid treatment algorithms, making it a valuable litmus test for AI’s ability to navigate complex orthopaedic scenarios.

In the search for a better understanding of AI’s integration into foot and ankle surgery, we evaluated the performance of ChatGPT-5.4, Gemini-3, and Copilot in standardized foot and ankle surgery–related questions. In alignment with prior studies, ChatGPT showed the highest overall accuracy rate in orthopaedic surgery SAEs compared with other LLMs across text-only and image-based questions alike.17,18 Previous literature based on earlier AI models’ iterations has shown that LLMs tend to perform significantly worse on image-incorporating questions across various orthopaedic subspecialty settings.7,19 However, in this study, newer versions of these LLMs were used, and neither ChatGPT nor Gemini exhibited a significant difference in performance between text-only and image-based questions. The only model testing significantly worse on image-based questions was Copilot. This divergence between LLMs suggests that the advances in multimodal processing may have not been consistent through different models, particularly on its capability to interpret images. Therefore, ongoing refinement and augmentation of LLM training might improve AI’s feasibility as an educational resource.

Given the integral role of imaging in foot and ankle surgery, a further stratification was performed to determine whether specific imaging modalities were associated with differential AI performance. With the numbers available, no significant difference in accuracy could be detected based on imaging type. Comparable findings have been described in other orthopaedic surgical settings, where AI’s accuracy did not vary significantly across distinct radiographic formats.7,20 These results suggest that the specific imaging modality may not serve as a primary determinant of AI performance. Rather, other factors, such as the degree of reliance on imaging to select the correct answer, may represent more meaningful sources of variability.21,22

Beyond the accuracy assessment, this study also evaluated each AI model’s capacity to provide pertinent sources to support their responses. Copilot provided the greatest total volume of references, significantly surpassing ChatGPT and Gemini, whereas Gemini rarely volunteered citations spontaneously. Both ChatGPT and Copilot were significantly more likely to provide references voluntarily when answering correctly, which suggests a degree of situational calibration regarding the confidence of their responses. Gemini did not exhibit this association. However, the tendency of these models to provide references, even when answers are incorrect, also highlights a potentially concerning feature of LLM behavior: rather than consistently acknowledging uncertainty or limitations, these systems may generate responses that appear authoritative and well supported.

When the credibility of the provided references was assessed, Copilot showed the lowest overall fabrication rate, and ChatGPT the highest. Although Copilot overall produced the fewest fabricated citations, all its fabricated references were completely fabricated. By contrast, most fabrications generated by ChatGPT and Gemini were partial, reflecting alterations in specific bibliographic elements rather than fabrication of nonexistent sources. Notably, ChatGPT produced significantly more fabricated references when answering questions incorrectly compared with correctly answered questions, a pattern not observed for Gemini or Copilot. This finding is particularly concerning because fabricated references may lend credibility to otherwise incorrect responses, making errors more difficult for users to recognize. Although, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate AI reference fabrication in the context of foot and ankle surgery, prior investigations in broader medical settings have identified substantial rates of AI-generated citation fabrication.9-12,23

The generation of fabricated references by AI systems introduces significant ethical concerns, particularly when AI is incorporated into clinical decision making or research without independent verification.10,11 This concern may be more significant during residency training, where reliance on AI-generated sources can potentially impair the development of critical assessment skills and lead to inadequate health care delivery.9,12 Importantly, the substantial fabricated references found in this study does not eliminate this concern; rather, infrequent but plausible fabrications may be especially problematic because they can appear credible and may escape routine scrutiny. As AI-generated responses become increasingly fluent and convincing, users may face greater difficulty identifying subtle inaccuracies or fabricated evidence. Despite AI’s advancements, it should serve as a complementary educational instrument rather than a replacement for expert judgment, and all AI-generated answers and references must be independently verified before academic or clinical use.

Despite its findings, this study has several limitations. Copilot’s inability to process video content impeded the evaluation of clinical video-based questions, which were accordingly excluded from analysis. Questions were not subcategorized by clinical subtopic. Therefore, it remains unknown whether certain topic distributions may have differentially influenced AI performance across models. Additionally, because images in standardized orthopaedic examinations may vary in resolution, clarity, and overall interpretability, marginal image quality variations may have differentially influenced each model’s performance, limiting our ability to separate the effects of image quality from the models’ inherent image-processing capabilities. Another limitation of this study is the possibility of LLMs’ being exposed during pretraining to publicly accessible orthopaedic examination questions or answer explanations, including resources such as OrthoBullets; therefore, model accuracy may partially reflect retrieval of previously encountered material rather than independent reasoning. Subgroup analyses by image type were limited by small sample sizes, and because no power analysis was performed, these findings may have been underpowered to detect true differences and should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Fabricated references were not stratified by resource type between journal articles, book chapters, or websites; thus, which category of academic source AI models are most prone to fabricating remains unclear. The requirement for LLMs to provide references when none were initially offered may have biased the models toward generating citations, potentially increasing the likelihood of fabricated references. Finally, although the correctness of the selected response was evaluated, the accuracy of their reasoning and explanations was not assessed. This limits conclusions regarding AI’s clinical rationale beyond the binary outcome of response correctness.

Conclusion

ChatGPT achieved the highest accuracy among the 3 models evaluated on foot and ankle surgery standardized questions, and the image-based performance gap previously documented with earlier AI versions appears to have attenuated for ChatGPT and Gemini, with Copilot remaining the exception. No imaging modality significantly influenced AI performance across models. Critically, reference fabrication rates were substantial, with ChatGPT demonstrating the highest overall fabrication rate. These findings emphasize the importance of verifying all AI-generated citations and reinforce the role of AI as a supplementary educational resource rather than a main learning source in foot and ankle surgery.
