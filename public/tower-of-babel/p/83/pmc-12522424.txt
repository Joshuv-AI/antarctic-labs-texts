Effect of intracerebral infusion rate on optic nerve tissue pressure and diurnal intracranial pressure in rats
Authors: Sera N. Ganearachchi, Brianna C. Afiat, Pei Ying Lee, Anh Hoang, Christine T. O. Nguyen, Peter Wostyn, Bang V. Bui, Da Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo characterize how optic nerve tissue sheath (ONSp) responds to changes in intracerebral fluid infusion rate and consider if low rate intracerebral ventricular (ICV) infusion changes diurnal intracranial pressure (ICP) in rats.MethodsAnesthetized adult male rats underwent cannula placement into the lateral ventricles, for infusion and ICP monitoring. ICV infusion rate controlled ICP. A second syringe pump delivered sodium fluorescein for quantification of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow to the optic nerve with fluorescence imaging (n = 14). Cannula were placed in lateral ventricle and under the optic nerve sheath (n = 6) for simultaneous pressure measurement at different ICV infusion rates, and at normal (n = 6, 95.6 ± 5.9mmHg) or high blood pressure (n = 4, 158.0 ± 9.5mmHg, intravenous Angiotensin II). Four rats were implanted with sub-dural sensors for awake ICP monitoring. Animals had ICV infusion of sterile saline at a low rate (2.5 ml/hr) for the first 4 weeks followed by a high rate (10 ml/hr) for week 5.ResultsHigher ICV flow rates produce predictable increases in ICP, which result in an increased ONSp. CSF pressure was 73 ± 3% lower at the optic nerve sheath compared with the lateral ventricle (ONSp = 0.71*ICP-5.69). At normal blood pressure, average ICP was 15.5 ± 0.4mmHg and ONSp was 3.2 ± 0.6mmHg. At high blood pressure, ICP increased to 16.8 ± 4.5mmHg and ONSp was 4.2 ± 4.4mmHg. For the chronic study, mean ICP levels were stable across the 5 weeks, however peak ICP was increased particularly during nocturnal hours.ConclusionChanges in rat ICP directly change ONSp in a predictable manner. Chronic low volume ICV fluid infusion does not substantially increase rat ICP.

Introduction

In addition to the pressure inside the eye (intraocular pressure, IOP), blood pressure (BP) and cerebrospinal fluid pressure (CSFp, or intracranial pressure, ICP) can also modify the shape and function of the optic nerve by impacting the optic nerve at its insertion to the eye [1]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is continuous between the brain and the sheath that surrounds the optic nerve, all the way up to its entry into the eyeball. Lower retrolaminar tissue pressure, which is affected by the optic nerve sheath pressure [2], has been associated with an increased risk of vision loss from glaucoma [3]. On the other hand excessively high optic nerve sheath pressure, as can occur in conditions that involve raised intracranial pressure (e.g. head trauma, idiopathic intracranial hypertension) can cause congestion at the optic nerve head and subsequent neuronal injury [4–7]. Increased pressure within the orbital subarachnoid space has also been hypothesized to be a contributing mechanism for the optic disc oedema observed in space flight associated neuro-ocular syndrome [8]. This elevated optic nerve sheath pressure may result from a rise in ICP due to microgravity-induced cephalad fluid shifts and/or from sequestration of CSF within the subarachnoid space of the optic nerve [8].

Although we know that brain, blood and eye pressures all impact the optic nerve, we lack a comprehensive understanding of how these three pressures affect the health of the optic nerve. For example, studies have shown that acute changes in ICP changes the way that IOP elevation affects the structure of the rat optic nerve and retinal function [1, 9], as measured using optical coherence tomography and electroretinography, respectively. In a range of preclinical models, including cat, dogs and non-human primates, it is known that changes to optic nerve sheath pressure is largely determined by the CSF pressure and is independent of IOP [10–12]. Less is known about this pressure relationship in rats. As such this study examines changes in optic nerve sheath pressure in rats at various ICP levels. Moreover, the CSF flow rate from the rat lateral ventricle to the optic nerve will be quantified.

A number of studies have described inducible rodent models for delivering fluid into the brain for understanding the effect of short- [1, 9, 13] and longer-term brain pressure alterations on optic nerve health [14–17]. Several challenges exist with attempts to chronically deliver fluid into rodent brains models. In particularly long-term and accurate measurement of ICP in awake animals is challenging, often requiring the animals to be tethered to a pressure transduction system [18]. However, improvements in implantable telemetry systems is making chronic ICP monitoring more feasible [19, 20]. These studies demonstrate that like humans [21, 22], rodents show a diurnal rhythm, with ICP being higher in rats during the dark phase [23]. This nocturnal ICP increase arises from an increase in CSF production of approximately 20% in rats [23]. Whether low rate fluid infusion into the lateral ventricle results in changes to diurnal ICP levels in rats is as yet unknown and is investigated in this study.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics

All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the National Health and Medical Research Council Australian Code of Practice for the care and use of animals for scientific purposes. Animal ethics approval was obtained from the Florey Institute Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee (21-017-UM). At the end of experimentation animals were sacrificed using an overdose of anesthesia (pentobarbitone sodium, Lethabarb, 100 mg/kg, Virbac, Milperra NSW, Australia).

Animals

Experiments were undertaken on adult male Long-Evans or Sprague Dawley rats aged 3–6 months. Animals were housed in a 21 °C environment in well-ventilated cages, with ad libitum access to normal rat chow and water. Lighting was regulated on a 12-hour light/dark cycle (50 lx in the cage, on at 8 am). All experiments were conducted under ketamine and xylazine anesthesia (60:5 mg/kg intraperitoneal injection, Troy Laboratory, Glendenning, NSW, Australia). Body temperature was maintained at 37.5 ± 0.5 °C using a circulating water heat-pad. Corneal anesthesia and mydriasis were achieved using drops of 0.5% proxymetacaine hydrochloride (Alcaine, Alcon Laboratories, Sydney, NSW, Australia) and 1% tropicamide (Mydriacyl, Alcon Laboratories), respectively.

Acute blood pressure monitoring

Blood pressure was monitored using a cannula placed in the femoral artery as previously described [24]. Briefly, a heparinized polyethylene cannula was inserted 3 cm proximally into the left femoral artery and secured to the surrounding tissue. The line was connected to a pressure transducer (Transpac, Abbott Critical Care Systems, Sligo, Ireland), whose signal (Bridge Amp ML 110, Amplifier ML 785, Powerlab/8SP, ADInstruments, Colorado Springs, CO, USA) gave direct and continuous BP monitoring (Lab Chart 7, ADInstruments).

Acute intracranial pressure monitoring

ICP was acutely manipulated using cannula placed into the lateral ventricle [1]. For Aim 1, measurement and fluid infusion cannula were placed on the contralateral and ipsilateral side to the imaging eye, respectively. Aim 2 employed a dual-lumen cannula, consisting of a 23G outer (0.6 mm diameter x 19 mm length, Becton Dickinson, Franklin, WI, USA) and a 30G (0.3 mm diameter x 13 mm length, Becton Dickinson) inner needle, which were connected via polyethylene tubing (0.8 mm) to a pressure transducer (Transpac, Abbott Critical Care System) and a syringe pump (Pump 11 Elite Syringe Pumps, Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA, USA), respectively. This allowed for simultaneous ICP manipulation (Bridge Amp ML 110, Amplifier ML 785, Powerlab/8SP, ADInstruments) and recording (Lab Chart 7, ADInstruments). To prepare for lateral ventricle cannulation, rats were anesthetized and placed on a stereotaxic platform (Model 900, David Kopf Instruments, Los Angeles, CA, USA). A 2 cm by 2 cm flap of skin above the skull was removed. Connective tissue around the calvarial area was removed to expose the coronal sutures. Using a dental burr attached to a drill (Model 300, Dremel®, Robert Bosch Tool Corporation, Racine, WI, USA), a hole was drilled through the skull at 1.5 mm caudal to bregma and 2 mm lateral to the midline. The cannula was then inserted to a depth of 3.5 mm into the rat lateral ventricle [25].

Experimental protocols

CSF flow rate in rat optic nerves

Experimental setup

Anesthetized adult rats underwent surgery to implant a cannula into the femoral artery for BP monitoring (as described above). Next, we exposed the optic nerve behind the right eye by removing the conjunctiva, cutting through the lateral rectus muscle, and removing enough orbital fat to allow visualization of the optic nerve. A small speculum was used to hold the eyelids open during imaging.

We then inserted a cannula (23G) into the lateral ventricle on both sides of the brain. The side contralateral to the imaging eye was connected to a pressure sensor for monitoring ICP (as above). The side ipsilateral to the optic nerve being imaged was connected to two syringe pumps via a 3-way valve (Fig. 1A). One syringe pump contained normal saline (0.9% Baxter, Deerfield, IL, United States), the infusion rate of which allowed us to control the ICP level. The other syringe pump contained 1% NaFl (Fluorescite 10%, Alcon, Geneva, Switzerland) in saline. Once the flow rate had been adjusted to the target ICP, the 3-way valve was switched to the NaFl containing pump, which now delivered NaFl at same flow rate. To ensure that the delivery time of fluorescein from the lateral ventricle to the optic nerve was comparable between animals, the duration of NaFl pumping was calculated such that for all animals 100 µl of fluorescein was delivered. Once 100 µl NaFl had been delivered, the 3-way valve was switched back to saline at the same rate. This approach ensured that there was no interruption or change in fluid flow into the lateral ventricle and thus throughout experimentation the ICP was maintained at the target level for a maximum of 60 min. Shorter monitoring durations were needed for faster flow rate (i.e. higher ICP).

Imaging

In vivo confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscopy (Spectralis OCT2, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) fluorescence imaging of the exposed optic nerve was undertaken every 15 to 30 s to monitor change in fluorescence at the optic nerve. Images were acquired at a fixed gain to ensure that signals could be clear identified.

Data analysis

Images were exported in .TIFF format and combined into a stack using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). To account for any movement during the procedure images were registered to the baseline image using the StackReg plugin (ImageJ). Once registered, a reflectance image was used as a reference to identify the position of the optic nerve, and to draw a region of interest (ROI) encompassing the optic nerve. The average brightness of the ROI was plotted as a function of time after the onset of NaFl infusion (Fig. 1B). Brightness profiles were fit with a saturating Hill plot, where brightness (B) is given by a baseline brightness (bbase), maximum brightness (bmax) as a function of time (t, min), a time to reach 50% brightness (t50, min) and a slope parameter. From this the time taken for brightness to exceed 10% baseline was calculated. This function was fit by minimizing the sum of squares error term using the Levenberg Marquart algorithm in the solver module (Excel, Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA).

Relationship between ICP and ONSp in rats

Experimental setup

Anesthetized adult rats underwent surgery to implant a cannula into the femoral artery for BP monitoring (as described above). A second cannula was inserted into the left femoral vein, this allowed for infusion of Angiotensin 2 to increase BP [24]. To consider the additional influence of BP, experiments were also undertaken at either normal BP (group average, 95.6 ± 5.9 mmHg) or high BP (158.0 ± 9.5 mmHg). Specifically, angiotensin II (Ang-II, 10 mmol/L in 0.9% saline, Auspep, Parkville, Australia), was infused at a rate of between 45 and 90 mg/kg/min to elevate BP to approximately 150 mmHg. Once BP had stabilized at normal or high levels, then lateral ventricle fluid infusion began.

As above, a dual lumen cannula was inserted into the ipsilateral lateral ventricle. As per Aim 1, the optic nerve behind the right eye was exposed and a 33G glass micropipette was inserted at a shallow angle (5-10o) into the space between the optic nerve and sheath. As it was not possible to visualise this space a number of approaches were used to confirm correct placement of the measurement needle. First, during insertion a positive pressure of 5 mmHg was maintained to minimise the chance of tissue blockage of the needle. Once in place a small amount of fluid was flushed (25–50 µL) into the subarachnoid space to ensure (i) cannula patency, (ii) absence of fluid leakage, (iii) a small but clear distension of the optic nerve sheath and (iv) a clear increase in pressure. Once correct placement was confirmed, the ONSp was allowed to return to baseline for at least 10 min prior to starting the protocol.

After allowing 10 min for BP and ICP stabilisation, fluid infusion into the lateral ventricle was initiated during which time ICP and ONSp were monitored continuously (Fig. 1C). The infusion rate was adjusted to produce moderate (~ 15 mmHg) and high (~ 25 mmHg) ICP to consider what effect these ICP levels had on ONSp.

Data analysis

Pressure traces were exported (Excel) and plotted as a function of time (Fig. 1C) and fit using Eq. 1, where pressure (P, mmHg) as a function of time (t, min) is given the baseline pressure (Pbase, mmHg), maximum pressure (Pmax, mmHg), the time to reach 50% of the peak pressure (t50, min) and a slope constant (n). From the function we can derive parameters for the latency for pressure increase (EC10) in both lateral ventricle and optic nerve tissue, as well as the time to reach peak pressure (EC90) (Fig. 1C).

Effect of chronic lateral ventricle fluid infusion on diurnal ICP in rats

In 4 adult anesthetized rats, to measure ICP chronically we implanted a sensor-tipped solid-state epidural catheter (Kaha Sciences Ltd, Auckland, New Zealand) connected subcutaneously to a telemetric transmitter placed in the abdominal cavity.

Telemeters were calibrated and tested for offset by immersing the telemeter into 37–38 °C sterile normal saline for at least 48 h. The telemeter was covered to avoid light exposure, thus mimicking the implantation environment. The offset was calibrated using a linear function (1 V = 0 mmHg, 2 V = 100 mmHg). Prior to implantation, the telemeter was turned on and allowed to stabilization for at least 4 h. The offset was rechecked after 4 h and immediately before implantation.

An antibiotic (10 mg/kg, Baytril, enrofloxacin, Bayer, Pymble, NSW, Australia) and analgesic (Meloxicam, 3 mg/kg, Troy Laboratories, Glendenning, NSW, Australia), were given prior to surgery subcutaneously and intraperitoneally, respectively. The scalp and abdomen were shaven and cleaned using povidone-iodine (Avrio Health, Stamford, CT). Body temperature was maintained at 37 °C throughout surgery.

A 3 cm midline incision was made in the lower abdomen and the telemeter body was placed in the abdominal cavity and sutured to the abdominal wall muscle. The muscle layer was sutured (5/0 suture, non-absorbable) leaving the catheter outside the muscle. The catheter with its sensor tip was then tunnelled through the subcutaneous space with a trocar to the base of the skull. The abdominal skin incision was closed using 5/0 suture.

Rats were then placed on a stereotaxic platform (Model 900, David Kopf Instruments, Los Angeles, CA, USA). A dorsal midline incision of the scalp was made and a 2 cm by 2 cm flap of skin above the skull was removed. Connective tissue around the calvarial area was removed to expose the coronal sutures. For the placement of the sensor tip, a dental burr attached to a drill (Model 300, Dremel®, Robert Bosch Tool Corporation, Racine, WI, USA), was used to drill a 1.3 mm hole on the right side of the skull, 3 mm posterior from bregma and 3 mm from midline. At the posterior edge of the hole a 1.3 mm wide channel, approximately 2 mm in length was made to allow the sensor wire to sit flat with the skull. The sensor tip was then inserted at a flat angle to lie epidurally (between the skull and dura mater). Gel Foam (Spongostan Dental, McPharland Medical, Surrey Hills, VIC, Australia) was gently inserted around the cable at the catheter insertion point. The insertion point was covered with a 2 × 2 cm mesh patch (Cytoplast, Osseodent, Miami. FL, USA) and secured using tissue adhesive (Histoacryl, B. Braun, Germany).

Two additional (0.6 mm diameter) holes for anchoring screws were drilled, the first 1 mm anterior to bregma and the other 6 mm posterior from bregma and 1 mm from midline on the left side. Two anchoring 0.6 (width) x 0.4 mm (depth) dental screws were anchored into the skull, with care taken to avoid penetrating the dura. Dental impression material (Silaform lab putty, Moorabbin, Vic, Australia) was applied around the anchoring screws and over the sensor and wire, after which the scalp incision was sutured (5/0 suture).

The fluid infusion was achieved using Alzet Pump Brain Infusion system (Durect Corporation, Cupertino, CA, USA). Prior to implantation, the pre-loaded Osmotic pump (2ML4 or 2ML1, Alzet) was soaked in 37 °C sterile normal saline overnight to absorb the fluid. On the skull contralateral to the implanted subdural pressure sensor, a smaller hole (0.36 mm) was drilled at 3 mm posterior to bregma and 2 mm away from midline. The infusion cannula (Alzet Brain Infusion Kit 1, Alzet) was carefully inserted into the left ventricle (to 3 mm depth) with the assistance of a removable flange on the cannula. No bleeding was noted. After securing the infusion cannula on the skull using tissue glue, the flange was sniped using surgical pliers. The pre-loaded Osmotic pump (2ML4 or 2ML1, Alzet) was connected to the infusion kit via polyvinylchloride tubing (Length 80 mm, diameter 1.14 mm). A sub-cutaneous pocket at the back of the rat was made to store the pump.

Rats were given warmed fluid subcutaneously, placed on a warming pad and monitored until fully recovery. After surgery and for the next two days, rats received 2–4 ml saline 10 mg/kg enrofloxacin (Baytril, Bayer) subcutaneously and Meloxicam (3 mg/kg) intraperitoneally.

Animals were placed in a quiet room, with lighting controlled on a 12 h light dark cycle (50 lx) with lights on at 8 am. Recordings were made continuously for 5 weeks at a frequency of 400 Hz. ICP readings were average for each hour of 24-hour cycles. Data for all 7 days was averaged to return the average for each animal for each week.

Statistics

In addition to individual values, data are shown as mean (± SEM). Data was assessed for normally distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov normality tests) across conditions and compared using a one- or two-way ANOVA (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

CSF flow rate in rat optic nerves

It was possible to image and quantify NaFl transit time from the lateral ventricle to the optic nerve (Fig. 2). In the case of a high flow rate (0.33 ml/min, Fig. 2A and B) increased fluorescence could be seen at the optic nerve after 4 min, with a maximal change in brightness after ~ 7 min. In contrast, a lower flow rate (0.002 ml/min, Fig. 2C and D) did not show a robust increase in fluorescence until after 35 min. Even after 45 min the peak fluorescence had not been reached. Also note that the absolute change in fluorescence was lower for the slow flow rate. Potentially with slow infusion rates, NaFl had greater time to be sequestered by binding to albumin [26] thus resulting in a smaller increase in fluorescence.

Relationship between ICP and ONSp in rats

Next, we considered how changes in ICP affect ONSp, by measuring the two simultaneously in the same animal. Figure 4A shows that increasing ICP results in increased ONSp after a latency. Figure 4B and C show that ONSp latency or peak time trails ICP by 2 to 6 min. Figure 4B shows that there was a significant longer (~ 50 s) latency for ONSp to increase (mixed effects, F(1,5) = 7.33, p = 0.04). Whilst there was a trend for higher BP to reduce latency, this was not significant (F(1,5) = 2.33, p = 0.19). Similarly, there was no effect of BP on the time taken for ICP or ONSp to reach peak pressure (F(1, 13) = 2.0, P = 0.17).

Effect of chronic lateral ventricle fluid infusion on diurnal ICP in rats

Next, we considered the effect of chronic ICV fluid infusion on ICP. Two flow rates were chosen based on the effect of flow rate on ICP (Fig. 3C); 2.5 and 10 µl/hr (4.17*10− 5 and 1.7*10− 4 ml/min). Both of these flow rates should produce a minimal increase in ICP as predicted by Fig. 3C. Fluid was delivered via an osmotic minipump into the lateral ventricle at 2.5 µl/hr for 4 weeks, after which the pump was swapped for one delivering fluid at 10 µl/hr.

Figure 6 shows the range of ICP responses to fluid infusion in each of the 4 rats across the 5 weeks of sterile saline infusion. Animal 1 was the most stable showing a peak ICP during the nocturnal phase, particularly between 2 and 7 am before light onset at 8 am. ICP was lowest during the daylight hours. ICP levels were stable across the 5 weeks. ICP in animal 2 was lowest in week 1 and consistently higher for the remaining weeks, again with a clear diurnal relationship. Animal 3 had higher ICP to start with. However, all weeks show a clear diurnal cycle. Furthermore, ICP was higher in weeks 3, 4 and 5 compared with weeks 1 and 2. Animal 4 follows similar trends to Animals 1 and 2. However its ICP did not increase over the first 4 weeks, and indeed was highest in week 1.

Figure 7 combines data across the 4 animals. Figure 7A shows that compared with weeks 1 and 2, subsequent weeks tended to show higher mean ICP. Discussion

CSF is expected to flow from the cranial subarachnoid space to the optic nerve subarachnoid space, down a volume gradient [27]. The optic nerve subarachnoid space ends blind behind the globe in a cul-de-sac fashion [27]. The exact mechanism by which CSF is reabsorbed out of the optic nerve subarachnoid space is incompletely understood [27]. It is unlikely that the CSF can change its direction of flow from the optic nerve subarachnoid space towards the intracranial subarachnoid space, given the large CSF-volume gradient between both subarachnoid spaces [27]. In addition to arachnoid villi in the meninges of the optic nerve [28], lymphatics in the dura mater of the human optic nerve have been proposed as a possible outflow pathway for CSF from the optic nerve subarachnoid space [27, 29]. Additionally, in 2016, Wostyn et al. [30] demonstrated the existence of a perivascular pathway within the human optic nerve following post-mortem administration of India ink into the optic nerve subarachnoid space. Since then, several studies have presented evidence suggesting CSF entry into the optic nerve via a glymphatic pathway [31–33].

Here we show using fluorescence imaging that CSF flows robustly from the lateral ventricle to the optic nerve in rats. This approach may prove to be a useful way for in vivo assessment of CSF flow rate in a range of optic nerve conditions. Currently, the approach in preclinical models is to use a tracer and to image the fluorescent traces in the post-mortem using microscopy [34, 35]. Alternatively, CSF production is imaged using sequential computed tomography scanning which does not have the resolution for CSF flow at the optic nerve [36]. More recently, contrast enhanced magnetic resonance imaging approaches have been used to document fluid flow [37]. However, as yet such approaches are not able to quantify outflow.

Here we have defined the relationship between ICV flow rate and ICP for adult rats. We show that adjusting flow rate produces predictable changes in ICP and the time taken (latency) for CSF to reach the rat optic nerve. The relationship between flow rate and ICP also helps us indirectly determine the basal flow rate. In particular, the flow rate that did not lead to an increase ICP (i.e. ICP < 10 mmHg) was ~ 2 ml/min. This value is consistent with experimentally measured flow rates for adult rats which ranged from 2.66 to 3.74 ml/min, in young and older animals [38, 39]. For brain volumes ranging from 204 to 275 ml, this represents total turnover of CSF volume over approximately 60–100 min. This provides a guide as to infusion rates that could be used for chronic experiments aimed at increasing CSF turnover, without impacting ICP. For example, to double CSF turnover one would require flow rates 4 to 7 ml/min, which should not lead to an acute elevation of ICP (Fig. 3).

Consistent with a strong relationship between the ONSp and ICP, we show that increasing ICP results in an increase in ONSp after a short delay ~ 1 min. Indeed, ICP is dampened at the peripheral location, being 73 ± 3% lower at the optic nerve sheath compared with the lateral ventricle as given by the equation ONSp = 0.71*ICP-5.69. This function provides a means by which changes in ICP can be equated to a change in the optic nerve head pressure gradient in rats. Previous work in a canine model point to a more 1:1 relationship between ICP and ONSp [40]. The much more substantive dampening effect between the ICP and ONSp seen in this study may have arisen from several sources. Firstly, with fluid infusion and higher ICP, some of the pressure may have been taken up by compensatory mechanism, including distension of the ventricles, reduced CSF production and distribution throughout the CSF network. Additionally CSF drainage via the choroidal plexus and through the blood brain barrier [41] as well as the lymphatic system known to be at the optic nerve [42] will also increase with higher ICP [43]. These mechanisms might account for the pressure dampening that we see in the rat optic nerve.

Diurnal changes in ICP in conscious rats

We also show that implanting the solid-state telemetry system for chronic ICP monitoring is highly feasible, as has been reported previously [44]. The system is well tolerated by rats and produced stable recordings over the course of the 5 weeks. In all animals there was clear evidence of a diurnal pattern to ICP; with high nocturnal pressures compared with daylight hours, consistent with the behaviour of rodent. The peak ICP often occurred before 4–7 AM, that is some 1–4 h before lights on. Our data show that the average ICP was 4–10 mmHg consistent with previous studies [16, 19, 44–50]. Our diurnal difference in ICP was on the order of 1 mmHg, which is somewhat higher than that observed by Eftekhari et al. [20] and Guild et al. [49]who reported a diurnal difference of ~ 0.3 mmHg. Lin and Liu [50] observed very little diurnal difference although the variance was higher with fluid filled sensors, as compared with the solid state sensors used in this study and those of others [20, 49].

Effect of chronic low volume fluid infusion on ICP in awake rats

Whilst our study did not specifically set out to increase ICP through chronic fluid infusion, it is relevant that there have been previous attempts to modulate ICP via fluid infusion, most of which have largely been acute [1, 9]. In the case of chronic infusion studies the animal has been tethered to a fluid reservoir [18]. Here we show that even very low-rates of continuous fluid infusion using an osmotic minipump can change ICP.

When considering the mean ICP, there was a small increase in weeks 2, 3 and 4 compared with week 1 (Fig. 8A). For these weeks there was an increase in peak ICP (Fig. 8C), as wider range of ICPs (Fig. 8E) and increased variability when data was considered as the standard deviation (Fig. 8B). Of these, the parameter most sensitive was the ICP range which was statistically significant. That ICP range increased after weeks 3 and 4, suggest that there was some lag prior to the onset of appreciable change in ICP. It is also worth noting that we do not have true baseline ICP without the pumps in place. However, based on comparisons with other studies [16, 19, 44–50] we believe that the range of ICPs observed in week 1 are likely to be reflective of baseline levels.

Given that the infusion rate during week 5 was 4 times higher than the preceding weeks, it was of interest that ICP in week 5 was not higher than weeks 3 and 4. It may be that following the implantation of the second pump there is a lag, such that one might only see more substantive changes in ICP after further weeks, as was the case after implantation of the first pump. Infusion rates of greater than 2.5 ul/hr, make chronic experiments, with this pump, of 4 or more weeks impractical. Studies seeking to model increase ICP will need higher infusion rates, particularly when one takes into account the compensatory mechanisms discussed above.

This study has several limitations. The first is that every attempt was made to ensure that the cannular was placed into the optic nerve sheath. However, it may be that the narrowness of this space could have partially blocked the needle. It may be possible to approach this measurement challenging using pressure sensors. The sample size for the chronic experiment was small. Despite this we were able to show significant differences in some ICP parameters.

Conclusions

We show that changes in pressure in the rat brain directly change the pressure in the optic nerve tissue in a predictable manner. We have defined acute infusion rates that produce increases in ICP in rats. We show that indwelling solid state sensors provide excellent measures of ICP in awake rats over an extended period of 5 weeks. We have established clear parameters from our continuous ICP recording protocols that define a robust diurnal cycle and that are useful for quantifying changes in ICP arising from fluid infusion into the rat lateral ventricle. We show that an infusion rate of 2.5 ul/hr increases CSF turnover rate without negatively impacting on the animal.
