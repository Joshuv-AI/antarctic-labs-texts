Seismic mapping of the central and southern segments of the Tanlu fault zone using P-wave receiver functions
Authors: Qian Liu, Ziqiang Lü, Jianshe Lei
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The central and southern segments of the Tanlu fault zone, located in the collision boundary between the Yangtze Plate and the North China Craton, underwent complex tectonic deformation associated with the Pacific Plate subduction. The crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio are important parameters for comprehending tectonic evolution and geodynamic processes. By integrating a newly dense seismic array, our results based on P-wave receiver function analyses reveal the crust thickness varies significantly across the Tanlu fault zone. The Yangtze Plate is characterized by a thinner crust, contrasting a thicker crust imaged in the North China Craton, indicating the Tanlu fault zone is a prominent structural block boundary. The Vp/Vs ratio within the Tanlu fault zone is visibly higher than its surroundings, probably correlating with abundant fluids activity along the fault zone, as a result of the subduction of the Pacific Plate. Additionally, we observe a gradually deepening Moho discontinuity beneath the Sulu orogenic belt with a low-angle dip, presenting direct seismic evidence for supporting the ancient Yangtze Plate underthrusting beneath the Sulu orogenic belt.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73008-4.

Introduction

The Tanlu fault zone (TLFZ), is the largest NNE-SSW-trending strike-slip fault system with a total length of over 2400 km in eastern China, extending from the Yangtze Plate (YZP) in the south through the North China Craton (NCC) to the Northeast China in the north1–3. Based on the tectonic evolution and different geological characteristics, the TLFZ is segmented into the southern, central, and northern sections, respectively3–5. The central and southern sections of the TLFZ are composed of several tectonic blocks, including the Luxi uplift to the northwest, the Xuhuai thrust fold belt and the Jiashan graben to the southwest, the Sulu orogenic belt to the northeast, and the Subei basin to the southeast (Fig. 1). The frequent earthquake activities can be attributed to the presence of widespread active faults. One notable historical earthquake was the Tancheng earthquake in 1668 (M 8.5), which caused many casualties and a large number of property losses6. Increasing numbers of studies on the deep crustal and upper mantle structures around the central and southern sections of the TLFZ have been performed using various geophysical methods in order to reconstruct its formation, evolution, and geodynamic mechanism. The P-wave upper-mantle tomography showed magma upwelling beneath the central and southern sections of the TLFZ, which is probably closely linked to the subduction of the western Pacific Plate8. Ambient noise tomographic study revealed a high-velocity zone extending from the lower to middle crust along the central and southern sections of the TLFZ9,10. Recent full-wave ambient noise tomography study revealed obvious lateral shear-wave velocity variations of the crust and uppermost mantle across the central and southern sections of the TLFZ, with high velocity dominating in the east and low velocity dominating in the west5. The Pn-wave velocity structure near the Tanlu central segment revealed the low-velocity anomaly was primarily situated in the uplifted region, implying a weakened lithosphere, conversely, the high-velocity anomaly was primarily located in the depression region, indicating a strong lithosphere11–13. Miao et al.1 observed crustal anisotropy in the TLFZ has a fast propagation direction parallel to the fault, which may be the result of the alignment of microcracks and foliated minerals induced by the prolonged shear motion within the fault zone.

The receiver function is a highly efficient method for investigating the variations of the velocity discontinuities14–16. Previous studies have applied the receiver function method to reveal velocity discontinuity variation in different scales. The study of the mantle transition zone found the deeper 660-km velocity discontinuity beneath the TLFZ, which may be related to the western Pacific subduction stagnation17. Lithospheric structural imaging showed dramatically thinned lithosphere and the thinnest crust thickness beneath the TLFZ18, indicating the TLFZ may be a main channel for asthenosphere upwelling due to the lithospheric thinning and continental extension19. Wei et al.20 detected the uplifted Moho, high Vp/Vs ratio, and vertically alternating high- and low-velocity layers beneath the TLFZ through a combined analysis of multi-frequency receiver function and surface waves, linking to the generation of strong earthquakes. The crustal thickness showed strong heterogeneity in different regions of the central and southern sections of the TLFZ, such as the thick crustal thickness in the Luxi uplift compared with the thin crustal thickness in the Subei basin21. However, these studies of crustal structure were limited to the permanent or linear seismic stations, resulting in relatively low resolution or only two-dimensional structures. The details of the deep crustal structure and regional differences in the central and southern sections of the TLFZ and surroundings are still insufficient.

In this study, a newly dense seismic array (TanluArray) deployed around the central and southern sections of the TLFZ (Fig. 1b) enables us to construct a new high-resolution crustal structure applying the P-wave receiver functions. Our results reveal a strong crustal thickness contrast across the TLFZ associated with past tectonic activities between the NCC and YZP. By integrating various geophysical datasets, we conduct a comprehensive analysis of the geometry of the Moho around the central and southern sections of the TLFZ. Our findings provide new constraints on the deformation and dynamic process of the TLFZ and its adjacent regions.

In order to more clearly describe variations of crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio in the study region, we calculate the crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio through H-κ stacking (Fig. 4). The crustal thickness varies in the range of 27–37 km, with an average value of 32 km, which is roughly related with the surface tectonic units. The thicker crust (33–37 km) is observed beneath south of the Jiashan graben, Xuhuai thrust fold belt, Sulu orogenic belt, and the Luxi uplift, whereas a thinner crust (27–32 km) is observed beneath the Subei basin. The higher Vp/Vs ratio is mainly distributed within the TLFZ compared to the lower Vp/Vs ratio observed in areas outside the fault zone. Figure S2 illustrates the statistical distributions of the Vp/Vs ratios for the NCC, TLFZ, and YZP. The pattern of Vp/Vs ratios demonstrates noticeable differences, with the TLFZ showing a larger Vp/Vs ratio (1.79 ± 0.056), while the region of the NCC (1.74 ± 0.041) and YZP (1.70 ± 0.029) have a smaller Vp/Vs ratio. The lateral variations of the Moho topography around the TLFZ are constructed by the common conversion point (CCP) stacking of receiver functions (Fig. 5). The W1-E1 and W2-E2 profiles are roughly perpendicular to the TLFZ revealing a distinct Moho variation across the fault with an offset of ~ 4 km. A deeper Moho (~ 34 m) is observed in the NCC to the west of the fault, while it becomes shallower (~ 30 m) in the YZP to the east of the fault (Fig. 5a and b). Furthermore, we observe strong lateral variations in Moho morphology along the TLFZ (Fig. 5c and d), which correlates well with major tectonic segments across the profiles. Small fluctuations of the Moho are detected beneath the southern NCC, and the Moho beneath the Luxi uplift is relatively flat at ~ 34 km depth (Fig. 5c). Discussion

It is widely acknowledged that the central and southern sections of the TLFZ experienced multiple tectonic evolutions linked to the effects of the Pacific Plate subduction. Detailed variations of the Vp/Vs ratio and Moho depth are significant for understanding the evolution process, deformation, and crustal material composition of the central and southern sections of the TLFZ. Based on the new dense seismic array, we observe notable lateral crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio contrasts crossing the central and southern sections of the TLFZ, which provides significant insights into the evolutionary dynamics of the TLFZ.

Compared with the previous receiver function result22 (Fig. S3), our results demonstrate a prominent variation in crustal thickness beneath different geological blocks. Li et al.22 revealed a thick crust beneath the Luxi uplift and a thin crust beneath the Subei basin only using spare (~ 150 km interstation spacing) stations, which is consistent with our results. However, our results also reveal some new features compared with the previous study. Li et al.22 observed a thin crust beneath the Sulu orogenic belt, the south of Xuhuai thrust fold belt, and the Jiashan graben, contrasting with a thick crust revealed in these regions by our results. Generally, our new results present a good correlation between the geological structure and crustal thickness distribution due to the dense (~ 30 km interstation spacing) coverage of seismic stations.

Our results exhibit a significant crustal thickness variation across the TLFZ, implying different tectonic evolution in the NCC and YZP. The correlation between the Moho depth variation and the corresponding variation in Bouguer gravity anomalies suggests an inhomogeneous density distribution across the fault23 (Figs. 4 and 6a). The magnetotelluric array study revealed a high resistance in the YZP24,25 (Fig. 6b), which mainly also corresponds to the observed thinner crust in our results (Figs. 4a and 5). The recent shear-wave velocity structures suggested a strong lateral velocity contrast across the TLFZ near the Moho depth, with a high-velocity anomaly in the YZP and low-velocity in the NCC5 (Fig. 6c), corresponding to our thin and thick crust (Figs. 4a and 5), respectively. The Vp/Vs ratio provides additional constraints on the mineralogical composition of the crust. The intensification of rock melting or fluids can result in a higher Vp/Vs ratio, which is attributed to a more rapid decline in shear wave velocity compared to compressional wave velocity26. Additionally, the Vp/Vs ratio can be categorized into three ranges: values less than 1.76 for felsic rocks, intermediate ratio for intermediate rocks, and ratio exceeding 1.81 for mafic rocks27. Our results show the estimated Vp/Vs ratios exceeding 1.81 are mainly distributed within the TLFZ and beneath the Sulu orogenic belt. However, the forming mechanism may be different in two regions (Fig. 7). Within the TLFZ, the high Vp/Vs ratios may be related to basic material distributions along the TLFZ, which mainly derived from asthenospheric upwelling as a result of subduction of the Pacific Plate8,28, suggesting that the lithospheric modification may have extended up to the crust along the TLFZ. The dramatically thinned lithosphere and obviously uplifted asthenosphere beneath the TLFZ also indicate the fault as a lithospheric channel for thermal upwelling19,29,30. Similar high Vp/Vs ratios have been observed in major strike-slip fault zones, such as the San Andreas Fault and the Northern Anatolian Fault31,32. The higher Vp/Vs ratios are often associated with the presence of fluids within fault zones. Moreover, the high Vp/Vs ratios beneath the Sulu orogenic belt may indicate the presence of a mafic-rich crust. This result may be related to the exhumation of abundant high-pressure and ultra-high-pressure metamorphic rocks due to the YZP subducted into the Sulu orogenic belt33,34. The formation of the TFLZ is widely attributed to the convergence of the NCC and the YZP during the Middle Triassic, following the closure of a Paleo-Tethy Ocean37. The dating results indicated that the initial subduction-related prograde high-pression to ultra-high-pression metamorphism took place between 257 and 242 Ma at the Dabie suture and between 247 and 244 Ma at the Sulu suture. This suggests that the onset of the YZP subduction commenced approximately 10 Ma earlier at the Dabie suture compared to the Sulu suture38. From the late Triassic to the early Cretaceous, two significant left-lateral strike-slip movements occurred in the TLFZ, which is attributed to the subduction of the Paleo-Pacific Plate3. Since the Late Cretaceous to the present, the TLFZ has been primarily influenced by the destruction and lithospheric thinning of the NCC as a result of the stagnant Pacific slab in the mantle transition zone839–41. Geochemical studies also revealed that intense crustal movements of the NCC during the Late Mesozoic to Cenozoic periods led to widespread volcanism and magmatic intrusion42,43, in response to the subduction of the Pacific Plate. Beneath the NCC, our observed thick crust, together with low-resistance and low-velocity anomalies may imply the existence of mantle upwelling, which caused a complex crust-mantle interaction during the NCC lithospheric modification stage in this region44. Meanwhile, compared with the YZP plate, the complex crust-mantle interaction may result in a heterogeneous component of the crust with relatively higher Vp/Vs ratios beneath the NCC. Alternately, the thin crust with high-resistance and high-velocity anomalies (Fig. 6b and c) revealed beneath the YZP may represent the remains of the ancient lithospheric mantle, which is in accord with the low Vp/Vs ratios (Fig. 4b), indicating the crust belongs to felsic components as a stable rigid block. This suggestion is also supported by previous geological, geophysical, and geochemical studies5,45,46. Interestingly, the observed high Vp/Vs ratios along the TLFZ offer additional evidence for an effective thermal upwelling channel, which may be related to extensive magmatic intrusion and volcanism resulting from the subduction of the western Pacific Plate8,41. The comparative analysis of the multidisciplinary studies provides more constraints on better understanding the tectonic evolution of the TLFZ.

Conclusion

In this study, we estimate the crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio across the central and southern sections of the TLFZ based on a newly dense seismic array. Our results reveal a thinner crust in the YZP and a thicker crust in the NCC, respectively, together with the differences in gravity, resistivity, and velocity anomalies, which may reflect different tectonic evolution in the two blocks. The Vp/Vs ratios in different blocks show obvious contrasts in the study region. The high Vp/Vs ratios within the TLFZ may be a result of the basic material upwelling along the fault zone, and the other high Vp/Vs ratios observed in the Sulu orogenic belt may be related to the existence of numerous high-pressure and ultra-high-pressure metamorphic rocks. Moreover, the Moho discontinuity gradually deepens beneath the Sulu orogenic belt, implying that the YZP has subducted beneath the Sulu orogenic belt. Our new findings provide fresh insights into the deformation of the TLFZ and the evolution of the NCC and YZP.

Data and methods

In this study, we collect teleseismic data recorded by 92 broadband seismic stations between July 2020 and August 2021, containing 25 permanent seismic stations from the Chinese Provincial Seismic Network (CPSN) and 67 temporary seismic stations from a newly deployed dense (~ 30 km interstation spacing) seismic array (TanluArray). Permanent stations are mainly distributed in the northern part of the study region, combining the temporary broadband dense seismic stations in the southern part of the study region, we can obtain more high-resolution crustal structural features.

The temporary seismic stations provide three-component broadband seismic data with a 40 Hz sampling rate. According to the earthquake catalog from the USGS, we select a total of 325 high-quality teleseismic events with a reasonable azimuthal coverage for receiver functions. All these events with a magnitude greater than 5.5 are distributed between 30° and 95° in epicenter distance (Fig. S4).

The P-wave receiver function is derived through a deconvolution algorithm, involving the deconvolution of the vertical component with the radial component47,48. We remove the instrument responses, linear trends, and mean values of the raw data, resample to 20 Hz, and perform band-pass filtering to 0.05–3 Hz. Based on the IASP91 velocity model49, we determine the delay times of the Ps conversions after the direct P-waves. The vertical-north-east (ZNE) components are converted into the vertical-radial-transverse (ZRT) components based on the azimuth distribution. The time-domain iterative deconvolution method is used to calculate the receiver function50. For each station, we cut all traces within a time window of 20 s before and 120 s after the theoretical direct P arrival times with a Gaussian parameter of 5, respectively. To ensure the quality of the receiver functions, we effectively eliminate data records with a signal-to-noise ratio of less than 2 and set a maximum number of 300 iterations with an iteration error threshold of 0.001. Finally, we select 7170 receiver functions for imaging the crustal structure beneath the central and southern sections of the TLFZ.

The optimal crustal thickness and Vp/Vs ratio can be determined using the H-κ stacking algorithm, achieved through coherent stacking of Ps, PpPs, and PpSs + PsPs phases originating from the Moho discontinuity51. In this study, we use the radial component receiver functions at the individual station for the H-κ stacking algorithm, and assign the weighting factors of 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2 to Ps, PpPs, and PpSs + PsPs phases of the Moho discontinuity based on the relative amplitudes of Ps converted phases, respectively. The average P-wave velocity is set to be 6.3 km/s to estimate the crust-thickness. To improve the computational efficiency, we select the crustal thickness range of 20–50 km and the Vp/Vs ratio range of 1.6–1.9, based on previous empirical values20.

The CCPstacking proves to be a more suitable method for addressing Moho topography when the stations are densely distributed. We perform the CCP imaging to better reveal the geometry of the Moho around the TLFZ in the study region. We first divide the stratum beneath the study region spatially into bins with a certain size and calculate Ps ray paths employing the IASP91 velocity model49,51. Next, all amplitudes at the receiver functions are back-projected along the respective ray paths to a proper depth by applying the back-projection migration techniques. Finally, we sum the amplitudes of all the receiver functions in the same bin to enhance Ps conversion signals and therefore allow a robust picking of the CCP imaging.
