Septin4 Regulates Cardiac Fibrosis After Pressure Overload
Authors: Doğacan Yücel, Natalia Ferreira de Araujo, Fernando Souza-Neto, Calvin Smith, Wei-Han Lin, Andrea A. Torniainen, Mikayla L. Hall, DeWayne Townsend, Brenda M. Ogle, Jop H. van Berlo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:In response to cardiac injury the mammalian heart undergoes ventricular remodeling to maintain cardiac function. These changes are initially considered compensatory, but eventually lead to increased cardiomyocyte apoptosis, reduced cardiac function and fibrosis which are important contributors to the development of heart failure. The small GTPase Sept4 (Septin4) has previously been implicated in the regulation of regeneration and apoptosis in several organs. However, the role of Sept4 in regulating the response of the heart to stress is unknown.METHODS:Ten-week-old wild-type (WT) and Sept4 knockout mice were subjected to transverse aortic constriction to induce cardiac injury. Genotype-dependent differences were investigated at baseline and at 1- and 4-week postinjury time points. To definitively establish the fibroblast-specific cardioprotective effects of Sept4, we generated a fibroblast-specific Sept4 conditional knockout model.RESULTS:Under homeostatic conditions Sept4 knockout mice showed normal cardiac function comparable with WT controls. In response to transverse aortic constriction, WT mice developed reduced cardiac function and heart failure, accompanied by an increase in cardiomyocyte apoptosis. In contrast, knockout mice were protected against injury with maintenance of normal cardiac function and reduced levels of cardiomyocyte apoptosis. Both at baseline and after transverse aortic constriction, knockout hearts exhibited decreased levels of cardiac extracellular matrix deposition and fibrosis compared with WT controls. In support of these data, the level of myofibroblast activation was lower after injury in knockout mice. Furthermore, the knockout group showed higher levels of cardiac compliance and improved diastolic function compared with WT controls. Mechanistically, we identified reduced fibrosis development due to alterations in calcineurin-dependent signaling in fibroblasts. These results were further verified in fibroblast-specific conditional Sept4 knockout mice subjected to cardiac pressure overload.CONCLUSIONS:We identified Sept4 as an important regulator of extracellular matrix remodeling in the heart. Sept4 controls the conversion of fibroblast to myofibroblast through calcineurin-dependent mechanisms.

In response to injury, the heart undergoes remodeling that can lead to heart failure, with fibrosis playing a central role. Fibrotic extracellular matrix is mainly produced by myofibroblasts, which are differentiated from fibroblasts. Here, we identified Sept4 as a novel regulator of this differentiation process. We show that Sept4 is required for the conversion of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts both in vivo and in vitro. Given that Sept4 is also expressed in nonfibroblasts, we generated a cardiac fibroblast-specific Sept4 conditional knockout mouse model, which showed substantially less fibrosis following cardiac pressure overload. Mechanistically, we show that Sept4 contributes expression of calcineurin, a calcium/calmodulin-dependent phosphatase previously implicated in myofibroblast activation. The deletion of Sept4 inhibits calcineurin, thereby reducing the level of myofibroblast activation and fibrosis.

Cardiovascular disease has been the leading cause of death in the United States for decades.1 In response to myocardial infarction, high-blood pressure or valvular disease, the heart undergoes a remodeling process, which is initially considered beneficial for preventing a decline in cardiac function. However, as the disease progresses, these changes become maladaptive and contribute to the development of heart failure.2 As mammalian cardiomyocytes are not capable of dividing effectively, the heart typically responds to a stressor with an increase in cardiomyocyte size.3 In addition to changes in cardiomyocyte size, there are many other adaptations, such as stimulation of angiogenesis, immune cells, and fibrosis.4–6 Recent papers have shown a direct correlation between the development of cardiac fibrosis and outcome.7,8 The diffuse fibrotic tissue that develops as a consequence of increased cardiac stress increases the overall stiffness of the heart.9,10 This change in stiffness hinders cardiomyocyte relaxation and cardiac diastole.11 Several studies have also shown that cardiac fibrosis increases the risk of cardiac arrythmias.12,13 Moreover, cardiac fibrosis is considered irreversible even after cardiac function is restored via conventional therapies.14 Therefore, it is critical to understand the underlying mechanisms that mediate cardiac fibrosis to prevent it at the early stages of disease. The development of cardiac fibrosis is mediated by cardiac fibroblasts (CFBs) that secrete collagen and other extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins in response to growth factors, such as activated TGF (transforming growth factor) β.6 Although TGF-β (transforming growth factor-beta) is arguably the most potent regulator of fibroblast activation, long-term inhibition is not a viable therapeutic approach as this leads to detrimental side effects, such as promoting susceptibility of infection and tumor formation.15

Sept4 (Septin4) is a small GTPase that has previously been identified as one of the downstream mediators of TGF-β induced apoptosis through its splice isoform ARTS (Apoptosis-Related Protein in the TGF-Beta Signaling Pathways),16 although the precise mechanism for TGF-β dependency was not identified. Genetic deletion of Sept4 and ARTS, prevented apoptosis of intestinal and skin stem cells and increased their proliferation, resulting in enhanced regeneration with complete resolution of scarring.17 However, despite being expressed at a higher level than in the small intestine, the role of Sept4 in the heart is unknown. Here, we show that Sept4 deletion alleviates cardiac injury through a reduction in the development of cardiac fibrosis.

METHODS

Data Availability

All data underlying the results provided here are available on reasonable request from the corresponding author. Please see the Supplement Material for the full expanded methods section.

Mouse Work

All animal experiments were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the University of Minnesota. Sept4 knockout mice were acquired from the Steller laboratory. Homozygous knockout female mice were bred to C57Bl/6j wild-type (WT) mice, and the heterozygous offspring was used to establish a breeder colony of WT and Sept4 knockout mice. Since Sept knockout males are infertile, we cross-bred Sept4 heterozygous knockout males with homozygous knockout females.18 We generated loxP targeted Septin4 mice, where exons 2 and 3 were flanked by loxP sites. This was done with the help of the Genome Engineering Shared Resource and the Mouse Genetics Laboratory at the University of Minnesota. Positive mice were obtained, and the resulting Sept4-loxP mice were cross-bred with Tcf21-MerCreMer/+ mice,19 acquired from the Tallquist laboratory at the University of Hawaii, to allow for fibroblast-specific deletion of Sept4. Male mice were subjected to cardiac pressure overload via constriction of the transverse aorta as previously described.20 Mice were randomly assigned to undergo either transverse aortic constriction (TAC) or sham surgery. Mice received buprenorphine SR (Zoopharm) as analgesic before the surgery. Following surgery, mice were allowed to recover, and then were subjected to echocardiography (Vevo2100) under isoflurane anesthesia.

Data Analysis

Sham and TAC surgeries were performed blinded for genotype. Whenever possible, we analyzed data in an automated fashion to avoid observer bias. The data from each experimental group was tested for normal distribution using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For groups that showed a non-normal distribution of data, a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was performed to compare groups. For all other comparisons, 2-tailed t tests were used. For RNA sequencing analysis, corrected P values below 0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed genes. Exact P values are displayed on all figures. If no P value is displayed, there was no significant difference between groups. A P value below 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analyses and data visualizations were done using GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.2) or R (version 4.3.2).

RESULTS

Septin 4 Is Dispensable for Normal Cardiac Function

To determine a role for Sept4 in the heart, we used a previously generated Sept4 homozygous knockout mouse line (knockout). In this genetic mouse model, exons 2 through 12 are replaced by a neomycin resistance gene, resulting in the deletion of all known isoforms of the Sept4 gene.18 To verify cardiac knockout, we assessed Sept4 expression in WT and knockout cardiac tissues. As expected, we failed to detect Sept4 expression at both mRNA and protein levels in knockout mice, although it was readily detected in WT samples (Figure 1A and 1B). Since it is not clear which cell types express Sept4 in the heart, using magnetic sorting, we separated the major cell types from the heart and measured the expression of Sept4 by quantitative polymerase chain reaction. Although we could detect Sept4 mRNA expression in all major cardiac cell types assessed, CFBs (CD31−CD45−) and endothelial cells (ECs, CD31+CD45−) showed higher Sept4 mRNA expression compared with cardiomyocytes and immune cells (CD31−CD45+; Figure 1C). To further assess if Sept4 expression is mainly present in ECs and CFBs, we performed immunostaining on WT cardiac tissues. Sept4 immunostaining colocalized predominantly with CD31 and Vim (vimentin), which were used to label ECs and CFBs, respectively. In contrast, Sept4 was undetected in Troponin-T+ CMs, demonstrating that Sept4 expression in cardiomyocytes is minimal compared with ECs and CFBs (Figure 1D). To further corroborate our observation that Sept4 expression is limited to ECs and CFBs, we analyzed a publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data set of uninjured adult mouse hearts (ArrayExpress ID: E-MTAB-8810) to analyze Sept4 expression levels in different cardiac cell types.21 We detected 12 distinct cell clusters (Figure 1E) and observed Sept4 expression mainly in CFBs, ECs, pericytes and smooth muscle cells with minimal to no expression in CMs and immune cells (Figure 1F).

Septin 4 Regulates ECM Composition and Mechanical Properties of the Heart

To investigate the potential impact of Sept4 deletion on the heart under homeostatic conditions, we performed RNA sequencing on uninjured WT and Sept4 knockout hearts. Knockout hearts showed a substantial decrease in RNA expression of many genes encoding for structural extracellular matrix proteins, such as Col1a1 and Col1a2 (collagen 1 type 1 alpha 1 and 2 chains) or mediators of collagen cross-linking, such as Lox (lysyl oxidase) as well as fibroblast activators, such as Postn (periostin; Figure 2A; Table S1). Moreover, unsupervised gene ontology (GO)-term analysis on differentially expressed genes revealed enriched changes in the RNA expression profile of mainly ECM and collagen related pathways in knockout samples (Figure 2B). Since ECM proteins are mainly produced by CFBs, we assessed if the frequency of CFBs was altered in knockout hearts using flow cytometry (Figure S1A). However, the frequency of CFBs as well as endothelial and immune cells was comparable between WT and knockout hearts, indicating that Sept4 deletion alters ECM gene RNA expression without affecting their overall abundance (Figure S1B). To determine if the observed changes in ECM gene expression would result in altered ECM composition, we decellularized uninjured WT and knockout hearts and performed mass spectrometry analysis on the ECM proteins (Figure 2C). In agreement with the observed reduced RNA expression, the relative molar abundance of multiple collagen isotypes was lower in knockout hearts compared with their WT counterparts (Figure 2C and 2D). To further support the finding that Sept4 deletion alters ECM composition and structure, we imaged decellularized knockout and WT hearts using SEM; Figure 3A). SEM analysis showed decreased ECM density (Figure 3B) as well as reduced ECM fibers in knockout hearts compared with WT hearts (Figure 3C). These results indicate that Sept4 deletion reduces ECM density and alters ECM composition in the heart. Cross-linked collagens provide structural support and stiffness to most organs including the adult heart.22,23 Excessive collagen deposition contributes to the increase in cardiac stiffness after injury.24 To directly assess whether the reduced collagen abundance and cross-linking in the ECM affected the mechanical properties of knockout hearts, we generated stress-strain curves from cardiac tissue compression experiments. These demonstrated a decreased compressive modulus in knockout hearts, indicating enhanced compliant properties, likely attributable to the overall decrease in collagen content (Figure 3D). It was previously established that compliance of the ventricles plays an important role in ventricular filling and overall diastolic function.25,26 Moreover, after injury, stiffness of the ventricles increases whereas compliance reduces, resulting in diastolic dysfunction. To assess if the more compliant knockout hearts also showed improved diastolic function compared with WT controls, we performed invasive hemodynamics on uninjured knockout and WT hearts, which showed lower end diastolic pressure at baseline and reduced preload-independent time constant of isovolumic-relaxation (Tau) in knockout hearts under dobutamine stimulation (Figure 3E and 3F), indicative of more efficient diastolic function. Similar trends were observed for end diastolic pressure and Tau measurements under dobutamine and baseline conditions, respectively (Figure S2A and S2B). The observed changes in diastolic functional parameters did not directly impact cardiac systolic function (Figure S2C and S2D). These results indicate that Sept4 deletion affects ECM deposition, composition, and structure, which results in increased compliance and enhanced diastolic function of the heart.

Septin 4 Knockout Mice Are Protected Against Cardiac Pressure Overload

To assess if the absence of Sept4 could alter the response to cardiac pressure overload, we performed sham or TAC surgeries on WT and knockout mice and first verified equal pressure gradients for WT and knockout mice 1 week after TAC (Figure 4A; Figure S3A and S3B). We measured ejection fraction and fractional shortening and noted a substantial reduction in ejection fraction and fractional shortening in response to TAC in WT mice both at 1 week and 4 weeks postinjury, although knockout mice were able to maintain cardiac function (Figure 4B; Figure S3C). At 1 week after TAC, there was no dilation yet (Figure S3D), but at 4 weeks post-TAC, the ventricular diameter during systole and diastole was increased in response to TAC in WT mice, although knockout mice did not develop cardiac dilation (Figure 4C). We measured lung weight to body weight ratios at 4 weeks after TAC as an indicator of heart failure and observed substantially increased ratios in WT mice after TAC. Knockout mice did not show an increased lung weight/body weight ratio after TAC compared with sham controls, indicating that the deletion of Sept4 confers protection against heart failure (HF) development after cardiac pressure overload (Figure 4D). Notably, none of the measurements thus far in knockout TAC animals were different from the knockout sham animals. Next, we examined the hypertrophic response to cardiac pressure overload. As expected, TAC induced an increase in heart weight to body weight ratios (heart weight/body weight), however, this response was comparable in both WT and knockout mice 1 and 4 weeks after TAC (Figure 4E; Figure S3E and S3F). Cardiomyocyte hypertrophy at the cellular level, measured by the averaged cross-sectional area per cardiomyocyte from histological sections was also similar in both groups 4 weeks post-TAC (Figure 4F and 4G). Taken together, these results indicate that Sept4 stimulates maladaptive ventricular remodeling, cardiac dysfunction, and heart failure after TAC.

Septin 4 Regulates Cardiac Fibrosis

Since we observed reduced ECM deposition in Sept4 knockout mice under homeostatic conditions, we hypothesized that TAC would result in a reduced fibrotic response in knockout animals. To assess the presence of fibrosis, we performed Sirius Red-Fast Green staining on cardiac tissues 4 weeks post-TAC and measured Sirius Red-positive fibrotic areas. Indeed, the level of fibrosis was lower in the knockout hearts both at interstitial (Figure 4G) and perivascular areas (Figure 4H), indicating that Sept4 deletion protects against the development of cardiac fibrosis in response to TAC. These findings were confirmed by Movat Pentachrome staining at 1 week post-TAC, where knockout hearts also showed reduced fibrosis (Figure S3G and S3H). Moreover, when we performed invasive hemodynamic measurements, we again noted that both the end diastolic pressure (Figure 4I) and Tau values (Figure 4J) were improved in the knockout group, indicating that knockout hearts are more compliant than their WT counterparts. We observed a similar trend after dobutamine stimulation (Figure S3I and S3J). Importantly, the slope of the end diastolic pressure volume relation, a measure of ventricular compliance, was higher in the WTs after dobutamine treatment compared with knockout, consistent with improved compliance of knockout hearts after TAC (Figure 4K). Fibrosis could be developed to replace lost cardiomyocytes. Previous studies have shown that the Sept4 splice isoform ARTS stimulates apoptosis in stem cells by catalyzing the degradation of XIAP (X-linked inhibitor of apoptosis).27 To assess if Sept4 might regulate cardiomyocyte cell death, we measured the level of CM apoptosis after cardiac pressure overload. As expected, WT mice showed enhanced CM apoptosis in response to TAC, measured by quantifying the cleaved-caspase 3+ cardiomyocytes, whereas knockout mice showed reduced levels of apoptotic cardiomyocytes compared with WT controls at both 1-week and 4-week post-TAC (Figure S4A through S4D). ARTS regulates apoptosis mainly through the degradation of XIAP.28 To assess whether the reduced number of cleaved-caspase-3+ cardiomyocytes was a direct consequence of the genetic deletion of Sept4, we measured the protein levels of XIAP 4 weeks after TAC. We failed to detect differences in XIAP levels in cardiac lysates after TAC (Figure S4E and S4F), suggesting that the observed difference in apoptosis is an indirect consequence of Sept4 deletion, and likely a reflection of the worsened cardiac remodeling in WT mice.

Septin 4 Regulates Fibroblast Activation In Vivo

Following injury, various paracrine mediators, such as TGF-β, are secreted into the cardiac ECM. These mediators then turn on molecular cascades required for CFBs activation, which causes increased production of ECM-related proteins.29 To determine whether Sept4 deletion prevents CFB activation, we stained cardiac sections 1 week post-TAC using αSMA (α smooth muscle actin) as a myofibroblast activation marker and quantified the fraction of cells costained with the fibroblast marker Vim. Knockout hearts showed reduced levels of αSMA+Vim+ myofibroblasts after TAC compared with WT controls (Figure 5A). We also noted reduced Postn+ staining in the ECM and a trend towards an overall reduction in Postn expression in the knockout hearts after TAC compared with WT hearts (Figure 5B; Figure S5A). These results suggested that the conversion of fibroblasts to myofibroblasts in response to cardiac pressure overload was impaired in the knockout heart. Since TGF-β signaling is involved in myofibroblast activation and the inhibition of TGF-β can prevent fibrosis in various organs,30 we next tested expression and activation of TGF-β signaling in response to cardiac pressure overload. TGFBR1 (transforming growth factor beta receptor 1) protein expression was reduced in the knockout hearts after TAC, although there was no difference between knockout and WT hearts in the sham group (Figure 5C). TAC stimulated SMAD2 (SMAD family member 2) phosphorylation in both WT and knockout hearts, but this response was blunted in the knockout group after TAC, consistent with reduced Tgfbr1 expression and reduced myofibroblast activation (Figure 5D). The amount of total SMAD2 protein trended to be increased in the WT group after TAC compared with knockout hearts (Figure S5B).

Fibroblast-expressed Septin 4 Controls TGF-β-Stimulated Myofibroblast Differentiation

To directly determine if Sept4 expression within CFBs was responsible for the observed reduced myofibroblast presence and TGF-β signaling activation in response to cardiac pressure overload, we isolated CFBs from WT and knockout hearts and measured their response to TGF-β. First, we treated CFBs with TGF-β1, followed by measurement of the amount of soluble collagen produced. Indeed, WT CFBs produced more collagen in response to TGF-β stimulation, whereas knockout CFBs remained unresponsive (Figure 6A). Next, we assessed stimulation of mRNA expression of various profibrotic genes, such as Ctgf (connective tissue growth factor) and Postn, after TGF-β treatment. The expression levels of these genes were comparable between WT and knockout CFBs in unstimulated cells (Figure 6B and 6C). Following TGF-β stimulation, WT CFB showed an abundant increase in expression of Ctgf and Postn, as expected. However, knockout CFBs failed to enhance Ctgf and Postn expression levels in response to TGF-β treatment (Figure 6B and 6C). A similar trend was observed for Col1a1 expression (Figure S5C). Next, we assessed stimulation of myofibroblast activation in vitro. TGF-β stimulation increased the frequency of αSMA+ Vim+ cells (myofibroblasts) in WT CFBs, although this stimulation was blunted in the knockout CFB (Figure 6D and 6E). Interestingly, we did not detect defects in the ability of knockout CFBs to stimulate SMAD2 phosphorylation on TGF-β stimulation (Figure S5D), suggesting that the defect observed in vivo (Figure 5D) is likely not due to an intrinsic defect in the TGF-β signaling pathway. To ensure that the difference in myofibroblast differentiation was indeed due to the deletion of Sept4 and not the consequence of an undefined compensatory response to Sept4 deletion, we isolated CFBs from WT mice and acutely reduced Sept4 expression using siRNAs. We first confirmed that Sept4 siRNA treatment reduced SEPT4 protein levels (Figure 6F and 6G). Acute reduction in SEPT4 protein resulted in reduced αSMA and POSTN expression compared with Scrambled siRNA treatment controls, demonstrating a direct effect of Sept4 on fibroblast activation (Figure 6H through 6J). As a functional measurement of CFB differentiation, we assessed collagen gel contraction and noted a blunted ability of CFBs to contract when Sept4 was knocked down (Figure 6K). These results highlight the importance of Sept4 expression in CFBs to respond to TGF-β stimulation. To better understand the role of Sept4 expression in the response to TGF-β stimulation, we measured the expression of Sept4 in WT CFBs. Intriguingly, we observed a downregulation of Sept4 in response to TGF-β stimulation (Figure S6A). To further corroborate this finding, we measured the expression of Sept4 in CFBs that were freshly isolated from WT hearts after TAC, which also showed downregulation of Sept4 (Figure S6B). These results are in agreement with our analysis of a previously published mRNA sequencing data set (GSE 180720)31 where we also observed downregulation of Sept4 in CFB in response to cardiac pressure overload (Figure S6C and S6D). These results indicate that Sept4 shows dynamic behavior in response to stress that could be important in the process of fibrosis development. This dynamic change could be due to a compensatory downregulation of Sept4 in stress conditions, to prevent exacerbated scarring. To test if overexpression of SEPT4 was sufficient to induce myofibroblast differentiation, we isolated CFBs and transduced them with an inducible lentiviral Sept4 overexpression construct or a Sept4 overexpression construct with a deleted GTPase domain. On stimulation of overexpression, in the absence of TGF-β stimulation, we observed an increase in the myofibroblast differentiation levels, but only if the GTPase domain was present, likely indicative of a functional requirement of the GTPase domain for polymerization with other Septin family members (Figure S6E).32

Calcineurin-Dependent Myofibroblast Differentiation Requires Septin 4

Mechanistically, Sept4 has previously been established as a regulator of NFAT (nuclear factor of activator of T cells) activation.33 Intriguingly, when we assessed CnA (calcineurin A) expression levels as the main mediator of NFAT activation, we observed a stark reduction in protein levels in response to Sept4 siRNA treatment (Figure 7A). To determine the functional impact of CnA activation in the setting of CBF activation and myofibroblast differentiation, we treated freshly isolated fibroblasts from WT and Sept4 knockout hearts with TGF-β, similar to the results presented in Figure 6D and 6E. We added the CnA inhibitor cyclosporin A in addition to TGF-β, which resulted in a substantial inhibition of myofibroblast differentiation in WT CFBs with no effect in knockout CFBs, suggesting that CnA plays a crucial role in myofibroblast differentiation that is dependent on Sept4 expression (Figure 7B). To determine if forced activation of Calcineurin could overcome any intrinsic defects in Sept4 knockout CFB to myofibroblast differentiation, we plated WT and knockout CFBs and treated them with TGF-β and an adenovirus that expresses activated CnA. Adding activated CnA onto WT CFBs that were stimulated with TGF-β did not yield a higher myofibroblast differentiation proportion (Figure 7C and 7D). However, Sept4 knockout CFBs stimulated with TGF-β in combination with activated CnA increased the proportion of myofibroblasts, indicating that there is an intrinsic defect in the CnA signaling arm of Sept4 knockout CFBs (Figure 7C and 7D). To test if altered CnA signaling might be important in Sept4 knockout hearts, we performed Western blotting for CnA and noted reduced expression in the Sept4 knockout hearts after cardiac pressure overload (Figure 7E).

Fibroblast-Specific Deletion of Septin 4 Prevents Fibrosis After Pressure Overload

To establish direct causality between fibroblast-expressed Sept4 and cardiac fibrosis, we engineered a fibroblast-specific Sept4 deletion model by flanking exons 2 to 3 of the Sept4 locus with loxP sites and cross-breeding with Tcf21-MerCreMer mice.19 This model allowed us to induce Sept4 ablation specifically in CFBs (Figure 8A and 8B). When challenged with pressure overload, fibroblast-specific Sept4 knockout mice exhibited markedly reduced cardiac fibrosis compared with littermate controls (Figure 8C). Histological assessment revealed decreased numbers of αSMA-positive cells with no change in Postn deposition (Figure 8D; Figure S7A) and Western blotting demonstrated reduced total αSMA protein expression, but no difference in SMAD2 phosphorylation after TAC (Figure 8E; Figure S7B), further highlighting the specific role of Sept4 in myofibroblast differentiation. These findings provide definitive genetic evidence that fibroblast-specific Sept4 deletion directly mediates myofibroblast differentiation and pathological cardiac fibrosis following pressure overload.

Discussion

Here, we assessed the role of Sept4 in the murine heart. Our findings identify a critical role for Sept4 as a regulator of cardiac ECM expression. We show that Sept4 expression is required for a normal response to TGF-β stimulation and that the absence of Sept4 results in blunted myofibroblast activation and reduced ECM production. Sept4 deletion protects the heart against pressure overload-induced injury by substantially attenuating maladaptive ventricular remodeling and preventing the progression to heart failure.

In recent years, it has become clear that the development of cardiac fibrosis is directly correlated with poor prognosis in heart failure with preserved and reduced ejection fraction.13,34,35 The long-term effects of cardiac fibrosis are detrimental, and there are currently no treatments to specifically block or reverse the development of fibrosis. Recently, the identification of a specific ECM-producing class of fibroblasts has garnered attention for their potential role in the transition from hypertrophy to HF.21,36 For example, these ECM-producing fibroblasts specifically express genes like Cilp (cartilage intermediate layer protein) or Cthrc1 (collagen triple helix repeat containing 1). Importantly, CILP serum levels are correlated with the presence of heart failure in patients,7 and Cthrc1-expressing fibroblasts are critical for repair after myocardial infarction, where it is known that ECM deposition is critical for the prevention of cardiac rupture.36 Whether these specific ECM-producing fibroblast subpopulations are initiating the transition to heart failure, and to what extent inhibition of these fibroblasts could be protective or detrimental, remains unknown. Regardless, it is important to note that in addition to other profibrotic genes, Cthrc1 mRNA expression was also reduced in our knockout mouse model (Figure 2A). We currently do not know whether this poses a risk for knockout hearts for developing cardiac rupture in cases such as myocardial infarction, but our results show that Sept4 deletion-mediated decrease in expression of Cthrc1 and other profibrotic genes alleviates pressure overload-mediated heart failure.

An important reason why antifibrotic therapies are proposed to have beneficial effects in the setting of chronic heart failure is that they reduce the overall stiffness of the heart and reduce the development of cardiac arrhythmias.12,37 Currently, angiotensin and aldosterone inhibition are the main ways to inhibit the development of fibrosis, although inhibition of angiotensin and aldosterone signaling has many more consequences, such as afterload reduction. Most specific antifibrotic therapies have failed in clinical trials thus far.38–40 One potential reason why antifibrotic drugs have failed could be the development of side effects. For example, TGF-β, which is considered the master regulator of profibrotic signaling, is essential for the development and homeostatic function of many organs.41 Embryonic deletion of TGF-β in mice causes stillbirth, and its deletion in adulthood causes global inflammation as well as muscle wasting.42 Moreover, due to its essential role in various cell types, TGF-β inhibition also affects cells, such as immune cells, including macrophages and monocytes.13 Taken together, these findings highlight the need for the discovery of safe targets to specifically prevent or reverse the development of cardiac fibrosis.

Our newly discovered role for Sept4 as a mediator of cardiac fibrosis extends the current knowledge of the role of Septins. Originally, Septins were identified as a critical part of the cytoskeleton in budding yeast.43 Septins are a separate part of the cytoskeleton and have been suggested to be important regulators of membrane organization, such as during cytokinesis.44 In addition, a mitochondrial splice isoform of Sept4, ARTS, has an important role in regulating stem cell apoptosis in highly regenerative organs, such as bone marrow, intestine, and skin.17,27 Although a link with the TGF-β pathway was identified from these studies on the splice isoform ARTS, a formal investigation into how Septin4 might be mediating TGF-β signaling has not been performed, to our knowledge. A more recent role for Sept4 was identified from a screen for novel NFAT activators.33

Our fibroblast-specific deletion of Sept4 results show that it plays an important role in CBFs. This is consistent with the observation that Sept4 is expressed by CFBs and further corroborated by our RNA sequencing results. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes showed specific alterations in genes involved in the regulation of the extracellular matrix. This was confirmed by a follow-on experiment where we measured the relative abundance of ECM proteins in decellularized WT and knockout hearts. In the knockout group, we noted decreased abundance of the most prominent collagen subtypes in the heart, such as COL1A1 and COL1A2, which provide stiffness to the heart through cross-linking activity of LOX, which was also reduced in expression in knockout hearts. Previous groups have hypothesized that fibrosis accelerates the development of heart failure by changing the stiffness of cardiac tissue, resulting in impaired cardiomyocyte relaxation.45 Indeed, when we measured cardiac stiffness, we observed that uninjured Sept4 knockout hearts showed a reduced compressive modulus compared with WT controls. Furthermore, we observed improved diastolic function in Sept4 knockout animals compared with WT controls. In response to cardiac pressure overload, we also observed a reduced fibrotic response in Sept4 knockout mice. Collectively, these results indicate an important role for Sept4 in regulating cardiac ECM production.

Mechanistically, we identified Sept4 as an important regulator of ECM protein production through modulating calcineurin signaling, downstream of TGF-β. The TGF-β cascade is the most well-known and potent signaling pathway that induces a fibrotic response.46 The cascade is activated on TGF-β binding to the TGF-β receptor complex, which then results in phosphorylation of receptor-regulated Smad proteins, such as SMAD2. On phosphorylation, SMAD2 translocates to the nucleus to transcriptionally induce expression of profibrotic genes.47 Our in vivo results show that deletion of Septin4 blunts the ability of fibroblasts to respond to TGF-β stimulation. We observed reduced expression of the TGF-β receptor 1 and reduced phosphorylation of SMAD2 after TAC. However, this phenotype was not replicated in isolated fibroblasts, suggesting that the altered ECM in the Sept4 knockout hearts might have contributed to the blunted TGF-β signaling. In isolated fibroblasts, we observed a blunted differentiation to myofibroblasts in response to TGF-β stimulation in knockout fibroblasts compared with WT fibroblasts. The role of the fibroblast as a regulator of cardiac ECM deposition and remodeling is much more complex than just responding to TGF-β. Direct TGF-β inhibition can have pleiotropic effects, due to effects on cardiomyocytes, fibroblasts, and immune cells, highlighting the need for identification of targets that can modulate fibrosis without affecting the positive remodeling effects of TGF-β signaling.29,48 It has become apparent that there are multiple subtypes of fibroblasts, such as myofibroblasts that display a contractile phenotype, as well as matrifibrocytes that are mainly produce structural ECM components. The precise role of Sept4 in regulating these different subtypes of fibroblasts is not clear, but based on our results, it appears that Sept4 is important in regulating myofibroblast activation as well as collagen deposition. In addition, even though TGF-β has been historically the most well-studied myofibroblast activator, recently other secretory factors, including interleukins, have been closely associated with cardiac fibrosis.49–51 It is possible that Sept4 contributes to cytokine-mediated myofibroblast differentiation. However, due to the established role of TGF-β in regulating CFB activation, we exclusively used TGF-β throughout our study.

In conclusion, our study identified Sept4 as a regulator of cardiac fibrosis in the setting of cardiac pressure overload. Sept4 enhanced the response to TGF-β, resulting in fibroblast activation, gene expression, and collagen deposition. Future studies will be needed to determine the exact role of fibroblasts and fibroblast-produced ECM proteins in regulating the adaptation to increased pressure and the development of heart failure.
