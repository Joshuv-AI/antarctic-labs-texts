The correlation between systemic inflammatory index and rebleeding after bronchial artery embolization: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Rong Lu, Yujia Gao, Changhao Sun, Minglei Wang, Ting Huang, Guanhua Yang, Yong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveInflammation and immune-related factors may play a crucial role in recurrent hemoptysis after bronchial artery embolization (BAE). This study aimed to investigate the relationship between inflammatory markers such as neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR), systemic immune-inflammatory index (SII), and pan-immune- inflammatory value (PIV) and recurrent hemoptysis after BAE.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on 420 patients with massive hemoptysis who underwent BAE in our hospital between January 2015 and January 2023. The demographic characteristics, laboratory test results (white blood cell count, platelet count, lymphocyte count, NLR, PLR, LMR, NMR, SII, PIV, etc.), and postoperative follow-up data of the patients were collected. The primary endpoint was recurrent hemoptysis after BAE, and the patients were divided into Recurrent Hemoptysis group and Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group according to the hemoptysis volume. The levels of inflammatory indicators in patients with and without recurrent hemoptysis were compared in each group, and univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to determine the independent risk factors for recurrent hemoptysis. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive ability of inflammatory indicators for recurrent hemoptysis, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated.ResultsAmong the 420 patients with massive hemoptysis who underwent BAE, 51.4% were male. The average duration of bronchiectasis before BAE was 14.8 years, and the average duration of embolization before BAE was 2.3 days. The most common underlying cause was bronchiectasis (42.5%). Compared to the non-recurrent group (N = 233), patients in the Recurrent Hemoptysis group (N = 187) exhibited significantly lower platelet counts, but higher levels of monocytes, neutrophils, NLR, and PLR (P < 0.0001). In the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group (N = 89), compared to the non-recurrent group (N = 331), significantly higher levels of monocytes, neutrophils, NLR, PLR, MLR, SII, and PIV were observed (P < 0.05). Multivariate Cox analysis identified that elevated PIV was an independent predictor for any recurrent hemoptysis (HR = 2.238, 95% CI: 1.765–3.256, P = 0.038), while elevated PLR independently predicted severe recurrent hemoptysis (HR = 1.289, 95% CI: 1.192–1.350, P = 0.027). The AUCs of PIV for predicting recurrent hemoptysis at 1 year and 3 years after surgery were 0.780 and 0.671, respectively, while the AUC of PLR for predicting recurrent severe hemoptysis at 1 year and 3 years after surgery were 0.863 and 0.824, respectively.ConclusionElevated PIV before embolization is associated with an increased risk of recurrent hemoptysis, and increased PLR is independently associated with an increased risk of recurrent severe hemoptysis. These markers could serve as potential biomarkers for predicting rebleeding after BAE.

Background

Massive hemoptysis, a potentially life-threatening respiratory emergency caused by various respiratory diseases such as tuberculosis, bronchiectasis, and lung cancer, requires prompt management [1, 2]. Bronchial artery embolization (BAE), a minimally invasive interventional procedure, effectively controls hemoptysis by occluding the abnormal bleeding bronchial arteries, often obviating the need for higher-risk surgery. It is now considered the first-line treatment for massive hemoptysis [3–5]. However, recurrent hemoptysis occurs in some patients following BAE, adversely impacting prognosis and potentially becoming life-threatening [6]. Therefore, accurately predicting the risk of recurrent hemoptysis after BAE surgery is crucial for guiding preventative strategies and improving patient prognosis.

At present, despite the advancement in BAE technology, the prediction and prevention of postoperative recurrent hemoptysis remains a clinical challenge. Previous studies have mainly focused on exploring the impact of factors such as imaging features and underlying diseases as predictors of recurrence, while the role of the host inflammatory response has received less attention [7]. As the body’s defensive response to damaging stimuli, inflammation is implicated in the pathogenesis of numerous diseases and is thought to play a significant role in the pathophysiology of hemoptysis [8, 9]. Inflammatory response can affect the occurrence and development of hemoptysis through various pathways. For example, inflammatory cells and inflammatory factors can stimulate the release of angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor, promote the formation of fragile new blood vessels in the vessel walls, which may rupture and cause bleeding [10, 11]. Furthermore, inflammation may induce bronchial artery dilation and promote the formation or opening of abnormal anastomoses between bronchial and pulmonary vessels, increasing blood flow to the affected area and exacerbating hemoptysis [12].

In recent years, various easily obtainable inflammatory indicators have been widely used in clinical practice, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR), systemic immune inflammatory index (SII), and pan-immune-inflammatory value (PIV) have gained attention. Due to their advantages of easy access and low cost, they are widely used in the prognosis assessment of various diseases such as tumors, autoimmune diseases, and cardiovascular conditions [13–17]. In recent studies, SII has a certain diagnostic value for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease combined with lung cancer, and PIV has a certain predictive ability for adverse coronary collateral circulation [18, 19]. However, the relationship between these inflammatory indicators and recurrent hemoptysis after BAE surgery remains unclear.

In summary, postoperative recurrent hemoptysis seriously affects the prognosis of BAE patients and brings great social and economic burdens. Inflammation is closely related to the occurrence and development of hemoptysis. While the predictive value of inflammatory indicators has been investigated for adverse outcomes such as restenosis following other vascular interventions like carotid artery surgery [20], there are few studies on the predictive value of these indicators specifically in recurrent hemoptysis after BAE surgery, which highlights the value of the current research. Therefore, this study aims to explore the relationship between inflammatory markers (NLR, PLR, LMR, NMR, SII, and PIV) and recurrent hemoptysis after BAE through a retrospective cohort study. The goal is to clarify their predictive value for recurrence risk, provide a basis for early clinical identification of high-risk patients, formulate individualized intervention measures, and ultimately improve patient prognosis.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study evaluated patients who underwent BAE treatment for persistent hemoptysis at the Department of Interventional Radiology in our institution between January 2015 and January 2021.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: [1] Age ≥ 18 years; [2] Patients who received BAE to control hemoptysis with a hemoptysis volume > 100 mL/d; [3] All underwent CT angiography (CTA) before surgery; [4] Had complete clinical information and follow-up data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: [1] Significant coagulation dysfunction; [2] Severe allergy to contrast agent; [3] Severe cardiopulmonary dysfunction; [4] Unstable vital signs due to shock; [5] Contraindications to interventional surgery; [6] Active infection before embolization; [7] Other diseases that may cause hemoptysis, such as active pulmonary tuberculosis, lung cancer, etc. BAE procedures were conducted by experienced specialists according to the 2022 European Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology (CIRSE) guidelines for bronchial artery embolization [21].

This study followed the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by our institutional ethics review committee.

Data collection

Data were retrospectively collected from patients, including:

Demographic information: age, gender, and smoking history;Clinical data: medical history (hypertension, thrombocytopenia, use of anticoagulants), amount of hemoptysis, duration of hemoptysis, pre-embolization imaging results (including abnormal arterial distribution), duration of embolism, and time of onset of embolism;Laboratory examination indicators: Complete blood count within 24 h before embolization (including white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, platelet count, monocyte count), inflammatory indicators (C-reactive protein, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, NLR, PLR, LMR, NMR, SII, PIV), and other indicators that may affect the prognosis (hemoglobin, albumin, coagulation function indicators APTT, PT, INR, liver and kidney function indicators ALT, AST, Cr, TG).

Follow-up and endpoints

The patients included in the study were followed up via telephone contact until January 2024 to ascertain clinical outcomes, including hemoptysis recurrence, time to recurrence, treatments received for recurrence, and survival status.

The primary endpoint of this study was recurrent hemoptysis after BAE, which was further divided into recurrent hemoptysis and recurrent severe hemoptysis according to the volume of hemoptysis. Secondary endpoints included the time of recurrent hemoptysis, the treatment after rebleeding, and the patient’s prognosis. Recurrent hemoptysis was defined as coughing up only blood in the airway or lung parenchyma after BAE, without mucus, with hemoptysis volume < 100mL/d. Recurrent severe hemoptysis was defined as hemoptysis requiring repeated BAE, lobectomy, or death from hemoptysis after BAE, with a hemoptysis volume > 300mL/d [2, 22, 23]. For patients without recurrence, the recurrence-free time was calculated from the date of BAE to the last follow-up date or the date of death.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS (Version 26.0), R software (Version 4.4.1), and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.5.0). All quantitative variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Continuous variables between the two groups were compared using independent sample t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables were compared using chi-square test. Univariate Cox analysis was used to determine the correlation between serum inflammatory parameters and recurrent hemoptysis/recurrent massive hemoptysis. Variables with statistically significance in the univariate Cox analysis were then included in a multivariate Cox regression analysis model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive ability of each inflammatory marker for rebleeding, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic information

The average duration of bronchiectasis before BAE was 14.8 years, and the average duration of embolization before BAE was 2.3 days. The most common cause of the disease was bronchiectasis (42.5%), and all patients did not have serious complications related to the procedure. Minor complications included chest tightness (N = 24), chest pain (N = 23), hematoma at the puncture site (N = 9), fever (N = 6), and dysphagia (N = 4). For comparative analysis, patients were grouped based on post-BAE recurrence status and severity: those with no recurrence, those with any recurrent hemoptysis (Recurrent Hemoptysis group), and a subset of the latter with recurrent severe hemoptysis (Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group). The results showed that in both groups, the duration of bronchiectasis before BAE and the average duration of embolization before BAE were significantly longer in patients with recurrent hemoptysis/recurrent severe hemoptysis compared to the control group. In addition, there was a significant difference in the etiology between the two groups of patients whether they had Aspergilloma infection (P < 0.05).

Comparison of abnormal arteries in patients

The analysis of abnormal artery distribution in the 420 patients included in the study revealed significant differences between the Recurrent Hemoptysis group, the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group, and their respective control groups (Table 2). Specifically, the proportion of patients with abnormal inferior phrenic arteries was significantly higher in the Recurrent Hemoptysis group compared to the non-recurrent group (49.7% vs. 32.2%, P = 0.037) and also significantly higher in the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group compared to its non-recurrent counterpart (59.6% vs. 34.7%, P < 0.001).

While the presence of an abnormal left bronchial artery (Left BA) was noted, there was no significant difference in its prevalence between the Recurrent Hemoptysis group and the non-recurrent group (P > 0.05). There was no significant difference in the distribution of other abnormal arteries between the two groups, such as the right intercostal bronchial trunk, common bronchial artery, right bronchial artery, left intercostal bronchial trunk, internal thoracic artery, costocervical trunk, lateral thoracic artery, thyrocervical trunk, subscapular artery, proper esophageal artery, intercostal artery, subphrenic artery, and left gastric artery (P > 0.05).

In the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group compared to the control group, only the detection rates of abnormal inferior phrenic arteries and abnormal bronchial arteries on CTA (abnormal AbBAs on CTA) were significantly different. In addition, abnormal aberrant bronchial arteries on CTA were more frequently observed in the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group (66.3% vs. 20.8%, P < 0.001).

Comparison of preoperative biochemical indicators

The biochemical indicators of the patients included in the study were compared. Compared to the non-recurrent group (N = 233), patients in the Recurrent Hemoptysis group (N = 187) exhibited significantly lower platelet counts (P = 0.0034), but higher monocyte counts (P = 0.0235), higher neutrophil counts (P < 0.0001), higher NLR (P = 0.0007), and higher PLR (P < 0.0001, Table 3).

Compared to non-recurrent patients (N = 331), those in the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group (N = 89) showed significantly higher monocyte counts (P < 0.0001), higher neutrophil counts (P < 0.0001), higher NLR (P < 0.0001), higher PLR (P < 0.0001), higher MLR (P < 0.0001), higher SII (P = 0.0052), and higher PIV (P = 0.0003).

Other measured parameters, including white blood cell (WBC) count, lymphocyte count, hemoglobin (Hb), albumin (Alb), coagulation markers (APTT, PT, INR), and liver/kidney function tests (ALT, AST, Cr, TG), did not differ significantly between either recurrence group (any or severe) and their respective non-recurrent counterparts (P > 0.05).

The indicators showing significant differences between the two groups were subjected to both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. The results showed that for patients in the Recurrent Hemoptysis group, PIV (HR = 2.238, 95% CI, 1.765–3.256) was identified as an independent risk factor for recurrent hemoptysis after BAE (Fig. 2). In the Recurrent Severe Hemoptysis group, PLR (HR = 1.289, 95% CI, 1.192–1.350) was identified as an independent risk factor for recurrent severe hemoptysis (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This retrospective study involving 420 patients undergoing BAE for hemoptysis investigated the prognostic utility of preoperative systemic inflammatory indices. Our primary finding is that specific, readily available inflammatory markers are independently associated with the risk of rebleeding after BAE. Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified elevated Pan-Immune-Inflammation Value (PIV) as an independent predictor for any recurrent hemoptysis, while elevated Platelet-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (PLR) independently predicted recurrent severe hemoptysis. Furthermore, ROC analysis indicated that PIV had moderate predictive accuracy for recurrence at 1 year (AUC = 0.780), and PLR demonstrated good predictive accuracy for severe recurrence at 1 year (AUC = 0.863), suggesting their potential clinical value.

The clinical relevance of predicting post-BAE recurrence is underscored by the fact that recurrent hemoptysis remains a significant challenge. Previous study indicates that recurrence rates after BAE range from approximately 10–55%, with established risk factors including underlying pulmonary diseases, aspergilloma infection, the choice of embolic material, and specific vascular anatomy [24–27]. Rebleeding significantly impacts patient quality of life and can lead to life-threatening complications, including asphyxia and hemorrhagic shock [6]. Accurate prediction of rebleeding risk is therefore crucial for guiding clinical decision-making, optimizing treatment strategies, and ultimately improving patient outcomes. However, prior research on predicting post-BAE rebleeding has primarily investigated factors such as imaging characteristics and underlying diseases, while the potential role of systemic inflammatory markers has been relatively overlooked [28, 29].

While the etiologies of hemoptysis are diverse, the proximate cause often involves abnormalities within the pulmonary or bronchial vasculature. Conditions such as pulmonary tuberculosis, lung cancer, and pulmonary aspergillosis, which directly compromise the integrity of the vascular wall, leading to massive and persistent hemoptysis, with a high risk of recurrence after embolization [30–32]. Additionally, structural vascular abnormalities, such as the hypertrophied bronchial arteries characteristic of bronchiectasis or malformations like bronchial artery-pulmonary artery fistulae, are significant causes of hemoptysis [33].

The observed association between preoperative systemic inflammatory indices and post-BAE rebleeding is biologically plausible. The underlying conditions leading to hemoptysis often involve chronic inflammation, and the systemic inflammatory response itself can contribute to vascular pathology. Inflammatory cells and mediators can promote detrimental angiogenesis leading to fragile neovessels, increase vascular permeability, and compromise endothelial integrity, all increasing bleeding risk [12, 34, 35]. Furthermore, inflammation can interface with the coagulation system, potentially promoting thrombosis or dysregulated hemostasis [36]. Our findings specifically implicate PIV and PLR. PIV, integrating neutrophil, monocyte, platelet, and lymphocyte counts, offers a composite snapshot of the systemic inflammatory and immune landscape [37–39]. In addition, PIV is also one of the predictors of poor prognosis in peritoneal dialysis patients, long-term adverse cardiovascular events in patients with myocardial infarction after percutaneous coronary intervention, and the activity of rheumatoid arthritis [40–42]. Increased PIV levels may increase the risk of rebleeding by causing increased vascular fragility and abnormal coagulation function [43]. PLR, reflecting the balance between platelets and lymphocytes, provides insight into thrombotic potential and immune modulation [44]. An elevated PLR (thrombocytosis with relative lymphopenia) might indicate a pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic state combined with suppressed adaptive immunity, potentially hindering effective vascular healing after embolization and increasing susceptibility to severe rebleeding [45]. The distinct associations—PIV with any recurrence and PLR with severe recurrence—may suggest that while a broad inflammatory state (captured by PIV) increases the general risk of rebleeding, factors related to platelet activation and immune function (reflected in PLR) might be more critical in determining the severity of subsequent bleeding events.

Our findings should be viewed within the context of existing literature. While the importance of inflammation in lung pathologies is recognized, and some studies have linked traditional markers like C-reactive protein (CRP) to post-BAE rebleeding, particularly in specific subgroups like tuberculosis patients [46], research systematically evaluating newer composite indices like PIV and PLR in predicting post-BAE recurrence is limited. The prognostic significance of PLR observed in our study resonates with findings in other thrombo-inflammatory vascular conditions, such as pulmonary embolism [45]. However, the identification of PIV as an independent predictor specifically for post-BAE rebleeding appears novel. It is also important to consider context dependency when comparing biomarker studies. For instance, Scalise et al. found that pre-intervention NLR and SIRI predicted restenosis (a different outcome driven by neointimal hyperplasia) after carotid interventions (a different vascular bed and procedure) [20], highlighting that optimal predictive biomarkers can vary significantly. Compared to traditional single markers like CRP, the composite indices PIV and PLR, by integrating multiple cellular components of the immune-inflammatory response, may offer enhanced predictive value for the complex outcome of rebleeding after BAE, a possibility supported by our results.

Beyond the primary inflammatory markers, our analysis corroborated the importance of other established risk factors and identified relevant associations. Consistent with prior reports [47], specific anatomical findings – namely, the presence of abnormal inferior phrenic arteries and aberrant bronchial arteries on CTA (AbBAs) – were significantly associated with recurrent severe hemoptysis in our cohort. This reinforces the importance of meticulous pre-procedural vascular assessment and suggests these particular anomalies may represent higher-risk lesions less amenable to durable embolization. Clinically, we also found that a longer duration of known bronchiectasis and a longer time from presentation to embolization were associated with increased recurrence risk, potentially reflecting greater underlying disease severity or more advanced inflammatory processes at the time of intervention. Furthermore, Aspergilloma infection emerged as a significant predictor of recurrence, aligning with its known potential for vascular erosion and persistent localized inflammation [48, 49]. The high prevalence of bronchiectasis as the leading etiology in our study [22, 50], viewed alongside external studies linking higher SII levels to exacerbation risk in such patients [51]. collectively underscores the complex interplay between chronic structural lung disease, systemic inflammation, and adverse post-interventional outcomes like rebleeding.

Our retrospective study has certain clinical significance. We explored for the first time the relationship between systemic inflammatory index (especially PIV and PLR) and rebleeding after BAE, and preliminarily proposed that PIV and PLR can be used as simple and easy-to-obtain hematological indicators to predict rebleeding after BAE. Future studies can further explore the specific mechanism of action of PIV and PLR, and build a multifactorial prediction model in combination with other clinical indicators to improve the accuracy of rebleeding prediction.

However, this study also has some limitations. This study benefits from a relatively large cohort size (N = 420) for investigations involving BAE and the comprehensive analysis of multiple systemic inflammatory indices. However, we acknowledge several limitations. First, the retrospective design is inherently susceptible to selection bias and potential confounding variables that were not fully accounted for. Second, its single-center nature may limit the direct generalizability of the quantitative findings to diverse populations or different healthcare systems. Third, reliance on telephone follow-up could introduce inaccuracies or recall bias regarding the specifics of recurrence events (timing, volume). Fourth, data on potential variations in embolic materials or specific BAE techniques employed over the extensive study period were not analyzed as potential covariates, which might influence outcomes. Finally, the inflammatory markers were assessed only at a single pre-procedural time point, precluding analysis of their dynamic changes post-intervention or in relation to recurrence events. Addressing these limitations necessitates future multi-center, prospective studies with standardized protocols for data collection, BAE procedures, follow-up, and endpoint adjudication to validate our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of this study suggest that the systemic inflammatory indices are closely related to rebleeding after BAE surgery, and PIV and PLR can be used as potential biomarkers for predicting rebleeding. These markers could serve as potential biomarkers for predicting rebleeding after BAE. Further investigation into these biomarkers may aid in the early identification of high-risk patients and inform the design of future studies aimed at developing personalized management strategies.
