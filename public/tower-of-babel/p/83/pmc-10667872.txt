Sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis: A rare cause of small bowel obstruction

Introduction

Sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis (SEP) is a rare clinical condition characterized by a dense fibrocollagenous membrane which partially or completely encapsulates intraperitoneal organs, potentially leading to intestinal obstruction [1]. SEP is divided according to its underlying etiology into primary and secondary encapsulating peritonitis, with the latter being more common [1]. Due to its distinct intraoperative appearance, SEP is also known as abdominal cocoon syndrome and was first described by Foo et al. in 1978 [2].

Case report

A 30-year-old male presented to the Emergency Department with a 1-day history of paraumbilical pain, abdominal distention, nausea, multiple episodes of vomiting, and constipation. He had no past medical or surgical history except several admissions for small bowel obstructions which resolved with non-operative management.

On physical examination the patient was hemodynamically stable with mild abdominal distention, tenderness in the paraumbilical area, and hyperactive bowel sounds on auscultation.

Laboratory investigations showed a white blood cell count of 11.8 × 10^9/L (normal range 4.00–11.00 × 10^9/L), C-reactive protein of 32.9 mg/L (normal range ≤ 5.00 mg/L) and lactate of 2.5 mmol/L (normal range 0.5–2.2 mmol/L). The remainder of his laboratory analysis including electrolytes, renal function test and hemoglobin were within normal limits.

Abdominal X-ray revealed a fecal loaded colon with non-specific small bowel gas pattern. Due to his recurrent history of bowel obstruction, and similar symptoms at presentation, a contrasted computed tomography (CT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis was obtained, which showed a cluster of dilated small bowel loops encapsulated within a sac of thickened peritoneal reflection in the mid abdomen and intraperitoneal proliferation of lipomatous tissue (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The proximal small bowel loops demonstrated multiple air fluid levels with collapsed distal ileal loops. An incidental urachal diverticulum was also noted.

Following the results of the CT scan, and given that he had no known inciting inflammatory events, the patient was diagnosed with small bowel obstruction due to primary SEP. The patient was admitted to the hospital, a nasogastric tube was inserted, and he was kept NPO with IV fluids and analgesics. The patient was taken to the operating room for therapeutic laparotomy with planned adhesiolysis by a consultant surgeon and two assistants. Through a midline incision, a thick visceral peritoneal membrane was found encasing the entire intraperitoneal small and large bowel as well as the stomach, liver and spleen (Fig. 3). Extensive adhesions were also found between the small bowel loops, colon and stomach. Thorough adhesiolysis was performed, excising the abdominal cocoon membrane, followed by releasing all inter-loop adhesions (Fig. 4). After completely freeing the small bowel, colon and stomach, a careful inspection was performed to evaluate for iatrogenic injuries. During exploration the cecum was identified in the right upper quadrant close to the midline. Considering patient condition and the high probability of appendicitis presenting in an atypical fashion, appendectomy was performed. The remainder of the exploration was unremarkable. Peritoneal capsule encasing the liver and spleen was not released due to concern for splenic and liver injury. Primary abdominal closure was performed using loop PDS® suture.

Intraoperative finding - demonstrating a thick peritoneal capsule encasing the small bowel (Black arrow indicates the capsule).

Intraoperative finding – showing a midline laparotomy incision, after excision of the fibrous capsule and extensive adhesiolysis with complete release of the small bowel.

The postoperative period was unremarkable with good overall recovery. Sips of water were started on postoperative day 1, and gradually advanced to regular diet. He had a bowel movement on postoperative day 2 and was discharged on postoperative day 5. During interval outpatient clinic follow-up at 7 days and 6 months post discharge the patient appeared in good health with no recurrent symptoms.

Histopathology of the membrane tissue showed fibrocollagenous tissue with congested blood vessels and patchy chronic lymphocytic inflammation with occasional reactive lymphoid follicles. Some mature adipose tissues were also noted. There was no evidence of granuloma or malignancy (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Encasement of the intraperitoneal organs by fibrous membrane was first reported in 1907 by Owtschinnikow who named it “peritonitis chronica fibrosa incapsulata” [3]. Throughout the years different terminology has been used to identify this clinical condition including icing sugar bowel, fibroplastic peritonitis, abdominal cocoon syndrome and, currently, sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis [4].

The exact pathogenesis of SEP is not well understood but is theoretically secondary to cytokine release and fibroblast activation during an episode of peritoneal inflammation leading to the formation of the thick fibrocollagenous peritoneal membrane [1]. While several aggravating factors have been identified, many cases are reported without any specific cause, and are diagnosed as primary or idiopathic SEP.

Secondary SEP required an identifiable causative factor. Peritoneal dialysis (PD) is the most commonly reported factor associated with the development of secondary SEP. Studies have identified increased risk of SEP with chronic PD, which may be secondary to higher rates of bacterial peritonitis and/or recurrent intraabdominal inflammation caused by the peritoneal dialysate [4,5]. Additionally, a wide range of potentially causative factors linked to SEP have been identified, including: intraabdominal infections, surgical procedures, gastrointestinal malignancies, autoimmune diseases, and pharmaceuticals [4,6]. In the present report, the patient had no significant history or identifiable causes that could be associated with the disease, and was therefore diagnosed as primary SEP.

Anatomically SEP is further classified into three types according to the involvement of bowel and other intra-abdominal organs; Type 1 - intestine is partially encapsulated by the membrane, Type 2 - entire small and large bowel encapsulated by membrane, and Type 3 - entire intestine and other peritoneal organs are covered with membrane [4,6]. A systematic review of 118 patients by Machado et al. reported a distribution of 43 % Type 1, 31 % Type 2 and 25 % Type 3 [4]. In the current report the patient had Type 3 SEP with complete encapsulation of the bowel, liver and spleen.

SEP is a rare disease, with a total of approximately 200 cases reported in English literature [1]. It is more common in males and can affect any age group. It is more prevalent in tropical and subtropical countries such as China, India and Turkey [1]. A literature review in 2015 showed that the greatest number of published cases of SEP was in China (104 cases), followed by India (35 cases), Turkey (17 cases) and Nigeria (5 cases) [1]. A 2022 study found a total of 8 reported cases of SEP in the Middle East: Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (3 cases), Iraq (2 cases), United Arab Emirates, Oman and Bahrain (1 case each) [7].

While the incidence of asymptomatic SEP is unknown, most symptomatic patients present with symptoms of bowel obstruction; recurrent abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting and constipation [8]. Weight loss, ascites and the presence of a palpable abdominal mass have also been reported in some patients [8,9]. Our patient presented to the hospital with classical signs and symptoms of small bowel obstruction.

An accurate preoperative diagnosis of SEP is extremely challenging as patients typically present with non-specific obstructive symptoms. However, patients with a history of predisposing factors and chronic symptoms should have SEP included in the differential diagnosis.

Despite recent advances in radiology, most cases are diagnosed intraoperatively. Regardless, a contrast enhanced CT scan can be helpful in diagnosing SEP preoperatively [5]. CT imaging also helps exclude alternative causes of bowel obstruction and can aid in preoperative planning.

Several radiological signs on CT scan can raise suspicion of SEP in patients presenting with bowel obstruction including: thickened peritoneum, thickened small bowel mesentery, mid abdominal bowel clusters, thick peritoneal capsule and peritoneal calcifications [7,10].

In our case, the “Cauliflower Sign” or “Accordion Pattern” was evident on CT, which refers to the appearance of clustered small bowel segments which congregate at the midline and are encased by a dense capsule with a contrast-free periphery. This is a classical CT finding of SEP. [8,10]

While there is debate over the optimal management of patients with symptomatic, non-obstructive SEP, surgery remains the main line of management in patients presenting with bowel obstruction [1,4]. The surgical principles consist of exploratory laparotomy with complete excision of the peritoneal capsule and interloop adhesiolysis. The required extensive adhesiolysis is often challenging and should include senior surgical expertise as there is an increased risk of complications such as enterotomies, enterocutaneous fistulae and intraabdominal collections [7,11]. Prophylactic appendectomy, as performed in our case, has been suggested by some authors to avoid difficult appendectomy in the future [7,10]. Recurrent symptoms after SEP membrane resection and adhesiolysis has been reported and attributed to inadequate adhesiolysis [7,11]. With both emergency and semi elective timing, postoperative ileus is common due to extensive intraoperative bowel manipulation and may require prolong bowel rest and parenteral nutritional support.

Conclusion

Sclerosing encapsulating peritonitis is a rare cause of bowel obstruction attributed to intraabdominal inflammatory processes. SEP is more common in men, with a higher incidence in tropical and sub-tropical countries. Patients typically present with bowel obstruction; some with a significant history of chronic subacute obstructive symptoms. While most cases are diagnosed in the operating room, contrasted CT scan can aid in preoperative identification and surgical planning. Surgery remains the primary management with good outcomes dependent on adequate and careful adhesiolysis.
