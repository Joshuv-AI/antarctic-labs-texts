An introgression from Triticum timopheevii reduces grain protein content in winter wheat populations
Authors: Sandip M. Kale, Cristiana Paina, Winnie S. Füchtbauer, Pernille Bjarup, Pernille Merete Sarup, Janus Asbjørn Schatz‐Jakobsen, Jihad Orabi, Finn Borum, Ahmed Jahoor, Henrik Brinch‐Pedersen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving grain protein content (GPC) in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is crucial for enhancing end‐use quality and ensuring efficient nitrogen (N) utilization, thereby reducing environmental damage caused by excess N. However, progress in increasing GPC has been limited because of the strong negative correlation between GPC and grain yield (GY), as well as the scarcity of multi‐location, multi‐year phenotypic data. In this study, we analyzed the variation in GPC, GY, and grain protein deviation (GPD) using multi‐location, multi‐year phenotypic data from winter wheat varieties in Scandinavian regions. As reported previously, we observed a negative correlation between GY and GPC, with recent cultivars showing higher GY but lower GPC. Additionally, a genome‐wide association study (GWAS) in two independent populations identified significant marker–trait associations (MTAs) for GPC and GPD, with key MTAs located on chromosome 2B (chr2B), highlighting its central role in the regulation of these traits. Interestingly, the MTA for GPD on chr2B coincided with an introgression from Triticum timopheevii, which was associated with reduced GPC and GPD in elite lines carrying this region. This introgression, which contains a powdery mildew resistance gene (Pm6), appears to negatively affect GPC, likely due to linkage drag. These findings emphasize the importance of chr2B in wheat breeding and suggest that advanced genomic techniques, such as mutagenesis and CRISPR‐Cas, could be employed to mitigate negative pleiotropic effects and improve GPC and GPD. Overall, this study provides valuable insights into the genetic architecture underlying GPC in wheat and offers directions for future breeding strategies aimed at enhancing protein content.

INTRODUCTION

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.), the second most consumed cereal crop after rice, covers about 20% of the total dietary calorie requirements of the global population. Additionally, it contributes an average of 19% of total dietary protein, with the proportion rising to one‐third in certain regions of North Africa, West Asia, and Central Asia (Erenstein et al., 2022), making it a crucial source of protein. The grain protein content (GPC) is pivotal in determining both end‐use quality and economic value of bread wheat (T. aestivum L.) and durum wheat (Triticum turgidum L. ssp. durum). However, a decline in GPC has been observed in wheat varieties developed over the last decades (Laidig et al., 2017; Paina & Gregersen, 2023). This is due to the strong negative correlation between GPC and grain yield (GY), with breeding efforts mainly prioritizing GY improvement. Total protein yield has increased primarily because of GY rather than enhancements in GPC. Additionally, the complex genetic control involving numerous small‐effect genes with low heritability, along with environmental influences on GPC, made conventional breeding for higher GPC even more challenging (Kumar et al., 2018). Consequently, it is essential to increase grain yield while also meeting the growing demand for high‐quality, nutritious wheat grains (Battenfield et al., 2016).

Several studies have used quantitative trait loci (QTLs) analyses to identify genetic factors influencing GPC in tetraploid, hexaploid, and synthetic wheat populations (Paina & Gregersen, 2023). Many of these studies have identified a number of small‐effect QTLs specific to particular environments (reviewed in Kumar et al., 2018; Paina & Gregersen, 2023). Notably, a major QTL, GPC‐B1, located on the short arm of chromosome 6B (chr6BS) of wild tetraploid wheat T. turgidum ssp. dicoccoides (an ancestor of durum wheat), has been associated with up to 66% of the phenotypic variation in GPC (Uauy et al., 2006). A map‐based cloning effort led to the identification of the NAC transcription factor gene, NAM‐B1, at the GPC‐B1 locus (Uauy et al., 2006). The wild‐type allele of the NAM‐B1 gene promotes early senescence, enhancing nutrient mobilization from leaves to developing grains, which increases GPC and boosts micronutrient levels like iron (Fe) and zinc (Zn; Uauy et al., 2006). However, the wild‐type allele had a negative impact on grain yield and was therefore not selected in wheat breeding programs (Hagenblad et al., 2012).

The negative relationship between GPC and GY can be addressed by calculating the grain protein deviation (GPD), a residual from the regression of GPC on GY, to identify genotypes with higher GPC than would be expected from their GY (Monaghan et al., 2001). A positive, high GPD is indicative of a genotype with high GPC and good yield. Further, it has been observed that the GPD is also genetically controlled (Mosleth et al., 2020) and that several QTLs associated with increased GPD have been identified recently using durum wheat populations (Nigro et al., 2019).

In recent decades, various genomic resources have been developed in wheat to support conventional breeding and accelerate QTL mapping. The amount of diversity present in these populations is key to the success of QTL mapping. A multi‐year, multi‐location replicated phenotyping strategy has been found to be effective in identifying stable QTL (Kale et al., 2015; Valenzuela‐Antelo et al., 2023). Generally, it is believed that the elite lines derived from a few founder populations with a series of selection events possess a narrow genetic base. Fortunately, the wheat genome has remarkable plasticity. Due to its polyploid genome architecture, chromosomal segments of 52 species from 13 genera have been successfully transferred into wheat (Wulff & Moscou, 2014). Such regions, which are called alien introgressions, have mainly been used for developing disease‐resistant cultivars (Wulff & Moscou, 2014) but were also found useful in providing tolerance against abiotic stresses and other agroeconomic traits (Gill et al., 2011; Johansson et al., 2024). Recent studies have reported an increase in the frequency of those introgressions in modern wheat varieties (Heuberger et al., 2024; Schulthess et al., 2022). Further, the wild relatives have been found superior in terms of grain quality characteristics such as GPC, iron, and zinc (Zeibig et al., 2024) and can serve as a valuable source of diversity.

In this study, we performed a genome‐wide association study (GWAS) using comprehensive phenotypic data from winter wheat cultivars in Scandinavian regions to identify genetic elements linked to GPC and GPD, which were further validated through GWAS analysis using a panel of European winter wheat cultivars. Additionally, we performed haplotype analysis using publicly available sequencing datasets to study the impact of introgression on GPC and GPD in winter wheat breeding.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant material

The study was conducted using two different panels of winter wheat accessions. The first panel consists of 255 winter wheat accessions (hereafter the SCAN‐WHEAT panel) bred for Scandinavian countries (Table S1), while the second panel was the GABI‐WHEAT panel of European elite cultivars comprising 358 winter and 14 spring wheat varieties released between 1975 and 2007 (Table S2; Gogna et al., 2022).

Phenotypic data

The phenotypic data for GY and GPC for 255 winter wheat accessions (SCAN‐WHEAT panel) recorded in National Trials (NT) and Official Variety Testing (OVT) from years 2000 to 2023 at 37 different locations were downloaded from https://sortinfo.dk. Every year, 80–100 winter wheat varieties were tested across 10–12 locations under both pesticide‐treated and ‐untreated conditions. Additionally, rigorous statistical analyses eliminate trials with high uncertainty, ensuring data accuracy. Individual accessions were evaluated using standard agronomic practices at a minimum of two locations and for 1 year, and at a maximum of 37 locations and for 24 years. Protein content (%) was estimated by near‐infrared (NIR) spectra on raw grain using a PerkinElmer (USA) DA 7440 On‐line NIR instrument. The best linear unbiased estimates (BLUEs) were calculated and used for GWAS along with the genotypic data. Initially, the mean values of GY and GPC were calculated if an accession was evaluated under both NT and OVT trials for the respective year and location. The BLUEs were estimated separately for GY and GPC (Table S1) using the “lmer” function from the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in the R statistical environment using the following equation: Yijkl=μ+Gi+Lj+Yk+S:Ljk+εijkl, where Yijkl represents the phenotypic observation (Pheno); μ is the overall mean; Gi is the fixed effect of genotype (i); Lj∼N(0,σL2) is the random effect of the location (j); Yk∼N(0,σY2) is the random effect of year (k); (S:L)jk∼N(0,σS:L2) is the random effect of sample nested within location (jk) and ϵ ijkl ∼N(0,σε2) is the residual error. The variance components calculated from the above model were used to estimate broad sense heritability.

The GPD values (Table S1) from BLUEs of GY and GPC were calculated by fitting the simple linear model function “lm” from package lme4 in the R statistical environment as: GPD=GPCobj−GPCexp, where GPCexp =β0+β1×GY.

Here, β0 is the intercept and β1 is the slope (indicating the effect of GY on GPC).

The BLUEs for GPC and GY for accessions from the GABI‐WHEAT panel were downloaded (Gogna et al., 2022), and GPD values (Table S2) were calculated by fitting a simple linear model function of the “lm” from package lme4 in the R statistical environment as described above.

Genotypic data

Genotypic data were generated for 255 out of 1080 accessions of the SCAN‐WHEAT panel. A total of 41, 25, 23, and 166 accessions were genotyped using the 7K, 15K, 20K, and 25K Affymetrix platforms, respectively (Table S1). The single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from the smaller arrays are subsets of the larger array. For instance, the SNPs from 7K are a subset of the 15K, while those from the 15K are a subset of the 20K. The genotypic datasets of all the accessions were combined based on SNP marker labels. The physical positions of SNP markers were determined by aligning the marker sequences (Gogna et al., 2022) against the reference genome assembly of wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) cv. Chinese spring (RefSeq v1.0; International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium, 2018) using minimap2 (v2.26; Li, 2018). A “vcf” file was created by using genotypic data and chromosomal positions of SNP markers and used for downstream analysis. The markers were filtered to remove SNPs with >30% missing data, and principal component analysis (PCA) was carried out using snpgdsPCA() function of the R package SNPRelate (v.1.12.2; Zheng et al., 2012) to ensure data accuracy after merging, and to study diversity within accessions. Further, the similarity between accessions was determined using identity‐by‐state (IBS) analysis performed using snpgdsIBSNum () function from SNPRelate (v.1.12.2; Zheng et al., 2012).

The SNP dataset for more than 8000 winter wheat accessions sequenced using genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) was downloaded (Schulthess et al., 2022). The dataset contains both plant genetic resources and elite lines from European regions (part of GABI‐WHEAT panel) and thus represents the entire diversity present in the winter wheat population (Schulthess et al., 2022). A combined genotypic dataset was generated by merging common SNPs from diverse winter wheat and SCAN‐WHEAT accessions, and diversity between the datasets was studied using PCA.

Genome‐wide association study

GWAS for GY, GPC, and GPD were conducted independently using genotypic and phenotypic datasets from both SCAN‐WHEAT and GABI‐WHEAT panels using the linear mixed model implemented in the gemma software (v0.98.3; Zhou, 2017). For the SCAN‐WHEAT panel, SNPs with less than 30% missing data and minor allele frequency greater than 0.01 were used for the analysis. In the case of GABI‐WHEAT panel, the SNP array data were downloaded (Gogna et al., 2022), and a “vcf” file was generated as mentioned above. The SNPs with less than 30% missing data were considered for GWAS analysis. The kinship matrices were calculated using the snpgdsIBSNum() function from R package SNPRelate (v.1.12.2; Zheng et al., 2012), separately for both panels. The significance of association was visualized by generating a Manhattan plot of p‐values against the physical position of SNP markers using the qqman package (Turner, 2018) from R statistical environment.

Sequencing‐based coverage study

The GBS sequencing data of accessions from GABI‐WHEAT were downloaded (Schulthess et al., 2022) and used for depth of coverage analysis as described in Schulthess et al. (2022). Briefly, the sequencing reads from each accession along with the reads of cv. Chinese Spring were trimmed using cutadapt (v1.16; Martin, 2011) to remove adapter and low quality bases. The trimmed reads were mapped separately against the reference genome assembly of cv. Chinese Spring (RefSeq v1.0; International Wheat Genome Sequencing Consortium [IWGSC], 2018) using minimap2 (v2.26; Li, 2018) and converted to binary alignment map (BAM) format using SAMtools (v.1.9; Li et al., 2009) and further sorted with NovoSort (v.3.06.05). The BAM file of each accession was used to calculate the number of reads in 5‐Mb bins. The reads were first normalized to sequencing coverage and then normalized to Chinese Spring reads from the same window. The normalized log values were used to generate chromosome‐wise coverage plot using pheatmap package in R statistical environment.

Haplotype‐based clustering analysis

The haplotype‐based clustering analysis was carried out using the SNPs identified from whole‐genome re‐sequencing (WGRS) of accessions from the GABI‐WHEAT panel. This was done by extracting SNPs present within the region (680,561,105–680,579,910) flanking the MTA identified for GPD. Initially, the genotype data were converted to the 0,1,2 format using VCFtools (Danecek et al., 2011) wherein 0 represents homozygous allele and 1 represents heterozygous allele, while 2 represents a homozygous alternative allele. The samples were then clustered using the pheatmap (https://github.com/raivokolde/pheatmap) package from the R statistical environment. The distribution of GPC and GPD content in the clusters was used to identify the cluster with the superior haplotype.

RESULTS

Phenotypic data confirmed a negative relationship between GY and GPC and a decrease in GPC in new cultivars

The multi‐year, multi‐location, phenotypic data were used to study the diversity of GPC and GY in the accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT panel. The study of the distribution of GPC and GY data showed a decrease in GPC and an increase in GY in the recently released cultivars (Figure 1a,c). The percentage of GPC ranged from 9% to 11.5%, while the GY ranged from 70 to 120 h kg/ha in the accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT panel (Table S1; Figure 1b,d). The correlation study using BLUEs of GY and GPC confirmed the negative relationship between GY and GPC (Figure S1) in the studied accessions. Further, we observed about 70% and 80% broad sense heritability in GY and GPC, respectively (Table S3), suggesting its suitability for GWAS. The large diversity and high heritability for GPC and GY were also reported using phenotypic data of accessions from the GABI‐WHEAT panel (Muqaddasi et al., 2020).

Genetic analysis identified similarities and differences between the populations studied

The 11,700 SNPs with less than 30% missing information and ≤10% heterozygosity of the SCAN‐WHEAT panel were used for PCA. We observed no sub‐populations in the accessions (Figure 2a), suggesting accurate merging of genotypic information without having introduced bias. A similar observation was made using the IBS‐based similarity analysis (Figure S2). The IBS analysis also showed a high diversity (an average of 70% similarity) among the accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT panel (Figure S2). We compared the diversity of accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT panel with the winter wheat diversity reported by Schulthess et al. (2022). As expected, the accessions from the two panels exhibited reduced diversity compared to the diversity from the entire winter wheat population (Figure 2b). The accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT and GABI‐WHEAT panels clustered together indicating similarity between the accessions; however, smaller differences between these two panels were identified (Figure 2b).

GWAS identified a major QTL on chromosome 2B

GWAS was carried out independently for accessions from the SCAN‐WHEAT and GABI‐WHEAT panels for the GY, GPC, and GPD traits. The GWAS on the SCAN‐WHEAT panel identified six MTAs for GY and six MTAs for GPC, while a single MTA for GPD was identified (Table S4; Figure 3a). Five of the MTAs for GY were present on separate chr2A, chromosome 2B (chr2B), chr3B, chr5D, and chr7D, while one marker mapped on multiple chromosomes and its position could not be determined. In the case of GPC, chr1A, chr5B, and chr6B harbor single MTAs, and chr2B harbors two, while the marker sequence of one MTA was mapped on multiple chromosomes (Table S4). The single MTA for GPD was located at 680,573,507 bp on chr2B and coincides with an MTA for GPC (Figure 3a; Table S4). The allele‐wise phenotypic distribution of the MTA for GPD showed variations for GPC and GPD, but not for GY (Figure 3b). The accessions with allele “A” (cluster01, Figure 3b; Table S1) had decreased GPC and GPD compared to those with allele “G” (cluster02, Figure 3b; Table S1). Single marker analysis showed that the MTA explains 11.5% and 14.45% of the variation in GPC and GPD, respectively, and has negligible effect on GY in the studied population.

(a) Manhattan plots showing the distribution of p‐values against the physical positions for grain yield (GY), GPC, and GPD; (b) distribution of GY, GPC, and GPD in the accessions from SCAN‐WHEAT clustered based on MTA on chr2B. Accessions from cluster02 showed increased GPC and GPD compared to cluster01.

GWAS analysis using GABI‐WHEAT panel identified a total of two, four, and nine MTAs for GY, GPD, and GPC, respectively (Figure S3; Table S4). The two MTAs for GY were exclusively present on chr6A. For GPD, the MTAs were identified on chr1A, chr2B, and chr7D, while one could not be mapped at unique positions but has homologs on subgenomes chr7A, chr7B, and chr7D. In the case of GPC, five MTAs were mapped on chr2B, most of them clustering around 650 Mb positions (Table S4; Figure S3).

The comparison of the positions of MTAs from the SCAN‐WHEAT and GABI‐WHEAT panels identified common MTAs for GPC and GPD on the long arm of chr2B, indicating the importance of this region in governing protein content in winter wheat (Table S4).

Haplotype analysis showed a negative effect of introgression on GPC

An earlier study reported a ∼100‐Mb introgression from Triticum timopheevii on the long arm of chr2B, ranging from 650 to 750 Mb (Schulthess et al., 2022). The MTAs for GPC and GPD identified in this study overlap with the same region. We carried out extensive analysis using publicly available data (Schulthess et al., 2022) to study the effect of the introgression on GPC and GPD in the winter wheat population. The GBS data were available for 190 out of 372 accessions from the GABI‐WHEAT panel. The coverage‐based clustering analysis grouped the accessions into two main clusters (Figure 4a; Table S2). A total of 75 elite lines from cluster02 were found to carry the introgressed region (Figure 4a; Table S2). The distribution of phenotypic data showed a significant decrease in GPC in elite lines within this cluster (Figure 4b). Further, the haplotype‐based clustering of 20 SNPs identified from the WGRS data and located in the 680,561,105 to 680,579,910 bp region on chr2B clustered the elite lines into three groups (Figure 5a; Table S2). Most of the accessions (90.63%) from cluster02 identified from haplotype analysis were part of cluster02 identified from coverage‐based clustering analysis and had an introgressed segment from T. timopheevii (Table S2). A comparison of phenotypic data confirmed the decrease in GPC in accessions carrying the introgressed region from T. timopheevii (Figure 5b).

DISCUSSION

Maximizing nitrogen use efficiency through effective protein deposition while ensuring nutritional adequacy is a key sustainability goal in breeding. Further, considering the impact of animal farming on the environment, the demand for plant‐based food is at its peak (Santo et al., 2020). While crop diversification is necessary for sustainability, it is also important to continuously improve our existing crops to meet projected future expectations. Wheat, one of the important global crops, fulfills about 19% of the daily protein requirement of the human population worldwide. Increasing GPC in wheat is beneficial in many ways. First, it will enhance the end‐use quality and economic value of wheat grains. Second, it will help to efficiently utilize the supplied N and thereby avoid environmental pollution caused by excess N. Finally, it will further strengthen the position of wheat in the current food chain. Although the GPC is heavily influenced by environmental conditions and agronomic practices, earlier studies also reported the involvement of genetic variation in controlling GPC in wheat (Paina & Gregersen, 2023). Unfortunately, most previous studies have identified QTLs with minor effects and strong environmental specificity, with GPC‐B1 being the only stable QTL identified for controlling GPC in wheat (Uauy et al., 2006). This may be due to the complex genetic architecture of the GPC trait, as well as the limited availability of multi‐location and multi‐year phenotypic data for GPC and GY. In the present study, we performed a GWAS analysis using GPC and GY data collected from 37 different locations continuously for 24 years and identified a significant MTA on chr2B for GPC and GPD in winter wheat population (SCAN‐WHEAT). The results were validated by an independent analysis using another winter wheat population (GABI‐WHEAT). The genetic control of GPC in wheat has been extensively investigated using various populations, including bi‐parental mapping populations, commercial cultivars, and germplasm collections employing both QTL mapping and GWAS approaches (Kumar et al., 2018; Paina & Gregersen, 2023). These studies have identified QTLs distributed on all chromosomes, suggesting that GPC is governed by polygenic control involving numerous QTLs with smaller effect and that only a few QTLs have major effect. In the case of chr2B, major QTLs were identified on both the short and long arm of the chromosome (Blanco et al., 2012; Suprayogi et al., 2009; Terasawa et al., 2016). Moreover, a meta‐QTL analysis integrating genetic maps of 48 different populations identified five meta‐QTLs on chr2B (Saini et al., 2022). Notably, GPC2, a paralog of GPC1, was also identified on the shorter arm of chr2B (Uauy et al., 2006), suggesting involvement of regions from chr2B in governing GPC in wheat. The region identified in the present study is in close proximity to the meta‐QTL MQTL2B.4 (Saini et al., 2022) which is reported to harbor genes encoding protein kinase domains and glycosyl transferase family 14 enzymes. The present MTA explains about 11.5% and 14.45% of the variation in GPC and GPD, respectively, which corresponds to a 0.5% increase in GPC (Figure 3b). Practically, the region is estimated to contribute an additional 50 kg of protein per hectare—without additional nitrogen input—under the field condition where the wheat yield is around 10 t/ha.

Recently, comparative analysis using the genome assemblies of multiple wheat varieties identified a 450‐Mb introgression from Triticum timopheevii on chr2B in cv. LongReach Lancer (Walkowiak et al., 2020). Further, the genome assembly of winter wheat cv. Attraktion identified a 55‐Mb (675–725 Mb) introgression on the long arm of chr2B overlapping with the introgression identified in cv. LongReach Lancer (Kale et al., 2022). This region is reported to harbor powdery mildew resistance gene (Pm6; (Jørgensen & Jensen, 1973). The MTA identified for GPC and GPD in the current study overlaps with this T. timopheevii previously reported introgressed region on chr2B. We carried out comprehensive analysis using publicly available sequencing data and identified a negative effect of the introgression on GPC and GPD. The higher frequency of this region in recent cultivars indicates the active involvement of this region in current breeding programs. These results suggest that while this introgression from T. timopheevii may be beneficial for providing resistance against powdery mildew infection, it also brought inferior allele(s) for GPC, through a mechanism of linkage drag (Gill et al., 2011). A plausible explanation would be that these regions are too diverged from domesticated crop species, making it challenging to break the recombination. Considering the tight linkage between the powdery mildew resistance and GPC loci, advanced genomic techniques such as mutagenesis and CRISPR‐Cas9 should be implemented to further identify and characterize the causal gene(s) related to GPC in wheat.

CONCLUSIONS

The multi‐location, multi‐year phenotypic data and use of advanced genomic approaches allowed us to identify robust MTA for GPC and GPD on chr2B in winter wheat populations. Further, we observed a negative correlation between the introgression from T. timopheevii with GPC and GPD and emphasized the need for new genomic techniques such as targeting‐induced local lesions in genomes (TILLING) and CRISPR‐Cas for the identification and characterization of candidate genes for GPC and GPD in wheat.
