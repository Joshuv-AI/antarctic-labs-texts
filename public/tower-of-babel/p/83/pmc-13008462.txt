A qualitative exploration of the implementation of a participation-focused physical activity intervention according to the family of participation related constructs framework
Authors: Gaela Kilgour, Ngaire Susan Stott, Michael Steele, Brooke Adair, Amy Hogan, Christine Imms
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParticipation-focused physical activity (PA) interventions are increasing in rehabilitation but can be difficult to replicate if the components of what is delivered and what is received are not clearly described. This study evaluated the implementation of a participation-focused PA intervention by mapping programme content and participant experiences to the theoretical framework: Family of Participation Related Constructs (fPRC).MethodsA descriptive process evaluation, embedded in a single-case experimental design study, explored the extent to which the intervention (‘Run Club’) achieved the goal of being participation-focused, by exploring what was designed, delivered and received through each intervention phase: before, after, and 9-months following a twice weekly 12-week intervention. The programme was designed to provide both participation and activity-level intervention elements. The programme elements and experiences of participating adolescents with cerebral palsy and their parents were mapped to the fPRC constructs and transactional elements.ResultsEight adolescents and 12 parents participated. Mapping of the ‘intervention delivered’ indicated that the 24 1-hour sessions of expert coaching in community settings, with family support, and promotion of attendance and involvement at all study phases were designed and delivered as intended. Experiences of ‘programme received’ were mapped across all fPRC constructs and transactions, providing examples of the transactional influences among the person-level constructs (activity competence, sense of self, preferences), the environment and the individuals' attendance and involvement, in the programme and beyond.ConclusionsThe findings demonstrated how a participation-focused intervention approach can be designed and evaluated using the fPRC.

Key PointsThe fPRC provides a useful framework for clinicians, researchers and exercise providers to better define, design and explore what is delivered to, and the experiences of what is received by those taking part in the intervention.Adolescents with cerebral palsy and their parents/carers play an important role in, and can learn from, reviewing their experiences of an intervention, both during and after delivery.The intervention - a targeted participation-focused physical activity intervention - built the adolescents’ running skills, increased their participation attendance and involvement in physical activity, and impacted their sense of self, activity competence and establishment of preferences for physical activity.

Key Points

The fPRC provides a useful framework for clinicians, researchers and exercise providers to better define, design and explore what is delivered to, and the experiences of what is received by those taking part in the intervention.

Adolescents with cerebral palsy and their parents/carers play an important role in, and can learn from, reviewing their experiences of an intervention, both during and after delivery.

The intervention - a targeted participation-focused physical activity intervention - built the adolescents’ running skills, increased their participation attendance and involvement in physical activity, and impacted their sense of self, activity competence and establishment of preferences for physical activity.

Physical activity is a key life situation in which regular participation is known to have immediate and long-term health and well-being benefits [1,2]. Physical activity for children and adolescents has a broad definition and can include play, structured and non-structured exercise, leisure, recreational and sporting activities. Despite the priority placed on regular physical activity, many adolescents lead sedentary lives, with those with disabilities representing a high-risk group for inactivity and poorer health outcomes [3]. High levels of sedentary behaviour in adolescence have been associated with increased adiposity, poorer cardio-metabolic and bone health and reduced sleep duration [4]. Although the benefits of regular participation in physical activity are well known, it remains difficult to change behaviour [5]. Designing and implementing effective and meaningful physical activity interventions for adolescents with disabilities is therefore essential to promote ongoing involvement in physical activity as an adult.

Adolescents with disabilities have identified priority actions to support increasing their physical activity. These actions include ensuring the environment is acceptable, affordable, accessible, has the available resources, and matches the adolescents' preferences and desire for enjoyment [6]. Participation-focused physical activity interventions are well placed to deliver on these priorities. Participation-focused physical activity interventions prioritise and promote attendance in physical activity by focusing on increasing frequency of taking part and/or diversity of physical activities undertaken. This might occur through providing opportunities to ‘have a go’ at different activities in their natural settings. As well, these interventions aim to foster experiences of involvement (engagement, motivation, persistence, affect and social connection in group activities) while attending the physical activity [[7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14]]. In comparison to motor skill based physical activity interventions, participation – attendance and involvement - is the primary aim and outcome measured in participation-focused interventions, as opposed to focusing on and measuring the skills required to do the activity or changes in body functions. Ideally, participation-focused interventions are individually tailored, goal-directed, family-centred, consider environmental modifications and activity adaptations, and involve collaboration with key stakeholders to support effective engagement [10,[14], [15], [16], [17], [18]].

Determining or designing a participation-focused intervention requires a framework to examine implementation elements. Having a framework can also assist a practitioner to know how to be participation-focused in practice and ensure clients are delivered and receive what is intended, thus assisting with the implementation challenge. The Family of Participation Related Constructs (fPRC) is a conceptual framework placing participation at the centre of working with people with a disability and their families [9]. The fPRC defines participation in a life situation (physical activity in this paper) as having two essential elements: attendance (‘being’ in the situation), and involvement (the experience of participation while attending). The fPRC proposes that participation should always be considered within a family of related constructs, because participation is the outcome of transactional exchanges between the person and the context. In the fPRC, there are three person-related constructs (preferences, sense-of-self, activity competence) along with an internal self-regulation construct, two environmental constructs (broader environment, close activity context) and the two participation constructs – attendance and involvement. When participating in physical activity, the premise is that a person must both attend and be involved in the physical activity to develop the intrinsic benefits of preference-based choices, improved sense of self and increased activity competence. Bi-directional transactions are proposed between constructs and are described using verbs to indicate active transactions e.g., complying, engaging, learning, acting. The verbs indicate the direction of influence e.g. a child is choosing to participate in basketball (preference to attend and be involved) or may be complying with non-preferred activities (reverse transaction).

Because the fPRC is a transactional framework, participation can be viewed as either (or both) a means or an outcome of interventions. When participation is seen as the ‘means’ for improving outcomes, the intervention is designed to focus on attendance and involvement (in the desired life situation), with outcomes of interest being changes in the person-level constructs (e.g., activity competence, preferences) or in the context (e.g., changes in others in the situation), or in future participation attendance and/or involvement in the same or broader life situations. When participation is seen as the ‘outcome’ (an ‘ends’ indicating multiple potential endpoints), then the focus of the intervention can be on the environment or context (e.g., modifications, adaptations, inclusiveness), the person (e.g., their skills, self-efficacy), as well as on supporting attendance and involvement in the intervention itself.

Since participation can be either (or both) the means or outcome of interventions, the fPRC framework proposes there are multiple entry points for the design of interventions. This means that it can be complex to design and evaluate interventions intending to be ‘participation-focused’. It can also be difficult to evaluate the extent to which (or the way in which) an intervention described as participation focused meets the intention. In 2020, a systematic review of prevention and management interventions for children with cerebral palsy demonstrated limited availability of evidence about participation-as-means interventions (Novak et al., 2020). In addition, of the 383 identified intervention outcomes, only 3 ​% addressed a combination of activity and participation outcomes. These findings highlight limitations in measurement across attendance and involvement participation constructs as well as the limited therapeutic focus on participation in real world life situations.

There is a growing body of research focused on improving participation in physical activity of young people with disability, but these interventions can be difficult to replicate if the components of what is delivered and what is received are not clearly described. We recently reported the quantitative outcomes and the experience of involvement in a 13-month, participation-focused training programme for adolescents with cerebral palsy [10]. The intervention was a sports coaching intervention, focused on running skills, and described by participants as the “Run Club”. The primary aim was to increase the adolescents’ attendance and involvement in general physical activity through sports coaching (e.g., turn up to sessions and be engaged, motivated and persist when attending) as well as a secondary aim of improving running skills through the specific training delivered (physical activity competency gains). Thus, the intervention delivered both participation as a means (participation in Run Club to build skills, self-efficacy, preference for future participation) and participation as an outcome (structured development of high-level running skills) with the goal of supporting future participation in physical activity.

Using a single-subject research design, we found that the participants (adolescents with cerebral palsy) met their physical activity participation (attendance/involvement) goals as well as their running competency goals following the 12-week community-based programme [10]. However, ongoing gains in participation in physical activity were variable across the following 9-months despite continued physical competency gains. Interestingly, within the embedded qualitative evaluation of the intervention, both adolescents and parents reported that the Run Club benefited them and/or their child in ways beyond their participation goals and physical competency [19].

The purpose of this study was to explore the implementation of this participation-focused physical activity intervention, the Run Club, by mapping programme content (what was designed and delivered) and participant experiences of the intervention (what was received) to the fPRC theoretical framework. We aimed to better understand the ways in which the delivery and receipt of this intervention was participation-focused.

Methods

This article reports secondary analyses of findings from a multiple methods study involving both single subject research design and qualitative approaches to investigate the effect of a sports coaching programme on participation in physical activity [10]. The primary research question for the overall study was ‘Does a 12-week community sports coaching programme improve participation attendance, involvement and physical capacity and performance of children with cerebral palsy GMFCS I-II, and were gains sustained?’

Participants

Eligible participants were those with cerebral palsy, aged 7–18 years who were ambulant and classified at GMFCS level I-II. There was no pre-requisite related to the ability to run. The study was conducted over 13 months (6 weeks baseline; 12 weeks intervention, 40 weeks follow-up, including a period of 12 weeks of COVID lockdown). Eight adolescents (mean age 13 years, 11 months, SD 1.5 years, range 11–16 years; 7 males) took part. Full details of participants and the intervention have been reported previously and are detailed in brief in Supplementary Table 1 [10]. The project had ethical approval from the Health and Disability Ethics Committee of New Zealand (19/STH/22) and Australian Catholic University Human Research Ethics Committee (2019–49R) and was registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (trial identification number ACTRN 12619000126112; universal trial number U1111-1226-8425).

Intervention design

The study was designed to provide an entry point for physical activity participation i.e. have a go and get involved. The intervention aimed to increase participation in home and community physical activities of participants' choice during and following the programme. Participation was framed and assessed using the fPRC key constructs of attendance and involvement [9]. Activity-focus formed the secondary aim, addressing running capacity and performance. The study premise was, if adolescents with cerebral palsy could attend and become involved in a running programme, could this lead to increased home and community physical activity participation in any activity? In addition, could greater participation and new skills learnt during the programme, lead to improve physical competency in running and other high-level skills. Study planning and development took place over 18 months in the context of the first author's doctoral research. The investigator team included health professionals and an individual with lived experience of cerebral palsy, and we engaged with families with lived experience of disability, community physical activity providers, and a parent advisory group. The intervention was modelled from previous research in young adults with traumatic brain injury (Williams & Schache, 2010) and children with cerebral palsy (Gibson et al., 2018). The programme equipment requirements were designed to be low cost, minimal and easily transported to community locations.

Intervention delivery

The intervention was implemented over 24 1-hour sessions, providing task-specific training of running and high-level mobility skills (e.g., hopping, bounding), delivered by an experienced physiotherapist and exercise trainer in a small group setting e.g., maximum 1 trainer: 4 participants. The intervention prioritised participation, promoting attendance and involvement at each session. Individualised, goal-directed physical activity focused on running skills training at the ‘just right’ challenge for each adolescent while maintaining running form. The sessions were delivered in a similar format to an athletics running session with an active warm up, skills and drills using own body, low load plyometric activities, followed by targeted training of the goals and then a cool down. Warm-up and cool down were game based. The ‘cool-down’ phase of group one was held at the same time as the ‘warm-up' phase of group two, so they participated together at this time. The coaching style focused on motor learning strategies, with use of both verbal feedback and visual feedback through videoing.

Study staff and training

GK, a qualified physiotherapist with over 30 years of coaching and physical activity implementation experience developed and delivered the programme. A professional exercise trainer JS, who was a New Zealand ranked athletics champion, athletics coach and had experience working with people with disabilities, was also involved in delivery of the intervention sessions. JS was provided with 3 ​h of training specific to intervention delivery for adolescents with cerebral palsy prior to the start of the programme and attended 12/24 sessions as planned. JG, an experienced paediatric physiotherapist conducted the physical capacity testing. JG was provided with the physical testing protocol and training prior to the first testing session and received a familiarisation session for 1 ​hour. She attended all 34 physical testing sessions.

Data analysis

A descriptive process evaluation was used to analyse what was planned, delivered and experienced. The design and planning of Run Club was described using the TIDieR checklist [20]. Run Club delivery was evaluated and analysed by mapping the delivery notes, personalised diaries and goal records to the fPRC constructs, within baseline, intervention, follow up phases and those delivered within every phase (Supplementary Tables 2-4). For example, the notes for each adolescent provided evidence for number of sessions delivered, the location, who attended and who delivered the session, components of the training session, home exercises provided, adverse events, adolescents level of involvement and a record of goal testing and attainment.

Deductive qualitative analysis was conducted using content from the 38 interviews and supporting sources that related to what was received during Run Club [22]. All interviews were conducted by GK, who was also the interventionalist, using an interpretative description approach [10,19,23]. Interviews were transcribed verbatim, coded and analysed to enable mapping the intervention to the fPRC constructs and the proposed transactional relationships. The transactional relationships are indicated on the fPRC by the bi-directional arrows and verbs i.e., relationship between attendance and involvement to the person-related factors of preferences (verbs: choosing, complying), sense of self (verbs: engaging, perceiving) and activity competence (verbs: acting, learning), relationship between the person-related factors (verbs: interpreting, experiencing), and the relationship of the context and environment to the adolescent and participation (verbs: providing, regulating/responding, influencing). Mapping to all constructs and possible transactions was conducted by GK and discussed throughout with CI.

Strategies for enhancing rigour and trustworthiness

The authors acknowledge the inherent biases of GK in delivering the intervention, interviewing, conducting and analysing the results of the intervention [24]. In this study, we also acknowledge the potential for bias related to roles and prior knowledge and experience of the team members. For example, GK is a physiotherapist, a runner, coach and sportswoman, and other team members having lived experience of cerebral palsy, being the developers of the fPRC, being involved in childhood disability research.

Potential biases were managed by all six authors checking initial coding and using multiple qualitative sources including interviews, journaling, an audit trail and open discussions among the authors and with people outside of the study. Interview transcripts were provided to adolescents and their parents for member checking and feedback helped clarify some mapping transactions. Two checklists were followed to assist with transparency and trustworthiness across all qualitative reporting: Standard of Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) [25] and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Studies (COREQ) [26].

Results

Participants

Eight adolescents with cerebral palsy (GMFCS I n ​= ​5, GMFCS II n ​= ​3; mean age 13 years, 11 months, SD 1.5 years, range 11–16 years; 7 males) attended a median of 23 (range 2–23) of 24 Run Club sessions in Christchurch, New Zealand (Supplementary Table 1). All eight adolescents completed the four physical testing sessions up to six months post intervention. Twelve parents contributed to interviews, four of whom were fathers. Seven adolescent-parent dyads completed all interview sessions; with one dyad unavailable at the nine-month post intervention session due to leaving New Zealand following the COVID lockdown. Interviews took between 15 and 45 ​min. No therapy intervention was received for any adolescent during the 13 months from any provider. The Run Club design addressed attendance through families choosing the day of the week and time for the sessions prior to the intervention beginning. Attendance was also promoted by including a diverse range of activities, drills, games and competitions and combining groups for more social interaction. The Run Club was specifically planned to foster involvement at an individual and group level. Coaching styles, instructions and feedback (e.g., photos, videos, verbal, demonstrations) were planned to optimise adolescent involvement. Using a variety of locations and joining groups for parts of each session (cool-down for one group, warm-up for second group) was designed to increase involvement and promote social connection. Individualised home exercise porgrammes (HEP) and My Physical Activity Charts (personalised diaries) provided a monitoring system of frequency, diversity and duration of physical activity and level of involvement over the 13 months. Intervention delivery

The second study question investigated the extent the intervention was delivered as intended by mapping to the fPRC participation, person, context and environmental-focused targets and constructs as displayed in Table 3 and in Supplementary Tables 2-4 for all study phases. All 24 intervention sessions and 4 physical testing sessions were carried out as planned. During the intervention, significant planning took place to ensure opportunities to be involved were individualised and implementation included relays, races, video feedback, timing and novel activities to enhance motivation, engagement and persistence e.g., competition was highly motivating for some adolescents and undesirable for others. Session delivery notes indicated at least one parent or family member (e.g., grandparent or sibling) attended the Run Club sessions with each adolescent (Supplement Table 1); with active physical participation of family members increasing as the sessions progressed including becoming involved in games, group activities and completing participation-focused running challenges e.g., 1 ​km have-a-go run. Incremental skill progression, using motor learning strategies, based on goal attainment and skill development of physical goals and involvement in new activities designed to challenge is evident e.g., circuits with skills related to goals. Planned progressions were also important for adolescent safety, with no adverse events during the intervention that limited ongoing programme participation. The Run Club was delivered using a multi-strategy approach by facilitating strategies known to work or found to be successful during the intervention for the adolescents. Strategies included those that were known to work (e.g., providing choice, positive peer and parent encouragement, working towards specific, individualised goals) or found to be successful during the intervention for adolescents (e.g., immediate video feedback, allowing time to rest and practice, competing against peers in running races or timed activities). Barriers were removed that hindered participation in the intervention. For example, providing alternatives in activities and equipment (e.g., running drills on the ground vs on a rebounder), changing session timing or group allocation to fit when there were competing demands. A variety of contexts (e.g., parents and siblings joining in for relay races, using rebounders and running ladder one session and hoops, cones and balls at the next) and environments were used to create opportunities for more novel activities (e.g., delivering a session at a local playground and using the slopes, steps, slide, and a native bush area as a cross-country course). Overall, Run Club was implemented as planned: the intervention was free for families, the outlay for equipment was minimal and was easily transportable, and all activities using equipment were fit for purpose (novice level, community use) and found to be safe. Success of the intervention being delivered as a participation-focused approach was highlighted in the delivery notes when a group of children from a birthday party asked to join our session at the local playground.

Discussion

This study explored what was implemented during and beyond the Run Club intervention by mapping to what extent the intervention design was participation-focused (explored using the TIDieR framework), delivered as intended (explored by mapping delivery to the fPRC), and adolescents' and parents’ experiences of receiving the intervention related to the fPRC (qualitative mapping of receipt). The mapping supported the proposal that the intervention was planned, delivered and received in ways that could be mapped to all fPRC constructs supporting its approach as participation-focused. In this study, the intervention was both participation as means (participation in Run Club to build skills, self-efficacy, preference for future participation) and participation as an outcome (promoting attendance and involvement in physical activity at Run Club, home and the community).

The fPRC proposes that transactional relations amongst constructs work as a feedback loop e.g., increased participation leads to gaining skills, improving confidence, learning about and developing preferences, which subsequently lead to increased participation. Receipt of interventions involving physical activity commonly focus on the acting and learning transaction e.g., practice of skills (acting) is hypothesised to build activity competence (learning) with the assumption that this intermediate goal (activity competence) will translate to increased participation in that and other activities. Currently there is little evidence to substantiate this assumption [28,29]. More attention needs to be paid to addressing individual goals and barriers to participation, both environmental and personal [[30], [31], [32]]. Mapping interventions using the fPRC may assist clinicians, researchers, and the child and family, to identify which participation transactions are in focus when planning and delivering interventions, as well as what aspects are a priority for the adolescents and their parents [9].

There are a group of children and adults with disabilities who describe themselves as ‘not being good enough’ to participate in physical activity, which influences their likelihood of attendance and involvement [42,43]. Whilst training the skill of running (activity competence) was a key component of the Run Club, study findings indicate there was also a relationship between the intervention and adolescents' experiencing an increasingly positive sense-of-self with reports of improved confidence (engaging and perceiving). The delivery of the Run Club in a small group format was reported to promote a sense of social connection, comradery, and success with peers seen as ‘similar’ in a world that offers adolescents with disabilities few role models. Mapping of delivery and receipt of the intervention aligned well with ‘quality participation elements’ of “autonomy, belongingness, challenge, engagement, mastery, and meaning associated with participating” ([33], p. 365).

The slow rate of uptake of participation-focused interventions in practice has been targeted as a necessary area to address for future research [16]. This study adds to growing evidence for the effectiveness of participation focused interventions beyond participation outcomes of attendance and involvement [15]. A participation-focused approach moves the intervention goals away from physical competency and capacity alone, to everyday involvement and ‘having a go’ opportunities. The benefits of being active through participating may be improved self-concept and self-esteem to match that of typically developing peers [34] and can be linked to improved mental health [35]. Being active in a context with friends and family, supportive teammates and coaches/trainers and in an inclusive environment can provide the conditions in which physical activity is perceived to be accessible, affordable, accommodating and acceptable (Imms et al.), which enables participation (attendance and involvement) and in turn may improve wellbeing [36,37].

A key relationship that was consistently mapped between fPRC constructs was that between participation and preferences. Shields and Synnot stressed the importance of choice when participating in physical activities, represented by their ‘One size does not fit all … it's about the choice’ theme [38]. Whilst not all components of the intervention were preferred or chosen (e.g., stretching, bounding and hurdle drills, rebounder activities, games, goal directed activities), adolescents' attendance was high, as was their effort, persistence and motivation to try and improve their goals at each session. The Run Club prioritised preferences by enabling adolescents to develop self-selected goals, decide how and when to participate in part or whole goal practice within each session and with whom e.g., peers, a parent, a sibling. Nyquist et al. suggested that there is a “… dynamic relationship between preferences, enjoyment and the sense of self, which is based on previous participation experiences and which will predict future involvement” ([39], p. 2507). The mapping of the receipt of the intervention supported the premise that behaviour change to increase physical activity was likely when people with disabilities have more opportunities for physical activity, are able to participate in their preferred activity, are motivated, and can improve their physical and psychological capabilities [40].

Strengths and limitations

The strength of this work lies in the use of a theoretical framework, the fPRC, to drive practice. The high levels of attendance and reported positive experiences of involvement in the Run Club further support the concept of beginning physical activity interventions with the aim of creating the initial experience of successful participation as a ‘means’ to the outcome of increased and further participation. The detailed exploration and description of what was delivered in this study, could support uptake of the approach into clinical and recreational practice, with translation into other settings or disabilities.

A further strength of this study was the voice of both adolescents and their parents in this research. Like most interventions, the Run Club was complex, and in this case was implemented for an extended duration of 13 months. One adolescent attended only 2/24 sessions and ended data collection early due to significant social strain after the COVID pandemic lockdown. The voice of ‘non attendees’ is rarely captured in research yet provides crucial knowledge to inform gaps in implementation or ways to make the intervention more effective in the future. This participant reported “I lack confidence” (an indicator of sense-of-self) and low preference for the activity “I just didn't want to do it. I'd rather be doing something else”. Her perceptions contrasted with her parent who had a strong desire for her to attend and be involved in both the Run Club and physical activity more generally. Using the interventionist to conduct the interviews may have been a benefit to obtaining this ‘voice’ and understanding the participant experiences. Furthermore, the fPRC mapping allowed such relationships to become clearer.

The mapping and analysis using the fPRC did not provide answers to what ‘ingredients’ were most effective in delivering a participation-focused intervention or enabling adolescents with cerebral palsy to sustain participation in physical activity. Whilst individuals from a range of professions, community groups, and with lived experience of disability, were involved in the intervention development, the research question was not derived from a lived experience perspective nor was the delivery co-designed. Future work to ensure co-design from inception and following implementation pathways specifically aligned to participation would further strengthen the intervention [41]. Finally, no data were mapped to preferred number of trainers or group size and this may be helpful for future interventions. Recruitment of younger children (7–10 years) and young people (17–18 years) with cerebral palsy was also not successful and only one female was recruited, which may impact the translation of findings regarding ‘intervention receipt’.

Implications for practice

Information obtained by mapping interventions to the fPRC may guide ongoing participation-focused intervention development and implementation in clinical and community physical activity settings, which in turn may enhance the participatory experiences. Perhaps more importantly, the mapping may support adolescents and family learning about what they need to effectively participate e.g., being active with friends socially in preferred activities or being active in competitive events with no distraction of friends or family. The fPRC is not age specific, nor condition or disability specific: it can be used for those with or without disabilities and with any form of participation-focused intervention (e.g., developing life-skills, employment or social opportunities). Translation of what was learned in this study, could be supported by the development of an fPRC checklist to support those wishing to consider the related constructs when choosing and implementing participation-focused interventions or seeking participation-focused outcomes. The goal must be to improve the quality and effectiveness of programmes, coaching and services, so that people with disability build preferences for sustained participation in physical activity across the life course.

Conclusions

The findings of this study demonstrated that the Run Club was delivered and received as a structured, planned, participation-focused physical activity intervention for ambulant adolescents with cerebral palsy. Purposefully seeking the reflections, experiences and feedback of adolescents with cerebral palsy and their parents who participated in the Run Club was crucial to learning and understanding how relationships exist between participation, the person, contextual and environmental constructs. Using the fPRC to map delivery and receipt/experiences of the participation-focused physical activity interventions is likely useful and possible to do in practice as well as research.
