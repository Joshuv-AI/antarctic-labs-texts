Enhancing agreement in cardiotocography interpretation between midwives and obstetricians through a rule‐based AI program: A comparative cross‐sectional study
Authors: Sriwipa Kaewsrinual, Nutta Homdee, Thanapa Rekhawasin Pinnington, Supitchaya Surasereewong, Pharuhas Chanprapaph
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate whether a rule‐based artificial intelligence (AI) program can enhance interrater agreement in cardiotocography (CTG) interpretation between nurse–midwives and obstetricians.MethodsCTG data from 50 singleton pregnancies at ≥32 weeks of gestation were used to develop a rule‐based AI program based on National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) 2008 guidelines, with content validity confirmed (item–objective congruence = 0.85). A 22‐item CTG test representing NICHD categories I to III was generated using a local obstetrician consensus reference standard, defined as ≥70% agreement among seven obstetricians. Twenty nurse–midwives interpreted the same CTG tracings twice, before and after AI support, with a 1‐ to 2‐month interval, while obstetricians completed the test once to establish the reference standard. Interrater agreement was evaluated relative to this local expert consensus, rather than neonatal outcomes or an external gold standard, using quadratic weighted kappa for ordinal data and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) for continuous data. Interpretation time and user satisfaction were also assessed.ResultsAI support significantly improved agreement across all ordinal parameters. Agreement on NICHD category interpretation increased from moderate (κ = 0.548) to almost perfect (κ = 0.906, P < 0.001). Improvements were also observed for baseline variability (κ = 0.459 to 0.853), fetal heart rate category (κ = 0.669 to 0.868), prolonged decelerations (κ = 0.719 to 0.963), and acceleration count (κ = 0.482 to 0.723). ICCs for variable and late decelerations improved from poor to good (0.328 to 0.725 and 0.304 to 0.676, respectively), whereas early decelerations remained low. Interpretation time decreased by a mean of 6.7 min with AI support (P < 0.001). Most midwives reported high satisfaction, with 70% strongly agreeing on its clinical utility.ConclusionThis exploratory study suggests that a rule‐based AI program was associated with improved interrater agreement between midwives and obstetricians and reduced CTG interpretation time, with high user satisfaction. These preliminary findings warrant confirmation in larger studies to assess generalizability and to determine whether improved agreement translates into better perinatal outcomes, which were not assessed in this study.

INTRODUCTION

Electronic fetal monitoring (EFM) is widely used in delivery rooms to assess fetal well‐being by generating cardiotocography (CTG) graphs showing fetal heart rate (FHR) and uterine contraction, especially in high‐risk pregnancies. 1 , 2 , 3 However, CTG interpretation remains subjective, with inter‐clinician variability, low interrater reliability, and high false‐positive rates in predicting fetal distress. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 Visual assessment is influenced by bias, fatigue, and clinical experience. 8 , 9 While guidelines such as those from the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD) aim to standardize interpretation, variability persists. 10 , 11

To address this challenge, artificial intelligence (AI) has been applied to CTG analysis. Current approaches include rule‐based systems, machine learning, and deep learning. 12 , 13 Rule‐based AI systems, which are designed based on established clinical guidelines such as NICHD or FIGO (International Federation of Gynecology & Obstetrics), are easier to understand, deliver consistent results, and align well with clinical reasoning. 8 , 12 , 14 These systems are more practical for bedside use, particularly in busy labor wards or where experienced staff are limited. Although AI‐assisted CTG systems may better detect patterns linked to fetal hypoxia, evidence on clinical outcomes such as reduced hypoxia or acidosis remains limited. 7 , 8 , 15 , 16

Midwives are typically the first professionals to assess CTG tracings during labor, and studies have shown considerable variability in their interpretations, particularly among those with less clinical experience. 17 Improving agreement between midwives and obstetricians is key to timely response and effective care. No prior studies have directly compared agreement across roles or with AI support. 12 , 16 Our study addresses this gap by developing a rule‐based AI program based on NICHD (2008) criteria 18 and evaluating its effect on interrater agreement between midwives and obstetricians, as well as on consistency, efficiency, and user satisfaction.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This comparative cross‐sectional study was conducted at Siriraj Hospital, Thailand, between January 2023 and January 2024. Data collection was retrospective, involving retrieval and anonymization of intrapartum CTG tracings from 50 singleton pregnancies ≥32 weeks of gestation. Participant assessment was prospective: obstetricians first generated consensus interpretations to establish a reference standard, after which midwives completed two interpretation rounds (without and with AI support) under predefined study protocols.

All CTG interpretations followed the NICHD 2008 guidelines, which serve as the standard framework for intrapartum fetal surveillance in our institution and most Thai obstetric units. Obstetricians and midwives are routinely trained under this system, and it is fully integrated into our intrapartum documentation. A rule‐based AI program, developed and content‐validated against these guidelines, was used to support midwives during the second (AI‐assisted) interpretation round. Study conduct and reporting followed the Guidelines for Reporting Reliability and Agreement Studies (GRRAS) recommendations.

Participants recruitment

Eligible obstetricians and midwives working in the labor ward at Siriraj Hospital were invited to participate voluntarily. Obstetricians with ≥3 years of labor room experience were invited through departmental announcements, whereas midwives currently working in the labor ward were informed via official letters and coordination with head nurses. Interested participants received detailed study information and provided written informed consent before participation. Participants who withdrew consent or did not complete both AI‐assisted and non–AI‐assisted interpretation rounds were excluded from analysis.

Study objectives

The primary objective was to compare the interrater agreement in CTG interpretation between obstetricians and midwives, with and without AI assistance. The secondary objectives included comparing the time required for CTG interpretation under both conditions (with and without AI) and evaluating midwives' satisfaction with the AI‐assisted program.

Sample size calculation

This study was designed as an exploratory reliability and feasibility assessment rather than a confirmatory trial. Therefore, the sample size aimed to ensure adequate precision for estimating interrater reliability rather than to detect small group differences. To determine the number of tracings required for the main study, a pilot assessment using six CTG tracings interpreted by four obstetricians was conducted. The obstetrician consensus—defined as agreement among at least three of four obstetricians across all key CTG parameters, including baseline, variability, accelerations, decelerations (presence, type, and number), and the overall NICHD category—served as the reference standard, against which the three midwives' interpretations yielded an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of ≈0.40 (poor agreement). ICC was selected for this calculation because it provides a more stable approximation of overall interrater variability when preliminary kappa estimates may be unstable or derived from very small samples. Weighted kappa calculations require sufficiently large and well‐populated contingency tables, which were not feasible in the six‐case pilot set despite coverage of all categories. Based on this estimate, 17 CTG cases were required to detect an ICC of ≥0.70 (good agreement) with a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), 80% power, and a margin of error of ±0.15, consistent with recommended precision‐based approaches for reliability studies.

The inclusion of 20 midwives and seven obstetricians was determined pragmatically according to the number of eligible labor‐ward staff available and willing to participate, with the aim of providing stable reliability estimates.

Study procedures

Study procedures were divided into four main components: (1) development of the rule‐based AI program, (2) validity assessment using the Index of Item–Objective Congruence (IOC), (3) establishment of an obstetrician consensus reference standard, and (4) midwife interpretation performed in two rounds (with and without AI assistance). Details of each component are described below.

Rule‐based AI program development and analysis

Intrapartum CTG tracings from Philips IntelliSpace Perinatal (K.00.2x) were retrospectively collected from 50 singleton pregnancies ≥32 weeks of gestation, admitted for delivery at Siriraj Hospital between January 2023 and January 2024. All patient identifiers were removed to ensure confidentiality. CTG data exported in tab‐separated (.cts) format from Philips IntelliSpace Perinatal were anonymized and provided to the research team for development of a rule‐based AI program based on NICHD 2008 guidelines. Each file included ≥20 min of FHR and uterine contraction signals. A diverse set of tracings representing NICHD categories I to III and key features (baseline variability, accelerations, decelerations) was curated for model development.

The rule‐based AI model was constructed by translating the NICHD 2008 criteria into an operational, stepwise decision algorithm. The model was implemented in Python 3.9 using standard scientific libraries (pandas, numpy, scipy, and matplotlib) for time‐series preprocessing, signal filtering, feature extraction, and event detection. Automated classification of accelerations and decelerations was performed using peak‐detection techniques, and the overall NICHD category was assigned via a hierarchical rule‐based logic incorporating baseline, variability, and deceleration patterns. The program outputs include FHR baseline, variability classification, accelerations, number and type of deceleration, and the overall NICHD category. Results are displayed on a CTG graph with color‐coded overlays (green for accelerations, red for decelerations, blue for baseline). An example AI output display is provided in Figure 1. A detailed description of the operational NICHD‐based rules used for baseline determination, variability classification, acceleration detection, and deceleration typing is provided in Appendix S1 (Rule‐Based AI Algorithm for CTG Interpretation), together with the full algorithm schematic in Figure S1 (Rule‐Based AI Algorithm Flowchart).

Rule‐based AI program validity assessment using the IOC

The IOC, a content‐validity measure ranging from −1 to +1, was used to assess agreement between expert consensus (specialist interpretation) and the instrument (AI program). IOC values ≥0.80 are generally considered to indicate strong validity. Importantly, IOC validation was performed using a separate, independent set of 50 CTG tracings that was not used for AI development. Three maternal‐fetal medicine specialists independently reviewed 50 CTG tracings, covering 254 key parameters. Their consensus was used as the reference, and the AI matched 215 of 254 items, yielding an IOC of 0.85, indicating high agreement between the AI program and specialist interpretation. This level of validity confirmed that this rule‐based AI program was sufficiently reliable for subsequent use in the interobserver agreement assessment.

Obstetrician consensus reference standard

After IOC validation, the researcher team selected a separate, nonoverlapping set of 22 CTG tracings for reference standard development. The tracings were purposefully selected to represent the full spectrum of NICHD categories I to III and to include key CTG patterns defined by the 2008 NICHD guidelines, including FHR baseline, baseline variability, presence and number of accelerations, and the presence, type, and number of decelerations (early, variable, late, and prolonged). Each tracing was derived from continuous intrapartum monitoring, and a standardized 30‐min segment was used for analysis. All participants were instructed to interpret the following CTG parameters according to the 2008 NICHD guidelines: FHR baseline, baseline variability, presence and number of accelerations, presence, type, and number of decelerations (early, variable, late, and prolonged), and the overall NICHD category. Seven obstetricians independently interpreted all 22 CTG tracings without AI assistance to establish the reference standard. For each CTG parameter, consensus was defined as agreement among ≥70% of obstetricians (at least 5 of 7). Although a minimum of 17 tracings meeting this threshold was initially planned, all 22 tracings exceeded the ≥70% agreement criterion. Consequently, no low‐consensus cases were included, and all 22 tracings were retained for subsequent nurse–midwife assessment to maximize data utilization and preserve the representativeness of NICHD categories I to III.

Midwife interpretation with and without AI assistance

Twenty midwives completed CTG interpretation in two rounds: first without AI assistance, then with AI support after an interval of 1 to 2 months. In each round, participants independently interpreted the same set of 22 CTG tracings, and the order of CTG presentation was randomized separately for each round to reduce recall of patterns. During interpretation, midwives assessed the same CTG parameters as obstetricians, including FHR baseline, baseline variability, accelerations, decelerations (early, variable, late, and prolonged), and the overall NICHD category.

In the second round, an AI‐assisted interface was provided, displaying preset outputs for FHR baseline, baseline variability, presence of accelerations, and presence of decelerations (early, late, variable, and prolonged), as well as the NICHD category. Participants were informed that the AI‐generated outputs were based on preset criteria and were encouraged to interpret the tracings as they normally would, without relying solely on the program. The total time required for each round was recorded, and all responses were collected immediately using a structured case record form. No patient randomization was required, as the study focused solely on interobserver interpretation rather than patient allocation. After completing both rounds, midwives completed a questionnaire evaluating the perceived usefulness, ease of use, and overall satisfaction with the AI‐assisted program. The User Satisfaction Questionnaire and the sample CTG Interpretation Form used by participants are available in File S1 (User Satisfaction Questionnaire) and File S2 (CTG Interpretation Form).

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed in accordance with the GRRAS recommendations. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.2.0.2 (IBM), with two‐sided P < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics, and five‐point Likert scales (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree) were used to evaluate satisfaction score. The validity of the AI algorithm was assessed using the IOC. Interrater agreement for ordinal CTG parameters (e.g., baseline variability, FHR category, and prolonged decelerations) was assessed using weighted Cohen κ with quadratic weights, with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported for all estimates. Agreement for continuous parameters (e.g., number of decelerations) was evaluated using the ICC, each accompanied by its 95% CI. Kappa values were interpreted according to the Landis and Koch classification, while ICC values were interpreted using standard thresholds (<0.50 poor, 0.50 to 0.75 moderate, 0.75 to 0.90 good, >0.90 excellent). To compare agreement between the non‐AI and AI‐assisted rounds, weighted κ was calculated separately for each midwife against the obstetrician reference, and mean κ values (with 95% CIs) were compared using paired t‐tests. Because ICCs reflect group‐level agreement rather than per‐rater measures, differences between rounds were evaluated descriptively based on the overlap of their 95% CIs. Differences in interpretation time between non‐AI and AI‐assisted conditions were also assessed using paired t‐tests.

RESULTS

A total of 20 nurse–midwives and seven obstetricians participated in the study. The obstetricians had a mean age of 32.4 ± 1.1 years, while the nurse–midwives had a mean age of 27.4 ± 6.5 years and an average labor ward experience of 59.2 ± 77.1 months. All midwives completed both interpretation rounds, 30 to 58 days apart. Consensus among obstetricians served as the reference interpretation for all analyses.

Interrater agreement

With AI assistance, interrater agreement between midwives and obstetricians improved significantly across all ordinal CTG parameters (Table 1). Agreement on NICHD category increased from κ = 0.548 to κ = 0.906 (P < 0.001), shifting from moderate to almost perfect agreement. Improvements were also observed for baseline variability (κ = 0.459 to 0.853, P < 0.001), FHR category (κ = 0.669 to 0.868, P = 0.002), prolonged decelerations (κ = 0.719 to 0.963, P = 0.015), and acceleration count (κ = 0.482 to 0.723, P = 0.004). For continuous parameters (Table 2), agreement on variable decelerations increased from and ICC of 0.328 (95% CI, 0.209–0.513) to 0.725 (95% CI, 0.601–0.846), and late decelerations from 0.304 (95% CI, 0.191–0.487) to 0.676 (95% CI, 0.544–0.813); in both cases, the nonoverlapping CIs indicate improvements from poor to moderate to good reliability. Agreement on early deceleration remained low, with overlapping CIs and a slight decrease ICC from 0.145 to 0.119. Interpretation time

As shown in Table 3, obstetricians interpreted the 22 CTG tracings in a mean of 16.2 ± 4.4 min. Midwives required 27.9 ± 10.6 min without AI and 21.2 ± 8.0 min with AI assistance. AI support significantly reduced midwives' interpretation time by an average of 6.7 ± 7.4 min (P < 0.001).

User satisfaction

Midwives' five‐point Likert‐scale assessments demonstrated low confidence (35%) and perceived speed (30%) during non‐AI interpretation, both of which improved markedly with AI assistance (≥95%). All participants found the AI display clear and easy to understand, with 95% considering it suitable for practice. Most agreed that adequate CTG knowledge is essential (90%), AI improves accuracy (95%), and all participants supported clinical implementation.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrated that a newly developed rule‐based AI‐assisted CTG interpretation program significantly improved interrater agreement between nurse–midwives and obstetricians. The enhancement was observed across both ordinal and continuous CTG parameters, particularly for the NICHD category, which is central to labor management decisions. 11 , 12 The AI‐assisted program and participant evaluation were both based on the NICHD 2008 framework, which remains the standard classification used in our institution and many Asian obstetric centers. These findings highlight the potential of rule‐based AI to address the well‐documented problem of interobserver variability in CTG interpretation 7 , 8 , 13 , 19 and to support more consistent decision‐making in intrapartum care. It should also be noted that the observed difference in agreement between midwives and obstetricians may partly reflect the hierarchical workflow in labor wards, where midwives typically perform the first‐line assessment and obstetricians become involved as a second‐line review. This structure may introduce selection bias, as obstetricians are more likely to encounter tracings already suspected to be abnormal, and should therefore be considered when interpreting the comparative results.

Given the exploratory design and the sample size being powered only to detect large differences in agreement, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary rather than definitive and warrant confirmation in larger studies.

Beyond accuracy, AI assistance also reduced interpretation time, indicating potential workflow benefits in high‐volume clinical settings. However, midwives required slightly longer interpretation time than obstetricians despite AI support, likely because they were instructed to use their own clinical judgment and treat the AI output only as a supportive guide, leading to a more deliberate interpretation process. Interpretation time was included to assess the cognitive and workflow impact of AI assistance rather than to reflect real clinical urgency. Because the study used standardized test tracings, the recorded time does not represent the speed required in actual intrapartum care. Instead, it reflects how efficiently clinicians interact with the AI system and how well the tool supports usability and workflow integration. To date, no previous studies have quantified or compared CTG interpretation time between midwives and obstetricians, highlighting the need for further research on this aspect of human–AI interaction in fetal monitoring. The majority of midwives expressed high satisfaction with the program, reporting that its interface was clear and easy to interpret. This supports the program's practicality and acceptability for routine use, especially in settings with limited staffing or varying experience levels.

Unlike earlier computerized systems such as PeriCALM Patterns, which achieved only modest agreement rates between experienced clinicians and software, 5 , 20 the present rule‐based program was deliberately designed for clinical clarity and interpretability. Its color‐coded overlays and concise summaries emphasize key parameters without overwhelming users with data, aligning more closely with clinical reasoning than with black‐box machine‐learning models. 1 , 12 , 13 While prior randomized trials have primarily focused on neonatal outcomes, 4 , 8 , 16 , 21 our work focuses on consistency of interpretation between different professional groups, a factor essential for timely and coordinated intrapartum care.

This study has several strengths. It employed real intrapartum CTG tracings interpreted by labor ward staff, and obstetrician consensus served as a practical reference standard that reflects real‐world clinical judgment. The rule‐based approach is transparent, interpretable, and consistent with established NICHD 2008 criteria, achieving acceptable content validity (IOC = 0.85) without requiring large training data sets. 1 , 12 , 13 This design promotes clinical adoption and safer task‐sharing, particularly in resource‐constrained environments.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, interrater agreement was evaluated against a local obstetrician consensus reference standard rather than an external gold standard or neonatal outcomes, and therefore reflects concordance with experienced clinicians' interpretations rather than validated diagnostic accuracy. Second, the CTG tracings were preselected and all exceeded a predefined ≥70% agreement threshold among obstetricians, which may bias the data set toward clearer or more typical patterns and potentially overestimate performance compared with routine clinical practice, where signal artifacts and ambiguous tracings are common. 13 Third, the study included a relatively small number of CTG tracings and participants, limiting statistical power and generalizability. Fourth, the before–after study design reused identical CTG cases for both interpretation rounds; although a 1‐ to 2‐month washout period and randomization of case order were applied, some degree of learning or recall bias cannot be excluded, particularly for distinctive tracings. Finally, because the CTG data set was anonymized and not linked to neonatal or perinatal outcomes, the study could not assess whether improved agreement was associated with differences in fetal acid–base status or clinical outcomes. These limitations underscore the exploratory nature of the findings and highlight the need for larger, prospective studies incorporating more diverse tracings, independent reference standards, and clinically relevant neonatal outcomes.

The extremely low ICC for early decelerations should be interpreted with caution, as these events were rare in our data set, limiting variability, ICC stability, and algorithm calibration. Their inherently ambiguous morphology further complicates reliable identification. The AI system identified more early decelerations than human reviewers, suggesting increased sensitivity in the setting of limited true examples. Generalizability remains uncertain, particularly in clinical settings that apply FIGO or physiology‐based CTG interpretation frameworks rather than NICHD 2008 guidelines.

Although AI‐assisted CTG systems may better detect patterns associated with fetal hypoxia, 13 evidence linking such systems to improvements in objective neonatal outcomes—such as cord pH, base deficit, or Apgar scores—remains limited, 7 and previous studies have shown that AI has not demonstrated statistically significant improvements in neonatal outcomes compared with standard care. 21 Assessing clinical outcomes is inherently challenging because neonatal distress arises from multiple intrapartum and fetal factors, and the true incidence of hypoxia or acidosis is relatively low. Accordingly, the enhanced agreement demonstrated in this study reflects concordance with a local experienced clinicians reference standard rather than validated improvement in diagnostic accuracy. Linking AI‐enhanced interpretation with meaningful perinatal outcomes therefore remains an essential next step for future research.

Future studies should evaluate applicability among general practitioners, midwives not routinely assigned to labor wards, and medical students. AI‐enhanced interpretation to improve perinatal outcomes remains the essential next step for future research with integration into nonstress test interpretation and incorporation of short‐term variability, a key marker of fetal hypoxia, potentially enhancing its clinical utility, diagnostic sensitivity, and predictive value. Expanding the data set to encompass more complex tracings will be critical for ensuring robustness and generalizability among varied clinical scenarios.

For clinical implementation, the model could operate as a Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) integrated with existing fetal monitoring platforms. The algorithm would run on a central hospital server rather than requiring additional bedside hardware. Implementation would need to comply with key medical software standards, including International Electrotechnical Commission (IEC) 62 304 for software life‐cycle processes and International Organization for Standardization (ISO) 14 971 for risk management. Although initial costs for system integration, regulatory validation, and ongoing maintenance may be substantial, these investments may be justified by the potential to provide consistent CTG interpretation support in settings with limited specialist availability.

CONCLUSION

This study suggests that a rule‐based AI program was associated with improved interrater agreement between midwives and obstetricians in CTG interpretation. The AI‐assisted approach aligned midwife assessments more closely with experienced clinicians' consensus and reduced interpretation time, with midwives reporting high usability and satisfaction. However, given the exploratory design and limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Importantly, no perinatal or neonatal clinical outcomes were assessed in this study. Larger, confirmatory studies are needed to validate the generalizability of the results and to determine whether improved agreement translates into better perinatal outcomes.
