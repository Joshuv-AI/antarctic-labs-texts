Heterologous expression of hagfish thread proteins EsTKα and EsTKγ in Pichia pastoris and in vitro self-assembly into an ordered fibrous network
Authors: Zhiwen Lin, Yuxuan Sun, Hao Shi, Wenlong Zhu, Hui Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Eptatretus stoutii thread keratin α and γ (EsTKα and EsTKγ) are essential structural components conferring hagfish slime with exceptional strength and toughness. These proteins exhibit exceptional mechanical properties, rendering them promising candidates for biomimetic material applications. However, the ability of conventional prokaryotic expression systems to produce these proteins is notably limited, as they often fail to achieve proper folding and post-translational modifications. In this study, Pichia pastoris strains with various phenotypic characteristics were screened, and key parameters, including signal peptides and fermentation conditions, were systematically optimized. The SMD1168H strain was identified as an optimal host for recombinant protein production. Following methanol induction in a 5-L fermentor, extracellular expression yields reached 350.0 mg/L for EsTKα and 385.0 mg/L for EsTKγ. The conformational changes in EsTKα and EsTKγ during in vitro self-assembly were characterized using multiple analytical techniques, which confirmed successful self-assembly into an ordered fibrous network. This study reports the successful heterologous expression of EsTKα and EsTKγ in the eukaryotic host P. pastoris, highlighting the potential of P. pastoris as a robust platform for the scalable production of these structurally complex proteins.

Introduction

Hagfish slime thread proteins belong to the intermediate filament protein family and are characterized by a central α-helical domain [1]. These proteins constitute the primary architectural framework that confers slime threads with remarkable mechanical strength and toughness [2]. Because of their unique structural configuration, these proteins have significant potential as sustainable biomaterials. Importantly, by controlling the secondary structure transitions of the proteins, materials derived from these proteins can have precisely engineered mechanical properties [3,4]. Consequently, these protein-based materials have potential in diverse applications, including medical uses such as scaffolds for tendon repair [5] and the development of environmentally friendly biomimetic materials [6].

Intermediate filaments in hagfish slime are composed of two distinct subunits: thread keratin α and thread keratin γ (designated EsTKα and EsTKγ from Eptatretus stoutii, respectively). These subunits intrinsically self-assemble in vitro, initially forming heterodimeric coiled-coil complexes, which further organize into higher-order nanofibers and ultimately into a macroscopic, continuous fibrous network. The amino acid sequences of EsTKα and EsTKγ were first reported and deposited by Koch et al., thereby providing a critical foundation for subsequent heterologous expression studies [7]. In 2015, Fu et al. achieved the first heterologous expression of EsTKα and EsTKγ in Escherichia coli, but the yield was only 10–20 mg/L. [8]. They successfully applied gradient dialysis to induce in vitro self-assembly with a conformational transition from α-helices to β-sheets under shear stress. More recently, Sun et al. reported yields of 189.07 mg/L for EsTKα and 194.65 mg/L for EsTKγ in E. coli through a simplified, single-step assembly method based on the self-assembly properties of the proteins [9]. The heterologous expression of hagfish slime thread proteins has predominantly been performed in E. coli systems. However, the complex structure of these proteins often results in low soluble yields, misfolding, and inclusion body formation, which necessitate cumbersome denaturation and refolding procedures. These challenges pose significant obstacles to the large-scale production and engineering applications of hagfish slime thread proteins.

Since its initial use in the early 1970s, the methylotrophic yeast P. pastoris has become a common host for heterologous protein production due to its strong methanol-inducible AOX1 promoter, efficient eukaryotic post-translational modification machinery, and genetically tractable background [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Although E. coli is easily genetically manipulated and undergoes rapid fermentation, its inability to perform sophisticated protein folding and post-translational modifications can cause improper folding, protein inactivation, and inclusion body formation when expressing complex eukaryotic proteins, such as hagfish thread keratins. In addition to wild-type strains, a diverse array of engineered P. pastoris strains has been developed to meet the specific requirements of heterologous protein production. These include protease-deficient strains (e.g., SMD1168) that enhance protein stability and auxotrophic strains (e.g., histidine-deficient) that enable efficient selection, thereby expanding the system's versatility [[14], [15], [16], [17]]. By selecting appropriate expression elements, including promoters and signal peptides, the yield and quality of heterologous protein expression in P. pastoris can be optimized [[18], [19], [20]]. Compared to prokaryotic systems such as E. coli, P. pastoris provides higher fidelity post-translational modifications, including glycosylation and disulfide bond formation [21]. This enables the production of recombinant proteins with native-like conformations and full biological activity, which is particularly useful for the functional expression of complex biomolecules such as hagfish thread proteins.

In this study, critical expression parameters that maximized the secretory production of EsTKα and EsTKγ in P. pastoris were systemically optimized, including codon usage bias, signal peptide selection, and host strain engineering. Subsequently, inducer concentration and feed were meticulously refined to achieve high-cell-density cultivation. The recombinant proteins were purified and underwent in vitro self-assembly. The assembly mechanism was characterized using a multidisciplinary approach to confirm the appropriate protein conformation. The results demonstrated the successful heterologous expression and in vitro assembly of EsTKα and EsTKγ in P. pastoris. This study established a scalable platform for producing these complex proteins.

Materials and methods

Strains, reagents, and culture medium

E. coli strain TOP10 from Beyotime (Shanghai, China) was used for plasmid construction and propagation. Several P. pastoris strains, including X33, GS115, SMD1168H, and KM71 (Beyotime), were evaluated as expression hosts for EsTKα and EsTKγ. Recombinant strains TOP10-EsTKα and TOP10-EsTKγ, constructed and preserved in our laboratory, were used to construct EsTKα and EsTKγ expression plasmids (UniProtKB: Q90501 and Q90502 [7,22]). Restriction endonucleases and a DNA ligation kit were obtained from Vazyme Biotech (Nanjing, China). Zeocin (antibiotic) was purchased from Bioroyee (Beijing, China). PrimeSTAR Max DNA polymerase was acquired from TaKaRa Biotechnology Co. (Dalian, China). E. coli recombinant strains were cultured at 37 °C in Luria–Bertani (LB) or low-salt LB medium supplemented with zeocin at appropriate concentrations. P. pastoris recombinant strains were cultivated at 30 °C in yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD) medium containing an appropriate antibiotic. The seed culture was grown in BMGY medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 1.34% YNB, 1% glycerol, 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 6.0). For induction, BMMY medium (1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 1.34% YNB, 1% methanol, 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 6.0) was used as the fermentation medium [23]. High-cell-density fed-batch fermentations were performed in a 5-L bioreactor, with BMGY as the batch medium.

Construction of recombinant strains

The recombinant plasmids were linearized by the appropriate restriction enzymes and introduced into four P. pastoris strains (X33, GS115, SMD1168H, and KM71) via electroporation [27]. The plasmids, each carrying a gene encoding EsTKα or EsTKγ fused to a specific signal peptide, were integrated into the SMD1168H genome to generate recombinant strains capable of secreting the target proteins. YPDZ plates supplemented with 100 μg/mL zeocin were used to screen for strains with optimal expression and potentially multiple integrants [27]. After cultivating at 30 °C for approximately 3 days, the successful genomic integration of the expression cassette was verified by colony PCR using the primers EsTKP-F, EsTKαP-R, and EsTKγP-R.

Shake flask cultures

After successfully constructing and validating the recombinant strains expressing EsTKα and EsTKγ, positive transformants were selected and inoculated into 50 mL of BMGY medium and cultured at 30 °C and 200 rpm for approximately 24 h. Cells were then harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in 50 mL of BMMY medium. The resuspended cells were incubated in BMMY medium at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 5 days. Each day, the cultures were supplemented with 1% methanol, and 1-mL samples were collected to measure the culture density and protein expression [23]. The influence of the inducer concentration on the secretory efficiency of the cells was systematically evaluated by supplementing methanol to final concentrations ranging from 0% to 3% (v/v) in 0.5% increments. Furthermore, to assess the effect of a mixed carbon source feeding strategy on protein secretion and to potentially reduce the metabolic burden, a sterile sorbitol solution (1 M) and 1.5% methanol were supplemented at different volume ratios (sorbitol to methanol: 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, 1:5, 1:6).

Fed-batch fermentation

Fed-batch fermentations were performed in a 5-L fermentor. First, a single colony was inoculated into 10 mL of YPD medium and cultivated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 24 h. Then, 4% of the seed culture was transferred into 200 mL of YPD and cultured at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 20 h. Finally, 8% of the second seed culture was used to inoculate BMGY (4% glycerol) medium in a 5-L fermentor. The initial temperature was maintained at 30 °C. After the glycerol was depleted, the culture underwent a 2-h carbon starvation. An induction phase followed with a methanol feed, which was controlled based on a dissolved oxygen-stat (DO-stat) strategy at 30% (25 °C, 1.5 vvm, 500 rpm, and pH 6.0 by adding 25% (v/v) ammonia solution). Fermentation broth samples were collected every 24 h to measure dry cell weight (DCW) and protein expression. Briefly, a 5-mL sample of the fermentation broth was placed in a pre-weighed tube and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min. The tube was dried at 105 °C and weighed after the water had completely evaporated [28]. The DCW was calculated and reported as g/L (g DCW/L of culture).

Western blot analysis

The culture supernatant was collected and concentrated using a 30-kDa ultrafiltration device at 4000 rpm and 4 °C for 20 min and then diluted to half its original volume with 1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.0). The proteins in the supernatant were separated by SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto a PVDF membrane. The membrane was incubated with mouse anti-polyhistidine antibody (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Sino Biological, Beijing, China) for Western blot analysis [3]. Protein bands were visualized using a Pro-light HRP chemiluminescent kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, China), and densitometric analysis was performed using a ChemiDoc XRS (Bio-Rad, USA) and Image Lab software [3].

Isolation of the target proteins and in vitro assembly

To isolate and purify the target proteins from the fermentation supernatant for subsequent structural and self-assembly analyses, a sequential precipitation method combining isoelectric point (pI) precipitation and ammonium sulfate was used. First, 120 h post-induction, 20 mL of fermentation broth was collected and centrifuged at 8000 rpm and 4 °C for 20 min and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was concentrated using a 30-kDa ultrafiltration device at 4000 rpm and 4 °C for 20 min and then diluted to the original volume with a 1 M Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.0). The dilution and concentration processes were repeated to remove residual medium components. The pH was then adjusted to its pI using dilute HCl or NaOH. Under constant stirring in an ice bath (0–4 °C), finely ground ammonium sulfate was gradually added to the suspension to 30% saturation [29]. Precipitates were collected by high-speed centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min, 4 °C), and the supernatant was removed. The flocculent precipitate was resuspended in a Tris-HCl buffer (pH 6.0) to redissolve the target protein and repeat the sequential precipitation procedure. The final flocculent precipitate was resuspended in a minimal volume of Tris-HCl buffer, transferred to a tube, and lyophilized to obtain a dry powder for subsequent analysis. To initiate in vitro self-assembly, dilute solutions (1 mg/mL in a 0.1 M Tris-HCl buffer, pH 9.0) of purified EsTKα and EsTKγ were combined at a 1:1 ratio [9]. The assembled mixture was concentrated using a 30-kDa ultrafiltration device and then lyophilized to obtain assembled protein powder.

Characterization of the target proteins and assembled protein

The microstructure of the freeze-dried assembled protein gel was analyzed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi SU1510). A specified quantity of freeze-dried protein gel was mounted on a sample stage using a conductive adhesive and coated by gold sputtering. The samples were visualized using SEM at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV. The UV-Vis absorption spectra of the proteins were recorded using an ONLAB EU-2600 spectrometer, scanning from 180 to 500 nm. The individually dialyzed EsTKα and EsTKγ proteins and the assembled product were diluted to 1.0 mg/mL in a buffer containing 2 mM Tris-HCl and 1 mM DTT. Triplicate measurements were collected from 190 to 260 nm at 1-nm intervals on a JASCO J-1500 circular dichroism (CD) spectrometer. The CD spectral data were pre-processed, including averaging and smoothing, and then analyzed by Spectra Manager software in the 190–260 nm region. The secondary structure of the assembled proteins was evaluated via Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) using the KBr pellet method. Lyophilized protein was snap frozen in liquid nitrogen and compressed. Absorbance spectra were collected in the 400–4000 cm−1 range (Nicolet 6700) by performing 64 scans at 25 °C with a resolution of 4 cm−1. Each sample was measured three times, and spectra in the 1600–1700 cm−1 region underwent baseline correction, Gaussian deconvolution, and second derivative and peak-fitting analyses using Peakfit software [9].

Results and discussion

Heterologous expression of the EsTKα and EsTKγ genes in the X33 strain

The wild-type strains X33 and GS115 are among the most commonly used P. pastoris strains for protein expression. The AOX1 promoter and Saccharomyces cerevisiae α-mating factor (α-MF) signal peptide are typically used for their efficient secretion of recombinant proteins into the culture supernatant [30,31].

The amino acid sequences of EsTKα and EsTKγ were retrieved from the UniProt database. The structural characteristics and physicochemical properties of EsTKα and EsTKγ were predicted and analyzed using a suite of computational tools. The three-dimensional structures of EsTKα and EsTKγ were predicted using AlphaFold 3, as shown in Fig. 1A and B. The models predicted a predominance of α-helical domains and random coils, with a notably low content of β-sheet elements (Fig. 1C and D). This α-helix-rich content is characteristic of intermediate filament family proteins. Models of both the individual protein subunits and of their assembled complex revealed large regions that were predicted to be disordered and solvent-exposed, rendering them potential targets for protease attack. These regions likely corresponded to flexible domains (e.g., loop regions) or areas for which a reliable homologous template or evolutionary information was not available. Excessively disordered regions generate a loose protein structure, reducing resistance to proteases. Furthermore, interactions between disordered regions can also interfere with protein folding and assembly, compromising protein stability [[32], [33], [34]]. The presence of disordered regions may be one of the reasons why EsTKα and EsTKγ readily formed inclusion bodies in E. coli and were prone to degradation in P. pastoris. Notably, the α-helical domains of EsTKα and EsTKγ were predicted to intertwine and form a pronounced superhelical coiled-coil architecture, a structural motif essential for the mechanical integrity of intermediate filaments. A comprehensive in silico analysis of the EsTKα and EsTKγ protein subunits was conducted using ProtParam, DeepTMHMM, NetPhos, and NetPhosYeast. The results are summarized in Table S4. Optimization of EsTKα and EsTKγ gene expression

While the X33 strain combined with the pPICZαA vector is a widely used and generally effective system for heterologous protein production [27,28], it may not be the optimal solution for every expression scenario, particularly with proteins that present specific challenges, such as proteolytic degradation susceptibility. The conditions associated with high-cell-density fermentation can exacerbate the inherent susceptibility of recombinant proteins to proteolytic degradation, often leading to the rapid cleavage of the target protein by extracellular or vacuolar proteases after secretion. The SMD1168H (his4 pep4) strain, engineered by deleting the prb1 gene encoding protease B, has been reported to significantly mitigate extracellular proteolysis by reducing the activity of key vacuolar proteases, thereby enhancing the stability and yield of recombinant proteins susceptible to proteolytic degradation [18]. The choice of a signal peptide is also a critical factor governing the heterologous protein secretion efficiency and subsequent yield in P. pastoris. For example, Liang et al. used SignalP 4.0 to predict signal peptides (Scw, Dse, and Exg) to successfully express EGFP and Candida antarctica lipase B (CALB) [36]. To enhance the secretory expression of the target proteins EsTKα and EsTKγ, sequential optimization was applied. First, the expression host was optimized. Next, a panel of heterologous signal peptides was screened using the optimized host to identify the most effective combination for heterologous protein secretion (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Recombinant strain construction process. To identify the optimal host strain for heterologous protein expression, four distinct P. pastoris strains, X33, SMD1168H, GS115, and KM71, were systematically evaluated. The four host strains were individually transformed with the previously constructed shuttle vectors, pPICZαA-EsTKα and pPICZαA-EsTKγ, via electroporation. A growth curve analysis indicated that the different host strains exhibited similar proliferation trends for both EsTKα and EsTKγ (Fig. 4A and B). Notably, the X33 and SMD1168H strains accumulated significantly more biomass than the GS115 and KM71 transformants, indicating more robust adaptation to the induction conditions. An SDS-PAGE analysis of the culture supernatant confirmed that the target proteins were successfully expressed and secreted by the X33, SMD1168H, and GS115 strains (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, a 42.0 kDa band detected by SDS-PAGE corresponded to a band of identical molecular weight observed in the Western blot analysis. This band likely represented a proteolytic fragment. A more accurate identification of the protein degradation products required mass spectrometry and other analytical techniques. The X33 and SMD1168H strains yielded comparable levels of the target protein, but the SMD1168H strain produced fewer lower molecular weight proteolytic degradation bands. In contrast, the GS115 strain exhibited only minimal protein expression. The KM71 strain (MutS phenotype [21]) failed to produce detectable levels of the target proteins, which was consistent with its impaired methanol metabolism that restricts protein expression, particularly expression of large or complex proteins. The wild-type X33 strain produced 52.8 mg/L of EsTKα and 47.1 mg/L of EsTKγ; under identical fermentation conditions, the SMD1168H strain yielded 51.75 mg/L of EsTKα and 43.59 mg/L of EsTKγ (Fig. 4D). With a secretion yield generally comparable to that of X33 but with superior proteolytic stability, the SMD1168H strain was selected for subsequent studies. To further enhance the secretory efficiency, 12 endogenous and heterologous signal peptides, including α-MF, were screened on the SMD1168H background (Fig. S1). The preOst1-pro-α-MF hybrid signal peptide was identified as the most effective for EsTKα secretion, achieving a yield of 72.76 mg/L. The PhO5 signal peptide yielded the highest production of EsTKγ at 69.63 mg/L (Fig. 5A). Optimization of fermentation conditions and scale-up in fermentors

In the methylotrophic yeast P. pastoris expression system, the transcriptional activity of the AOX1 promoter is tightly regulated by the concentration of methanol as an inducer, which directly governs the efficiency of recombinant protein production. However, supra-optimal methanol concentrations can also cause cytoxicity in host cells. This toxicity ultimately impairs protein synthesis and reduces cell viability [37]. Co-feeding strategies in which methanol is supplemented with an auxiliary carbon source can reduce cytotoxic metabolite accumulation by modulating central carbon metabolism, thereby maintaining robust cell growth and viability during the induction phase [38].

After determining the optimized host strain and signal peptides in an initial shake-flask screening, recombinant strains SMD1168H-preOst1-pro-α-MF-EsTKα and SMD1168H-PhO5-EsTKγ underwent further optimization of the methanol induction concentration by incrementally increasing the methanol concentration from 0% to 3.5% (v/v). Both the OD600 and the extracellular yield of the target proteins were maximized at an optimal methanol concentration of 1.5% (v/v) after 120 h of induction (Fig. 5B). At this optimized methanol induction level, the extracellular yields of EsTKα and EsTKγ reached 115.3 mg/L and 109.9 mg/L, respectively, which were significant increases of 58.5% for EsTKα and 57.9% for EsTKγ. In a parallel set of experiments, the effects of carbon source co-feeding on process stability and protein yield were evaluated. Methanol was supplemented with sorbitol at varying volume ratios (sorbitol to methanol: 1:1, 1:2, 1:3, 1:4, 1:5, 1:6) during the induction phase. When the sorbitol-to-methanol ratio increased from 0:1 (methanol alone) to 1:3, the secretory levels of both EsTKα and EsTKγ were comparable to those achieved with methanol as the only carbon source (Fig. S2). However, increasing the sorbitol proportion beyond 1:3 reduced the levels of both targets (Fig. S2). This decline in productivity was likely due to an imbalance in carbon catabolite regulation. A high sorbitol ratio may repress the methanol metabolism pathway by diverting cellular resources toward sorbitol utilization. The production process was then scaled up to a high-cell-density fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor. As shown in Fig. 5C and D, the initial glycerol batch phase finished at approximately 20.5 h. After a brief carbon source starvation period, a continuous methanol induction phase was initiated to induce recombinant protein expression. AOX1-promoter-driven expression of EsTKα and EsTKγ was successfully induced by methanol, with final extracellular protein yields of 350.0 mg/L for EsTKα and 385.0 mg/L for EsTKγ (Fig. 5C and D). This demonstrated successful scale-up to a production process. Compared to the E. coli expression system, in which EsTKα and EsTKγ levels were typically within the range of 150–200 mg/L, the protein levels in P. pastoris were enhanced.

Crude separation and in vitro assembly of EsTKα and EsTKγ proteins

Ammonium sulfate precipitation and pI precipitation are established protein purification methods that exploit differences in protein solubility based on their physicochemical properties to effect separation. Their sequential application, typically with pI precipitation used first to remove bulk impurities followed by ammonium sulfate fractionation for fine purification, provides an efficient and specific purification process. Here, this pretreatment process effectively removed medium components, including YNB, thereby preventing their co-precipitation with the target protein and ensuring accurate downstream characterization. The pH was adjusted to the pI and ammonium sulfate was added gradually, producing an abundance of flocculent precipitates. The final purified precipitates were collected by centrifugation, frozen at −80 °C, and lyophilized to obtain a dry powder of the crude protein for subsequent analyses. The resulting lyophilized powders of EsTKα and EsTKγ exhibited a fluffy and flocculent physical morphology, which is characteristic of many fibrous or filamentous proteins [3]. A SEM analysis (Fig. S3) revealed that both proteins exhibited a three-dimensional morphology with linear, filamentous structures and a degree of inherent disorder [10].

To initiate in vitro self-assembly of the heterodimers, the purified EsTKα and EsTKγ proteins were combined at an equimolar ratio (1:1). The assembled protein products underwent a morphological analysis using SEM. SEM micrographs (Fig. 6) revealed that the proteins self-organized into distinct supramolecular structures following a single-step ultrafiltration-assisted assembly process. Specifically, an extensive network-like architecture (Fig. 6A and B) and well-defined filamentous assemblies (Fig. 6C and D) were observed. This type of morphology is characteristic of linear protein-based biomaterials and confirmed the successful in vitro self-assembly of EsTKα and EsTKγ into complex structures. Following the morphological analysis, the individual protein subunits (EsTKα and EsTKγ) and their assembled complex were characterized using UV-Vis spectroscopy to evaluate their spectroscopic properties and sample purity. UV-Vis absorption spectroscopy (Fig. 7A) showed that EsTKα, EsTKγ, and the assembled complex exhibited distinct absorption profiles. The secondary structures of the protein subunits (EsTKα and EsTKγ) and the in vitro assembled complex were comparatively analyzed using CD and FT-IR spectroscopy to explain the conformational changes induced by the self-assembly process. As shown in Fig. 7B, the CD spectra of EsTKα, EsTKγ, and the assembled complex showed distinct spectral signatures. The individual EsTKα and EsTKγ subunits exhibited strong negative peaks at 198.8 nm and 196.9 nm, respectively. These spectral features were characteristic of the π→π∗ electronic transitions associated with the peptide bond chromophore and indicated a predominantly random coil conformation [40] of both monomeric proteins. In contrast, the CD spectrum of the assembled complex was characterized by a significant positive peak at 191.4 nm and weaker negative peaks at 210.8 nm and 221.7 nm. This pattern was consistent with the signature of a well-defined α-helical secondary structure, particularly the double minima at ∼208 nm and ∼222 nm [40]. Collectively, the CD spectral data demonstrated a pronounced conformational transition from a disordered, random coil state of the individual subunits to an ordered, predominantly α-helical architecture of the assembled complex, thereby validating successful structural ordering through the in vitro self-assembly process. To complement the CD analysis and more quantitatively assess the secondary structure, an FT-IR spectroscopy analysis was performed. The amide I band (approximately 1600–1700 cm−1), which arises primarily from CO stretching vibrations of a peptide backbone, is highly sensitive to secondary structure; and here, it served as the primary analytical target [41]. The FT-IR spectrum of the assembled protein (Fig. 7C) exhibited a well-defined amide I band. A second derivative analysis and spectral deconvolution of the band resolved the overlapping contributions of the different secondary structural elements. The maximum absorbance of the amide I band was observed at 1654.62 cm−1, a wavelength region typically associated with α-helical structures. A quantitative deconvolution of the amide I band was performed to determine the relative percentages of various secondary structure elements. The quantitative analysis revealed a secondary structure composition of 19.48% random coils, 51.97% α-helices, 18.76% β-sheets, and 9.79% β-turns, confirming that α-helices were the predominant conformation (constituting over 50% of the structure) of the assembled complex (Fig. 7D). In summary, the complementary CD and FT-IR analyses provided evidence that EsTKα and EsTKγ conformationally rearranged upon in vitro assembly. The transition from a disordered state to a structured complex dominated by α-helices demonstrated the efficacy of the biomimetic self-assembly process used in this study. The mechanical properties and biological activity of the assembled protein warranted further investigation to validate its potential for use in biomimetic materials.

Conclusions

This study reports the successful heterologous secretory expression of hagfish slime thread proteins EsTKα and EsTKγ with P. pastoris as the host. Protein production was systematically optimized, including a comparative screening of P. pastoris strains with distinct phenotypic characteristics (X33, GS115, KM71, SMD1168H), rational signal peptide engineering, and fermentation parameter refinement. The protease-deficient strain SMD1168H was identified as the optimal production host, which enhanced the final protein integrity and yield. The process was scaled up to a high-cell-density fed-batch fermentation in a 5-L bioreactor, which achieved extracellular yields of 350.0 mg/L for EsTKα and 385.0 mg/L for EsTKγ. Compared to the E. coli system, proteins produced by P. pastoris exhibited their native conformation and activity. This eliminated the need for cumbersome and costly protein extraction and purification steps, particularly inclusion body denaturation and renaturation, while also increasing protein expression. To further enhance the secretory expression of functional proteins, subsequent optimization methods may include increasing gene copy number and co-expressing chaperones or factors involved in efficient protein expression pathways. Here, a sequential purification protocol combining pI and ammonium sulfate precipitation was used to recover and isolate recombinant proteins from complex fermentation supernatant. EsTKα and EsTKγ were subsequently incubated at an equimolar ratio (1:1) to undergo in vitro self-assembly. The results of multiple analytical techniques verified that EsTKα and EsTKγ underwent conformational changes and transitioned from a disordered state to an ordered fibrous network during in vitro self-assembly. This study established a theoretical and methodological foundation for the efficient expression of hagfish slime thread proteins. This advancement supports their use in biomedical materials in clinical settings and as a sustainable, functional alternative to conventional petroleum-based materials, thus providing a basis for future innovations in biomimetic materials and related disciplines.
