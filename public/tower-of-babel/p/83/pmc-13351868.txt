A tunable, ultrasensitive threshold in enzymatic activity governs the DNA methylation landscape
Authors: Kwadwo A Bonsu, Nandor Laszik, Annie Trinh, Timothy L Downing, Elizabeth L Read
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDNA methylation is a widely studied epigenetic mark, affecting gene expression and cellular function at multiple levels. DNA methylation in the mammalian genome occurs primarily at cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides, and patterning of the methylation landscape (i.e., the presence or absence of CpG methylation at a given genomic location) exhibits a generally bimodal distribution. Although much is known about the enzymatic writers and erasers of CpG methylation, it is not fully understood how these enzymes, along with genetic, chromatin, and regulatory factors, control the genome-wide methylation landscape. In this study, methylation is analyzed at annotated CpG islands (CGIs) and independent CpGs as a function of their proximity to other CpG substrates. Analysis is aided by a computationally efficient stochastic mathematical model of methylation dynamics, enabling parameterization from data. We find that methylation exhibits a switch-like dependence on local CpG density with a threshold of 7–8 CpGs per 100 bp and a Hill coefficient of 4–5. The threshold and steepness of the switch is modified in cell lines in which key enzymes are knocked out. Modeling further elucidates how enzymatic parameters, including catalytic rates and lengthscales of inter-CpG interaction, tune the properties of the switch. Together, the results support a model in which competition between opposing TET1-3 demethylating enzymes and DNA methyltransferases (DNMT3A/B) results in an ultrasensitive switch, analogous to the protein phosphorylation switch (termed “zero-order ultrasensitivity”). Our study provides insight to the mechanisms underlying establishment and maintenance of bimodal DNA methylation landscapes, and further provides a flexible pipeline for gleaning molecular insights to the cellular methylation machinery across cell-specific, epigenomic data sets.

Significance

DNA methylation is a widely studied epigenetic modification. In mammalian genomes, DNA methylation typically appears at dinucleotide sequences known as a cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) sites. Whether a region of the genome is methylated, or not, is related to the regulation of gene expression. Changes in methylation patterns across the genome occur in aging and disease. Gaps remain in our understanding of how genome-wide patterns of methylation are established and maintained. In this study, we quantified how DNA methylation abruptly switches from high to low with increasing local prevalence of CpG sites. Using bioinformatics, biophysical modeling, and analysis of enzyme knockout cell lines, our study sheds light on the molecular mechanisms underlying the function of this epigenetic switch.

Introduction

DNA is organized into chromatin structures that help regulate activation and silencing of the genome. A number of epigenetic mechanisms affect chromatin structure and help to control gene expression (1,2). Of these, DNA methylation is one of the most widely conserved among mammals, and as such has been broadly studied. In the mammalian genome, methylation typically occurs at cytosine-phosphate-guanine (CpG) dinucleotides at the fifth carbon position of cytosine. CpGs are known to cluster at certain loci, termed CpG islands (CGIs), with variable sizes across the human genome (3,4,5). Promoter regions are frequently associated with CGIs, and CGI methylation is anticorrelated to promoter accessibility/activation (2,6,7,8). CpG content varies across the genome. For example, transposable elements such as short- and long-interspersed retrotransposable elements tend to have low CpG content (9). These regions are predominantly methylated, such that their ability to disrupt normal regulation is suppressed (10).

Catalytically active DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) are responsible for deposition of methyl marks, and they actively contribute to methylation patterning during replication, development, and in disease. DNMT1 is primarily responsible for maintenance methylation, or re-establishment of methyl groups on nascent DNA after replication, primarily targeting hemimethylated substrates (11,12,13). DNMT3A/B performs de novo methylation of unmethylated, or hemimethylated substrates (14). Active demethylation occurs via ten-eleven translocation (TET) proteins, which prime 5-mC (methylated cytosine) residues for removal via successive oxidation reactions (15). Decreased expression of TET proteins and low 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5-hmC) levels are associated with multiple cancer types (16) and developmental misregulation (17,18). TET activity is associated with driving global and loci-specific demethylation in pluripotent cells. These methyl “writers” and “erasers” compete with one another to regulate both benign and malignant phenotypes in cells (19).

Despite the relatively thorough understanding of the key enzymes controlling DNA methylation, open questions remain about how the global methylation landscape is established and maintained, in concert with other epigenetic and structural/genetic (sequence) mechanisms. Underlying genetic sequence context has been shown to play a significant contributing role in determining methylation, particularly the local density of CpGs (9,20,21,22,23,24). Other studies suggested that CpG density alone is not enough to determine methylation, pointing to other genetic and epigenetic characteristics such as transcription factor binding (25), regional guanine/cytosine richness (26), DNA secondary structure (27), and preferential recruitment of methylation machinery to specific genomic features (i.e., imprinting control regions, retrotransposons, flanking sequences) (28,29,30).

In this work, we quantitatively analyzed the dependence of methylation on the presence of neighboring CpGs both in the context of annotated CGIs and genome-wide at single-CpG-resolution. We quantify global dependence of methylation on CpG density in two human embryonic stem cell (hESC) cell lines in which genome-wide methylation profiling (whole-genome bisulfite sequencing [WGBS]) data are available, including cells in which key enzymes of the DNA methylation machinery have been knocked out (14,19).

Using a Hill model, we show that local CpG density governs a genome-wide, ultrasensitive switch in methylation. We theorize that this switch arises, at least in part, from the so-called futile cycle of opposing enzymes (namely DNMTs versus TETs), in analogy to classical zero-order ultrasensitivity (31). We demonstrate that the methylation switch is quantitatively shifted, in terms of threshold and steepness, in response to enzymatic perturbations. We further develop a novel approximation for a stochastic mathematical model of methylation dynamics involving CpG interactions, enabling efficient parameter fitting to data. The model further supports how the “futile” competition between writer and eraser enzymes controls the quantitative properties of the switch. All in all, our results support a view in which dynamic interplay of the opposing writer/eraser methylation enzymes, together with local interactions among CpGs, shapes the global methylation landscape.

Materials and methods

Cell line experimental data and sources

Fourteen WGBS data sets from human cells were reanalyzed. All figures and analyses are based on either CGIs across the genome, or all captured CpGs from chromosome 1. Data are from hESC lines HUES64 and HUES8 and a fetal lung fibroblast cell, IMR90. The hESC data sets include varying types and passage numbers of enzyme knockouts, including single knockouts of DNMT1, DNMT3A, and DNMT3B along with DNMT3A/B double knockouts (DKOs) in HUES64 (14). Furthermore, DNMT3A/B DKOs and TET1-3 triple knockouts (TKO) were analyzed in HUES8 (19).

Calculated threshold metrics: EL50 and ED50

The EL50 describes the lengthscale in bp at which it is equally probable to find a CGI in either the high- or low-methylation macrostates (i.e., hypermethylated versus hypomethylated, irrespective of the CGIs in the intermediate category). Similarly, the ED50 signifies the density value at which it is equally probable to find a single CpG in either the high- or low-methylation fractions.

Mathematical model description and fitting to data

The model, adapted from Haerter et al. (33), is comprised of “standard” reactions, containing only linear transitions of CpG states, “collaborative” reactions, simulating nonlinear transitions via distance-dependent bystander reactions, and cyclic replication events.

The methylating reactions, u → h and h → m, are assumed to be catalyzed by methyltransferases, with DNMTs 3 A/B (DNMT3A and B) regarded as responsible for de novo methylation, and DNMT1 primarily responsible for maintenance methylation over mitotic cycles. DNMT1 shows a high preference for the hemimethylated CpG substrate (34), and thus plays a central role in catalyzing h → m post-DNA replication, but the roles of DNMT3A/B versus DNMT1 in de novo versus maintenance methylation cannot be clearly separated (35).

The demethylating reactions, m → h and h → u, are assumed to happen either actively (catalyzed mainly by TET1-3 enzymes (36)) or passively, as a result of DNA replication, where cytosine on the nascent strand is unmethylated initially. The classical model of DNA methylation inheritance (37,38) gives that every methylated CpG becomes hemimethylated in both daughter cells (until DNMT1 acts to re-assert symmetric methylation), and every hemimethylated CpG yields one unmethylated and one hemimethylated daughter CpG. Model overview: Collaborative model

We modeled a system of N CpGs as a nonuniform 1D lattice (i.e., the distances between neighbor CpGs need not be uniform). We assume that a CpG in a given state can impact the rate of a reaction on a nearby CpG via a distance function, f(d), where d is the inter-CpG distance in bp. We include the following collaborative reactions: {u +m, h +m, h −u, m −u}, where, for example, u +m denotes a CpG in the u state that is methylated on one strand to become h, in a way that is dependent on a nearby CpG in the m state. Our model assumes that the collaborative methylating reactions ({u +m, h +m}) are governed by an exponential distance function, fm(d)=e−d/λm. Likewise, the collaborative demethylating reactions ({h −u, m −u}) have distance function fu(d)=e−d/λu. Coarse-grained model approximation

We used a chemical master equation (CME) framework to compute the steady-state distribution of methylation states, given a set of model parameters. We introduced a novel coarse-grained approximation of the full model that enables rapid computation of the methylation landscape, and is thus amenable to fitting parameters to data. Our approach is related to a recent mean field method introduced by (39). Whereas their method is focused mainly on nearest-neighbor interactions, our method uses a distance function to compute interactions and incorporates the natural variation in inter-CpG spacing. However, our model cannot distinguish behavior of CpGs in a cluster, e.g., in the center versus the edge. Fitting model to data

The chemical system can be expressed as dp⃗/dt=Ap⃗, where p⃗ is the TN+1 × 1 vector holding probabilities for the group to be found in each possible macrostate, and A is the TN+1 × TN+1 reaction rate matrix, holding reaction rate constants connecting each macrostate as in Eq. 13. The steady-state probabilities over each methylation macrostate for a given set of parameters were computed using MATLAB’s eigenvalue solver. To compute hypo/inter/hyper versus CpG-density curves for each trial set of parameters, the simulated system density was varied by fixing N CpGs and varying the inter-CpG distance d (we used N = 27 and d = {2, 3, 4, 5, …100} bp). Then for parameter set θ⃗ and interdistance d, the rate matrix A(θ⃗,d)p⃗=0 is solved to obtain the corresponding distribution. The computed probability vector is mapped onto the hyper/inter/hypo classification according to the methylation ratio of each macrostate, given by (0.5Nh + Nm)/N. (That is, m are given a value of 1, h of 1/2, and u of 0.) The MATLAB optimization routine fmincon was used to optimize parameters. The best parameter set fit to HUES8WT in Figs. 6 and 7 is {u+, h˜−, h+, m˜−, h+m, u+m, h−u, m−u} = {0.733, 0.372, 1.000, 0.238, 0.053, 2.199, 14.6, 0.023} (note the units are taken as h+−1). The fit collaborative lengthscales are {λm, λu} = 78.1, 47.2 bp.

Temporal model of enzyme perturbations

After obtaining θ⃗ that optimizes the fit of hypo/inter/hyper curves to the wild-type (WT) data, enzyme perturbations are modeled by decreases in the rates of select reactions, giving a modified set θ′⃗ and corresponding modified rate matrix A′. The dynamics are then propagated by dp⃗/dt=A′p⃗ using a standard ODE solver (ode45).

Software packages and code availability

Data was analyzed via a combination of Python, Jupyter Notebook, and the Pandas software package (40); model fitting and figure generation were performed in MATLAB 2023b.

Results

A CGI’s methylation depends on its size/length

Bivariate heatmaps of CGI-level methylation versus CGI length show increased occurrence of lower methylation for longer islands (Fig. 1). To characterize this trend, we binned CGIs according to length and then classified each CGI into hypomethylated (<20% methylation fraction, also known as methylation ratio), hypermethylated (>80% methylation fraction), and intermediate methylation, thus capturing population density in the high/low peaks (or in between) of the characteristically bimodal methylation distribution. In all data sets, occurrence of hypomethylated CGIs increases with length (Fig. 1). Similar trends are observed when CGIs are classified according to their size (number of CpGs associated with the island) or average CpG density instead of their length, and CGI length and size are linearly correlated (Figs. We defined the “effective length of CpG island at 50% probability of hypomethylation,” EL50, as the length at which the blue and red curves cross; that is, the length at which an equal number of CGIs are classified as hypo- versus hypermethylated. Similar EL50s are found in HUES64 (351 bp) and HUES8 (322 bp). Under this classification, the EL50 in IMR90 is shorter (214 bp, close to the minimum size of CGIs). While the chosen thresholds (80/20) are well-suited to HUES64 and HUES8, the upper peak in the bimodal landscape of IMR90 is not fully captured. Instead, using thresholds of 60/20 that capture the upper peak, the EL50 in IMR90 is similar, at 310 bp.

Even at longer CGI lengths, bimodality of CGI methylation in all cell types is evident, as a small population of CGIs persists in the hypermethylated class even as the majority of long islands are hypomethylated. These results demonstrate that, while CGI methylation is bimodal at all lengthscales, the relative populations in the two modes are linked to CGI length (or size). Increasing length of CGIs shifts the population from predominantly hypermethylated to hypomethylated, and a lengthscale in the range of 310–350 bp is associated to maximum bimodality of CGIs.

Discrete CpG methylation depends on local density, recapitulating trends at the CGI level

A minority of CpGs occur within CGIs genome-wide, representing ≈8% of total CpGs in the current analysis. By definition, in-CGI CpGs represent the high end of the genome-wide CpG density distribution (Fig. S4). To test if the findings apply outside of CGIs, we classified all individual CpGs (regardless of CGI membership) into hyper- or hypomethylation categories and analyzed them as a function of their local CpG density within a ±50 bp window. An individual CpG was classified as hypermethylated if greater than 80% of reads at that CpG were methylated (and so on). CpGs were binned according to their discrete, local, normalized density value (see materials and methods). The bimodality seen at the CGI-level and methylation trend are also observed at the resolution of local CpG density (Fig. 2).

Individual CpGs with low local density (i.e., fewer neighboring CpGs in ±50 bp) are more likely to be hypermethylated, whereas high-density CpGs are predominantly hypomethylated. In HUES64 and HUES8, the increase of hypomethylation with density is smooth and monotonic (except where samples become sparse). Due to the higher prevalence of intermediate methylation, IMR90 presents a more complicated dependency on density, but the general trend of increased hypomethylation with density is the same. Curve shapes were largely insensitive to the choice of method for pooling CpGs by density (see materials and methods). However, slight quantitative shifts were observed, and these were used to estimate uncertainties.

Analogous to the EL50, we defined the “expected density of 50%” methylation, ED50, as the density at which an individual CpG is equally likely to be hypo- or hypermethylated. (When the crossover occurred between discrete density values, interpolation was performed.) Similar ED50s were found for HUES64 and HUES8 (0.154 and 0.151, respectively). These correspond to an average of ≈7.5 neighbor CpGs in the window ±50 bp. A slightly shorter ED50 of 0.149 was found for IMR90. Results were qualitatively similar when in-CGI CpGs were excluded from the analysis. In contrast, in-CGI CpGs alone showed prevalence of hypomethylation at all densities (Fig. S4).

In all, these results reveal that, at the level of individual CpGs regardless of genomic context, local CpG density is a strong predictor of methylation. Unlike in CGIs, where bimodality is evident at all lengths, bimodality of individual CpG methylation was not evident at the highest density values, as few high-density CpGs are hypermethylated. At intermediate densities in all cell types studied, there was significant population of CpGs in both the hypo- and hypermethylated modes. Density values near the ED50 corresponded to the most pronounced bimodality of individual CpG methylation.

Methylation switch shifts with enzymatic knockouts, quantifying DNMT/TET competition

We reanalyzed WGBS data from HUES64 cells harboring single or double DNMT3A/B knockouts and from HUES8 cells harboring combinatorial DNMT/TET knockouts to ascertain how CGI-length- or CpG-density-dependent characteristics depend on the key enzymes controlling methylation dynamics. The knockout cells show shifts in both the CGI-level and individual-CpG-level curves (Fig. 3, A–D). DKOs of DNMT3A/B display increased representation of hypomethylated CGIs at all CGI lengths, with this fraction of islands also increasing with time (i.e., number of passages) compared with the WT fraction (Fig. 3, A and C). At the individual CpG level, this behavior is seen predominantly at lower CpG densities. Similar shifts from WT are observed in HUES8 DKO as in HUES64 DKO cells. Conversely, HUES8 cells harboring TKOs of TET1-3 active demethylases show decrease in hypomethylated CGIs at all lengths (along with an increase in hypermethylated CGIs).

For both HUES8 and HUES64, DKO cells show a markedly decreased EL50 and ED50 relative to WT (Fig. 3, E–H). In HUES64, EL/ED50 further decreases from early to late passage numbers. EL50s from single knockouts of DNMT3A and DNMT3B are only slightly decreased compared with WT, with no clear effect of passage number. ED50s for single knockouts in HUES64 show no or minimal change from WT, echoing previous findings of minimal change of the methylation landscape in response to single DNMT3 KO, whereas DKO clearly decreases methylation (14).

In HUES8 cells, TET1-3 TKO also shifts, but in the opposite direction, with EL50 and ED50 increasing compared with WT. Combinatorial knockout of TET1-3 with DNMT3B alone or both DNMT3A/B show that the calculated metric values shift back toward WT; similar trends are observed in ED50s.

In all, these results show that the trends of CGI methylation versus CGI length, or CpG methylation versus CpG density, are broadly maintained under various perturbations to the methylation enzyme system, but the methylation switch is quantitatively shifted. The similar shifts in EL/ED50, toward higher or lower values in TET1-3 or DNMT3A/B knockouts, respectively, suggest that the EL/ED50 quantifies the point of balanced competition between the eraser and writer enzymes. Weakening the methyl “erasers” (TETs) enables CGIs to reach larger size/lengths (and similarly, CpGs to reach higher local densities), before becoming predominantly hypomethylated. Conversely, weakening the “writers” (DNMT3A/B, in this case) leads to predominant hypomethylation at shorter CGI lengths (and similarly, lower densities).

Susceptibility to enzyme KO-induced changes in methylation depends on CGI length

We classified unique CGIs based on their change in methylation between WT and knockout (Fig. 4). In DKO (DNMT3A/B knockout) systems, there is an enrichment of islands that experience moderate/drastic methylation loss at shorter CGI lengths. There is accumulating time-dependent loss of methylation, seen in early versus late passage time points. In contrast, TKO-induced methylation is more broadly homogeneous over varying sizes of methylated CGIs. Results are qualitatively similar for individual CpGs when their methylation change is compared across data sets. Net susceptibility of the distribution to TET1-3 KO-induced changes was variable across CpG densities, but most pronounced at intermediate densities (Fig. S5). Together, these results show that CGI size (or local CpG density) is a factor determining susceptibility to enzyme-knockout-induced methylation changes, particularly for methylation loss induced by DNMT3A/B knockout.

CpG methylation switch is ultrasensitive to local CpG density

To further characterize the methylation switch, we also considered mean and median CpG methylation for individual CpGs as a function of local CpG density in the hESCs and knockout systems (i.e., no longer relying on the hyper/hypo/inter classification) (Fig. 5 A). Mean and median methylation decrease sharply with density (an initial slight increase is apparent in some cells, especially in median). Bimodality is apparent in that the median switches even more sharply than the mean, reflecting that the population is shifting from one mode to the other. Remarkably, taking HUES64 as an example, median CpG methylation decreases from 0.87 to 0.17, over a corresponding density change from 0.14 to 0.16, i.e., the addition of a single additional CpG neighbor (from 7 neighbors to 8) within ±50 bp.

Data same as Fig. 3D, but here as mean and median/IQR methylation versus density, rather than hyper/hypo/interclassification. Hill model fits to experimental mean methylation shown as green dashes. Error bars reflect differences in estimates of quantities (n, K, slope, and ED50) according to the chosen weighting/filtering method.

Mean methylation is generally well-fit by a Hill function (see materials and methods), with n (Hill coefficient) giving a measure of steepness of the switch and K giving a measure of the switch threshold (half-max). The fitted Hill coefficient is n = 4.5 for HUES64 and n = 5 for HUES8 (without enzyme perturbation). Therefore, the methylation switch is strongly ultrasensitive (characterized by n > 1 (41)).

We quantified the methylation switch by n and K for a slate of enzyme knockout experiments and stages (Fig. 5 B). In addition to the aforementioned systems (Fig. 3), we also studied RRBS data from DNMT1 knockout. To explore different methods of quantifying the switch, we also employed a “direct” method, i.e., not relying on Hill fitting, but using the hyper/hypo/inter classification. Under the direct method, the switch threshold is taken as the afore-defined ED50, and the switch steepness is taken as the slope of the difference curve (i.e., hyper − hypo curve), measured at the ED50 threshold (Fig. 5 C).

K and ED50 are strongly positively correlated across experiments, indicating robustness to the choice of method for characterizing methylation switch threshold (Fig. S6; Table S2). The direction of the shift in switch threshold depends on the type of enzyme perturbation. In HUES8, all TETx or TET-DNMT3 combination cells show increased K values, indicating a shift of the switch to higher densities, echoing shifts seen in ED50 (Fig. 3). Conversely, in knockouts of either DNMT1 (HUES64 only) or DNMT3AB (DKO, both HUES64 and HUES8), the methylation switch shifts to lower densities (decreased K/ED50). DNMT3 single knockouts (A or B) shift minimally from WT, either slightly decreasing or slightly increasing, depending on the experiment and the fit method.

In general, steepness of the switch tends to decrease in the enzyme-perturbed cells: all knockouts show decreased n relative to WT in both HUES64 and HUES8. (In the Direct method, only two of 15 knockout data sets (TET1-3 and one of the TET1-3/DNMT3 combinations) show slightly increased steepness relative to WT). Steepness (n versus slope) is correlated across the two fitting methods, but less strongly/significantly as compared with threshold.

In all, these results show that sequence-encoded CpG density drives an ultrasensitive methylation switch. Furthermore, perturbing the methylation enzyme system causes quantitative shifts in this methylation switch. Qualitatively similar shifts across related experiments (i.e., same enzyme knockout class) and across cell types (where data are available, i.e., DNMT3 DKO) support that these shifts are robust and characteristic of the specific enzyme perturbation. Generally, we observed a decrease in ultrasensitivity with respect to CpG substrate density in all knockouts, supporting the idea that removing methyl writers and/or erasers, i.e., weakening either side of the competitive balance, reduces the cell’s ability to maintain ultrasensitive switch-like behavior required for regulated bimodal gene expression.

A “collaborative” mathematical model with CpG-interaction-dependent rates fits experimentally observed density dependence of methylation

We performed mathematical modeling to investigate the mechanistic basis for the observed ultrasensitive methylation switch, and for the enzyme KO-induced shifts in these trends. Our model is based on a previous framework employing CpG collaboration, which refers to the effect of nearby CpGs (and their state) on rates of enzymatic reactions at a given CpG (33). The model is shown schematically in Fig. 6 A and described in materials and methods. Briefly, the model assumes that each CpG exists in one of unmethylated (u), hemimethylated (h), or methylated (m) states. Transitions between these states occur either independently or collaboratively (dependent on the presence and methylation status of neighbor CpGs), with specified kinetic constants. Demethylating reactions also occur passively due to DNA replication, dependent on the length of the mitotic cell cycle. The “standard” model includes only CpG-independent transitions (including replication-associated methylation loss). The “collaborative” model adds distance-dependent neighbor effects on transition rates (Fig. 6 B) to simulate locally cooperative and/or processive enzymatic activity. The full, stochastic model requires simulation of many trajectories to achieve good estimates of model-predicted methylation distributions, and this poses a challenge for parameter fitting against data. To overcome this challenge, we developed a novel, coarse-grained approximation based on collective CpG “macrostates.” The steady-state distribution is then directly computed using the framework of the CME (42,43).

The model quantitatively captures the density-dependent methylation distribution of individual CpGs, achieving an excellent fit to the hyper/hypo/inter curves (Fig. 6 C). In contrast, the standard model, with no consideration for collaborating/interacting methylation events, is unable to exhibit any density dependence. To fit the data well, the standard reactions must favor methylation (i.e., faster rates) while the collaborative reactions must favor demethylation, driving the collective switch toward hypomethylation with increased CpG density (see materials and methods). The best-fit models include collaborative effects in both directions (+) and (−) and different lengthscales for the competing collaborative effects. Fitting parameters indicated that collaborative methylation (+), although slower than collaborative demethylation (i.e., less probable reaction event per unit time), had a longer lengthscale (extending to neighbors 80 bp away, as compared with 50 bp away). This weaker, but further extending, collaborative methylation process was necessary to protect methylation of CpGs at lower densities up to ρ ≈ 0.1; it was also necessary to reproduce the slight increase of hypermethylation (red curve, Fig. 6 C) at low densities. A hypothetical, “reverse-switch” could be achieved if the rates were reversed (Fig. 6 D).

According to the original theory of Goldbeter and Koshland, the switch in the futile cycle occurs where enzyme concentrations are such that forward and reverse reaction velocities are equal (31). Similarly, according to our model the ED50 methylation threshold reflects the CpG density at which net methylation strength balances net demethylation strength (Fig. S7; Note S2). For example, decreasing collaborative demethylation shifts the switch toward a higher threshold density (Fig. 6 D).

These results support that CpG collaboration together with enzyme competition are responsible for the observed ultrasensitive methylation switch. The results further support that a quantitative, mechanistic model based only on sequence-encoded CpG density (and no other details of genomic context or other layers of the epigenome) is sufficient to broadly, quantitatively fit the genome-wide methylation landscape.

A temporal model of the methylation switch reveals complex effects of enzyme knockout

To explore model predictions, we developed a temporal CME model, which can simulate the evolution of the methylation landscape in time after a perturbation to the enzyme system, thus mimicking the knockout experiments. Enzyme knockouts are modeled as a decrease in the strength of a given parameter, relative to the baseline (i.e., steady-state WT fit), thus capturing a decrease in enzyme concentration and/or activity. To explore model behavior, we changed one to two parameters at a time; such “parsimonious” changes were sufficient to replicate experimental results. Under such perturbations, the methylation distribution, and hence the methylation switch, changes over time, concomitant with shifts in threshold (K) and/or steepness (n) (Fig. 7 A).

The model predicts that decreasing strength of methylating enzymes (i.e., parameters u+, h+, h+m, u+m, or pairs therefrom) generally shifts K to lower densities, while in contrast, decreasing strength of demethylating enzymes (h−, m−, h−u, m−u) shifts K to higher densities (Fig. 7 B). These model predictions are qualitatively in line with the knockout experiments (i.e., K increase in TET knockouts and decrease in DNMT1/3AB knockouts). Steepness (n) could shift up or down in both classes of model perturbation, depending on the parameters/time point.

While such (+) or (−) in silico knockouts qualitatively match experimental shifts, achieving good quantitative fits of the shifted methylation switch across all experimental conditions required complex combinations of enzyme perturbations (i.e., simultaneous modulation of a pair of (±) parameters). To assess which model parameters were most likely to be affected in each type of knockout experiment, we sampled all single- and pairwise combinations and scored each parameter by its average relative change (relative to the WT baseline), in successful fits, for each class of knockout experiment (Fig. 7 C). In general, the parameter most strongly reduced in DMNT (1 or 3) knockouts, relative to WT, is h+m, the collaborative h → m rate. The parameter most strongly reduced in TET1-3 KOs is m−u, the collaborative m → h rate. The combinatorial experiments (i.e., with knockout of TET1-3 as well as either DNMT3A or both DNMT3A/B) showed strong reduction of both of these parameters. Interestingly, the DNMT3 single knockouts showed a qualitatively similar parameter profile, but less perturbed (i.e., more similar to WT). Finally, we analyzed the model in terms of density dependence of susceptibility of enzyme KO-induced changes. The model predicts that intermediate-density CpGs are most susceptible to TET KO-induced methylation gain, in line with experimental results. The highest-density CpGs, while naively the lowest in methylation and thus good candidates for methylation gain, are shielded by nearby CpGs, i.e., they are afforded protection by the strong collaborative demethylation reaction (h−u) (Fig. S8).

Together, these results support that the model not only can quantitatively fit cell-type-specific methylation landscapes, but can also capture the temporal changes of the landscape that occur when the methylation enzyme system is perturbed. The model is capable of predicting the qualitative direction of shift of the methylation switch, depending on whether the competitive enzyme balance is weakened on the methylating or demethylating side. At the same time, the model suggests a more nuanced effect of enzyme knockout experiments, where the balance is perturbed in complex ways (i.e., not simply on one side or the other), suggesting cooperativity between forward and reverse processes in regulation of the switch. Finally, the modeling approach connects the molecular players to the physical processes underlying the ultrasensitive switch.

Discussion

In this work, we combined quantitative analysis of DNA methylation dependence on local CpG context (either in CGIs or genome-wide) with mathematical modeling to characterize the dynamic interplay between writing and erasing enzymes in a given cell type. We developed a framework to quantitatively measure the CpG density-dependent bimodality of the landscape for comparison across cell types. We found that the length- or density-dependent methylation trend can be characterized as an ultrasensitive switch. Enzymatic perturbation affected both the threshold (EL50, ED50, or K) and steepness (Hill coefficient or slope) of the methylation curves. Mathematical modeling shows that the methylation switch relies on collaboration among CpGs, i.e., where the methylation status of nearby CpGs affects both methylating and demethylating reaction rates at a given CpG. The shift in threshold (to higher/lower density in TET1-3 and DNMT knockouts, respectively) results from a shifted balance of opposing enzymes. TET1-3, DNMT3A/B, and DNMT1 knockouts all showed reduced switch steepness, which was linked to a reduction of collaborative reaction strength on either the (+) or (−) side of the competitive balance. Our analyses show that behaviors at CGIs fall within the broader trends of genome-wide CpG sequence context. All in all, our results show how dynamic enzyme levels act on top of stable sequence determinants (CpG density) to determine whether loci are methylated, unmethylated, or bistable. In the future, this approach may be extended to other data sets to investigate modulation in DNA methylation due to differentiation, disease modalities such as cancer, aging, and effects of drug perturbations on the regulatory landscape.

The data and modeling support that competition between TETs and DNMTs occurs globally, with CpG density acting as a continuously varying tuner of the competitive balance. This is complementary to more locus-specific views, for example, in TET1-3 KO, where methylation changes have been described as being dependent on the presence of bivalent chromatin (44,45). We propose that methylation changes can be broadly understood in terms of the global shifts of the methylation switch under enzyme perturbations. This is also evident, for example, in the TET1-3 KO, where the methylation versus density curve shows shifts across the full CpG density range (Fig. 3 D), and CGIs of varying sizes showed methylation gains (Fig. 4). We have introduced a new formalism for quantifying these changes using the framework of ultrasensitive Hill switch functions.

Ultrasensitivity of DNA methylation with CpG density resulting from opposing enzymes

DNA methylation machinery may be considered to participate in a “futile cycle,” since there are two separate, opposing enzyme-catalyzed reactions operating on the same substrate in opposite directions (i.e., broadly, the DNMT-catalyzed “writing,” versus the TET-catalyzed “erasing”). Futile cycles are widespread in biology, with classic examples being in metabolism (e.g., glycolysis versus gluconeogenesis) and signaling (phosphorylation/dephosphorylation cascades) (46). The direction of net flux in a futile cycle is controlled by differential regulation of the opposing enzymes (47). In their seminal work (31), Goldbeter and Koshland proposed that this type of control could achieve extreme, switch-like ultrasensitivity (which they termed “zero-order ultrasensitivity” under conditions of enzyme saturation) wherein the net flux switches directions abruptly at a threshold ratio in concentrations of the opposing enzymes. This mechanism is thought to be one contributing factor underlying observed ultrasensitivity in various systems (48,49,50,51) (reviewed in (52)). We propose that the ultrasensitivity we observed in DNA methylation versus CpG density is another example of zero-order ultrasensitivity, with the additional impact of the sequence context of the CpG substrate on kinetics making it distinct from other systems. We found that DNA methylation exhibits an ultrasensitive switch-like dependence on CpG density, with a Hill coefficient >4. In the original theory, the futile cycle switch occurs where the effective rates of forward and reverse enzymatic reactions are balanced. Here, we demonstrate through experimental data analysis and modeling that the ED50 reflects the CpG density at which the net rates of methylating and demethylating reactions are balanced.

A prediction of the theory is that changes in relevant enzyme concentrations should shift the ED50; for example, decreasing TETs should increase ED50, since a higher CpG density is required for the net fluxes to equalize again under the diminished TET concentrations. Indeed, this prediction is confirmed by our results for the DNMT3A/B and TET1-3 KO cell lines, which show the predicted directional shifts (Figs. 3, B and D, and 5). These results further support the conclusion that the ultrasensitive switch in DNA methylation is explainable by an adapted form of the Goldbeter-Koshland theory, pointing to a mechanism whereby the interplay of CpG density and differential expression of writing/erasing enzymes can achieve relatively targeted methylation modifications. This view is further supported by our finding that susceptibility of CGIs to methylation change as a result of enzyme perturbations is not uniform, but rather dependent on their relative size/length (Fig. 4). In all, our results suggest that the methylation futile cycle, combined with the enzymes’ sensitivities to local CpG sequence context, is a major driver of globally bimodal methylation landscapes. Although we show that the methylation system shares hallmark characteristics of the Goldbeter-Koshland system (i.e., an ultrasensitive switch between two directions of flux in an enzymatic futile cycle), we caution that differences between the Goldbeter-Koshland model and the methylation model may limit the applicability of the former to the latter, and/or more study may be required to formalize the connection. For example, in the Goldbeter-Koshland model, steepness is determined solely by degree of enzyme saturation, whereas the steepness of the methylation switch is controlled by the interplay of many kinetic parameters (see Fig. 7 B), even at conditions of complete enzyme saturation (the implicitly assumed condition of our model).

Relationship between CpG density and DNA methylation

The relationship between local CpG density and DNA methylation has been studied previously, with most reports supporting a general inverse relationship. CGIs, which have relatively high local density of CpGs, are recognized to be predominantly hypomethylated (53). An early genome-scale study pointed to increased methylation levels of CpGs with decreased local CpG density (54). Within CGIs specifically, CpG-rich (as well as GC-rich) sequence patterns were found to be over-represented in unmethylated (versus methylated) islands (26,55). Relatedly, under a genome-scale definition of “CpG clusters” in human DNA, Lövkvist et al. reported that clusters with fewer or less densely spaced CpGs are predominantly hypermethylated, while larger clusters are hypomethylated (23). In a variety of human and mouse tissues, when CpGs were binned by density, genome-wide, in a 2500 bp sliding window, a strong negative correlation was found between CpG density and methylation (20), with decreasing methylation vs. density curves qualitatively similar to our mean methylation curves (Fig. 5). Another study of the mouse genome showed smoothly decreasing methylation versus CpG density using essentially the same density measure as our study (25), finding a half-maximum point near 8 CpGs per 100 bp, which is quantitatively close to the ED50/K thresholds we report here. These studies suggest that the methylation versus CpG density trend is broadly consistent across mouse and human cell types. Our approach to quantifying this trend as a switch, demonstrated on hESCs and enzymatic perturbations, could be extended to other cell types in the future.

However, in partially methylated domains, the opposite trend was reported: in these regions, methylation was found to increase with local CpG density at lengthscales of ±20 bp (21) and ±35 bp CpG (22). An earlier study reported that methylation initially increases with increasing CpG density, but then sharply decreases again at higher densities (9). This broader genome-wide trend (increase, followed by decrease) may reconcile the conflicting observations for CpG density effect on methylation. Although our focus here was on the switch threshold (higher density), we also observed a slight increase of methylation at lower densities, especially in IMR90. Our modeling revealed how collaborative effects can explain these patterns: due to collaborative methylation (+), methylation initially increases due to neighbor effects (see Fig. 6 C, where the model reproduces well the initial increase of hypermethylated CpGs). But eventually, collaborative demethylation (−) dominates for CpGs with large numbers of neighbors (⪆8 in 100 bp).

Molecular mechanisms of collaboration

“Collaboration” in this work, adopted from Haerter et al. (33), refers to any mechanism by which CpGs (either directly or indirectly) influence the rates of enzymatic writing/erasing at another CpG, in a manner that depends on their current methylation state. A number of mathematical modeling studies have provided support for the presence of such interactions among CpGs in dynamic methylation processes (33,56,57,58,59). Various molecular mechanisms may underlie this collaboration. For example, DNMT1 is preferentially recruited to sites with more neighboring hemimethylated sites (60), and these CpGs show faster methylation rates (61). The latter finding is consistent with DNMT1 processivity, where the enzyme can successively methylate neighboring sites. DNMT1 processivity has been characterized in vitro (62,63,64,65,66,67), and indirectly in vivo, evidenced by locally correlated maintenance methylation kinetics (59,68). UHRF1 helps recruit DNMT1 to hemimethylated CpGs (69,70,71). Monoubiquinated histone H3 helps recruit DNMT1 to DNA stretches with multiple, but not one, hemimethylated CpGs (60), supporting the concept that UHRF1 may preferentially direct DNMT1 to CpG-dense regions.

Active demethylation processes carried out by TET enzymes also show some evidence of collaboration. DNA strand sliding by a TET protein was observed by high-speed atomic force microscopy in a DNA origami nanochip (72), although another study saw no evidence of this type of processivity (73). The binding domains of TET1 and TET3 prefer CpG-rich regions (74,75) and TET1 binding correlates with CpG density (74,76,77).

Lengthscale of CpG interactions

While the current model cannot distinguish detailed molecular mechanisms of collaboration, it sheds light on effective lengthscales of the interactions. Good model fits were achieved with collaboration lengthscales on the order of 40–80 bp for the exponential distance functions. These values are in line with our previous work supporting a processive lengthscale of DNMT1 of 38 bp in hESCs, based on exponential decay of kinetic correlation (59). Other evidence provides support of the importance of these relatively short-range interactions. For example, it was found that CpG density within windows of ±35 bp was optimal for predicting PMD-specific hypomethylation (22). In a study of the effect of local sequence context on CpG methylation (21), the strongest determinant at a central CpG was the presence of adjacent CpGs, although other neighboring CpGs had an impact on methylation that decreased continuously with distance to roughly ±40 bp. More work is required to further elucidate the lengthscales, distance-dependence, and mechanisms of CpG collaborative interactions.

Relationship to previous mathematical models

Our model was adapted from previous works (23,33) and modified in key ways. First, we have formulated the model as a stochastic chemical reaction network, with kinetic rates as free parameters. Second, we devised a mean field approximation to enable rapid solution of the steady-state distribution over methylation states. This approach enabled us to efficiently calculate the methylation distribution as a function of neighbor CpG density without simulating many trajectories, enabling investigation of a broad parameter space for relatively large system sizes. Third, we used the genome-wide, cell-type-specific distribution of CpG methylation classes (hypo/hyper/inter) as a function of local CpG density to fit the model parameters. We also investigated how key parameters control the threshold and steepness of the methylation switch. We found that purely exponential distance functions (i.e., decreasing over a lengthscale determined by λ) were sufficient to recapitulate the experiment-derived density curves. The choice of exponential functions was made as a simplified base assumption to limit fitting parameters. This choice is supported by our previous studies that revealed kinetic correlations largely (though not entirely) described by exponential decay (59,68), consistent with 1D diffusion of the methyltransferases along DNA. Another supportive line of evidence is the rapid decay with distance of sequence effect on methylation (21). However, it is important to note that our study cannot discount the possibility of more complex and long-ranged interactions. In their study, Lövkvist et al. (23) suggested that collaborative methylation reactions have a more long-ranged, power-law dependence on distance. Further study of how CpGs are coupled over short and long ranges is needed.

It is important to note that our density-dependent switch model, while providing good fits to the global methylation trend, does not take into account many details of local epigenetic/chromatin context. One study found that, while CpG density alone is a significant determinant of methylation genome-wide, in CGIs there was a principal role for additional factors such as transcription factor binding sites (25). A number of other features have been shown to be important in determining methylation at specific loci, such as Zfp57 binding sites (28), high G/C content (26), G-quadruplex sequences (27), and CpG-flanking bases (30). Also, enzymatic perturbations may involve more complex regulatory mechanisms, which are not treated in our model. Our study suggests that the global determinants of CpG density and enzyme activity set the broad characteristics of the methylation switch, upon which more local features could more precisely tune methylation in specific loci. In the future, the analysis and modeling approach presented here could be augmented with more detailed genomic/epigenomic context factors to better predict methylation.
