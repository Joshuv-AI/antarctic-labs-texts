Silibinin attenuates ferroptosis in acute kidney injury by targeting FTH1
Authors: Yijian Deng, Liying Zeng, Huaxi Liu, Anna Zuo, Jie Zhou, Ying Yang, Yanting You, Xinghong Zhou, Baizhao Peng, Hanqi Lu, Shuai Ji, Ming Wang, Yigui Lai, Hiu Yee Kwan, Xiaomin Sun, Qi Wang, Xiaoshan Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Acute kidney injury (AKI) is primarily caused by renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), which is one of the most prevalent triggers. Currently, preventive and therapeutic measures remain limited. Ferroptosis plays a significant role in the pathophysiological process of IRI-induced AKI and is considered a key target for improving its outcomes. Silibinin, a polyphenolic flavonoid, possesses diverse pharmacological properties and is widely used as an effective therapeutic agent for liver diseases. Recent studies have reported that silibinin may improves kidney diseases, though the underlying mechanism remain unclear. In this study, we investigated whether silibinin protects against IRI-induced AKI and explored its mechanism of action. Our findings indicated that pretreatment with silibinin alleviated renal dysfunction, pathological damage, and inflammation in IRI-AKI mice. Furthermore, the results demonstrated that silibinin inhibited ferroptosis both in vivo and in vitro. Proteome microarrays were used to identify silibinin's target, and our results revealed that silibinin binds to FTH1. This binding affinity was confirmed through molecular docking, SPRi, CETSA, and DARTS. Additionally, co-IP assays demonstrated that silibinin disrupted the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction, inhibiting ferritinophagy. Finally, the inhibitory effects of silibinin on ferroptosis were reversed by knocking down FTH1 in vitro. In conclusion, our study shows that silibinin effectively alleviates AKI by targeting FTH1 to reduce ferroptosis, suggesting that silibinin could be developed as a potential therapeutic agent for managing and treating AKI.

Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a commonly clinical syndrome, described by rapid kidney dysfunction that is characterized by elevated serum creatinine and reduced urinary output [1]. Each year, approximately 13.3 million people experience AKI, a condition frequently linked to high mortality rates, adverse outcomes, and significant societal burden [2,3]. Renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) is the most common trigger of AKI, usually caused by surgery or trauma, sepsis, dehydration and medications nephrotoxicity [1,4]. Multiple pathological mechanisms are involved in renal IRI, including inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, tubular cell injury and death [4]. Currently, our understanding of the prevention and treatment of IRI is limited. Hence, it is imperative to investigate and comprehend the regulatory mechanisms that contribute to renal IRI. By doing so, we can develop more effective therapeutic strategies and ultimately enhance the prognosis for patients with AKI.

Ferroptosis is a non-apoptotic cell death, initiated by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation [5]. Current researches have elucidated the prominent roles of ferroptosis in the pathophysiological mechanisms of IRI-AKI, and the ferroptosis inhibitors have significantly prevented or alleviated IRI-AKI [6,7]. Multiple metabolic pathways are involved in regulating ferroptosis, among which system xc−/glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) pathway is the canonical pathway and is widely investigated. Glutathione (GSH) is the vital antioxidant against oxidative stress in cell metabolism. The import of cystine via system xc-is necessary for GSH biosynthesis and maintains the function of GPX4. GPX4 is the crucial enzyme that catalyzes the reduction of phospholipid hydroperoxides (PLOOHs). It also inhibits system xc-, a cystine-glutamate antiporter. When GPX4 activity is inhibited, it results in the accumulation of PLOOHs, which ultimately triggers a form of programmed cell death known as ferroptosis. Cellular iron metabolism is another major regulatory mechanism of ferroptosis, and is recognized as central initiators and mediators of ferroptosis [8]. In the cytosol, free ferrous iron increases reactive oxygen species (ROS) production via the Fenton reaction that mediate ferroptosis [9]. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) is an iron storage protein with ferroxidase activity, which converts the ferrous form (Fe2+) to the ferric form (Fe3+), enabling iron in an inert form store into the ferritin shell [10]. Recent studies further emphasized the key role of FTH1 in ferroptosis, lacking FTH1 in cardiomyocytes promoted cardiac ferroptosis and facilitated cardiomyopathy subsequently [11]. Therefore, the abundance of FTH1 is an important factor in regulating ferroptosis.

Silibinin, a polyphenolic flavonoid and the foremost active compound extracted from Silybum marianum, it has been widely utilized as a therapeutic agent for liver disease [12]. Mounting evidence demonstrates that silibinin exhibits diverse pharmacological properties, including anti-oxidant, anti-apoptosis, anti-inflammation, anti-cancer and many more [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Besides, recent studies have reported that silibinin improves various diseases conditions by inhibiting ferroptosis [[18], [19], [20]]. However, few attentions have been focused on the role of silibinin in kidney disease. Hence, we investigated whether silibinin alleviated AKI by limiting ferroptosis-mediated kidney injury. Here, we have validated the vital role of ferroptosis in the process of IRI-AKI and revealed that silibinin effectively protected against ischemia reperfusion induced renal injury by targeting FTH1 and reduced ferroptosis-mediated cell death. Our data suggests that silibinin can be developed as a potential therapeutic agent for managing and treating AKI.

Materials and methods

Reagents

Silibinin (#A0131) was obtained from Must Bio-Technology Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China) with a purity of 98.89 % determined by HPLC. Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1, #S7243), erastin (#E7781), and RSL3 (#S8155) were purchased from Selleck Chemicals (Houston, US). The antibodies to NGAL (#DF6816), FTH1 (#DF6278), FTL (#DF6604) and GAPDH (#AF7021) were obtained from Affinity Biosciences (Cincinnati, US). The antibodies to GPX4 (#67763-1-IG), F4/80 (#29414-1-AP), Kim-1 (#26420-1-AP) and HO-1 (#10701-1-AP) were obtained from Proteintech (Rosemont, US). The antibodies to NCOA4 were obtained from abcam (#ab314553, Cambridge, UK) and santa cruz (#sc-373739, Dallas, US). Secondary antibodies were purchased from CST (Danvers, US). TUNEL assay kit (#C1088) was purchased from KeyGEN BioTECH (Nanjing, China). ELISA assay kit of TNF-α (#BS-E9347M1), IL-1β (#BS-E8668M1) and IL-6 (#BS-E8682M1) were purchased from Boshen biotechnology (Nanjing, China).

Cell culture

HK-2 cells and NRK-52E cells were purchased from Procell Life Science&Technology (Wuhan, China), cultured in MEM (Biolnd, Israel) and DMEM (VivaCell, Germany) respectively, with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Biolnd) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin supplement. Mouse primary renal tubular epithelial cells (PRTC cells) were obtained from Procell Life Science&Technology and cultured in corresponding specific growth medium. The cells were maintained in a cell incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2.

Animals and treatment

Male C57BL/6J mice weighing 22–24g were obtained from Guangdong Medical Laboratory Animal Center (Guangzhou, China). Mice were housed in SPF conditions with 22 ± 2 °C temperature, 50–60 % humidity and 12/12 h light/dark cycle. They were randomly allocated into six groups: sham group (n = 8), model group (n = 8), Fer-1 group (2 mg/kg Fer-1, n = 8), SB-H group (100 mg/kg silibinin, n = 8), SB-M group (50 mg/kg silibinin, n = 8) and SB-L group (25 mg/kg silibinin, n = 8). Fer-1 and silibinin were dissolved in DMSO and administered intraperitoneally daily for 3 consecutive days before renal IRI surgery, while sham group and model group were administered with DMSO solution. All groups were performed renal IRI surgery to induce AKI as previously described [21], except sham group. Briefly, mice were administered 1 % pentobarbital sodium for anesthesia and placed in supine position. Utilizing a midline abdominal incision, bilateral renal pedicles were separated meticulously and clamped with nontraumatic vascular clips for 30 min. After the ischemia time was up, removed the clips, observed and confirmed the reperfusion of the kidneys. During the surgical procedure, mice were positioned on a heating pad to maintain body temperature. As a control, sham group mice underwent the same procedure except clamping the renal pedicles. All groups of mice were sacrificed at 24 h after reperfusion, the serum and kidney tissues were collected for later experimental detection. All animal experiments adhered to the guidelines outlined by the China Council on Animal Care and Use and received approval from the Animal Ethical Committee of Southern Medical University (Approval No. SMUL202311024).

Histological assessment, immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

The formalin-fixed kidney tissues were embedded in paraffin and sliced 4 μm thick. Then the kidney tissue sections were stained with H&E and PAS to assess renal histopathological changes. The degree of renal interstitial damage was scored from 0 to 4 [22]. Prussian blue staining was used to assess free iron deposition. For immunohistochemistry, sections were heated for antigen retrieval with Tris-EDTA buffer pH 9.0 for 10 min at 98 °C. F4/80 (1:1000), NGAL (1:200), and Kim-1 (1:200) antibody were used. The sections were visualized under optical microscope and the positive intensities of F4/80, NGAL and Kim-1 were evaluated by Image J. For immunofluorescence, sections were permeabilized with permeabilization buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) for 10 min. The sections were incubated with anti-FTH1 (1:200) and anti-NCOA (1:100) at 4 °C overnight. After secondary antibody incubation and staining of nuclei with DAPI, the sections were visualized using a fluorescence microscope.

TUNEL staining

The apoptotic cell death of kidney tissue was detected by TUNEL assay kit, according to the manufacturer's instructions. A fluorescence microscope was utilized for the observation of green TUNEL positive cell nuclei and photographed. Each photo was detected to estimate the ratio of apoptosis cells with Image J. TUNEL positive cell ratio: The number of TUNEL positive cells/total number of cells × 100 %.

Cell viability

Cell viability was evaluated with either the MTT or CCK8 assay. For MTT, HK-2 and NRK-52E cells were cultured in 96-well plates at a density of 7000/well and 5000/well, respectively, and treated with specific compounds depend on different groups (20 μM Erastin, 50 μM silibinin, 5 μM Fer-1). The plates were placed in incubator for 24 h. Then, 10 μL of MTT was added in each well, discarded after 4 h of incubation, and 100 μL of DMSO was added. The absorbance at 570 nm was measured. For CCK8 assay, 10 μL CCK8 solution (#521942, Bioshark, Hefei, China) was added to the HK-2 and PRTC cells, which had been treated with different compounds (1 μM RSL3, 30 μM silibinin, 5 μM Fer-1), and incubated for 0.5 h. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured.

Biochemical and ELISA assays

The concentration of Scr and BUN in the serum were detected using commercial assays kits (Nanjing Jiancheng, Nanjing, China). The homogenate of kidney and lysate of HK-2 and NRK-52E and PTRC cells was detected using commercial reagents for SOD, MDA and GSH (Nanjing Jiancheng). For measurement of Fe2+, cell ferrous iron colorimetric assays kits were used (Elabscience, Wuhan, China). The concentrations of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α in kidney homogenate were quantified using corresponding commercial ELISA kits following the manufacturer's instructions.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR)

The total RNA isolation kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used for obtaining total RNA of the kidney tissue or cells according the instructions. 2 μg RNA was used to synthesize cDNA using Evo M-MLV RT Kit (AG, Changsha, China). Quantitative PCR was performed using SYBR Green Pro Taq HS qPCR Kit (AG11701, AG). The relative mRNA expression were analyzed by the 2-△△Ct method, normalized to GAPDH. The primers utilized in this study are detailed in Table S1.

Western blot

Kidney tissues and cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. The total protein concentration was quantified using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Equivalent protein quantities were separated by 12–15 % SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. Following this, the membranes were blocked and subjected to an overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C. The primary antibodies used were as follows: anti-FTH1 (1:1000), anti-FTL (1:1000) anti-GPX4 (1:1000), anti–HO–1 (1:1000), anti-NCOA4 (1:1000) and anti-GAPDH (1:2500). Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 1 h at room temperature. Blots were visualized by the ECL immunoblot detection system and band intensities were analyzed using Image J software.

Cellular thermal shift assay (CETSA) and drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS)

CETSA and DARTS were performed to detect the interaction of silibinin with FTH1 as previously described [23,24]. For CETSA, the kidney tissues lysate was treated with either DMSO or silibinin (50 μM) for 3 h at 4 °C on a shaker. Then, the mixture was divided into different tubes and heated at different temperatures for 3 min. After cooling, the kidney lysate was centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent Western bolt analysis.

For DARTS, the kidney tissues lysate was equally divided into separate tubes and incubated at 4 °C for 3 h with either DMSO or various concentration of silibinin (50 μM, 25 μM, 12.5 μM). Then, the tubes were added with 20 μg/mL Pronase E (Roche, Switzerland) or not for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, loading buffer was added immediately to terminate the reactions and the samples were heated to prepare for Western bolt analysis.

JC-1

The mitochondrial membrane potential of HK-2, NRK-52E and PTRC cells was detected by using the JC-1 fluorescent probe (Beyotime), in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were treated with JC-1 solution and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. After washing, cells were observed and photographed using fluorescent microscope.

Lipid ROS detection

Lipid ROS of cell were assessed by flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were incubated with 5 μM BODIPY 581/591 C11 probe (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, US) for 30 min at 37 °C in dark. After washing, cells were analyzed by flow cytometry.

Co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Kidney tissues were lysed with IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor cocktails and the total protein concentration was assessed with BCA assay kit. The lysate supernatant was incubated with 3.675 μg NCOA4 antibody for 3 h at 4 °C and the protein A/G agarose mixture was added to captured the immunocomplexes. Then the immunocomplexes were washed 5 times with precooled IP lysis buffer, and samples were boiled in SDS-PAGE loading buffer and subjected to Western blot.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA)

HK-2 cells were cultured on 12-well plates and transiently transfected with siRNA targeting FTH1 (si-FTH1 sequence: GTCCATGTCTTACTACTTT) or the negative control, in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. The FTH1 siRNA and negative control were designed and synthesized by RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China).

Human proteome microarrays

The HuProt proteome microarray was performed by Wayen Biotechnologies (Shanghai, China) for identification of silibinin targeting proteins. Briefly, the microarrays were blocked with blocking buffer in the dark and incubated with Biotin and Biotin-Silibinin. After washing, the microarrays were immersed in 0.1 % Cy5-Streptavidn Solution. Then, the microarrays were washed again and centrifuged for drying. Finally, the microarrays were scanned with Axon GenePix 4000B scanner and GenePix Pro 6.0 software was used for data processing.

Molecular docking

The molecular docking was used to predict the potential binding of silibinin and FTH1. The structure of silibinin and FTH1 were obtained from PubChem (CID: 31553) and Protein Data Bank (PDB ID: 3AJO), respectively. Discovery studio software (Accelrys, San Diego, US) was utilized for binding site analysis and drawing. The binding affinity of silibinin with FTH1 was evaluated with LibDock score.

Surface plasmon resonance imaging (SPRi)

The binding activity between silibinin and FTH1 was detected by surface plasmon resonance imaging (SPRi) using the PlexArray® HT system (Plexera, United States). Briefly, a 3D photo-crosslinking chip with immobilized 10 mM silibinin molecule was prepared, and FTH1 protein at different concentrations (125 nM, 250 nM, 500 nM, 1000 nM and 2000 nM) were injected on the chip surface in order. Phosphate-buffered saline was served as a running buffer and 10 mM glycine-HCl buffer (pH 2.0) was acted as the regeneration buffer. The SPRi signal was measured at 23 °C at a flow rate of 2 μL/s. The BIAevalution Software (version 4.1, GE Healthcare) was used to analyze the data and obtain kinetic curves.

Statistical analysis

Graphing and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9. Experimental data were expressed as the mean ± SD. Statistical significance between groups was determined using a two-tailed t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by LSD or Dunnett post hoc test. P values of<0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Silibinin protects against renal dysfunction and pathological damage in IRI-AKI mice

To investigate the pharmacological effects of silibinin on kidneys, AKI model induced by bilateral renal ischemia-reperfusion was established (Fig. 1B). As shown in Fig. 1C–D, IRI-AKI mice manifested significant renal function impairment, the levels of serum BUN and Scr were significantly increased. IRI-AKI also caused significant renal congestion aera and severe pathological changes. H&E and PAS staining indicated remarkable tubular injury, including brush-border decrease, tubule lumen dilation, proteinaceous cast formation and necrosis (Fig. 1E–F, H). To verify the role of ferroptosis in IRI-AKI, we evaluated the effect of Fer-1, a ferroptosis inhibitor. Apparently, Fer-1 treatment mitigated renal function impairment and pathological damage in IRI-AKI mice. Similar to the results observed with Fer-1, silibinin treatment significantly alleviated IRI-AKI. To further confirm the renoprotective effect of silibinin, the protein expressions of Kim-1 and NGAL (two markers of renal tubular injury) were examined using IHC. The results showed the increasing trends of Kim-1 and NGAL induced by IRI-AKI, which were significantly abolished by silibinin treatment (Fig. 1G–I-J). The results were consistent with the mRNA expressions (Fig. 1K–L). To further examine if silibinin exerts protective effects after the onset of reperfusion injury, the mice were administrated with silibinin 30 min after ischemia. The results showed that silibinin was still effective in improving Scr and BUN levels, although the improvement was not as significant as pre-administration (Figs. S1A–C). Silibinin ameliorates renal macrophage infiltration and inflammation in IRI-AKI mice

Since tubulointerstitial inflammation is a common pathological feature of AKI, we next investigated the impact of silibinin on the inflammatory status of the kidneys in IRI-AKI. Renal macrophage infiltration was significantly increased in the IRI-AKI mice, as evidenced by increased F4/80 positive staining (Fig. 2A–B). Silibinin treatment obviously attenuated macrophage infiltration based on fewer F4/80 positive staining (Fig. 2A–B). ELISA assays of the inflammation-related cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α were performed to examine the changes in the inflammation process. Relative to the sham group, the model group showed a significant elevation in IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α levels, which were subsequently attenuated with silibinin treatment. (Fig. 2C–E). Consistently, the upregulated mRNA levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α induced by IRI-AKI were markedly rescued by silibinin treatment (Fig. 2F–H). TUNEL assay results showed that Fer-1 or silibinin treatment suppressed the increased kidney cell death in the model group (Fig. 3D–E). Western bolt and qPCR were used to measure the expressions of GPX4 and HO-1, two key ferroptosis-related proteins. Since iron metabolism imbalance is recognized as a crucial characteristic of ferroptosis, we next examined the iron content in the kidneys. The model group had increased levels of ferrous iron in kidney homogenates in comparison to the sham group, and the increased ferrous iron in the IRI-AKI mice were abolished by Fer-1 or silibinin treatment (Fig. 3K). The findings imply that the silibinin's inhibitory effect on ferroptosis could potentially be a mechanism by which it improves IRI-AKI.

Silibinin inhibits ferroptosis in renal tubular cell

Next, we established in vitro models with erastin-induced ferroptosis in renal tubule epithelial cells HK-2 and NRK-52E to confirm whether silibinin possesses anti-ferroptosis effect. MTT assay showed that cell death induced by erastin in HK-2 and NRK-52E cells was inhibited by silibinin, similar to the results observed with Fer-1. (Fig. 4A–B). We next determined the changes in lipid peroxidation. The results demonstrated that along with the increase of MDA, GSH and SOD levels were obviously decreased following treated with erastin both in HK-2 and NRK-52E cells. However, Fer-1 or silibinin reversed the effects of erastin on MDA, GSH and SOD expressions (Fig. 4C–H). Lipid ROS detected by flow cytometry also showed that silibinin significantly reduced the lipid ROS accumulation caused by erastin (Fig. 4I–L). Consistently, we confirmed that the erastin-induced overexpression of ferrous iron was reduced by Fer-1 or silibinin in both cell lines (Fig. 4M–N). The results of Western bolt and qPCR also indicated that silibinin treatment up-regulated GPX4 expression and down-regulated HO-1 expression in vitro (Fig. 4O–U). Furthermore, as mitochondrial damage is a prominent feature of ferroptosis, we next measured the mitochondrial membrane potential using JC-1 fluorescent probe and the results demonstrated that silibinin treatment alleviated mitochondrial damage in erastin-treated HK-2 and NRK-52E cells (Fig. 4V–Y). Additionally, as a more specific ferroptosis inducer, RSL3 was used to confirm the inhibitory effect of silibinin, and the results similarly revealed that silibinin significantly suppressed RSL3-stimulated ferroptosis (Fig. S2). Since primary cells more closely reflect the in vivo environment compared to cell lines, mouse primary renal tubular epithelial cells (PRTC cells) were utilized, and the results further verified the anti-ferroptotic properties of silibinin (Fig. S3). FTH1 plays a critical role in silibinin-mediated inhibition of ferroptosis

To further explore the underlying mechanism of silibinin in regulating ferroptosis, we identified the binding proteins of silibinin with HuProt human proteome microarray. Briefly, Biotin-Silibinin was synthesized and Biotin-Silibinin or Biotin were incubated with the proteome microarrays, the Cy5-Streptavidn (Cy5-SA) was used to identify the proteins binding with Biotin-Silibinin subsequently (Fig. 5A). As illustrated in Venn diagram, 546 candidate silibinin target proteins were identified (Fig. 5B). Referring to the above experimental results, we found that FTH1 was a key protein in the ferroptosis pathway (Fig. 5C). FTH1 showed great potential for silibinin-binding with Z-score of 3.158 and IMean ratio of 41.482, the higher Z-score and IMean ratio mean the proteins with better silibinin-binding capacity (Fig. 5D). To further investigate the interaction between FTH1 and silibinin, molecular docking, SPRi, CETSA, and DARTS analyses were performed. Fig. 5E showed the predicted interaction conformation of the FTH1-silibinin interaction. The molecular docking results showed that silibinin bound to FTH1 with ASN50 (2.67 Å), TYR54 (3.23 Å), and GLN141 (2.91 Å). Silibinin inhibits ferroptosis by disrupting the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction

Next, we further explored the potential mechanism in which the silibinin and FTH1 binding regulated ferroptosis. Since FTH1 can store iron and silibinin treatment reduced the amount of ferrous iron, we speculated the binding of silibinin and FTH1 might interrupt the process of ferritinophagy. Ferritinophagy is a type of selective autophagy that is mediated by NCOA4; it promotes ferroptosis through the degradation of ferritin [25]. To validate the hypothesis, we first examined the expressions of NCOA4 and FTH1 with Western blot analysis. The results demonstrated the expressions of FTH1 and NCOA4 were visibly downregulated in IRI-AKI mice, indicating that the occurrence of ferritinophagy [26]. However, silibinin treatment enhanced the expression of FTH1 and NCOA4 (Fig. 5K–M). Considering that FTH1 and FTL form a complex to regulate iron storage and release [27], we also examined the expression levels of FTL. Similarly, we found that the changes in FTL expression levels were consistent with those of FTH1 (Figs. S4A–B). Then, we carried out immunofluorescence assay to assess the location of FTH1 and NCOA4. Interestingly, although IRI reduced the expression of FTH1 and NCOA4, IRI strengthened the colocalization fluorescence of FTH1 and NCOA4, whereas silibinin reduced the colocalization puncta of FTH1 and NCOA4 (Fig. 5N). In order to validate that the binding between silibinin and FTH1 affected ferritinophagy mediated by NCOA4, we investigated whether silibinin abolished the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction with co-IP assay. As shown in Fig. 5O–P, the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction was significantly interrupted by silibinin. These results demonstrated that silibinin interrupted the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction to inhibit ferroptosis.

Knockdown of FTH1 alleviates the ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of silibinin

To further examine if ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of silibinin was mediated by FTH1, we knockdown FTH1 in HK-2 cells. The knockdown efficiency of FTH1 by siRNA was confirmed by qPCR. As expected, FTH1 siRNA significantly diminished the mRNA expression of FTH1 (Fig. 6A–B). Then, the transfected cells were treated with erastin and silibinin. MTT assay showed that the downregulated FTH1 diminished the protective effect of silibinin in erastin-treated HK-2 cells (Fig. 6C). As shown in Fig. 6D–F, FTH1 knockdown partially reversed the increased GSH and decreased SOD and MDA levels induced by silibinin treatment. Similarly, knockdown of FTH1 reversed the reduced lipid ROS levels (Fig. 6G–H) and ferrous iron levels (Fig. 6I) induced by silibinin treatment. In addition, the reduced expression of GPX4 in the HK-2 cells induced by silibinin treatment was abolished after FTH1 was knockdown (Fig. 6J–L). As revealed by JC-1 assay, the mitochondrial damage alleviated by silibinin in erastin-treated cells was also restrained by FTH1 knockdown (Fig. 6M–N). The results suggest that FTH1 plays a crucial role in the inhibition of ferroptosis by silibinin in the kidney. Furthermore, when FTH1 was partially knocked down, it resulted in a partial alleviation of the ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of silibinin. Discussion

This study demonstrated that ferroptosis was a significant pathological feature and plays a crucial role in IRI-AKI. Our data also showed that silibinin protected against renal dysfunction and pathological damage in IRI-AKI. Furthermore, we identified the mechanism by which silibinin exerted its protective effects, which involved targeting FTH1 and disrupting the FTH1-NCO4-mediated ferritinophagy process. This disruption leaded to the inhibition of ferroptosis and improvement of IRI-AKI. Overall, these findings suggest that silibinin can alleviate kidney damage by targeting FTH1 and reducing ferroptosis-mediated cell death. This study expands the potential of silibinin as a therapeutic agent for the treatment of AKI.

AKI is a heterogeneous syndrome associated with high morbidity and mortality and adversely affects patient long-term prognosis. Diagnosis of AKI relies on the increase of serum creatinine and/or the decrease of urinary output. However, both serum creatinine and urinary output are imperfect markers in detecting early stages of kidney injury [28]. Timely diagnosis and prevention of AKI remain challenging due to the lack of effective therapeutics for AKI. Silibinin is a naturally derived polyphenol with multitudinous biological and pharmacological activities. The efficacy of silibinin in improving liver disease and many other diseases has attracted substantial attention to the effect of silibinin on kidney disease. Previous studies showed that silibinin suppresses ROS-mediated MAPK signaling pathway and attenuates mitochondrial dysfunction in cisplatin-induced AKI [29,30]. Recent studies have reported that combining silibinin with valsartan or lisinopril enhances the anti-fibrosis effect on renal fibrosis [31,32]. These findings indicated that silibinin has a promising potential to improve kidney disease. Nevertheless, the effect of silibinin on kidney disease requires further investigated. In our study, we explored the therapeutic effect of silibinin in IRI-AKI model and reveal its underlying mechanism of action. Our results showed that, in comparison to model group, silibinin treatment reduced the levels of Scr and BUN, and improved the pathological damage as detected by H&E and PAS staining. The expressions of NGAL and Kim-1 were also significantly reduced after silibinin treatments. In addition, previous studies have highlighted the significance of inhibiting inflammation in alleviating kidney injury [33]. We found that silibinin treatment suppressed inflammatory infiltration as demonstrated by reduced F4/80 positive staining and the reduced levels of inflammation-related cytokines IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. These results indicated that silibinin can improve renal dysfunction, ameliorate pathological damage, and resolve inflammation, all of these strongly suggest silibinin is a potential therapeutic agent for the treatment.

Ferroptosis, as a new form of programmed cell death, has been widely studied in recent years and is considered to contribute significantly to the pathogenesis of ischemia-reperfusion injury. Using ferroptosis inhibitors or targeting ferroptosis-related genes can effectively improve ischemia-reperfusion injury of various organs [[34], [35], [36], [37]], which indicates that targeting ferroptosis may become a promising treatment strategy for ischemia-reperfusion injury [38]. To further investigate the renal protection mechanism of silibinin, we dissected the role of ferroptosis. Consistent with previous studies [21,39], our results demonstrated that inhibiting ferroptosis by Fer-1 attenuated the kidney injury of IRI-AKI. Iron overload and excessive lipid peroxidation have been proved to be critical factors in trigging ferroptosis [5]. Massive ROS production during ischemia-reperfusion, which causes lipid peroxidation and facilitates mitochondrial damage and eventually culminating in cell death. Meanwhile, ischemia-reperfusion injury is often accompanied by iron homeostasis imbalance [40], excessive free iron accelerates ROS production and lipid peroxidation via Fenton reaction, which exacerbate ferroptosis. We also verified the effect of silibinin on iron metabolism and lipid peroxidation. In this study, we found that silibinin treatment not only reduced ferrous iron and MDA levels, but also enhanced the GSH and SOD activities in both IRI-AKI mice and ferroptosis inducers-treated cells. Furthermore, the results from flow cytometry revealed that silibinin treatment inhibited lipid ROS production, and the JC-1 assays demonstrated that silibinin treatment alleviated mitochondrial damage. Taken together, our results support the idea that silibinin alleviates kidney injury by inhibiting ferroptosis. To further elucidate the molecular mechanism by which silibinin regulated ferroptosis, we used HuProt human proteome microarray to identify the proteins that bind to silibinin directly. A total of 546 candidate silibinin target proteins involving in various regulatory pathways were identified, which further suggests that silibinin is highly biologically active and possesses various pharmacological properties. Among the candidate target proteins, we focused on those closely that were related to ferroptosis. We found that FTH1 bound well to silibinin. FTH1 is an intracellular protein that plays an important role in maintaining iron metabolism homeostasis, through its ferroxidase activity and ability to store iron. Exogenous Fe3+ binds to transferrin and is taken into cells by transferrin receptor (TfR1). Then, Fe3+ is released from transferrin in an acidic environment and reduced to Fe2+ by the STEAP family [41]. Fe2+ released in the cytosol participates in the regulation of various metabolic processes and oxidative stress, whereas excessive accumulation of Fe2+ triggers the Fenton reaction and causes lipid peroxidation damage. The Fe2+ also binds to ferritin, which is composed of the light (FTL) and heavy ferritin chains (FTH1) and stored in a stable form. Thus, it is necessary to explore whether ferroptosis-inhibiting effect of silibinin was through FTH1. In our study, we first validated the interaction between silibinin and FTH1 with molecular docking and SPRi, which demonstrated a high binding affinity between silibinin and FTH1. Then, we performed CETSA and DARTS assays to confirm the stability of the binding between silibinin and FTH1. More importantly, we further explored the potential mechanism by which the binding of silibinin and FTH1 regulated ferroptosis. Growing evidence has indicated that autophagy is closely associated with ferroptosis and involved in regulating iron-dependent lipid peroxidation and ROS accumulation during ferroptosis [42]. The term ferritinophagy refers to the removal of the major iron storage protein ferritin through the autophagy mechanism. NCOA4 is a selective autophagy receptor that binds ferritin and mediates the subsequent autophagic degradation of ferritin [43]. Herein, we found the expression of FTH1 and NCOA4 underwent substantial downregulation in IRI-AKI mice, which was consistent with the previous study [44], but silibinin treatment reversed the decrease. We further detect the colocalization of FTH1 and NCOA4 by immunofluorescence, as well as the interaction of FTH1 and NCOA4 by co-IP. Our results revealed that ferritinophagy was obviously activated in IRI-AKI, whereas silibinin treatment abolished the NCOA4-FTH1 interaction and suppressed ferritinophagy. Furthermore, we found that knockdown of FTH1 in HK-2 cells diminished the anti-ferroptosis effects of silibinin. These results strongly suggest that FTH1 is a target of silibinin and silibinin exhibits anti-ferroptosis through FTH1.

Conclusion

In summary, our results suggest the significant role of ferroptosis in IRI-AKI and have revealed that silibinin protects against ischemia reperfusion induced renal injury by targeting FTH1 to inhibit ferroptosis. Our findings have provided robust scientific evidence supporting the potential therapeutic value of silibinin in IRI-AKI, which serve as a foundation for future development and research on the use of silibinin as a treatment option for IRI-AKI.
