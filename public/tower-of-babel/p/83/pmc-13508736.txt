Electrical Resistivity Change Upon Crystallization as a Robust Descriptor for Metallic Glass Forming Ability
Authors: Haechan Jo, Hongxi Duan, Mingxing Li, Liwei Hu, Yanhui Liu, Dongwoo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRapid identification of compositions with high glass forming ability (GFA) remains a major bottleneck in the discovery of new metallic glasses. Here we show that the electrical resistivity change induced by thermal annealing, a rapidly measurable parameter, serves as a robust descriptor of crystallization resistance for identifying high‐GFA compositions within given alloy systems. Combinatorial thin film libraries comprising ∼3500 distinct alloy compositions across multiple metallic glass‐forming systems reveal well‐defined compositional landscapes, in which regions of minimal resistivity change coincide with alloys exhibiting high GFA. This trend persists in melt‐spun ribbons and correlates directly with the crystallization kinetic parameters: alloys with smaller resistivity change exhibit lower Avrami exponents and higher activation energies of crystallization. As resistivity measurements require only seconds per composition point, more than two orders of magnitude faster than diffraction or calorimetry‐based characterization, the descriptor provides an efficient route for mapping microstructural stability across complex compositional spaces and enables rapid exploration of new bulk metallic glasses.Keywords: combinatorial synthesis, crystallization, electrical resistivity, glass‐forming ability, high‐throughput experiment, metallic glass

Introduction

Metallic glasses (MGs) combine densely packed short‐range order with the absence of long‐range periodicity [1, 2], enabling exceptional combinations of mechanical and functional properties [3, 4, 5]. Only a relatively narrow fraction of the vast MG composition space has been explored [6], though, emphasizing the need for rapid and scalable strategies capable of evaluating glass forming ability (GFA) across broad alloy ranges. Although numerous empirical and thermodynamic indicators of GFA have been proposed [7, 8, 9], most rely on labor‐intensive measurements, which limits their applicability for exploring vast compositional spaces. These constraints have driven growing interest in high‐throughput experimental methodologies [10, 11, 12] and machine‐learning workflows [13, 14, 15, 16] that can assess thousands of alloys in parallel.

Combinatorial thin‐film synthesis provides a powerful high‐throughput route for exploring vast metallic glass forming compositions [17, 18, 19, 20]. Magnetron sputter deposition with multiple metal sources readily produces amorphous films across wide compositional ranges, as the rapid quench suppresses nucleation and traps atoms in a low‐mobility configuration [21, 22]. When combined with spatial scanning measurement techniques, such as x‐ray diffraction (XRD) [23], nano‐calorimetry [24, 25], microstructural features and various physical properties can be efficiently accessed. Notably, the width of the primary amorphous halo in XRD (Δq) has been shown to reliably delineate high‐GFA regions [26]. Nevertheless, Δq may not always resolve closely spaced maxima in GFA. In addition, high‐density XRD mapping requires progressively longer acquisition times as the number of measurement points increases, potentially restricting compositional resolution in large combinatorial libraries.

Electrical resistivity provides a fast and sensitive measure of structural disorder, responding strongly to local atomic rearrangements, short‐range ordering, and crystallization‐induced structural changes, thereby offering insight into structural stability and transformation behavior [27, 28]. Previous studies on melt‐spun ribbons and bulk MGs demonstrated that resistivity responds sensitively to structural relaxation, the glass transition, and the onset of crystallization [29, 30, 31]. Recently, resistivity mapping combined with machine‐learning analysis has been shown to distinguish amorphous and crystalline states across combinatorial libraries with high fidelity [16]. Owing to its rapid scanning capability at sub‐millimeter spatial resolution, electrical resistivity measurement is particularly well suited for interrogating combinatorial thin‐film libraries with continuous composition gradients, enabling the resolution of fine compositional variations that often govern GFA [32].

Recent combinatorial studies have further revealed that composition regions exhibiting high electrical resistance often overlap with regions of high GFA [33], reaffirming that electrical resistivity encodes meaningful information about the stabilization of the amorphous structure of MGs. At first glance, this association appears to contrast with the traditional view that good glass formers are characterized by specific short‐range ordering that dominates the densely packed atomic structures [34], which might be expected to reduce electron scattering. However, advances in atomistic simulations and diffraction analysis have clarified this apparent contradiction. High‐GFA MGs are now understood to be characterized by a wide dispersity of distinct local atomic clusters [26, 35, 36, 37], which may increase electrical resistivity. The dispersity of local environments enables efficient space filling while simultaneously preserving pronounced topological disorder, thereby reconciling dense atomic packing with enhanced GFA.

Previous studies have shown that high‐GFA MGs exhibit a smaller increase in density upon crystallization, corresponding to a reduced density difference between the amorphous and crystalline states, which implies reduced structural contrast and lower atomic mobility during transformation [11]. In addition, the presence of multiple competing local atomic environments favors complex crystallization pathways involving several competing crystalline phases, rather than rapid evolution into coarse‐grained structures [25, 38, 39, 40]. Because electrical resistivity can be sensitive to structural ordering, its evolution during annealing captures the extent to which the disordered glassy structure reorganizes during crystallization. In this context, the resistivity change upon annealing reflects not only the magnitude of disorder in the as‐deposited glass, but also the resistance of that disorder to structural ordering, thereby providing a physically grounded kinetic descriptor of GFA.

Results and Discussion

Under the applied annealing conditions, low‐GFA compositions are expected to undergo more extensive crystallization with well‐developed crystalline domains, whereas high‐GFA compositions tend to exhibit less‐developed crystalline features together with locally disordered regions during annealing. Because crystallization in the present study occurs during post‐annealing (upon heating), the resulting microstructural evolution and resistivity response are likely to be influenced by the extent of crystal growth and long‐range structural rearrangement after the initial formation of nuclei or locally ordered regions [41]. Δρ/ρ i takes negative values upon annealing in general as reduced resistivity is found in annealed samples. To assess whether resistivity changes provide a robust and transferable measure of GFA, we investigated nine MG‐forming alloy systems that include binary, ternary, and quaternary systems. Each system spans broad composition ranges (Cu26‐86Zr14‐74, Cu29‐89Ti11‐71, Ni39‐81Ta19‐61, Ni4‐80Nb20‐96, Ni24‐96Zr4‐76, Zr15‐66Cu29‐78Al6‐22, Zr26‐75Cu14‐61Ti5‐39, Zr28‐77Cu14‐60Ag5‐36 and Zr30‐71Cu14‐27Ni2‐44Al9‐16). Overall, these composition ranges cover approximately 3500 distinct alloys at 1 at.% spacing. The alloy systems encompass diverse transition‐metal‐based alloy chemistries with well‐documented yet composition‐sensitive variations in amorphous stability, offering a stringent test of whether Δρ/ρ i consistently tracks GFA across alloy systems. As illustrated in Figure 1b, combinatorial thin‐film libraries were synthesized for each system by magnetron co‐sputtering, producing continuous composition gradients under identical deposition conditions.

Wafer‐scale resistivity mapping was then performed on both as‐deposited and annealed samples (Figure 1c). Spatially resolved four‐point‐probe measurements yielded resistivity landscapes, while energy‐dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) determined the local composition and XRD mapping identified amorphous regions in the as‐deposited state. XRD measurements after annealing revealed microstructural changes associated with crystallization across the entire wafer, enabling direct comparison with the corresponding Δρ/ρ i map.

To evaluate whether the resistivity trends observed in thin films reflect intrinsic behavior, representative compositions of the Cu‐Zr and Zr‐Cu‐Al systems were prepared as melt‐spun ribbons (Figure 1d), which were characterized by XRD, differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and four‐point‐probe measurements. Details of the experimental procedures are provided in “Methods” section.

Cu64Zr36 and Cu50Zr50 were reported to form bulk metallic glasses (BMGs) with critical diameters of ∼1mm [42]. Previously suggested GFA indicators, such as the reduced glass transition temperature (Trg = Tg /Tl , where Tg and Tl denote the glass transition and liquidus temperatures, respectively) [43], the γ parameter (γ = Tx /(Tg + Tl ), where Tx denotes the crystallization onset temperature) [44], and the electrical resistivity of the amorphous state alone (Figure S1a) fails to resolve the two GFA maxima in the covered compositional range. They identify only Cu56Zr44 as the single composition with high GFA. In contrast, the Δρ/ρ i clearly discriminates the two Cu‐Zr BMG compositions (Figure 2a). The powerfulness of Δρ/ρ i is also found in other binary systems, including Cu‐Ti, Ni‐Ta, Ni‐Nb, and Ni‐Zr (Figure 2b–e), where compositions reported to exhibit high GFA systematically coincide with small resistivity drops.

We next extend this analysis to ternary Zr‐Cu‐X systems (X = Al, Ti, Ag). In Zr‐Cu‐Al (Figure 2f), compositions with well‐established high GFA consistently lie within regions exhibiting only modest resistivity reduction. For instance, Zr50Cu40Al10 with a strong GFA characterized by a Dmax ≈ 16 mm [45] exhibits a substantially less negative Δρ/ρ i of approximately −9%. A similar less negative Δρ/ρ i region appears near Zr46Cu46Al8 with Dmax ≈ 8 mm [46]. In contrast, Cu‐rich compositions undergo resistivity decreases exceeding −50%, consistent with their poor GFA [47, 48].

A comparable behavior is observed in Zr–Cu–Ti (Figure 2g), where small Ti additions (7.5–12.5 at.%) are well known to enhance GFA in Cu‐Zr alloys [49]. This effect manifests as a narrow corridor of less negative Δρ/ρ i values (approximately −9%), encompassing Zr30Cu60Ti10 and Zr42.5Cu50Ti7.5 (Dmax ≈ 5 mm [42]). In contrast, Zr‐rich alloys exhibit resistivity drops up to ‐66%, indicative of a markedly stronger crystallization propensity in this compositional region.

A similar pattern emerges in Zr–Cu–Ag (Figure 2h). Minor Ag additions (∼10 at.%) produce a less negative Δρ/ρ i region that includes several well‐established BMGs, such as Zr50Cu40Ag10 (Dmax ≈ 5 mm [50]) as well as Zr45Cu45Ag10 and Zr36Cu54Ag10 (Dmax ≈ 6 mm [51]). In contrast, Ag‐rich compositions show resistivity reductions approaching −60%, indicating markedly accelerated atomic ordering and crystal growth.

These compositional trends persist in the quaternary Zr‐Cu‐Ni‐Al system (Figure 2l). Compositions known to form bulk metallic glasses (Zr51 − 53Cu19 − 28Ni9 − 12Al12 − 16 [52]) reside within regions of less negative Δρ/ρ i , whereas compositions prone to crystallization show substantially larger resistivity reduction. The persistence of this correlation across binary, ternary, and quaternary compositional spaces demonstrates that the resistivity‐based descriptor captures a consistent compositional trend within each alloy system, although the absolute magnitude of Δρ/ρ i varies across different systems.

As Δq is a well‐established structural indicator of GFA, we also characterized Δq for all systems through XRD mapping (Figure 2a–e,i–k,m) [26]. In most alloy systems investigated, compositions exhibiting less negative Δρ/ρ i align well with regions of large Δq, and these overlapping regions consistently host known high‐GFA compositions. This correspondence indicates that both descriptors capture a common underlying characteristic of good glass formers.

To quantitatively examine this relationship, we performed Pearson correlation analysis using the thin‐film combinatorial dataset across all investigated systems. A clear positive correlation between Δρ/ρ i and Δq is observed (r = 0.68), indicating that compositions exhibiting smaller resistivity reduction generally correspond to larger Δq values (Figure 3a).

This trend is consistently observed across individual alloy systems (Figure 3b), although the strength of correlation varies depending on the system. For instance, Ni‐Ta and Ni‐Nb exhibit strong correlations (r ≈ 0.92), whereas multicomponent systems such as Zr‐Cu‐Ni‐Al show relatively weaker correlation (r ≈ 0.63), reflecting increased compositional and structural complexity.

Note that the presence of systematic but non‐unity correlation suggests that the resistivity‐based descriptor is not merely a direct proxy for the peak broadening, but rather captures additional aspects of the crystallization response beyond the structural information reflected in Δq.

To verify that the observed electrical response is not specific to sputtered thin films, we also examined melt‐spun ribbons for two representative systems of Cu‐Zr and Zr‐Cu‐Al, spanning a wide range of GFA. These ribbons exhibit quench rates (∼105−106 K/s for melt‐spun ribbon [53]) that differ by several orders of magnitude from those of sputtered films (∼108−109 K/s [54]), providing a stringent test of whether the resistivity response reflects intrinsic crystallization behavior rather than processing‐specific effects.

The crystallization kinetics of ribbon samples, quantified by the Avrami exponent (n) and the activation energy (Ea ), show systematic compositional variations that closely track the resistivity response. As shown in Figure 5b,c, compositions exhibiting less negative Δρ/ρ i consistently display lower n together with higher Ea , consistent with sluggish crystallization kinetics characterized by strongly suppressed growth.

The GFA indicators derived from DSC measurements (Figure S13) also exhibit correlated compositional trends. The Trg (Figure 5d) and the γ parameter (Figure 5e) are highest in composition regions that also exhibit large Δρ/ρ i , low Avrami exponents, and high activation energies.

Note that the suppressed resistivity change in high‐GFA compositions cannot be ascribed merely to higher onset of crystallization temperatures (Tx ). Up‐scan DSC measurements on representative melt‐spun ribbons (Figure S13) reveal that alloys with stronger GFA do not consistently exhibit the highest Tx , yet they display markedly less negative Δρ/ρ i .

A Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to quantify the linear relationships between Δρ/ρ i and representative GFA descriptors (Figure 5f) for the ribbon samples. Δρ/ρ i shows a positive correlation with the XRD‐derived structural parameter Δq (rΔρ/ρi−Δq:0.51), further confirming the consistency between the resistivity‐based descriptor and structural indicators of GFA. In addition, Δρ/ρ i exhibits a negative correlation with the Avrami exponent (rΔρ/ρi−n:−0.57) and a strong positive correlation with the activation energy (rΔρ/ρi−Ea:0.79). It also correlates positively with the GFA indicators Trg and γ, with correlation coefficients of 0.64 and 0.48, respectively. These correlations demonstrate that the electrical response systematically reflects variations in intrinsic crystallization kinetics across compositions. The corresponding pairwise relationships are further illustrated by scatter matrix plots shown in Figure S14.

Previous atomistic analyses have revealed that high‐GFA metallic glasses exhibit pronounced structural heterogeneity, characterized by a broad distribution of distinct local atomic clusters [59]. Such heterogeneous local ordering enables efficient space filling while preserving pronounced topological disorder, which is closely associated with suppressed crystallization kinetics [60]. The coexistence of multiple local atomic environments further promotes competition among several energetically comparable crystalline phases [25]. Under the annealing conditions employed in the present study, these structurally heterogeneous states can hinder the progression of crystalline order during annealing [40]. Reduced Avrami exponents in high‐GFA compositions are also consistent with kinetically constrained crystallization behavior involving restricted long‐range structural rearrangement during post‐annealing.

Additional TEM analyses further support this interpretation (Figure S19). Under the limited annealing duration above Tx , low‐GFA compositions exhibit clearer nanocrystalline diffraction features, indicating more extensive development of crystalline order during annealing. In contrast, high‐GFA compositions show more diffuse nanocrystalline‐like scattering features, suggesting more constrained structural evolution during annealing.

In addition, high‐GFA compositions are known to exhibit small density changes upon crystallization [11, 47]. These structural and kinetic characteristics are manifested in the electrical resistivity response upon annealing.

Beyond these structural considerations, electronic factors can also influence the resistivity evolution during crystallization. Electrical resistivity in metallic glasses is governed by electron scattering and by the electronic density of states near the Fermi level. Prior studies have shown that certain high‐GFA alloys exhibit a reduced density of states at the Fermi level [28], which lowers the electronic free energy and stabilizes the amorphous structure. In such cases, the electronic contrast between the amorphous and crystalline states remains small, contributing to the modest resistivity decrease observed upon annealing.

It should be noted that the absolute magnitude of Δρ/ρ i varies across alloy systems because the electrical resistivity change upon annealing is influenced not only by crystallization resistance, but also by the electronic structure of the alloy, the resistivity contrast between the amorphous and crystallized states, the nature and complexity of the crystallization products, and the compositional complexity of the alloy system. Therefore, Δρ/ρ i should be interpreted as a system‐specific descriptor for identifying high‐GFA compositions within a given compositional space, rather than as a universal metric.

Because electrical resistivity directly reflects changes in both structural disorder and electronic structure, it provides a sensitive and rapid probe of glass stability. Compared with diffraction or calorimetry‐based high‐throughput characterization, resistivity measurements are more than two orders of magnitude faster, requiring only seconds per composition point. Moreover, the present approach does not require complex microfabrication, MEMS‐based device structures, or elaborate sample geometries. This simplicity makes the method experimentally straightforward and readily scalable for large‐scale compositional screening.

Conclusion

In this work, we demonstrate that the electrical resistivity change induced by crystallization provides a simple yet quantitative measure of crystallization resistance across a wide range of glass forming ability in metallic glasses. High‐throughput thin‐film libraries reveal composition‐dependent Δρ/ρ i landscapes that reliably isolate known high‐GFA compositions, and these trends persist in melt‐spun ribbons despite their distinct thermal histories. The correspondence between Δρ/ρ i , the Avrami exponent and the activation energy establish that the electrical response captures the intrinsic crystallization kinetics of metallic glasses. Physically, this relationship arises from the high sensitivity of electrical resistivity to topological disorder and heterogeneity, which can be preferentially retained during sluggish and kinetically constrained crystallization pathways characteristic of good glass formers. It has been demonstrated that Δρ/ρ i offers a practical and scalable route for screening crystallization kinetics and accelerating the discovery of bulk metallic glass compositions in complex, multi‐component alloy systems.

Methods

Sample Preparation

Combinatorial metallic glass thin films were deposited by magnetron co‐sputtering using multiple confocally arranged elemental targets under a base pressure of approximately 2.0 × 10−7 Torr. High‐purity targets of Zr (99.9 wt.%) and elemental Ni, Al, Cu, Ag, and Ti (≥ 99.99 wt.%) were employed as sputtering sources. Argon (Ar) was introduced as the working gas at a pressure of 3 mTorr. Deposition was performed on 4‐inch Si (100) wafers (525 µm thick) capped with a 200 nm LPCVD Si3N4 layer to prevent interdiffusion between the film and the substrate. DC power applied to each target was independently adjusted to generate continuous compositional gradients across the substrate during co‐sputtering. Prior to each deposition, all targets were pre‐sputtered for 10 min at 100 W DC power to remove surface oxides and contaminants. To mitigate delamination that may occur during post‐annealing, the film thickness was maintained at approximately 500 nm (Figure S15).

Metallic glass ribbon samples were fabricated using conventional arc‐melting and melt‐spinning techniques. Before alloying, all metallic elements were mechanically polished and ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol to remove surface oxides and contaminants. The arc‐melting chamber was evacuated to ∼ 6.0 × 10−6 Torr prior to melting. Master alloy ingots of the selected Cu‐Zr‐based compositions were prepared from high‐purity elements, Zr (99.9 wt.%) and Cu, Al (≥ 99.99 wt.%), under a Ti‐gettered Ar atmosphere. Each ingot was remelted more than five times with flipping to ensure chemical homogeneity.

The homogenized ingots were then crushed into small pieces and loaded into quartz tubes for melt‐spinning. The melt‐spinning chamber was evacuated to ∼ 6.0 × 10−6 Torr and back‐filled with high‐purity Ar. The alloys were induction‐melted and ejected through a nozzle onto a rotating Cu wheel (3000 rpm) under an Ar atmosphere, yielding continuous metallic glass ribbons with a typical thickness of ∼ 30 µm.

Crystallization Procedure

To induce controlled crystallization and examine the relationship between resistivity change and structural evolution, both thin‐film and ribbon samples were annealed under tailored thermal conditions suitable for each configuration.

Thin‐film libraries were annealed inside the sputtering chamber under high vacuum (∼ 2.0 × 10−7 Torr) to suppress oxidation. To further protect the film surface, two wafers were stacked face‐to‐face, with the Si3N4‐coated sides and the film surfaces facing each other. Samples were heated at 20 K/min to a target temperature above the reported onset crystallization temperature (Tx ) for each alloy system, based on literature values, and subsequently held for 10 min. Detailed annealing conditions for each alloy system are summarized in Table S1. This annealing condition was selected based on preliminary annealing temperature‐ and time‐dependent resistivity measurements (Figure S16). During annealing, the substrate was rotated at 10 rpm to promote temperature uniformity across the 4‐inch wafer. After annealing, films were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature inside the chamber before removal to avoid thermal‐shock‐induced delamination or cracking.

Ribbon samples were annealed in a horizontal tube furnace (SN‐1200, Henan Chuanghe Laboratory) under high vacuum (∼ 6.0 × 10−6 Torr). The annealing temperature was set to be identical to that used in the thin‐film experiments, corresponding to a temperature above Tx for both Cu‐Zr and Zr‐Cu‐Al alloys. The heating rate was identical to that used for thin‐film annealing (20 K/min). These conditions were selected to enable direct comparison of the crystallization behavior between thin‐film and ribbon specimens.

Characterization

The structural state of the films and ribbons was characterized by x‐ray diffraction (XRD, Empyrean, Malvern Panalytical) using Cu‐Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) in Bragg‐Brentano (θ–2θ) geometry at room temperature to distinguish between amorphous and crystalline phases before and after annealing. Prior to measurement, z‐axis height calibration was performed to ensure precise alignment between the sample surface and the diffractometer axis. The broad hump peak, originating from the amorphous atomic structure, was fitted with a Gaussian function to determine its full‐width at half maximum (Δq, FWHM), which is widely used as a structural indicator of GFA in MGs [26]. The dependence of resistivity changes on the resulting phase constitution, as well as the resistivity response of partially crystallized metallic glasses are presented in Section S4.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, DSC 404 F3 Pegasus, NETZSCH) was performed on ribbon samples to examine thermal stability and crystallization kinetics. Prior to DSC analysis, all ribbons were confirmed to be fully amorphous by XRD (Figure S18). DSC measurements were carried out at a constant heating rate of 20 K/min using alumina (Al2O3) crucibles under a high‐purity Ar atmosphere, with approximately 20.0 mg of ribbon sample per measurement. Non‐isothermal DSC measurements were conducted to determine the characteristic temperature, including the glass transition (Tg ) and onset crystallization (Tx ) temperature. Based on these results, isothermal DSC experiments were subsequently conducted at five temperatures within the range Tg < T < Tx . Each isothermal annealing process was continued until the exothermic peak had completely vanished, indicating the completion of crystallization at a given annealing condition.

The cross‐sectional image of the films was measured using a field‐emission scanning electron microscope (FE‐SEM, MIRA 4, TESCAN). The in‐plane compositional gradient of the combinatorial films was analyzed by Energy‐Dispersive X‐Ray Spectroscopy (EDS, Phenom XL, Thermo Fisher Scientific), capable of large‐area (4‐inch scale) mapping, enabling rapid screening across the entire substrate. To minimize substrate effects, compositional analyses were additionally performed on cross‐sectional regions at selected points. The local atomic structure before and after annealing of the ribbons was further analyzed by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM, JEM‐2100F, JEOL) for two representative compositions exhibiting high and low GFA. Selected‐area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns were obtained to confirm the amorphous and nanocrystalline structures.

The electrical resistivity of the thin films and ribbons was measured using a custom‐built 4‐point measurement system configured in a linear array with 1 mm probe spacing integrated with a motorized x‐ and y‐stage for high‐throughput mapping. Spring‐loaded contact probes were used to minimize surface damage during measurements. A constant current of 1 mA was applied, and the sheet resistance (R) was recorded using a digital multimeter (3058e, RIGOL).
