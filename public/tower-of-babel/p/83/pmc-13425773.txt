Cystic echinococcosis in Austria 2009–2024: national surveillance and two rare cases of musculoskeletal manifestations including clinical management strategies
Authors: Eva Kalcher, Marisa Valentini, Peter Ferlic, Jasminka Igrec, Iva Brcic, Ines Zollner-Schwetz, Vivien Brait, Jana Herret, Susanne Scheipl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCystic echinococcosis (CE), caused by Echinococcus granulosus, is a parasitic disease that predominantly involves the liver and the lungs, whereas primary involvement of the musculoskeletal system is rare and diagnostically challenging. This study examines isolated musculoskeletal echinococcosis (MSKE) through two uncommon cases treated at an Austrian tertiary centre and proposes a tailored diagnostic and therapeutic approach. To contextualise their rarity, national epidemiological data on CE in Austria over a 15-year period are also presented.MethodsWe queried our institutional database for cases of MSKE managed at an Austrian tertiary orthopaedic centre (2002–2024) and present these as case reports. In addition, we analysed all nationally reported cases of CE in Austria (2009–2024) using data from the electronic epidemiological reporting system.ResultsTwo patients with MSKE were treated at our centre. A 34-year-old Syrian woman presented with a slowly enlarging thigh mass, while a 53-year-old Turkish woman developed recurrent bilateral sacroiliac joint involvement 16 years following surgical resection of a primary hydatid cyst. In both cases, disease was confined to the musculoskeletal system.A total of 323 CE cases from all anatomical sites were included (median age 38 years, IQR 27–52; 50.8% male). Among cases with known infection origin, 53.6% were imported, and among those with available sample data, 81.2% were diagnosed via serum. The annual case count peaked in 2017 (n = 40) and was lowest in 2009, 2012, and 2015 (n = 4 each). A significant temporal trend was observed over 2009–2024 (p = 0.006).ConclusionHydatid cysts should be considered in the differential diagnosis of patients presenting with slow-growing musculoskeletal masses, particularly when initial imaging and clinical history suggest a parasitic aetiology. When localised, complete surgical excision with removal of the intact cyst is curative. Systemic anthelmintic treatment is administered perioperatively or as a conservative option in patients who are not suitable candidates for surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-10012-x.

Background

Echinococcosis is a zoonotic parasitic infection caused by the larval stages of four species of tapeworm belonging to the genus Echinococcus [1–4]. Of these, cystic echinococcosis (CE), caused by Echinococcus granulosus, is the most clinically relevant form in humans, with occasional involvement of the musculoskeletal system [3–7]. CE results from infection with the Echinococcus granulosus sensu lato species complex, comprising several distinct species, as well as E. felidis. Each of these is characterised by specific geographic distributions and host preferences [8, 9]. Alveolar echinococcosis (AE), caused by E. multilocularis [2], represents the second most clinically relevant manifestation. In contrast, the remaining forms - unicystic and polycystic neotropical echinococcosis, caused by E. oligarthra and E. vogeli, respectively - are only rarely observed in humans [10, 11].

CE occurs worldwide and has been reported on every continent except Antarctica. The disease is classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the most neglected and geographically widespread parasitic diseases [12–14]. In hyperendemic areas such as Argentina, Peru, East Africa, Central Asia and China the yearly incidence of CE varies from > 1 to 200 cases per 100,000 people with a mortality rate of 2% − 4% [1].

Transmission occurs through the ingestion of parasitic eggs present in food, water, or soil, or after direct contact with animal hosts. Humans thereby act as accidental intermediate hosts, meaning they become infected but do not transmit the parasite to definitive hosts. CE mainly affects domesticated animals such as dogs, sheep, goats, camels and yaks [15, 16]. After an incubation period of several years, the developed hydatid cysts cause symptoms related to the affected organs. The liver and lungs are most commonly affected, whereas involvement of the musculoskeletal system, kidneys, spleen, and central nervous system is less frequent [5, 6, 17]. Although uncommon, musculoskeletal involvement may mimic neoplastic or infectious conditions, frequently resulting in delayed diagnosis and inappropriate management [17–19]; hydatid cysts, the hallmark of CE, should therefore be included in the differential diagnosis of slow-growing soft tissue or osseous masses [17, 19].

Ultrasonography is the primary modality for screening hepatic cysts [20], complemented by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and/or computed tomography (CT), as well as serological assays for the detection of specific antibodies to confirm the diagnosis [1, 19, 21, 22]. In hyperendemic settings, WHO mass-screening programmes facilitate the identification and classification of hepatic cysts during the asymptomatic phase, enabling early intervention [15, 20, 23]. The lungs are the second most commonly affected organ [24], where ultrasonography has a limited role; diagnosis is typically established by chest radiography, either incidentally or in the context of respiratory symptoms following cyst rupture or secondary infection [25].

The treatment is costly [26], and its success depends on an early diagnosis. Four therapeutic approaches for CE have been described [2, 27]. Complete surgical excision of all cysts is curative. Alternatively, percutaneous management using the “Puncture, Aspiration, Injection, Re-aspiration” (PAIR) technique may be employed [3, 28]. Under ultrasound or fluoroscopic guidance, the cyst is punctured and approximately 50% of contents aspirated, followed by injection of contrast medium. After confirmation of cyst integrity, the remaining fluid is evacuated and a scolicidal agent (e.g. absolute alcohol or hypertonic saline [15% or 30%]) mixed with contrast medium is instilled. Following endocyst detachment after approximately 30 min, the contents are re-aspirated [12, 28, 29].

The anthelmintic drug albendazole is administered perioperatively or prior to intervention. In disseminated or inoperable disease, or in patients unfit for surgery, a conservative “watch and wait” approach with continuous albendazole therapy under clinical surveillance can be chosen. The recommended dose is 10–15 mg/kg/day (maximum 400 mg per dose), administered in two divided doses without treatment interruptions [30, 31]. Discontinuation of therapy is associated with disease progression.

Under the Austrian Epidemic Law [32], notifiable infectious diseases, including CE, must be reported through the epidemiological notification system (“epidemiologisches Meldesystem“, EMS) upon laboratory confirmation. Since late 2013, the Institute for Surveillance and Infectious Disease Epidemiology (INF) at the Austrian Agency of Health and Food Safety (AGES; www.ages.at) has overseen EMS data quality in close collaboration with local health departments, while ownership remains with the Federal Ministry for Labour, Social Affairs, Health, Care and Consumer Protection [33].

Given the rarity of the disease in Austria and its complex clinical presentation, diagnostic delays and inappropriate management are common. Furthermore, epidemiological data on CE in Austria and Central Europe are limited and largely outdated [34–36]. Musculoskeletal echinococcosis (MSKE) presents particular challenges for orthopaedic surgeons, and the lack of evidence-based surgical guidelines complicates management. By reporting our cases and examining the incidence of CE in Austria, including epidemiological trends over recent decades, this study aims to raise awareness and support clinical decision-making in diagnosis and treatment.

Accordingly, we focus on the diagnostic work-up and management of MSKE and provide an overview of CE epidemiology in Austria from 2009 to 2024 as contextual background.

Methods

Retrospective case series

We searched the in-house electronic database from the introduction of electronic records in 2002 to December 2024 to identify patients with MSKE treated at an Austrian tertiary orthopaedic centre. Cases were retrieved by screening ambulatory and discharge letters, as well as surgical reports, for relevant free-text terms (e.g., echinococ* and hydatid*) and their multilingual variants.

Data on patient’s demographics, clinical presentation, diagnostic modalities, treatments and follow-up were retrieved from the electronic files. The anonymised case reports comprised clinical information (age, sex, migration background, symptoms, surgical and medical management, and follow-up) alongside radiological, histological, serological, and/or molecular findings.

Collection and analysis of epidemiological data in Austria

Secondly, epidemiological data on CE cases in Austria were obtained from the AGES-INF surveillance dataset, covering January 2009 to December 2024. This pseudonymised, case-based dataset included demographic and epidemiological variables, namely age, sex, nationality, probable origin of infection, region of occurrence within Austria, hospitalisation status, sample material and diagnostic methods. Data on cyst localisation (e.g. hepatic, pulmonary, or musculoskeletal) were not available, precluding organ-specific analyses. Statistical methods

The extracted dataset comprised individual case records, with each row representing a confirmed CE case. Variables included categorical (sex, nationality, origin of infection, region of occurrence within Austria, hospitalisation status, sample material, and diagnostic methods) and numerical data (ID, age, and year). Descriptive analyses were performed to summarise variable distributions.

To assess temporal trends, annual case counts were aggregated and the null hypothesis of no change in incidence between 2009 and 2024 was tested. Incidence rates were modelled using log-linear quasi-Poisson regression to account for overdispersion, with yearly population included as an offset and year treated as a continuous variable. Results are reported as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) with 95% confidence intervals. A p-value < 0.01 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.1.

Results

Case reports of musculoskeletal echinococcosis, 2002–2024

A search of our hospital´s in-house electronic database identified 11 patients with CE: eight with hepatic cysts, one with pulmonary cysts, and two with isolated musculoskeletal involvement, the latter forming the basis of this case series.

Case 1

A 34-year-old woman presented with a painless, non-mobile swelling on the distal lateral aspect of her left thigh. She had first noticed the swelling two years earlier, with gradual enlargement over time. The patient was originally from Syria and had been living in Austria with her family for five years. She reported occasional episodes of diarrhea and abdominal pain but had no history of chronic disease, comorbidities, or immunosuppression.

Ultrasonography and MRI demonstrated a heterogeneous mass within the left vastus medialis muscle, characterised by large cystic components and a subtle surrounding capsule. The lesion was closely adherent to the fascia adjacent to the neurovascular structures, without evidence of fascial breach.

Based on the characteristic radiological findings (Fig. 1), parasitic infection was initially suspected. Additional lesions were excluded by hepatic ultrasonography, which revealed haemangiomas but no cystic changes. Serological testing was positive for both E. granulosus and multilocularis on Echinococcus IgG Western blot. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) yielded 35 Arbitrary Kinetic Enzyme (AKE) Units for E. granulosus and 30 AKE for E. multilocularis (reference < 20 AKE for both).

Particular care was taken to avoid capsular rupture to prevent contamination of adjacent tissues and potential anaphylactic reaction. The resection specimen is shown in Fig. 2.

A conventional PCR was performed using formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue samples at the Institute of Specific Prophylaxis and Tropical Medicine, Centre for Pathophysiology, Infectiology and Immunology, Medical University of Vienna, which confirmed E. granulosus as the causative agent. Anthelmintic treatment with albendazole (400 mg twice daily) was continued for 13 weeks postoperatively. Follow-up consisted of clinical assessment and hepatic ultrasonography. According to telephone follow-up, the patient has remained disease-free for two and a half years after surgery. No recent in-person follow-up was conducted at our institution due to the patient´s relocation to another federal state.

Case 2

A 53-year-old woman of Turkish origin presented in July 2009 with left-sided sciatica. She had previously undergone three surgical procedures for Echinococcus cysts in the sacral region at a hospital in Vienna. Initial biopsies were obtained in November 1993, followed by wound revision two months later due to postoperative dehiscence. In January 1994, definitive surgery was performed, including cyst excision with L5/S1 laminectomy and reconstruction using a gluteus maximus flap. The patient subsequently received albendazole therapy for two years (1993–1994). A recent MRI scan (Fig. 5) demonstrated residual and/or recurring cystic, lobulated lesions involving both lateral masses of the sacrum, the presacral region, and the left iliac bone. The patient had no neurological deficits, comorbidities, or immunosuppressive conditions.

Echinococcus serology (ELISA and IgG Western blot), performed at the Institute of Specific Prophylaxis and Tropical Medicine, Centre for Pathophysiology, Infectiology and Immunology, Medical University of Vienna, confirmed infection with E. granulosus. Concomitant lesions were excluded by hepatic ultrasonography and MRI.

Management was determined in close collaboration with the Division of Infectious Diseases, Medical University of Graz. Given the patient’s symptoms and radiological evidence of recurrent and/or residual cysts, an initial treatment strategy combining anthelmintic therapy and surgery was considered. Surgery was proposed following approximately 14 days of albendazole (400 mg twice daily), for a total preoperative duration of 26 days, with continuation of therapy depending on residual disease. However, owing to the size, anatomical complexity, and limited accessibility of the lesions, as well as the anticipated morbidity of surgery, the PAIR (puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration) procedure was considered as an alternative for symptomatic cysts.

Following interdisciplinary tumour board discussion, PAIR was selected as the preferred approach. As this procedure is palliative - reducing cyst viability and volume rather than achieving definitive eradication - lifelong albendazole therapy and active surveillance were deemed necessary.

The patient was admitted for PAIR in 2010 after approximately 14 weeks of continuous pre-interventional albendazole therapy. Pre-procedural MRI demonstrated a marked reduction in cyst size (Fig. 6). As a consequence, it was impossible to access the cyst with the aspiration needle, and the procedure could not be performed. In light of significant symptomatic improvement, no further intervention was undertaken, and conservative management with ongoing clinical surveillance was continued.

The patient was discharged with follow-up appointments scheduled every six months, including MRI to monitor lesion progression. The most recent follow-up at our institution was in December 2025.

Echinococcosis: overview of Austrian epidemiology, 2009–2024

Between 2009 and 2024, a total of 373 CE cases from all anatomical sites were reported in Austria. Of these, 50 (13.4%) were excluded following diagnostic review and data validation procedures (including changes in echinococcosis subtype classification) conducted in collaboration with the National Reference Laboratory. Excluded cases comprised those reclassified as AE, cases with negative serology, uncertain species identification, and duplicate notifications (Fig. 8). After applying these exclusion criteria, 323 cases remained for analysis (Table 3).

Within Austria, the largest proportion of cases was reported in Vienna (n = 129; 39.9%), followed by Lower Austria and Upper Austria (each n = 44; 13.6%), and Styria (n = 31; 9.6%). Among the 209 patients with available data on the origin of infection, 112 (53.6%) were classified as imported.

Information on nationality was available for 278 patients and reflected a heterogeneous distribution (Supplement 1). Austrian citizenship was most frequently reported (n = 70; 25.2%). Among patients with a migratory background, the most common countries of origin were Turkey (n = 39; 14.0%), Romania (n = 35; 12.6%), and Syria (n = 30; 10.8%). Nationality data were unavailable for 45 cases (13.9%).

Hospitalisation data were available for 239 patients, of whom 117 (49.0%) were hospitalised. No information was available regarding duration of hospital stay or disease severity. Sample material was reported for 282 patients, with serum samples analysed in 229 (81.2%). Diagnostic methods were specified for 287 patients, most commonly serological testing (n = 233; 81.2%).

The regression analysis demonstrated a statistically significant temporal trend (p = 0.006) in the number of reported CE cases between 2009 and 2024 (Fig. 9). The estimated IRR per year was 1.09 (95% CI 1.03–1.15), corresponding to an average annual increase of 9%.

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis spanning more than two decades, we identified two cases of MSKE treated at our tertiary centre, underscoring the exceptional rarity of this manifestation. MSKE accounts for only 0.5–4% of all cases of CE [5, 42], which is consistent with our findings of only two cases over a 22-year period. The rarity of MSKE further compounds its diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as limited clinical familiarity with its non-specific presentation increases the risk of mismanagement and associated serious complications.

To address this research gap, we present two detailed case reports aimed at raising awareness and supporting clinicians in the diagnosis and management of this rare entity. In addition, we analysed national EMS data over a continuous 15-year period to characterise the epidemiology of CE in Austria. To our knowledge, no previous study has examined echinococcosis in Austria over a comparable timeframe, allowing for a robust assessment of temporal trends.

Our national epidemiological analysis provides important context for these findings. Although the overall incidence of CE in Austria remains low compared with endemic regions [26, 34, 43], we observed a statistically significant increase in reported cases between 2009 and 2024. This trend suggests that CE - and, by extension, rare manifestations such as MSKE - may become increasingly relevant in the differential diagnosis of slowly growing musculoskeletal lesions, particularly in patients with epidemiological risk factors. More than half of cases with known origin were classified as imported, highlighting the influence of migration and travel patterns on disease epidemiology in non-endemic settings [15, 19].

Echinococcosis remains a global health concern, with cases reported on every inhabited continent and substantial impact on both human and animal health, as well as agriculture and trade [1, 12, 15, 16, 35, 44–46]. Despite advances in diagnostic techniques, including molecular and immunological methods, the true incidence and prevalence of echinococcosis remain incompletely characterised, particularly for rare manifestations such as MSKE [5, 42, 43].

Clinically, MSKE is marked by an insidious course, typically presenting as a slowly enlarging, painless soft tissue mass [6, 7, 47, 48]. In our series, one patient presented with such a lesion, while the other showed lumboradicular pain due to sacral involvement. These non-specific symptoms frequently lead to delayed diagnosis or misinterpretation as neoplastic or infectious conditions [17, 18]. Similar presentations have been described in the literature, with several comparable case reports identified [3, 7, 47, 48]. Ertem et al. [7] described a patient presenting with slowly progressive swelling and pain around the knee with negative serology but MRI findings suggestive of a parasitic cyst. The diagnosis was confirmed histopathologically following complete surgical resection, and post-surgical anthelmintic treatment with albendazole for three months was administered with uneventful recovery. A second report [47] highlighted an important diagnostic pitfall: a thigh swelling initially misidentified as an abscess was aspirated prior to MRI imaging, resulting in cyst rupture. Despite this, complete resection was achieved, and the diagnosis was histopathologically confirmed. This case underscores the importance of establishing the diagnosis before any interventional procedure to avoid mistreatment. Notably, the recurrence rate following intraperitoneal rupture of hepatic hydatid cysts has been reported at approximately 11% despite optimal adjuvant medical therapy [49], though comparable data for musculoskeletal lesions are currently lacking.

Accurate diagnosis relies primarily on imaging [20, 31]. Ultrasonography serves as an accessible initial modality, routinely used in the screening and monitoring of hepatic cysts [20, 23], whereas MRI is the investigation of choice for detailed characterisation of musculoskeletal lesions and assessment of their relationship to adjacent neurovascular structures [1, 19, 22]. Serology may provide supportive evidence but lacks sufficient sensitivity and specificity to exclude the diagnosis [38, 39, 50]. A critical pitfall is the performance of blind biopsy or aspiration, which may result in cyst rupture, dissemination, and potentially life-threatening anaphylaxis [6, 47].

Surgical management remains the cornerstone of treatment for MSKE [4, 6, 48]. Complete en-bloc resection of the cyst with an intact capsule represents the only curative approach and should be pursued whenever feasible [1, 2, 4]. In our case, successful R0 resection was achieved without intraoperative rupture. When complete resection is not possible due to anatomical constraints or extent of disease, the PAIR (puncture, aspiration, injection, re-aspiration) technique may be considered as an alternative, although it is generally regarded as palliative in musculoskeletal disease [4].

Adjunctive medical therapy is essential in all cases [27, 30]. Systemic antiparasitic therapy has been available for over 50 years and has substantially improved the treatment of echinococcosis [12, 27, 30, 31]. However, progress in the development of new drugs for this neglected disease has been limited, partly due to the lack of commercial incentives for pharmaceutical investment [51]. Currently, only two systemic agents are licensed for use in humans: the benzimidazole carbamates albendazole and mebendazole [27, 30, 41]. Albendazole, administered at 10–15 mg/kg/day, remains the first-line treatment due to its favourable bioavailability and ease of administration [27, 30, 52]. However, it is parasitostatic rather than parasiticidal [27, 30, 52], and optimal treatment duration has not been clearly established [27]. Current recommendations suggest initiation at least 24 h prior to intervention and continuation for 1–3 months following complete resection or PAIR, while lifelong therapy may be required in unresectable disease [27, 31]. Monitoring for adverse effects, including hepatotoxicity and neutropenia, is mandatory. Praziquantel is not routinely used in the treatment of human echinococcosis but may be administered as adjunctive therapy, particularly in cases of cyst rupture, to reduce the risk of secondary dissemination [53, 54].

Given these complexities, a structured and interdisciplinary approach is crucial. Early recognition, appropriate imaging, avoidance of invasive diagnostic procedures, and careful surgical planning are key to preventing complications and improving outcomes [1, 2, 6]. The algorithm proposed in this study aims to support clinicians in navigating these challenges and standardising care in suspected cases of MSKE.

The epidemiological data presented in this study highlight the need for awareness in non-endemic regions; although the 323 confirmed CE cases in Austria remain low in absolute terms, they provide important insight into disease occurrence in such settings. Annual case numbers varied considerably, ranging from four cases in 2009, 2012, and 2015 to a peak of 40 in 2017, with regression analysis confirming a statistically significant temporal trend. The observed increase in CE cases, together with the substantial proportion of imported infections and the predominance of cases in urban centres such as Vienna, likely reflects both migration patterns and referral bias to specialised institutions [16]. The median age was 38 years (range 6–97), with a near-equal sex distribution, consistent with previous reports indicating that CE affects all age groups, with a slight predominance in younger adults [43].

Among patients with known origin of infection, more than half (n = 112; 53.6%) were classified as imported, underscoring the role of migration and travel in the epidemiology of CE [37, 55, 56]. Although Austrian citizens constituted the largest nationality group (n = 70; 25.2%), many patients originated from endemic countries, such as Turkey, Romania, and Syria, highlighting the need for targeted awareness and screening strategies in migrant populations [24, 37, 56].

Hospitalisation was required in approximately half of cases with available data (n = 117/239; 49.0%), indicating a considerable clinical burden even in a low-incidence setting [16, 24, 26]. Serology was the most frequently used diagnostic method and was performed in over 80% of patients for whom information on diagnostic methods was available; however, it serves only as a supportive tool in clinical decision making [38, 39, 50]. While ultrasonography remains the primary modality for hepatic CE [20], particularly in screening settings [23], MRI is central for the evaluation of musculoskeletal involvement [22].

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design introduces potential and information bias and limits the ability to control confounding factors or draw causal inferences. However, given the rarity of MSKE, prospective studies are unlikely to be feasible. The epidemiological analysis is constrained by incomplete data within the EMS, including missing information on cyst localisation, which precluded organ-specific analyses. Consequently, our analysis encompasses all reported CE cases regardless of organ involvement and primarily provides contextual insight rather than MSKE-specific evidence. In addition, differentiation between E. granulosus and E. multilocularis is not always reliable in routine practice. Serological methods lack species specificity, and definitive diagnosis often requires histopathology or PCR following surgical intervention, which is not consistently performed. In such cases, classification may rely on indirect information, such as the presumed origin of infection. Data completeness may also have been affected by modifications to the EMS and increased workload during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Despite these limitations, our study provides detailed clinical insights into this uncommon manifestation and, in combination with national epidemiological data, contributes to a better understanding of its clinical relevance.

Conclusion

Echinococcosis remains an important and potentially under-recognised public health and socioeconomic concern in Austria and Central Europe. The observed increase in CE incidence over the 15-year study period suggests that this diagnosis is likely to gain clinical relevance, particularly in the context of ongoing migration from endemic regions.

Although musculoskeletal involvement is rare, it should be considered in patients presenting with slowly growing soft tissue masses and a relevant exposure history, even in non-endemic settings. En-bloc resection of intact cysts remains the only curative treatment for MSKE and should be combined with antiparasitic therapy to minimise recurrence risk and prevent complications such as anaphylaxis and dissemination.

Improved clinical awareness, structured diagnostic pathways, and interdisciplinary management are essential to optimise outcomes. Strict adherence to established principles - including avoidance of unplanned invasive procedures, appropriate perioperative anthelmintic therapy, and long-term follow-up - is critical to reducing morbidity and preventing potentially life-threatening complications.
