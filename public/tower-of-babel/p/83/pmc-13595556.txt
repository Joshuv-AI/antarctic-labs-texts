Latent profiles of health insurance literacy, numeracy and supplementary voluntary health insurance enrollment
Authors: Tong Liu, Lei Liu, Lizheng Xu, Chaofan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow enrollment threatens the sustainability of China’s voluntary health insurance system. Limited health insurance literacy and numeracy may create a cognitive barrier to informed decision-making regarding enrollment. This study aims to identify latent profiles of health insurance literacy and numeracy, and to evaluate their associations with Supplementary Voluntary Health Insurance (SVHI) enrollment.MethodsA cross-sectional survey utilizing face-to-face computer-assisted personal interviews was conducted between July and August 2024 across three Chinese provinces (Shandong, Henan, and Sichuan). Using multistage stratified sampling, 1,326 valid responses were collected from residents aged 18 years or older (out of 1,359 approached). Health insurance literacy was assessed using a validated scale comprising self-rated abilities and an objective knowledge quiz, while numeracy was measured via an adapted 3-item General Numeracy Scale. Latent profile analysis identified unobserved cognitive subgroups. Multivariable logistic regression with robust standard errors evaluated the associations between latent profiles and SVHI enrollment, adjusting for relevant covariates.ResultsA total of 1,326 participants were included in the analysis, of whom 369 (27.8%) were male and 610 (46.0%) were aged between 18 and 44 years. Of the participants, 195 (14.7%) had an education level of elementary school or lower. Three distinct cognitive profiles were identified: Profile 1 (“High health insurance literacy, High numeracy”, 32.9%), Profile 2 (“High health insurance literacy, Low numeracy”, 52.0%), and Profile 3 (“Low health insurance literacy, Low numeracy”, 15.1%). In multivariable logistic regression, participants in profile 1 (OR = 2.14; 95% CI, 1.23–3.70) and profile 2 (OR = 2.40; 95% CI, 1.43–4.02) were significantly more likely to enroll in SVHI than those in Profile 3.ConclusionsThis study found that distinct cognitive profiles of health insurance literacy and numeracy were associated with SVHI enrollment. Individuals with low health insurance literacy and low numeracy were significantly less likely to enroll in SVHI than those with higher levels of both. These findings may inform targeted communication and support strategies to improve equitable access to SVHI among populations with lower health insurance literacy and numeracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00814-z.

Background

Health insurance schemes serve as critical mechanisms for improving access to healthcare, promoting health outcomes, and providing financial protection [1–3]. Over the past several decades, China has made substantial progress toward universal health coverage (UHC) through the rapid expansion of its basic medical insurance system. China’s medical security system is now organized as a multi-layered framework, with basic medical insurance as the core, supplemented by critical illness insurance, medical assistance, and other supplementary protection mechanisms. The basic medical insurance system mainly consists of the Urban Employee Basic Medical Insurance (UEBMI), which covers formal employees and retirees, and the Urban and Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance (URRBMI), which covers residents not enrolled in UEBMI, including unemployed residents, students, children, and older adults without formal employment-based coverage [4]. China’s medical insurance coverage increased from 13.4% in 2003 to 94.6% in 2023, indicating near-universal medical insurance coverage [5]. However, partly due to limited benefit packages, low reimbursement rates and ceilings, the incidence of catastrophic health expenditure (CHE) remains high in China [6]. Moreover, disparities in benefit coverage and financial protection across insurance schemes may continue to reinforce socioeconomic and urban–rural inequalities in healthcare access and health outcomes, suggesting that near-universal coverage alone is insufficient to ensure equitable protection [7, 8].

Since 2020, Huimin insurance has rapidly emerged as an inclusive form of commercial supplementary voluntary health insurance (SVHI) within China’s multi-layered medical security system. It is characterized by government guidance and market-based operation: local governments provide policy endorsement and implementation support, whereas commercial insurers undertake product operation and risk management [9]. Most Huimin insurance products require residents to be enrolled in local basic medical insurance and feature relatively low entry barriers, with limited underwriting restrictions related to age, occupation, health status, or pre-existing conditions [10]. Huimin insurance is commonly promoted with the participation of local governments, while enrollment is often facilitated through offline service outlets and online platforms. Enrollment is voluntary and generally renewed on an annual basis. This arrangement gives local programs flexibility to revise premiums, benefit packages, and reimbursement rules in light of claims experience, actuarial considerations, and local medical security priorities. Unlike national insurance schemes, Huimin insurance is organized at the municipal level and adapted to local healthcare capacity, socioeconomic conditions, and residents’ ability to pay. As a result, although Huimin insurance products serve a broadly similar supplementary function, their core design features—including premiums, deductibles, benefit ceilings, covered services, and reimbursement rules—vary across cities, as illustrated by the products available in the six sampled cities (see Additional file 1). With relatively affordable premiums, typically ranging from USD 7 to USD 56 per year in 2023, Huimin insurance provides supplementary protection for medical expenses insufficiently covered by basic medical insurance, including out-of-pocket expenses within the basic medical insurance reimbursement catalog, medical expenses outside the basic medical insurance reimbursement catalog, and high-cost specialty drug coverage [11, 12]. However, participation in Huimin insurance remains limited, with an average enrollment rate of 19.3% across all cities [10].

Understanding the determinants of enrollment is important for promoting SVHI uptake. Previous studies demonstrate that health insurance enrollment is driven by individuals’ sociodemographic characteristics, health status and access to healthcare. Sociodemographic factors, such as age, education, income and risk preference, are associated with insurance enrollment decisions [13–16]. Suboptimal health status and the presence of diseases are associated with insurance demand [13, 14, 16, 17]. Furthermore, the accessibility and availability of healthcare services are closely correlated with enrollment decisions [13, 14, 16, 17].

In recent years, researchers in the United States have focused on how the understanding of health insurance terms and details, such as health insurance literacy and numeracy, influences individuals’ enrollment decisions [18]. Given that insurance directly impacts an individual’s financial and health-related well-being, a comprehensive understanding of complex terms—including premiums, deductibles, reimbursement rates, ceilings, benefit packages, and provider networks—has become increasingly important for individuals to make informed enrollment choices. Health insurance literacy (HIL) is defined as an individual’s capacity to find and evaluate information about health insurance plans, select the most appropriate plan based on financial capacity and health status, and effectively utilize the plan upon enrollment [19].

Previous research in the United States indicates that higher HIL is associated with a stronger inclination to enroll in health insurance [20–22]. Similarly, evidence from China suggests that individuals familiar with relevant policies were 2.14 times more likely to express willingness to enroll than those unfamiliar with such policies [23]. Numeracy, defined as the ability to understand and calculate probabilities, represents another critical cognitive factor influencing health insurance enrollment. Relative to individuals with higher numeracy, those with lower numeracy exhibit a significantly reduced likelihood of enrolling in health insurance [24, 25].

Based on prior research, HIL reflects the ability to acquire, understand, evaluate, and use insurance information, whereas numeracy supports the quantitative evaluation of financial risk and cost-sharing arrangements [24, 26, 27]. As distinct but complementary cognitive dimensions, HIL and numeracy may jointly shape individuals’ informed enrollment decisions [28]. Because these abilities may vary across individuals, some individuals may have adequate insurance-related knowledge but limited numeracy, whereas others may experience deficits in both domains. Therefore, analyzing HIL and numeracy only as separate variables may obscure meaningful HIL–numeracy configurations associated with voluntary enrollment. A person-centered approach, such as latent profile analysis (LPA), can address this limitation by identifying unobserved cognitive subgroups based on different combinations of HIL and numeracy. This provides the methodological rationale for examining the association between cognitive profiles and SVHI enrollment. Accordingly, we developed a conceptual framework in which subjective HIL, objective HIL knowledge, and numeracy serve as indicators of latent cognitive profiles, which are subsequently examined in relation to SVHI enrollment after adjusting for relevant covariates (see Fig. 1).

Several critical research gaps remain. First, while HIL and numeracy have been extensively studied in the United States, empirical evidence within the Chinese population remains scarce. Second, prior literature has largely examined HIL and numeracy in isolation. However, low HIL and numeracy often coexist and may exert a combined influence on insurance decision-making [29–31]. Third, existing research has frequently overlooked distinct cognitive configurations within the general population, thereby limiting the identification of vulnerable subgroups who may benefit from targeted educational or communication support. Latent profile analysis (LPA) offers a suitable data-driven, person-centered approach to identify unobserved subgroups based on a set of selected variables—a critical step for developing personalized interventions for specific sub-populations [32, 33].

We aimed to present the distribution of HIL and numeracy in China, identify their latent profiles, and examine the associations between these profiles and SVHI enrollment.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional survey was conducted from July to August 2024. A multistage stratified sampling strategy was employed. In the first stage, three provinces with diverse geographical distributions and various levels of economic development across eastern (Shandong), central (Henan) and western (Sichuan) regions were chosen as representative examples for this study. In the second stage, two cities were selected from each province based on their economic development levels. In the final stage, one urban district and one rural county were randomly selected from each city. The sample size for each location was determined based on the requirements for discrete choice experiments (DCE) used to elicit SVHI preferences, for which a minimum of 100 respondents was deemed sufficient [34]. Accounting for a potential 10% non-response rate, 110 residents from each urban district or rural county were invited to participate, resulting in a total target sample size of 1,320. Ultimately, 1,359 residents were recruited from six urban districts and six rural counties, yielding 1,326 valid responses. The overall valid response rate was 97.6%.

Dependent variable

The primary outcome variable in this study was enrollment in SVHI. This variable was operationalized as a binary variable based on participants’ self-reported enrollment status in Huimin insurance (classified as “yes” or “no”).

Independent variables

HIL was assessed using the Health Insurance Literacy Measurement Tool developed for the Chinese context. The instrument was constructed based on Paez et al.'s conceptual model and underwent rigorous scale-development processes, including literature review, two rounds of expert consultation, cognitive interviews, and empirical validation. The final scale comprises two components: self-rated scale (9 items, see Additional file 2, eTable 1) and objective health insurance knowledge quiz (8 items, see Additional file 2, eTable 7). The self-rated scale consisted of three dimensions: the ability to collect insurance information (3 items), compare insurance schemes (3 items), and enroll in and utilize insurance (3 items). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale, yielding a total score ranging from 9 to 45. The knowledge quiz was scored as 1 for correct, 0 for incorrect or “don’t know”, with a total score range of 0–8. Higher scores indicated higher levels of HIL.

In the current sample, the self-rated HIL scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.920 and McDonald’s ω = 0.934. The objective HIL knowledge quiz showed acceptable internal consistency, with Cronbach’s α = 0.731. In addition, objective HIL knowledge was positively correlated with the three self-rated HIL dimensions (r = 0.384–0.423, all p < 0.01). (Additional reliability and validity evidence for the HIL measures is provided in Additional file 2, eTables 2–6.)

Participants’ numeracy was assessed using an adapted version of the 3-item General Numeracy Scale (see Additional file 2) [35]. For each item, respondents were presented with five quantitative answer options and an additional “I don’t know” option. Responses were scored dichotomously: 1 point was awarded for a correct answer, and 0 points were assigned for an incorrect answer or a response of “I don’t know.” The item scores were then summed to create a composite numeracy score ranging from 0 to 3. In the current sample, the scale showed acceptable reliability and a single-factor structure (see Additional file 2, eTable 8).

Covariates

Based on prior studies on health insurance enrollment behavior, this study included sociodemographic, health-related, and risk preference variables as covariates. Sociodemographic variables included sex, age, educational attainment, place of residence (urban or rural), enrollment in basic medical insurance type, household monthly income per capita, and number of offspring [36–38]. The type of basic medical insurance was categorized into UEBMI and URRBMI [36, 38, 39]. Health-related variables, selected in line with commonly used indicators in prior research, comprised self-rated health, the presence of chronic diseases, and inpatient experience in the past year [37, 38, 40]. Individual risk preference was measured using a self-reported investment choice question, in which respondents were asked to indicate their preferred investment option given a hypothetical sum of money (high risk–high return, moderate risk–moderate return, low risk–low return, or unwillingness to take any investment risk). Prior studies on voluntary health insurance have shown that greater risk aversion was significantly associated with a higher probability of enrollment, justifying the inclusion of risk preference as a control variable in the model [41].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the sociodemographic and health-related characteristics of the participants. Categorical variables (e.g., sex, age group, education) were reported as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Due to the non-normal distribution of continuous variables (self-rated ability, objective health insurance knowledge and numeracy), data were presented as medians and interquartile ranges (IQR, Q1–Q3). Differences in cognitive indicators across latent profiles were examined using Kruskal–Wallis rank-sum tests, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests with Bonferroni correction for post-hoc comparisons.

LPA was performed to identify subgroups characterized by varying patterns of HIL and numeracy indicators. All indicator variables were z-standardized prior to analysis. Models ranging from one to six profiles were estimated, and several statistical criteria were employed to determine the optimal number of latent profiles. We examined information criteria, including the Akaike information criterion (AIC), the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the sample-size adjusted BIC (aBIC), with lower values indicating better relative fit [42]. The bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) and the Lo-Mendell-Rubin (LMR) test were employed to assess whether each k-profile model significantly improved over the (k–1) profile model, with a significant p indicating a superior fit for the k-profile model [43]. Entropy was used to evaluate classification accuracy, with values above 0.8 considered acceptable [44]. We also considered the proportion of cases in each class, avoiding solutions where the smallest class contained less than 5% of the sample [44]. The final number of profiles was determined by jointly considering these statistical criteria and theoretical interpretability [44].

Following profile identification, a chi-square (χ²) test was used to examine the unadjusted association between cognitive profiles and SVHI enrollment status (1 = enrolled, 0 = not enrolled). Prior to fitting the multivariable logistic regression models, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using generalized variance inflation factors (GVIF). Finally, multivariable logistic regression models with robust standard errors were employed to examine the adjusted association between distinct cognitive profiles of HIL and numeracy and SVHI enrollment, adjusting for all sociodemographic, health-related, and risk preference covariates. Model performance was further evaluated using Nagelkerke pseudo-R² and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Given the overrepresentation of women in the sample, we additionally tested sex-by-profile interaction terms in the multivariable logistic regression model to examine whether the association between cognitive profiles and SVHI enrollment differed by sex.

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.2). All statistical tests were two-tailed, and a p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of the study population

Of the 1,326 included participants, 72.2% were women, and the largest age group was 18–44 years (46.0%). In terms of socioeconomic characteristics, 24.5% had received a college education or above, 51.2% lived in urban areas, and 44.3% had a monthly per capita household income of USD 138.89–347.22. Regarding health status, 24.0% of the participants reported having chronic diseases, while the vast majority (84.5%) had not been hospitalized within the past year. Regarding basic medical insurance type, 56.2% were enrolled in UEBMI and 43.8% in URRBMI. Most participants (96.5%) exhibited low-to-moderate risk preferences. The median scores for self-rated ability, objective health insurance knowledge and numeracy were 31 (26–36), 6 (4–7) and 1 (0–2), respectively (see Table 1).

In contrast, the five-profile solution included a small subgroup representing only 5.13% of the sample, raising concerns about potential over-extraction and reduced profile stability. Further comparison of the three- and five-profile solutions suggested that the five-profile model mainly subdivided cognitive patterns already captured by the three-profile solution, rather than identifying qualitatively distinct new profiles. In the five-profile solution, Profile 4 and Profile 5 showed very similar median values for self-rated HIL, objective HIL knowledge, and numeracy: 33 vs. 32, 6 vs. 5, and 0 vs. 1, respectively. In addition, the newly separated Profile 2 still showed values below the overall medians across all three indicators, indicating that it remained within the broader “low HIL, low numeracy” pattern (see Additional file 3).

Therefore, balancing parsimony, interpretability, and practical usefulness, the three-profile model was retained. Ultimately, three distinct profiles were identified based on participants’ standardized Z-scores for self-rated ability, objective health insurance knowledge, and numeracy (see Fig. 2).

Description of the latent profiles

The three latent profiles were labeled as Profile 1 (High HIL, High Numeracy), Profile 2 (High HIL, Low Numeracy), and Profile 3 (Low HIL, Low Numeracy). The distribution of these profiles was as follows: Profile 1 (High HIL, High Numeracy) constituted 32.9% (n = 436), Profile 2 (High HIL, Low Numeracy) accounted for 52.0% (n = 690), and Profile 3 (Low HIL, Low Numeracy) represented 15.1% (n = 200). Normality assessment indicated that the three LPA indicators were not normally distributed (all Shapiro–Wilk p < 0.001; see Additional file 4, eTable 1). Therefore, Kruskal–Wallis tests were used and showed significant differences across the three profiles for self-rated HIL, objective HIL knowledge, and numeracy (all p < 0.001). Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons further showed significant pairwise differences across profiles for all three indicators (all p < 0.001). Profiles 1 and 2 exhibited similarly high scores for self-rated ability (medians, 34 and 32) and objective knowledge (both medians = 6), significantly surpassing the scores of Profile 3 (medians, 22 and 2). Conversely, regarding numeracy, Profile 1 scored the highest (median, 3), while Profiles 2 and 3 exhibited consistently low levels (both medians = 0) (see Table 3).

(see Additional file 4, eTable 2) Table 5 presents the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis with robust standard errors examining the associations between individual characteristics and SVHI enrollment. After adjusting for covariates, cognitive profiles remained strongly associated with SVHI enrollment. Compared with the reference group (Profile 3: Low HIL, Low Numeracy), individuals in Profile 1 (High HIL, High Numeracy) had significantly higher odds of enrolling in SVHI (OR = 2.14, 95% CI, 1.23–3.70). Similarly, membership in Profile 2 (High HIL, Low Numeracy) was also associated with increased odds of enrollment (OR = 2.40, 95% CI, 1.43–4.02). Among demographic characteristics, respondents aged 45–59 years were more likely to enroll in SVHI than those aged 18–44 years (OR = 1.66, 95% CI, 1.13–2.43), whereas urban residents had significantly lower odds of enrollment compared with rural residents (OR = 0.65, 95% CI, 0.49–0.86). The full model showed modest explanatory and discriminatory performance, with a Nagelkerke pseudo-R² of 0.071 and an AUC of 0.645 (95% CI: 0.611–0.679). Collectively, these findings suggest that cognitive profiles characterized by HIL and numeracy, along with age and place of residence, were significantly associated with SVHI enrollment.

The overall sex-by-profile interaction was not statistically significant based on a Wald χ² test (χ² = 3.35, df = 2, p = 0.187), suggesting that the association between cognitive profiles and SVHI enrollment did not differ significantly by sex. Discussion

This cross-sectional study identified three distinct HIL–numeracy profiles and found that these profiles were significantly associated with SVHI enrollment. Compared with the “Low HIL, Low Numeracy” reference group, individuals in both the “High HIL, High Numeracy” and “High HIL, Low Numeracy” profiles had significantly higher odds of SVHI enrollment after adjustment for sociodemographic, health-related, and risk preference covariates.

By employing LPA, this study moved beyond traditional variable-centered approaches and adopted a person-centered perspective to identify distinct combinations of HIL and numeracy [45]. The value of LPA lies in showing how these two cognitive dimensions coexist within individuals rather than treating them only as separate predictors. The largest subgroup, labeled “High HIL, Low Numeracy,” accounted for 52.0% of the sample. Despite lower numeracy, the “High HIL, Low Numeracy” subgroup had a slightly higher unadjusted SVHI enrollment rate than the “High HIL, High Numeracy” profile (28.1% vs. 25.9%) and also showed a larger adjusted OR in the multivariable model (2.40 vs. 2.14). This non-monotonic pattern suggests that HIL and numeracy are not simply additive cognitive resources. Individuals in the “High HIL, Low Numeracy” profile may have sufficient insurance-related knowledge and self-rated understanding, while still facing difficulties in numerical processing, such as calculating premium burden, deductible thresholds, reimbursement rates, and expected out-of-pocket costs. The higher enrollment propensity in this subgroup might occur because navigating the SVHI enrollment process may inherently serve as a learning experience that actively boosts an individual’s health insurance literacy.

The “High HIL, High Numeracy” profile accounted for 32.9% of the sample and was characterized by higher performance across all three indicators. Individuals in this subgroup may have greater capacity to understand insurance information and interpret quantitative features of insurance products. The “Low HIL, Low Numeracy” profile accounted for 15.1% of the sample and showed lower scores across all indicators, suggesting that this subgroup may face greater challenges in understanding health insurance concepts and interpreting insurance-related numerical information. Identifying these distinct cognitive configurations is important because it provides a more nuanced understanding of SVHI enrollment and may help inform more targeted communication strategies for different population subgroups [46].

Compared to the “Low HIL, Low Numeracy” baseline, individuals in both the “High HIL, High Numeracy” and “High HIL, Low Numeracy” profiles exhibited significantly higher odds of SVHI enrollment. These results are consistent with previous studies. Higher HIL and numeracy skills have been shown to enhance an individual’s capacity to evaluate and make decisions regarding SVHI plans. For example, Hoerl et al. reported that among adults who were uninsured in 2013 in the United States, those with high HIL had a 9.2-percentage-point higher probability of obtaining coverage by 2015 compared with those with low HIL [47]. Similarly, Weedige et al. found that higher insurance literacy among middle-class consumers in Sri Lanka was associated with stronger intentions to purchase personal insurance, both directly and indirectly through increased trust, perceived benefits, and more favorable attitudes [48]. Evidence from 16 European countries also demonstrates a positive association between numeracy and life insurance holdings [49]. This relationship is further corroborated by Lambregts and Schut in the context of the U.S. Long-Term Care Insurance (LTCI) market [50]. They found that more numerate individuals are significantly more likely to hold LTCI.

By integrating HIL and numeracy into a unified cognitive framework, this study provides a more nuanced understanding of insurance enrollment than analyses of each dimension separately. From a policy perspective, to support more equitable access to SVHI, policymakers and insurers should consider designing communication strategies that are tailored to the specific needs of each cognitive profile. Individuals in the “Low HIL, Low Numeracy” profile may benefit from simplified explanations of eligibility, coverage scope, reimbursement procedures, deductibles, and benefit limits. This framework may also have implications for research in other insurance contexts. In market-based systems such as the United States, identifying individuals with coexisting HIL and numeracy limitations could help guide efforts to lower informational barriers to enrollment. For low- and middle-income countries implementing community-based or voluntary health insurance schemes, adapting HIL and numeracy measures to local settings may help identify subgroups that require more accessible communication and enrollment support.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design in this study limits causal inference. Future longitudinal studies are needed to examine the causal relationship between health insurance literacy and SVHI enrollment, and intervention studies could be used to assess the effectiveness of health insurance literacy interventions on SVHI enrollment. Second, the use of non-probability sampling, typical of cross-sectional surveys, may introduce selection bias. However, we minimized this limitation by collecting data from three large provinces across eastern, central, and western China. With distinct economies, these regions helped improve geographic and socioeconomic diversity in the sample. Third, while female overrepresentation may limit generalizability, women often serve as primary household healthcare decision-makers [51]. Their insurance choices typically reflect overall household enrollment status, suggesting that female respondents may effectively proxy household-level decision-making and partially mitigate this imbalance. To address this concern, we tested sex-by-profile interaction terms, and the overall interaction was not statistically significant, suggesting that the association between cognitive profiles and SVHI enrollment did not differ significantly by sex. Nevertheless, future studies should use more balanced samples.

Conclusions

Distinct cognitive profiles that integrate HIL and numeracy are significantly associated with SVHI enrollment. Specifically, residents in the “Low HIL, Low Numeracy” profile were least likely to enroll in SVHI, suggesting that they may face greater informational and numerical barriers.

From a methodological perspective, this study highlights the value of moving beyond unidimensional measures of cognitive capability to examine joint HIL–numeracy configurations. This person-centered approach may help identify subgroups with distinct informational and numerical barriers to voluntary insurance enrollment.

From a policy perspective, the findings suggest that promotion strategies for SVHI should consider heterogeneous cognitive profiles. Policymakers may consider incorporating brief assessments of insurance literacy and numeracy into community health initiatives or grassroots social programs to identify individuals with lower HIL and numeracy and provide targeted educational support and enrollment assistance. In addition, SVHI promotion efforts should ensure that groups with lower HIL and numeracy have equitable access to simplified communication materials, intuitive visual aids, and in-person enrollment assistance.
