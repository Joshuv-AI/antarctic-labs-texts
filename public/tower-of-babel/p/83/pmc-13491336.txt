The Persistent Failure of Catheterization Laboratory Safety: Time for Enhanced Radiation Protection Devices
Authors: David G. Rizik, David A. Cox
Published: 2026

Nearly half a century has passed since Andreas Grüntzig’s landmark coronary intervention—the world’s inaugural balloon angioplasty procedure. That single event launched an entire specialty of medicine and revolutionized how we treat patients with heart disease, yet the safety of the physicians and nurses in our cardiac catheterization laboratories has barely budged beyond his era’s primitive standards.

As we celebrate the success of the 2026 Society for Cardiovascular Angiography & Interventions (SCAI) Scientific Sessions, it is especially crucial to put our advances in innovation and research into perspective. We must make it clear that radiation safety in the catheterization laboratory cannot be sacrificed in the name of clinical progress.

We recall, during the principal filming of the Emmy Award-winning PBS documentary Scattered Denial, sitting across from Dr Spencer King in Atlanta, absorbing his measured wisdom about our field. He showed us a grainy, deeply moving interview with Dr Grüntzig from the 1980s. In it, Grüntzig, then at the vanguard of medical innovation, voiced a concern that should have echoed through the ages—the unaddressed, invisible threat of radiation exposure to those performing balloon angioplasty.

That warning, spoken in the infancy of interventional cardiology, rings more urgently today. Despite enormous technological advances in imaging, stenting, structural interventions and pharmacology,1, 2, 3, 4 when it comes to radiation safety, our practices remain stuck in the past. The risks are not theoretical. Every day in catheterization laboratories across the world, teams are bathed in ionizing radiation. Our only consistent answer for decades has been the lead apron—a relic, a heavy, archaic, burdensome garment that is as emblematic of institutional inertia as it is of protection.

The apron leaves vital areas—head, neck, face, arms, and legs—completely exposed. And the result? A wave of preventable injury and disease—debilitating spinal and orthopedic injuries, a disproportionate number of head and neck tumors (with left-sided predominance), cataracts, leukemia, and reproductive concerns. These are not rare, statistical outliers. They are the predictable, well-documented, historically verifiable consequences of ignoring safety in the pursuit of progress. The standard-issue lead apron, after all, is not unassailably intact, and we need not review what has been exhaustively stated previously—its shortcomings are already a matter of public and professional record.

The gendered impact is also severe.5 For women, these risks are not a distant abstraction or actuarial footnote. They are a palpable deterrent; they represent a glass ceiling in lead, a modern barrier to full participation in the field. Radiation exposure, pregnancy and fertility concerns, and the daily physical strain of heavy lead make clear that this is not simply a safety issue; it is an issue of equity, recruitment, and professional survival. A specialty cannot credibly ask why more women do not enter or remain in its ranks while refusing to eliminate dangers it already knows how to reduce. Every woman pushed away by these preventable risks is an indictment of the profession’s failure to align its values with its conduct. The consequence is not merely a less diverse workforce, but a weaker one—poorer in talent, perspective, leadership, and the very excellence the field claims to prize. A specialty cannot claim to value diversity if it refuses to confront and eliminate the barriers it has constructed.6

It is long past time to state this plainly: the failure to protect catheterization laboratory staff from radiation is not merely a lapse in best practice but a profound ethical and legal violation. The science is unequivocal. Over the past 25 years, far too many colleagues in fluoroscopic specialties have died from radiation-induced brain cancers linked to their work environments.7 Two-thirds of interventional cardiologists will suffer irreversible, often career-ending orthopedic, spine, and neurologic disabilities.8 The most promising young physicians, especially women, turn away not because of a lack of drive or talent but because the profession has failed to protect them.

The excuses for inaction are evaporating. Enhanced radiation protection devices (ERPDs) have been rigorously studied and repeatedly shown to offer far superior protection compared with standard lead aprons.9,10 Multiple peer-reviewed studies have demonstrated that ERPDs, whether in the form of radiation-attenuating shields, ceiling-suspended barriers, or robotic assistance, can reduce operator dose by >90%.9, 10, 11 They are lighter, more comprehensive, and address precisely the vulnerabilities that conventional aprons neglect. In many cases, ERPDs safely free physicians from the physical burden of wearing a heavy lead apron. Implementation of these devices does not require an act of Congress, only a willingness to invest in the safety of the human beings who power our hospitals.

The regulatory landscape is equally clear. As Low As Reasonably Achievable is not a suggestion. It is a federal regulatory mandate enforced by agencies such as the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and Department of Energy and written into the fabric of medical practice. As Low As Reasonably Achievable requires that radiation exposure to both workers and the public be kept not just below legal limits, but as far below as is reasonably possible, balancing safety with economic and social considerations. With the proven availability and superiority of ERPDs, the regulatory calculus has shifted. What was once ‘reasonably achievable’ with lead aprons alone is now inexcusable. Continued reliance on outdated protection is a direct violation of federal law.

Hospital systems that resist the adoption of ERPDs are not simply penny-pinching. They are disregarding their most valuable assets—their people. To push back on enhanced safety measures when the evidence is overwhelming, the legal requirements are explicit, and the technology is readily available is a gross abdication of both moral and legal responsibility. In no other high-risk industry would such disregard for worker safety be tolerated, nor should it be here. Our mandate is clear and nonnegotiable: we must insist that health care systems invest in ERPDs, not as a luxury, but as a baseline requirement for protecting the health of everyone who steps into the catheterization laboratory. This is no longer a matter of gentle persuasion; it’s about demanding what’s necessary for a safe workplace.

In recent months, Arizona’s State Legislature has taken up the question of promoting ERPDs in cardiac catheterization laboratories, a matter that, by any reasonable standard, ought to transcend partisanship. Yet, instead of swift consensus on a clear-cut issue of worker safety, the debate in both chambers devolved into needless contention and delay. One female health care professional bravely stepped forward during the senate hearings to share her story. She spoke of the silent fear that shadows every hour she spends in the cardiac catheterization laboratory, the gnawing worry about what the constant radiation might be doing to her own body, her thyroid, her breasts. But what brought the room to a standstill was not her fear for herself, but for her children. Her voice trembled as she recalled the pregnancies she carried while serving others. “When I was pregnant, standing for hours in that lab, was I unknowingly risking my unborn children’s lives so that I could help save someone else’s?” She laid bare the impossible moral calculus: was her devotion to healing others worth the haunting possibility that she might be putting her own children in harm’s way?

The COVID-19 pandemic put workplace safety and physician well-being at the center of public consciousness.12 Nowhere is this more urgent than in our catheterization laboratories. We cannot call ourselves a specialty of innovation and research while turning a blind eye to the day-to-day hazards facing our teams. To do so is to betray not just the promise of our field, but the trust of those who have dedicated their lives to it.

If we are serious about attracting and retaining the best minds in cardiology, if we believe in diversity, in innovation, and in basic decency, then the adoption of ERPDs can no longer be optional. It is a moral imperative and a legal requirement. Anything less is a failure, not just of leadership, but of conscience. This is our moment. Let us lead—not just in the science of saving lives but in the equally vital work of protecting the people who save them.
