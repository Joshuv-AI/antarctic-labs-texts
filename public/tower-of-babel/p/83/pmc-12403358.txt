Exploring perceptions of vitamin D deficiency and cultural influences in people from minority ethnic groups aged 60 + in East London: a qualitative study
Authors: Charlotte Zheng, Ashlee Teakle, Rashpal Gill, Oyinlola Oyebode
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore the perceptions and attitudes of vitamin D deficiency (VDD) and cultural influences in people from minority ethnic groups aged 60 + in East London through thematic analysis of qualitative semi-structured interviews.ResultsNine participants were interviewed (7 in-person, 2 by phone). Participants were recruited by purposive and snowball sampling through East London organisations, local council authorities and website email contacts. A total of 4 themes were generated: vitamin D knowledge, sources of knowledge, barriers to vitamin D intake, and solutions to improve vitamin D intake. Solutions included 2 sub-themes: food fortification and supplementation, and education – further divided into (i) community and (ii) women as targets for education. There was some knowledge of vitamin D, however this was limited as few could name dietary sources of vitamin D. Barriers included cost of supplementation, accessibility to sunshine, and cultural factors (e.g. dietary behaviour, perceptions of sun exposure). Attitudes towards vitamin D supplementation were largely positive, whereas food fortification was more polarising. Targeted education was a suggested key solution, particularly within the community and amongst women – identified as a central figure in most families. Policy makers should engage communities and develop approaches that reflect the needs and characteristics of the population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13104-025-07439-1.

Introduction

This research aimed to explore perceptions, attitudes, and knowledge of vitamin D deficiency (VDD) in people aged 60 + from minority ethnic groups in East London, to better understand how public health can target barriers for more inclusive practice and policymaking. Background

Vitamin D Deficiency (VDD) is a global health problem, particularly prevalent in the United Kingdom (UK) where sunlight exposure is lower compared with more equatorial latitudes [1]. Although, in East London, Black and Asian ethnic groups are the majority, the term “minority ethnic groups” will be used to describe all ethnic groups that are not White British in this paper. Minority ethnic groups with dark skin are at greater risk for VDD as they may have higher levels of melanin skin pigment which decreases UVB sunlight absorbance and reduces the amount of cutaneous vitamin D synthesis [2]. The older population (aged 60+) are also at higher risk for VDD because of increased risk of co-morbidities, indoor sedentary lifestyles, and institutionalisation [3, 4]. The health consequences of VDD are vast, including osteomalacia, cardiovascular disease, and increased risk of infections [5, 6]. This places both a health and socioeconomic burden on the individual and society [3]. One public health intervention aiming to reduce VDD and improve health, while raising awareness of the importance of vitamin D is a free vitamin D supplementation program for all residents aged 60+, currently operating in Newham, an East London borough [7]. Greater understanding of knowledge and perceptions of vitamin D is important to develop additional inclusive public health interventions that reflect the needs of the population and better address health inequalities. This greater understanding can be gained from qualitative research. Public health research in the UK on VDD has rarely used a qualitative paradigm [8]. This gap is particularly profound in minority ethnic groups despite increased susceptibility, along with consideration of cultural and dietary influences.

Main text

The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) framework was used to inform the methodology and reporting of this qualitative study [9].

Materials and methods

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews. Purposive and snowball sampling were used to recruit participants by email or text message, and distribution of an approved flyer designed by the lead researcher (CZ) through East London local authorities (e.g. Newham Council) and researcher contacts. Snowball sampling was applied by asking interested participants to share information about the study in their community. To be eligible for the study, participants were required to be English-speaking adults, aged ≥ 60 years, be of minority ethnic background, and recruited in East London. Participants were excluded if they did not meet the above-mentioned categories.

Participants received a Participant information sheet and Consent form by email or text message, and a suitable interview date, time, and mode (in person or phone) arranged. In person interviews were conducted at organisational or community sites. All interviews took place during May 2024. Participants were rewarded with a £10 Sodexo voucher for giving their time to the study. Interviews were audio-recorded and automatically transcribed via Microsoft Teams if participants consented. Interviews began with an open question to allow participants to share in an iterative manner and were only prompted with questions where necessary. The interview topic guide was developed from relevant literature by CZ and reviewed by OO and AT [8, 10–14] (Additional file 1).

One researcher (CZ) completed all interviews independently. CZ is a female medical student with training in qualitative methods and preliminary experience building rapport and eliciting testimonies with members of the public in clinical settings.

Data analysis

Interviews were audio-recorded and automatically transcribed via Microsoft Teams. For participants who did not consent to audio-recording, notes were taken during/after the interview instead. Participant data was stored in a password protected Excel spreadsheet file. Transcript data was pseudoanonymised. Thematic analysis (TA) was undertaken by one researcher (CZ) and reviewed by a second researcher to confirm data coherence (OO) [15]. All transcripts and contemporary notes were included in the analysis. Data were manually colour-coded with hand-drawn mind maps created to aid theme generation; one example is provided (Additional file 2).

Theme 1: Vitamin D knowledge

Most participants were aware of the importance of vitamin D to health as an essential nutrient, wherein a lack of it would be harmful, and some could identify sunlight as the main source. However, some could not justify why vitamin D was important, and most did not know any dietary sources or awareness of any UK guidelines. Health benefits identified include: the role of vitamin D in immunity, infection prevention, and bone health. Also, awareness of specific symptoms of VDD were lacking. Most participants knew the UK lacked sunshine and the importance of going out for sun, however few were able to relate this to vitamin D skin synthesis.

Theme 2: Sources of knowledge spread

This theme discusses where participants heard/learnt about vitamin D and how they share information. Sources of knowledge through word of mouth was common, with many explaining how they heard and shared information with family/friends, neighbours, the community, and religious spaces such as church and mosques. Furthermore, people may tend to trust the information received from relatives as they can share personal experiences passed down generationally. Posters and advertisements in the community were also sources of knowledge of vitamin D. The COVID-19 pandemic raised vitamin D knowledge which can also be seen in Theme 1 with immunity being suggested as one of the roles of vitamin D.

Theme 3: Barriers to vitamin D intake/sufficiency

Barriers refer to factors that may prevent or limit individuals from accessing and/or maintaining good vitamin D levels. Cost and affordability barriers existed to buying supplements, accessing vitamin D-rich foods, and traveling abroad to sunny countries. Lack of money to support other expenses also had a knock-on effect, as individuals must prioritise other health and social needs. Time constraints presented a barrier, largely due to busy work schedules, limited breaktimes, and wish to rest inside after a long day of work. This can then decrease motivation and energy to go out afterwards, further limiting outdoor time. Potential cultural barriers were also discussed including shade-seeking behaviour due to perceptions of beauty with lighter skin, as well as cultural dress such as modest ladies’ gowns and hijabs limiting sun exposure. Confusion and lack of clear information was another barrier with participants expressing an overload of public health information.

Theme 4: Solutions to improve vitamin D intake

This theme explores how participants felt public health could address VDD. Discussion on food fortification and supplementation was prompted to understand attitudes towards these interventions.

Food fortification and supplementation

Food fortification was met with a mix of polarising attitudes, as some expressed dislike for fortification – based on the artificial and unnatural concept – while others felt it would be a good thing. Most expressed a strong preference for natural foods with the belief this was better for health.

Participants were asked about a typical day of eating, and their preferences in relation to cultural eating habits and food palates. This topic was important to identify any vitamin D-rich food, or potential food “vehicles” for food fortification. Participants from Black African and Afro-Caribbean backgrounds mentioned typical foods including: yam, okra, cassava flour, and mackerel; participants of Indian background mentioned chapati and rice; Chinese background participants mentioned noodles, fried rice, seafood, and meat-based soups as common foods to their culture.

Education: community and women

The predominant solution identified was education and raising awareness e.g. through media, posters, but importantly through people. Community arose as a key sub-theme, acting as an anchor for education and knowledge spread, given the strong trust in community and willingness to engage in local programs. The role of women was also identified as important, largely due to their central role in the family. Many expressed the importance of educating women and empowering female voices.

Critical discussion

This qualitative study enhances our understanding of perceptions of VDD in people from minority ethnic groups aged 60 + in East London. This study highlights barriers to attaining vitamin D sufficiency such as time and cost of spending time outdoors, eating healthily or dietary supplementation, as well as cultural factors that influence lifestyle and dietary behaviour. Time barriers to engaging in healthy practices due to busy work schedules and constraints are consistent with other studies [14, 16]. Furthermore, aesthetic preferences to seeking sunshade as a potential barrier, mirrors Kung et al.’s (2006) study with Chinese women who expressed negative attitudes to sun exposure [17]. This contributes to existing debates between sun exposure versus sun protection, a potential barrier to giving clear knowledge to the public. For minority ethnic groups in the UK, where sunlight may not be sufficient for vitamin D generation, and cultural dress and aesthetic preferences also limit sun exposure, interventions to support dietary intake of vitamin D may be particularly important.

Understanding relevant cultural factors was a key objective of this research given its importance to health, and the use of cultural contexts in health approaches has been employed by the World Health Organisation (WHO) policy brief [18]. A recent qualitative paper explored how culture, family, and community as key components could enable mechanisms to better engage minority ethnic groups in their health [19]. Additionally, culture and diet play an intrinsic bidirectional role: food is a part of someone’s culture, as is culture influencing dietary choices. One study describes food to be part of someone’s “cultural identity” as what someone chooses to eat often resonates with their culture’s traditions and practices [20]. For example, participants of Black African and Afro-Caribbean ethnicity described eating okra and fish in their diet which was a traditional part of their culture and lifestyle. Therefore, food and culture may be key psychological parts of someone’s sense of self and what motivates certain behaviours, which may be important factors informing public health interventions.

Our study showed food fortification was viewed as largely negative. These opinions are mirrored in other studies, including Kotta et al.’s (2015) focus group study expressing negative views towards food fortification due to its “unnatural” concept and lack of freedom or control over their food [8, 21]. A sense of control and clarity is seen as important [22], which may explain why personal intake of vitamin D supplements were met with greater enthusiasm compared with food fortification in our study. Interestingly, in another UK study, those aged 65 + were less likely to take a vitamin D supplement compared to those aged 25–64 [10]. Future initiatives to educate and garner public support for fortification may help address such barriers.

Study participants felt that targeted education within the community may be an effective public health intervention, while simultaneously stating that public health advice was overwhelming. The 2015 Lancet Commission on Women and Health illustrates how women are critical drivers of wealth and national health, thus key targets for education which was a view mirrored by our participants [23]. Exploring or conducting further research that evaluates attitudes before and after educational interventions may help evaluate any improvements.

This research has the potential to facilitate and improve future public health interventions including locally based approaches such as Newham Council’s free vitamin D supplementation program for age 60 + residents [7].

Conclusion

This study suggests that there may be limited knowledge about vitamin D and sources of vitamin D in food among people aged 60 + from minority ethnic groups in East London. A diverse array of socioeconomic and cultural barriers influence vitamin D intake and must be addressed in public health to limit health inequalities generated from VDD. Public health interventions could consider the importance of community, cultural representation, and the role of women in families as ways to improve health education and healthy behaviours.

Limitations

Limitations of the study largely owe to the relatively small sample size and recruiting predominantly through local authority contacts. As such, the pragmatic sample size restriction in the current study meant data saturation was not achieved. Further research in a larger sample would be necessary. This may have also influenced findings as authority contacts may be more educated and engaged in local community services than the general population. Newham participants may have more knowledge on vitamin D than someone from a borough without a vitamin D programme. In addition, all interviews were conducted in English, which limits the potential to gain deeper insight from people who do not speak English. The sample was skewed towards women which may have influenced the themes that arose in the data, in particular, the role of women. Furthermore, the trustworthiness of TA in this study may be limited by the single researcher, as themes generated were based predominantly on one individual’s interpretation [24, 25]. However, a second researcher supported and helped guide theme generation, offering an alternative perspective to aid results interpretation. Nevertheless, this study supports the growing potential for qualitative research to inform interventions to reduce VDD.
