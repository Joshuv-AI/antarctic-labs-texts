Asthma screening in children with sickle cell disease in French Guiana
Authors: Gabriel Bafunyembaka, Mathieu Nacher, Nadia Nathan, Chimène Maniassom, Narcisse Elenga
Published: 2026

Children with sickle cell disease (SCD) are at increased risk of asthma, which contributes to respiratory morbidity and acute chest syndrome.1 However, data on asthma prevalence and screening strategies remain limited in tropical settings such as French Guiana, where SCD prevalence is high and environmental exposures are specific.2 This study aimed to estimate asthma prevalence in children with SCD and to assess the diagnostic performance of systematic screening tools implemented in routine care.

We conducted a prospective multicentre study including children aged 5–17 years with confirmed SCD followed at the three main public hospitals in French Guiana between January and July 2025 (online supplemental Figure S1). Children under 5 years of age were excluded, as well as those who declined participation or did not complete the screening procedures. A total of 140 children were ultimately included in the analysis. The participant selection process is illustrated in figure 1.

Screening was integrated into routine haematology follow-up and included the International Study of Asthma and Allergies in Childhood (ISAAC) questionnaire,3 standardised clinical examination, spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility testing, allergy skin prick tests and parasitological stool examination (online supplemental Table S1; online supplemental Figures S2 and S3; online supplemental Tables S2-S4). Systematic allergy assessment was performed in all children using standardised skin prick tests conducted according to international recommendations. Stool parasitology was systematically performed using standard microscopic examination methods to detect intestinal parasites commonly encountered in tropical settings. Asthma was defined according to Global Initiative for Asthma criteria,4 based on airflow obstruction on spirometry with significant bronchodilator reversibility. Spirometry was performed in clinically stable children using Global Lung Initiative reference equations5 (online supplemental Figure S3). Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics and logistic regression models.

The prevalence of asthma and the diagnostic performance of spirometry are illustrated in online supplemental Figure S11, with detailed screening performance indicators shown in online supplemental Figure S9. Overall, spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility testing demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance, whereas questionnaires and allergy tests alone were insufficient for individual diagnosis and mainly contributed to phenotypic characterisation.

Receiver operating characteristic curves showed poor diagnostic performance of ISAAC questionnaire scores (online supplemental Figure S10).

The study included 140 children with SCD, with a mean age of 9.4±3.8 years (range: 5–17 years), and 45.4% were male. The prevalence of spirometry-confirmed asthma was 30.7% (95% CI 23.2 to 39.1). Spirometry was interpretable in 76.6% of children, with 46.1% showing normal results and 22.7% having non-interpretable tracings (online supplemental Table S8; online supplemental Figures S3 and S6).

Spirometry feasibility is detailed in online supplemental Table S1 and online supplemental Figure S3.

Comparisons between children with confirmed asthma and those without asthma are shown in online supplemental Tables S2, S6 and S9; children with confirmed asthma were more frequently of rural origin (88.4% vs 67.3%, p=0.016), and eczema showed a borderline association with asthma (23.3% vs 9.3%, p=0.050) (online supplemental Figures S7 and S8; online supplemental Tables S3 and S7).

Results of allergy skin tests are detailed in online supplemental Tables S5 and S10. Allergic sensitisation, predominantly to moulds (56.7%) and house dust mites (14.2%), was frequent but not independently associated with asthma diagnosis in univariate analysis (online supplemental Tables S5 and S9; online supplemental Figures S2 and S4).

Stool parasitology results are presented in online supplemental Table S11. Intestinal parasites were detected in more than one-third of children (35.7%), mainly hookworms (21.4%) and Strongyloides stercoralis (8.6%), the latter showing a non-significant protective trend after adjustment (online supplemental Tables S4, S6 and S11; online supplemental Figures S5 and S6). In multivariable analysis, no factor remained independently associated with asthma occurrence, although a non-significant protective trend was observed for S. stercoralis (adjusted OR 0.24, p=0.087; online supplemental Table S7).

This study provides the first prospective data on asthma prevalence and screening in children with SCD in French Guiana.6 The high prevalence observed places this territory at the upper range of values reported in the literature for children with SCD and is markedly higher than estimates reported in the general paediatric population.4 6 Although this association was described descriptively in this screening study, it is consistent with our previous work showing asthma to be an independent risk factor for acute chest syndrome in children with SCD.6 Our findings support spirometry with bronchodilator testing as the cornerstone of asthma screening in paediatric SCD, even in tropical and resource-constrained settings, in line with international recommendations.4 5

While parasitological screening should not be systematic, it may improve diagnostic accuracy and patient safety in endemic areas, particularly before corticosteroid initiation.6 Three priority areas emerge from these findings. First, systematic spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility should be integrated into routine follow-up of children with SCD to allow early and objective identification of asthma. Second, screening strategies should take into account environmental and contextual factors specific to tropical settings, including rural residence and parasitic exposure, which may influence respiratory phenotypes and diagnostic performance. Finally, allergy assessment and parasitological screening should be interpreted as complementary tools that contribute to phenotypic characterisation rather than standalone diagnostic tests, particularly in resource-limited settings.4

In conclusion, asthma affects nearly one-third of children with SCD in French Guiana. Screening strategies adapted to tropical settings, centred on spirometry, are essential to improve early diagnosis and respiratory management in this high-risk population.
