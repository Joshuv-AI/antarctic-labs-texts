Impact of Immunogenicity on Clinical Outcomes in Patients With Moderate‐to‐Severe Inflammatory Bowel Disease Receiving Subcutaneous Infliximab: A Post Hoc Analysis of the LIBERTY Trials
Authors: Jean‐Frédéric Colombel, Silvio Danese, Stefan Schreiber, Bruce E Sands, Andres J Yarur, Aram Kang, Dong‐Hyeon Kim, Young Nam Lee, Stephen B Hanauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsLIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC demonstrated superior efficacy of subcutaneous infliximab (IFX SC) to placebo in patients with Crohn's disease (CD) or ulcerative colitis (UC). Here, we investigated the impact of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) on clinical outcomes.MethodsPatients randomized to IFX SC maintenance treatment in the LIBERTY trials were included (CD, n = 231; UC, n = 294). ADAs were tested using a highly sensitive, drug‐tolerant assay. Patients were grouped by ADA occurrence and titer and evaluated for outcomes up to Week (W) 54.ResultsAmong patients with CD and UC, 150 (64.9%) and 187 (63.6%) were ADA‐positive, respectively. No statistically significant differences were observed between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups in W54 efficacy (CD: clinical remission, 69.5% [95% confidence interval: 61.6–77.5] vs. 79.7% [70.2–89.2], p = 0.134; endoscopic response, 57.5% [48.9–66.1] vs. 65.2% [54.0–76.5], p = 0.359; UC: clinical remission, 49.1% [41.3–56.8] vs. 57.0% [46.5–67.4], p = 0.284), drug persistence (CD: 84.7% [79.1–90.6] vs. 85.2% [77.8–93.3]; UC: 84.5% [79.5–89.8] vs. 77.6% [70.1–85.9]), and safety (patients with ≥ 1 treatment‐emergent adverse event; CD: 71.2% [64.0–78.3] vs. 74.4% [64.9–83.8]; UC: 69.5% [62.9–76.0] vs. 64.2% [55.0–73.3]), despite lower drug concentrations in ADA‐positive patients at W54 (CD: 10.6 μg/mL [9.1–12.2] vs. 17.9 μg/mL [15.8–20.1]; UC: 12.2 μg/mL [10.8–13.5] vs. 21.0 μg/mL [18.4–23.6]). Introduction

Tumor necrosis factor (TNF) plays a pivotal role in the pathologies of Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [1, 2]. The introduction of TNF inhibitors, the monoclonal antibodies that target and inhibit TNF, has led to improved outcomes for patients with CD and UC [2]. Infliximab (IFX) as an intravenous (IV) formulation was the first TNF inhibitor approved for use in patients with CD or UC and remains a recommended option in treatment guidelines for induction and maintenance of remission for both diseases [3, 4].

While the clinical relevance of anti‐drug antibodies (ADAs) is linked to various factors, such as neutralizing activity, titer, and persistence [5, 6], the development of ADAs to IFX is associated with loss of response to IFX treatment [7, 8]. ADAs may reduce drug concentrations, alter drug bioavailability, and accelerate the metabolic clearance of the drug, all of which may contribute to treatment failure [9]. A negative association between immunogenicity and efficacy of IFX IV has been reported in the literature [10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. In addition, previous research has shown an increased risk of infusion reactions in patients who developed ADAs while on treatment with IFX IV [10, 12, 15]. Therapeutic drug monitoring, which involves measuring IFX drug level and ADAs, is currently an important clinical tool for optimizing treatment in patients treated with IFX IV [16].

A subcutaneous (SC) formulation of IFX was approved by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) in 2020 [17] for the treatment of moderately‐to‐severely active CD and UC following a Phase 1 non‐inferiority study comparing the SC and IV formulations of IFX [18]. The Phase 3 LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC trials subsequently showed IFX SC to be superior to placebo as maintenance therapy and well tolerated in patients with moderately‐to‐severely active CD and UC who responded to IFX IV induction [19], leading to its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2023 [20]. Recent meta‐analyses have suggested that IFX SC may offer clinical benefits (i.e., higher efficacy rates) compared with the IV formulation [21, 22]. In addition, IFX SC offers the potential benefit of self‐administration compared with IV administration and may improve convenience for patients receiving long‐term treatment [18, 23, 24].

As an important therapeutic agent in the treatment of CD and UC, it is critical to understand the impact of ADAs on drug levels and clinical outcomes with the novel IFX SC formulation. The present post hoc analysis aimed to evaluate the impact of ADAs on efficacy and safety in patients treated with IFX SC in the LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC trials.

Methods

Study Design and Population

A full description of the Phase 3 LIBERTY‐CD (NCT03945019) and LIBERTY‐UC (NCT04205643) study designs and methodology has been previously reported [19]. Briefly, two randomized, placebo‐controlled, double‐blind, multicenter, parallel‐group, Phase 3 trials were conducted to assess the efficacy and safety of IFX SC as maintenance therapy in adult patients with moderately‐to‐severely active CD or UC [19]. Patients who responded to IFX IV 5 mg/kg treatment during the induction phase (Weeks 0–6) were randomized (2:1) at Week 10 to receive IFX SC 120 mg or placebo every 2 weeks (Q2W) as maintenance therapy (Weeks 10–54). From Week 22 onwards, dose escalation to IFX SC 240 mg Q2W was allowed for patients in either group who initially responded but subsequently lost response. All patients provided written informed consent.

Study Assessments

Blood samples for immunogenicity testing were obtained pre‐dose at Weeks 0, 10, 14, 22, 30, 38, 46, 54, and end of study during the LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC trials. Immunogenicity evaluations used a highly sensitive, drug‐tolerant electrochemiluminescent (ECL) affinity capture elution assay with screening sensitivity of 1.33 ng/mL, confirmatory sensitivity of 2.35 ng/mL, and drug tolerance ≥ 25.0 ng/mL antibody detected in the presence of 120 μg/mL IFX (CT‐P13). The ADA assay was validated according to the EMA guidelines on immunogenicity assessment of therapeutic proteins and the FDA guidance on immunogenicity testing of therapeutic protein products [25, 26].

Patients were classified into two categories according to ADA occurrence: ADA‐positive (confirmed positive samples at any time after initiation of treatment) and ADA‐negative (no positive samples after treatment initiation). ADA‐positive patients were also divided into three groups based on the highest serum dilution factor used for ADA testing during the study: Low (< 100), Moderate (≥ 100 to < 1000), and High (≥ 1000).

Clinical Outcomes

For LIBERTY‐CD, the following endpoints at Week 54 were assessed: clinical remission, endoscopic response, Crohn's Disease Activity Index (CDAI) score, and Simplified Endoscopic Activity Score for Crohn's Disease (SES‐CD). For LIBERTY‐UC, the following endpoints at Week 54 were assessed: clinical remission per modified Mayo score, clinical response, endoscopic‐histologic mucosal improvement, corticosteroid‐free remission, and modified Mayo score. For descriptive purposes, two outcomes in each trial (LIBERTY‐CD: clinical remission and endoscopic response; LIBERTY‐UC: clinical remission and clinical response) were considered key efficacy outcomes.

Additionally, serum IFX levels throughout the treatment period, safety in terms of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) through Weeks 10–54, drug persistence up to 1 year, and time to loss of response were assessed for both trials. Definitions for all efficacy outcomes, and loss of response are presented in the Supporting Information S1.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were expressed as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges and compared using the Student's t‐test, one‐way analysis of variance, or Mann–Whitney test. Categorical data were expressed as proportions or percentages and compared using the Fisher's exact test or the chi‐square test. Efficacy analyses were conducted on an as‐observed basis, with non‐responder imputation (NRI) and last observation carried forward (LOCF) approaches employed as sensitivity analyses for missing data. Patients who underwent dose escalation to IFX SC 240 mg Q2W were imputed as non‐remitter/non‐responder in efficacy analyses. Statistical significance for efficacy endpoints by ADA titer was assessed using the chi‐square test for trend. As sensitivity analyses, propensity score matching was performed to construct matched cohorts for the comparison of the key efficacy outcomes.

Analyses of pre‐dose serum IFX levels were performed on an as‐observed basis and included data collected before initiation of dose adjustment in applicable patients. Drug persistence up to 1 year and time to loss of response were assessed as observed using Kaplan–Meier plots with log‐rank tests. Safety analyses included all randomized patients who received ≥ 1 complete or partial dose of IFX SC at Week 10 or thereafter, and Poisson regression was used to compare incidence rates per 100 person‐years (PYs). Statistical analyses were conducted in a descriptive manner, with statistical significance defined by p < 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (v4.4.1; R Core Team, 2024).

Results

Patients

Data were analyzed for 231 patients in LIBERTY‐CD and 294 patients in LIBERTY‐UC, who were randomized to IFX SC maintenance arms (Table 1). ADA positivity was confirmed in 64.9% (150/231) of the patients with CD and 63.6% (187/294) of the patients with UC. Baseline patient demographics and clinical characteristics were generally comparable between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups in both trials, with lower immunomodulator use at baseline noted in the ADA‐positive groups relative to ADA‐negative groups (CD: 25.3% vs. 40.7%, p = 0.017; UC: 13.9% vs. 36.4%, p < 0.001).

Efficacy Outcomes by ADA Occurrence

In LIBERTY‐CD, no statistically significant differences were observed by ADA occurrence for clinical remission (69.5% vs. 79.7%, p = 0.134), endoscopic response (57.5% vs. 65.2%, p = 0.359), CDAI score (86.0 vs. 83.6, p = 0.834), or SES‐CD (3.9 vs. 3.4, p = 0.380) at Week 54 (Figure 1A). Similar results were reported when clinical remission and endoscopic response were analyzed with the LOCF and NRI approaches.

Similarly, in LIBERTY‐UC, no significant differences between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups were observed for clinical remission (49.1% vs. 57.0%; p = 0.284), clinical response (65.4% vs. 62.8%; p = 0.678), endoscopic‐histologic mucosal improvement (40.9% vs. 46.0%; p = 0.501), or corticosteroid‐free remission (40.0% vs. 50.0%; p = 0.416) at Week 54 (Figure 1B). No differences were noted across the different imputation methods. Also, no significant difference was noted in the modified Mayo score at Week 54 between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups (1.9 vs. 1.8; p = 0.912).

To account for baseline differences in clinically relevant characteristics such as immunomodulator use, patients were matched on propensity score and compared for the key efficacy outcomes (Supporting Information S1: Tables S1 and S2). There were no statistically significant differences in the matched ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups in both CD and UC studies (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1).

Kaplan‐Meier analysis showed no significant difference between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups in time to loss of response up to Week 54 for both CD and UC studies (p = 0.592 for LIBERTY‐CD, p = 0.176 for LIBERTY‐UC; Supporting Information S1: Figure S2).

Serum IFX Levels by ADA Occurrence

In both LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC, higher serum IFX levels were detected in the ADA‐negative group relative to the ADA‐positive group, which was observed as early as Week 6 and persisted through to Week 54 (Figure 2). At Week 54, the mean serum IFX levels of ADA‐positive versus ADA‐negative patients were 10.6 μg/mL versus 17.9 μg/mL in LIBERTY‐CD, and 12.2 μg/mL versus 21.0 μg/mL in LIBERTY‐UC, respectively.

Drug Persistence by ADA Occurrence

In both LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC, drug persistence did not differ by ADA occurrence up to Week 54 (p = 0.952 for LIBERTY‐CD, p = 0.116 for LIBERTY‐UC; Figure 3). One‐year drug persistence for the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups was 84.7% and 85.2%, respectively, for LIBERTY‐CD, and 84.5% and 77.6%, respectively, for LIBERTY‐UC. Clinical Outcomes by ADA Titer

Clinical outcomes of ADA‐positive patients were assessed by the highest ADA titer levels. For both trials, the highest ADA titer ranging from ≥ 100 to < 1000, termed as the moderate group, was the most common (68/150 [45.3%] and 102/187 [54.5%] of ADA‐positive patients in LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC, respectively; Supporting Information S1: Table S3). No significant trend was observed in efficacy endpoints at Week 54 across ADA titer subgroups in both studies (Supporting Information S1: Figure S4), including the modified Mayo score at Week 54 in patients with UC (Negative vs. Low vs. Moderate vs. High: 1.8 vs. 1.6 vs. 1.9 vs. 2.2; p = 0.683).

Trajectories of the serum IFX levels varied by titer group for both LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC (Supporting Information S1: Figure S5). The mean serum IFX levels followed the ADA titer levels, with the ADA‐negative group showing the highest serum IFX level and the high‐ADA‐titer group showing the lowest serum IFX level in both studies.

Drug persistence up to 1 year was not different among the three titer groups of ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative patients (p = 0.821 for LIBERTY‐CD, p = 0.438 for LIBERTY‐UC; Supporting Information S1: Figure S6).

Discussion

In this post hoc analysis, no statistically significant difference according to ADA occurrence was detected in efficacy and safety outcomes in patients with moderately‐to‐severely active CD and UC at the first year of treatment with IFX SC. While mean serum IFX concentrations were lower in the ADA‐positive group compared with the ADA‐negative group, 1‐year drug persistence was comparable between the groups.

Reported rates of ADAs to IFX IV vary widely, ranging from 0% to 65.3%, possibly due to the diversity in the method of detection, timing of serum sampling, patient populations, and study designs [14]. In our study, immunogenicity testing by a highly sensitive, drug‐tolerant ECL assay confirmed ADA development in 64.9% and 63.6% of the patients treated with IFX SC in LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC, respectively.

The high ADA incidence reported in this study may be explained by improvement in the ADA assay over the years. The US FDA recommends using more sensitive ADA assays to test the immunogenicity of therapeutic protein products, from 250–500 ng/mL to at least 100 ng/mL, as clinically relevant responses have been observed at this level [26]. Consequently, highly sensitive assays have been developed [6], including the ECL assay used in the LIBERTY trials (screening and confirmatory sensitivity of 1.33 ng/mL and 2.35 ng/mL, respectively). In addition, the drug tolerance of this ECL assay (≥ 25.0 ng/mL ADA in the presence of 120 μg/mL serum IFX) is a significant improvement from the commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assays widely used in the early 2000s (tolerance > 1690 ng/mL of ADA in the presence of < 1.40 μg/mL serum IFX) [10] and is comparable to highly sensitive commercial assays (homogenous mobility shift assay: ≥ 36 ng/mL ADA in the presence of 60 μg/mL serum IFX; ECL assay: ≥ 22 ng/mL ADA in the presence of 100 μg/mL serum IFX) [27, 28].

This post hoc analysis noted higher immunomodulator use in the ADA‐negative groups, suggesting that immunomodulator usage resulted in lower ADA development. This is consistent with a previous study linking concomitant immunomodulator use to lower ADA‐positive conversion in patients on maintenance IFX SC therapy [29]. To account for potential confounding effects, a sensitivity analysis was performed in the propensity‐score‐matched populations for the key efficacy outcomes. Comparable efficacy rates between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups were observed, consistent with the original analysis and supporting the robustness of our findings. Nonetheless, combination therapy with immunomodulators to reduce immunogenicity may still be considered for selected patients, carefully weighing the benefits against the risks, including potentially serious infections [29].

It is notable that despite the clear decrease in serum drug levels in the ADA‐positive group relative to the ADA‐negative group, there was no difference in all efficacy outcomes examined, including drug persistence. This occurrence may be partly attributable to the observed serum drug profile of IFX SC. For IFX IV, it is well known that the current posology of every 4–8 weeks creates a cycle of peak and trough serum drug levels, with the trough levels at times being below 3 μg/mL, a concentration that may increase the probability of developing clinically relevant immunogenicity [30]. Although the corresponding cut‐off for serum IFX SC levels is yet unclear, it is possible that the persistent and high drug levels of IFX SC create an excess of serum drug levels even in the presence of ADAs, allowing IFX SC to engage its target and mediate a therapeutic effect seen in these analyses. In addition, although no statistically significant differences were seen for efficacy outcomes between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative groups at Week 54 based on the current population and analysis method, a consistent trend toward numerically lower efficacy outcomes in the ADA‐positive population, in line with previous findings on IFX IV, may indicate the possibility of a Type II error. It is possible that the small difference seen up to Week 54 may have translated to a significant difference between the ADA‐positive and ADA‐negative patients if the follow‐up had been longer or the sample size larger.

In addition to the presence or absence of ADAs, we sought to differentiate the effect of ADA titer by dividing the ADA‐positive population into three subgroups based on the highest dilution factor used to measure the ADA levels. By doing so, we observed a corresponding and expected pattern: increasing ADA levels corresponded to lower serum drug levels. However, even with the clear decrease in serum drug levels by ADA titer level, all outcomes examined still did not show a statistically significant trend or difference. Nevertheless, a pattern of lower efficacy with higher ADA titers can be seen, suggesting that titer is closely linked to clinical relevance of ADA in patients treated with IFX SC and an even finer separation in the high‐ADA‐titer group, or a longer follow‐up beyond 54 weeks, may result in a clear difference. In addition, the clinical impact of low ADA titer appeared unclear up to 1 year, with only mildly decreased, but still constant, serum IFX levels compared with those in the ADA‐negative patients. Thus, studies are also warranted to investigate the longer‐term trajectories of low‐ADA‐titer patients and to establish an optimal treatment strategy for these populations.

Additionally, pharmacokinetic (PK) variables other than serum drug levels may warrant consideration when evaluating the efficacy of IFX SC treatment. For example, it has been proposed that total drug exposure, rather than serum drug levels, should be considered when comparing IV and SC IFX [31]. SC administration exhibits relatively constant serum concentrations compared with the IV route, and it is possible that even though ADAs led to reductions in serum IFX levels, the drug exposure was maintained within a range that sufficed to maintain a response. Another variable recently shown to be important in predicting the efficacy of biological drugs is drug clearance [32], which was not evaluated in our study. These findings suggest that multiple PK variables should be considered in patients receiving IFX SC treatment, and the impact of ADAs on these variables—and ultimately on clinical outcomes—should be confirmed in larger studies.

Similar to the efficacy outcomes, ADA occurrence did not have a sizable effect on the nature or frequency of TEAEs in either study, with generally similar levels of safety incidents reported between the two groups within each study. Although the incidences of hypersensitivity and anaphylactic events were numerically higher in the ADA‐positive group than in the ADA‐negative group in LIBERTY‐UC, the difference was not statistically significant, and most of the events were grade 1–2 in intensity. Nonetheless, to better elucidate the impact of ADAs on safety, future studies with extended follow‐up or larger sample sizes are warranted.

The primary strength of this study is the analysis of multiple clinical endpoints by both ADA occurrence and titer. As immunogenicity is known to significantly contribute to the secondary loss of IFX IV efficacy, whether the same could be said of the SC formulation remained a question. In this study, we not only analyzed the primary efficacy endpoints of the main LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC trials, but also other clinically relevant efficacy measures, serum drug levels, and drug persistence by both ADA occurrence and titer, providing a comprehensive report on efficacy of IFX SC in patients who develop ADAs.

A limitation of the study is its limited statistical power. This post hoc analysis was conducted in a subset of the patients originally enrolled in the main LIBERTY trials with no predefined effect or sample size, and it relied on descriptive statistics. Therefore, the study is not adequately powered to answer the questions conclusively. Another limitation is that patients were grouped by ADA occurrence and the highest titer, without detailed analysis of ADA persistence, which limits the ability to distinguish patients with consistently positive ADA responses from those with transient or single positive readings, who may differ clinically. Lastly, as the current study evaluated clinical outcomes up to 1 year only, the impact of immunogenicity on longer‐term outcomes has yet to be evaluated.

Conclusions

This post hoc analysis of LIBERTY‐CD and LIBERTY‐UC examined the immunogenicity of IFX SC tested with a drug‐tolerant ECL assay, and its impact on 1‐year clinical outcomes. Although ADAs affected drug levels, no significant differences by ADA occurrence were observed in 1‐year efficacy, drug persistence, or safety among patients receiving IFX SC maintenance treatment. Similarly, in contrast to studies of IFX IV, reductions in serum drug levels associated with high ADA titers with IFX SC did not translate to significant differences in efficacy outcomes or drug persistence at 1 year. These findings suggest that factors beyond serum drug levels, particularly in patients who develop ADAs, may affect the efficacy of IFX SC, warranting further studies to optimize therapeutic drug monitoring for these patients.
