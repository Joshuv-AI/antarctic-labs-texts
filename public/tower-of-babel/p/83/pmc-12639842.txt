Benign and malignant bone lesion diagnosis based on self-supervised and radiomics fusion using SPECT/CT images
Authors: Weiming Xie, Xiaozhou Bai, Miao Liu, Haonan Shangguan, Ying Zhan, Xiaodan Wu, Yingxin Dai, Yusong Pei, Guoxu Zhang, Zhiguo Wang, Zhaomin Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•SPARC-Net fuses SPECT-guided self-supervision with CT radiomics for classification.•Achieves 82.32% accuracy, 0.89 AUC, and 90.71% specificity for lesion diagnosis.•Fusion of deep learning and radiomics unifies metabolic and textural features.•Grad-CAM validates interpretability, highlighting metabolically active regions.

Introduction

Bone metastasis is a common and clinically significant complication in patients with advanced solid tumors, particularly in those with breast cancer, prostate cancer, and lung cancer [1,2]. Bone metastasis not only represents disease progression but is also closely associated with a range of severe clinical consequences, such as refractory pain, impaired mobility, and skeletal-related events, including pathological fractures, spinal cord compression, and hypercalcemia [3,4]. These complications significantly reduce patients' quality of life and complicate treatment strategies, highlighting the critical importance of early and accurate identification of bone involvement.

However, malignant bone lesions frequently exhibit imaging characteristics that closely resemble those of benign conditions, including osteoarthritis, bone infarction, and inflammation, posing significant challenges for accurate diagnosis even by experienced radiologists [5,6]. Reliable differentiation between benign and malignant lesions is essential for guiding appropriate treatment strategies; however, subtle variations in lesion morphology or anatomical location often fail to offer definitive diagnostic clues, thereby increasing uncertainty in clinical decision-making.

99mTc-MDP SPECT/CT has become a widely used imaging technique, combining the metabolic sensitivity of SPECT with the high-resolution anatomical detail of CT, theoretically providing a more comprehensive perspective for characterizing bone lesions [7,8]. However, in clinical practice, the interpretation of SPECT/CT images still heavily relies on expert visual assessment, introducing subjectivity and diagnostic variability, particularly in cases of ambiguous tracer uptake, marginal lesions, or accompanying degenerative changes, leading to limitations in reproducibility and clinical reliability.

Radiomics, a technique that extracts a large number of quantitative features from medical images, has emerged as an effective tool for improving diagnostic objectivity and accuracy [9]. By characterizing lesion heterogeneity, radiomics has the potential to enhance image-based diagnostic precision [10,11]. However, radiomics methods depend on manual feature extraction and precise lesion segmentation, which are time-consuming and rely on the operator’s expertise [12,13]. Additionally, the limitations of radiomics in modeling global semantic context and its sensitivity to image quality and imaging protocols further restrict its widespread application.

In contrast to radiomics, deep learning, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs), offers a powerful alternative by automatically learning hierarchical features from raw image data [14,15]. Deep learning does not rely on predefined features and has demonstrated exceptional performance in medical image classification tasks [16,17]. Due to the scarcity of labeled datasets, transfer learning is widely employed to adapt models trained on natural images for medical image analysis. However, the domain gap between natural and medical images often impacts the model’s generalization ability, especially in specialized tasks such as bone lesion classification.

Recently, self-supervised learning methods have emerged as an innovative strategy to learn potentially useful information from images without the need for labeled data [18,19]. By solving pre-defined tasks on unlabeled data, self-supervised models can discover latent structural features that are helpful for downstream tasks [20]. However, existing self-supervised methods are typically unimodal and lack effective integration of clinical semantics, limiting their effectiveness in complex anatomical contexts.

To overcome these limitations, we propose an innovative dual-stage feature modeling framework that combines handcrafted radiomic features with SPECT-guided self-supervised deep learning models. Specifically, we introduce SPARC-Net, a self-supervised framework that utilizes metabolic signals from SPECT images as semantic priors to guide the extraction of global discriminative features from CT images, without requiring manual annotations. Subsequently, we fuse these deep features with traditional radiomic descriptors to create a unified representation that captures both local structural details and global semantic context. We hypothesize that this multi-scale, multi-source fusion approach will significantly enhance the accuracy, robustness, and interpretability of the classification of malignant and benign bone metastases, providing more reliable support for clinical decision-making.

Methodology

Fig. 1 presents the overall pipeline of our dual-stage feature modeling framework for classifying benign and malignant bone lesions. The process begins with the acquisition and preprocessing of paired SPECT and CT images, which are aligned to generate SPECT/CT fusion scans for feature extraction. In the first stage, SPARC-Net, a self-supervised framework, leverages metabolic information from SPECT as semantic priors, which are used to provide the model with relevant prior knowledge that helps it focus on key patterns in the data, based on the relationships between metabolic activity and tissue characteristics. This guides the extraction of global features from CT images, enabling the model to autonomously capture clinically relevant patterns and improving feature generalization. In the second stage, radiomic features are extracted from the segmented CT lesion regions to capture detailed local structural and textural characteristics. These radiomic features are then fused with the global features derived from SPARC-Net to form a multi-scale representation of the lesion. To reduce redundancy and enhance classification performance, feature selection is performed using LinearSVC, followed by a Logistic Regression classifier for binary prediction. This combined approach of semantic-aware deep learning and radiomics provides a robust, accurate, and interpretable solution for bone lesion characterization in clinical practice.Fig. 1Overall flow chart.

Data collection

This retrospective study analyzed the clinical and imaging data of 741 patients who underwent SPECT/CT examinations at a tertiary medical center between January 1, 2021, and June 30, 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the use of anonymized data. For each patient, the largest lesion area was selected for analysis. The lesions were primarily located in the spine, ribs, sternum, skull, and long bones of the extremities.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a confirmed history of malignancy; (2) completion of SPECT/CT bone scintigraphy; and (3) a definitive diagnosis of bone metastasis or benign bone lesion established through pathology, imaging, or clinical follow-up. Exclusion criteria included: (1) indeterminate diagnosis of bone lesions; (2) presence of primary malignant bone tumors; (3) history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or surgical treatment; and (4) poor image quality or severe artifacts that precluded accurate lesion delineation.

Imaging data were acquired using a Siemens SPECT/CT scanner equipped with low-energy high-resolution (LEHR) collimators and a 16-slice helical CT system. Each patient received an intravenous injection of 740–925 MBq of 99mTc-MDP, prepared using a molybdenum-technetium generator. Whole-body bone scintigraphy was performed 3 to 4 hours after tracer injection to identify regions of abnormal radiotracer uptake, followed immediately by targeted SPECT/CT imaging of those regions.

SPECT imaging was conducted using a dual-head gamma camera with 180° rotation, 10 seconds per frame, and a matrix size of 128 × 128. CT parameters included 130 kV and 190 mA, with automatic exposure control applied to minimize radiation dose. All images were reconstructed using a high-resolution B70s sharp convolution kernel, with a bone window setting of 1500 HU width and 50 HU level, and a slice thickness of 3 mm. Following image fusion, all SPECT/CT data were analyzed using a Siemens post-processing workstation.

In this study, SPECT provided functional imaging information, reflecting bone metabolic changes associated with either metastases or benign lesions, while CT offered detailed anatomical information for assessing lesion morphology and cortical bone involvement. The integration of SPECT and CT enabled precise spatial localization of the lesions and improved diagnostic accuracy by combining functional and structural data. Data preprocessing

To ensure the quality and consistency of the training and evaluation datasets, a standardized image preprocessing and partitioning workflow was established. All lesion images were imported into 3D Slicer in BMP format, and the axial slice with the largest cross-sectional area of the lesion was selected for analysis [21]. Regions of interest corresponding to areas of abnormal radiotracer uptake were manually delineated on the SPECT, CT, and fused SPECT/CT images by trained nuclear medicine physicians. Each annotation was subsequently reviewed and verified by a senior physician with 3–5 years of clinical experience. To focus the analysis on a single lesion, the images were cropped to retain only the target region. The cropped images were then resized to a uniform resolution of 224 × 224 pixels, and pixel intensity values were normalized to a range of 0–1. This normalization step minimized grayscale discrepancies across imaging modalities and enhanced data consistency, thereby facilitating robust model training and evaluation.

SPARC-Net

To address the limitations of traditional medical image diagnostic methods, such as over-reliance on manual annotations and insufficient representation of local features, we propose SPARC-Net (Self-supervised feature extractor and cross-modal fusion Network) as a core module for global feature extraction. SPARC-Net leverages a self-supervised pretraining approach and multi-source information fusion architecture to learn global semantic representations of images. As shown in Fig. 3, it combines these representations with radiomics features using cross-modal fusion to ensure both global semantic consistency and detailed local structure. During feature extraction, SPARC-Net uses a shared EfficientNet encoder to process both SPECT and CT images. Since medical images are typically single-channel grayscale, the input is first transformed into three channels using a 1 × 1 convolution kernel, which is a small filter that helps convert the single-channel input into a multi-channel format without changing the spatial dimensions of the image. The encoder then extracts global feature vectors for each modality. To improve consistency, both modalities share the same network parameters, and a semantic alignment mechanism minimizes modality discrepancies, enhancing classification performance. Additionally, SPARC-Net includes an advanced decoder structure in the CT branch for spatially reconstructing feature vectors, improving both image clarity and the model’s interpretability. The model is trained in a fully self-supervised manner, without the need for manual annotations. We incorporate a cross-modal alignment loss and multi-scale image reconstruction loss to optimize feature extraction and reconstruction quality. Finally, the model is used for downstream tasks.Fig. 3SPARC-Net model architecture.

Radiomics feature extraction

To comprehensively characterize lesion-specific features and enhance the differentiation between benign and malignant bone lesions, this study employed radiomics analysis to extract high-dimensional quantitative features from CT and SPECT images. The open-source PyRadiomics library was used to extract three major categories of features from the preprocessed lesion regions: shape-based features (Shape2D), first-order statistical features, and texture features based on the gray level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) [[22], [23], [24]]. Shape features quantified the geometric structure of the lesions, capturing metrics such as elongation, flatness, major and minor axis lengths, perimeter, and sphericity, which reflect the morphological complexity and irregularity of tumor boundaries. First-order features described the distribution of voxel intensities within the region of interest, including measures such as mean, variance, entropy, kurtosis, and skewness, thereby offering insights into the signal heterogeneity and instability within the lesion. GLCM texture features characterized the spatial relationships of pixel intensities, capturing parameters such as contrast, homogeneity, correlation, and texture entropy—key indicators of the microstructural complexity and metabolic disorganization often observed in malignant tumors. Together, these multi-dimensional features provided a comprehensive representation of lesion phenotype, laying the foundation for accurate and robust classification.

Feature selection algorithm

To enhance model interpretability and reduce redundancy in the high-dimensional radiomic feature space, we employed the LinearSVC algorithm for feature selection on each imaging modality [25]. LinearSVC assigns a weight wi to each feature based on its contribution to the decision boundary, where higher absolute weight values indicate greater discriminative power. Specifically, the decision function of the linear SVC can be expressed as, as shown in Eq. (1).(1)fx=wTx+bwhere x represents the feature vector, w denotes the learned weight vector, and b is the bias term. Based on the magnitude of wi, features were ranked in descending order, and the top 30 features with the highest absolute weights were selected from each modality. This approach allowed us to retain the most informative features while discarding less relevant or redundant ones, thereby improving the robustness and generalization ability of the subsequent classification model.

Results and discussion

We compared five distinct feature representation approaches: deep features extracted from pretrained convolutional neural networks, features learned by the proposed SPARC-Net framework, traditional radiomics features, and two fusion strategies—radiomics combined with deep features from pretrained models and radiomics combined with SPARC-Net features. These feature sets were evaluated across four commonly used machine learning classifiers: Random Forest, Logistic Regression, XGBoost, and Support Vector Machine (SVM).

The primary evaluation metric was the AUC, supplemented by accuracy, F1 score, SN, SP, FPR, and FNR to provide a comprehensive performance assessment. Further analysis revealed that the deep features extracted by SPARC-Net, when used independently, outperformed those obtained through conventional transfer learning models. Specifically, under the same classifier, SPARC-Net achieved an AUC of 0.804, surpassing the 0.771 achieved by pretrained models, indicating a significant improvement in discriminative power. This result suggests that the SPECT-guided self-supervised mechanism in SPARC-Net enhances the model's ability to semantically perceive lesion regions in CT images, providing greater adaptability, particularly in cases with limited or inconsistent annotations.

Among all the feature modeling strategies, the combination of SPARC-Net deep features and radiomics features showed the most superior performance. Using Logistic Regression, this hybrid feature representation achieved an AUC of 0.890, an accuracy of 82.3 %, an F1 score of 72.3 %, along with a SN of 66.6 % and a SP of 90.7 %. This combination outperformed all other approaches, demonstrating the complementary advantages of integrating global semantic features from SPARC-Net with local structural information provided by radiomics. This fusion significantly enhanced the model's ability to differentiate between benign and malignant bone lesions.

Fusion of local and global features for bone lesion classification

To systematically evaluate the synergistic effect of deep features extracted by SPARC-Net and traditional radiomics features in fusion strategies, this study compared three feature modeling approaches: the use of SPARC-Net features alone, radiomics features alone, and a combination of both. Fig. 4 illustrates the optimal classification threshold for each method based on the Youden index on the validation set: SPARC-Net achieved a threshold of 0.310, radiomics reached 0.360, and the fusion model had a threshold of 0.450. The higher threshold corresponding to the fusion model indicates stronger discriminatory stability, which may result from the more accurate lesion region modeling and clearer classification boundaries provided by the combined features.Fig. 4Optimal classification thresholds for the three feature combination strategies.

Optimal classification thresholds for the three feature combination strategies.

As shown in Fig. 5, further performance comparisons were carried out through paired difference tests to evaluate SN, SP, and classification consistency across the three models. The fusion model demonstrated significantly higher SN than the SPARC-Net model, with a t-value of −2.886 and a p-value of 0.045, indicating a stronger capacity for identifying positive cases. In terms of SP, the fusion model also outperformed both SPARC-Net and the radiomics model, with t-values of −4.237 and −6.035 and corresponding p-values of 0.013 and 0.004, respectively. These results suggest a notable reduction in false-positive predictions. Moreover, the McNemar test revealed significant differences in classification outcomes between the fusion model and each of the other two models. The comparison with SPARC-Net yielded a chi-square value of 7.504 and a p-value of 0.006, while the comparison with the radiomics model produced a chi-square value of 7.787 and a p-value of 0.005. These findings highlight the superior classification stability and consistency of the fusion model.Fig. 5Paired performance comparison of the three feature models in terms of SN, SP, and classification consistency.

In conclusion, the fusion of SPARC-Net and radiomics features not only significantly enhances the model's detection ability for bone lesions but also effectively reduces the false positive rate and demonstrates stronger robustness in individual classification consistency. These results suggest that this fusion strategy holds substantial clinical value, particularly in intelligent diagnostic scenarios that require high SN and SP.

The removal of the guidance branch resulted in a marked decline across all metrics, particularly in AUC and SN, suggesting that the absence of SPECT-derived semantic priors severely hampers the model’s ability to attend to diagnostically relevant regions. This confirms the guidance branch’s critical role in aligning feature extraction with functional metabolic cues, thereby enhancing the network’s lesion-level focus and semantic discrimination. Eliminating the reconstruction branch also led to performance degradation, though to a lesser extent. The observed drop in F1 score and SN indicates that spatial reconstruction not only regularizes feature learning but also reinforces the structural integrity of encoded representations. Nevertheless, its influence appears subordinate to the guidance mechanism, which directly anchors the model’s attention to clinically significant areas.

The complete SPARC-Net model, integrating both the guidance and reconstruction strategies, achieved the most balanced and robust performance, particularly in terms of SP and AUC. This synergy between metabolic priors and structural constraints highlights the efficacy of cross-modal supervision in extracting rich, interpretable, and generalizable features—validating the architectural rationale and offering a powerful solution for automated bone lesion characterization.

Performance on the independent test set

To further confirm the generalizability of our proposed method, an independent validation was conducted using additional lesions from the same cohort of patients. A total of 75 patient cases were included, comprising 26 malignant and 49 benign lesions. The proposed model achieved an accuracy of 80.0 %, an AUC of 0.873, a precision of 69.4 %, a SN of 65.4 %, and a SP of 87.8 %. These results demonstrate that the model maintains strong and stable diagnostic performance on independent data, supporting its robustness and potential for clinical application.

Interpretability analysis

To further enhance the interpretability of the model, we conducted Grad-CAM visualization on the filtered features extracted by SPARC-Net, enabling the identification of discriminative regions contributing to the classification of benign and malignant bone metastases [29,30]. By aggregating activation responses from multiple informative feature channels and projecting them onto the original CT images, we revealed the spatial distribution of the model's attention, as illustrated in Fig. 7. The highlighted regions of interest correspond closely to lesion areas, showing strong spatial alignment with the high-metabolism regions marked by the SPECT scans. Conclusion

This study demonstrates the effectiveness of integrating SPECT-guided deep learning with radiomics for the accurate classification of benign and malignant bone lesions. The superior diagnostic performance across multiple metrics, including high AUC, accuracy, and SN, highlights the model’s potential to enhance lesion characterization, improve diagnostic precision, and reduce false positives in bone metastasis detection. By combining the semantic power of SPARC-Net’s self-supervised with the robust feature extraction capabilities of radiomics, this framework shows promising results in both localizing key lesion regions and maintaining global structural integrity.

The ability of SPARC-Net to focus on clinically significant areas, as evidenced by Grad-CAM visualizations, provides clear interpretability and enhances the trustworthiness of AI-assisted decisions. This model not only improves classification performance but also offers critical insights into lesion-specific patterns, making it a valuable tool for clinical decision support.

Future research should focus on validating these findings using larger, multi-center datasets to enhance the model's generalizability and robustness. Integration of SPARC-Net into clinical workflows, especially for personalized treatment planning of bone metastases, should be prioritized. Further investigations into its potential for multi-modal imaging fusion, real-time clinical performance, and application to other musculoskeletal diseases would expand its utility. To improve diagnostic accuracy to near-perfect levels, expanding the dataset to cover a broader range of cases and exploring advanced techniques like ensemble learning or reinforcement learning could be beneficial. Incorporating additional clinical data, such as patient history or genetic markers, might improve context-aware diagnosis. Moreover, real-time performance, along with continuous clinician feedback, will be crucial in refining the model for clinical application. While the current model shows considerable potential, ongoing validation and improvement in clinical settings will be essential to meet the rigorous standards required for routine use.
