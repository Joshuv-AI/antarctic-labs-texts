Depressive and anxiety symptoms in COPD: associations with diffusion impairment and echocardiographic pulmonary hemodynamic burden
Authors: Tetsuya Hiramoto, Satoshi Izuno, Kenta Toda, Atsushi Moriwaki, Makoto Yoshida
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPsychological distress is common yet often underrecognized in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Physiological correlates beyond spirometry may support biopsychosocial assessment in routine care, but their relation to depressive and anxiety symptoms remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsIn this single-center cross-sectional study, 56 clinically stable Japanese COPD outpatients (mean age 73 years; 87.5% men) completed the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). Clinical assessments included spirometry, %DLCO/VA, echocardiographic tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient (TRPG), body mass index (BMI), and blood biomarkers. Associations were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation and multivariable linear regression adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 1 included %DLCO/VA and Model 2 included TRPG. Bootstrap resampling was used to assess the stability of key regression coefficients.ResultsDepressive symptoms (SDS ≥ 40) were present in 46.5% of the patients (severe 5.4%), and elevated state anxiety in 41.1% (severe 12.5%). SDS scores were correlated with lower %DLCO/VA, higher TRPG, and lower BMI. STAI-state scores were correlated with higher TRPG. In adjusted analyses, lower %DLCO/VA was independently associated with higher SDS scores, whereas higher TRPG was independently associated with higher SDS and STAI-state scores. %FEV1 was not associated with SDS or STAI scores.ConclusionsIn this Japanese outpatient COPD cohort, depressive and anxiety symptoms were common. Elevated TRPG showed a more consistent association with psychological symptoms in bootstrap analyses, whereas lower %DLCO/VA was associated with depressive symptoms but showed less stable bootstrap support than the TRPG findings. These findings support an integrated biopsychosocial assessment approach to routine COPD care, incorporating physiological information beyond spirometric airflow measures.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13030-026-00364-0.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) imposes a substantial medical and social burden through acute exacerbations (AECOPD) and a wide spectrum of comorbidities [1, 2]. Globally, COPD is the third leading cause of death, and its prevalence and mortality are expected to rise further with population aging [1, 2]. Comprehensive management of COPD has therefore become an urgent public health priority extending beyond the field of respiratory medicine.

Beyond airflow limitation, COPD is increasingly recognized as a systemic disorder accompanied by comorbidities such as cardiovascular disease, sarcopenia, osteoporosis, metabolic abnormalities, and malignancies. Among these, depression and anxiety are highly prevalent and profoundly impair quality of life (QOL). Meta-analyses have reported that depression affects approximately one-third of patients with COPD, and anxiety is also common, with prevalence estimates generally in the range of 25–40% [3–5]. Compared with non-COPD populations, COPD has been associated with higher odds of depression and anxiety in pooled analyses [3–5]. These rates are comparable to or exceed those seen in other chronic diseases such as heart failure and diabetes.

Depression and anxiety are not merely comorbid conditions but independent predictors of adverse outcomes in COPD. Patients with these symptoms have a 1.4–2.1-fold increased risk of acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPD) and consistently show higher rates of hospitalization, health-care utilization, and mortality [4, 6]. Nevertheless, these mental health comorbidities are often underrecognized and undertreated in routine COPD care [6].

Although the severity of airflow limitation (%FEV1 predicted) has traditionally been emphasized as a determinant of mental health, it does not adequately explain the heterogeneity of symptoms or patient-reported outcomes (PROs) [1, 4]. Recent evidence further suggests that anxiety and depression in COPD are associated not only with breathlessness in daily life but also with exertional breathlessness during standardized cardiopulmonary exercise testing, independent of conventional markers of disease severity [7]. By contrast, the diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), or diffusing capacity per unit of ventilated alveolar volume (DLCO/VA), reflects integrated abnormalities of ventilation, pulmonary perfusion, and alveolar-capillary diffusion. DLCO or DLCO/VA has emerged as an independent predictor of exacerbations, hospitalization, mortality, and pulmonary hypertension (PH) [8–12]. The tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient (TRPG), derived from echocardiography, is another clinically relevant marker that reflects pulmonary hemodynamic burden and may be associated with pulmonary vascular involvement, exercise intolerance, and impaired QOL, all of which may contribute to psychological distress [13, 14]. Furthermore, low body mass index (BMI) and fat-free mass index (FFMI), reflecting undernutrition and sarcopenia, may promote depression and anxiety through systemic inflammation and neuroimmunological mechanisms [15].

Despite accumulating indirect evidence linking %DLCO/VA, TRPG, and nutritional status with depression and anxiety, few studies have examined these physiological factors together within a single clinical framework. We therefore conducted a cross-sectional study in Japanese outpatients with COPD to (i) determine the prevalence of depressive and anxiety symptoms and (ii) to investigate their associations with gas exchange capacity (%DLCO/VA), pulmonary hemodynamic burden assessed by TRPG, and nutritional status (BMI and FFMI).

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was conducted at the outpatient clinic of Fukuoka National Hospital between June 2014 and March 2015. COPD was diagnosed according to the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria, based on smoking history and a post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC ratio < 0.70. Patients were eligible if they had been clinically stable for at least four weeks before assessment, defined as having no acute exacerbation and requiring no systemic corticosteroids or antibiotics during that period, and were able to complete the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) and the State–Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI). The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee (approval No. 26–11), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Depressive symptoms were assessed with the SDS [16] and anxiety with the STAI [17]. Japanese standard cut-offs were applied to classify symptom severity as follows: SDS, mild 40–47, moderate 48–55, and severe ≥ 56; STAI-1 (state), mild–moderate (IV: men 41–49, women 42–50) and severe (V: men ≥ 50, women ≥ 51); STAI-2 (trait), mild–moderate (IV: men 44–52, women 45–54) and severe (V: men ≥ 53, women ≥ 55).

Spirometry was performed using a Chestac-8900 spirometer (Chest Co., Tokyo, Japan) [18]. DLCO and alveolar volume (VA) were measured using the single-breath method [19], and percent predicted DLCO/VA (%DLCO/VA) was calculated. BMI was calculated as weight/height² (kg/m²). Body fat percentage was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). Fat-free mass (FFM) was calculated as body weight (kg) × [1 − body fat percentage/100], and fat-free mass index (FFMI) was calculated as FFM (kg)/height² (m²).

Echocardiography was used to measure left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and the tricuspid regurgitation pressure gradient (TRPG) using an Aplio 400 ultrasound system (Toshiba Medical Systems Corporation, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan). TRPG was calculated from the maximal tricuspid regurgitation velocity using the simplified Bernoulli equation (ΔP = 4 V²) [20, 21]. Additional echocardiographic parameters, including mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP), TAPSE/sPAP ratio, and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), were not systematically available in this cohort. Additional clinical characteristics were assessed by measuring serum C-reactive protein (CRP), total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, albumin, triglycerides, N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c). Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables as n (%).

Associations between SDS/STAI scores and physiological variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. False discovery rate-adjusted p values were also calculated for the correlation analyses. Multivariable linear regression was used to examine independent associations of SDS and STAI (state and trait) with physiological parameters. To minimize overfitting in this relatively small sample (n = 56), two prespecified models were fitted based on clinical considerations: both were adjusted for age, sex, and BMI, with Model 1 including %DLCO/VA and Model 2 including TRPG. In contrast, the univariable correlation analyses were intended as exploratory and supportive analyses. Bootstrap resampling was additionally performed to assess the stability of the multivariable regression findings.

A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using EZR (Easy R), a graphical user interface for R Commander, based on R version 1.68 and R Commander version 2.9-1. EZR is a modified version of R Commander that incorporates statistical functions frequently used in biostatistics [22].

Discussion

This study comprehensively examined psychological symptoms in Japanese outpatients with COPD and their physiological correlates. Depressive symptoms, including mild cases, were present in 46.5% of the patients, and elevated state anxiety was present in 41.1%. Severe depression requiring specialized intervention was observed in 5.4%, and severe anxiety in 12.5%. These frequencies are broadly consistent with previous domestic reports [23–25] and with international evidence showing that depression and anxiety are common in COPD [3–5]. Meta-analytic data have shown that depression affects approximately one-third of patients with COPD and occurs more frequently than in non-COPD comparators [3, 5]. Anxiety is likewise prevalent, and bidirectional associations between clinically relevant depression or anxiety and COPD have been reported [4].

Depression and anxiety are not merely comorbidities; they are associated with worse clinical outcomes, including higher risks of acute exacerbations, rehospitalization, and mortality [4, 6]. These conditions often remain underrecognized and undertreated, which may further worsen outcomes [6]. Our findings therefore support guideline-based recommendations emphasizing the importance of identifying and addressing psychological comorbidity in COPD and reinforce the value of incorporating validated screening tools into routine outpatient care [1, 2]. In addition, comprehensive nonpharmacological management, including pulmonary rehabilitation and patient education, may provide an important framework for addressing symptom burden, physical inactivity, and psychosocial distress in COPD [26].

A noteworthy finding of this study is that depressive symptoms were associated not with %FEV1 but with indices reflecting gas exchange impairment and pulmonary hemodynamic burden. In multivariable analysis adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and %DLCO/VA (Table 3-A), lower %DLCO/VA was independently associated with a higher SDS score. Although DLCO reflects the overall diffusing capacity of the lungs, DLCO/VA may better represent diffusion efficiency relative to alveolar volume and may therefore more precisely capture gas-exchange impairment in emphysematous lungs. Prior studies have shown that impaired gas exchange, as reflected by lower DLCO or DLCO/VA, is associated with poorer exercise tolerance and worse clinical outcomes, including hospitalization and mortality [8–12]. Reduced diffusion efficiency may contribute to exertional dyspnea and fear of breathlessness, potentially promoting activity avoidance and reinforcing a cognitive-behavioral cycle.

From a broader clinical perspective, the present findings can be interpreted in the context of growing recognition that COPD assessment should extend beyond airflow limitation alone. Contemporary COPD frameworks, including GOLD, emphasize multidimensional assessment incorporating symptom burden, multimorbidity, and clinically relevant traits beyond spirometric severity alone [1, 2]. In parallel, recent literature has highlighted the clinical importance of impaired diffusing capacity in COPD, particularly in relation to symptoms, exercise intolerance, pulmonary vascular involvement, and adverse outcomes [11, 12, 27]. In this context, our findings raise the possibility that diffusion impairment may contribute to the heterogeneity of psychological symptom burden beyond that captured by %FEV1 alone.

Among the physiological variables examined, TRPG showed a more consistent association with psychological symptoms in the bootstrap analyses. In multivariable models adjusted for age, sex, BMI, and TRPG (Table 3-B), higher TRPG was independently associated with higher SDS and STAI-1 scores. In bootstrap resampling analyses, the association between TRPG and SDS was supported by a consistently positive coefficient and a 95% confidence interval that did not cross zero. Similarly, TRPG showed a positive coefficient for STAI-1 in 99.6% of bootstrap samples. Lower %DLCO/VA also showed a directionally consistent inverse association with SDS; however, the bootstrap support for this association was less stable than that for TRPG because the bootstrap confidence interval lay close to zero.

TRPG is a core echocardiographic parameter underlying the estimation of systolic pulmonary artery pressure and may be useful as a noninvasive marker of pulmonary hemodynamic burden in COPD. Elevated pulmonary hemodynamic burden may contribute to exercise limitation, dyspnea, and greater symptom burden, and similar associations with depression and anxiety have been reported in pulmonary hypertension cohorts [13, 14, 28, 29]. In pulmonary arterial hypertension, marked impairment in health-related quality of life, including emotional and social domains, has also been documented, supporting the clinical relevance of pulmonary circulatory burden to patient-perceived health status [30]. In a recent PVRI GoDeep meta-registry analysis of COPD-associated pulmonary hypertension, pulmonary vascular resistance, but not FEV1, was identified as a major predictor of outcome, underscoring the clinical importance of pulmonary vascular burden beyond airflow limitation alone [31]. Clinically, worsening diffusion impairment and increasing pulmonary circulatory load may coexist with fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, and declining health-related quality of life. Our findings suggest that TRPG may capture a clinically relevant cardiopulmonary burden associated with psychological distress. At the same time, TRPG is a noninvasive parameter calculated from tricuspid regurgitation velocity measured by Doppler echocardiography using the simplified Bernoulli equation, and should be interpreted in conjunction with other right-heart findings; it is not equivalent to directly measured invasive hemodynamic assessment [21].

From a pathophysiological and clinical perspective, diffusion impairment and increased pulmonary hemodynamic burden may be linked to depressive and anxiety symptoms through several interrelated pathways, including gas-exchange abnormalities, a tendency toward hypoxemia, exertional breathlessness, fear of breathlessness, activity restriction, systemic inflammation, and neurohumoral stress responses. These pathways may increase symptom burden and impair daily functioning, thereby contributing to psychological distress in patients with COPD. Although symptom-burden measures such as the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale and COPD Assessment Test are clinically important, the present study focused primarily on objective clinical indices; including pulmonary function, echocardiographic parameters, and biochemical markers; in relation to psychological symptoms. Therefore, formal mediation analysis involving dyspnea perception or activity limitation was beyond the scope of this manuscript. Accordingly, these interpretations remain speculative and should be viewed as hypothesis-generating. Future studies incorporating symptom-burden measures and mediation analysis are needed to clarify these potential pathways.

In addition, lower BMI and reduced FFMI showed modest associations with depressive symptoms in univariable analyses. In COPD, malnutrition and sarcopenia are closely linked to systemic inflammation and loss of muscle mass, and both have been associated with adverse outcomes, including increased mortality [15, 32]. Several biological pathways, such as inflammatory cytokine effects on central nervous system function, and functional pathways, such as muscle weakness, reduced activity, and social withdrawal, may plausibly contribute to depressive symptoms. It should also be recognized that the SDS, although widely used as a screening instrument for depressive symptoms, may partly capture somatic distress and fatigue in patients with chronic respiratory disease. Accordingly, some of the observed associations may reflect not only depressive symptomatology per se but also the physical burden associated with COPD. At the same time, the relations among BMI, reduced physical activity, pulmonary dysfunction, and psychological symptoms are likely to be complex, and may be bidirectional and/or partially mediated by interrelated clinical factors. Thus, caution is warranted in interpreting these associations as representing simple unidirectional effects.

In addition to these physiological domains, psychosocial and social-contextual factors may also influence symptom perception, coping behavior, and illness burden. These factors should therefore be considered within a comprehensive biopsychosocial assessment of COPD.

Taken together, these results suggest that the evaluation and management of psychological symptoms in COPD may benefit from moving beyond an airflow-obstruction-centered perspective toward a more multidimensional approach. Routine psychological screening remains important, but physiological indices such as %DLCO/VA, TRPG, and nutritional status may provide additional information regarding clinical heterogeneity and psychological burden. This multidimensional view is also consistent with a recent study of severe hyperinflated COPD showing that anxiety and pulmonary arterial hypertension were independently associated with worse survival, supporting a treatable-traits perspective in which psychological burden and pulmonary vascular burden may represent complementary clinically relevant dimensions of disease [33]. GOLD appropriately incorporates patient-reported symptom measures such as the CAT into routine COPD assessment; however, symptom scores may partly reflect psychological distress as well as respiratory burden [1, 2]. In this context, our findings raise the possibility that, at least in selected patients, adding physiological markers such as TRPG may help to further characterize clinical heterogeneity beyond airflow limitation and questionnaire-based symptom assessment alone. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating and requires validation in larger studies.

Limitations

First, this was a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, which may have limited statistical power and the stability of multivariable models. Although bootstrap analyses supported the stability of the association between TRPG and psychological symptoms, the inverse association between %DLCO/VA and SDS showed less stable bootstrap support, with a bootstrap confidence interval close to zero, and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Larger studies will be needed to validate these findings.

Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Although physiological impairment may contribute to depressive and anxiety symptoms, psychological distress itself may also worsen physical activity, symptom perception, and overall clinical status. Therefore, the observed associations should not be interpreted as evidence of simple one-way causation.

Third, despite the use of prespecified parsimonious multivariable models to reduce the risk of overfitting, residual confounding and unmodeled clinical pathways remain possible. Several clinical factors, including hypoxemia, exacerbation history, symptom burden, quality of life, exercise tolerance, medications, smoking status, and comorbidities, may have influenced the observed associations. Although symptom-burden measures such as the modified Medical Research Council dyspnea scale and COPD Assessment Test are clinically important, formal mediation models involving dyspnea perception or activity limitation were beyond the scope of the present analysis. Future studies should clarify these potential mediating pathways between objective physiological indices and psychological symptoms.

Fourth, the use of self-report instruments (SDS and STAI) may not fully correspond to formal psychiatric diagnoses and may have been influenced by unmeasured factors such as sleep disturbance, pain, or medication use.

Fifth, TRPG was derived from echocardiography and should be regarded as a surrogate marker of pulmonary hemodynamic burden. Because echocardiographic estimation is subject to measurement limitations and is less accurate than right heart catheterization, the relation between pulmonary hemodynamic burden and psychological symptoms should be interpreted cautiously [20, 21]. In addition, other right-heart echocardiographic parameters, such as mPAP, TAPSE/sPAP ratio, and PVR, were not systematically available in this cohort.

Finally, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the characteristics of the study population, which consisted predominantly of older Japanese male outpatients with COPD and a relatively low mean BMI. Because body habitus, obesity prevalence, and phenotypic distribution may differ substantially from those of Western COPD cohorts, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to other populations.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations between depressive and anxiety symptoms and physiological parameters in Japanese patients with COPD. Depressive symptoms were independently associated with lower %DLCO/VA and elevated TRPG, whereas state anxiety was associated with elevated TRPG. Among the physiological variables examined, elevated TRPG showed a more consistent association with psychological symptoms in bootstrap analyses, whereas lower %DLCO/VA was associated with depressive symptoms but showed less stable bootstrap support than the TRPG findings. These findings support a multidimensional, biopsychosocial assessment approach in COPD that extends beyond spirometric airflow measures and incorporates gas-exchange impairment, pulmonary hemodynamic burden, and nutritional status. Further large-scale studies are needed to confirm these findings and clarify the underlying mechanisms and directionality of these associations.
