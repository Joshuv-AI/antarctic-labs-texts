Decoding natural products for neuroprotection: Pathway networks and structural insights for drug development
Authors: Suman Ghosh, Ishita Debnath, Sobhanjan Bhunia, Sumit Nandi, Sumel Ashique, Aditi Nayak, Susmita Mallick, Souvik Basak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurodegenerative diseases (NDs), including Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, Huntington’s, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, are progressive disorders marked by neuronal dysfunction and death, driven by pathological mechanisms such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, apoptosis, and protein misfolding. Despite scientific advances, current treatments remain largely palliative, underscoring the need for multitargeted therapeutic strategies. This narrative review synthesizes preclinical and clinical evidence to explore the neuroprotective potential of natural products, with a focus on their ability to modulate key molecular pathways implicated in NDs. A comprehensive literature search across Scopus, ScienceDirect, PubMed, MDPI, and Web of Science identified relevant studies. Bioactive compounds such as curcumin, resveratrol, ginsenosides, quercetin, and marine-derived molecules like fucoxanthin and phlorotannin demonstrated antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, anti-amyloidogenic, and mitochondrial-protective effects by modulating pathways including PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and Nrf2/ARE, thereby mitigating neuronal damage and promoting cell survival. Natural products from diverse sources, including honey, ginseng, marine macroalgae, and cyanobacteria, exhibited broad-spectrum neuroprotective properties, with advances in nano-formulations improving bioavailability and brain penetration. Furthermore, emerging approaches such as gene-drug interaction studies and scaffold-based drug design offer promising avenues for enhancing clinical translation. While natural products provide a holistic, multitargeted approach to combat NDs, challenges related to bioavailability and therapeutic translation persist, necessitating future research that integrates advanced drug delivery systems, precision medicine, and synthetic modifications to develop innovative and effective treatment paradigms.Keywords: curcumin, fucoxanthin, ginsenosides, natural products, neurodegenerative diseases, phlorotannin, quercetin, resveratrol

Introduction

Neurodegenerative diseases (NDs) are a broad category of disorders affecting the central nervous system (CNS) that are characterized by the gradual death and malfunctioning of neurons (Gadhave et al., 2024). Prominent examples include Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), Huntington’s disease (HD), and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), multiple sclerosis (MS), which impose significant social and economic costs due to the irreversible degeneration of neurons, often directing to cognitive, motor, and behavioural impairments (Giri, Banerjee, Ghosal, & Layek, 2024). They are primarily linked to aging populations, and despite advancements in understanding their pathophysiology, there remains a lack of effective treatments (Gadhave et al., 2024, Van Schependom and D’Haeseleer, 2023). NDs share common underlying mechanisms, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial malfunction, excitotoxicity, and protein aggregation (Peggion, Cali, & Brini, 2024). Oxidative stress emerges when an imbalance between the body’s capacity to neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS) with antioxidants occurs, which further leads to cellular damage (Pizzino et al., 2017, Schieber and Chandel, 2014). In NDs like AD and PD, oxidative stress due to ROS is closely linked with the aggregation of amyloid-beta (β) and τ protein in AD and alpha-synuclein (α-syniclein) in PD (Alqahtani et al., 2023, Pathak et al., 2022). These proteins aggregate into plaques or tangles, disrupting cellular function (Salahuddin et al., 2021). Neuroinflammation is another hallmark of NDs, where immune cells in the CNS, particularly microglia, become chronically activated, which results in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines and, in the future, increases neuronal damage (Chen et al., 2016, Subhramanyam et al., 2019). Parallel to this, many NDs show early signs of mitochondrial malfunction, which include decreased energy generation and increased oxidative damage. For instance, mitochondrial deficits in dopaminergic neurons contribute to PD pathogenesis (Schapira & Gegg, 2011).

The work against ND had been started in the middle of the 20th century. In the 1970s and 1980s, the methods to use horseradish peroxidase to trace neuronal pathways became popular, and our understanding of neural circuitry advanced at a more rapid pace (Graybiel and Devor, 1974, Ochs, 1972). Through those works, neurodegenerative research has emerged as a potential field of work over the decades to date. Across this review, it has started to summarize the findings from this era, where the literature review has been conducted from 2002 to 2025 date.

Natural products are becoming more and more popular as a possible ND treatment option. The multifactorial nature of NDs suggests that targeting multiple pathways simultaneously, as natural products often do, could be a more effective approach compared to conventional single-target treatments (Bawa et al., 2016, Gong et al., 2022, Makhoba et al., 2020). The exploration of natural products for treating NDs is a promising avenue due to their broad-spectrum neuroprotective properties (Zhang et al., 2024). These compounds target multiple pathological processes, offering potential therapeutic strategies for preventing the progression of debilitating neurodegenerative conditions. Considering the intricacy of NDs, incorporating natural products into existing treatment plans is a promising avenue for future research and development. Given these factors, the increasing number of studies on natural products shows promise as multidimensional therapeutic agents, providing new optimism for the creation of more comprehensive and successful treatment plans to fight NDs.

Mechanisms of neurodegeneration

In NDs, the CNS’s dynamic and selective loss of neurons can be described by AD, HD, PD, and ALS (Alqahtani et al., 2023). The mechanisms leading to neurodegeneration in these diseases often involve multiple pathological pathways, such as oxidative stress, mitochondrial malfunction, protein misfolding, and neuroinflammation (Peggion et al., 2024, Picca et al., 2020). One of the crucial contributors is the excessive activation of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDAR), leading to calcium overload and subsequent neuronal death (Armada-Moreira et al., 2020, Ma et al., 2020). The overstimulation of NMDARs leads to excessive calcium (Ca2+) influx into neurons. Elevated intracellular Ca2+ disrupts cellular homeostasis, triggering multiple pathological pathways, including oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excitotoxicity. A key mechanism of neuronal cell death associated with NDs primarily involves NMDAR activation, which triggers several neurotoxic pathways (Armada-Moreira et al., 2020, Jakaria et al., 2018). Here’s a detailed breakdown of the neurodegenerative mechanism:

Calpain activation and lysosomal dysfunction

Increased intracellular Ca2+ activates calpain, a calcium-dependent cysteine protease. Calpain leads to lysosomal rupture, releasing cathepsins, which causes the release of cell-destructive enzymes, triggers the apoptotic pathways, and leads to proteolytic damage to cellular components. The leakage of lysosomal enzymes into the cytoplasm exacerbates oxidative damage and cell death (Llorens et al., 2017).

Mitochondrial dysfunction and ROS accumulation

Disrupted mitochondrial function leads to excessive production of ROS. ROS accumulation induces oxidative stress, damaging lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids, further impairing mitochondrial function. Cytochrome c is released from mitochondria, triggering apoptotic pathways (Kowalczyk et al., 2021).

Caspase-3 activation and apoptotic cascade

Cytochrome c release activates Caspase-3, a key executioner of apoptosis. Caspase-3 cleaves cellular proteins, leading to neuronal shrinkage, chromatin condensation, and eventual neuronal death (Nguyen, Gillet, & Popgeorgiev, 2021).

AIF release and caspase-independent cell death

Mitochondrial dysfunction also promotes the release of apoptosis inducing factor (AIF). AIF translocates to the nucleus, causing DNA fragmentation and caspase-independent cell death (Sevrioukova, 2011).

nNOS activation, NO radical production, and ONOO− formation

Elevated Ca2+ levels activate neuronal nitric oxide synthase (nNOS), leading to the production of nitric oxide (NO). NO reacts with superoxide (O2−) to form peroxynitrite (ONOO−), a potent oxidant that contributes to lipid peroxidation and protein nitration, further damaging neuronal membranes (Pérez de la Lastra, Juan, Plou, & Pérez-Lebeña, 2022).

Excitotoxicity and synaptic dysfunction

NMDAR overactivation leads to excitotoxicity, characterized by prolonged depolarization, excessive glutamate release, and Aβ-induced neurotoxicity. This results in synaptic dysfunction, loss of neuronal plasticity, and eventual neurodegeneration (Wang & Reddy, 2017).

Neuroinflammation and microglial activation

ROS, NO, and ONOO− act as pro-inflammatory signals, activating microglia and astrocytes. Activated microglia release pro-inflammatory cytokines [e.g., tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1beta (IL-1β), and IL-6], exacerbating oxidative stress and neuronal damage (Lull & Block, 2010).

Protein misfolding and aggregation

Oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction contribute to protein misfolding and aggregation. Accumulation of misfolded proteins (e.g. Aβ, tau, α-synuclein, huntingtin) triggers proteotoxic stress, disrupting cellular functions and leading to neuronal death (Abramov, Potapova, Dremin, & Dunaev., 2020) The mechanistic overview of neurodegeneration by NMDAR-mediated excitotoxicity and neuronal death was described in Fig. 1.

Etiology of some prevalent NDs

These combined mechanisms, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipid peroxidation, culminate in neuronal cell death, a hallmark of NDs like AD, HD, PD, ALS, MS, frontotemporal dementia, and prion diseases.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD)

AD involves Aβ plaque and neurofibrillary tangle accumulation, causing synaptic dysfunction and neuron loss. Aβ deposition activates glial cells, triggering neuroinflammation via cytokine release. Oxidative stress from ROS imbalance worsens neuronal damage, while mitochondrial dysfunction disrupts energy metabolism, promoting apoptosis (Bloom, 2014), (Kaur, Sharma, & Deshmukh, 2019), (Houldsworth, 2024). Natural compounds like curcumin and resveratrol (RSV) reduce Aβ aggregation, regulate τ phosphorylation, suppress NF-κB-mediated inflammation, and enhance antioxidant defense by upregulating superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (Mazzanti and Di Giacomo, 2016, Silvestro et al., 2021).

Huntington’s disease (HD)

The huntingtin (HTT) gene’s cytosine, adenine, and guanine repeats grow larger in HD, producing mutant huntingtin protein (mHTT), which builds up in hazardous aggregates in neurons (Bhat et al., 2024). These aggregates interfere with proteasomal degradation, contributing to protein misfolding and autophagy disruption (Ojalvo-Pacheco et al., 2024, Pogoda et al., 2021). Moreover, mHTT disrupts mitochondrial activity, resulting in energy shortages and elevated ROS formation, which promotes neurodegeneration (Oliveira, 2010, Tassone et al., 2023). Studies have examined the potential of natural substances such as some flavonoids and polyphenols like luteolin, curcumin, RSV, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), etc. as well as the autophagy enhancer trehalose (natural disaccharide) helps to delay the progression of HD by improving the function of mitochondria, lowering oxidative stress, and improving protein clearance (Ahmed et al., 2024, Singh et al., 2022).

Parkinson’s disease (PD)

PD is described by the destruction of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra part of the brain, which is linked to the aggregation of α-synuclein aggregates (Gómez-Benito et al., 2020). This protein misfolding is linked to mitochondrial dysfunction, impaired autophagy, and excessive ROS production (Dias et al., 2013). Neuroinflammation, driven by microglial activation and increased release of cytokines (e.g., TNF-α and IL-1β), further promotes neuronal death (Muzio et al., 2021). Additionally, impaired dopamine metabolism leads to oxidative damage in the striatum (Li et al., 2020). Natural products like quercetin, ginsenosides, and withanolides exhibit neuroprotective effects by enhancing autophagy, lowering α-synuclein aggregation and oxidative stress, along with modulating inflammatory pathways, providing potential therapeutic avenues for PD (Das et al., 2023).

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)

ALS is the damage to motor neurons in the brain and spinal cord (Nijssen, Comley, & Hedlund, 2017). One of the primary mechanisms involves the aggregation of misfolded proteins like SOD1 or TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP-43), leading to proteostasis dysregulation (Prasad, Bharathi, Sivalingam, Girdhar, & Patel, 2019). Apoptosis and neuronal damage are caused by oxidative stress and mitochondrial malfunction, which are essential components of ALS pathogenesis (Obrador et al., 2020). Furthermore, glutamate excitotoxicity, an excessive stimulation of glutamate receptors, causes calcium overload in neurons, promoting cell death (Foran & Trotti, 2009). RSV, ginkgolides, protopanaxatriol, Genistein, EGCG, and vitamins, omega-3-fatty acids are examples of chemical constituents that have shown neuroprotective qualities in ALS by lowering oxidative stress, improving mitochondrial function, and modifying glutamate receptor activity, which attenuates excitotoxicity (Hassan et al., 2022, Rahman et al., 2020).

Multiple sclerosis (MS)

In MS, the immune system targets the myelin sheath of neurons, causing demyelination in the CNS, leading to neuronal loss and neurodegeneration (Dulamea, 2017). T-cell infiltration, a breakdown of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (such as IFN-γ and TNF-α) are some of the mechanisms. Oxidative stress and dysfunction of mitochondria further contribute to neuronal injury (Schreiner, Romanescu, & Popescu, 2022). Natural products such as curcumin, which suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokine production, and green tea polyphenols, which exhibit immunomodulatory effects by reducing T-cell activation, have shown promise in attenuating the neurodegenerative processes of MS (Azam et al., 2019).

Frontotemporal dementia (FTD)

FTD is stated by atrophy of the frontal and temporal lobes of the brain, with symptoms of behavioural changes and cognitive decline (Bang et al., 2015, Warren et al., 2013). The underlying mechanisms involve tauopathies and TDP-43 proteinopathies, where abnormal tau phosphorylation and TDP-43 aggregation disrupt cellular homeostasis. These abnormalities lead to oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, as well as synaptic dysfunction. Autophagy impairment and mitochondrial dysfunction are also implicated in FTD (Chornenkyy, Fardo, & Nelson, 2019). Natural compounds like berberine and curcumin have been extensively studied for their ability to modulate τ pathology, enhance autophagy, and reduce oxidative stress, thereby offering potential therapeutic benefits for FTD (Can and Sanlier, 2025, Shinjyo et al., 2020).

Prion diseases

Prion diseases, such as Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease, are caused by the prion protein (PrP) misfolding into an abnormal form, which induces other PrP molecules to misfold and aggregate, leading to neurodegeneration (Baiardi Mammana, Capellari, & Parchi, 2023). These aggregates cause neuronal damage through mechanisms involving oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, and mitochondrial dysfunction. Prion aggregation causes gliosis, spongiform abnormalities, and extensive cell loss in the brain (Gandhi et al., 2019, Saá et al., 2016). Natural products like polyphenols, flavonoids such as RSV, and curcumin have shown potential in reducing prion aggregation and mitigating the neuroinflammation and oxidative stress linked with prion diseases (Freyssin et al., 2018, Genchi et al., 2024, Henríquez et al., 2020).

Oxidative stress

The pathophysiology of NDs is significantly influenced by oxidative stress, which results from an imbalance between the production of ROS and the effectiveness of antioxidant defence mechanisms. (Chandra Dash et al., 2024). Neurons are particularly vulnerable to oxidative injury due to their high oxygen utilization and abundant mitochondrial composition (Salim, 2017). Oxidative stress in the central nervous system is characterized by the overproduction of ROS, such as hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), superoxide anions (O2−), and hydroxyl radicals (OH•), which are mostly caused by leakage in the mitochondrial electron transport chain. These ROS react with cellular macromolecules, leading to widespread damage. Lipid peroxidation, for instance, compromises the integrity of neuronal membranes, affecting membrane fluidity and leading to ion dysregulation (Afzal et al., 2023, Jomova et al., 2023, Schieber and Chandel, 2014). Protein oxidation impairs the function of key enzymes, including those essential for adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, while ROS-induced DNA damage leads to mutations and compromised cell repair mechanisms (Davalli et al., 2018, Juan et al., 2021). These disruptions enhance mitochondrial dysfunction, decrease energy production, and release pro-apoptotic factors like cytochrome c, further amplifying neuronal death (Zong et al., 2024). Furthermore, oxidative stress triggers redox-sensitive transcription factors, like NF-κB, which contribute to the clinical course of NDs by promoting inflammatory responses and speeding up neuronal breakdown (Olufunmilayo, Gerke-Duncan, & Damian Holsinger,2023). This mechanism is a progressive pathway of diseases like AD, PD, ALS, and HD.

Neuroinflammation

Neuroinflammation is widely acknowledged as a complex biological process involved in the progression and pathogenesis of NDs. This complex process includes both the innate and adaptive immune responses within the CNS, leading to a cycle of neurodegeneration and immune activation that exacerbates neuronal damage and disease progression (Amor and Woodroofe, 2014, Zhang et al., 2023). This mentioned mechanism is responsible for AD, PD, ALS, MS, and NDDs.

Microglial activation and neuroinflammatory signalling in NDs

Microglia, one type of resident immune cell of the CNS, are central to initiating and regulating neuroinflammatory responses (Qin et al., 2023). In the healthy brain, microglia maintain homeostasis by monitoring the CNS environment (Gao et al., 2023). However, in response to pathological stimuli such as protein aggregates (Aβ in AD, α-synuclein in PD), neuronal death, or trauma, microglia undergo rapid morphological changes, becoming activated (Deyell et al., 2023, Miller et al., 2022). Various proinflammatory cytokines (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6), chemokines, ROS, NO, prostaglandins, and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) are released by activated microglia. This release sets off a series of inflammatory signaling events that lead to further damage to neurons (Rodríguez-Gómez et al., 2020).

Neuroinflammation induces oxidative stress and excitotoxicity leads to neuronal death

Chronic neuroinflammation contributes to neuronal death through several mechanisms. Two characteristics of NDs, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, can be the reason for an excessive release of ROS and pro-inflammatory cytokines (Teleanu et al., 2022). Furthermore, neuroinflammation may impair synaptic function and encourage excitotoxicity by releasing excess glutamate, which damages neurons by over-activating NMDAR and causing calcium influx. In AD, activated microglia have been shown to exacerbate Aβ plaque formation and τ pathology, perpetuating neurodegeneration (Armada-Moreira et al., 2020, Mehta et al., 2013, Li et al., 2018).

Role of reactive astrocytes in neuroinflammation and neurodegeneration

Astrocytes, another CNS glial cell type, contribute to a secondary but significant role in neuroinflammation. In response to signals from activated microglia, astrocytes can also adopt a reactive state, marked by increased production of cytokines, chemokines, and neurotoxic molecules (Linnerbauer et al., 2020, Pawate and Bhat, 2008, Reid and Kuipers, 2021). Reactive astrocytes have been implicated in both exacerbating and modulating neuroinflammatory responses (Cekanaviciute & Buckwalter, 2016). While they can contribute to neuronal death by releasing toxic mediators, they also secrete neurotrophic factors that may aid in tissue repair and neuroprotection under certain conditions (Ding et al., 2021). However, in chronic neurodegenerative conditions, their repair mechanisms may become overwhelmed, contributing to glial scarring and inhibition of neuronal regeneration (Moeendarbary et al., 2017, Pekny and Pekna, 2016).

Involvement of innate and adaptive immune system

The adaptive immune system also contributes to neuroinflammation through the recruitment of peripheral immune cells, like T-cells, into the CNS. T-cell infiltration, observed in diseases like multiple sclerosis and AD, can amplify the inflammatory response, leading to further neurodegeneration (DeMaio et al., 2022, Schetters et al., 2018, Zeng et al., 2024). Additionally, neuroinflammation is associated with increased BBB permeability, penetration of immune cells and inflammatory mediators through the CNS, further fuelling the inflammatory cycle (Małkiewicz et al., 2019).

Dual role of neuroinflammation in CNS development and pathology

It is significant to note that neuroinflammation is not universally detrimental. During the reactive state of astrocytes, they produce a range of inflammatory mediators, which mediate the behaviours of surrounding neurons by playing a protective role, facilitating debris clearance, and promoting tissue repair. However, in NDs, this balance is disrupted, and the chronic activation of inflammatory pathways becomes maladaptive, driving neuronal death and disease progression (Adamu et al., 2024, Troncoso-Escudero et al., 2018).

Targeting neuroinflammation as a therapeutic strategy

Targeting these pathways has become a viable therapeutic approach due to the critical role that neuroinflammation plays in NDs. Although clinical trial outcomes have been inconsistent, anti-inflammatory medications, such as non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) targeting COX-2, have been studied (Giri et al., 2024, Hill and Zawia, 2021). More targeted therapies, including inhibitors of specific cytokines (e.g., IL-1β) or immune modulators that reduce microglial activation, are under active investigation (Xu et al., 2023, Zhang et al., 2023). Additionally, therapies aimed at promoting the clearance of toxic protein aggregates while modulating neuroinflammation are being explored as potential treatments for diseases like AD and PD.

Mitochondrial dysfunction

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a primary factor in the advancement and pathogenesis of NDs (Hroudová et al., 2014). Mitochondria are responsible for producing energy through oxidative phosphorylation and play essential roles in regulating intracellular calcium levels, ROS production, and cellular apoptosis. The breakdown of these functions, particularly through excessive Ca2+ influx and overproduction of ROS, leads to a cascade of harmful incidents contributing to neuronal death. Mitochondrial dysfunction is a key feature of several NDs, including AD, PD, HD, and ALS (Kausar et al., 2018, Walters and Usachev, 2023). Here is a breakdown of the key aspects of mitochondrial dysfunction and its role in neurodegenerative processes.

Role of mitochondria in cellular function and calcium homeostasis

Mitochondria are primarily known for their role in ATP production via oxidative phosphorylation, a crucial process in energy metabolism. In addition to energy production, mitochondria regulate Ca2+ homeostasis by maintaining low cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations. This regulation controls neuronal excitability, neurotransmitter release, and cellular survival. However, under pathological conditions, mitochondria may fail to buffer Ca2+ effectively, leading to toxic accumulations in the cytosol, contributing to mitochondrial and cellular damage (Boyman et al., 2020, Vilas-Boas et al., 2023, Walkon et al., 2022).

Calcium dysregulation and ROS generation in mitochondrial dysfunction

Severe oxidative stress leads to excessive Ca2+ being absorbed into the mitochondria and a rise in ROS (Tirichen et al., 2021). ROS is the byproduct of the mitochondrial electron transport chain and damages the cell’s DNA, proteins, and lipids. The collapse of the mitochondrial membrane potential, essential for preserving mitochondrial function, is brought on by this oxidative stress in conjunction with calcium overload. This collapse is commonly seen in neurodegenerative conditions and is an early event in mitochondrial failure (Juan, Pérez de la Lastra, Plou, & Pérez-Lebeña, 2021).

Mitochondrial permeability transition pores and membrane potential collapse

A key factor in mitochondrial failure is mitochondrial permeability transition pores (mPTPs). Large, non-specific channels called mPTPs develop in reaction to oxidative stress and Ca2+ overload (Endlicher et al., 2023). The electrochemical balance across the mitochondrial membrane, which is required for ATP generation, is disrupted when they open, causing the mitochondrial matrix to enlarge. The outer mitochondrial membrane ruptures, mitochondrial uncoupling occurs, and pro-apoptotic substances are released into the cytosol as a result of this disruption (Endlicher et al., 2023, Halestrap, 2009, Yalamanchili et al., 2022).

Release of caspase-dependent apoptosis and pro-apoptotic factors

Apoptotic factors such as cytochrome c and AIF are released from the mitochondrial intermembrane gap when the outer mitochondrial membrane ruptures (Arnoult et al., 2002, Checchetto et al., 2018). In caspase-dependent apoptosis, cytochrome c binds with apoptotic protease-activating factor-1 (Apaf-1) and procaspase-9, creating an apoptosome complex. After activating Caspase-3, this complex starts the cleavage of cellular substrates, such as poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase-1 [PARP-1], which results in programmed cell death. Caspase-mediated neuronal apoptosis is a hallmark of NDs, exacerbating neuronal loss (V. Singh et al., 2022, Ulukaya et al., 2011, Wójcik et al., 2024).

Caspase-independent mechanisms: AIF and nuclear damage

Both caspase-dependent and caspase-independent pathways play a major role in neurodegeneration. Once released from the mitochondria, AIF extensively fragments DNA in the nucleus and promotes chromatin condensation. This route is crucial in conditions where apoptosis happens even when caspase activity is low, since it is not dependent on caspase activation. AIF-mediated nuclear damage is another key factor contributing to the loss of neurons in neurodegenerative conditions (Norberg et al., 2010, Zhu et al., 2007, Zong and Liang, 2023).

Apoptosis

Apoptosis is a key factor in the neuronal loss observed in NDs. This programmed cell death occurs in neurons, which are highly vulnerable due to their limited regenerative capacity. Progressive neurodegeneration results from the ongoing activation of apoptotic pathways, and specific patterns of neuronal loss are responsible for the clinical manifestations of conditions including PD, AD, HD, and ALS. These mechanisms are potential contributors to these diseases. Here is a breakdown of the key aspects of apoptosis in neurodegenerative processes.

Cellular and molecular characteristics of apoptosis

Apoptosis is typified by specific cellular alterations, including DNA fragmentation, membrane blebbing, chromatin condensation, and cell shrinkage (Banfalvi, 2014, Majtnerová and Roušar, 2018). These morphological alterations contrast with necrosis, which is associated with cellular swelling and membrane rupture. In neurodegeneration, these apoptotic features are often accompanied by energy-dependent processes that require ATP for protein synthesis, activation of signaling cascades, and cellular reorganization. The fact that neurons have high metabolic demands makes them particularly vulnerable to apoptotic stimuli, especially when mitochondrial dysfunction occurs, as in PD and AD (Banfalvi, 2014, Majtnerová and Roušar, 2018, Elmore, 2007).

Extrinsic and intrinsic pathways

The extrinsic and intrinsic pathways are the two main mechanisms by which apoptosis is initiated (Lossi, 2022). Death ligands like TNF and Fas ligands link to their receptors to initiate the extrinsic route, which then activates the death pathway’s caspase performers (Xu & Shi, 2007). The intrinsic pathway action is through mitochondrial signals, whereby the enactment of apoptotic signals, like cytochrome c, which is released into the cytosol from mitochondria. Both extrinsic and intrinsic pathways lead to the activation of effector caspases, which break down the structural and functional elements of the cell (Bedoui et al., 2020, Krautwald et al., 2010).

Mitochondrial dysfunction in apoptosis

The intrinsic apoptotic pathway depends on mitochondria, especially in neurodegenerative conditions, where mitochondrial failure is a prevalent feature (Wu et al., 2019). Damage to mitochondria results in the loss of membrane potential, impaired energy formation, and increased ROS, all of which contribute to neuronal apoptosis (Bhat et al., 2015, Kausar et al., 2018). In diseases like PD, the suppression of mitochondrial complex I results in oxidative stress and the production of apoptogenic substances, which promote neuronal damage through both caspase-dependent and caspase-independent routes (Perier et al., 2012, Winklhofer and Haass, 2010).

Caspase-dependent and caspase-independent apoptosis

Apoptosis can proceed through caspase-dependent or caspase-independent mechanisms. In the caspase-dependent pathway, initiator caspases (Caspase-9 and Caspase-8) activate effector caspases (Caspase-3), which cleave cellular proteins to induce cell death (McIlwain et al., 2013). However, caspase-independent pathways involve factors like endonuclease G and AIF, which move from the mitochondria to the nucleus and promote DNA breakage and cell death without the involvement of caspases (Bhadra, 2022, Puyal et al., 2013, Wang and Youle, 2009).

Neuroinflammation and apoptosis

Neuroinflammation is closely linked with apoptotic processes in NDs. Pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-1β are released when glial cells, especially microglia and astrocytes, are chronically activated. These cytokines activate death receptors on neurons and start the extrinsic apoptotic pathway (Hsieh and Yang, 2013, Radi et al., 2014, Shabab et al., 2017). Furthermore, inflammation exacerbates oxidative stress, which triggers the intrinsic pathway, forming a vicious cycle of neuroinflammation and neuronal apoptosis that accelerates disease progression (Agostinho et al., 2010).

Excitotoxicity

Excitotoxicity (neurotoxic process) arises from the the overactivation of glutamate receptors, particularly NMDA and alpha-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazole propionic acid (AMPA) receptors, which leads to neuronal injury and death (Caudle and Zhang, 2009, Gautam et al., 2023, Hanada, 2020). Glutamate, the CNS’s main excitatory neurotransmitter, is essential for memory, learning, and synaptic transmission. However, its dysregulation, as seen in NDs, results in prolonged activation of NMDA and AMPA receptors, causing excessive calcium influx into neurons. Many calcium-dependent enzymes, such as phospholipases, nitric oxide synthase (NOS), and proteases (calpains), are activated by elevated intracellular calcium levels. These enzymes break down cellular constituents and produce free radicals. These enzymes break down essential proteins and phospholipids, leading to structural and functional damage in neurons (Costas-Ferreira and Faro, 2021, Dong et al., 2009, Ji et al., 2016, Zhang et al., 2016). The sustained activation of NMDA receptors also disrupts mitochondrial function, leading to increased ROS production and reduced ATP synthesis, exacerbating energy failure in the affected neurons (Kamat et al., 2016, Mira et al., 2019). Furthermore, excitotoxicity is compounded by impaired glutamate clearance from the synaptic cleft (Armada-Moreira et al., 2020). In neurodegenerative conditions, astrocytes lose their ability to efficiently uptake glutamate through excitatory amino acid transporters (EAATs), leading to extracellular accumulation of glutamate, which perpetuates excitotoxicity (Todd & Hardingham, 2020). The combination of mitochondrial dysfunction, excessive calcium influx, and the overactivation of calcium-dependent enzymes ultimately leads to neuronal swelling, DNA fragmentation, and cell death, driving the progressive loss of neurons observed in NDs (Jung et al., 2020, Klemmensen et al., 2024).

Role of some explored natural products and their bioactive substances towards NDs

Srivastava et al. have proposed that, several natural items like conventional pharmacological substances are used for the treatment of NDs (Srivastava et al., 2016). It has been seen that honey, propolis, Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal, ginseng, Uncaria rhynchophylla (Miq.) Miq. ex Havil., marine macroalgae, luteolin, quercetin, RSV, apigenin, hesperidin, and cyanobacteria are those natural products that have potential effects against neurodegenerative disorders. Examples include RSV, a naturally occurring phytoalexin polyphenol that shares structural similarities with stilbenes (Rocha-González et al., 2008). It is found in numerous plants, including hellebore, mulberry, the skin of grapes, red wine extracts, and peanuts. Studies both in vitro and in vivo have revealed the neuroprotective effect of RSV, which penetrates the blood–brain barrier and increases antioxidant enzymes to produce its antioxidant effect. It works well for epilepsy, AD, and HD (Murlimanju, 2020). On the other hand, hesperidin, a naturally occurring flavanone glycoside found in citrus fruits, has anti-inflammatory, neuroprotective, and antioxidant properties. The natural substances can help with NDs and cross the BBB. It works by boosting the expression of Mn-SOD, glutathione reductase, glutathione peroxide, choline acetyltransferase (CAT), and glutathione. Another mechanism underpinning the anti-oxidative impact is the extracellular signal-regulated kinase and nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (ERK/Nrf2) signaling pathway, which enhances antioxidant cellular defenses (Hajialyani et al., 2019). The following section will go into greater detail on the therapeutic potential of natural compounds and their bioactive substances to have a neuroprotective activity on the pathologies of NDs:

Honey

Honey is a naturally occurring food product that is produced from floral nectar by honeybees (Apis mellifera; Family: Apidae). The term “apitherapy”, which refers to the medicinal application of honey and bee products, combines contemporary research with traditional honey expertise. Strong neuroprotective qualities are exhibited by the natural phytochemicals found in honey, including chrysin, gallic acid, caffeic acid, ellagic acid, p-coumaric acid, apigenin, quercetin, luteolin, myricetin, and naringenin. Through a variety of routes, including oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, mitochondrial malfunction, apoptosis, and excitotoxicity, these substances are essential in preventing NDs. Those exhibit antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and neuroprotective properties by reducing reactive oxygen species (ROS), inhibiting neuroinflammation, preventing apoptosis, and modulating mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby contributing to potential therapeutic benefits in neurodegenerative disorders (NDs).

Chrysin

Chrysin (5,7-dihydroxyflavone) is a flavonoid that exhibits anxiolytic and anti-inflammatory properties by acting on gamma aminobutyric acid-A (GABA-A) receptors, showing potential in modulating neurotransmission. It inhibits microglia activation, preventing neuroinflammation, which is key in neurodegenerative conditions. Chrysin activates the Nrf2 pathway, which upregulates antioxidant enzymes including SOD and glutathione peroxidase (GPx), thereby reducing ROS and preventing lipid peroxidation. It also inhibits monoamine oxidase-A (MAO-A) and monoamine oxidase-B (MAO-B) enzymes, which are critical in dopamine metabolism, thus aiding in dopamine recovery in Parkinson’s disease (Mishra et al., 2021).

Gallic acid

Gallic acid has strong antioxidant properties that neutralize free radicals and prevent Aβ-induced neurotoxicity and oxidative stress-induced neuronal damage. It also reduces neuroinflammation by preventing pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β. This helps in protecting neurons from inflammation-induced degeneration, a hallmark in AD and PD. Gallic acid’s ability to prevent neuronal cell death through both anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms makes it a potential candidate for treating AD and HD (Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014, Mori et al., 2020).

Caffeic acid

A significant antioxidant is caffeic acid (phenolic acid), which is found in honey, coffee, fruits, and vegetables and has neuroprotective properties. An in vitro study with caffeic acid showed suppression of lipid peroxidation in mouse brain homogenates. This study has shown that caffeic acid has neuroprotective effects on neurons (Jeong et al., 2011). Another in vitro study shows its ability to suppress neuroinflammation by reversing aluminium-induced overexpression of 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) in brain tissues and preventing neuronal death in the hippocampus, which is linked to cognitive deficits (Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014). Caffeic acid has also been reported to enhance acetylcholinesterase activity in various brain regions, both in vitro and in vivo, suggesting its role in memory improvement through cholinergic signaling modulation, alongside its neuroprotective benefits (Ehtiati et al., 2023).

Chlorogenic acid, a caffeic acid derivative found in honey, exhibits dose-dependent neuroprotective effects. It reduces apoptosis in PC12 cell lines exposed to methyl mercury by lowering ROS and activating Caspase-3 (Li et al., 2008). In scopolamine-induced memory impairment, chlorogenic acid significantly improved cognitive performance in behavioural tests by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase and malondialdehyde in the frontal cortex and hippocampus. Furthermore, chlorogenic acid showed analgesic and anti-inflammatory properties by blocking inflammatory mediators like NO and TNF-α. Thus, chlorogenic acid in honey may help alleviate neuroinflammation and enhance memory function (Nguyen et al., 2024).

Ellagic acid

Ellagic acid is a potent neuroprotective agent in honey, primarily due to its antioxidant properties. In NDs, oxidative stress and inflammation are common pathological processes. Ellagic acid mitigates oxidative damage by scavenging free radicals and reducing lipid peroxidation. It has been demonstrated to protect neurons by downregulating pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibiting microglial activation. This compound also regulates apoptosis pathways by modulating pro-apoptotic proteins and enhancing mitochondrial function. Research highlights its potential in preventing AD and PD by inhibiting Aβ-aggregation and stabilizing cellular redox balance (Ardah et al., 2020, Gupta et al., 2021, Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014).

p-Coumaric acid

Another polyphenolic molecule found in honey, p-coumaric acid, has potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant qualities that are essential for neuroprotection. By increasing the activity of catalase and superoxide dismutase, antioxidant enzymes, it lowers the oxidative stress linked to NDs. By preventing ROS from having neurotoxic effects and lowering inflammatory reactions in brain tissues, p-coumaric acid also has neuroprotective properties. Its promise as a treatment for disorders like AD and PD is suggested by its capacity to alter signaling pathways, including nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB). P-coumaric acid was able to decrease lipid peroxidation while simultaneously raising glutathione (GSH), SOD, and catalase activity levels. In contrast to 5-S-cysteinyl-dopamine-induced neurotoxicity, p-coumaric acid had neuroprotective properties (Iranshahy et al., 2022, Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014).

Apigenin

Apigenin is a flavonoid present in honey, which has been demonstrated to have neuroprotective activities. By inhibiting the brain’s production of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and interleukins, it prevents neuroinflammation. Through the inhibition of lowered GSH levels and the quenching of ROS, apigenin also prevents the dose-dependent excitotoxicity of kainic acid-induced hippocampus cells. By scavenging free radicals, apigenin also has antioxidative properties that shield neurons from oxidative injury. Apigenin inhibits the expression of CD40 generated by interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), while CD40 signaling plays an important role in immunological responses linked to microglia in the brain. Apigenin has also been shown to promote neurogenesis and enhance memory and learning by modulating neurotransmitter activity and synaptic plasticity (El-Seedi et al., 2020, Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014).

Quercetin

Quercetin, a potent antioxidant flavonoid, plays a crucial role in neuroprotection by scavenging free radicals as well as inhibiting lipid peroxidation, which is prevalent in NDs. It modulates oxidative stress by enhancing antioxidant defences, such as glutathione levels, and inhibits the pro-inflammatory pathways mediated by microglial activation. Quercetin’s neuroprotective effects extend to its ability to prevent neuronal apoptosis by inhibiting caspase activation and mitochondrial dysfunction. It is effective in reducing amyloid-beta deposition and τ-protein aggregation in AD models, thus slowing cognitive decline (Talebi et al., 2021, Zhang et al., 2022, Li et al., 2023).

Luteolin

Luteolin, another key flavonoid in honey, exhibits powerful anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects. It attenuates neuroinflammation by suppressing microglial activation and inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α and IL-6. Luteolin also inhibits the formation of NO and ROS, reducing oxidative damage to neurons (Zhu et al., 2024). In AD, luteolin has been demonstrated to protect against Aβ-induced neurotoxicity by preventing the production of toxic aggregates. Its ability to enhance synaptic plasticity and memory makes it a promising candidate for treating cognitive decline in NDs (He et al., 2023, Jiang et al., 2023).

Myricetin

Myricetin, present in honey, has significant neuroprotective properties, largely due to its antioxidant activity. It combats oxidative stress by lowering ROS levels and raising antioxidant enzyme activity. Myricetin also protects neurons by stabilizing mitochondrial membranes and inhibiting apoptotic pathways. Its ability to chelate metal ions, which catalyse the production of ROS, further enhances its neuroprotective potential. In the context of NDs, myricetin inhibits Aβ-aggregation and reduces τ-phosphorylation, both key factors in Alzheimer’s disease pathology (Iftikhar et al., 2022, Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014, Semwal et al., 2016).

Naringenin

Naringenin is a flavonoid with strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. It protects against neuronal damage by reducing oxidative stress and preventing the inflammatory response. Naringenin modulates key signaling pathways, like NF-κB, which is involved in neuroinflammation. It also enhances mitochondrial function and prevents apoptosis by regulating pro-apoptotic and anti-apoptotic proteins. In AD and PD, naringenin has been demonstrated to prevent Aβ-toxicity and dopaminergic neuron degeneration, respectively, making it potentially effective for these conditions (Cai et al., 2023, Emran et al., 2023).

Kaempferol

Kaempferol, a flavonoid found in honey, plays a crucial role in neuroprotection. Studies show that it prevents dopamine loss in the brain by protecting neurons against oxidative stress and apoptosis. Specifically, kaempferol attenuates neurotoxin-induced damage, such as from 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP), a PD model neurotoxin, which depletes dopamine levels and impairs neuronal activity. Kaempferol has also demonstrated protective effects in inhibiting mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis through modulating antioxidant defenses, like SOD and GSH peroxidase. Kaempferol has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, as well as anti-apoptotic properties. It can chelate metal ions that contribute to ROS generation, thereby reducing oxidative stress. Its neuroprotective effects are closely linked to these antioxidant actions, which help preserve neuronal viability by reducing mitochondrial dysfunction and promoting autophagy to clear damaged cellular components (Iftikhar et al., 2022, Przedborski et al., 2004).

Galangin

Galangin is a flavonoid present in honey, which inhibits neuroinflammation by suppressing the formation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-10) in microglial cells (Fadzil et al., 2023). In experimental models, galangin mitigates neurodegeneration by preventing the activation of these inflammatory pathways, making it a promising agent for conditions like AD and PD. Galangin exhibits powerful anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. It modulates neuroinflammatory processes by blocking inflammatory signaling pathways in microglia, reducing oxidative stress, and protecting neurons from excitotoxicity. The inhibition of nitric oxide production and ROS formation is essential in preventing neuron damage in neurodegenerative conditions (Choi et al., 2017, Zhang et al., 2023).

Catechin and epicatechin

Catechin and epicatechin, which are present in honey, help to protect neurons by lowering inflammation and oxidative stress (Fadzil et al., 2023, Mijanur Rahman et al., 2014). These substances have been demonstrated to scavenge free radicals and strengthen the body’s natural antioxidant defenses, thereby shielding neuronal cells from oxidative damage. Additionally, it has been shown that catechins enhance neurotrophic factors like brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), which activate synaptic plasticity and neuronal survival. Iron-chelating capabilities and the regulation of oxidative stress pathways are two aspects of catechins’ neuroprotective bioactivity. These substances can stop mitochondrial dysfunction, a defining feature of many neurodegenerative illnesses, and neurotoxicity brought on by substances like Aβ. By promoting synaptic plasticity and shielding neurons from apoptosis, catechin has been demonstrated to enhance memory and cognitive performance in animal models (Bernatoniene and Kopustinskiene, 2018, Li et al., 2024, Ozdemir, 2020, Sebastiani et al., 2021).

Withania somnifera

One of the main medications in the Ayurvedic and Unani systems is W. somnifera. In English, it is frequently referred to as “Indian Ginseng” or “Indian Winter Cherry”. “Ashwagandha” is its Sanskrit name, and “Asgand” is its Urdu name. A tiny woody shrub, W. somnifera, is a member of the Solanaceae family of plants. Anxiety, inability to conceive, sleep issues, rheumatoid arthritis, spermatorrhea, scabies, neurosis, painful swellings, asthma, leucorrhea, polyarthritis, leucoderma, ulcers, and lumbago are among the conditions for which it has been used traditionally. Alkaloids (isopelletierine, anaferine, cuseohygrine, anahygrine, etc.), flavonoids, steroidal lactones (withanolides, withaferins), steroids, salts, and compounds containing nitrogen are among the many plant phytochemicals it contains. The main bioactive components present in W. somnifera are withaferin A, withanolide A, withanolide B, withanone, withanoside (IV, V, VI), withanamide (A, C) withanolide Q, anaferine, and sominone, except that it has more than 12 alkaloids, 40 withanolides (Lerose et al., 2024, Wongtrakul et al., 2021). By modifying phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase and Protein Kinase B (PI3K/Akt), MAPK signaling pathways, and anti-apoptotic cascades, extracts and active phytoconstituents or metabolites of W. somnifera have been demonstrated in various cellular and animal models to primarily possess antioxidant activities and rescue neuronal cells against toxic insults and inflammation. This protects neurons from degenerative conditions like AD, PD, and HD. The claims that W. somnifera can be used to treat a broad area of clinical issues are quite encouraging. It has been demonstrated that W. somnifera root powder considerably reduces the alterations in neuronal cell bodies in the stress-affected CA2 and CA3 areas of the hippocampus. Furthermore, standardized root powder of W. somnifera decreased immobilization stress-induced neuronal damage in the hippocampal CA2 and CA3 regions as compared to animals that were not under stress (Dar & Ahmad, 2020). In a recent in-silico study with W. somnifera, withaferin A, withanolide A, withanolide B, withanolide D, anaferine, beta-sitosterol showed a greater affinity for GluN2B-containing NMDA receptor and interacted with residues that were crucial in mediating inhibition by binding at the interface subunit (Kumar & Patnaik, 2016). Withanolide A

In neurons from a rat model of PD, withanolide A has been demonstrated to promote the regeneration of axons, dendrites, and pre- and post-synapses. Additionally, it demonstrated the effects of proteinopathies by reducing α-synuclein levels by 38 % in the Parkinson’s disease model, NL5901 Caenorhabditis elegans, indicating that withanolide A may be helpful for PD patients (Wongtrakul et al., 2021).

Withanone

Withanone, isolated from W. somnifera root extract at doses orally administered to Wistar rats for 21 d exhibited noteworthy enhancement of sophisticated ability by lessening amyloid β42 and decreasing the raised levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, monocyte chemotactic protein 1 (MCP-1), NO, lipid peroxidation as well as both β and γ secretase activity. Additionally, one treatment dramatically stopped the drop in GSH and cholinergic activity (Pandey et al., 2018).

Withaferin A

Withaferin A exhibits anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities by inhibiting pathways like NF-κB, which are implicated in neuronal inflammation. It also protects dopaminergic neurons, making it particularly effective in PD models. Furthermore, withaferin A prevents mitochondrial dysfunction and stabilizes the levels of neurotransmitters (Heyninck et al., 2014, Kumar et al., 2023, Zhao et al., 2021). The neuroprotective function of withaferin A against Aβ secretion and aggregation in vitro was recently examined for the first time, and it was demonstrated that withaferin A can lower Aβ (Lerose et al., 2024).

Withanoside IV

Withanoside IV facilitates synaptic regeneration by promoting axonal and dendritic growth, leading to improved cognitive function. In Alzheimer’s models, it reduces Aβ plaques and protects against neurodegeneration by improving memory and synaptic plasticity. It also helps in the detoxification of neurons by enhancing antioxidant defenses and preventing oxidative damage (Kuboyama et al., 2002, Lerose et al., 2024).

Panax ginseng (Ginseng)

Panax ginseng C. A. Mey. is a perennial plant in the Araliaceae family’s Panax genus. Because of its restorative and tonic properties, it has been used in folk medicine for over 3 000 years and is particularly popular in China, Korea, and Japan as a traditional herbal remedy. Since nearly all ginsenosides have 30 carbon atoms organized in four rings of steroid nuclei, their basic structures are similar. Based on the location and quantity of the sugar moiety, ginsenosides are divided into three different categories: A-panaxadiol group (Rb1, Rb2, Rb3, Rc, Rd, Rg3, and Rh2), B-panaxatriol group (Re, Rg1, Rg2, and Rh1), and C-oleanolic acid group (Ro) (Zheng et al., 2018). Ginsenosides, the primary bioactive component of P. ginseng, are what give it its neuroprotective properties. The most effective ginsenosides among them are Rb1, Rg1, Rd, and Re. Other minor phytoconstituents, like notoginsenoside R2, pseudoginsenoside-F11, and P. ginseng polysaccharides, have also garnered a lot of attention as potential anti-Parkinson drugs and neuroprotective agents (González-Burgos et al., 2015, Wang et al., 2021). The neuroprotective activities of P. ginseng, mediated by its bioactive compounds, were illustrated in Fig. 6.

Phytoconstituents present in Panax ginseng contribute to neuroprotective activities. The figure shows the phytoconstituent ginsenosides (Rb1, Rg1, Rg3, Re, and Rd) exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic effects, modulate signaling pathways, inhibit amyloid-β deposition, regulate nerve growth factors, and promote neurogenesis, thereby supporting neuroprotection in neurodegenerative disorders.

P. ginseng shows potential as a neuroprotective agent against disorders like PD, AD, ALS, and cognitive impairments. Ginsenosides act as neurotransmitter modulators by targeting ion channels (Ca2+, K+, Na+) and receptors such as GABA-A and NMDA, influencing various neural functions. Key signaling pathways involved include PI3K/Akt, TLR4/NF-κB, ROS/Thioredoxin-Interacting Protein (TXNIP)/Nod-like Receptor Protein 3 (NLRP3), HO-1/Nrf2, Wnt/β-catenin, and Ca2+. Nearly 150 ginsenosides have been isolated from different parts of P. ginseng and its species, offering a rich source for further therapeutic exploration (González-Burgos et al., 2015, Zhou et al., 2024). Here are some most bioactive compounds of P. ginseng and their mechanism of action:

Ginsenoside Rb1 exerts antioxidant effects by selectively scavenging hypochlorous acid and hydroxyl radicals, while also preventing oxidative stress-induced endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and inhibiting mitochondrial, ER, and death receptor-mediated apoptosis in PC12 cells. It enhances synaptic plasticity via protein expression, protecting against cognitive decline, and inhibits Aβ plaque formation in AD, preserving neuronal viability and synaptic integrity. Rb1 also exhibits anti-inflammatory activity by suppressing TNF-α-induced c-un N-terminal kinase pathway (JNK), p38, and NF-κB signaling, thereby reducing inflammatory marker expression in endothelial cells. Various investigations on rat models proved the usefulness of ginsenoside Rb1 as an anti-AD and anti-PD (Gong et al., 2022).

Ginsenoside Rg1 exerts antidepressant-like effects by enhancing hippocampal neurogenesis and activating the BDNF-tyrosine receptor kinase B (TrkB) signaling pathway (Jiang et al., 2012). In APP/PS1 mice, Rg1 and Rg2 reduced hippocampal Aβ(1−42) accumulation and lowered levels of p-Tau, Aβ(1−42), and C-terminal fragments (CTFs), indicating neuroprotection through downregulation of AD-related proteins and BDNF-TrkB activation (Li et al., 2016, Zheng et al., 2018). In MPTP-induced Parkinson’s disease models, Rg1 alleviated motor deficits, reduced α-synuclein expression, preserved tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) levels, and protected dopaminergic neurons in the striatum and substantia nigra, suggesting its therapeutic potential for PD (Heng et al., 2016, Zheng et al., 2018, Zhou et al., 2015).

Ginsenoside Rg3, a saponin isolated from P. ginseng, has shown anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in disease models involving oxidative stress and inflammation. It conferred neuroprotection by suppressing ROS and preventing mitochondrial swelling in H2O2-induced rat forebrain mitochondria. In H2O2 treated human astrocyte CRT cells, Rg3 reduced senescence by inhibiting NF-κB, p38, and proinflammatory cytokines IL-6, IL-8, and MCP-1 (Hou, Kim, Sung, & Choi, 2017). In AD, Rg3 reduced the neurotoxicity of Aβ42. Also, there are various neurological mechanisms of Rg3, which are briefly described by Joo et al. (2008) and Wang et al. (2022).

Ginsenoside Re, a protopanaxatriol-type saponin from P. ginseng roots, exhibits strong antioxidant activity by scavenging free radicals and reducing lipid peroxidation in brain cells. It also improves memory and cognition in Alzheimer’s models by modulating cholinergic function and reducing Aβ accumulation (Gao et al., 2022, Shi et al., 2022). Ginsenoside Re exhibited antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects against dopaminergic neurotoxicity in vivo. In methamphetamine-treated prodynorphin knockout mice with PD, these effects were enhanced via substance P-mediated downregulation of the NK1 receptor following dynorphin-induced κ-opioid receptor overexpression. Targeting mitochondrial dysfunction, SH-SY5Y cells restored methamphetamine-induced mitochondrial burden in vitro and rescued the disrupted NO-regulated PINK1-Hsp90/LRPPRC-Hsp60-complex IV signaling in PINK1-null neurons (González-Burgos et al., 2017). Ginsenoside Re was shown by Gao et al., to reduce Aβ levels in N2a/APP695 cells in vitro, while Chen et al. reported that oral Re (25 mg/kg, i.g, for 18 h) significantly lowered Aβ(1-40) and Aβ(1-42) in Tg2576 mice brains in vivo (Ahmed et al., 2011, Chen et al., 2006, Gao et al., 2022).

Ginsenoside Rd exerts anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, anti-apoptotic, Ca2+ influx-inhibitory, and mitochondrial-protective effects, thereby modulating nerve growth factors, reducing excitotoxicity, and promoting neuronal regeneration (Chen et al., 2022). Ginsenoside Rd, a dammarane-type steroid, has shown promising neuroprotective effects in both preclinical and clinical studies involving humans, dogs, and rodents, using techniques like tandem mass spectrometry, electrospray ionization, and liquid chromatography. While Phase I trials of P. ginseng reported no adverse effects, Phases II and III revealed elevated liver enzymes and blood creatinine or urea nitrogen not seen in the placebo group. Notably, Rd exhibited a higher brain/serum ratio than BBB-impermeable albumin, indicating its ability to cross the intact BBB (Ye et al., 2013).

Uncaria rhynchophylla

The plant U. rhynchophylla, which belongs to the Rubiaceae family, is utilized in traditional Chinese medicine. Alkaloids, rhinchophylline, isorhynchophylline, hirsutine, hirsuteine, corynanthine, corynoxine, and dihydrocorynantheine are all present in its extract (Ling and Zhang, 2020, Mohd Sairazi and Sirajudeen, 2020, Xian et al., 2012). U. rhynchophylla has been used to treat a variety of neurological conditions and reduce blood pressure in traditional oriental medicine. It is used to eliminate heat, assess hepatic hyperfunction, and alleviate vertigo, tremors, and convulsions. It also acts as a sedative, anticonvulsant and antispasmodic (Suk et al., 2002). The main alkaloids in U. rhynchophylla as neuroprotective agents are rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline (Wang et al., 2018, Yang et al., 2020). Although one new alkaloid (4-geissoschizine N-oxide methyl ether) was isolated by Qi et al. at the year of 2014. There have some known alkaloids with 3-epi-geissoschizine methyl ether (1), geissoschizine methyl ether (2), 4-hirsuteine N-oxide (3), hirsuteine (4), hirsutine (5), 3α-dihydro-cadambine (6), 3β-isodihydro-cadambine (7), cadambine (8), strictosamide (9), and akuammigine (10). Among them, 1, 2, 4, and 5 exhibited potent neuroprotective effects in an in vitro test against glutamate-induced HT22 cells, and the rest showed weak neuroprotective activity (Qi et al., 2014). Both rhynchophylline and isorhynchophylline exhibit similar activity in an in vivo study with PC12 cells, where they significantly decrease Aβ(25−35) induced cell death, calcium overloading, and τ protein hyperphosphorylation. U. rhynchophylla has been shown to enhance cell viability, attenuate dopaminergic neuronal significant nigra, reduce striatum and inhibit heat-shock protein 90 and autophagy. The neurotoxin 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium (MPP+) can reduce dopaminergic neurons to mimic PD-induced SH-SY5Y and MPTP mouse cell models (Lan et al., 2018, Rahman et al., 2021, Xian et al., 2012). In a recent study, U. rhynchophylla on experimental animals showed neuroprotective efficacy by up-regulating the PI3K/Akt/mTOR signaling pathway, thereby shielding neurons from the neurotoxicity caused by MPTP in mouse models of PD (Zheng et al., 2021). Rhynchophylline stimulates myocyte enhancer factor 2D (MEF2D) via activating the PI3K/Akt/GSK3β cascade, which prevents MPP+-triggered neurotoxicity. As a new MEF2D enhancer, rhynchophylline may be a good option for additional preclinical research in the prevention of PD. When combined, these findings show that U. rhynchophylla exhibits a neuroprotective effect via a variety of neural defense mechanisms against injury. Recent research, however, has shown that isorhynchophylline readily crosses the BBB. These findings showed that isorhynchophylline might be used to treat NDs as an anti-inflammatory drug (Hu et al., 2018, Rahman et al., 2021). Marine macroalgae

Seaweeds, often known as macroalgae, are marine organisms that contain naturally occurring physiologically active substances. Rhodophyta (red algae), Chlorophyta (green algae), and Phaeophyta (brown algae) are among the various classes of macroalgae. Seaweeds have long been used as herbal medicine to treat tumors, urinary tract conditions, gastrointestinal issues, cough, boils, hemorrhoids, ulcers, asthma, and migraines, particularly in Asian nations. There are many species of marine macroalgae in which phytoconstituents are effective in neuroprotection. Each species has different components responsible for neuroprotection. Such as Fucus serratus, Saccharina latissima, Codium duthieae, Amphiroa beauvoisii, Gelidium foliaceum, Laurencia complanata, Rhodomelopsis africana, Hypnea musciformis, Ochtodes secundiramea, Ecklonia cava (Lomartire and Gonçalves, 2023, Olasehinde et al., 2019). It has been demonstrated that the majority of the neuroprotective mechanisms attributed to these effects in marine macroalgae include preventing acetylcholinesterase and Aβ aggregation, reducing oxidative stress and kinase activity, promoting neurite outgrowth, reducing inflammation, and safeguarding dopaminergic neurons (Alghazwi et al., 2018). Fucoxanthin, phlorotannins, laminarin, fucoidan, phloroglucinol, dieckol, and phlorofucofuroeckol A (PFFA) are the phytoconstituents extracted from the above marine macroalgaes, which have potent neuroprotective activity. Although there are various phytoconstituents like fucosterol, eckmaxol, sarahydroquinoic acid from marine macroalgae that are responsible for neuroprotective activities (Alghazwi et al., 2018, Ignacimuthu, 2020, Lomartire and Gonçalves, 2023, Park et al., 2020, Yang et al., 2022). Fucoxanthin

A common carotenoid, fucoxanthin, found in edible brown seaweeds, has been used as a bioactive component in nutritional supplements and functional foods. The conjugated alkenyl system in fucoxanthin gives it color and antioxidant properties. By blocking the activity of β-secretase (BACE-1), a crucial enzyme for the cleavage of Aβ precursor proteins, it reduces the production of Aβ and prevents the aggregation of amyloid proteins. Molecular docking indicates that the BACE-1 enzyme is inhibited by a connection between the hydroxy groups of fucoxanthin and the amino acid residues of BACE-1. Fucoxanthin’s antioxidant properties contribute to neuroprotection. It may stop H2O2-induced neurotoxicity in PC12 cells, activate the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway, which strengthens cellular defences against oxidative stress, and lower intracellular ROS levels in SH-SY5Y cells as well as primary cerebellar granular neurons in vitro. In RAW 264.7 macrophages, anti-inflammatory activity prevents NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK) from being activated. By blocking the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways in BV2 cells, it also lowers the release of IL-6, TNF-α, and iNOS that is brought on by LPS. Fucoxanthin has numerous additional neuroprotective properties, including anti-gut microbiota problems, anti-neuronal loss, and anti-neurotransmission dysregulation (Yang et al., 2022).

Phlorotannin

Tannins are polyphenolic compounds of plants. One of the many algal polyphenol families, phlorotanins has important pharmacological implications. Size, age, tissue type, salinity, nutritional level, season, herbivory intensity, water temperature, and light intensity all affect how much of it is present. It is only found in brown algae. Numerous phloroglucinol (1,3,5-trihydroxybenzene) molecules joined in different ways make up their composition. It possesses several biological characteristics, including antidiabetic, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, and anticancer effects. Polyphenolic chemicals are more abundant in the following brown algae species: Hizikia fusiformis, Ecklonia cava, Ecklonia kurome, and Ecklonia bicyclis. The neuroprotective effects of phenol tannins such as dieckol, phlorofucofuroeckol-A, eckol, and 8,8′-bieckol, as well as Phlorofucofuroeckol A (PFF-A) and 974-A, which are isolated from Ecklonia species, were evaluated. Both 974-A and phenolfuroeckol-A (PFF-A) function as potent neuroprotective agents and antioxidants that shield neuronal PC12 cells from the harmful effects of H2O2, lipid peroxidation through tert-butyl hydroperoxide (t-BHP), and Aβ(1-42) (Lomartire and Gonçalves, 2023, Shrestha et al., 2022).

Laminarin

Laminarin is a polysaccharide extracted from brown algae and possesses several biological and pharmacological functions, including anti-inflammatory and antioxidant qualities. Treatment with laminarin, in ischemia–reperfusion (IR) injured gerbils, expressed antioxidant activity by increasing expressions of superoxide dismutase and showed anti-inflammatory activity by increasing anti-inflammatory cytokines (IL-4 and IL-13) in the CA1 pyramidal neurons (Park et al., 2020).

Fucoidan

Fucoidan, a sulfated polysaccharide found in brown algae like Fucus vesiculosus and Laminaria japonica, shows potent neuroprotective effects through its anti-inflammatory and anti-amyloidogenic activities (Anisha et al., 2022). Fucoidan inhibits the formation of Aβ peptides and reduces their aggregation, thus preventing the formation of amyloid plaques in AD models. It also modulates the MAPK/ERK pathway, enhances neuronal survival, and prevents neuroinflammation by inhibiting the NF-κB signaling pathway (Wang et al., 2018, Zayed et al., 2023).

Phloroglucinol

One of the phlorotannins that was isolated from E. cava, phenolglucinol (1,3,5-trihydroxybenzene), exhibits therapeutic effects in AD. In the HT-22 hippocampus cell line, phenolglucinol reduces the rise in ROS accumulation brought on by oligomeric Aβ42 therapy. Additionally, it was shown that phloroglucinol reduces the loss of dendritic spine density caused by Aβ42 treatment in vivo in rat primary hippocampal neuron cells. Consequently, it demonstrated that giving phloroglucinol to the hippocampus area reduced the cognitive dysfunction deficits in experimental mice (Lomartire & Gonçalves, 2023).

Cyanobacteria

Cyanobacteria, or blue-green algae, are photosynthetic microorganisms known for their bioactive compounds with neuroprotective potential. These microorganisms have been recognized for their ability to combat NDs such as AD, PD, ALS, etc (Mohd Sairazi and Sirajudeen, 2020, Rodrigues et al., 2024). The neuroprotective effects of Cyanobacteria are attributed to various secondary metabolites, including carotenoids, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), phycobiliproteins (notably phycocyanin), phenolic compounds, and specific cyanotoxins like anatoxins (Tiwari and Tiwari, 2020, Sun et al., 2023). Phycocyanin, a major compound, exhibits potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic activities (Fig. 9), helping protect neurons from oxidative stress and inflammation, which are the primary factors in neurodegeneration (Pentón-Rol et al., 2021, Trotta et al., 2022). These compounds act by modulating oxidative stress, reducing neuroinflammation, and preventing neuronal cell death, positioning cyanobacteria as promising candidates in neuroprotective activities, which are described below.

Challenges and limitations

NDs are a progressive disorder characterized by the loss of the structure and function of neurons (Alghazwi et al., 2018). Despite extensive research, therapeutic options remain limited, primarily offering symptomatic relief without addressing the underlying causes. Natural products, derived from plants, fungi, and marine sources, have emerged as promising candidates for neuroprotection due to their different bioactive substances, like polyphenols, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenes, and tannins. These substances possess potent anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-apoptotic properties in preclinical models, making them attractive therapeutic agents for NDs (Chang et al., 2019, Mohd Sairazi and Sirajudeen, 2020). However, the application of natural products in ND therapy faces significant challenges and limitations.

Limited bioavailability and stability of natural neuroprotective compounds for effective brain targeting

Natural products’ low bioavailability is one of the biggest obstacles to using them for neuroprotection. Many natural compounds, such as quercetin and RSV, display promising neuroprotective effects in preclinical models. However, their poor absorption, rapid metabolism, and low permeability across the BBB significantly limit their effectiveness in vivo. For example, quercetin, while potent in vitro, suffers from low systemic circulation due to quick metabolic degradation. Similarly, despite its antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties, RSV has poor bioavailability, reducing its therapeutic potential. These limitations necessitate strategies like nanoparticle or nanocarrier formulations to enhance their stability and brain-targeted delivery (Pavlović et al., 2023, Shaito et al., 2020).

Challenges in standardization and consistency of bioactive components in natural products for neurotherapeutics

Another significant limitation is the lack of standardization and consistency in the active components of natural products. Plant extracts often contain a mixture of bioactive compounds, and variations in growth conditions, harvesting, and extraction processes can lead to significant differences in their composition. This inconsistency makes it difficult to reproduce results across different studies or develop precise dosing regimens for therapeutic use. Unlike synthetic drugs, where the active compound is well-defined and controlled, natural products require rigorous standardization to ensure consistency in their effects (Nishad, 2022, Sasidharan et al., 2011).

Toxicological considerations of natural products in neuroprotection and safe dosage evaluation

Natural products have been used for a long time because they are frequently thought to be safe, but several compounds may exhibit toxicity at higher concentrations or with prolonged use. For instance, high doses of quercetin, a flavonoid used for its neuroprotective effects, can induce cytotoxicity and exacerbate oxidative stress. Berberine, another commonly studied natural compound, has shown hepatotoxicity and gastrointestinal side effects in high concentrations. This highlights the need for extensive toxicological studies to evaluate the safe dosage limits of these compounds in humans (Elblehi et al., 2022, Gaston et al., 2020, Koperska et al., 2022).

Complex mechanisms and multifactorial nature of neurodegeneration

NDs like AD and PD are multifactorial, involving oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and protein misfolding. While many natural products have demonstrated activity against one or more of these mechanisms, no single compound has shown comprehensive efficacy across all pathological processes. For instance, while polyphenols like curcumin exhibit potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties, their effects on protein aggregation, such as Aβ and τ-tangles in Alzheimer’s, are less conclusive. Therefore, combination therapies involving multiple natural products or combined with synthetic drugs may be needed to address the multifactorial nature of these diseases (Reddy et al., 2018).

Lack of clinical evidence and regulatory challenges

The regulatory approval of natural products as therapeutic substances for NDs also presents a challenge. Many natural products are classified as dietary supplements rather than drugs, which means they are not subject to the same rigorous testing and approval processes as pharmaceuticals. This regulatory gap complicates their development into mainstream therapies and poses challenges in terms of ensuring quality, efficacy, and safety.

In the case of curcumin, a bioactive compound in turmeric, it has shown promising neuroprotective properties but has faced regulatory hurdles due to issues with bioavailability and clinical efficacy. Curcumin has demonstrated antioxidant and anti-inflammatory potential, which is relevant to mitigating oxidative stress and neuroinflammation mechanisms central to NDs like AD and PD. However, despite positive preclinical results, curcumin has struggled to advance in clinical settings (According to the pharmacokinetic evaluation, curcumin did not affect the pharmacokinetics of irinotecan, NCT01859858). Clinical trials often reveal challenges like low bioavailability, rapid metabolism, and poor solubility, resulting in inconsistent therapeutic outcomes and dosage concerns, which hamper its approval for clinical use (Jacob et al., 2024, Wainwright et al., 2022).

Cost and sustainability

While natural products offer a promising therapeutic avenue, the large-scale production of certain plant-based compounds can be expensive and unsustainable. For example, harvesting bioactive compounds from rare or slow-growing plants, such as ginkgo or curcumin, can present ecological challenges and drive-up costs. These factors must be considered when developing natural products for widespread use in treating NDs (Iqbal et al., 2024, Lin et al., 2022).

Future perspective

Future research and perspectives on natural products or phytoconstituents used in the treatment of NDs focus on advancing both the therapeutic potential and clinical translation of these compounds.

Improving neurotherapeutic potential of natural products through nanotechnology

A significant obstacle to the use of natural products in ND treatment, including AD, PD, ALS, etc, is their low bioavailability and restricted BBB crossing capacity. Future studies should investigate the formation of sophisticated drug-delivery vehicles like nanoparticles, nanogels, and nanostructured lipid carriers. Bioactive substances such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and saponins can have their stability, targeting specificity, and bioavailability enhanced by these systems. By enabling continuous and regulated release, nanotechnology not only raises the concentration of these substances in brain tissues but also improves their therapeutic efficacy (Lim et al., 2024; Rahman et al., 2022a; Rahman et al., 2022b).

Advancing molecular insights into neuroprotection by natural compounds for targeted therapeutics

Future studies must focus on developing a more accurate knowledge of the molecular mechanisms via which natural compounds provide neuroprotection. Current evidence suggests that mechanisms include modulation of oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, neuroinflammation, and protein misfolding. New research should delve deeper into specific pathways, such as how these compounds influence microglial activation, regulate Nrf2 pathways, and modulate synaptic plasticity. For instance, understanding how compounds like ginsenoside Rg3 and berberine regulate the Nrf2/ARE pathway could open new therapeutic strategies (Calkins et al., 2009).

Advancing clinical translation of neuroprotective phytochemicals through rigorous research

Numerous natural substances exhibit promise in preclinical models, however, there is still a lack of translation of these results into human therapeutic applications. Future research should prioritize clinical trials assessing the safety, efficacy, and optimal dosage of these compounds. Research from prestigious sources points out the need for robust pharmacological standardization to ensure the reproducibility of results across diverse populations. Moreover, new researches are needed to address potential toxicity, drug interactions, and long-term effects of chronic consumption of phytochemicals like quercetin, RSV, and luteolin (Salehi et al., 2020, Srivastava et al., 2016).

Epigenetic modulation of ND by natural products for personalized therapy

Another exciting area for future research is investigating how natural products modulate gene expression and epigenetic factors involved in NDs. Substances like curcumin and RSV have been shown to influence the expression of genes related to oxidative stress and inflammation. Understanding how these compounds affect gene-environment interactions could lead to personalized therapeutic approaches for neurodegeneration (Daverey and Agrawal, 2018, Inchingolo et al., 2022, Islam et al., 2024).

Exploring marine-derived novel bioactive compounds as potential neuroprotective agents in NDs

Bioactive chemicals originating from marine sources have untapped medicinal potential. In preclinical models of NDs, substances produced from algae, such as agaropentaose and agaro-oligosaccharides, have demonstrated early promise. Future studies should explore novel and exotic sources of natural compounds, particularly from marine environments, to identify new neuroprotective agents. Additionally, studying fewer common plants from diverse ecosystems could uncover new bioactive phytoconstituents with unique mechanisms of action (Mohd Sairazi and Sirajudeen, 2020, Xie and Cheong, 2022, Ye et al., 2019).

Discussion and conclusion

Natural products have enormous potential as neuroprotective medicines against NDs because they can target various pathogenic pathways, including protein misfolding, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, and mitochondrial dysfunction. To slow the progression of NDs like AD, PD, and ALS, bioactive compounds obtained from plants, marine organisms, and other natural sources demonstrate various neuroprotective qualities, such as antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-apoptotic effects. These substances can significantly improve cognitive and motor abilities and fight neuronal degeneration by modifying important signalling pathways like PI3K/Akt, NF-κB, and Nrf2. Numerous natural compounds have been investigated as food supplements and nutraceuticals and have shown effectiveness against neurodegeneration. These include luteolin, hesperidin, RSV, genistein, and other modern ayurvedic formulations and botanicals (dietary supplements, foods, and medicines from various plants, algae, fungi, or lichens). However, their therapeutic potential is limited by a lack of rigorous scientific evidence for solubility, efficacy, and safety, as well as standardization and quality control issues that impede their clinical translation. Overall, integrating these natural agents into treatment strategies offers a promising strategy for addressing the complex multifactorial nature of NDs, potentially leading to more potent, holistic therapies that mitigate disease progression and increase patients’ quality of life.

On perturbations to various chemical structures and scaffolds (Table S1), it has been found that six major scaffolds have been responsible for the neuroprotective action of the natural products. The first one is a chalcopyrone or benzopyrone nucleus with polyphenolic hydroxyl groups; the second one is a cyclopentanoperhydrophenanthrene ring with epoxy group or polyphenolic substituent attachment; the third indole derivative with various substitutions; the fourth polyphenolic compound with fused pyran or dioxin; the fifth long chain fatty acid mimicking eicosapentaenoic acids (EPAs), the fifth curcuminoids scaffold with flexible double bonds and hydroxyl groups on the aromatic ring and quinoline ring with fusion where nitrogen is at the fused position. The indole derivatives exhibited a lot of variations in the scaffold, for example, the following chemical spaces have been seen: indane-2-one, indane-2-one fused with cyclopentane or pyrrole, or a pyrrolidine ring. It is interesting to note that indane fused with quinoline by direct fusion or joined by a bridge possessed potent activity against neuroinflammation or neurodegeneration. The EPA probably exerts anti-prostaglandin-like activity. The flavonoid derivatives that exhibited by the direct fusion of benzene with a six-membered pyran ring, where the hydroxyl and carbonyl groups are present as well. Thiazolidine derivatives produce a lot of variations in scaffolds, which could be some five-membered heterocyclic compounds containing one nitrogen atom or one sulfur atom within the ring structure, or oxygen and sulfur present as double bonds. Another ring system is the bicyclic compound composed of seven-membered rings fused to a five-membered tetrahydrofuran ring. The mechanism of action of some representative compounds has also been summarized in Fig. S1.

In this review, this study hypothesized that with respect to future drug discovery, the use of an indane ring with an electron donor such as an oxygen or nitrogen atom as part of a five-membered or six-membered ring could be a good option for future drug design in this field. The addition of an epoxide ring with variable stereochemistry could be another testing scaffold for newer drug design in this field. In another aspect, the chalcopyrone ring could be fused with such a kind of indane derivative to proliferate the resultant compound’s activity in multiple pathways. Also, the fusion of steroidal nuclei with such a kind of polyphenolics or indane ring may improve the bioavailability as well. Toxicological evaluations might be undertaken for such kinds of modifications; however, the use of such scaffolds judicially may aid in the development of new drugs.
