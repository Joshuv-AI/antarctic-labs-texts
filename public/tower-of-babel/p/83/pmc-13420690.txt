Percutaneous Vegetectomy for Severe Right-Sided Infective Endocarditis Complicated by Cardiac Arrest and Multiorgan Failure
Authors: MHD Osama Srour, Haris Ahmed, Youssef Al-Saghir, Ahmed Zaky
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRight-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) is a life-threatening condition predominantly affecting the tricuspid valve. For patients with prohibitive surgical risk, percutaneous vegetectomy (PV) is emerging as a viable alternative.Case SummaryA 38-year-old man with active intravenous drug use presented with cardiac arrest, profound shock, and multiorgan dysfunction. Echocardiography revealed a large tricuspid valve vegetation (2.0 × 3.1 cm) with severe regurgitation. Because of high surgical risk, he underwent salvage PV using the AngioVac system. The procedure achieved 90% debulking, leading to marked hemodynamic improvement and successful weaning of vasoactive agents. He was subsequently discharged to complete antimicrobial therapy pending reassessment for definitive surgery.DiscussionAlthough PV is increasingly used for RSIE, data regarding its use in extreme hemodynamic instability remain limited. Our case highlights its utility as a temporizing, life-saving intervention to reduce microbial burden and provide critical source control when definitive surgical debridement is not feasible.Take-Home MessagesPV is a viable rescue strategy for RSIE in high-risk patients. It provides critical source control to stabilize hemodynamics and bridge patients to recovery or definitive surgery.Key Words: AngioVac, intravenous drug use, percutaneous vegetectomy, right-sided infective endocarditis, shock, tricuspid valve endocarditis

History of Presentation

A 38-year-old man was brought to the emergency department after a pulseless electrical activity cardiac arrest. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after 10 minutes of cardiopulmonary resuscitation. On admission to the intensive care unit, the patient was intubated, sedated, and mechanically ventilated. Physical examination was notable for a grade III/VI holosystolic murmur at the left sternal border, elevated jugular venous pressure, and anasarca.

Past Medical History

The patient's medical history was significant for active intravenous drug use (IVDU).

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for a patient presenting with cardiac arrest, shock, and a new murmur in the setting of active IVDU includes infective endocarditis with acute valvular dysfunction, acute myocardial infarction, massive pulmonary embolism, and severe sepsis with stress-induced cardiomyopathy.

Investigations

Initial laboratory evaluation revealed severe anemia (4.1 g/dL), leukocytosis (26,200/μL), profound lactic acidosis (6.2 mm/L), and severe thrombocytopenia (32,000/μL). Urine drug screening was positive for cocaine and methamphetamine. A transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a markedly dilated left ventricle with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 4%, alongside a large vegetation on the tricuspid valve (TV) associated with severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR) (Figure 1, Video 1). Bedside hemodynamic monitoring indicated a cardiac output of 2.8 L/min, a cardiac index of 1.4 L/min/m2, and a systemic vascular resistance of 950 dyne/s/cm5. Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed a large, mobile vegetation attached to the anterior and septal leaflets of the TV, measuring 2.0 cm × 3.1 cm (Figure 2, Video 2). Blood cultures were positive for Streptococcus gordonii and Haemophilus influenzae.

Management

Intravenous ceftriaxone was initiated based on culture sensitivities, but bacteremia persisted despite appropriate antibiotic coverage. The patient's clinical status continued to deteriorate, requiring escalating doses of norepinephrine, vasopressin, and epinephrine. His clinical course was further complicated by acute kidney injury requiring hemodialysis, shock liver, and worsening thrombocytopenia (nadir 12,000/μL), necessitating multiple transfusions.

A multidisciplinary team involving cardiac surgery, anesthesiology, interventional cardiology, and heart failure services deemed the patient a prohibitively high surgical risk. The rationale against definitive surgical treatment was multifactorial: 1) profound hemodynamic compromise with multiorgan failure; 2) inability to safely tolerate cardiopulmonary bypass; and 3) high risk of early prosthetic valve reinfection given ongoing active IVDU.

Consequently, the decision was made to pursue percutaneous vegetectomy (PV) as a salvage source-control strategy. Bilateral common femoral veins (CFVs) were accessed under ultrasound guidance via 8-F sheaths. A 26-F DrySeal Flex (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc) sheath was then inserted via exchange in the right CFV through which the AngioVac (AngioDynamics, Inc) suction catheter was introduced, advanced through the inferior vena cava to the right atrium, and positioned over vegetations at the anterior and septal leaflets of the TV under combined TEE and fluoroscopic guidance. Aspirated blood was filtered through the extracorporeal circuit and returned via the left CFV.

Outcome and Follow-Up

The procedure achieved approximately 90% debulking of the vegetation (Figure 3), with residual subvalvular material noted in the chordal apparatus on postprocedural TEE (Figure 4, Video 3). The patient's hemodynamics markedly improved, allowing for successful weaning of all vasoactive agents, and repeat echocardiography demonstrated recovery of left ventricular ejection fraction to 25% to 30%. Bacteremia subsequently resolved with continued antimicrobial therapy. Definitive valve surgery was deferred pending completion of the antibiotic course and reassessment of surgical candidacy. The patient was discharged on hospital day 21 to complete a prolonged course of intravenous antibiotics with close outpatient follow-up. The patient was nonadherent with follow-up care and did not return for the scheduled surveillance echocardiogram.

Discussion

Right-sided infective endocarditis (RSIE) is a life-threatening condition driven largely by the rising prevalence of IVDU and indwelling catheters. Although surgical intervention remains the gold standard for large vegetations (>20 mm), persistent bacteremia for more than 7 days despite adequate antibiotics, recurrent embolic events, periannular abscess formation, or refractory right heart failure,1, 2, 3 traditional open-heart surgery carries prohibitive mortality in unstable patients. Consequently, PV has emerged as a crucial salvage or temporizing source-control strategy in patients who cannot tolerate traditional surgery.

The AngioVac is a minimally invasive percutaneous aspiration system used to debulk TV vegetations in patients with a high operative risk. The rationale is to reduce embolic and microbial burden to facilitate antibiotic efficacy, mitigate the risk of septic embolic events, and stabilize critically ill patients as a bridge to definitive surgery or recovery.4 Although systematic reviews have demonstrated the feasibility and clinical benefit of this system in high-risk cohorts,5,6 data regarding its use in extreme hemodynamic collapse remain limited.

This case illustrates the utility of PV using the AngioVac system as a rescue source-control strategy in a profoundly unstable patient with RSIE complicated by combined cardiogenic and septic shock. Our case is notable for the extreme degree of hemodynamic compromise, including postcardiac arrest state and multiorgan dysfunction, in which the procedure was successfully executed.

The decision to proceed with PV in our case was guided by a multidisciplinary team, a practice that has been shown to significantly reduce mortality in infective endocarditis.7 The safe execution of PV requires a highly coordinated expertise, including large-bore venous access, management of the extracorporeal aspiration circuit, intraoperative TEE guidance, device navigation, and contingency planning for complications such as intracardiac injury or embolization of vegetative material into the pulmonary vasculature.

Meticulous preprocedural planning is critical to establish anatomical feasibility and optimize device engagement. Preprocedural TEE is essential to delineate the relationship between the vegetation and the valvular apparatus, thereby mitigating the risk of structural injury or unsuccessful debulking. In this case, the vegetation's localization to the atrial surface of the anterior and septal leaflets facilitated favorable device engagement. Furthermore, the femoral vein was preferentially selected over the internal jugular vein for cannulation to avoid potential scarring and thrombosis associated with active IVDU.

The impact of PV on postprocedural echocardiographic parameters remains inconsistently reported in the literature, with some studies documenting unchanged or worsened TR severity.5,6 In our patient, TR severity remained unchanged after the procedure. However, the substantial reduction in vegetation burden achieved by PV provided critical source control, facilitating the clearance of bacteremia and allowing the patient to survive the acute phase of his illness.

Finally, it is important to recognize that PV achieves substantial debulking rather than the complete excision of infected valvular tissue, which remains the cornerstone of definitive operative management. Therefore, patients who survive the acute phase and achieve hemodynamic stability should be re-evaluated for definitive surgical intervention when clinically appropriate.

Conclusions

PV using the AngioVac system serves as a critical salvage and temporizing intervention for patients with severe RSIE who present with extreme hemodynamic collapse and are deemed prohibitively high risk for traditional open-heart surgery. Although it does not replace the need for definitive surgical excision of infected valvular tissue, this minimally invasive approach offers a vital bridge to clinical stability and potential future surgical candidacy. Multidisciplinary team coordination and meticulous preprocedural planning remain essential to optimize patient selection and procedural success in these highly complex clinical scenarios.
