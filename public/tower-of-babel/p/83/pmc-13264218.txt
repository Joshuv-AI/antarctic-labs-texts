Validation of IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS Computed Tomography–Based Contouring Recommendations Using Prebrachytherapy Magnetic Resonance Imaging in Patients With Cervical Cancer Treated With Definitive Radiation Therapy
Authors: Abhishek Shinghal, Soham Sanyal, Satyajit Pradhan, Ankita Rungta Kapoor, Vinay Saini, Samapika Bhaumik, Avradeep Datta, Tanvee Singla, Prashanth Giridhar, Lincoln Pujari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeConsensus guidelines from IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS (Indian Brachytherapy Society- Groupe Européen de Curiethérapie - European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology- American Brachytherapy Society) for computed tomography (CT)–based contouring have recently introduced possibilities of CT-based adaptive brachytherapy (BT) for cervical cancer. The present study attempts to validate the guidelines by using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at diagnosis and in a pre-BT environment.Methods and MaterialsThe study involved patients with stages IIB to IVA carcinoma of the cervix who had pretreatment MRI and were treated with definitive chemoradiation and hybrid-interstitial BT from April 1, 2022, to October 1, 2023. The target volumes—high-risk clinical target volume (HRCTV), intermediate-risk clinical target volume (IRCTV), and organs-at-risk that included bladder, rectum, and sigmoid—were prospectively delineated on BT planning CT using BT planning MRI for guidance, as per IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS recommendations by a radiation oncologist. The dimensions, volumes, doses, and Dice similarity coefficient on CT were compared with those on MRI images.ResultsTwo hundred eight scans from 104 sets of 52 patients of CT and magnetic resonance scans were the basis for this study. With respect to dimensions, CT overestimated HRCTV maximum width, IRCTV width at point A, and maximum thickness of HRCTV and IRCTV compared with MRI (P values, <.001, <.001, .049, and .007, respectively). In analysis of organs-at-risk volumes, CT overestimated sigmoid and rectum volumes, with median difference of paired values of 7.2 (Interquartile range [IQR], 3.1-13.6) and 7.8 (IQR, 3.6-12.8) and P values of <.001 and .003, respectively. Bladder 2cc and sigmoid 2cc doses were underestimated with CT images, with median difference of paired values of 0.5 (IQR, 0.3-0.9) and 0.5 (IQR, 0.3-0.8) and P values of <.001 and .003, respectively. There was an insignificant difference in HRCTV and IRCTV doses between CT and MR images. The median Dice similarity coefficients of HRCTV, IRCTV, bladder, rectum, and sigmoid were 0.72, 0.78, 0.76, 0.68, and 0.46, respectively.ConclusionsDespite the dimensional differences, there was no dosimetric difference in HRCTV and IRCTV. CT-based contouring using IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS recommendations, with MRI at diagnosis and BT, similar to the MRI at diagnosis + pre-BT MRI + CT at brachytherapy environment, is safe, convenient, and addresses logistic issues in institutions, especially in low- and middle-income countries.

Introduction

Brachytherapy (BT), an integral component of the treatment of cervical cancer, confers superior cause-specific survival and overall survival (OS) compared to that achieved with highly conformal and advanced teletherapy techniques like intensity modulated radiation therapy, volumetric modulated arc therapy, and stereotactic body radiation therapy.1 BT in cervical cancer has seen remarkable evolution in treatment techniques while transitioning from the low-dose-rate to the high-dose-rate era. Challenges of dose visualization of target and organs-at-risk (OARs) in conventional two-dimensional BT were successfully overcome using cross-sectional imaging with three-dimensional BT. There is convincing established evidence that, along with significantly lesser toxicity, image-guided three-dimensional BT offers better progression-free survival, local control, and OS compared with 2D two-dimensional BT.2,3

The GEC-ESTRO (Groupe Européen de Curiethérapie - European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology) group determined that incorporating magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in gynecological BT planning provided excellent soft-tissue definitions that aid in precise target coverage assessment and BT applicator selection.4 This led to further refinement and recommendations in the International Commission on Radiation Units and Measurements (ICRU)-89 report for use worldwide.5 The incorporation of image-guided adaptive brachytherapy (IGABT) in the treatment of cervical cancer has resulted in significant improvements in disease outcomes. RetroEMBRACE6 (Retrospective IntErnational MRI-guided BRAchytherapy in CErvical Cancer) and EMBRACE-I (IntErnational study on MRI-based BRAchytherapy in CErvical cancer) studies7 demonstrated superior 3-year and 5-year local control and pelvic control rates, with the incorporation of magnetic resonance (MR)-based IGABT in the treatment of cervical cancer. This resulted in a 10% increase in OS compared with data from historical controls, emphasizing the role of IGABT in improving treatment outcomes.

Practical implementation on a global platform of MR-based IGABT, the current gold-standard in the treatment of cervical cancers, poses several limitations. Low- and middle-income countries, with high incidence of cervical cancer, suffer from severe resource constraints with regard to manpower and MRI machines. High patient loads and inadequate professional expertise in several places limit their use even where they are available. Furthermore, patient-related factors such as claustrophobia and MR-incompatible body implants further impede its usage. In a 2019 online survey that included 116 respondents from different centers, Chatterjee et al8 found that only 9% of centers regularly incorporated MRI-based BT planning. Computed tomography (CT)–based planning was reported by 65% of respondents, and in 66% of centers, point A–based planning was the most prevalent method.8

There has been a renewed interest in CT imaging for BT planning in the treatment of cervical cancer. CT scan is a widely available imaging modality with advantage of quicker acquisition time and increased availability as CT simulators in dedicated radiation therapy (RT) setups. However, poorer soft-tissue resolution resulting in inaccuracy in determining parametrial disease extent, uterine body involvement, and artifacts from metallic implants, if any, is the disadvantage. Incorporation of CT scan in place of MRI, as a comparable modality in IGABT, has been attempted. Additional information, in the form of clinical diagrams with measurements, with near maximum distance determination and transrectal ultrasound findings as an aid to target volume delineation to mimic MRI at the BT environment, have been successfully used for CT-based IGABT.9,10 Furthermore, a few studies have explored the feasibility of using pre-BT MRI along with CT at BT, or MRI at first and CT at subsequent BT applications.

Mahantshetty et al11 published the consensus IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS (Indian Brachytherapy Society- Groupe Européen de Curiethérapie - European Society for Radiotherapy and Oncology- American Brachytherapy Society) practice guidelines, wherein CT-based IGABT was discussed with regard to 4 different patterns of tumor regression after external beam RT and the availability of different imaging modalities at various time points of treatment. The minimum requirements were robust clinical examination with pictorial documentation, MRI or CT imaging at the time of diagnosis, and CT imaging with the applicator in situ. The role of transabdominal and transrectal ultrasonography has also been discussed in detail, and they have been recommended, whenever available, to appropriately delineate extensions of clinical target volume and aid CT-based IGABT. However, these consensus guidelines warrant a scientific validation for use of the approach in larger population groups. In the present study, an attempt has been made to validate the principles outlined in IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS consensus guidelines and compare CT- and MR-based IGABT parameters in a clinical setting.

Methods and Materials

Fifty-two patients with intact uterus, newly diagnosed with stages IIB to IVA (using International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics [FIGO] 2018) carcinoma of the cervix on pretreatment MRI, were screened for the study conducted from April 1, 2022, to October 1, 2023. Patients were treated with curative-intent definitive RT along with concurrent chemotherapy, followed by MR-based IGABT using Cobalt-60 high-dose-rate BT. An institutional ethics committee approval was obtained (study no. 11000703), along with a consent waiver, as the study involved less-than-minimal risk to the participants. The primary endpoint was the assessment of dosimetric differences in dose received by 90% of high-risk clinical target volume (D90 HRCTV) between CT- and MR-based contouring at the time of BT. The secondary endpoints included: (1) dosimetric differences in dose received by 98% of intermediate-risk clinical target volume (D98 IRCTV), bladder dose of 2 cc volume (B2cc), rectum dose of 2 cc volume (R2cc), and sigmoid dose of 2 cc volume (S2cc); (2) dimensional and volumetric differences in HRCTV, IRCTV, and volumes of bladder, rectum, and sigmoid; and (3) Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of HRCTV, IRCTV, bladder, rectum, and sigmoid in CT- and MR-based contouring at BT, respectively.

Patients received external beam radiation therapy to a dose of 45 Gy in 25 fractions at 1.8 Gy per fraction, 5 days a week, along with weekly concurrent cisplatin at 40 mg/m2. Further, all patients received 2 hybrid Intracavitary + Interstitial (IC + IS) BT applications delivering 28 Gy in 4 fractions, ie, 7 Gy per fraction in 2 applications, with 2 fractions delivered at least 12 hours apart per application. The procedure was performed as per available standard guidelines.12 All patients underwent BT planning using T2-weighted para-axial, parasagittal, and paracoronal 3-mm MRI images acquired using “Philips Ignitia 3.0” 3-Tesla machine. Further, patients were taken up for axial 2.5 mm to 3 mm BT planning CT cuts on a Siemens Somatom Confidence, 20-slice CT Scanner. The image cuts were taken from the level of the upper border of the L3 vertebra to the mid-thigh region and were then imported to the Elekta Oncentra Treatment Planning System (TPS), version 4.6.0.

Target volumes (HRCTV and IRCTV) and OARs (bladder, rectum, and sigmoid) were delineated on MRI using standard guidelines.5 The applicator reconstruction was performed using the applicator library; the applicator model was overlaid on the MRI planning images and was manually aligned. Reconstruction of each ovoid was automatically performed with the source path placed either at the channel centerline or along a measured source path provided by the manufacturer. Interstitial tube reconstruction was performed manually on MRI images. BT planning CT images were used as an aid in delineation and cross verification of the length and the exact tip of inserted interstitial tubes required for planning. Using the tandem and ovoid, standard loading of source dwell positions was done with normalization at point A. The interstitial needles encompassing the HRCTV area were loaded with 10% weightage of standard loading time. Plans were evaluated and optimized using manual and/or graphical optimization to achieve desired doses of target volumes and OARs, as per standard guidelines.13 Finalized plans were exported to BT treatment console for execution of treatment.

Target volume and OARs delineation were prospectively performed on BT planning CT using IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines by a single radiation oncologist.11 Delineation of HRCTV and IRCTV at BT was aided by MRI at diagnosis and BT planning MRI (which was simulated as pre-BT MRI [PreBTMR] and was used for assessing the lateral extent of disease at BT. The BT planning CT was registered with BT planning MRI using an applicator point-based registration technique on Oncentra TPS version 4.6.0 (Fig. 1A-C). Dosimetric and volumetric data of structures delineated on CT and MRI were undertaken. Dimension analysis of HRCTV and IRCTV was performed using the measuring tool in Oncentra TPS version 4.6.0. The maximum height was obtained in the sagittal sections of BT planning CT and MRI. The maximum width, width at point A, and maximum thickness were taken in the axial sections of the respective BT planning CT and BT planning MRI. Normality of data was tested using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which was subsequently analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. MRI-based contours were then copied to the registered CT images and exported to Varian Velocity version 4.1 for DSC analysis, comparing CT and MRI for HRCTV, IRCTV, bladder, rectum, and sigmoid, respectively. Where structures A and B were considered, DSC was calculated as: DSC = 2(A∩B)/(A+B). Discussion

The present study compared the gold-standard MRI at diagnosis + MRI at brachytherapy approach (MRDG-MRBT) with MRI at diagnosis + pre-BT MRI and CT at brachytherapy (MRDG-Pre-BTMR and CTBT) environment for hybrid-interstitial applications using IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines. There was no significant difference in D90HRCTV and D98IRCTV doses and the respective volumes in the CT and MRI environments. However, lower doses of B2cc and S2cc, but higher rectum and sigmoid volumes, were reported on CT images. While the width (maximum and at point A) and the maximum thickness of HRCTV and IRCTV were significantly overestimated on CT, no difference was seen in maximum height between CT and MRI images.

The findings of our study align with the existing literature and show overestimation of the width of HRCTV and IRCTV on CT compared with MRI.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 This could be attributed to errors caused in an attempt to manually measure and extrapolate pre-BT MRI findings over CT. Hence, estimating HRCTV width throughout the long axis of the uterus becomes challenging, especially in patients with less mesorectal fat, which leads to looping of adjacent normal bowel around the target (Fig. 3A). In this study, a similar reason may also explain the significant overestimation of the thickness of HRCTV and IRCTV on CT compared with MRI. Hence, it can be concluded that CT-based delineation of target volumes (HRCTV and IRCTV) should be performed using registered pre-BT MRI in TPS for improving the accuracy of width and thickness. Although some studies have shown uncertainty in height estimation on CT,15,17 especially in cases of uterine body involvement (Fig. 3B), there was no difference in height estimation in the present study. This suggests that the strategy of estimating maximum height using sagittal section helps in achieving near correct estimates of HRCTV and IRCTV height in accordance with the IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines.Figure 3Poor visualization for target delineation using computed tomography compared with magnetic resonance imaging. The differences in the estimated width and thickness of HRCTV and IRCTV did not translate into differences in volumes, a finding similar to that seen in the existing literature.14,17, 18, 19 There was a reasonably good concordance of HRCTV and IRCTV between CT and MRI images, with median DSC of 0.72 and 0.78, comparable with figures in the existing literature.20 With respect to the OARs, there was no significant difference in bladder volumes, and a good concordance was seen between CT and MRI with a DSC of 0.76. However, studies have shown differences with rectal volumes overestimated on CT, a finding similar to that seen in this study. Moreover, sigmoid volumes were also higher in our study, especially in cases with indistinct planes between the uterus and sigmoid (Fig. 3A). Both rectum and sigmoid, compared with other structures, showed poor concordance between CT and MRI, with a DSC of 0.68 and 0.46 (Fig. 4). The poor concordance for sigmoid between CT and MRI points to the shortcomings of IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines with respect to more specific OARs contouring. This could result in heterogeneity and larger variability in the delineation of the sigmoid, more evident in the cranial direction, possibly due to bowel movements.Figure 4Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) of structures on computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging.Figure 4 dummy alt text

Overestimation of width and thickness on CT did not translate into any significant dosimetric differences in D90HRCTV and D98IRCTV. However, there are contradictory observations in the literature, with few studies showing no dosimetry differences17,21, 22, 23, 24 and others showing D90HRCTV underestimation on CT.15,18,19,25 An explanation for this could be the lack of a particular set of guidelines for target delineation in the earlier studies. The present study is unique in assessing differences in D98IRCTV, an aspect that was not evaluated in the earlier studies. In our study, even though the volumes of bladder did not differ significantly between MRI and CT, a lower D2cc was reported on CT-based planning. In their meta-analysis, Wang et al26 observed lower bladder D2cc in MR-based planning. Conversely, uncertainty in contouring the posterior bladder wall on CT, an entity that is better appreciated on MRI, could have resulted in underestimation of bladder D2cc dose, in this study. Although there were differences in rectum volumes, the D2cc was not significantly different and could be attributed to rapid dose falloff resulting from adequate vaginal packing, variable time gap between acquisition of CT and MR images, and adherence to specified bowel protocols. On the other hand, sigmoid doses were underestimated on CT owing to their higher volumes. Incorporating sigmoid dose points in CT-based planning may reduce these uncertainties in high-dose areas in the sigmoid and may further mitigate the late side effects.27

In the present study, to reduce interobserver variability, the contouring was performed by a single radiation oncologist. However, the study was not blinded. Moreover, intraobserver variability, simulating BT planning MRI as PreBTMR, and systematic error cannot be ruled out and could be the limitations of the study. This study has used a hybrid-interstitial, MR-compatible applicator in all BT applications. Hence, the findings and conclusions of the study may not be generalized to situations where stainless steel intracavitary applicators, having additional challenges of metal artifacts, are used.

The present study is one of the largest series that compared CT- and MR-based contouring using hybrid-interstitial applications in all patients for validating IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines in MRDG-PreBTMR and CTBT environment. It is concluded that this environment can be used safely with CT-based planning, preferably with image-based registration of pretreatment MRI on TPS. As all patients were treated with MR-based planning, clinical outcomes and toxicity data comparing CT-based with MR-based planning could not be done in this study. Clinical studies in the future, in the form of randomized controlled trials, are encouraged to further validate these guidelines based on clinical outcomes. Further, there lies a scope of exploring the other environments for CT-based contouring as per IBS-GEC ESTRO-ABS guidelines: CTDG-CTBT, MRDG-CTBT, CTDG-PreBT MR and CTBT, which the CT EMBRACE Registration Study might address in the near future.
