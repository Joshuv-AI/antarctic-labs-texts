The earliest amber from the Middle Devonian of China
Authors: Cihang Luo, Huiyuan Yang, Honghe Xu, Zixiao Yang, Shengyu Wang, Michael S. Engel, Bingcai Liu, Edmund A. Jarzembowski, Brendon E. Boudinot, Xingqian Cui, Haichun Zhang, Bo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Resin production is a major innovation in land plants, signaling the evolution of specialized secretory systems, complex terpenoid biosynthesis, and defensive and wound-sealing functions. Yet its origin remains poorly constrained because fossilized resin (amber) is exceedingly rare in Paleozoic deposits, with the earliest widely accepted amber record from the Late Carboniferous. Here we report the earliest known amber from the Middle Devonian of China (~385 million years old), extending the geological record of amber by ~65 million years. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and gas chromatography–triple quadrupole mass spectrometry reveal chemical signatures comparable to conifer-type amber. These data indicate that some Middle Devonian vascular plants—most plausibly progymnosperms or arborescent lycopsids—had already evolved biosynthetic capacity consistent with terpene synthase. The early emergence of resin production may have promoted the ecological success of Devonian-Carboniferous terrestrial floras by enhancing protection and wound sealing.

INTRODUCTION

Amber, specifically fossilized resin, is thought to be one of the ancient exudates of seed plants (1, 2). Seed plants usually secrete resin through specialized secretory tissues of plant surfaces (e.g., glandular trichomes) or, on a larger scale, via their internal secretory systems (e.g., parenchyma cells and resin canals) associated with the phloem (bark) and xylem (wood) (1, 3). These resins, which are complex mixtures of primarily terpenoid and/or phenolic compounds (1), help plants recover from various biotic and abiotic injuries, including those from pests, microbial pathogens, and wildfires. Secreted resin hardens and transforms into amber during diagenetic and catagenetic processes, under elevated temperatures and pressures (1, 4). The viscous resin sometimes traps organisms, which are subsequently preserved as fossil inclusions within amber, providing critical information on the evolution of terrestrial ecosystems (5). Furthermore, the amber itself, as an organic aggregate, preserves abundant biomarker molecules over geological timescales due to its high structural recalcitrance (6, 7), holding clues about the source plants and the paleoenvironment during deposition (8, 9).

Although amber is a unique repository of environmental, diagenetic, and biological data (6), its occurrence is not continuous through Earth history (7, 10). Before the Permian, there are only two definite records of amber—both from the Carboniferous—with one from the Late Carboniferous of the United States [~320 million years ago (Ma)], which may have been produced by cordaitalean gymnosperms (Cordaitales), an extinct seed plant lineage closely related to conifers (11), and another from the latest Carboniferous of Canada (~300 Ma), probably produced by pteridosperms (seed ferns) (12). More recently, Song et al. (13) reported diterpenoid-rich organic films associated with Devonian plant cuticles and interpreted them as externally secreted surface resins, possibly produced via glandular trichomes. Their study suggests that resin production may already have been present in Devonian plants. However, discrete amber pieces that can be directly isolated and chemically characterized have not previously been documented from pre-Carboniferous deposits. As a result, the timing of the first confirmed appearance of discrete, chemically verified amber and the plant groups in which it first evolved have remained uncertain. Consequently, our understanding of the origin and early evolution of this critical biological innovation has remained unclear.

In this study, we report the earliest known and chemically verified amber from the Middle Devonian Hujiersite Formation (Givetian; ~385 Ma) of Xinjiang, China. Using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy and gas chromatography–triple quadrupole mass spectrometry (GC-MS/MS), we demonstrate that the chemical components of Hujiersite amber are comparable with those of previously confirmed gymnospermous amber. This finding provides insights into the evolution of plant ecology and its ecological role in early terrestrial ecosystems.

RESULTS

Geological setting and botanical composition

The amber specimens were discovered within the cutinitic coal seam of the Hujiersite Formation, located in the northwestern region of Hoxtolgay, Xinjiang, northwest China (Fig. 1, A and B, and Supplementary Text). The Hujiersite Formation is predominantly composed of tuffaceous breccia, siltstone, argillaceous siltstone, and mudstone, interbedded with thin coal seams (Fig. 1, C to E). The sample area has yielded abundant plant fossils (the Hujiersite Flora; see Supplementary Text), including lycopsids, zosterophylls, iridopteridaleans (fig. S1), and progymnosperms (fig. S2) (14). The age of the amber layer is Middle Devonian (~385 Ma; see Supplementary Text) (15).

Morphology and chemistry of Hujiersite amber

The Hujiersite amber is sparsely embedded in coal and typically forms small clusters (Fig. 2, A to C). These amber pieces are mostly microscopic in size, ranging from 0.1 to 0.5 mm (Fig. 2D), although some can exceed 0.5 mm (Fig. 2, E and F) with the largest specimens measuring up to 1.5 mm (Fig. 2, A and B). This small size is consistent with other known Paleozoic amber, which is generally smaller (less than 5 mm across) compared to Cretaceous and Cenozoic amber (11, 12, 16, 17). In terms of morphology, Hujiersite amber exhibits irregular shapes, and its light transmission ranges from translucent to opaque (Fig. 2). The colors range from light yellow to dark brown (Fig. 2D). One specimen contains abundant bubbles (Fig. 2G). Under ultraviolet light (wavelength, 365 nm), Hujiersite amber exhibits a prominent blue fluorescence, which contrasts with the dark color of the surrounding coal (Fig. 2B).

The FTIR spectra of Hujiersite amber strongly resemble those of both extant and fossil resins (Fig. 3). Because all spectra are normalized, differences in peak heights are interpreted qualitatively. Key similarities include strong absorption in the ranges of 3050 to 2750 cm−1 (aliphatic single C─H bonds) and 1800 to 1600 cm−1 (C═O double bonds in carboxyl groups and O─H bonds), as well as prominent peaks at ~1455 and ~1385 cm−1 (C─H bonds of methyl and methylene groups) (18, 19). However, there are subtle differences in the IR spectra between Hujiersite amber and amber/resin samples from different plants (Fig. 3). Compared to angiospermous amber, Hujiersite amber differs from the IR characteristics of fabalean ambers (e.g., Oise amber) (19) by having a different amplitude between peaks at ~1455 and ~1385 cm−1 and by lacking a prominent peak at 887 cm−1. The Hujiersite amber also lacks the absorption triplets between 1360 and 1400 cm−1 and a peak at 1050 cm−1, which are characteristic of dipterocarpaceous ambers (e.g., Zhangpu amber) (19). In contrast, Hujiersite amber exhibits IR characteristics similar to those of both pinaceous (Pinaceae) and cupressaceous (Cupressaceae and Araucariaceae) resins (18). The Hujiersite amber lacks the absorption peak at 2848 cm−1, which is consistent with the IR spectra of pinaceous resin, where this peak is either weakly developed or absent (18, 19). In addition, Hujiersite amber has weaker absorption at ~1385 cm−1 compared to that at ~1455 cm−1, which is typical of cupressaceous resin (18, 19). Together, these results suggest that Hujiersite amber is distinct from the angiosperm-derived resin/amber and is broadly consistent with a gymnosperm/conifer-type resin source, although FTIR alone does not warrant a firm family-level assignment (Fig. 3).

The total ion chromatogram from the GC-MS/MS analysis of Hujiersite amber is primarily characterized by sesquiterpenoids, diterpenoids, and naphthalenic acids (Fig. 4, figs. S3 to S5, and Supplementary Text). Notably, abundant abietane-type diterpenoids, such as trisnorabieta-8,11,13-trienes, dehydroabietane, and simonellite are commonly diagnostic indicators of coniferous resin (20, 21). In addition, 16,17,19-trisnorabieta-8,11,13-trienes, diagenetic products of pimarane diterpenoids (8, 22), are also commonly found in conifers (20, 21). Dehydroabietane, a representative component of both pinaceous and cupressaceous resins (22, 23), is present in Hujiersite amber. However, the absence of phenolic diterpenoids suggests that its botanical origin is less closely related to the Cupressaceae (8, 20, 24). Naphthalene carboxylic acids (1,4a,6-trimethyl-1,2,3,4,4a,7,8,8a-octahydronaphthalene-1-carboxylic acid, trimethylsilyl [TMS] derivative and 1,4a,5,6-tetramethyl-1,2,3,4,4a,7,8,8a-octahydronaphthalene-1-carboxylic acid, TMS derivative), which are found in Manila copal (amber precursor from Araucariaceae: Agathis dammara) (25, 26), are highly abundant in Hujiersite amber. Furthermore, 16,17-bisnordehydroabietic acid, potentially derived from isoprimaranes, is linked to a pinaceous origin (22). Overall, the co-occurrence of cadalene-type sesquiterpenoids and abietane-type diterpenoids, coupled with the absence of bicadinanes and triterpenoids in Hujiersite amber, rules out an angiospermous origin and supports a gymnospermous attribution, especially to conifers (2, 6, 21). The presence of abundant naphthalene carboxylic acids and dehydroabietane, potential derivatives of pimarane and isoprimarane, alongside the absence of phenolic diterpenoids, suggests that the chemical components of Hujiersite amber are most similar to resins from Pinaceae and Araucariaceae (20, 24).

DISCUSSION

Our chemically verified Hujiersite amber represents the earliest confirmed amber record thus far. Previous studies have only sporadically mentioned putative fossil resin from Devonian coals (27–29), typically referred to as “resinite” because of its microscopic size and occurrence within coal (30). These putative ambers were commonly recognized on the basis of optical properties (e.g., reflected light fluorescence), but sample sizes have generally been too limited for robust chemical characterization. Where chemical analyses have been conducted on comparable Carboniferous resinous occurrences (e.g., resin rodlets/resinite), the compositions can differ markedly from typical amber chemistries (30, 31), despite their occurrence within plant tissues (e.g., parenchyma and resin canals or other secretory tissues) and superficial optical similarity (1). Thus, comprehensive chemical analyses are required to evaluate whether the seemingly resinous Devonian materials represent true fossil resin/amber or other plant-derived exudates.

Recent work by Song et al. (13) interpreted brown fluorescent organic films associated with Devonian plant cuticles as surface resins rich in tetracyclic diterpenoid compounds. Their observations, including cuticle-associated films and probable glandular trichomes, imply that some Devonian plants may already have secreted resin externally onto plant surfaces. However, these materials differ substantially from the Hujiersite amber reported here. Song et al. (13) focused on cuticle-associated surface films, whereas the Hujiersite amber occurs as discrete amber pieces that can be directly isolated from the coal. Moreover, although tetracyclic diterpanes are consistent with a resinous interpretation, they are not uniquely diagnostic of fossil resin/amber on their own, because comparable compounds in Devonian deposits have long been regarded as potentially source ambiguous, with possible contributions from early vascular plants and/or microbial producers (32, 33). By contrast, the Hujiersite amber reported here occurs as discrete, directly observable amber pieces and is independently analyzed and supported by our chemical characterization—a complex mixture of primarily terpenoid and/or phenolic compounds (1), providing unambiguous evidence for fossil resin. This finding extends the now known temporal range of amber by ~65 Ma (11), pushing back the earliest confirmed record from the Late Carboniferous to the late Middle Devonian.

Our Hujiersite amber shares comparable chemical components with coniferous ambers, providing insights into the early evolution of terpenoid resin biosynthesis in vascular plants. Terpene synthases (TPSs) are important enzymes responsible for producing numerous terpenoids, and these terpenoids can regulate development and mediate ecological interactions (34, 35). The innovation of TPS-mediated biosynthesis has played an important role in the evolution of land plants (35, 36). Fossils usually provide limited evidence for the origin and early evolution of TPS genes (34, 36), as the related phenotypic secretory structures are rarely discovered (8, 36). Molecular analyses suggest that the TPS genes encoding TPSs originated in nonseed plants at around 450 Ma (Late Ordovician) and subsequently diversified in gymnosperms and angiosperms (34, 35, 37). Notably, gymnosperms have specific TPSs (i.e., the TPS-d subfamily) that can synthesize a vast array of terpenoid compounds involved with secondary metabolism including resin (35, 37). However, the upstream supply of isoprenoid precursors for terpenoid biosynthesis is broadly conserved across land plants, such that different plant groups may share similar early steps of terpenoid production (38, 39). Our analyses show that chemical components of Hujiersite amber are comparable to those of coniferous amber, which suggests that some Middle Devonian vascular plants had already evolved (or recruited) TPS-mediated pathways that can produce complex terpenoid resins. This finding provides a deep-time benchmark for the emergence of terpenoid resin chemistry (34, 37).

Given its Middle Devonian age, the Hujiersite amber most plausibly derives from nonseed plants (1, 6, 7). Previously, all known fossil resin has been derived from seed plants (spermatophytes) (1, 7), a monophyletic lineage that includes pteridosperms (seed ferns), gymnosperms, and angiosperms. Seed plants first emerged and radiated extensively in the Late Devonian (Famennian) (40), later than the age of Middle Devonian Hujiersite amber. Therefore, it is unlikely that spermatophytes are the source plants although the chemical properties of Hujiersite amber and gymnospermous resin are similar. If this inference is correct, Hujiersite amber would represent the earliest known fossil resin produced by nonseed plants.

Because no diagnostic secretory anatomy is preserved, the source plant of the Hujiersite amber cannot presently be identified with confidence. In the Hujiersite Flora, potential source plants include progymnosperms and tree-like lycopsids known from the formation (14, 41, 42). Progymnosperms are plausible because aneurophyte-type progymnosperms (Aneurophyton and Tetraxylopteris) have been reported from contemporaneous sediments adjacent to the amber-bearing coal seam (41, 42); however, there is currently no direct anatomical evidence linking resin production to these taxa. Tree-like lycopsids such as Hoxtolgaya also cannot be excluded, because they are prominent components of the Hujiersite Flora and some fossil arborescent lycopsids have been reported to preserve putative secretory tissues in the periderm (43). Regardless of the precise producer, our results suggest that resin production had already appeared in some Middle Devonian vascular plants.

Resin assists extant plants in recovering from abiotic physical damage and defending against pathogen or arthropod attack (44). In the Devonian, however, the best-supported selective pressures for the early evolution of resin are abiotic damage and pathogen attack. Wildfires, a major cause of abiotic physical damage to plants, have been widespread and recurrent since the latest Silurian (45). In addition, several types of plant-parasitic fungi have been discovered in the Lower Devonian Rhynie Chert (46), and some of the early Late Devonian progymnosperms were already able to defend themselves against pathogen infection by forming tyloses (47). By contrast, evidence for arthropod herbivory on Devonian vascular plants remains limited (48). More extensive exploitation of roots, leaves, wood, and seeds and clearer evidence for arthropod boring in vascular plant tissues became established later, mainly from the Carboniferous onward (49), although sparse Late Devonian evidence for possible arthropod borings in large vascular plant debris has been reported (50). Accordingly, the origin of resin in early vascular plants may have been more closely linked to wound sealing and defense against fire-related damage and pathogen attack than to arthropod herbivory. In this context, resin production would likely have enhanced the resilience of early vascular plants by facilitating self-sealing after physical injury and by limiting fungal or other pathogen invasion, thereby conferring an important ecological advantage. Meanwhile, the apparent rarity of Devonian-Carboniferous amber may partly reflect underrecognition, because early resinous occurrences were likely minute and highly localized. Along with the bifacial cambium, leaves, deep rooting systems, and the rise of arborescence, the ability to produce resin may have represented an additional adaptive innovation in some Devonian vascular plants.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Amber preparation

We collected approximately 10 kg of coal from the coal seam of the ancient petroleum reservoirs (APR) section (Global Positioning System: 46°39′22.6″N, 86°1′28.7″E) of the Upper Member of the Hujiersite Formation, Hoxtolgay, West Junggar, Xinjiang, northwestern China. Amber was first recognized using an ultraviolet flashlight (365 nm), where it was found to be concentrated in exceptionally small portions of the coal and almost absent in other portions. We then carefully handpicked these amber pieces with stainless steel needles from the bedding surfaces of the coal under a Zeiss Stereo Discovery V16 microscope at the Nanjing Institute of Geology and Palaeontology, Chinese Academy of Sciences (NIGPAS). A total of 241 amber pieces were extracted from the coal matrix, and most of the clean amber without coal attached was submitted for GC-MS/MS analysis.

Optical microscopy and figure preparation

The photographs were taken with a Zeiss Stereo Discovery V16 microscope system at NIGPAS. Incident and transmitted light were used simultaneously in most cases. Photomicrographic composites of 5 to 150 individual focal planes were digitally stacked using the Helicon Focus 6.7.1 software (https://heliconsoft.com/heliconsoft-products/helicon-focus/) to enhance the illustration of three-dimensional structures. Line drawings were prepared using CorelDRAW 2022 graphic software. Figures were produced using Adobe Photoshop 25.11.0 and CorelDRAW 2022 graphic software.

FTIR spectroscopy

Attenuated total reflection (ATR) infrared spectra of the amber samples (Fig. 3) were collected using the Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 with an integrated ZnSe ATR module at the School of Earth Sciences and Engineering, Nanjing University, China. The collection parameters were set as follows: resolution of 4 cm−1, 256 scans, and scan region of 7800 to 450 cm−1. Raw spectra were then baseline corrected and ATR corrected using the OMNIC 9.2.41 software. The Kachin amber is from the mid-Cretaceous deposits near Tanai Town in Kachin State of northern Myanmar (51). Early Eocene Fushun amber is from Fushun, Liaoning Province, northeastern China (52). Early Eocene Oise amber is from Le Quesnoy, Oise, France (53), and Middle Miocene Zhangpu amber is from the Fotan Group of Zhangpu County, Fujian Province, southeastern China (54). All amber samples are deposited in the NIGPAS, China.

GC-MS/MS analysis

All samples were first cleaned with methanol under ultrasonic agitation and then dissolved completely in a dichloromethane/methanol mixture (DCM:MeOH; 9:1, v/v) also under ultrasonic agitation. All extracts were evaporated at room temperature in a fume hood. After refrigerating with excessive hexanes overnight to exhaustively precipitate the asphaltene, the soluble maltene fraction was collected. Aliquots of maltene were added to N,O-bis(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide to derivatize for 3 hours at 70°C before the subsequent analyses. Meanwhile, silica sand (burned at 800°C for 4 hours) was used as a procedural blank and subjected to the same processing steps as Hujiersite amber.

Total derivatized extracts of Hujiersite amber and procedure blanks were analyzed using an Agilent 8890 gas chromatograph (GC) interfaced to a 7010B triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (GC-MS/MS) in both full scan and dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) mode. The injector and oven were programmed constantly in both modes and were detailed as follows. Samples were introduced in splitless mode, with injector temperature at 320°C. The GC was equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (60 m by 250 μm by 0.25 μm) to achieve the separation of compounds and operated with constant helium as carrier gas at a flow rate of 1.2 ml/min. The temperature of the GC oven was initially held at 60°C for 1 min, then ramped to 320°C at a rate of 4°C/min, and maintained isothermally at 320°C for 40 min. The electron ionization (EI) source was set to 270°C with an ionization energy of 70 eV. In full scan mode, the mass spectrometer scanned a mass range of 50 to 650 Da with a scan time of 650 ms. For dMRM analyses, helium was used as the quench gas at a flow rate of 2.25 ml/min, and nitrogen served as the collision gas at 1 ml/min, with a constant collision energy of 10 eV for each transition. Compounds were monitored using specific precursor-product ion transitions in dMRM mode (figs. S3 to S5).
