Iatrogenic ureteric catheterisation during routine community catheter change resulting in obstructive uropathy, urosepsis and multi-organ failure: a case report
Authors: Jawwad Mihran Haider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inadvertent ureteric catheterisation during urethral Foley catheter insertion is a rare iatrogenic complication, reported predominantly in females with neurogenic bladder. We describe an 80-year-old man with a long-term catheter for benign urethral stricture who presented with abdominal pain and acute kidney injury 24 hours after routine community catheter exchange. Computed tomography demonstrated the inflated balloon within the distal left ureter, with obstructive uropathy. Following repositioning of the catheter, he developed Klebsiella pneumoniae bacteraemia and hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring intensive care, recovered, and was discharged home. The case highlights risks during community catheter management and the value of early cross-sectional imaging.

Introduction

Urethral catheterisation is one of the most frequently performed clinical procedures, with an estimated 100 million catheters sold worldwide each year.1 Although generally regarded as safe, it carries a recognised burden of complications including catheter-associated urinary tract infection, urethral trauma, and the creation of false passages.2 A particularly rare complication is inadvertent advancement of the catheter into the ureter with subsequent inflation of the retention balloon, which may precipitate obstructive uropathy, ureteric rupture, urinoma formation and urosepsis.3,4

To date fewer than 50 cases of inadvertent ureteric cannulation have been described in the international literature, with most reported patients being female and having an underlying neurogenic bladder.3,5 Risk factors identified in published series include long-term indwelling catheterisation, contracted bladder volumes, vesicoureteral reflux with patulous ureteric orifices, and impaired bladder sensation.3,5

We present a case of inadvertent left ureteric catheterisation in an 80-year-old male with a long-term urethral catheter for benign urethral stricture, performed during routine catheter exchange in the community. The patient subsequently developed obstructive uropathy, Klebsiella pneumoniae bacteraemia, hypercapnic respiratory failure and new-onset fast atrial fibrillation requiring intensive care unit admission. This case is unusual both in its patient demographic and in the severity of the multi-system clinical deterioration that followed and offers learning points for clinicians involved in long-term catheter care across primary and secondary settings.

Case presentation

An 80-year-old man was brought to the emergency department by ambulance with a 24-h history of generalised abdominal pain associated with nausea and subjective fever. Approximately 24 hours prior to symptom onset, his long-term urethral Foley catheter had been routinely changed at home by community (district) nurses. Insertion was reportedly uncomplicated, and the patient noted no immediate pain or peri-catheter leakage.

His past medical history included a urethral stricture necessitating a long-term urethral catheter (which he ordinarily exchanged himself), hypertension, bladder cancer, type 2 diabetes mellitus, prior ischaemic stroke, left above-knee amputation, prior pulmonary embolism (on therapeutic apixaban), iliac artery stenting, right carotid endarterectomy, and reflux oesophagitis. He was an active smoker, consumed 2–3 units of alcohol per day, lived alone and had no formal carers.

On arrival, observations were: temperature 36.9 °C, heart rate 71 beats per minute, respiratory rate 21 breaths per minute, blood pressure 114/68 mmHg, and oxygen saturation 99% on 4 L/min via nasal cannulae. Abdominal examination revealed global tenderness without peritonism. Initial laboratory investigations demonstrated a leucocytosis (white cell count 17.5 × 109/L; neutrophils 15.3 × 109/L), C-reactive protein 65 mg/L, and acute kidney injury with a creatinine of 139 μmol/L (baseline 81 μmol/L) and estimated glomerular filtration rate of 41 mL/min/1.73 m2 (baseline 80 mL/min/1.73 m2). The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated extension of the urinary catheter tip into the distal left ureter (Fig. 1), resulting in left-sided hydroureteronephrosis with perirenal and periureteric fat stranding. The patient was commenced on intravenous fluid resuscitation and broad-spectrum antimicrobial cover (intravenous cefuroxime, metronidazole and a stat dose of intravenous gentamicin), with adjustment for his documented penicillin allergy. He was reviewed urgently by the urology team in the emergency department. The catheter balloon was deflated, the catheter withdrawn into the bladder under sterile conditions, and the balloon reinflated; this manoeuvre resulted in immediate drainage of frankly purulent urine. He was admitted under the urology service for ongoing antimicrobial therapy, fluid management and close monitoring.

Blood cultures taken on admission subsequently flagged Gram-negative rods in both bottles, and antimicrobial therapy was rationalised to intravenous ceftriaxone with stat amikacin in line with microbiology advice. Bedside ultrasound on day 1 of admission confirmed appropriate central positioning of the catheter balloon within a collapsed bladder, with no residual hydronephrosis.

On day 2 of admission, the patient was reviewed acutely for hypotension (systolic blood pressure 86 mmHg) and pyrexia (38.3 °C). He was fluid responsive, with blood pressure improving to 99/66 mmHg following a 250 mL crystalloid bolus, and a further dose of amikacin was administered. Blood tests that day showed a C-reactive protein of 314 mg/L and a deteriorating estimated glomerular filtration rate of 33 mL/min/1.73 m2.

The patient acutely deteriorated on the third day of admission, becoming hypotensive (systolic blood pressure 64 mmHg), profoundly tachypnoeic (respiratory rate 42 breaths per minute), and acutely confused with reduced visual responsiveness. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated severe acute hypercapnic respiratory failure (pH 7.0, pCO2 10 kPa). He was commenced on non-invasive ventilation (continuous positive airway pressure) and metaraminol by the critical care outreach team and was admitted to the intensive care unit. Computed tomography of the head excluded an acute intracranial event, and a stroke team review attributed his transient neurological deterioration to sepsis-related hypoperfusion and hypercapnia.

Blood cultures were finalised as Klebsiella pneumoniae (wild-type, susceptible to ceftriaxone), and antimicrobial therapy was streamlined accordingly. Computed tomography pulmonary angiography excluded pulmonary embolism but demonstrated bilateral pleural effusions and pulmonary oedema. The patient also developed a self-terminating episode of fast atrial fibrillation, attributed to the combination of sepsis and pulmonary congestion, which reverted to sinus rhythm without specific intervention. He was started on oral furosemide for presumed cardiogenic pulmonary oedema and was successfully weaned from non-invasive ventilation and vasopressor support within 24 hours.

The patient was discharged from the intensive care unit on day 4 of admission. Subsequent ward-based care comprised continuation of intravenous ceftriaxone, gradual diuresis, and close monitoring of renal function. By day 9, his inflammatory markers and renal function had improved (white cell count 9.5 × 109/L, C-reactive protein 130 mg/L, creatinine 120 μmol/L, estimated glomerular filtration rate 49 mL/min/1.73 m2), and he was switched to oral co-amoxiclav. He completed a total of 14 days of antibiotics from the day of catheter repositioning and was discharged home in his pre-morbid functional state.

Discussion

Inadvertent ureteric cannulation by a urethral Foley catheter is a rare event, with most authors reporting fewer than 50 cases in the cumulative international literature.3,5 The risk factors most consistently reported in the literature relate to alterations in bladder anatomy and innervation. In a review of 48 patients with ureteric cannulation, approximately two-thirds were female, two-thirds were on long-term catheters, and a substantial proportion had neurogenic bladder, contracted bladder volumes or impaired sensation.3 These patients are predisposed because contraction of the bladder over time alters the relationship between the bladder neck and the ureteric orifices, and vesicoureteric reflux can render the ureteric orifice patulous. Impaired sensation may also mask the pain that would normally accompany ureteric balloon inflation.3,5

Our patient sits at the edge of this risk profile. He was male, had no known neurogenic bladder, and had been performing long-term catheter self-exchange without difficulty. Several features may nevertheless have predisposed him to this complication. Firstly, prolonged indwelling catheterisation is itself a recognised risk factor for bladder contracture and altered trigonal anatomy.3,5 Furthermore, his prior history of bladder cancer with intravesical treatment may have contributed to reduced bladder compliance and altered ureteric orifice anatomy. Finally, the fact that the catheter exchange was performed by another operator on this occasion, rather than by the patient himself, introduced an unfamiliar technique into a previously stable routine. The combination of these factors is plausibly sufficient to permit ureteric advancement of the catheter tip in a male patient without classic neurological risk factors.

Of note is the range and severity of the clinical complications that ensued. Within 72 hours of presentation, our patient progressed from isolated obstructive uropathy and an acute kidney injury to Klebsiella pneumoniae bacteraemia, hypercapnic respiratory failure requiring non-invasive ventilation, vasopressor-dependent septic shock, transient global cerebral hypoperfusion with reduced consciousness, and new fast atrial fibrillation with pulmonary oedema. Although prompt source control was achieved at the bedside on the day of admission, sepsis physiology evolved despite appropriate antimicrobial cover. This trajectory underscores that early radiological diagnosis and mechanical correction do not preclude subsequent multi-organ deterioration, particularly in elderly patients with significant cardiovascular comorbidity.

Several practical lessons emerge. The European Association of Urology Nurses recommends that balloon inflation should never be performed against resistance or in the presence of pain, and that the catheter should be advanced to the hub before the balloon is inflated, with subsequent gentle traction to seat it against the bladder neck.6 Where there is any clinical suspicion of misplacement (pain on inflation, peri-catheter leakage, sudden cessation of urine output, or unexplained flank pain) early imaging is advised. Ultrasound is a reasonable first-line investigation but, as in our case, may fail to demonstrate the catheter tip if it has already been repositioned. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography remains the definitive investigation, allowing assessment of catheter position, hydronephrosis, and the presence or absence of contrast extravasation suggestive of frank rupture.4,7 In suspected rupture, retrograde pyelography and ureteric stenting may be required. In the absence of rupture, simple deflation, withdrawal and reinflation of the balloon, as performed in our patient, is usually sufficient.3,8

This case also has implications for the governance of community catheter care. A substantial and growing proportion of urethral catheter exchanges in the United Kingdom and similar healthcare systems is performed by community nursing teams, often without immediate availability of specialist urology teams or imaging. Existing literature on iatrogenic catheter injuries has tended to focus on inpatient and emergency department settings.2,9 Clear escalation pathways, structured training in recognising signs of misplacement, and a low threshold for re-referral to secondary care when post-catheterisation symptoms develop are essential.

Conclusion

Inadvertent ureteric cannulation during routine catheter change is a rare but potentially serious iatrogenic complication. While most reported cases involve female patients with neurogenic bladder, this case demonstrates that the complication can occur in a male patient with a long-term catheter for benign urethral stricture, and that the resulting clinical cascade can include rapidly progressive multi-organ failure even when source control is achieved promptly. Clinicians supervising long-term catheter care. Particularly in the community, should maintain a high index of suspicion for catheter misplacement when patients develop unexplained pain, fever or biochemical derangement following catheter exchange, and should have a low threshold for cross-sectional imaging and urological referral.
