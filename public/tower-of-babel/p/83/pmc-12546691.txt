Physicochemical properties, in vitro digestibility, and 3D printing performance of buckwheat starch complexes co-gelatinized with large-leaf yellow tea soluble dietary fiber
Authors: Hui Xu, Jiameng Wang, Huijun Sun, Yixuan Xu, Zhongyun Zhao, Zongjun Wu, Mingming Zheng, Yibin Zhou, Yiqun Du, Zhenyu Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the effects of soluble dietary fiber from large-leaf yellow tea (TSDF) on the physicochemical properties, in vitro digestibility, and 3D printing performance of buckwheat starch (BS). The results show that TSDF effectively inhibits short-term retrogradation of BS, reduces its crystallinity and short-range order, and does not form V-type complexes with BS. As the TSDF content increases, peak viscosity decreases, gelation temperature increases, and gelation enthalpy decreases from 13.93 J/g to 12.31 J/g. Additionally, TSDF reduces the digestibility of BS and promotes an increase in pore size and thickening of pore walls in the colloidal structure. A moderate amount of TSDF (0.3 %) improves the 3D printing accuracy of BS. This study provides new insights for dietary fiber-modified starch and its application in low glycemic index and 3D printed foods.Keywords: Buckwheat starch, Large-leaf yellow tea, Physicochemical properties, In vitro digestibility, Low glycemic index, 3D printing

Introduction

Large-leaf yellow tea, abundantly available due to summer and fall harvests, is characterized by larger leaves, longer stalks, and higher dietary fiber content. Large-leaf yellow tea not only has a distinctive rice crust–like aroma, but also has a variety of health care functions, and has received widespread attention in recent years because of its functionality. Numerous studies have shown that the dietary fiber in tea is a key component responsible for its antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and immune-regulating biological activities(Liu et al., 2018). The extraction of dietary fiber typically employs chemical, physical, or enzymatic methods, often combined with ultrasonic or microwave technologies to enhance the yield of soluble dietary fiber. Among these, ultrasonic technology is environmentally friendly and highly efficient, offering broad application prospects in the food industry. Xu et al. (2018) found that feeding high-fat diet mice with water extracts of large-leaf yellow tea for 12 weeks significantly reduced blood lipids and alleviated inflammation. Teng, Li, Guruvaiah, Xu, and Xie (2018) reported that a 20-week intervention with large-leaf yellow tea aqueous extract effectively slowed the increase in blood glucose levels in severely diabetic obese mice. It has been reported that approximately 90 % of tea leaves become tea residues after processing and consumption, which are typically used as fuel, fertilizer, or bioadsorbents, but the utilization rate of their nutritional components remains low. Therefore, applying large-leaf yellow tea soluble dietary fiber (TSDF) in the preparation of high-fiber foods can not only enables the high-value utilization of tea leaves but also meets people's dietary health needs.

Starch, as an important biological macromolecule, is not only a major energy source for humans but is also widely applied in both food and non-food fields. However, native starch exhibits several shortcomings in practical applications, such as susceptibility to retrogradation, poor thermal stability, and low shear resistance. In order to effectively improve these deficiencies, a variety of modification methods have been developed, among which physical modification techniques are favored for their unique advantages. In particular, physical modification by adding natural components such as hydrocolloids and polysaccharides is not only easy to operate, cost-effective and environmentally friendly, but also effective in avoiding food safety hazards that may be caused by chemical modification. This modification strategy is fully in line with the current food industry's pursuit of clean labeling. A variety of advanced analytical techniques have been used to characterize the pasting, regrowth and rheological properties, which lays the foundation for further research on the digestive properties of starch. Englyst et al. (2018) classified starch according to digestion rate into rapidly digestible starch (RDS), slowly digestible starch (SDS), and resistant starch (RS), among which RS provides health benefits such as preventing colon cancer, promoting mineral absorption, aiding weight loss, and lowering blood glucose and cholesterol levels. In addition, modified starch has demonstrated application potential in the field of 3D food printing. This technology enables the conversion of digital designs into edible products through the layered stacking of materials, allowing for personalized shapes and nutritional customization to meet the needs of diverse populations. It also expands food processing methods, which has important practical significance in food applications. Previous studies have reported that soluble dietary fiber from pomegranate peel can reduce the swelling power and hardness of sweet potato starch granules while increasing the proportion of resistant starch (Xiong et al., 2024). In the study by Xue et al. (2025). it was found that the incorporation of Rhizoma Anemarrhenae polysaccharide promoted the formation of an ordered structure of the composite gel and enhanced the thermal stability of the gel system, with SEM results showing enlarged pore size and thickened pore walls. L. Zheng, Zhang, Yu, Luo, and Jiang (2023) reported that hydrothermal treatment significantly reduced the viscosity and amylopectin molecular weight of wheat starch, further improving the printing precision of 3D-printed samples. Due to the potentially significant differences in the effects of various dietary fibers on starch gel systems, their mechanisms of action still require further investigation and validation.

In this study, we systematically investigated the interaction law between dietary fiber of large-leaf yellow tea and BS. The differences in structure, physicochemical properties, in vitro digestibility and 3D printing performance of different BS-TSDF composite gels were analyzed by changing the addition ratio of TSDF. The use of dietary fibers to inhibit starch regrowth extends the shelf life of 3D printed food and solves the problems of storage instability caused by the easy aging of natural starch, thus improving the texture, taste and nutritional value of food and meeting consumer demand for healthy, safe and convenient food, providing a new strategy for starch-based 3D printing materials that combine stability, nutrition and printability.

The interaction mechanism between large-leaf yellow tea soluble dietary fiber (TSDF) and buckwheat starch (BS) is illustrated in Scheme 1. In the absence of TSDF, starch granules undergo full swelling and amylose is extensively leached, which facilitates molecular reassociation and crystallization during retrogradation. When a low level of TSDF is added, dietary fiber moderately hinders starch swelling and limits the mobility of starch chains, thereby slowing the retrogradation process. At higher concentrations, the dense tiber network significantly impedes water penetration and starch gelatinization, furtherrestricting starch chain mobility and strongly inhibiting recrystallization. Overall, the incorporation of TSDF alters the gelatinization and retrogradation behavior of buckwheat starch by interfering with water absorption, restricting starch chain migration, and disrupting the formation of crystalline structures.

Materials and methods

Materials

Large-leaf yellow tea powder was obtained from Anhui Province BaoErZhongXiu Tea Industry Co., Ltd., China. The cultivar is ‘Jinji’ and its Latin name is Camellia sinensis. Buckwheat starch was laboratory-extracted from the cultivar ‘Chitianqiao’ with the Latin name Fagopyrum esculentum. α-Amylase was purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Porcine pancreatic α-amylase was obtained from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Ethanol, sodium hydroxide, and potassium bromide were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All chemicals are analytical grade.

Preparation of TSDF

Based on the method of K. Wang, Li, Wang, Liu, and Ni (2021) and optimized. The specific operation was as follows: Firstly, 100 g of yellow tea powder was accurately weighed, and 1600 mL of 3 % citric acid solution was slowly poured into the beaker containing the tea powder, and magnetic stirring was turned on to avoid agglomeration of the powder. Subsequently, assisted extraction was carried out under ultrasonic conditions. Extraction process parameters: The ultrasonic power was 150 W, the temperature was 60 °C and the extraction time was 1 h. The extract was centrifuged (5000 rpm for 15 min) and the supernatant was collected and concentrated to 1/4 of the original volume. Next, four times the volume of anhydrous ethanol was added to the concentrated solution and left to stand at 4 °C for 12 h. After standing, the mixture was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min to collect the precipitate, which was then washed three times with anhydrous ethanol. Finally, the precipitate was freeze-dried and ground to obtain TSDF powder.

Preparation of BS-TSDF

The BS-TSDF gel was prepared with reference to the method described by Li, Liang, Huang, Huang, and Feng (2021) with appropriate modifications. Precisely 3.0 g of BS was weighed into a dedicated aluminum canister for RVA, and TSDF was added at mass fractions of 0 %, 0.3 %, 0.6 %, 0.9 %, and 1.2 % (based on the mass of BS), followed by the addition of 25.0 g of deionized water. The mixture was stirred thoroughly to ensure uniformity. The resulting suspension was then heated using a Rapid Visco Analyzer (Techmaster, Perten, Sweden). The specific testing conditions were as follows: the instrument was preheated for 30 min prior to measurement; the sample was held at 50 °C for 1 min, then dynamically heated at a rate of 12 °C/min until the temperature reached 95 °C and maintained for 3 min. This was followed by cooling at a rate of 12 °C/min until the temperature dropped to 50 °C, which was then held for another 2 min. All measurements were performed in triplicate. The resulting BS-TSDF gels (BS-0 %TSDF, BS-0.3 %TSDF, BS-0.6 %TSDF, BS-0.9 %TSDF, and BS-1.2 %TSDF) were freeze-dried, ground, and passed through a 100-mesh sieve to obtain BS-TSDF powders for further use.

Dynamic viscoelasticity

The gel prepared in Section 2.3 was equilibrated at room temperature for 20 min before measurement. The viscoelastic properties of the gel were characterized using a rheometer (DHR-1, TA Instruments, Newcastle, USA). The sample was evenly spread at the center of the bottom plate, and excess gel was removed using a rubber scraper. A thin layer of silicone oil was applied to the edges of the gel to prevent moisture evaporation. A strain sweep was first performed at a frequency of 1 Hz, with the strain ranging from 0.01 % to 10 %, in order to determine the strain value within the linear viscoelastic region. Based on the selected strain, a dynamic frequency sweep was then conducted. The specific measurement conditions were as follows: temperature of 25 °C, frequency range of 0.1 to 10 Hz, and strain of 0.1 %. The storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G"), and loss factor (tanδ) were recorded as a function of frequency.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

X-ray diffractometer (PANalytical X' Pert MPD, The Netherlands) was used to distinguish the internal crystalline structure of the starch and to analyze the crystallinity of the material. The samples were BS-TSDF powders prepared according to the method described in Section 2.3. Wrap the quantitative sample tightly packed in glass sample plates. The testing conditions were as follows: samples were scanned using a Cr target, with a tube voltage of 40 kV and a current of 30 mA. The scanning range was 5° to 40°, with a scanning rate of 4°/min.

Iodine binding capacity

The method described by Niu, You, and Liu (2024) was referenced, with some modifications. Accurately weigh 0.1 g of BS-TSDF powder prepared according to the method in Section 2.3, add 4.8 mL of distilled water and gelatinize at 95 °C for 30 min while stirring and heating, wait for cooling and add 0.1 mL of iodine solution (2.0 % KI and 1.3 % I₂, w/v). After vortex mixing, the solution is left to stand for 15 min. The absorption spectra in the range of 500–900 nm were recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (PE Lambda 35, PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA).

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

A thermogravimetric analyzer (TA Q5000IR TGA, USA) was used to measure the mass changes of the BS-TSDF powders prepared according to the method described in Section 2.3 during heating, in order to analyze their thermal stability and composition. An appropriate amount of sample was weighed into an aluminum crucible for testing. The testing conditions were as follows: the whole experiment was carried out under nitrogen protection from 30 °C to 600 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

The method described by Hao et al. (2025) was referenced, with appropriate modifications. The thermodynamic properties of the samples were determined using a differential scanning calorimeter (DSC 8000, Perkin Elmer, USA). Weigh 0.6 g of BS and add 0 %, 0.3 %, 0.6 %, 0.9 %, and 1.2 % (w/w of starch) of TSDF. Add 9.4 mL of distilled water to the mixture and shake until homogeneous. Place 8.0 mg of the mixed sample in a crucible and let it stand at 4 °C for 12 h. With the empty crucible as the reference, the temperature scanning range was set from 30 to 100 °C, and the heating rate was 10 °C/min. The thermodynamic parameters of the samples were determined, including onset temperature (To), peak temperature (Tp), conclusion temperature (Tc) and melting enthalpy (ΔH).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

The short-range ordered structure of the sample was measured using an FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Marseilles, USA). The freeze-dried sample from Section 2.3 was mixed with potassium bromide at a 1:100 ratio, ground, and then compressed using a tablet press for 30 s until it became transparent. The experimental conditions were set with a scanning range of 4000 to 400 cm−1, a resolution of 4 cm−1, using air as the background, and 64 scans were accumulated. The obtained spectra were processed using OMNIC 9.2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA), first with baseline correction, followed by Fourier self-deconvolution in the range of 1200–800 cm−1, with a bandwidth of 32 cm−1 and an enhancement factor of 1.9. The absorbance ratios of 1047 cm−1 to 1022 cm−1 (R1047/1022) and 1022 cm−1 to 995 cm−1 (R1022/995) in the deconvoluted spectra were used to evaluate the short-range ordered structure of the samples.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The microstructures of the samples were observed at 100 x and 300 x lenses using a scanning electron microscope (Hitachi Flex 1000, Hitachi, Japan). The method used to prepare the BS-TSDF gels is described in section 2.3, processed by freeze-drying, cut into thin slices with a razor blade, and then adhered in cross-section to conductive adhesive tapes with a 30 s gold-spraying treatment. Recording of the micro-morphological characteristics of the samples at an operating voltage of 5.0 kV.

3D printing

Weigh 3 g of BS, and add 0 %, 0.3 %, 0.6 %, 0.9 %, and 1.2 % of TSDF, respectively. After mixing thoroughly, add 25 mL of distilled water. Then, magnetic stirring was performed at 95 °C for 15 min. While still hot, transfer the mixed gel into the material cylinder, ensuring that no air bubbles are trapped before proceeding with the printing. The 3D printing model is a rectangular block with a height of 15 mm, width of 30 mm, and length of 30 mm, with the top view and side view shown in the Fig. 5. The specific printing parameters are as follows: nozzle diameter 0.84 mm, printing temperature 70 °C, layer height setting 0.7 mm, travel speed 15 mm/s.

The effect of different concentrations (0 %, 0.3 %, 0.6 %, 0.9 % and 1.2 %) of TSDF on the 3D printing properties of BS-TSDF gels was investigated.

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR)

The gel samples prepared in Section 2.3 were transferred to NMR-specific tubes and moisture distribution was determined after removing excess air bubbles. The distribution and mobility of water in different BS-TSDF gels were analyzed using a nuclear magnetic resonance imaging analyzer (NMI20–15, Shanghai Neumay Electronic Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The experimental parameters are set as follows: the CPMG pulse sequence is selected, the number of sampling points is 1,200,250, the number of repetitive samples is 8, the number of echoes is 3000, and the wait time for repeated sampling was set to 3000 ms. The obtained data were normalized.

Statistical analysis

Each experiment was repeated three times, and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Graphs were plotted using OriginPro 2024 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA). One-way analysis of variance was performed using SPSS 26.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), and significance was evaluated using Duncan's multiple range test (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Pasting properties

The adhesion properties of BS mixtures containing different concentrations of TSDF were determined using a rapid visco analyzer, with the viscosity profiles obtained under the standard program shown in Fig. 1, and the results summarized in Table 1. As the TSDF content increased, the peak viscosity decreased, likely due to the strong water absorption capacity of dietary fiber, which competes with starch for water during heating, thereby limiting starch granule swelling and reducing the degree of pasting (Ma, Zhu, & Wang, 2019). As the TSDF content increases from 0 % to 1.2 %, the trough viscosity increases from 2723.33 cP to 3223.67 cP. This suggests that TSDF may compete with starch for water, promoting the swelling of starch granules and increasing the concentration of free amylose (Z. Gan et al., 2023). Additionally, TSDF forms strong interactions with amylose and low molecular weight amylopectin in starch through non-covalent forces such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, which in turn increases the trough viscosity (Niu et al., 2024). The reduction in moisture delays the swelling of starch granules, which is macroscopically manifested as an increase in the gelatinization temperature.

After the addition of TSDF, the breakdown (BD) value decreased significantly from 1485.33 cP to 201.33 cP, indicating a marked enhancement in the thermal shear stability of BS. Meanwhile, the setback (SB) value gradually declined with increasing TSDF content, suggesting that TSDF can inhibit the short-term retrogradation of starch (N. Chen, Feng, Gao, He, & Zeng, 2024). This inhibitory effect may be attributed to hydrogen bonding between TSDF and amylose or to steric hindrance that interferes with the recrystallization of amylose. The experimental data showed that the incorporation of TSDF can simultaneously improve the thermal processing tolerance and storage stability of the starch system. This dual optimization property makes it promising as a natural anti-aging agent to reduce the use of chemical additives.

Rheological properties

In rheological analysis, the storage modulus (G') represents the gel's ability to store elastic potential energy under external force, reflecting its solid-like behavior, while the loss modulus (G") expresses the amount of energy that is dissipated by the material under external forces, reflecting the liquid-state properties of the material. The loss tangent (tan δ) is used to evaluate the viscoelastic behavior of the gel, a higher value indicates a stronger liquid-like characteristic of the system. Fig. 1(B-D) shows the effect of different TSDF addition levels on the dynamic rheological properties of BS. The experimental results show that within the tested range of angular frequencies, all BS-TSDF gels exhibited G′ values significantly higher than G″ values, and tan δ was less than 1, suggesting that elasticity dominated the gels and that they belonged to a weak gel system (Ning et al., 2020). With the increase in angular frequency, G′, G″, and tan δ all showed a clear upward trend. Moreover, as the TSDF addition level increased, G′, G″, and tan δ also increased, indicating that the incorporation of TSDF could enhance the elastic properties of the gel. The trend noted by Bu et al. (2024) aligns with this observation. Who found that increasing the concentration of Flammulina velutipes polysaccharides also led to an enhancement in both G′ and G″ values.

XRD analysis

Structural changes of TSDF mixed with BS at different additions were analyzed using XRD. It can analyze the crystal structure characteristics and crystallinity of the sample. According to Fig. 2A and Table 2, natural BS showed obvious diffraction peaks at 15.3°, 17.2°, 18.2° and 23°, indicating that it is an A-type crystalline structure. After gelatinization, the crystal structure of BS is disrupted, with the original diffraction peaks disappearing. At the same time, new diffraction peaks appear at 16.9° and 19.8°, indicating the formation of new ordered crystalline structures during the retrogradation process (X.-J. Zhu, Guo, & Zhu, 2023). With the increase in the amount of TSDF added, both the intensity of the diffraction peaks and the crystallinity decrease, indicating that the addition of TSDF inhibits the recrystallization of starch molecules. This may be due to the interaction between the hydroxyl groups of TSDF and amylose, which hinders the orderly arrangement of the starch chains, thereby reducing the relative crystallinity of the composite system and potentially affecting the compactness of the gel structure (D. Zhu et al., 2022). This conclusion was further supported by the combination of scanning electron microscopy observations that the addition of TSDF increased the pore size of the gel. The RC values reflect the extent of starch regeneration, and the results showed that TSDF inhibited the regeneration of BS, which was consistent with the decreasing trend in retrogradation values obtained from the RVA analysis shown in Table 1. In addition, XRD patterns showed that the crystal structure of starch did not change after co-pasteurization of TSDF with BS, indicating that the interaction between TSDF and BS mainly relies on non-covalent forces, rather than bonding through chemical bonds.

Iodine binding capacity analysis

The two types of starch (straight chain vs. branched chain) differ structurally and form different complexes when combined with iodine. Among them, the straight-chain amylose‑iodine complexes usually show strong absorption peaks at 570–620 nm (blue region), while the absorption peaks of branched-chain amylose‑iodine complexes mainly appear at 460–550 nm (violet-red region). As shown in Fig. 3E, the BS-TSDF showed an obvious absorption peak near 600 nm, indicating that iodine has a high binding capacity to the straight-chain starch in BS. As the TSDF addition increases, the intensity of the iodine absorption peak also decreases, indicating that TSDF could reduce the intensity of the iodine peak. This may be due to the fact that TSDF, as a hydrophilic substance, is rich in hydroxyl groups and binds to straight-chain starch through hydrogen bonding to form a stable complex (Xiao et al., 2021). On the other hand, TSDF and iodine molecules share a common binding site on straight chain starch. When the concentration of TSDF is increased, its binding to straight-chain starch inhibits the binding of iodine molecules, ultimately leading to a decrease in the peak absorption value (Niu, You, Zhou, Fan, & Liu, 2023). This result further suggests that TSDF is able to bind to the leached straight-chain starch, thereby affecting the molecular conformation and macroscopic property performance of the starch.

Thermogravimetric analysis

Thermogravimetric analysis is widely used to study the mass changes of starch and its mixtures during heating. Fig. 2C shows that the thermal decomposition of BS–TSDF undergoes four stages of mass loss. In the initial stage, the mass change occurs around 120 °C, which is primarily related to the evaporation of physically adsorbed water in the sample. In the second stage, the thermogravimetric curves of BS-TSDF remained stable in the range of 150 °C–220 °C, indicating that the samples have high thermal stability in this temperature range. In the third stage (220 °C–400 °C), BS-TSDF underwent a violent thermal decomposition. It resulted in the breakage of carbon chains, hydrogen bonds and glycosidic bonds. The fourth stage occurs in the range of 400 °C–750 °C, when the biomass undergoes a carbonization phase, when the stable structure between starch and fibers is gradually pyrolyzed and broken. From the DTG curve shown in Fig. 2D, the maximum heat loss temperature of BS-TSDF decreased with the increase of TSDF additions, indicating that the addition of TSDF reduced the thermal stability of the gel at high temperature. This may be due to the fact that the combination of TSDF and BS increases the spatial site resistance between the starch chains, which inhibits the orderly arrangement and interaction between the starch molecular chains, leading to the decrease of the thermal stability of the composite system. Combined with the XRD analysis, BS-1.2 % TSDF showed the lowest thermal stability, which could be attributed to its lower crystallinity. This finding is consistent with the results reported by J. Gan et al. (2020), namely that samples with lower crystallinity usually exhibit poor thermal stability.

DSC analysis

The thermodynamic properties of starch reflect its energy change and and molecular reorganization behavior during heating, mainly including pasting, thermal stability and enthalpy change. Fig. 2B and Table 3 show the effect of different TSDF additions on BS pasting temperatures (To, Tp, Tc) and enthalpy changes (ΔH). With the increase of TSDF addition from 0 % to 1.2 %, the To, Tp, and Tc values of the samples increased from 61.87 °C, 67.82 °C, and 73.37 °C to 63.60 °C, 69.74 °C, and 75.88 °C, respectively, and the heat-absorption peaks were shifted to higher temperatures, which indicated that the addition of TSDF slowed down the pasting process of the gels. This may be due to the fact that TSDF, as a hydrophilic polysaccharide, can be dispersed in the periphery of starch granules to form a molecular coating layer, and during the heating process, TSDF competes with starch for water, which reduces the water utilization rate in the system, and the rate of diffusion of straight-chain starch molecules from the interior of the granules into the solution phase is reduced due to the impeded swelling of the starch granules (Khanna & Tester, 2006). At the same time, TSDF binds to starch molecules through hydrogen bonding, inhibiting the breaking of the hydrogen bonds of starch molecules and reducing starch solubilization, thus increasing the temperature required for pasting. Enthalpy represents the energy required for the disintegration of crystalline or helical structures of starch granules during the pasting process (Feng, Sheng, Yu, Lin, & Shao, 2023). According to the data in Table 3, the ΔH value decreased from 13.93 J/g to 12.31 J/g as the TSDF concentration increased from 0 % to 1.2 %. This change suggests that the addition of TSDF reduced the energy required for starch pasting. This phenomenon may be attributed to the hydrophilic nature of TSDF, which competes with starch molecules for water, preventing the BS from fully decomposing and absorbing heat, which ultimately manifests itself as restricted swelling of starch granules and incomplete pasting (D.-N. Zhou, Zhang, Chen, & Chen, 2017). In addition, TSDF interferes with the orderly arrangement of straight-chain and branched starch by restricting the mobility of the starch molecular chains, leading to a decrease in the stability of the starch double helix structure and a reduction in the energy required for dissociation (Dang, Imaizumi, Nishizu, Anandalakshmi, & Katsuno, 2023).

FTIR analysis

The effect of TSDF addition on the BS functional groups and short-range ordered structure was analyzed by FT-IR. Infrared spectral analysis shows that the hydroxyl (-OH) stretching vibration peak is at 3400 cm−1, 2930 cm−1 is attributed to the characteristic absorption of methylene (-CH2), and 1645 cm−1 is related to the bending vibration of -0H bound to water in the amorphous region (F. Yang et al., 2022). Fig. 3(A-B) demonstrates the FTIR spectra of different BS-TSDF samples. As can be seen from the figure, compared to BS-0 %TSDF, none of the samples with added TSDF showed new characteristic peaks and there was no disappearance of characteristic peaks, indicating that no new chemical groups were formed between BS and TSDF. As the TSDF content increased from 0 % to 1.2 %, the hydroxyl absorption peak of BS around 3400 cm−1 shifted from 3432 cm−1 to 3419 cm−1, and the peak shape became broader, which may be associated with enhanced hydrogen bonding between TSDF and BS (Bu et al., 2024).

By performing Fourier self-deconvolution on the FTIR spectra in the 1200–800 cm−1 region. The effect of TSDF on the short-range ordered structure of BS was investigated. In this region, the characteristic peaks at 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 are sensitive to the crystalline and amorphous regions of starch, respectively. The intensity ratio of these peaks (R₁₀₄₇/₁₀₂₂) is commonly used for the quantitative analysis of the short-range molecular order of starch (Li et al., 2024). As shown in Table 2. After the addition of TSDF, the R₁₀₄₇/₁₀₂₂ value decreased, indicating a reduction in the short-range order of the starch in BS. This could be due to TSDF competing with water molecules, interfering with the ordered assembly of branched starch during the retrogradation process (M. Zheng et al., 2018). The data obtained by both XRD and DSC characterization means are consistent: the crystallinity and paste enthalpy of the composite system composites are reduced by 6.45 % and 11.63 %, respectively, which jointly confirms from different perspectives that TSDF is effective in reducing the molecular ordering of starch.

In vitro digestibility

Table 4 shows the effect of different TSDF additions on the rate of BS hydrolysis, with the increase in the percentage of TSDF additions, the RDS content decreased from 40.51 % to 30.87 % and the RS content increased from 21.44 % to 36.23 %. This trend suggests that TSDF reduces the in vitro digestion rate of BS and contributes to the reduction of postprandial blood glucose and insulin levels. This phenomenon may be due to the formation of a protective hydrogel layer on the surface of TSDF starch granules, a physical barrier that reduces the chance of contact between digestive enzymes and starch molecules (Wu et al., 2024). On the other hand, TSDF binds to the leached straight-chain starch in BS through non-covalent forces, forming molecular tangles and overlaps, and this reconfiguration of the gel network structure creates a spatial site-blocking effect that prevents effective contact between the digestive enzymes and the starch molecules, which in turn reduces the degree of starch digestion (Yuris, Goh, Hardacre, & Matia-Merino, 2018). During starch pasting, TSDF inhibited the solubilization of straight-chain starch and short-branched starch through hydrogen bonding, while during regeneration, TSDF interfered with the orderly rearrangement and assembly process of starch molecules, and this molecular conformation change directly affected the sensitivity of the enzymatic digestion site. The above multiple interactions jointly regulate the digestive properties of starch, ultimately leading to changes in the macroscopic properties of starch (X. Chen, He, Fu, Zhang, & Huang, 2017). Examples include a significant decrease in regrowth values and structural remodeling of the gel network (SEM observations show an increase in pore size).

First-order kinetics analysis

The in vitro digestion data of BS-TSDF composite gels were fitted and analyzed using first-order kinetic equations, and the resulting in vitro digestion curves and LOS plot are shown in Fig. 3C–D. Table 4 shows the theoretical maximum hydrolysis rate (C∞), hydrolysis rate constant (k), and coefficient of determination (R2) obtained from the LOS plot, and the predicted glycemic index (pGI) was calculated. The C∞ value reflects the relative content of the fast-digesting component (RDS) in starch, with higher values indicating that the starch is more readily digested. k reflects the speed of the starch hydrolysis reaction, with increasing values indicating that the enzyme-substrate binding efficiency is increased (X.-J. Zhu et al., 2023). The pGI is commonly used to assess the effect of different starch or starch-based foods on blood glucose levels. Higher pGI values indicate that the starch in the food is readily digested and absorbed, which may lead to a rapid rise in blood glucose. The experimental results showed that all R2 values were close to 1, indicating a good fit of the first-order kinetic model to the hydrolysis data. With increasing TSDF addition, both C∞ and k values decreased, suggesting that TSDF effectively inhibited the starch hydrolysis process, mainly due to its binding with leached amylose in BS, which reduced the leaching of straight-chain starch and hydrolysis by digestive enzymes (Shi et al., 2023). In addition, the pGI value gradually decreased with the increase of TSDF, indicating that the incorporation of TSDF can effectively slow down the digestibility of starch, which can be applied to the development of food products for diabetes or low-GI dietary needs.

Microstructure analysis

The microstructure of BS–TSDF gels can be observed using scanning electron microscopy, as shown in Fig. 4. At 100× and 300× magnifications, the gels exhibit a honeycomb-like porous morphology, which is associated with the evaporation of water during the rearrangement of starch molecules (Zhang et al., 2020). This micro-morphological feature has similarity to the corn starch-fresh grass polysaccharide gel system reported in the literature (Niu et al., 2024). It was observed that TSDF altered the porous structure of the composite gels, in which the BS-0 % TSDF gels had relatively smaller pore sizes and were more tightly arranged. With the increase of TSDF addition, the composite gels exhibited obvious pore size increase and pore wall thickening, indicating that TSDF can improve the microstructure of BS-TSDF gels. This may be due to the non-covalent interaction between BS and TSDF, which contributes to increase the repulsive force and water distribution state of the BS-TSDF gel, and the formation of larger ice crystals during freeze-drying, leading to an increase in the pore size of the porous structure (W. Wang et al., 2018). In the high-concentration BS-TSDF system, a large amount of TSDF interacted with the straight-chain starch, prompting the gel network to form a thicker pore-wall structure, and the formation of a dense physical barrier effectively reduced the chance of enzyme contact with starch molecules, thus decreasing the starch digestibility. This is consistent with previous findings on the enhancement of gel pore wall thickness by burdock nanofibrillar cellulose, further confirming the regulatory mechanism of dietary fiber on starch digestion (Li et al., 2021).

The effect of different concentrations (0 %, 0.3 %, 0.6 %, 0.9 % and 1.2 %) of TSDF on the microstructure of BS-TSDF gels was investigated.

3D printing performance

In the 3D printing process, the print material is stacked layer by layer, and whether or not the print sample appears to be dented and collapsed is a valid criterion for evaluating the print quality (Ji et al., 2021). Fig. 5 shows the variation of BS gel printed sample products with different TSDF additions. The results show that the TSDF addition has a significant modulating effect on printing accuracy and structural stability. Specifically, compared to BS-0 %TSDF, BS-0.3 %TSDF gel prints a smoother product surface, the four side profiles have regular edges and corners after printing, and the lines of each layer are clearly visible (Li et al., 2024). SEM-based analysis results further confirmed that the mesh structure when the TSDF addition reaches 0.3 % is denser, providing good mechanical support for the printed products. However, as the amount of TSDF added continued to increase, the edges of the printed product became undefined, visible protrusions appeared, and the ability of the structure to self-support was reduced, ultimately leading to an overall collapse (Ji et al., 2022). According to the experimental results, the moderate addition of TSDF can improve the printing accuracy of BS. However, excessive addition rather reduces the support of the gel, which affects the accuracy of the printed products.

LF-NMR analysis

Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance (LF-NMR) enables precise differentiation and quantitative characterization of water molecules in different binding states within food systems by detecting the relaxation behavior of hydrogen protons in water molecules. According to the analysis results in Fig. 3F and Table 5, three characteristic relaxation peaks (T₂₁, T₂₂, and T₂₂) were detected for all the samples in the range of relaxation times from 0.1 to 10,000 ms, which corresponded to water molecules in different binding states, respectively. Among them, T₂₁ represents strongly bound water that is tightly bound to polar groups (e.g., hydroxyl, carboxyl, etc.) in starch and dietary fiber molecules, which has the highest degree of restriction on the movement of water molecules; T₂₂ reflects the weakly bound water that exists in micropores of the gel network, which is partially mobile although the water molecules are restricted to a certain extent; and T₂₃ characterizes the free water that exists in the large pores or at the interface of phases, which has the highest molecular Mobility (Jiang, Ma, Wang, Wang, & Zeng, 2022). Larger T₂ values indicate that water is more mobile and water molecules are less constrained by the gel network (R. Zhou et al., 2021). The water distribution parameters are shown in Table 5, and the relaxation behavior of the three water molecules showed regular changes with the increase of TSDF addition, and the relaxation time of T₂₁ was shortened compared with that of BS-0 % TSDF. This may be due to the fact that TSDF is rich in hydrophilic groups such as hydroxyl groups, which enhanced the degree of binding between the gel and the fixed water (Wu et al., 2024). Meanwhile, the relaxation times of T₂₂ and T₂₃ were significantly prolonged with the increase in TSDF concentration, increasing from 5.06 ms and 95.48 ms to 8.23 ms and 191.16 ms, respectively. This change indicates that the mobility of weakly bound water and free water was enhanced. This may be due to the fact that the incorporation of TSDF prevents the gel network from forming a dense structure, as evidenced by the increase in pore size and the enhanced mobility of water in larger pores, which prolongs the T₂₂ and T₂₃ relaxation times (L. Yang et al., 2024). This corroborates with the SEM observations and together confirms the mechanism of action of TSDF by changing the microstructure of the gel and thus affecting its water-holding properties.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the effects of the addition of large-leaf yellow tea soluble dietary fiber on the structure, physicochemical properties, in vitro digestibility, and 3D printing properties of BS. The results showed that with increasing TSDF content, the peak viscosity decreased significantly, and the endothermic peak shifted to a higher temperature region, which may be related to the competitive water-binding effect of TSDF that inhibited starch granule swelling. FTIR analysis revealed that the absorption peak at around 3400 cm−1 shifted to a lower wavenumber, suggesting that hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions may occur between TSDF and BS. Meanwhile, the setback value, crystallinity, and molecular order all decreased with higher TSDF levels, and the thermal stability at high temperatures also declined, indicating that TSDF might interfere with the recrystallization of amylose. In addition, TSDF induced the formation of a gel network with larger pore sizes and thicker walls. As the TSDF content increased, both G′ and G″ rose to varying degrees, and the digestibility decreased, possibly due to the formation of a reinforced gel structure that acted as a physical barrier, limiting the contact and interaction between enzymes and starch. The 3D printing results show that the BS-0.3 % TSDF gel has good printing results and can maintain the structural integrity better, and when it exceeds 0.3 %, the gel structure collapses which affects the printing quality and stability. This study contributes to the understanding of the effect of dietary fiber on the structure and properties of natural starch, providing theoretical support for its application in the food industry. However, the current analysis mainly relies on characterization methods such as FTIR and XRD to speculate the existence of hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions from the side, which lacks direct evidence yet. Therefore, future work will introduce molecular dynamics simulation techniques to quantitatively study the interaction energies, and further evaluate their practical effects in regulating blood glucose in conjunction with animal experiments to make up for the lack of characterization means.
