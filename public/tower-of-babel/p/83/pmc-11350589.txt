Genetic Manifestations and Phenotype Spectrum in Infants With Feeding Difficulty
Authors: Mingyu Han, Wei Shi, Xiangxiang Chen, Dingwen Wu, Yi Sun, Weiyan Wang, Canyang Zhan, Lingling Hu, Tianming Yuan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundFeeding difficulties frequently co‐occur with multisystem disorders attributed to rare genetic diseases. In this study, we aimed to describe the genetic manifestations and phenotype spectrum in infants experiencing feeding difficulties.MethodsThis case series included infants under 6 months old with feeding difficulties admitted to the neonatal department of Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine from October 2018 to May 2022. All infants underwent whole‐exome sequencing (WES) during hospitalisation, and their clinical phenotypes and genetic results were analyzed.ResultsAmong 28 infants studied, nine were preterm and 19 were full‐term. Median admission age was 13.5 days (IQR 6.5, 35), with a median hospital stay of 16 days (IQR 10.5, 30). Overall, 12 (42.9%) cases were complicated with multiple malformations. Abnormal muscle tone (53.6%) and neurological issues (42.9%) were notable prevalent in these infants. Cranial MR abnormalities were noted in 96.2% of cases. Based on the combined analysis of WES results and clinical phenotypes, a total of 22 (78.3%) patients displayed disease‐related genetic variation identified through WES; among them, 15 (53.6%) patients received genetic diagnoses, while 7 (25%) patients were suspected diagnoses. Positive findings were more frequent in full‐term (89.5%) than preterm infants (55.6%). Ultimately, 24 (85.7%) patients were discharged alive, with 75% requiring post‐discharge tube feeding. Following discharge, five patients developed new symptoms linked to genetic variants, and two patients died.ConclusionsFeeding difficulty may constitute a facet of the phenotypic spectrum of rare genetic diseases. Whole‐exome sequencing can enhance molecular diagnosis accuracy for infants with feeding difficulties.

Introduction

Feeding difficulty, including feeding and swallowing disorders and dysphagia, is a common but woefully understudied problem in newborns and young infants. There are no population‐based studies that have described the prevalence of pediatric feeding disorders (Kovacic et al. 2021). Research suggests that approximately 1% of children in the US might experience swallowing difficulties (Bhattacharyya 2015) and there is general agreement that the incidence of swallowing dysfunction is increasing (Hawdon et al. 2000). Unfortunately, there is no corresponding data for Chinese Han population.

Feeding difficulty may occur in large patient populations, including infant with acquired brain injury or other neuromuscular disorders, craniofacial or airway malformations, as well as those with respiratory, cardiac, or gastrointestinal disease (Dodrill and Gosa 2015). But in some cases, feeding difficulties are not acquired but congenital. Congenital feeding and swallowing disorders consist of a large spectrum including developmental physiology, coordination of swallowing and ventilation, and metabolic factors that control hunger‐satiety balance (Goday et al. 2019), hence, often combined with multiple system disorders. Feeding difficulties might be the “tip of the iceberg” in the clinical manifestations of some rare genetic diseases that are difficult to diagnose by clinical phenotype only. In this study, our objective was to characterise the genetic manifestations and spectrum of phenotypes observed in infants grappling with feeding difficulties, while also exploring the diagnostic utility of whole‐exome sequencing (WES) in this context.

Methods

Ethical Compliance

This was a retrospective study with anonymised data. The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (2022‐IRB‐184).

Study Design and Setting

This study was a retrospective observational study of 28 infants aged under 6 months, presenting with feeding difficulties and admitted to the neonatal department of Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine from October 2018 to May 2022. Children's Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine is the largest tertiary hospital and the most comprehensive center of pediatric healthcare in Zhejiang Province. As a pivotal referral center, the neonatal department of this hospital caters to newborns from all over the country, delivering vital care during their earliest stages of life. All enrolled patients underwent WES during their hospitalisation to identify potential genetic factors. Genetic manifestations and phenotype spectrum of them were analyzed.

Data Collection

We gathered initial information about the enrolled patients, including age at admission, gender, chief complaint, gestational age, birth weight, whether they were small for gestational age (SGA), the presence of cesarean section and its underlying reasons, as well as the condition of sucking and swallowing. We reviewed medical records to gather data on the presence of features like craniofacial, bones and joints, genitourinary malformation and the existence of a single transverse palmar crease. Physicians from various specialties assessed changes in muscle tone and neural system, respiratory system involvement, and occurrences of recurrent infections. Diagnostic procedures such as fibrolaryngoscopy, EEG or amplitude‐integrated electroencephalogram (aEEG), brainstem auditory evoked potential (BAEP), and cranial MRI were performed as appropriate for each patient's condition. The resulting data were collected and analyzed. Patient outcomes were documented at discharge and follow‐up, including length of hospital stay, weight gain, feeding methods at discharge, rehabilitation therapy after discharge, new symptoms appeared after discharge, and death before or after discharge.

Whole‐Exome Sequencing

Whole‐exome sequencing was performed on enrolled patients. Total genomic DNA was extracted with Universal genomic DNA Purification Kit MagaBio plus (Bioer Technology, Hangzhou, China), and the whole genome exon region DNA was captured and enriched by human whole exon sequence capture kit KAPA HyperExome (Roche, Basel, Switzerland), followed by high‐throughput sequencing (Illumina, PE150). The obtained DNA sequence was compared with hg19 reference sequence of human genome provided by UCSC database, and the coverage and sequencing quality of target area are evaluated. Bioinformatics analysis and pathogenicity analysis were conducted for variation with coverage of 10× and above. The data interpretation refers to the relevant guidelines of ACMG (American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics). This test adopts trio mode (next generation sequencing were performed on both proband and whose parents). The reported high‐quality variation sites were not verified by Sanger sequencing, and the reported low‐quality variation sites were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Definitions

The following two conditions were considered disease‐associated genetic variation: (1) Patients with pathogenic or likely pathogenic genetic variations, along with corresponding clinical symptoms, received a definitive genetic diagnosis. (2) Patients with genetic variations of uncertain significance, but with consistent clinical symptoms, were considered for a potential diagnosis. Gestational age was determined as the most accurate estimate based on prenatal ultrasonography, menstrual history, obstetric examination, or a combination of these factors. SGA was defined as birth weight <10th percentile for the GA and sex according to the Chinese neonatal birth weight values. For feeding, both nasogastric tube (NGT) and gastrostomy tube placement (G‐tube) were categorised as tube feeding. Rehabilitation therapy referred to interventions aiming to improve function and reduce disability in individuals with health issues. New symptoms after discharge was defined as additional symptoms linked to genetic variants that manifested after the patient's discharge from the hospital. Weight gain was calculated with the formula below: Weight gaing/kg/day=Discharge weightkg−Admission weightkg/Admission weightkg/Length of hospital stayday×1000.

Statistical Analysis

To comprehend the distinct attributes of feeding difficulties between full‐term infants and preterm infants, we conducted a comparison of baseline information and outcomes among the enrolled patients based on their gestational age, specifically categorised as <37 or ≥37 weeks. The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS; version 27.0; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were presented as median with interquartile range (IQR) or mean with standard deviation, while categorised variables were described as percentages. The difference between normally distributed continuous variables was analyzed by the 2‐sample t test, whereas nonnormally distributed variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson χ 2 test or the Fisher exact test. Statistical significance was determined by p‐values <0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 76 cases of patients were diagnosed with feeding difficulty, and 28 of them were included in the study according to inclusion and exclusion criteria (Figure S1).

All 28 patients in the study came from nonconsanguineous Chinese Han population couples. Among them, 9/28 cases were preterm infants, while the remaining 19 were full‐term infants. Detailed patient characteristics are provided in Table 1 and Table S1. Overall, the average birth weight was 2747 g, with 32.1% of patients classified as SGA. Slightly over half of the patients were male and were delivered via cesarean section, with 9/28 cases attributed to fetal issues. The median age at admission was 13.5 days (IQR 6.5, 35). In the case series, only half of the patients had a chief complaint related to feeding problems, while the parents of the remaining half did not mention any feeding difficulties.

Outcomes

In this study involving 28 cases, the median length of hospital stay was 16 days (IQR 10.5, 30). On average, the patients experienced a weight gain of 5.7 g/day relative to their body weight while in the hospital. 24/28 patients were successfully discharged alive, and most of them (75%) still required tube feeding even after discharge. Following discharge, half of the patients engaged in rehabilitation therapy to enhance their functionality. After leaving the hospital, five patients developed new symptoms linked to genetic variants and two patients died, highlighting the importance of continued monitoring. Remarkably, no significant outcome disparities were observed between preterm and full‐term infants regarding feeding difficulties (Table 3).

Discussion

Feeding difficulty represents a persistent challenge, with its prevalence likely influenced by the increased survival rates of preterm births and infants with multiple malformations (Lefton‐Greif and Arvedson 2007). Unfortunately, the overall attention given to this issue remains notably insufficient. Typically categorised as neonatal encephalopathy, the treatment for feeding difficulty predominantly involves tube feeding methods such as nasogastric tube (NGT) and gastrostomy tube placement (G‐tube). Despite this, our understanding of its potential genetic origins remains limited. Feeding difficulty may serve as an indicator of rare genetic diseases, contributing to high rates of misdiagnosis and delayed intervention. In this study, our aim is to analyze the diverse clinical manifestations of feeding difficulty and unveil its genetic causes when the etiology is unknown.

Phenotypic Evaluation

In our study, merely half of the patients presented with the primary concern of a feeding problem. Alarmingly, some parents exhibited remarkably poor awareness regarding feeding difficulties and their potential consequences. Patients No. 25 and No. 28 were admitted at 158 and 149 days of age, respectively, without specifically noting feeding problems in their initial complaints. Their weights had significantly dropped, falling well below the third percentile on the growth chart.

32.1% of patients were identified as SGA, and an equal percentage underwent cesarean section due to fetal issues. This implies a potential correlation between feeding difficulties and abnormal intrauterine development in this subgroup. A significant portion of patients faced complications related to multiple malformations, accompanied by varying degrees of muscle tone and neurological abnormalities. Nevertheless, the majority of patients did not exhibit the classic phenotype required for a definitive diagnosis. Clinicians typically categorise them as having chromosome disorders or encephalopathy in a general sense. The final diagnosis ultimately relies on WES examination.

Clinical evaluations play a crucial role in assessing feeding difficulties. The use of fibrolaryngoscopy can help identify conditions such as laryngomalacia and recurrent respiratory issues, commonly observed in pediatric patients with swallowing dysfunction and aspiration (Irace et al. 2019). In our study, laryngomalacia was detected at a relatively high rate (42.9%), often without apparent stridor. Confirmation of reflux was achieved through laryngoscopy in three cases where patients exhibited recurrent cyanosis and respiratory infections, necessitating corresponding management strategies, including feeding strategies, family and caregiver counseling, and, in some instances, supraglottoplasty.

The coordination of sucking, and swallowing requires collaborative functioning across multiple systems, with a crucial emphasis on the central nervous system drive. The neurological system was evaluated through various perspectives, including EEG/aEEG, BAEP, and cranial MRI.

The amplitude‐integrated electroencephalogram (aEEG) serves as a valuable tool for prolonged monitoring of brain function, particularly in cases of perinatal asphyxia and seizure detection (Hellström‐Westas 2018). Research indicates a significant correlation between aEEG background patterns and cerebral white matter damage, influencing neurodevelopmental outcomes (Song et al. 2014). In our study, positive findings in EEG/aEEG were observed in 4 out of 18 patients. Among them, three patients exhibited abnormal function or depressed activity. Notably, Patient No. 17 displayed a burst suppression pattern, further investigations revealed a KCNQ2 gene variant, ultimately leading to the diagnosis of Early Myoclonic Encephalopathy (EME).

A notable percentage (43.8%) of neurological hearing impairment was identified among the 16 children who underwent brainstem auditory evoked potentials (BAEP). An abnormality in BAEP is recognised as an early indicator of brain insult (Karmel et al. 1988). Interestingly, in our study, the positive rate of BAEP was significantly higher than that of aEEG. This suggests that brain injuries are likely to be prevalent in a substantial proportion of patients experiencing feeding difficulties.

There is unequivocal importance attached to imaging in the assessment of neurological development and lesions. Surprisingly, 96.2% of patients displayed positive findings in cranial MRI, with a substantial fraction (44%) exhibiting widened extracerebral space or enlarged ventricles. These findings suggest a potential association between feeding difficulties and cerebral atrophy as well as white matter loss (McArdle et al. 1987), an aspect we intend to delve into more comprehensively in our ongoing research.

Genetic Manifestations

There are about 7000 rare diseases with genetic causes, contributing to nearly 80% of all cases, often characterised by misrepresented symptoms that can lead to incorrect diagnoses (Nisar et al. 2021). Whole‐exome sequencing encompasses around 85% of heritable Mendelian disease‐causing mutations (Choi et al. 2009). This powerful tool allows for the identification of the underlying genetic cause in 25%–35% of children with unexplained presumed genetic disorders or fetal structural anomalies (Petrovski et al. 2019).

In our study, we discovered that a substantial number of infants experiencing feeding difficulty of unknown origin can be attributed to rare genetic diseases. Remarkably, 89.3% of patients in our study exhibited positive results in WES, with 53.6% receiving a confirmed genetic diagnosis and 25% having a suspected diagnosis. The genotype spectrum spans a diverse range, predominantly comprising rare diseases.

The clinical presentation proved incomplete for a subset of patients. For instance, Patient No. 4 was admitted with the chief complaint of “poor intake for 20 days,” exhibiting hyperkalemia, hyponatremia and hyperglycemia during hospitalisation. Initially misdiagnosed with pseudohypoaldosteronism, WES revealed compound heterozygous variants of the KCNJ1 gene, leading to the final diagnosis of Bartter syndrome, type 2, which paradoxically presented with hyperkalemia. Studies indicate that mutations in the potassium channel ROMK gene (KCNJ1) often manifest with transient hyperkalemia in the first month of life (Peters et al. 2002). Consequently, the WES results corrected the misguided treatment approach.

Similarly, Patient No. 12 presented with uncoordinated swallowing, micrognathia, high palate and hypotonia, initially clinically diagnosed as Robin sequence. However, WES identified a de novo heterozygous variant of the ASXL3 gene, leading to the final diagnosis of Bainbridge‐Ropers syndrome (Srivastava et al. 2016). Since the ASXL3 variant was not identified in either parent, the risk to siblings was presumed to be low, although genetic counseling remains advisable (Balasubramanian and Schirwani 2020).

Particularly in the case of young infants, whose clinical characteristics may manifest atypically and evolve with disease progression, WES proves valuable in identifying causative variants even before clinical manifestations emerge (Pogue et al. 2018). For example, Patient No. 15 initially presented with feeding difficulty, failure to thrive, and hypotonia. WES revealed compound heterozygous variants of the SBDS gene, leading to the diagnosis of Shwachman‐Diamond syndrome. Subsequent follow‐ups revealed the gradual development of typical clinical manifestations, including thrombocytopenia and neutropenia (Topa et al. 2016).

Several of our patients were incidentally discovered to carry other genetic diseases that had not yet exhibited clinical symptoms or were identified as carriers of recessive genetic disorders. This underscores the importance of increased vigilance in these areas for future research and clinical practice.

Patients No. 16 and No. 21 were both genetically diagnosed with Nemaline Myopathy 2, exhibiting compound heterozygous variants of the NEB gene. Since muscle enzymes and electromyogram results were unremarkable, the diagnosis of nemaline myopathies relied on histological features observed in skeletal muscle biopsy and genetic correlations, with muscle MRI contributing to the diagnostic process (Sewry, Laitila, and Wallgren‐Pettersson 2019). However, given the invasive nature and limitations of muscle biopsies, genetic examinations become more acceptable to parents.

Clinical phenotypes among patients with rare genetic diseases exhibit remarkable diversity. In a subset of individuals within this study, multiple genetic abnormalities, including copy number variations (CNVs), were identified. Patient No. 19, for instance, presented with multiple malformations, hypothyroidism, feeding difficulty, and recurrent respiratory infections. Whole WES revealed a heterozygous variant in the CREBBP gene and compound heterozygous variants in the DUOX2 gene. Additionally, a significant increase in signal intensity on chromosome 16 was detected and confirmed by chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA). This comprehensive genetic analysis led to the diagnosis of Menke‐Hennekam syndrome 1, thyroid dyshormonogenesis 6 and Chromosome 16 fragment duplication (Allach El Khattabi et al. 2020).

The seven patients with suspected diagnoses were found to have variants of unknown significance (VUS) accompanied by corresponding clinical manifestations. Patients No. 8 and No. 13, sharing a similar history of asphyxia and post‐feeding hypotonia, exhibited manifestations of hypoxic–ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) on the MRI. Variants in the maternal ATP7A gene were identified in both cases, raising suspicion of Menkes disease. However, Patient No. 8 carries KCNN2 gene mutation additionally, which was initially classified as VUS with unknown associated diseases at the beginning of our research. In 2023, before our study was published the classification of the KCNN2 gene has been updated in OMIM to likely pathogenic, associated with disease Neurodevelopmental disorder with or without variable movement or behavioral abnormalities. This suggests that the clinical phenotype of Patient No. 8 is actually caused by the mutation of KCNN2 gene. As genetic research continues to advance, current VUS may undergo further study to elucidate their clinical significance. Continued follow‐up and research are essential for a more comprehensive understanding.

In conclusion, feeding difficulty may constitute a component of the phenotypic spectrum associated with rare genetic diseases, warranting careful consideration and thorough investigation. WES emerges as a crucial diagnostic tool, particularly for young infants exhibiting misrepresented symptoms. We strongly advocate for the incorporation of WES alongside comprehensive neurological assessments, including cranial MRI, BAEP and aEEG, in the diagnostic evaluation of patients with feeding difficulty. Additionally, to alleviate financial burdens, we propose the development of a targeted genetic panel specifically tailored for the relevant genetic spectrum associated with feeding difficulty.

Limitations

Our study was limited by its design. The criteria for judging feeding difficulty came from previous articles and were subjective to some extent due to the lack of quantitative indicators. Feeding difficulties caused by specific etiology such as severe cleft palate were excluded in this study. Premature infants within 34 weeks of gestation were excluded due to a history of mechanical ventilation or nasogastric tube insertion after birth. For other diseases such as spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) and PWS with typical clinical manifestations are not suitable for definite diagnosis with WES method and were excluded in this study. Some patients were unable to complete relevant examinations due to their critical condition, resulting in biased results.

Generalisability

In spite of the limitations, this study has good generalisability and is suitable for implementation. The genetic causes of feeding difficulties are not limited to the results of the cases in this study. The disease spectrum of feeding difficulties may be gradually enriched in further studies.
