Early-stage identification of heat-tolerant cotton genotypes under simulated episodic heat stress in controlled conditions
Authors: Aqsa Ijaz, Muhammad Kashif Riaz Khan, Sajjad Haidar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEscalating global temperatures pose an ongoing threat to cotton production by disrupting essential morphological, physiological, and metabolic processes during early plant development. These early stages are critical for crop establishment, yet the genetic basis of heat tolerance at this phase remains insufficiently characterized. Therefore, advancing our understanding of early-stage responses is essential for the development of heat-tolerant genotypes.MethodsA total of 79 cotton genotypes were assessed under controlled conditions and two elevated temperature treatments, HST-1 (45 °C) and HST-2 (48 °C), with emphasis on their morphological and physiological responses to heat stress under glasshouse conditions. Box plot was used to visualise trait distributions. Correlation analysis elucidated interrelationships among various traits. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted to reduce dimensionality and identify major axes of variation. The multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) was employed to rank genotypes based on overall performance.ResultsHeat stress significantly reduced shoot and root elongation, biomass accumulation, and relative water content, while increasing excised leaf water loss. Photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and instantaneous water use efficiency declined markedly, although transpiration rate remained relatively stable. PCA effectively distinguished tolerant from susceptible genotypes and revealed key traits associated with tolerance, while MGIDI identified eight top performing lines: G1 (NIAB-377), G4 (NIAB-868), G8 (NIBGE-IR-15), G9 (NIAB-512 33/4), G39 (NIA-Noori), G67 (Cyto-535), G69 (NIAB-512), and G78 (SLH-01).ConclusionOur results show substantial genotypic variability in early-stage heat stress responses in cotton and establish a rigorous multivariate framework for trait-based selection. This integrative multivariate approach facilitates the targeted selection of thermo-tolerant genotypes with enhanced morphological and physiological traits, advancing the breeding of climate-resilient cotton.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-06939-3.Keywords: Heat stress, Cotton, Seedling, Photosynthesis, Relative water content, MGIDI index

Background

Cotton serves as a crucial fiber source, meeting the increasing demands of the global population while providing income and livelihood support to over 100 million households [1]. As the leading natural fiber in global production and consumption, cotton has an estimated annual economic impact of around $600 billion [2]. Beyond fiber production, cotton contributes to the food chain, with about 65% of its conventional products utilized as food sources, either directly through cottonseed oil or indirectly via livestock fed on cottonseed meal and ginning residues [3].Among cultivated cotton species, Gossypium hirsutum (Upland cotton) dominates global production, grown across more than 35 countries, covering approximately 34 million hectares and yielding about 120 million bales per year [4].

However, climate change is exerting growing pressure on cotton cultivation by intensifying the frequency and severity of abiotic stresses such as heat, drought, salinity, and waterlogging. Among these, elevated temperature is one of the most detrimental, particularly during early plant development, where it disrupts critical physiological, morphological, and biochemical processes [5, 6]. Cotton is highly sensitive to temperature fluctuations, especially during its early stages. Pakistan, a major cotton-producing country, experiences the highest average temperatures among all cotton-growing nations [7, 8]. In its cotton-growing regions, typical temperatures reach 37/25 °C and may rise to 40-45 °C in May and June, occasionally exceeding 50 °C. These extreme conditions hinder seedling emergence, weaken early growth, and ultimately reduce yield potential. While cotton seedlings perform optimally at 30 °C, genotypes with larger seed size and weight can tolerate temperatures up to 40 °C. However, emergence fails entirely at 50 °C [1, 9].

In addition, high temperatures impair key growth processes such as leaf expansion, node and branch development, and photosynthesis. Damage at the cellular level includes reduced chlorophyll content, membrane instability, and a decline in enzyme activity essential for carbon assimilation [10–12]. Temperatures above 32 °C significantly impair photosynthesis, with stomatal closure and increased evapotranspiration exacerbating the impact. Notably, reproductive development is more adversely affected than vegetative growth, resulting in flower drop, reduced boll formation, and yield loss [13, 14].

Crops, particularly cotton, are highly vulnerable to heat stress induced by sudden weather fluctuations, especially sharp increases in temperature was observed at 50 °C. Early seedling stages are particularly vulnerable, yet most existing heat-tolerance studies focus on later growth phases and evaluate limited traits or genotypes under field conditions [15–18]. Therefore, identifying tolerant cotton genotypes at the early seedling stage remains challenging, as no effective screening protocols are currently available. Given the complex nature of heat tolerance, which involves multiple interacting traits, a deeper understanding of morphological and physiological responses is critical.[19, 20]. Thus, multivariate analysis provides a powerful tool to identify tolerant genotypes and traits linked to heat resistance [21]. The multi-trait genotype-ideotype distance index (MGIDI) reduces this process by merging multiple parameters, enabling breeders to screen tolerant genotypes under stresses [22].

This study aimed to screen cotton genotypes, identify efficient heat tolerance traits and their correlations and select superior heat-tolerant genotypes using MGIDI. Therefore, we screened 79 genotypes for heat tolerance at the early seedling stage under glass house conditions. Morphological and physiological traits were assessed, and heat stress was applied at two distinct time intervals to simulate episodic heat events, mimicking field conditions. This approach aimed to identify early-stage heat-tolerant lines, reduce reliance on extended field trials, and provide a time- and resource-efficient screening method.

Among the physiological parameters, Pn was significantly and positively correlated with Tr. In contrast, a significant negative correlation was detected between gs and instantaneous WUE (Fig. 2a). Under HST-1 conditions (Fig. 2b), a significant positive correlation was observed among FSL, FSW, DSW, FRL, FRW, and DRW, similar to the correlations observed under CT. RWC and ELWL were negatively correlated with each other, while RWC showed a positive association with Pn and ELWL was positively correlated with Tr. Likewise, Pn and gs showed a strong positive association with instantaneous WUE, while Tr exhibited a negative correlation with instantaneous WUE (Fig. 2b). Under HST-2 conditions (Fig. 2c), a similar trend in morphological traits was observed as in CT and HST-1. Specifically, significant positive correlations were found among FSL, FSW, DSW, FRL, FRW, and DRW, consistent with the patterns observed under CT. Physiological parameters revealed significant positive correlations among RWC, instantaneous WUE, Pn, and gs, while ELWL was positively associated with Tr. RWC and ELWL exhibited a negative correlation with each other, whereas Pn and gs showed positive associations with instantaneous WUE. However, instantaneous WUE was negatively correlated with Tr (Fig. 2c).

Discussion

Cotton breeders are increasingly focused on how plants respond to heat stress, especially given the concerns surrounding climate change and its impact on ecosystems [23]. Current climate models project a 2-5° C rise in global average temperatures by 2100, accompanied by increased frequency and intensity of extreme weather events, including heat waves, droughts, and erratic rainfall [24, 25]. These changes pose severe risks to crop productivity, particularly for temperature-sensitive species like cotton [1]. Developing crop varieties with greater tolerance to extreme conditions is therefore crucial for the future of agriculture [23]. To address this challenge, researchers are exploring germplasm diversity, an untapped reservoir of genetic potential [26]. At the seedling stage, a systematic multivariate approach is crucial for identifying key traits involved in making crops tolerant to heat stress, ultimately aiding in the selection of favourable genotypes for further study [27, 28]. Thus, this research focused on testing 79 cotton genotypes for heat stress tolerance at two critical temperature points: HST-1 (45 °C) and HST-2 (48 °C) at early seedling stages using different approaches.

Impact of heat stress on biomass accumulation

Heat stress had a clear and measurable impact on biomass accumulation in cotton seedlings. Both fresh and dry weights of shoots and roots declined significantly under elevated temperatures, with the higher stress level HST-2 causing more pronounced reductions than HST-1. Among the traits evaluated, dry shoot and root weights proved to be reliable indicators of genotypic response to heat (Fig. 1 and Table 1). This observation aligns with earlier studies showing that greater dry matter accumulation is often associated with improved tolerance during the seedling stage [29, 30]. The gradual decline in biomass under increased temperature reflects the sensitivity of early growth processes to thermal stress [31, 32]. Similar outcomes have been reported in other crops. For instance, in alfalfa, just one week of high-temperature exposure resulted in notable reductions in shoot and root biomass [33]. Likewise, chili pepper seedlings experienced similar losses after only a few hours of heat exposure [34]. In the present study, variation in dry matter accumulation helped differentiate tolerant genotypes from those more sensitive to heat, as shown in Table 1 and the Fig. 1. Root systems appeared particularly affected by heat stress [35]. A decline in water and nutrient uptake likely disrupted shoot development and altered overall plant architecture [36, 37]. This was reflected in shorter root and shoot lengths observed under both treatments, with more severe reductions at the higher temperature. These results point to a clear, temperature-dependent impact on seedling development. Genotypic differences were evident. Tolerant lines, including NIAB-512 and NIAB-135, maintained relatively higher biomass, while sensitive genotypes such as FH-114 and FH-552 recorded the lowest values under stress (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

High temperatures impair root function, reducing water and nutrient uptake, which in turn disrupts shoot development and alters overall plant architecture [36, 38]. Consistent with this, root and shoot lengths declined under both HST-1 and HST-2, with more pronounced reductions at the higher temperature (Fig. 1 and Table 1). These results reflect a clear temperature-dependent intensification of developmental impairment. High temperatures impair root function, reducing water and nutrient uptake, which in turn disrupts shoot development and alters overall plant architecture. Genotypic variation was evident, as heat-tolerant genotypes such as NIAB-512 and NIAB-135 maintained higher biomass across both treatments, while heat-sensitive lines like FH-114 and FH-552 showed the lowest values (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Heat-induced changes in leaf water status

Heat stress markedly reduced RWC while significantly increasing ELWL across the majority of cotton cultivars, exhibiting a trend consistent with the reductions observed in biomass accumulation. Notably, a subset of genotypes demonstrated a superior capacity to maintain both RWC and ELWL stability under elevated temperature conditions at both HST-1 and HST-2 levels (Table 1 and Fig. 1). These findings are in agreement with previous studies on rice, wheat, cotton, and alfalfa [14, 33, 39–41]. As reported by Saleem et al. (2024) heat-tolerant cotton cultivars exhibited significantly higher RWC than their heat-sensitive genotypes. The observed water loss in leaves likely disrupts critical metabolic functions, ultimately stunting plant development and reducing biomass yield [42]. Moreover, heat stress commonly limits root development and number, which can further reduce RWC due to diminished capacity for water and nutrient uptake. The significant drop in both biomass and RWC highlights cotton's vulnerability to heat stress [14, 41].

Role of gas exchange in genotypic heat stress response

High temperatures have a marked impact on photosynthetic efficiency, primarily by damaging the structural integrity of chloroplasts and interfering with their normal metabolic activity. One of the first physiological responses to heat stress is stomatal closure, which helps minimize water loss through transpiration [43, 44] However, this response also limits carbon dioxide intake and consequently reduces photosynthetic capacity [45, 46]. In this study, distinct genotype-specific responses to heat stress were observed. Cotton genotypes classified as heat-tolerant were able to maintain consistent stomatal conductance and transpiration rates, which allowed them to sustain relatively stable photosynthetic performance under high-temperature conditions (Fig. 1 and Table 1). The capacity to retain functional gas exchange traits, including photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, and transpiration, is widely regarded as a key indicator of heat tolerance [47, 48]. In contrast however, in our study, susceptible genotypes showed less efficient stomatal regulation. Some closed their stomata prematurely, limiting CO₂ uptake, while others failed to control water loss effectively, which led to dehydration without supporting continued photosynthetic activity. The significant reduction, and in some cases near-complete suppression, of net photosynthesis in these genotypes appears to result not only from reduced gas exchange (Fig. 1 and Table 1) but also from underlying biochemical impairments [46, 49]. These include degradation of chlorophyll, increased ion leakage across thylakoid membranes, and a decline in RuBisCO activity, which is essential for carbon fixation [50–52]. Overall, the findings suggest that disruption of the photosynthetic system under heat stress is more severe in genotypes lacking physiological adaptability.

Multivariate analysis for trait integration and genotype selection

PCA was applied as a powerful statistical tool to effectively examine data of complicated traits, which reduced the dimensionality and found key changes [53]. PCA also provides a data-centric analysis to study relationships between genotypes and traits under heat stress conditions, as proved by successful applications across multiple crop species [27, 54]. For assessing crop tolerance to abiotic stresses, PCA has been used as a validating tool, serving two key functions: [1] to demonstrate important stress-responsive traits, and [2] to decrease data attributes by removing excessive variables in complicated plant datasets [55].

In our study, PCA provided clear separation of genotypes based on trait performance under heat stress (Fig. 3). In the HST-1 biplot, genotypes such as NIBGE-IR-15, NIAB-896, FH-Supper Cotton, NIAB-868, NIAB-135, NIA-Noori, NIAB-377, FH-631, IUB-13, and NIAB-512-7A clustered closely with traits including gs, DRW, FRW, FSW, DSW, FRL, Pn, RWC, and instantaneous WUE, indicating superior physiological performance under stress (Fig. 3b). Similarly, under HST-2, genotypes including Cyto-535, NIAB-377, FH-631, NIAB-868, NIAB-512–33/4, NIAB-135, NIAB-512-7A, NIA-Noori, and NIAB-512 were associated with similar traits, reflecting strong phenotypic alignment with heat tolerance (Fig. 3c). Selecting genotypes positioned near traits like gs, Pn, RWC, and instantaneous WUE in the PCA biplot may effectively identify heat-tolerant lines (Fig. 3). Sivakumar et al. (2023) reported similar results, reporting that PCA effectively identified two salt-tolerant and two salt-susceptible genotypes [56]. Likewise, Nandhini et al. (2022) employed PCA to identify drought-tolerant cotton genotypes under stress conditions [57], further supporting the utility of this method in genotype classification across different abiotic stresses. We applied MGIDI (Fig. 4) alongside PCA (Fig. 3) and correlation analysis (Fig. 2), using this multi-trait index to score and rank genotypes by their resemblance to an ideal heat tolerant genotype. MGIDI has consistently shown its effectiveness in improving the performance, quality, and stress tolerance of a wide range of crops in both challenging environmental and biological conditions [58]. The method enables reliable multi-trait selection across crops, simultaneously evaluating yield, quality, physiological adaptation, and developmental characteristics to identify elite genotypes [59, 60]. For example, Al-Ashkar et al. (2023) applied the MGIDI index to find four superior wheat genotypes from 20 genotypes and grouped 20 traits into eight factors against abiotic stress [61]. Similarly, superior genotypes were selected from 9 genotypes, which grouped 10 different traits into four factors under drought stress [62]. In this study, 12 top-performing heat tolerant cotton genotypes were found from 79 tested genotypes based on 12 traits, and 8 genotypes were common across both heat stress groups (Fig. 4a, b and Table 3). A strengths and weaknesses analysis was also conducted, categorizing traits into three factors under HST-1 and two factors under HST-2 (Fig. 5a, b and Table 3).

The strong positive correlations among morphological traits (FSL, FSW, DSW, FRL, FRW, DRW) under all conditions (CT, HST-1, HST-2) suggest coordinated biomass allocation and robust growth regulation in cotton, even under thermal stress (Fig. 2). This aligns with findings by Abro et al., who reported that heat-tolerant cotton genotypes maintain growth by enhancing root-shoot synergy, a critical trait for stress adaptability [49]. Similar integrative trait behavior under stress has been documented in wheat, where strong interdependence among shoot and root biomass contributed to better heat resilience [63]. Under CT, negative correlations between FSL and both RWC and ELWL indicate that shoot elongation may compromise water conservation, potentially due to increased evaporative surface area. The positive relationship between Pn and Tr reflects typical stomatal function, while the negative gs-WUE correlation suggests low intrinsic water-use efficiency when stomata are fully open (Fig. 2a), consistent with Ijaz et al. [5]. Similar results also reported in rice and maize under non-stress environments [64, 65]. Under HST-1, the physiological adjustments become more apparent. Pn and gs showed positive correlations with WUE, suggesting enhanced stomatal regulation and CO₂ assimilation efficiency under moderate heat stress. Meanwhile, the negative association between Tr and WUE indicates that higher transpiration does not necessarily improve carbon gain (Fig. 2b), a pattern consistent with observations in heat-stressed cotton [66]. In HST-2, the strengthened positive relationships among RWC, gs, Pn, and WUE highlight the importance of maintaining cellular hydration and functional stomata under severe stress (Fig. 2c). This suggests that genotypes capable of sustaining high photosynthetic rates while conserving water have superior heat resilience. This is consistent with findings in heat-tolerant rice lines, where enhanced RWC and photosynthetic capacity under stress were tightly linked to improved WUE and biomass production [67]. The persistent negative correlation between Tr and WUE underscores the inefficiency of uncontrolled water loss, reinforcing the role of tight stomatal regulation under extreme heat [68]. These results demonstrate that morpho-physiological integration; especially the balance between gas exchange and water conservation is key to cotton's thermo-tolerance. Breeding efforts should target genotypes that optimize RWC, gs, and Pn under stress to enhance water use efficiency and biomass accumulation.

Materials and Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

This study involved 79 cotton genotypes (Table S1) sourced from various the Nuclear Institute for Agriculture and Biology (NIAB), the National Institute for Biotechnology and Genetic Engineering (NIBGE), the Ayyub Agricultural Research Institute (AARI), and the Central Cotton Research Institute (CCRI), all located in Pakistan. The genotype pedigree details are listed in Table S1. The experiments were carried out under glasshouse conditions at NIAB, Faisalabad, Pakistan, on September 18, 2022, using a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications (Fig. S1 and S2). Sand was thoroughly cleaned, measured, and placed into 711 plastic pots. Healthy and uniform seeds were surface sterilized using 10% sodium hypochlorite and sown 3 cm deep in plastic pots filled with sand. Germination was carried out under optimal conditions, maintaining a temperature of 30/25 °C (day/night) and 60% humidity, with supplemental lighting provided during the night. Throughout the experiment, a digital temperature and humidity meter (Model HTC-1) was used to monitor the growth chamber conditions, ensuring accurate temperature control. Temperature was regulated using an inverter air conditioning (AC) unit (2 Ton floor standing energy saving inverter reversible unit, ASCON vertical type air conditioner, remote controller & nano shield air filter, Capacity 24,000-BTU/R410A, power supply single phase with outdoor unit, with copper piping with cable indoor and indoor & outdoor frame stand) which was programmed to maintain the desired day and night temperatures throughout the experiment.

Experimental site

The control temperature was maintained for the first 20 days after sowing. The pots were divided into three sets: Control (CT) under normal conditions, Heat Stress Treatment 1 (HST-1) at 45 °C, and Heat Stress Treatment 2 (HST-2) at 48 °C. On the 21 st day, a T1 and T2set of plastic pots was moved to another glass house of the same type, equipped with an automated heating system. Heat stress was imposed by maintaining daytime temperatures of 45 °C and 48 °C for five consecutive days, from 21 to 25 days after sowing. for four hours.

The automated heating system, (operated on diesel fuel with digital display, auto thermostat system, ON/FF auto switch, heating temperature set as per requirement), was activated and adjusted to maintain a temperature of 45 °C and 48 °C. After each treatment session, the plants were returned to the normal-temperature glasshouse, and water was applied to the heat-stressed plants. Under field conditions, heat stress often occurs in episodes [23, 69, 70]. To mimic this, a second stress cycle was applied. This approach was necessary because screening genotypes based on survival during the first stress cycle alone does not accurately reflect field stress The second stress cycle was applied 20 days after the first stress cycle. The same procedure as the first cycle was repeated for five days, with four hours of stress each day. The following traits was taken after second stress termination immediately.

Physiological traits

After second stress, RWC was measured using the method described by Shivakrishna et al. (2018) [71]. In brief, fresh leaf samples (at least 0.5 g) from the control, treatment 1 (T1), and treatment 2 (T2) were immersed in 50 mL of deionized water for 12 h. Afterward, the turgid weight of each sample was recorded using a digital balance after gently removing surface water with blotting paper. The samples were dried at 80 °C until their weights became constant, after which the dry weights were recorded. ELWL was measured using the described method by McCaig et al. (1989) [72] Fresh weight of leaf samples was recorded immediately after excision. The samples were then partially dried in a drying oven at 28 °C for 6 h, followed by complete drying at 70 °C for 24 h, as described by Clarke and Townley-Smith (1986) [73]. Gas exchange parameters

After the second stress termination, photosynthesis (Pn), stomatal conductance (gs), transpiration rate (Tr), and instantaneous water use efficiency (WUE) were also measured as key gas exchange traits. Instantaneous WUE was calculated by dividing Pn by Tr. Fully expanded leaves were used for these measurements, which were taken using an IRGA Portable Infrared Gas Analyzer (Model Cl-340) between 10:00 to 14:00 under controlled conditions. The molar flow of air per unit leaf area was maintained at 403.3 mmol m⁻2 s⁻1, with atmospheric pressure recorded at 99.9 kPa. Water vapor pressure inside the chamber ranged between 6.0 and 8.9 mbar. The photosynthetically active radiation (PAR) at the leaf surface reached a maximum of 1711 mol m⁻2 s⁻1. Leaf temperature varied between 28.4 °C and 32.4 °C, while the ambient temperature ranged from 22.4 °C to 27.9 °C. The ambient CO₂ concentration was measured at 352 mol mol⁻1.

Seedling parameters

Seedling traits, such as the fresh shoot length (FSL), fresh root length (FRL), fresh shoot weight (FSW), fresh root weight (FRW), dry shoot weight (DSW) and dry root weight (DRW) were recorded for three replications of seedlings after the second stress.

Fresh shoot and root length

We meticulously removed seedlings from plastic pots and then washed them with distilled water to remove soil particles. After that root and shoot was cut from stem and root junction. And then shoot and root lengths were measured from the root-shoot junction to their respective leaf and root tips by using a scale.

Fresh and dry shoot and root weight

Fresh weights of shoots and roots were measured immediately after harvesting by using a weighing balance. To measure dry weight, the samples were blot dried to remove moisture. Then, root and shoot samples were kept in drying oven at 80 °C for 24 h using a laboratory oven (DSO-300D, Digisystem Laboratory Instruments Inc., Taiwan). After drying samples, dry root and shoot weight was measured by using weighing balance.

Statistical analysis

The analysis of variance (ANOVA) [74] was performed to evaluate the studied traits under a two-factorial design (temperature regimes × genotypes) (Statistic 8.1). Descriptive statistics were also calculated using the same software (Statistic 8.1). Correlation analysis was performed on the mean data to examine relationships among traits in Python (https://www.python.org/). Additionally, PCA was conducted in Python. We identified the best genotypes using the MGIDI index via the"metan"package in R software, with a 15% selection pressure. Hierarchical agglomerative clustering was used to construct a dendrogram for grouping genotypes based on similarity.

Conclusion

Our results indicated that heat stress significantly compromised both morphological and physiological performance in cotton seedlings, with increasing severity from HST-1 (45 °C) to HST-2 (48 °C). Morphological parameters such as shoot and root length, fresh and dry biomass, and root-shoot balance were substantially reduced under elevated temperatures, reflecting impaired growth and resource allocation. Physiologically, heat stress caused sharp declines in Pn, gs, and RWC, while increasing Tr and ELWL, suggesting disrupted water balance and gas exchange regulation. The strong genotypic variation observed across treatments emphasizes the importance of genotype selection in heat stress adaptation. Correlation analyses revealed tightly linked trait networks under stress, particularly among photosynthetic activity, water status, and biomass traits. PCA and MGIDI analyses effectively distinguished superior genotypes such as G1 (NIAB-377), G4 (NIAB-868), G8 (NIBGE-IR-15), G9 (NIAB-512–33/4), G39 (NIA-Noori), G67 (Cyto-535), G69 (NIAB-512), and G78 (SLH-01) that maintained higher photosynthetic efficiency, RWC, and instantaneous WUE under heat, and were closely associated with desirable multivariate trait profiles. This study provides robust evidence that traits like photosynthetic rate, stomatal conductance, RWC, and biomass retention serve as reliable indicators for heat tolerance at the seedling stage. The integration of PCA, MGIDI, and correlation approaches proved effective for identifying promising heat-resilient cotton genotypes, offering valuable tools for breeding programs targeting climate-resilient cultivars. Selection of genotypes combining physiological efficiency with morphological stability under heat stress holds strong potential for improving cotton productivity under rising global temperatures. Although controlled environment experiments enable precise stress application and trait measurement, field conditions are more variable and complex. Therefore, validation of the selected heat-tolerant genotypes under multi-location field trials and different environmental backgrounds is necessary to confirm their adaptability and stability across agro-ecological zones.
