Oncologic outcomes of surgical delay by interval and tumor size in T1b–T2aN0M0 renal cell carcinoma
Authors: Kang Jia, Mingsong Wang, Yu Wang, Dan Yuan, Long Huang, Kang Li, Xiao Zhong, Shiqiang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSurgical delay is often unavoidable in the management of localized renal cell carcinoma (RCC), but the safe duration for postponing surgery remains unclear, particularly for RCC of varying sizes.MethodsPatients diagnosed with T1a-T2bN0N0 RCC between 2000 and 2022 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess the impact of surgical delays of more than 1, more than 2, and more than 3 months on cancer-specific survival (CSS). Subgroup analyses were performed according to tumor size categories (4.0–5.9 cm, 6.0–7.9 cm, and 8.0–10.0 cm) at each surgical delay interval.ResultsA total of 39,242 patients were included, with a median follow-up time of 80 months (Interquartile range [IQR], 37–138 months). Surgical delays of more than 1 month did not worsen CSS in patients with tumors 4.0–5.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.05, 95% CI, 0.93–1.18, p = 0.412), 6.0–7.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.04, 95% CI, 0.92–1.17, p = 0.512), or 8.0–10.0 cm (adjusted HR = 1.07, 95% CI 0.93–1.23, p = 0.376). Delays of more than 2 months showed no significant CSS difference in tumors 4.0–5.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.91–1.21, p = 0.496) or 6.0–7.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.90–1.25, p = 0.463), but CSS worsened in tumors sized 8.0–10.0 cm (adjusted HR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.06–1.57, p = 0.012). For delays of more than 3 months, CSS was unaffected in tumors 4.0–5.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.11, 95% CI 0.92–1.31, p = 0.508), but was worse in tumors 6.0–7.9 cm (adjusted HR = 1.28, 95% CI 1.03–1.66, p = 0.021) and 8.0–10.0 cm (adjusted HR = 1.29, 95% CI 1.09–1.52, p = 0.003).ConclusionSurgical delay was not associated with worse CSS for RCC measuring 4.0–5.9 cm within 3 months. For tumors measuring 6.0–7.9 cm and 8.0–10.0 cm, delays of more than 3 months and more than 2 months, respectively, appeared to be linked with worse CSS. However, due to the retrospective nature and potential residual confounding of this study, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.Keywords: Renal cell carcinoma, Surgical delay, Tumor size, Cancer-specific survival

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is one of the most common malignant tumors worldwide [1, 2]. For patients with localized RCC, a variety of treatment options are available, mainly including nephrectomy and various tumor ablation techniques [3–5]. Currently, nephrectomy remains the primary recommended treatment. When technically feasible, partial nephrectomy is preferred, as it offers comparable oncological outcomes while better preserving renal function [6]. In addition, renal masses smaller than 4 cm are generally considered to be of low malignancy, so active surveillance can also be an option in such patients [7].

Due to various factors, including limited access to medical resources, delays between RCC diagnosis and surgical treatment are not uncommon [8, 9]. For T1a-stage RCC, a moderate delay in surgery has been shown to be safe [10–12]. However, the impact of surgical delay on oncologic outcomes in patients with larger localized RCC remains controversial [8, 9, 13–19]. Previous studies have yielded conflicting results, and most investigations have examined surgical delay solely based on T stage and a single predefined time interval. Because tumor size can vary considerably within the same T stage, patients with different tumor sizes may not share the same tolerance for surgical delay. This gap highlights the need for a more refined approach to defining acceptable delay intervals.

Therefore, based on the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database, the aim of this study was to systematically evaluate the association between surgical delay and survival in patients with T1bN0M0 to T2aN0M0 RCC, with a particular focus on stratification by tumor size. By moving beyond traditional T-stage-based analyses, this approach allows for a more nuanced understanding of how surgical delay may differentially affect patients, providing useful reference points for clinical decision-making in situations where surgery must be postponed.

Methods

Data sources and patient selection

This study utilized data from the SEER database, which is administered by the National Cancer Institute (NCI) and integrates cancer survival registry data covering approximately half of the United States population [20]. Specifically, we used the SEER-17 dataset (2000–2022), which covers approximately 26.5% of the United States population [21].

Malignant tumors originating from the renal parenchyma (C64.9) were identified using the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology, Third Edition (ICD-O-3) codes. The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: 1) patients diagnosed with T1bN0M0-T2aN0M0 (4.0–10.0 cm) RCC as their only or first primary malignancy; 2) patients who underwent nephrectomy; and 3) patients aged > 18 years. The exclusion criteria were: 1) missing race or tumor grade; 2) missing oncological outcome data, including cancer-specific mortality and follow-up time; and 3) missing information on the time interval from diagnosis to surgery. Statistical analysis

Restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves were used to explore the association between the duration of surgical delay (modeled as a continuous variable) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) in the overall cohort. Knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution of surgical delay time to balance flexibility and model stability. The overall association between delay time and CSS was tested using the Wald test (reported as "p-overall"), and non-linearity was assessed by testing whether spline terms beyond the linear term were jointly equal to zero (reported as "p-nonlinear"). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were subsequently performed to assess the effect of surgical delay on CSS, adjusting for covariates including age, sex, race, histological subtype, tumor grade, and tumor size.

After determining the effect of the duration of surgical delay on CSS in the overall population, we further investigated the impact of specific surgical delay interval points on CSS. To account for tumor heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were performed according to tumor size to determine whether the effects of specific delayed surgery interval differed across size categories. Considering that surgical delays rarely exceed 3 months in clinical practice, we examined three predefined intervals of 1 month, 2 months, and 3 months, defined by the time between cancer diagnosis and surgery recorded in SEER [8, 9]. For tumor size stratification, we employed two approaches: first, we divided tumors according to traditional T staging into T1b and T2a categories; second, to achieve more refined stratification, we categorized tumors by diagnostic size in 2-cm intervals: 4.0–5.9 cm, 6.0–7.9 cm, and 8.0–10.0 cm. All survival analyses were adjusted for covariates including age, sex, race, histological type, tumor grade, and tumor size (only in the overall population analysis, not in subgroup analyses). All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.0), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study cohort. A total of 39,242 patients were included, with a median age of 61 years (Interquartile range [IQR]: 52–69 years), and the majority were male (63.5%). The patient population was predominantly White (81.6%), followed by Black (11.6%) and other racial groups (6.8%). Clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) was the most common histological subtype (63.1%), while papillary renal cell carcinoma (pRCC) and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (chRCC) accounted for 12.2% and 4.4%, respectively. Tumor grades were distributed as follows: 11.9% for Grade I, 55.0% for Grade II, 28.2% for Grade III, and 4.8% for Grade IV. The median tumor size was 5.50 cm (IQR, 4.50–7.00 cm), with T1b and T2a stage patients comprising 74.9% and 25.1%, respectively. The median time from diagnosis to treatment was 0.10 months (IQR, 0.00–1.43 months), and the median follow-up time was 80 months (IQR, 37–138 months).

Cancer-specific survival

As shown in the RCS curves (Fig. 2), there was a statistically significant association between longer surgical delay and worse CSS in the overall cohort (p-overall = 0.025). However, the test for non-linearity was not significant (p-nonlinear = 0.872), indicating no evidence that the association deviates from linearity. Thus, although longer delays were associated with worse CSS, the shape of the association is compatible with an approximately linear trend over the observed delay range. Similarly, as presented in Table 2, multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that longer surgical delay time was associated with worse CSS in the overall population (Adjusted HR = 1.03, 95% CI, 1.01–1.04, p = 0.002).

Figures 3, 4, and 5 illustrate the impact of surgical delays of more than 1 month, more than 2 months, and more than 3 months on CSS across different tumor sizes, respectively. As shown in Fig. 3, after adjusting for covariates, a surgical delay of more than 1 month was not associated with worse CSS in the overall population (Adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI, 0.96–1.15, p = 0.661, Fig. 3A). Subgroup analyses based on tumor size demonstrated that a delay of more than 1 month was not linked to worse CSS in patients with tumors measuring 4.0–5.9 cm (Adjusted HR = 1.05, 95% CI, 0.93–1.18, p = 0.412, Fig. 3B), 6.0–7.9 cm (Adjusted HR = 1.04, 95% CI, 0.92–1.17, p = 0.512, Fig. 3B), or 8.0–10.0 cm (Adjusted HR = 1.07, 95% CI, 0.93–1.23, p = 0.376, Fig. 3B). Similarly, subgroup analyses based on T staging showed that a surgical delay of more than 1 month was not significantly associated with worse CSS in either T1b (Adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI, 0.98–1.16, p = 0.156, Fig. 3C) or T2a (Adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI, 0.95–1.19, p = 0.279, Fig. 3C) patients.

As shown in Fig. 4, after adjusting for covariates, a surgical delay of more than 2 months was not associated with worse CSS in the overall population (Adjusted HR = 1.08, 95% CI, 0.97–1.17, p = 0.205, Fig. 4A). Subgroup analyses by tumor size revealed that a delay of more than 2 months did not worsen CSS in patients with 4.0–5.9 cm (Adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI, 0.91–1.21, p = 0.496, Fig. 4B) or 6.0–7.9 cm tumors (Adjusted HR = 1.06, 95% CI, 0.90–1.25, p = 0.463, Fig. 4B), but did result in worse CSS in patients with 8.0–10.0 cm tumors (Adjusted HR = 1.29, 95% CI, 1.06–1.57, p = 0.012, Fig. 4B). T staging-based subgroup analyses showed that a delay of more than 2 months did not affect CSS in T1b patients (Adjusted HR = 1.05, 95% CI, 0.94–1.17, p = 0.384, Fig. 4C), but resulted in worse CSS in T2a patients (Adjusted HR = 1.26, 95% CI, 1.08–1.47, p = 0.003, Fig. 4C).

As shown in Fig. 5, after adjusting for covariates, a surgical delay of more than 3 months did not result in worse CSS in the overall population (Adjusted HR = 1.14, 95% CI, 0.98–1.24, p = 0.167, Fig. 5A). Subgroup analyses by tumor size indicated that a delay of more than 3 months did not worsen CSS in patients with 4.0–5.9 cm tumors (Adjusted HR = 1.11, 95% CI, 0.92–1.31, p = 0.508, Fig. 5B), but resulted in worse CSS in patients with 6.0–7.9 cm (Adjusted HR = 1.28, 95% CI, 1.03–1.66, p = 0.021, Fig. 5B) and 8.0–10.0 cm tumors (Adjusted HR = 1.29, 95% CI, 1.09–1.52, p = 0.003, Fig. 5B). T staging-based subgroup analyses indicated that a delay of more than 3 months did not affect CSS in T1b patients (Adjusted HR = 1.13, 95% CI, 0.95–1.29, p = 0.576, Fig. 5C), but resulted in worse CSS in T2a patients (Adjusted HR = 1.29, 95% CI, 1.11–1.42, p = 0.002, Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Small renal masses, typically defined as renal tumors with a diameter less than 4 cm, are generally associated with low malignant potential and slow growth rates [10, 11]. Previous studies have demonstrated that active surveillance does not compromise oncologic outcomes in this population [12]. Therefore, active surveillance has been endorsed by several clinical guidelines as a safe and effective management strategy for small renal masses [3–5, 7]. However, for larger renal tumors, no clear recommendations exist regarding surgical delay, and prompt surgical intervention remains the standard of care [3–5]. Nonetheless, delays between diagnosis and surgical treatment are not uncommon among patients with localized RCC [8, 9]. On one hand, urologists often regard early-stage RCC (T1-T2N0M0) as having a favorable prognosis, and may thus consider a short surgical delay to be safe. On the other hand, systemic issues such as limited healthcare resources may result in unavoidable delays. For instance, Carlos et al. reported that in their Canadian center, the median time from initial consultation to surgery was 41 days overall, with clinical T1, T2, and T3-T4 tumors experiencing median wait times of 57.12, 36.8, and 30.32 days, respectively [8]. Similarly, a Korean study found that for patients with stage II or higher RCC, the median time from diagnosis to surgery was 20 days, with a mean of 25.9 days [9]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying acceptable delay intervals tailored to patient and tumor characteristics.

Several previous studies have investigated the impact of surgical delay on oncological outcomes in early-stage RCC, but the results remain controversial. For instance, Russo et al. reviewed medical records of 1,484 adult patients with non-metastatic RCC larger than 4 cm and found that longer delays exceeding 3 months were associated with worse overall survival (HR = 1.17, 95% CI, 1.08–1.27, p = 0.0002) [13]. Similarly, Ou et al., based on 762 Chinese patients with pathological T1 stage RCC who underwent nephrectomy, found that in T1b RCC, a surgical delay greater than 5 weeks was associated with worse disease-free survival (HR = 2.90, 95% CI, 1.46–5.75, p = 0.002) and overall survival (HR = 2.49, 95% CI, 1.09–5.70, p = 0.030) [14]. Conversely, Srivastava et al. analyzed data from 29,746 RCC patients who underwent nephrectomy and found that surgical delays beyond 3 months did not significantly increase the risk of pathological upstaging to pT3a among patients with cT1b (OR = 0.90, 95% CI, 0.77–1.05, p = 0.170), cT2a (OR = 0.90, 95% CI, 0.69–1.19, p = 0.454), or cT2b (OR = 0.96, 95% CI, 0.62–1.51, p = 0.873) patients [15]. Carlos et al. similarly reported that a median surgical delay of 41 days was not significantly associated with tumor upstaging [8]. Ginsburg et al. further reported that in healthy T2 RCC patients with a Charlson Comorbidity Index of 0, surgical delays of 3–4 months did not adversely affect overall survival (HR = 1.08, 95% CI, 0.93–1.26, p = 0.309) [16]. These conflicting findings likely reflect multiple sources of heterogeneity. Differences in patient selection, healthcare systems, and baseline comorbidities across countries and centers may partly explain the discrepancies. However, a major contributing factor may be that most previous studies defined surgical delay solely based on T stage and a single predefined time interval. Such an approach does not account for the substantial variability in tumor size within the same stage, meaning that patients with different tumor sizes may tolerate surgical delays differently. Consequently, even subtle differences in cohort composition or the chosen delay interval can produce seemingly contradictory results. Recent studies have also provided additional insights into prognostic factors, surgical considerations, and minimally invasive techniques in RCC, which may further affect outcomes across centers [6, 22, 23].

By stratifying patients according to tumor size in addition to T stage, our study provides a more refined assessment of surgical delay, allowing for a better understanding of which patients may safely tolerate short-term postponement and which are at higher risk. We found that a surgical delay of more than 1 month was not associated with worse CSS in the overall cohort or in any tumor size subgroup (4.0–5.9 cm, 6.0–7.9 cm, and 8.0–10.0 cm). While a delay of more than 2 months did not worsen CSS in the overall population, it was significantly associated with worse CSS in patients with tumors measuring 8.0–10.0 cm. Similarly, a delay of more than 3 months was associated with worse CSS in patients with tumors sized 6.0–7.9 cm and 8.0–10.0 cm, though not in the overall cohort. These results suggest that different sizes of RCC have different safe surgical delay intervals. However, given that the SEER database predominantly represents White patients, the generalizability of our findings to other racial and ethnic groups may be limited. Moreover, as this is a retrospective study lacking granular clinical details, the observed thresholds should be interpreted with caution and should not be regarded as definitive guidance for clinical practice. Furthermore, when comparing stratification by T staging versus tumor size, we found that tumor size stratification was more accurate. For instance, with a delay of more than 3 months, a considerable portion of patients with 6.0–7.9 cm tumors (specifically those with 6.0–6.9 cm tumors) would be classified as T1b stage, and T1b patients overall were considered to tolerate such delays. In fact, the T staging classification for RCC is quite broad, with many patients having significantly different tumor sizes being classified into the same stage, which is inappropriate for the precise control required for surgical delay timing.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. We conducted a systematic analysis of the impact of surgical delay across different tumor sizes in patients with non-metastatic RCC. This approach provides more practical and detailed guidance for clinical decision-making, especially in situations where surgery must be postponed. Additionally, by using a large, population-based dataset, our findings are more generalizable to real-world practice.

Nevertheless, several important limitations should be acknowledged. First, as with all retrospective studies using SEER, unmeasured confounding cannot be excluded. SEER does not capture comorbidities, performance status, socioeconomic factors, or the underlying reasons for surgical delay, all of which may influence both the likelihood of delay and survival outcomes, thereby introducing potential selection bias. For example, patients with significant comorbidities might be more likely to experience surgical delay, which could confound the observed associations. Second, as mentioned above, the majority of included patients were White, and all participants were from the United States, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse racial, ethnic, and international populations. Third, follow-up time was relatively short for patients diagnosed in recent years, potentially leading to an underestimation of long-term outcomes. These limitations highlight that our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and prospective studies with detailed clinical data are warranted to validate and extend our results.

Conclusion

In summary, our study suggests that the impact of surgical delays in patients with T1-T2N0M0 RCC may vary according to tumor size. For tumors measuring 4.0–5.9 cm, surgical delays of up to 3 months were not associated with significantly worse CSS. For tumors measuring 6.0–7.9 cm, surgical delays of more than 3 months appeared to be associated with worse CSS, while for those measuring 8.0–10.0 cm, delays of more than 2 months suggested a potential adverse impact on CSS. However, these results are hypothesis-generating and should be interpreted with caution, given the observational nature of the study and the lack of detailed individual-level clinical information. Prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings before definitive clinical recommendations can be made.
