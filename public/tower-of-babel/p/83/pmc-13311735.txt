Validation and Clinimetric Properties of the Persian Version of the Leg Ulcer Measurement Tool
Authors: Qasem Hajilo, Manigeh Dehi, Zahra Jamshidi, Mehdi Raei, Mehdi Jafari‐Oori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Leg Ulcer Measurement Tool is a clinician‐rated instrument designed to assess wound characteristics and monitor healing in patients with leg ulcers. No validated Persian version exists for use in Iranian healthcare settings. This study aimed to culturally adapt the tool into Persian and evaluate its clinimetric properties. This methodological study was conducted between December 2024 and October 2025 in Iran. Cross‐cultural adaptation included forward translation, synthesis, back‐translation, expert review and pilot testing. Face and content validity were assessed qualitatively and quantitatively. Construct validity was examined via comparison with the Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool and known‐groups analysis based on ulcer severity. Predictive validity was evaluated using logistic regression for amputation and healing outcomes. Intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability were assessed using kappa, Gwet's AC, percent agreement and intraclass correlation coefficients. Predictive accuracy for amputation was analysed with receiver operating characteristic curves. All items demonstrated acceptable face and content validity. Severe ulcers showed significantly higher scores than mild/moderate ulcers (p < 0.001). Convergent validity with the comparative tool was high (r = 0.96–1.00) and total scores were strongly correlated (r = 0.864, p < 0.001). Higher baseline scores predicted increased odds of amputation (odds ratio = 1.13) and lower odds of complete healing (odds ratio = 0.93). Reliability was excellent (intraclass correlation coefficient > 0.99) and predictive accuracy for amputation was good (area under the curve = 0.80). The Persian Leg Ulcer Measurement Tool is valid, reliable and clinically accurate for assessing wound characteristics and predicting outcomes in Iranian patients, supporting its use in clinical practice and research.Keywords: Iran, leg ulcer, measurement tool, validation, wound assessment

Introduction

The term leg ulcer describes a chronic, non‐healing wound on the lower limb and should be understood not as an independent diagnosis but as a manifestation or long‐term outcome of various underlying pathologies [1]. Leg ulcers are commonly observed in clinical practice and represent an important public health concern, largely because they are associated with highly prevalent underlying conditions such as chronic venous insufficiency, diabetes mellitus and peripheral arterial occlusive disease [2]. They constitute a substantial global health burden, particularly among older adults and individuals with chronic illnesses, including diabetes, venous insufficiency and peripheral arterial disease [3, 4].

The most common underlying causes of leg ulcers include venous insufficiency, which accounts for approximately 43%–85% of cases, followed by arterial insufficiency (10%–20%), diabetes mellitus (10%–15%) and mixed etiologies involving multiple vascular impairments (10%–15%) [1, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Although these conditions represent the majority of cases, more than 180 distinct etiologies of leg ulceration have been documented. These additional causes include other vascular disorders, vasculitic processes, immunologic and infectious diseases, malignant neoplasms and various hematologic abnormalities [5].

Accurate epidemiological estimates remain limited; however, available evidence indicates that leg ulcers affect approximately 0.18% to 2% of individuals in European populations and may rise substantially with age, with prevalence estimates ranging from 3.6% to 5% among adults aged 65 years and older [10]. Reported prevalence rates include 32% for venous leg ulcers with an incidence of 17% [11], and approximately 6.2% for diabetic foot ulcers [12]. Additionally, a recent meta‐analysis estimated the overall prevalence of chronic leg ulcers at 1.51 per 1000 individuals [13].

Chronic leg ulcers are associated with prolonged healing times, high recurrence rates, substantial healthcare costs and reduced quality of life [14, 15]. Effective assessment and monitoring are therefore essential components of evidence‐based wound management [16].

Accurate wound assessment requires standardised, reliable and valid measurement tools that allow clinicians to document wound characteristics, evaluate healing trajectories and guide treatment decisions [17, 18]. While several tools exist for wound evaluation, many focus on limited wound attributes or lack robust measurement properties. The Leg Ulcer Measurement Tool (LUMT), developed by Woodbury and colleagues, is one of the most comprehensive clinician‐rated tools available [19]. The LUMT consists of two complementary sections comprising 17 total domains: 14 clinician‐rated domains assessing wound characteristics (e.g., exudate, necrosis, wound edges, depth, granulation tissue and edema) and 3 patient‐ or proxy‐rated domains evaluating pain and quality of life. Together, these domains provide a comprehensive assessment of ulcer severity and healing progression. The LUMT has demonstrated strong validity and reliability in its original English version and is widely used in clinical and research settings [19].

However, the applicability of wound assessment tools across different cultural and linguistic contexts cannot be assumed. Cross‐cultural adaptation is necessary to ensure semantic, conceptual and experiential equivalence, allowing tools to remain clinically meaningful and psychometrically sound when used in new settings [20, 21]. In Iran, leg ulcers—particularly diabetic and venous ulcers—constitute a major public health challenge, yet no standardised, validated Persian instrument exists for comprehensive ulcer assessment [22]. The absence of such tools contributes to variability in documentation and clinical decision‐making, limiting the comparability of data across settings and hindering quality improvement initiatives.

To date, no study has adapted and validated the LUMT for Persian‐speaking healthcare providers. Given the tool's comprehensiveness and strong performance in international research, establishing a culturally adapted Persian version is an important step towards improving wound care practice, enhancing clinical consistency and supporting national wound management programs. Therefore, this study aimed to translate, culturally adapt and evaluate the clinimetric properties of the Persian LUMT among Iranian healthcare providers and patients. Establishing a valid and reliable Persian LUMT will support standardised wound assessment in Iran and enable its use in clinical practice, training and research involving Persian‐speaking populations.

Methods

Study Designs

This methodological study was conducted from December 2024 to October 2025 in wound clinics and various hospital wards in Iran. The research proceeded through several structured phases and included participation from a variety of target groups (Figure 1).

Flow chart of the study.

Study Population/Sampling/Settings

A total of 48 healthcare providers (including wound care nurses, general nurses and clinical specialists) and 75 patients with 90 leg ulcers were recruited through convenience sampling. Recruitment was conducted across the two wound and ostomy clinics and multiple wards in a hospital in Iran, including critical care units, intensive care units, surgical wards, internal medicine wards and psychiatric wards, between April and July 2025. The diverse hospital settings and patient profiles were selected to enhance the clinical representativeness and generalizability of the findings.

Healthcare providers were eligible if they had at least 1 year of clinical experience and were involved in wound assessment or management. Patient participants were included if they had a diagnosed leg ulcer (venous, arterial, diabetic, or mixed aetiology) and were able to participate in wound evaluation procedures.

All participants were approached in person by members of the research team; participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained prior to data collection. Wound assessments using the Persian LUMT were conducted at the bedside by trained wound care nurses.

Clinimetric Properties

Face Validity

Face validity refers to the extent to which an instrument appears understandable, appropriate and relevant for measuring the intended construct from the perspective of users and experts [24]. The qualitative face validity of the Persian LUMT was evaluated through cognitive interviews with a panel of 10 experts, including wound care nurses and clinical specialists. The experts were purposefully selected based on their professional experience in wound management, clinical education and familiarity with leg ulcers. This purposeful selection ensured that participants had sufficient clinical and theoretical expertise to evaluate the clarity, comprehensibility and cultural relevance of each item within the context of Iranian healthcare practice. During the qualitative face‐validity assessment, items were reviewed and revised if multiple experts identified concerns regarding clarity, wording, comprehensibility, or cultural appropriateness. Specifically, items were considered for modification when at least 20% of participants reported conceptual ambiguity or difficulty understanding the item content.

Further, the quantitative face validity of the Persian LUMT was computed. Ten wound care nurses were invited to review the instrument and rate the importance of each item on a 5‐point Likert scale (1 = not important at all to 5 = very important). For each item, an impact score was calculated using the following formula: Impact Score = Frequency (% of respondents rating 4 or 5) × Importance Mean. An impact score of ≥ 1.5 was considered acceptable and indicative of adequate face validity.

Quantitative Content Validity

Content validity evaluates whether the items of an instrument adequately represent all relevant aspects of the construct being measured [23]. To quantitatively evaluate the content validity of the Persian LUMT, three indices were calculated: the Content Validity Ratio (CVR), the Item‐Level Content Validity Index (I‐CVI) and the Scale‐Level Content Validity Index using the averaging method (S‐CVI/Ave). Eleven wound‐care experts from academic and clinical settings were purposefully recruited based on their experience in wound assessment, education and instrument development.

For CVR, experts rated each item as ‘essential,’ ‘useful but not essential,’ or ‘not necessary,’ and values were computed using Lawshe's formula. Based on Lawshe's criteria, a minimum CVR of 0.59 is acceptable for a panel of 11 experts [25].

For I‐CVI, experts rated item relevance on a 4‐point scale, and I‐CVI was defined as the proportion rating the item as 3 or 4. According to Lynn, an I‐CVI ≥ 0.78 is required when using six or more experts [26]. The S‐CVI/Ave was calculated as the average of all I‐CVIs, with values ≥ 0.90 indicating excellent overall content validity as recommended by Polit and Beck (2006) [27].

Criterion Validity

Criterion validity is an estimate of the extent to which a criterion matches a gold standard [28]. Predictive validity was evaluated by examining whether baseline Persian LUMT scores could predict clinically meaningful outcomes over a 2‐month follow‐up period. Specifically, we assessed the association between initial Persian LUMT total scores and subsequent wound outcomes, including amputation and wound healing. Logistic regression analysis was performed to determine whether higher baseline Persian LUMT scores were linked to an increased likelihood of amputation or healing events. A total of 45 patients with 60 chronic leg ulcers were included in this stage.

A 2‐month follow‐up period was selected based on expert‐panel consensus and clinical considerations. The expert panel, consisting of an orthopaedic surgeon, an infectious disease physician and an experienced wound care nurse with surgical wound‐care expertise, agreed that a 2‐month interval would be sufficient to observe clinically meaningful outcomes such as wound healing progression, non‐healing, or lower‐limb amputation in patients with chronic leg ulcers. In addition, this interval was considered long enough to capture prognostic outcomes while minimising substantial loss to follow‐up.

Construct Validity

Construct validity assesses whether an instrument behaves as theoretically expected and accurately distinguishes between clinically meaningful groups or correlates with related constructs [29].

Construct validity was evaluated using the known‐groups method. Known‐groups validity of the Persian LUMT was examined by comparing the Persian LUMT total and domain scores across ulcer severity levels defined by the Wagner classification. Only patients with diabetic foot ulcers were included because the Wagner classification system is specifically designed for diabetic foot ulcer severity assessment. Patients with venous, arterial, or mixed‐aetiology ulcers were not included in this analysis because Wagner classification is not specifically validated for those wound types [30]. Ulcers graded as Wagner 1–3 (mild‐to‐moderate depth without gangrene) were compared with those graded as Wagner 4–5 (deep ulcers with localised or extensive gangrene) [31]. It was hypothesised that ulcers with higher Wagner grades would show significantly higher (worse) Persian LUMT scores. Known‐groups validity analysis included all 17 Persian LUMT domains (14 clinician‐rated and 3 patient/proxy‐rated domains) across 60 chronic leg ulcers. To ensure consistent application across clinical contexts, scoring logic was adapted to the Wagner classification. For Wagner grades 1–3, Persian LUMT items were scored according to observable wound characteristics (superficial to deep infection). For Wagner grades 4–5, where localised or extensive gangrene precluded assessment of some parameters, predetermined default scores were assigned to represent maximal severity for relevant items (e.g., A4 = 4 for bone involvement; A5 = 4 for hard necrosis; A10 = 4 for unattached edges; A14 = 4 for systemic infection).

Construct validity was further evaluated using the Convergent validity by comparing total and item scores of the Persian LUMT with those of the Bates‐Jensen Wound Assessment Tool (BWAT), a widely validated paper‐based measure of wound condition [32]. Both instruments were administered simultaneously by trained wound care nurses for 30 chronic leg ulcers. Convergent validity analysis was limited to the clinician‐rated domains of the Persian LUMT that had conceptually comparable items within the BWAT and was conducted in a separate sample of 30 chronic leg ulcers.

Reliability Analysis

Inter‐Rater Reliability

Reliability refers to the consistency and reproducibility of measurements across raters or repeated assessments over time [33]. Inter‐rater reliability was evaluated to determine the consistency of Persian LUMT scores between independent assessors. Two wound care nurses, each experienced in leg ulcer management and trained in the standardised use of the LUMT, independently assessed the same 30 patients on the same day under similar clinical conditions. Neither assessor had access to the other's ratings or clinical notes during data collection to ensure independence of evaluation. Each nurse independently scored all 14 clinician‐rated domains and 3 patient–proxy‐rated domains of the Persian LUMT for each wound based on direct visual examination and, when necessary, palpation. Total Persian LUMT scores for each rater were calculated by summing all item scores.

Intra‐Rater Reliability

Intra‐rater reliability was assessed to determine the consistency of repeated Persian LUMT assessments performed by the same clinician over time. One of the wound care nurses who participated in the inter‐rater reliability phase and had extensive experience in chronic wound assessment independently rescored the same 30 chronic leg ulcers on two occasions with a 72‐h interval between assessments. A 72‐h interval was selected to reduce recall bias while maintaining relative clinical stability of the wounds. In addition, advanced wound dressings were routinely used for patient care in our clinical setting and were typically changed every 72 h according to standard wound‐management protocols. Therefore, the same interval was adopted in this study to align reliability assessments with routine clinical practice while also minimising unnecessary dressing changes and additional treatment costs associated with expensive advanced wound dressings. During this interval, no debridement or invasive clinical procedures were performed that could substantially alter wound appearance, except for routine advanced wound dressing care. The assessor was blinded to the initial ratings during the second assessment to minimise recall bias and ensure independent evaluation.

All 14 clinician‐rated domains, 3 patient−/proxy‐rated domains and the total Persian LUMT score were analysed for both inter‐rater and intra‐rater reliability. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) with 95% confidence intervals were used to evaluate agreement for the total score of the Persian LUMT. Because all individual LUMT domains were measured using ordinal response categories, linear‐weighted Cohen's kappa coefficients [34] and Gwet's agreement coefficient (AC) were calculated for item‐level analyses to account for partial agreement and potential prevalence‐related bias. Percent agreement was also reported.

Interpretation of reliability indices followed established recommendations: κ < 0.20 = slight; 0.21–0.40 = fair; 0.41–0.60 = moderate; 0.61–0.80 = substantial; > 0.80 = almost perfect agreement [35]; ICC < 0.50 = poor; 0.50–0.75 = moderate; 0.75–0.90 = good; > 0.90 = excellent reliability [36]. Further, a sample of 30 wounds was deemed adequate based on previous methodological recommendations for reliability studies [37, 38].

Predictive Accuracy and Agreement Assessment

The predictive accuracy of the Persian LUMT was evaluated to determine its ability to predict lower‐limb amputation. The expert clinical outcome (amputation vs. no amputation) served as the reference standard. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to assess the discriminative performance of the Persian LUMT total score. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated as an overall indicator of predictive accuracy.

To identify the most clinically useful thresholds, multiple the Persian LUMT cut‐off points were examined. For each cut‐off, predictive performance indices—including sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative likelihood ratios (LR+ and LR−) and the proportion of correctly classified cases—were computed. These metrics allowed evaluation of both rule‐in and rule‐out capabilities of the tool. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Interpreting predictive accuracy was guided by established criteria: AUC values of 0.70–0.80 were considered acceptable, 0.80–0.90 good and > 0.90 excellent [39].

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed using Stata/MP version 17. Descriptive statistics summarised participant characteristics as means ± SD for continuous variables and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. Content validity was assessed using CVR, I‐CVI and S‐CVI/Ave, with acceptable thresholds set at CVR ≥ 0.59 [25], I‐CVI ≥ 0.78 [26] and S‐CVI/Ave ≥ 0.90 [27]. Criterion validity was evaluated with logistic regression, reporting odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and construct validity was assessed via known‐groups comparisons between Wagner 1–3 and 4–5 ulcers; normality was checked using Shapiro–Wilk tests, with t‐tests for normally distributed and Mann–Whitney U tests for non‐normal variables. Convergent validity was examined using Pearson's correlations between Persian LUMT and BWAT items and total scores. Intra and inter‐rater reliability was assessed using linear‐weighted Cohen/Conger's kappa, Gwet's AC, percent agreement and ICC for individual and average ratings. Predictive accuracy for amputation was evaluated using ROC curve analysis, reporting AUC with 95% CIs, sensitivity, specificity, likelihood ratios and correctly classified cases across multiple cut‐offs. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Adaptation and Qualitative Content Validity

The cross‐cultural adaptation process followed standardised guidelines and was completed without major challenges. Forward and back translations demonstrated strong conceptual equivalence, with only minor wording adjustments required during synthesis. During the qualitative content validity assessment, the expert panel confirmed that all items were linguistically clear, culturally relevant and conceptually consistent with the original LUMT. Feedback primarily related to minor wording preferences and clarification of clinical terminology. All suggested refinements were incorporated. In the pilot testing phase, the 10 wound care nurses reported that the Persian version was easy to understand and apply in clinical wound assessment. No item was identified as confusing or irrelevant. Only minor formatting modifications were made to improve scoring flow and usability. Overall, the qualitative evaluation supported the clarity, cultural suitability and clinical applicability of the Persian LUMT.

Face Validity

The qualitative face validity assessment confirmed that the Persian LUMT was clear, relevant and culturally appropriate for use in Iranian clinical settings. Feedback obtained from cognitive interviews with 10 wound care experts indicated that all items were understandable, clinically meaningful and appropriately reflected wound characteristics encountered in practice. No item was identified as ambiguous or difficult to interpret.

Quantitative face validity results further supported these findings (Table 1). Ten wound care nurses rated the importance of each item using a 5‐point Likert scale, and item‐level impact scores were calculated. All items exceeded the minimum acceptable threshold of 1.5, with impact scores consistently indicating high perceived importance and relevance. These results confirm that all items of the Persian LUMT possess satisfactory face validity and require no modification prior to psychometric evaluation.

Content Validity

Quantitative content validity analysis demonstrated that all items of the Persian LUMT possessed strong content relevance and necessity based on ratings from eleven 11 wound care experts. The CVR for all items met or exceeded the minimum acceptable threshold of 0.59, as defined by Lawshe's criteria for a panel of 11 experts (Table 1). This indicates that all items were judged to be essential for assessing leg ulcer characteristics. The I‐CVI values for the scale ranged from acceptable to excellent, with all items achieving I‐CVI scores > 0.78, meeting the minimum criterion recommended by Lynn for panels of six or more experts (Table 1). These findings indicate strong consensus among experts regarding the relevance and appropriateness of each item for evaluating clinical features of leg ulcers. At the scale level, the S‐CVI/Ave was calculated 0.91, exceeding the benchmark suggested by Polit and Beck for excellent content validity. This demonstrates a high level of expert agreement regarding the overall comprehensiveness and relevance of the scale. Overall, the quantitative content validity results confirm that the Persian LUMT has excellent content adequacy, with all items meeting established standards for necessity, relevance and representativeness.

Clinimetric Properties

Criterion Validity

Among the 60 chronic leg ulcers included in the predictive‐validity analysis, amputation occurred in 30 ulcers (50.0%), whereas complete healing was observed in 12 ulcers (20.0%) during the 2‐month follow‐up period. Logistic regression analysis demonstrated that higher baseline Persian LUMT total scores were significantly associated with an increased likelihood of amputation (OR = 1.13, 95% CI = 1.06–1.21, p < 0.001) (Table 2). The model explained approximately 31% of the variance in amputation risk (Pseudo R 2 = 0.31). Conversely, higher Persian LUMT scores were significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of complete wound healing (OR = 0.93, 95% CI = 0.89–0.98, p = 0.003), accounting for 16.6% of the outcome variance (Pseudo R 2 ≈0.17).

Construct Validity

The normality of Persian LUMT item scores was assessed separately for the Wagner 1–3 and Wagner 4–5 groups using the Shapiro–Wilk test (Table 3). In the Wagner 1–3 group, the majority of items showed a normal distribution (p > 0.05), including exudate type, exudate amount, size, depth, necrotic tissue amount, granulation tissue type and amount, edges, number of peri‐ulcer areas, leg edema type and location, assessment of bioburden and pain amount. Only Undermining (p = 0.002) and Necrotic tissue type (p = 0.007) deviated from normality. In contrast, the Wagner 4–5 group displayed a greater number of non‐normal distributions (p < 0.05), including size, depth, undermining, necrotic tissue type and amount, granulation tissue type and amount and edges, whereas exudate type, exudate amount, leg edema type and leg edema location remained normally distributed. Given these findings, independent t‐tests were applied to normally distributed variables and Mann–Whitney U tests to non‐normally distributed variables when comparing Persian LUMT total and item scores between the two Wagner severity groups to evaluate construct validity.

With regard to convergent validity (Table 5), most corresponding items demonstrated very strong positive correlations (r = 0.96–1.00, p < 0.001), indicating a high degree of agreement between analogous clinical domains of the LUMT and the BWAT. Because the LUMT does not include items directly comparable in content or scoring to BWAT items 9, 10, 11 and 13, correlations were not computed for these domains. Discussion

This study aimed to translate, culturally adapt and evaluate the clinimetric properties of the Persian LUMT for use in Iranian clinical settings. Overall, the findings demonstrated that the Persian LUMT possesses excellent validity, reliability and predictive accuracy. To our knowledge, this is the first study to provide a comprehensive psychometric evaluation of the LUMT in a Persian‐speaking population, fulfilling an important gap in wound assessment resources in Iran.

Following internationally accepted guidelines for instrument translation and adaptation [20, 21], the Persian LUMT maintained strong conceptual and semantic equivalence with the original English instrument. Both experts and clinicians consistently reported that items were clear, culturally appropriate and clinically relevant. These findings agree with the approach emphasised by Polit and Beck [27], who highlight that cultural congruence is fundamental for achieving content validity in adapted clinical tools.

Face validity was strong, with all items achieving impact scores above the minimum threshold, indicating high perceived importance among wound care nurses. Quantitative content validity indices further confirmed the robustness of the tool. All items exceeded the CVR threshold of 0.59 [25] and the I‐CVI threshold of 0.78 recommended by Lynn [26]. The S‐CVI/Ave > 0.90 aligns with the benchmark for excellent content validity [27]. Similar results have been reported in previous adaptations of wound and skin assessment instruments [18], suggesting that the comprehensive clinical nature of LUMT domains enhances its suitability for cross‐cultural use.

The known‐groups validity analysis in this study was performed exclusively in patients with diabetic foot ulcers using the Wagner classification system. Wagner classification was selected because it is a widely recognised severity‐grading system specifically developed for diabetic foot ulcers and reflects clinically relevant dimensions such as ulcer depth, tissue involvement, necrosis and gangrene. Therefore, the observed differences in Persian LUMT scores between Wagner grade 1–3 and Wagner grade 4–5 wounds support the instrument's ability to discriminate between clinically distinct levels of diabetic foot ulcer severity. However, this classification system was not applied to venous, arterial, or mixed‐aetiology ulcers because Wagner classification is not specifically designed for those wound types.

The Persian LUMT demonstrated excellent ability to distinguish between ulcers of differing clinical severity, as defined by the Wagner classification. The Wagner system was selected for the known‐groups validity analysis because it is one of the most widely used and clinically established grading systems for diabetic and ischemic foot ulcers [30]. It provides a clear, hierarchical staging based on ulcer depth, the presence of necrosis and the extent of gangrene—clinical features that closely align with the wound attributes assessed within the LUMT [40]. In addition, the Wagner classification is strongly predictive of clinical outcomes, including infection, non‐healing and amputation risk, making it an appropriate external criterion for evaluating whether the LUMT can discriminate between clinically meaningful severity levels [41].

In our study, most clinical parameters—such as size, depth, necrosis, granulation, undermining, edges and pain—showed significantly higher Persian LUMT scores in the Wagner grade 4–5 ulcers compared with mild‐to‐moderate (grades 1–3) ulcers. These findings support previous evidence demonstrating that wound characteristics systematically worsen with ulcer severity [14, 15]. The ability of the tool to discriminate severe from mild‐to‐moderate ulcers provides strong evidence of construct validity. The non‐significant differences observed in exudate amount, leg edema and quality of life may reflect the underlying pathophysiology of advanced ulcers. Diabetic foot ulcers are known to substantially reduce patients' quality of life, especially in terms of physical functioning, social engagement and mobility [42]. These impairments are largely driven by physical limitations resulting from underlying risk factors or active ulceration [43]. Since limited mobility is common across both Wagner groups, this similarity may explain why quality‐of‐life scores did not differ significantly between groups. Similarly, edema is more strongly influenced by systemic factors such as venous insufficiency and comorbid conditions, rather than by localised wound severity alone [44].

Moreover, the Persian LUMT showed very strong convergence with the BWAT. We selected the BWAT as the comparator for known‐group testing because it is a well‐established, widely used and validated instrument for chronic wound assessment, including leg ulcers, pressure ulcers and other chronic wound types. The BWAT evaluates multiple wound characteristics—such as size, depth, edges, undermining, necrotic tissue type and amount, exudate type and amount, granulation tissue, exudate and epithelialization—using a structured scoring system [45]. Because the domains assessed by BWAT overlap significantly with those included in LUMT, it provides a relevant external standard against which to test Persian LUMT's discriminative capacity.

Furthermore, reliability and validity evidence support BWAT as a benchmark wound‐assessment tool. For example, in a large prospective study among nursing home residents with pressure injuries, BWAT total scores demonstrated high reliability and significant differences across ulcer stages and healing outcomes [45]. Uses of BWAT in chronic wound research—including venous, diabetic and leg ulcers—also suggest its suitability to represent ‘real‐world’ wound severity and healing potential [46].

Hence, by demonstrating strong convergence between the Persian LUMT and BWAT scores, our findings not only support Persian LUMT's construct validity, but also highlight that Persian LUMT captures the same clinically relevant wound characteristics as an established, widely accepted instrument. This strengthens confidence in using Persian LUMT as an alternative or complementary tool for wound assessment in clinical and research settings.

Likewise, the Persian LUMT demonstrated robust predictive validity for clinically meaningful outcomes. Higher baseline scores significantly increased the odds of amputation while lowering the likelihood of complete healing. These results align with existing evidence linking severe wound characteristics—such as necrosis, depth and poor tissue quality—to negative outcomes and complications [47, 48]. For example, in a study validating a wound classification system, increasing wound depth was strongly associated with a higher risk of amputation: wounds that probed to bone had dramatically higher amputation rates than more superficial wounds [49]. Or in patients with chronic (particularly diabetic) foot ulcers, the presence of necrotic or purulent‐necrotic tissue and lack of granulation tissue—that is, poor tissue quality—was significantly associated with non‐healing outcomes [50]. The magnitude of effect observed in this study (OR = 1.13 for amputation; OR = 0.93 for healing) supports the tool's prognostic utility and highlights its applicability for risk stratification in clinical practice.

In addition, intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability were high across all parameters, with percent agreement ranging from 90% to 100%, weighted kappa values between 0.70 and 0.96, and Gwet's AC coefficients between 0.81 and 0.97. These indices reflect substantial to almost‐perfect agreement, consistent with the interpretation thresholds proposed by Landis and Koch [28]. Strong agreement across multiple reliability indices indicates that the Persian LUMT provides stable, reproducible measurements of wound characteristics, regardless of the evaluator.

Comparable reliability levels have been documented for the original English LUMT [19], confirming that the Persian adaptation maintains the tool's conceptual integrity and operational consistency. Similar findings have also been reported for other established wound‐assessment tools such as the BWAT [45]. The convergence of evidence from the instruments reinforces that structured wound‐assessment tools tend to achieve high reliability when item definitions are clear and scoring anchors are explicit.

The ROC analysis demonstrated good discriminative ability for predicting amputation (AUC = 0.80), exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of AUC ≥ 0.70 for adequate predictive performance [31]. The best‐performing cut‐off (≥ 46) provided sensitivity–specificity balances suitable for clinical screening. These results reinforce the clinical relevance of the Persian LUMT, suggesting that it may serve not only as an assessment tool but also as an early warning indicator for limb‐threatening deterioration.

Conclusion

The Persian LUMT demonstrated strong face, content, construct, convergent and criterion validity, along with excellent intra‐ and inter‐rater reliability and good predictive accuracy for predicting amputation. These findings confirm that the Persian LUMT is a valid, reliable and clinically meaningful instrument for the comprehensive evaluation of leg ulcers in Iranian healthcare settings. Its robust psychometric performance indicates that the tool can effectively support clinical decision‐making, enhance the quality and consistency of wound assessment and facilitate systematic monitoring of wound changes over time. In addition, the availability of a culturally adapted and psychometrically sound instrument expands opportunities for clinical research on chronic wounds within Persian‐speaking populations.

The results of this study support the integration of the Persian LUMT into routine wound care practice in Iran. Owing to its strong validity, reliability and predictive capability, the tool can be used confidently for baseline and follow‐up assessment of leg ulcers, enabling clinicians to document wound characteristics consistently and observe healing trajectories with greater accuracy. Its ability to identify patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes, including delayed healing and amputation, further strengthens its clinical relevance as an early warning indicator that can guide timely interventions and resource allocation. Moreover, the structured scoring system of the Persian LUMT offers a valuable educational framework for training wound care nurses and other healthcare professionals. Finally, the tool's standardised format enhances the quality of clinical data and supports research efforts aimed at improving chronic wound outcomes in Persian‐speaking populations.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several notable strengths. It represents the first Persian validation of a comprehensive leg ulcer assessment tool and employed rigorous methodological standards to ensure robust psychometric evaluation. In addition, the study evaluated not only the psychometric properties but also the prognostic accuracy of the instrument, thereby supporting its clinical applicability in predicting adverse outcomes such as amputation.

However, several limitations should be considered. The study used convenience sampling from a single hospital setting, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare environments or community‐based populations. Known‐groups validity was assessed only in patients with diabetic foot ulcers using the Wagner classification system; therefore, construct validity was not evaluated for venous, arterial, or mixed‐aetiology ulcers using diagnosis‐specific severity classifications. Inter‐rater reliability was assessed using only two evaluators, and the 72‐h interval used for intra‐rater reliability may have allowed minor wound changes despite the absence of major interventions. In addition, convergent validity was evaluated in a relatively small sample, and the number of complete‐healing events was limited, which may have affected the precision of some statistical estimates. Although external responsiveness was not formally assessed, the strong predictive validity of the Persian LUMT for clinically meaningful outcomes provides indirect evidence that the instrument is sensitive to clinically relevant differences in wound severity and prognosis. Moreover, only hospital‐based patients were included, leaving uncertainty about the tool's applicability in community or primary care settings.

Future studies should evaluate the Persian LUMT in larger and more diverse patient populations across multiple healthcare settings, including outpatient wound clinics, community‐based wound‐care services and primary healthcare centers. Additional studies are also needed to assess the instrument's responsiveness to clinical change over time and to determine minimal clinically important differences. Because the present study included only two evaluators and used a 72‐h interval for intra‐rater reliability assessment, future research should examine reliability using larger groups of assessors and same‐day repeated assessments under randomised conditions. Furthermore, the relatively small sample sizes used for some clinimetric analyses, particularly convergent validity and healing‐outcome prediction, should be addressed in future multicenter studies with larger samples. Finally, construct validity should be further investigated across different chronic wound etiologies using diagnosis‐specific severity classification systems for venous, arterial and mixed‐aetiology ulcers.
