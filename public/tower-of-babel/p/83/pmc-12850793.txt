Status of training in the use of lumen-apposing metal stents for therapeutic endoscopic ultrasound during advanced endoscopy fellowship: a survey questionnaire
Authors: Yasi Xiao, Maham Hayat, Abdul S Mohammed, Abdullah Abbasi, Sagar Pathak, Saurabh Chandan, Daryl Ramai, Deepanshu Jain, Muhammad K Hasan, Mustafa A Arain, Kambiz K Kadkhodayan, Natalie Cosgrove, Dennis Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsThe lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS) has revolutionized endoscopic ultrasound (EUS). However, there is a paucity of data on the optimal approaches to training on the use of LAMSs. This survey study aimed to evaluate the status of training in LAMSs among advanced endoscopy fellowship (AEF) trainees.MethodsAEF (academic year 2023-2024) trainees were invited to complete a 15-question survey on LAMS training. The questions included the following domains: demographics, exposure to LAMSs before AEF, number of observed and hands-on participation using LAMSs for EUS-guided pancreatic fluid collection drainage (EUS-PFCD), choledochoduodenostomy (EUS-CD), gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD), and gastrojejunostomy (EUS-GJ) and EUS-directed transgastric endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (EDGE). Trainees were asked about their level of confidence in performing these procedures independently upon completion of their AEF.ResultsForty-three of 92 trainees completed the survey. Trainees reported performing a median of 400 (interquartile range [IQR], 305-525) EUS and 405 (IQR, 345-500) endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography procedures. Most (88.4%) had exposure to hands-on LAMS training in explant/inanimate models during their AEF. More than one-half of trainees (23; 53.5%) reported hands-on participating in >10 EUS-PFCD cases but ≤2 for each of the non-pancreatic fluid collection drainage (PFCD) indications. Although most trainees (88.4%) reported feeling confident in their ability to perform EUS-PFCD independently, only 34.9% and 39.6% indicated being confident for EUS-GJ and EUS-CD, respectively. Trainees who were confident had a significantly greater mean number of hands-on cases during AEF training when compared with their nonconfident counterparts.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates significant variability in LAMS training exposure during AEF, with few hands-on opportunities for non-PFCD indications. Additional research is needed to establish optimal approaches to training in LAMS procedures.

Introduction

The landscape of advanced endoscopy continues to evolve as new technologies and techniques become available. Among these, endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided interventional procedures have undergone an exponential growth, catapulted in the last decade by the introduction of a revolutionary novel type of lumen-apposing metal stent (LAMS).1 The unique design of the LAMS, with bilateral flanges in a dumbbell configuration, has significantly facilitated transluminal drainage procedures by reducing the risk of leak/perforation and stent migration.2 Given LAMSs’ relative ease of use and antimigratory properties, we have continued to witness an expanding repertoire of both on-label and off-label applications, ranging from transluminal drainage of fluid collections to the creation of stable fistulas between adjacent cavities/structures.

Despite the increasingly widespread application of LAMSs, there is a paucity of data on the optimal approaches to procedural training and learning curves associated with the use of LAMSs.1 Although the focus of most advanced endoscopy fellowships (AEFs) remains on EUS and endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP), trainees continue to gain exposure to an ever-growing list of complex procedures, including LAMS placement in various settings.3 In the absence of defined competency criteria or regulatory oversight on the training provided by AEFs, there is limited information regarding the adequacy of training in therapeutic EUS procedures including LAMS placement. The aim of this study was to conduct a survey to evaluate the status of training in the use of LAMSs for therapeutic EUS procedures among AEF trainees.

Methods

Survey participants

AEF trainees (academic year 2023-2024) were invited to complete a 15-question survey on LAMS training during their fellowship. The electronic survey (Appendix 1) was created in docs.google.com. The link to the survey was embedded in the e-mail invitation. E-mail addresses were obtained via publicly available directories (ie, AEF program websites, gastroenterology societies, and industry-sponsored training courses) and provided by program directors from the American Society for Gastrointestinal Endoscopy (ASGE) AEF participating programs. A total of 92 e-mail addresses of trainees were obtained. An e-mail invitation was sent to these 92 AEF trainees to participate in the study, with a subsequent e-mail invitation sent biweekly to nonresponders for a total of 4 weeks. Subject completion of the survey served as their consent to participate. The survey study was launched between May 2024 and June 2024. The study was approved by the institutional review board at AdventHealth, Orlando, Florida, USA.

Design of the survey

The survey was designed to elicit information regarding the following areas:

•Trainee's demographics (gender, training setting [academic vs community-based hospital], geographic region of training [West Coast, Midwest, East Coast, South] number of EUS/ERCPs performed, previous experience with LAMSs).

Trainee's demographics (gender, training setting [academic vs community-based hospital], geographic region of training [West Coast, Midwest, East Coast, South] number of EUS/ERCPs performed, previous experience with LAMSs).

•AEF training in LAMSs, including observed and hands-on cases, in the following scenarios: EUS-guided pancreatic fluid collection drainage (EUS-PFCD), choledochoduodenostomy (EUS-CD), gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD), and gastrojejunostomy (EUS-GJ) and EUS-directed transgastric ERCP (EDGE).

AEF training in LAMSs, including observed and hands-on cases, in the following scenarios: EUS-guided pancreatic fluid collection drainage (EUS-PFCD), choledochoduodenostomy (EUS-CD), gallbladder drainage (EUS-GBD), and gastrojejunostomy (EUS-GJ) and EUS-directed transgastric ERCP (EDGE).

•Trainee's level of confidence in performing these procedures independently as an indirect measure of their perception of competence. In addition, trainees also were asked about any perceived barriers to LAMS training during fellowship.

Trainee's level of confidence in performing these procedures independently as an indirect measure of their perception of competence. In addition, trainees also were asked about any perceived barriers to LAMS training during fellowship.

Statistical analysis

All responses from the completed surveys were included for analysis. Responses were summarized descriptively, with categorical variables summarized with frequencies and proportions. Trainees were stratified on the basis of their confidence level in LAMS placement across the various clinical scenarios. Trainees who answered “fairly confident” or “very confident” for any given survey item were defined as confident, whereas those who answered “neutral,” “not very confident,” or “not confident at all” were defined as nonconfident. Two-sided χ2 analysis was used to detect differences for all categorical variables and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test to compare continuous variables. A P value <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study participants

Forty-three of the 92 AEF trainees completed the survey. Respondents' demographics are summarized in Table 1. Most respondents trained in a university-based hospital (36; 83.7%) and reported participating in a median of 400 (interquartile range [IQR], 305-525) EUS and 405 (IQR, 345-500) ERCP procedures during their fellowship. Before their AEF, 44.2% of trainees had observed cases with LAMS placement, whereas 9.3% and 20.9% reported hands-on experience on animal models and live patients, respectively.

Training with LAMSs during AEF

Nearly all trainees (38; 88.4%) indicated undergoing hands-on training with LAMSs in explant/inanimate models during their AEF (Table 2). Training opportunities with these models included industry-sponsored events to endoscopy courses or gastroenterology societal meetings. A low proportion of trainees reported not having observed LAMS placement for EUS-PFCD (2; 4.7%), EUS-CD (10; 23.3%), EUS-GBD (7; 16.3%), EUS-GJ (5; 11.6%), and EDGE (6; 14%), during their AEF. Nearly all trainees (42; 97.7) reported hands-on participation in at least 1 case of EUS-PFCD (median 14; IQR 5-18). Conversely, a significant number of trainees indicated having no hands-on participation in LAMS placement for the following indications: EUS-CD (22; 51.2%), EUS-GBD (14; 32.6%), EUS-GJ (20; 46.5%), and EDGE (14; 32.6%).

Level of confidence in the use of LAMSs and potential barriers to training

Figure 1 depicts the level of confidence among trainees at the end of their training in performing therapeutic EUS procedures using LAMSs. Most respondents reported feeling very/fairly confident in being able to perform EUS-PFCD (88.4%), EUS-GBD (60.5%), and EDGE (60.4%) independently. Conversely, only 34.9% and 39.6% indicated feeling very/fairly confident in performing EUS-GJ and EUS-CD, respectively. Trainees who were confident in performing LAMS placement had a significantly higher mean number of hands-on cases during AEF training, irrespective of the indication (Fig. 2).

Subgroup analysis: use of LAMSs for pancreatic fluid collection drainage versus non-pancreatic fluid collection drainage indications

A subgroup analysis was performed comparing participants' answers with regard to the use of LAMSs for pancreatic fluid collection drainage (PFCD) versus non-PFCD indications (EUS-CD, EDGE, EUS-GBD, EUS-GJ). There was a significantly greater proportion of trainees who indicated having undergone hands-on participation in >5 cases of LAMSs for PFCD versus non-PFCD indications: 27.9% versus 12.8% (P = .02). As such, it was not unexpected that more trainees indicated feeling fairly/very confident in terms of placing LAMSs for PFCD versus non-PFCD indications: 69.8% versus 16.9% (P < .0001).

Discussion

Despite the widespread adoption of LAMSs among endoscopy units and their expanding array of indications within the field of therapeutic EUS, there has been no systematic assessment regarding the status of training in LAMS procedures during AEF. This survey study demonstrated that the overall volume of cases using LAMSs for therapeutic EUS interventions remains relatively low for most AEF trainees, even in academic settings. Hands-on participation appears to have the greatest impact in determining a trainee's level of confidence in performing these procedures during independent practice. Although most trainees reported adequate training in EUS-PFCD, low volume of cases and lack of hands-on opportunities were perceived as the main barriers to training for LAMS placement for non-PFCD indications.

AEF programs initially were introduced to meet the need for training in ERCP and subsequently in EUS.3 Traditionally, training has been based on an apprenticeship model, with volume of cases commonly serving as an imperfect surrogate for competence. In the past several years, training programs have begun the transition from an apprenticeship and time-based model to a competency-based medical education model of training, requiring formal assessment of competence in ERCP and EUS using validated standardized assessment tools and performance milestones to determine readiness for independent practice.4, 5, 6

The practice of advanced endoscopy and its breadth of procedures continues to increase. Inevitably, trainees are faced with the daunting task of seeking and attaining proper training in these evolving technologies. It can be argued that no other device has accelerated the growth of therapeutic EUS as the introduction of LAMSs has over recent years. Hence, understanding the status of training in the use of LAMSs is imperative in ensuring the safety and quality of care of patients. Most of the participants in this study reported exposure to the use of LAMSs (either through case observations or hands-on on simulation models) before their AEF training. Importantly, nearly all the trainees (88.4%) indicated hands-on sessions using the LAMSs in explants/inanimate models during their AEF. These practices are in line with current ASGE recommendations on LAMS training, which advise individuals to seek additional education through hands-on dedicated LAMS courses, simulators, and industry vendor support.2 However, most of these models are primarily designed to simulate LAMS placement into a well-defined fluid collection and may not necessarily address some of the complexities associated with other indications, including EUS-CD and EUS-GJ. Novel viable training models are currently being investigated.7 Furthermore, although hands-on exposure with these models can be beneficial, most of these courses vary significantly in length, content, and structure, and most importantly, are not designed to ensure procedural competency in their present format. Hence, a standardized curriculum involving training in LAMSs during AEF is highly desirable, with the goal of enforcing the rigorous standards of training necessary to ensure readiness for independent practice.

Learning curve or competency assessment data on the use of LAMSs are surprisingly scarce. In a survey study by Teoh et al8 on EUS-PFCD, a minimum number of 10 procedures was proposed by 68% of the participants as the minimum threshold to gain competency. A subsequent study on EUS-GB indicated that efficiency was attained after 19 cases.9 Additional single-provider studies on EDGE and EUS-GJ have suggested procedural efficiency being attained after the 9th and 25th procedures, respectively.10,11 However, there are several major drawbacks when attempting to interpret these data, including the marked variability in technique (ie, cold vs electrocautery-enhanced LAMS), type of stent (ie, plastic, LAMS), and procedural indications. Notably, these studies primarily focused on procedure time as central determinants to learning curves, when arguably other parameters, including clinical success and adverse event rate, may be more reflective of proficiency and relevant to clinical practice. Importantly, information from these studies is derived from the experience of expert therapeutic EUS endoscopists and thereby can be difficult to extrapolate for LAMS training among beginners. Our study is the first, to our knowledge, to evaluate the status of training in the use of LAMSs among AEF trainees. There are several important takeaway messages from our results. For one, the overall number of procedures using LAMSs in which the trainees were involved (observation and/or hands-on participation) was relatively low, particularly when excluding cases for PFCD. Although one-half of the trainees (53.5%) reported hands-on involvement in >10 cases of PFCD, only between 11.6% and 18.6% of the respondents indicated hands-on participation in >5 cases for non-PFCD indications. Indeed, between 32.6% and 51.2% of the participants reported no hands-on training with LAMSs for non-PFCD indications. Not unexpectedly, our study demonstrated that hands-on volume was directly associated with the trainees' level of confidence in performing the procedure independently. In aggregate, the median number of cases with LAMSs for PFCDs reported by trainees was 14 as compared to a median of 0 to 2 for non-PFCDs indications. Hence, nearly all the respondents (88.4%) reported feeling confident in their ability to complete PFCD with LAMSs, as compared with a lower proportion (34.9% to 60.5%) for non-PFCD indications. Despite the limitations of absolute case volumes as a marker for competence, thresholds still hold value as they provide a reference to training programs as to the minimum number of procedures they should offer trainees and when they can realistically begin to make cumulative skill assessment. In all, our study suggests that the number of opportunities for LAMS training during AEF, particularly for indications beyond PFCD, appears to be limited, and comprehensive training within the constraints of a standard 12-month AEF can be challenging. With this in mind, the responses from this survey on case volume and the participants' perceptions on the adequacy of their training may help provide volume thresholds for programs to determine whether these are procedures for which they can realistically offer training. We suggest that procedural volumes, particularly those in which the trainee has been involved with hands-on participation, should be recorded and reported by each institution on the ASGE AEF match website. Notably, for highly specialized procedures in which volume of cases is low, postgraduate training will be necessary to safeguard best practices. There is an urgent need to identify and establish ongoing training pathways for those individuals seeking proficiency in these highly specialized procedures.

The study is not without limitations. The study may have not included all trainees and ASGE AEF programs despite our efforts to distribute the survey tool through various venues. Although a significant proportion of trainees responded to the survey, it is possible that a larger sample size may have resulted in additional responses achieving statistical significance. Furthermore, the survey did not inquire the participants about their purpose for pursuing a fourth year of training. It is conceivable that a proportion of trainees may not have been interested in performing high-level/complex therapeutic EUS procedures, which may have affected the overall results. The study is subject to the limitations associated with survey questionnaires, primarily the potential for recall and reporting bias. We should also acknowledge that the survey is only able to capture trainees' perceptions and aptitudes on several aspects of their training, including their opinion regarding their own readiness on the use of LAMSs in clinical practice. In a previous study, we demonstrated how self-reported readiness for independent practice in endoscopic procedures may not necessarily correlate with competence based on performance evaluation.12 Hence, future prospective studies in which AEF trainees' procedural volume and progress are systematically monitored and tracked are urgently needed to further determine the status of LAMS training.

In conclusion, the use of LAMSs has become an indispensable component in therapeutic EUS. Our study demonstrates significant variability in training exposure among AEF trainees, with low case volume for the use of LAMSs in non-PFCD indications. Trainees' confidence in adequately performing these procedures in independent practice was directly associated with hands-on training opportunities. Additional research is needed to establish optimal approaches to training in LAMS procedures, including defining competence criteria and standardization of training to safeguard best practices in this growing field.
