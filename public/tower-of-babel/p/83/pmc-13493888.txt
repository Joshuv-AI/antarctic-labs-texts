Bootstrapping multimodal large language model with medical knowledge for automatic esophagogastroduodenoscopy diagnosis and reporting
Authors: Miaojing Shi, Zijie Yue, Huihui Sun, Bo Li, Yikun Ma, Tianyu Cen, Ying Chen, Xiaofen Wu, Hanli Wang, Jun Wang, Shuchang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEsophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) is an essential clinical procedure for diagnosing gastrointestinal (GI) diseases, while the subsequent EGD reports play a vital role in clinical decision-making and therapeutic interventions. This paper makes the first attempt to bootstrap Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLM) with medical knowledge for EGD diagnosis and reporting (EDR). We collected the largest multicentric EGD dataset so far, containing 4461 participants with 203,838 EGD images and 4461 corresponding EGD reports. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method, MLLM-EDR, achieves an average diagnostic accuracy of 0.882 for nineteen GI diseases, outperforming both state-of-the-art AI models (0.720) and junior endoscopists (0.784). The completeness and facticity scores of our generated reports match the quality of those created by senior endoscopists. Moreover, our method substantially reduces the endoscopists’ workload from 7 minutes to 13.48 seconds. These results highlight the vigor and significance of MLLM-EDR for AI-assisted EGD diagnostic and reporting applications.Subject terms: Machine learning, Gastrointestinal cancer, Image processing, Stomach diseases

Introduction

Gastrointestinal (GI) diseases (e.g., gastric cancer, duodenal ulcer, and reflux esophagitis) are among the most prevalent diseases worldwide, affecting approximately 20% of the global population each year1–4. Esophagogastroduodenoscopy (EGD) serves as an essential clinical procedure for diagnosing GI diseases5. Within the EGD workflows, endoscopists meticulously examine each anatomic site (e.g., esophagus, cardia, duodenum) in the GI tract and document their observations with detailed text descriptions6. These descriptions correspond to the “Examination findings” section of the EGD reports7. Based on them, endoscopists diagnose background gastric mucosae and potential GI diseases to write the “Examination conclusion” section of the report. Given that the global incidence of gastric and esophageal cancer exceeds 1.4 million every year and continues to rise significantly2, EGD reports are crucial for determining therapeutic strategies, making clinical recommendations, and assessing therapeutic efficacy8. Nevertheless, it has been reported that the “Examination conclusion” in EGD reports has only 81% specificity and 85% sensitivity9; in particular, the misdiagnosis rate of early gastric cancer and early esophageal cancer is approximately 25%10 by endoscopists with less than 10 years of clinical experience. Furthermore, endoscopists often invest a substantial amount of time (i.e., an average of 7 min11, almost 67.2% time of the entire EGD workflow12) into the EGD report writing. This labor-intensive work largely distracts endoscopists from patient care13. Studies have also reported a low completeness of these manually generated reports14: Boys et al. 15 reported that only 42% of EGD reports have completely described the lesion details; Barbetta et al. 16 reported that 66% of EGD reports have missed descriptions of some lesions. All in all, there is an urgent need for an automatic EGD diagnosis and reporting method to alleviate the workload of endoscopists, improve diagnostic reliability and reporting completeness, and especially benefit endoscopists who struggle with the intricacies of report writing.

Artificial intelligence (AI) techniques have demonstrated remarkable performance in various EGD image analysis tasks, including anatomic site identification17 and polyp detection18. Notwithstanding, the task of automatic EGD diagnosis and reporting remains largely unexplored. A few studies attempt to decompose this task into multiple subtasks12,19, such as site identification, lesion detection, and disease classification. Each is addressed by training a separate deep neural network (DNN), while their outputs are subsequently aggregated into a structured template to produce the final EGD report. For instance, Zhang et al. 12 employed twenty distinct DNNs (e.g., VGG-1620, ResNet-5021) to handle twenty EGD image analysis subtasks, such as mucosal visibility assessment and gastric lesion classification. The results are integrated into pre-defined templates to produce the reports. While these modular approaches have demonstrated a certain degree of automation, they suffer from severe limitations. First, the efficiency is notably poor due to the overhead of running multiple DNNs, making them unsuitable for real-time clinical applications. Second, the completeness and facticity of the generated reports are dependent on the performance of each individual DNN. Errors in any model can propagate through the overall system, reducing the reliability and clinical efficacy of the final report. Third, the reliance on structured templates limits their flexibility to generate contextually rich descriptions for complex cases.

So far, existing AI methods for EGD diagnosis and reporting remain far from being trusted and readily used in clinical practice. Except for aforementioned technical issues, the key reason lies in the fact that they rely solely on visual cues in EGD images to make diagnosis, while visual cues themselves are often insufficient to support reliable judgements. In contrast, endoscopists, as illustrated in Fig. 1, make diagnosis based on both visual cues and their extensive medical knowledge. The latter, accumulated over years of training and experience, often plays a decisive role in the diagnosis. For instance, when encountering a lesion with mucosal break and surrounding redness in the gastric antrum, an AI model may simply classify it as a gastric ulcer; in contrast, an experienced endoscopist, being aware that similar features can also appear in early gastric cancer, would further investigate into details such as the presence of irregular or slightly elevated margins, or the absence of fibrin coating, to make the final prediction. Hence, how to effectively integrate the medical knowledge originally stored in endoscopists’ minds or summarized in medical textbooks into the AI model is critical to the ultimate success of automatic EGD diagnosis and reporting; and this is previously untouched.

Overview of endoscopists, existing AI methods, and MLLM-EDR for EGD diagnosis and reporting.

Endoscopists perform diagnosis and reporting based on both observations from EGD image series and reasoning from medical knowledge. Existing unimodal AI methods rely solely on visual cues from single inputs. In contrast, our proposed MLLM-EDR emulates the diagnostic process of endoscopists by leveraging multimodal information (i.e., visual cues from image series and medical knowledge in text prompts) to enhance the accuracy of EGD diagnosis and reporting.

Recently, the advent of multimodal large language models (MLLMs), especially the Vision-Language Models, showcases a strong encoding capability of both vision and language inputs and thereby paves a new way for jointly leveraging them for downstream tasks. When MLLM meets automatic EGD diagnosis and reporting, we introduce MLLM-EDR, the first approach to bootstrap MLLM with medical knowledge for EGD diagnosis and reporting. The input to MLLM-EDR consists of a series of EGD images captured under EGD, while the output is a comprehensive report consisting of both “Examination findings” and “Examination conclusion” sections. The medical knowledge is prompted from the large language models (LLMs), which have been pretrained on resources including a vast body of medical textbooks and clinical literature. MLLM-EDR pioneers the use of medical knowledge of nineteen representative GI diseases (e.g., reflux esophagitis, duodenal ulcer, erosion) derived from an LLM and verified by expert endoscopists to facilitate the GI disease diagnosis; and designs novel multimodal prompts that integrate visual embeddings with specific instructions to guide MLLM to auto-regressively generate the descriptions of the findings. Overall, MLLM-EDR is constructed in a multitask learning framework to simultaneously cope with multiple EGD image analysis tasks, including anatomic site identification, background gastric mucosa classification, GI disease diagnosis, and text description generation.

The evaluation of MLLM-EDR is conducted on 203,838 EGD images and 4461 EGD reports collected from four hospitals in China. Experimental results on the held-out test set, three external test sets, and a prospective test set demonstrate that MLLM-EDR significantly outperforms existing methods and endoscopists. For example, on the held-out test set, the accuracy comparison across nineteen GI diseases is 0.882 (MLLM-EDR) vs. 0.720 (state-of-the-art AI method) vs. 0.784 (junior endoscopists) vs. 0.868 (senior endoscopists). Notably, it significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy for early gastric cancer from 0.650 (state-of-the-art AI method), 0.746 (junior endoscopists), and 0.829 (senior endoscopists) to 0.926 (MLLM-EDR), and for early esophageal cancer from 0.693 (state-of-the-art AI method), 0.868 (junior endoscopists), and 0.932 (senior endoscopists) to 0.944 (MLLM-EDR). The improvements over existing methods can be attributed to MLLM-EDR’s multimodal reasoning ability. Unlike existing unimodal AI methods that rely solely on visual cues, MLLM-EDR leverages additional medical knowledge embedded in disease-specific text prompts to improve diagnostic performance. MLLM-EDR produces detailed and comprehensive descriptions for lesions, mucosae, and anatomic sites. The completeness and facticity of the generated reports match the quality of those created by senior endoscopists, with completeness (out of 10) being 8.81 (MLLM-EDR) vs. 8.93 (senior endoscopists) and facticity (out of 10) being 8.46 (MLLM-EDR) vs. 8.38 (senior endoscopists). Furthermore, MLLM-EDR substantially reduces the endoscopists’ manual workload for EGD reporting from 7 min11 to 13.48 s. Last but not least, we develop an AI-assisted workflow to utilize our model as an auxiliary tool to support endoscopists in clinical practice. The results reveal that, with the assistance of MLLM-EDR, junior endoscopists can perform even beyond those unaided senior endoscopists, and this workflow takes only 30.03 s for the whole EGD diagnosis and reporting process. All above results suggest the great potential of MLLM-EDR to streamline EGD workflows and improve diagnostic accuracy.

Results

Data resources

This study involves 4461 participants with 203,838 EGD images and 4461 corresponding EGD reports collected from four hospitals: Shanghai Tongji Hospital of Tongji University, Xinhua Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Ruijin Hospital Affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, Huashan Hospital Fudan University. The participant recruitment process is depicted in Supplementary Fig. 1. To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest EGD dataset so far that includes EGD images, detailed text descriptions and comprehensive diagnostic results. Specifically, the primary data comes from Tongji Hospital, termed as TJ-EGD, which comprises 118,802 EGD images and 2631 corresponding clinical reports from 2631 participants (each participant has at least 6 EGD images), encompassing six representative background gastric mucosa types and nineteen representative GI diseases. TJ-EGD dataset is randomly divided into training and held-out test sets at the participant level, following a 70:30 split ratio. Clinical data collected from the other three hospitals form three external test sets, which are referred to as external test set A, external test set B, and external test set C. They contain 2832, 36,932, and 15,457 images from 472, 594, and 241 participants, respectively. The TJ-EGD dataset and three external test sets are collected between August 2023 and September 2024. As shown in Fig. 2, we train our proposed MLLM-EDR on the training set of TJ-EGD, and its performance is subsequently evaluated on both the held-out test set and three external test sets. Moreover, we also conduct prospective validation on a prospective test set collected from Shanghai Tongji Hospital of Tongji University between October 2024 and January 2025, which includes 29,815 images from 523 participants. Detailed data distributions by background gastric mucosa types and GI diseases are presented in Table 1. Additional dataset details can be found in the Methods section.

Model performance

Given EGD images, the generated reports by our MLLM-EDR consist of two sections: “Examination findings” and “Examination conclusion”. The “Examination findings” section provides generated text descriptions of the visual features observed in the EGD images, for which we report natural language generation (NLG) metrics, including BLEU and ROUGE, as presented in Table 2. These metrics measure the similarity between the generated and ground truth (reference) descriptions by counting the matching n-grams (i.e., word overlap)22. The “Examination conclusion” section contains the background gastric mucosa classification results and GI disease diagnosis results, for which we report clinical efficacy metrics, including accuracy (Acc), specificity (Spe), sensitivity (Sen), and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), as shown in Table 3 and Table 4. We compare MLLM-EDR with five baseline models, referred to as UM-Base1, UM-Base2, UM-Base3, UM-Base4, and UM-Base5. The first three models are adapted from R2Gen23, XrayGPT24, and Jing et al. 25. While UM-Base4 and UM-Base5 are advanced transformer-based EGD image classification models26,27. Since UM-Base4 and UM-Base5 do not support text generation, we only report their performance on the background gastric mucosa classification and GI disease diagnosis tasks. Notice all these methods are unimodal (vision input only) DNN approaches and can only process a single image at a time. We run them repeatedly over the image series of each patient to generate the final reports. We follow the common practice in existing studies to directly evaluate both our model and the baselines on the external test sets without any fine-tuning or re-training3,28. Details can be found in the Methods section.

Text description generation

First, as shown in Table 2, MLLM-EDR shows excellent performance in the quality of the generated text descriptions on the held-out test set, significantly outperforming baseline models across two NLG metrics. For instance, it achieves 16.8% improvement in BLEU (P < 0.001) and 16.6% improvement in ROUGE (P < 0.001) than the best baseline model, UM-Base2. The BLEU score of 0.733 indicates MLLM-EDR’s effectiveness in capturing both surface-level lexical matches and deeper syntactic structures when compared to reference reports. The ROUGE score of 0.800 further confirms the high degree of overlap between the generated and reference reports, ensuring the narrative flow and content consistency in the generated descriptions. These improvements can be attributed to MLLM-EDR’s multimodal reasoning ability. By incorporating disease-specific text prompts, MLLM-EDR can leverage their embedded medical knowledge to attend the extracted visual features based on their clinical relevance. For example, when MLLM-EDR identifies localized protrusions with reddish coloration and well-defined margins, the medical knowledge helps the model recognize these visual patterns as indicative of a polyp and guides it to describe key attributes such as color, shape, and texture. This enables the model to generate more accurate, informative, and complete descriptions than single-image, unimodal baselines. Second, as shown in Table 2, MLLM-EDR also improves the baselines by large margins on three external test sets. For example, MLLM-EDR achieves an average BLEU improvement of 23.0% and an average ROUGE improvement of 21.0% on external test set B. These results demonstrate that MLLM-EDR maintains a strong generalization ability across diverse clinical settings.

GI disease diagnosis

Table 4 presents the performance of GI disease diagnosis in the “Examination Conclusion” section of the generated EGD reports. MLLM-EDR demonstrates a clear advantage over baseline models. On the held-out test set, the average diagnostic accuracy for nineteen GI diseases is 0.882 for MLLM-EDR, which significantly outperforms that (0.720, P < 0.001) of the previously best-performing AI model, UM-Base4. The improvement is further manifested on the external test sets, for instance, on the external test set B, MLLM-EDR substantially improves over UM-Base4 by 12.4% on accuracy (P = 0.002), 6.1% on specificity (P = 0.019), 17.4% on sensitivity (P < 0.001), and 6.2% on AUC (P = 0.016). The reasons behind this performance improvement can be summarized as two-fold. On one hand, these models are limited to making diagnosis based on a single EGD image only, which can miss critical information that often appears across multiple EGD image views. In contrast, MLLM-EDR is specifically designed to jointly analyze multiple EGD images from different angles or positions within the GI tract. This enables the model to aggregate complementary visual cues across different views, leading to a comprehensive understanding of the lesion’s attributes. On the other hand, the substantial performance gains can also be attributed to MLLM-EDR’s utilization of multimodal features. Specifically, it incorporates medical knowledge embedded in disease-specific text prompts to help distinguish between visually similar abnormalities, resulting in clinically trustworthy diagnosis. Figure 4e–h illustrates the specific diagnosis performance by MLLM-EDR for nineteen GI diseases on the held-out test set and three external test sets. The best performance is observed for early esophageal cancer, with an accuracy of 0.944 on the held-out test set. Moreover, it achieves an accuracy of 0.926 for early gastric cancer, markedly surpassing the reported 0.750 accuracy for endoscopists’ diagnosis10. The superior performance observed in this task suggests the superiority of MLLM-EDR to capture fine-grained visual patterns from EGD images to improve diagnostic accuracy and reliability in clinical practice.

We measure the average report generation time for both the endoscopists and MLLM-EDR. According to previous studies11, an endoscopist writing an EGD report from scratch can take up to 7 min on average. While in the clinical practice we conducted, endoscopists were asked to complete the reports by simply filling key information into the predefined template in the hospital’s electronic medical record (EMR) system, which can already considerably reduce the reporting time. We further design an AI-assisted EGD diagnosis and reporting workflow suitable for clinical practice. To evaluate the effectiveness of this workflow, we conduct a case study based on the held-out test set. Eight junior endoscopists and four senior endoscopists participate in this study, utilizing this workflow to generate EGD reports. To verify this, we additionally collected 47 EGD videos from Shanghai Tongji Hospital of Tongji University and evaluated the trained MLLM-EDR (from image sequences) on them. Robustness on different devices

The EGD images used in this study were collected from two devices, Olympus CV-260 and CV-290. We further evaluated our method across these two devices to assess its robustness. Specifically, we divide the held-out test into two subgroups based on their devices (586 cases for CV-260 and 206 cases for CV-290) and report the results in Supplementary Fig. 4. We observe that MLLM-EDR performs slightly better on CV-290 than on CV-260 across all three tasks (i.e., text description generation, background gastric mucosa classification, and GI disease diagnosis). For example, for GI disease diagnosis, MLLM-EDR improves by 2.1% in accuracy, 2.2% in specificity, and 2.0% in sensitivity on the CV-290 subgroup compared with CV-260. This is likely because CV-290 has improved image processing techniques (e.g., reduced noise/halation and enhanced color contrast), providing higher-quality EGD images that help the model extract more discriminative visual features. Importantly, although performance on CV-260 is slightly lower than on CV-290, it remains strong and still significantly outperforms existing methods. In the future, we will further evaluate and improve the applicability of our approach to additional EGD devices from different manufacturers.

Parameter analysis

The training configurations of our method were validated through cross-validation to obtain the best-performing setting. We provide the ablations on the number of designed interaction blocks, the learning rate, the training batch size, the data augmentation operations, and the Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)-based finetuning paradigm for MLLM on the held-out test set.

We first investigate the number of interaction blocks in Supplementary Table 13. This block incorporates medical knowledge embedded in disease-specific text prompts to guide the model to differentiate between diseases with visually similar characteristics. We denote Interaction block (N=1) as a variant of our method with one interaction block; the other rows are defined similarly. We observe that performance improves as the number of interaction blocks increases and becomes largely stable when N≥3. Considering the additional computational cost introduced by more blocks, we set N=3 as the default configuration.

Second, we vary the learning rate from 10−5 to 9×10−5. The results are shown in Supplementary Table 14. We observe that the model achieves the best performance when the learning rate is 5×10−5, which is our default setting.

Third, we adjust the batch size from 8 to 24. The results are shown in Supplementary Table 15. We observe that the model performs the best when the batch size is 16, which is our default setting.

Fourth, we denote MLLM-EDR w/o aug as a variant of MLLM-EDR without using the augmentation operations. The performance comparison is reported in Supplementary Tables 16 and 17. We observe that removing augmentation leads to a clear performance drop, especially for relatively low-prevalence diseases (e.g., DU (A1), DU (A2), GAU (A1), GAU (A2), GBP). These results indicate that the augmentation operations are important for improving diagnosis performance.

Fifth, we report the model performance without using the LoRA-based finetuning paradigm in Supplementary Table 18 and Supplementary Fig. 5, where performance drops significantly, confirming that finetuning MLLM is necessary to achieve high report quality. Moreover, we follow common practice31,32 to set the LoRA rank as r=16 and the LoRA scale factor as s=2. In Supplementary Tables 19 and 20, we can observe that the performance remains rather stable across different settings, with the best occurs when r=16 and s=2.

Failure analysis

To specifically analyze the cases when the model fails to produce correct GI disease diagnosis, two expert endoscopists conduct an in-depth examination of the incorrectly predicted cases in the held-out test set. The reasons can be summarized into two main factors: First, the main factor is the quality of the EGD images. During the EGD procedure, the presence of food residues, body fluids, or other obstructions in the GI tract can obscure lesions and mucosal surfaces. This occlusion makes it difficult for the model to extract effective visual features. This often leads to incorrect diagnosis, as the model struggles to discern critical lesion details from occluded or unclear images. Second, some lesions exhibit very subtle visual characteristics in their early stages33, making them inherently difficult to identify. For example, early-stage polyps may present as only slight elevations, which can be difficult to detect even for expert endoscopists. The lack of prominent visual features in such cases can lead the model to misclassify them as normal mucosa or overlook them entirely.

Discussion

EGD plays a prominent role in GI disease screening34. Accurate and timely documenting of EGD findings and diagnostic conclusions in EGD reports is essential for patient care35. However, endoscopists normally have to manually record all observations, particularly the details of lesions, during or immediately after EGD procedures36,37. In recent years, several studies were proposed to tackle this labor-intensive process using multiple deep learning-based classification models12,19. However, these solutions are limited to processing one image at a time and lack the multimodal ability to incorporate relevant medical knowledge to support diagnostic reasoning. Moreover, they are inevitably inefficient due to the overhead running multiple models; also, the reliance on structured templates limits their flexibility, not to mention generating contextually rich descriptions for complex cases.

This study presents MLLM-EDR, the first approach to bootstrap MLLM with medical knowledge for EGD diagnosis and reporting. We are the first to train MLLMs to simultaneously address the background gastric mucosa classification, GI disease diagnosis, and text description generation tasks. As demonstrated in Tables 2–4, MLLM-EDR achieves exceptional performance in generating clinically accurate EGD reports, significantly outperforming baselines by a large margin.

Our disease selection for nineteen representative GI diseases follows two criteria: clinical prevalence and clinical significance. First, we include the sixteen most frequently encountered GI diseases (Table 1, excluding early esophageal cancer and early/advanced gastric cancer) in routine EGD examinations according to clinical statistics38–41. For example, the prevalence rate is approximately 9.51% for gastric polyps40, 16.6% for reflux esophagitis, 4.6% for gastric/duodenal ulcers39, and 8% for gastric erosion41. We select these diseases to address high-frequency clinical needs in routine clinical practice. Second, gastric cancer and esophageal cancer are two of the most important and prevalent malignant carcinomas worldwide, with approximately 1.5 million new cases annually42–44. The 5-year survival rate for these cancers ranges from 5% to 25% in their advanced stages, but can reach up to 90% in early stages43. Therefore, timely and accurate diagnosis of these cancers remains a significant challenge in clinical practice, especially with a reported diagnostic accuracy of only 60% for early-stage cancers45. Therefore, we additionally select these cancers in our study to enhance the potential clinical benefit of our proposed method. Figure 3d illustrates two instances where endoscopists made misdiagnosis. This difficulty arises because the lesions often present only subtle mucosal changes, which require endoscopists to be well trained and armed with a thorough medical knowledge33. To overcome this challenge, our proposed MLLM-EDR integrates prior medical knowledge derived from the LLM to improve the diagnosis performance for both cancers, as well as other sixteen representative GI diseases. This prior knowledge guides the model to differentiate between diseases with visually similar characteristics, such as distinguishing the edge patterns of duodenal bulb ulcers with different levels. As demonstrated in Fig. 5e, MLLM-EDR outperforms both junior and senior endoscopists for most diseases, with particularly notable improvements for early gastric cancer (accuracy: 0.926 vs. 0.746 vs. 0.829, sensitivity: 0.966 vs. 0.713 vs. 0.821) and early esophageal cancer (accuracy: 0.944 vs. 0.868 vs. 0.932, sensitivity: 0.991 vs. 0.813 vs. 0.916) on the held-out test set. As shown in Fig. 5a, it also surpasses most junior endoscopists and demonstrates highly competitive performance to senior endoscopists in terms of overall report Completeness (8.81 vs. 8.01 vs. 8.92) and Facticity (8.46 vs. 7.33 vs. 8.38). These results highlight the importance of MLLM-EDR in detecting early-stage cancers.

The quality of EGD reports is often influenced by clinical experience and potential memory lapses46. Expert endoscopists, with their accumulated experience, tend to be more sensitive and responsive to abnormalities observed during procedures12. However, in clinical practice, the majority of EGDs are performed by junior endoscopists who may lack the expertise required for comprehensive reporting and accurate diagnosis. To address this gap, we design an AI-assisted EGD diagnosis and reporting workflow to support junior endoscopists. As shown in Fig. 5b, d, e, compared with the traditional workflow, the AI-assisted workflow shows significant improvements in report completeness (8.99 vs. 8.31), background gastric mucosa classification accuracy (0.914 vs. 0.885), and GI disease diagnosis accuracy (0.863 vs. 0.784). This workflow not only enables junior endoscopists to produce high-quality EGD reports consistently but also allows them to gain valuable experience and improves their skills under the guidance of an AI-assisted system.

In our study, we apply a standard data curation protocol commonly required for training robust medical AI models, where cases with incomplete EGD reports or fewer than six EGD images are excluded. In fact, after the model is trained, our model still has the ability to handle the above rare cases at testing time. For instance, we evaluate MLLM-EDR on a subset of cases that were originally excluded from the training set because each case contains fewer than six images. This subset is denoted as imperfect test set. The results shown in Supplementary Table 22 indicate that performance on this subset is comparable to that on the original held-out test set. Specifically, for text generation, the BLEU score is 0.733 (held-out) vs. 0.725 (imperfect). For anatomic sites that are not covered by the input images, the model typically reports them as not observed. For background gastric mucosa classification, the accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity are 0.919, 0.948, and 0.890 on the imperfect subset versus 0.932, 0.957, and 0.911 on the held-out test set, respectively. For GI disease diagnosis, the accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity are 0.877, 0.922, and 0.831 on the imperfect subset versus 0.882, 0.931, and 0.831 on the held-out test set, respectively. These results suggest that MLLM-EDR maintains robust performance even when the input image set is incomplete. In fact, in routine clinical EGD examinations, procedures are typically recorded as videos or sequences of images (tens to hundreds). Therefore, acquiring fewer than six images is highly unlikely under standard clinical protocols. The presence of such cases in our dataset is mainly due to data storage issues (e.g., incomplete data saving or transmission errors) rather than clinical practice.

There are also several limitations to this study. First, the EGD images are mostly RGB images, as they are commonly used in clinical practice. Hence, our model has not been optimized to analyze other image modalities, such as ultrasound images, CT, and MRI. Our future work will focus on extending the model’s capability to integrate information from multiple modalities to further improve its performance. Second, we are constrained to collect data only from hospitals in China because EGD datasets with large-scale EGD images and reports are not publicly available. Moreover, cross-border data sharing is difficult because of the patient privacy and regulatory requirements. However, this study still has global generalizability because the GI diseases included in it (e.g., reflux esophagitis, gastric/duodenal ulcer, erosions, and polyps) are widely recognized in international clinical practice38–41, and their diagnostic criteria are largely consistent across regions47. Therefore, although the patient population originates from a single country, the visually diagnostic cues that the model has learned are not country/race-specific. Moreover, the EGD devices in this study (Olympus CV-260 and CV-290) are widely deployed in routine EGD examinations worldwide43, which further supports the applicability of our approach. Third, this study concentrates on the immediate improvement in the quality of the report, and further investigation should focus on exploring the benefit of the model on patient prognosis, such as its assistance to the follow-up treatment plans. Fourth, this study assessed whether model performance is consistent across key demographic cohorts (age and sex). In the future, we will explore more factors (e.g., occupation and body mass index) and conduct studies with richer metadata to further evaluate and improve generalizability.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji University (No. tjdxsr2025041) and Tongji Hospital of Tongji University (No. SBKT-2022-301). Given the non-invasive nature of the methodology and the anonymization of all data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the relevant institutional review boards.

Data collection

We consecutively collected 121,451 original EGD images and 2849 EGD reports from 2849 participants at the Department of Gastroenterology, Tongji Hospital of Tongji University, between August 2023 and September 2024 to construct the TJ-EGD dataset. In this procedure, we set reliable inclusion criteria made by three expert endoscopists: participants aged 18 years or older with histologically confirmed diagnoses of six specific types of background gastric mucosa or nineteen specific GI diseases are eligible for this study. Each participant has various numbers of EGD images and a corresponding EGD report. Each report includes an “Examination findings” section and an “Examination conclusion” section. The former details the endoscopist’s observations across eight anatomic sites of the GI tract: esophagus, cardia, gastric fundus, gastric body, gastric angle, gastric antrum, duodenum bulb, and descending part of the duodenum. It is manually documented by a senior endoscopist in the Electronic Medical Record System. While the latter includes diagnostic conclusions regarding background gastric mucosa and GI diseases based on either pathology results (e.g., early esophageal cancer, advanced gastric cancer, and early gastric cancer) or the senior endoscopists’ diagnosis (e.g., reflux esophagitis, gastric erosion, gastric antrum polyp), as summarized in Supplementary Table 23. All images are captured by Olympus CV-260 and Olympus CV-290 devices during clinical procedures. We apply a standard data curation protocol that is commonly required for training robust medical AI models12,48,49:

Incomplete vision-language pairs may introduce noise during model training. If a case contains an EGD report with complete descriptions of the eight anatomic sites but only a limited number of EGD images are available (e.g., corresponding to only five anatomic sites), enforcing vision-language alignment would confuse the model because it cannot generate descriptions for absent sites. Conversely, if the image set is sufficient but the report is incomplete (e.g., missing descriptions for some lesions), enforcing vision-language alignment would bias the model learning to incorrectly consider the extracted lesion features as normal findings because of the absence of corresponding descriptions. Therefore, we excluded cases with insufficient images and incomplete reports from model training. Specifically, we first excluded participants with fewer than 6 EGD images. This was implemented by directly counting the number of images per case and removing cases that did not meet the threshold. Second, we excluded participants with incomplete or incorrect EGD reports. Two expert endoscopists independently reviewed each report from three aspects. (i) They checked whether the text descriptions were consistent with the visual content of corresponding EGD images, and cases with obvious inconsistency were excluded. (ii) They checked whether the report missed clinically important information, such as missing descriptions for an anatomic site or missing key findings (e.g., a polyp observed in images but not documented). (iii) They checked whether the “Examination conclusion” section, including the labels for background gastric mucosa and GI diseases, was correct. If either endoscopist identified an issue, the case was excluded.

The prospective test set is collected from Tongji Hospital of Tongji University between October 2024 and January 2025 through the following process. The inclusion criteria for the prospective validation study are new participants over the age of 18 who need to undergo EGD examination. Before the procedure, we fully inform participants of the content of the prospective study. Only after they agree to participate in the study and sign the informed consent form can their EGD images and corresponding reports be collected. A total of 32,540 EGD images and 571 reports are initially collected. After applying the exclusion criteria, the remaining 29,815 EGD images and 523 reports are included to construct the prospective test set.

Moreover, we additionally conduct a label re-assessment study. Specifically, we randomly sample 300 cases from these datasets and invite two expert endoscopists (>8 years of clinical experience) to respectively make diagnosis for these cases. We then quantify inter-rater agreement using Cohen’s kappa. The resulting agreement for background gastric mucosa is 0.93 (Expert 1 vs. ground truth), 0.96 (Expert 2 vs. ground truth), and 0.94 (Expert 1 vs. Expert 2). Correspondingly, the agreement for GI diseases is 0.91 (Expert 1 vs. ground truth), 0.93 (Expert 2 vs. ground truth), and 0.92 (Expert 1 vs. Expert 2). These results indicate a high level of inter-rater consistency and support the reliability of the ground-truth labels used in this study.

Baseline models

We include five baseline models into our experimental comparison, as presented in Tables 2–4. Specifically, UM-Base1, UM-Base2, and UM-Base3 refer to three state-of-the-art medical report generation methods: R2Gen23, XrayGPT24, and Jing et al. 25. These methods were originally designed for text generation rather than classification. Therefore, we keep their original vision encoders and add two lightweight classification heads on top of the encoders for background gastric mucosa classification and GI disease diagnosis, respectively. This adaptation enables a fair comparison with our method across all three tasks. Moreover, we additionally include two more advanced transformer-based EGD image classification models for comparison26,27, which are denoted as UM-Base4 and UM-Base5. Note that these methods cannot be directly applied to our task, as they can only process a single image at a time, while our task requires handling a series of EGD images. Therefore, we have to embed them into visual features through the model and then aggregate them to produce the final report. Specifically, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 7, given a series of EGD images for a case, a baseline model first performs the forward pass on each EGD image separately to extract the visual feature using a shared encoder. These per-image features are then aggregated (via concatenation) to form a case-level visual representation, which is subsequently fed into the text decoder and classification heads (i.e., linear layers) for text description generation, background gastric mucosa classification, and GI disease diagnosis. The entire pipeline is trained in an end-to-end manner. In contrast, our MLLM-EDR inherently has the capability to process EGD image series. Most importantly, note that all these baselines are unimodal methods that rely exclusively on visual inputs, while our MLLM-EDR leverages both visual features and disease-specific text prompts (i.e., embedded medical knowledge) to extract discriminative cues for EGD diagnosis and reporting. As shown in Tables 2–4, the accuracies of baseline models are calculated at the patient’s case level by comparing the generated reports with the ground-truth reports, ensuring a fair comparison with our method.

Model description of MLLM-EDR

Given a series of EGD images I∈RN×H×W×3 from a participant, where N represents the number of images varying for each participant, H and W denote the height and width of the images, our MLLM-EDR aims to generate the corresponding EGD report, which includes the “Examination conclusion” (i.e., the background gastric mucosa classification and GI disease diagnosis results) and “Examination findings” (i.e., the detailed text descriptions) sections. As shown in Fig. 6, our method is built upon a multitask learning framework and is trained in an end-to-end manner. Below, we detail it.

Framework of the proposed MLLM-EDR.

It solves background gastric mucosa classification, GI disease diagnosis and text description generation tasks simultaneously. The input EGD images are first processed by the vision encoder of MLLM. Then the visual embeddings are interacted with the textual embeddings of prompts generated from an LLM through interaction blocks. Finally, we leverage a multimodal decoder to output final EGD reports.

Anatomic site identification: To automatically generate EGD reports, we first utilize the vision encoder from a pre-trained MLLM (i.e., MedCLIP50) to encode the images into visual features. The vision encoder is a vision transformer that divides each image into patches and encodes them as a sequence of embeddings. Additionally, a [CLS] token is included for each image to represent its global feature. We denote by V={vi∈Rn+1×d,i∈[1,N]} the obtained visual embeddings for I and specifically, Vc={vic∈R1×d,i∈[1,N]} the embeddings of the [CLS] tokens. n is the number of local patch embeddings and d is the feature dimension. Next, we apply a linear layer to predict the anatomic sites of the images based on Vc. Our method considers eight anatomic sites within the GI tract: esophagus, cardia, gastric fundus, gastric body, gastric angle, gastric antrum, duodenal bulb, and descending part of the duodenum. This anatomic site identification task serves as an auxiliary task, aiding the vision encoder in capturing site-specific information, which is crucial for generating accurate descriptions for each site in the subsequent phase.

Background gastric mucosa classification: Next, we apply a temporal adaptive pooling layer after the vision encoder to unify the number of visual embeddings. This results in visual embeddings V^={v^i∈Rn+1×d,i∈[1,M]}, which are subsequently refined using a self-attention layer. We take the maximum value of the embeddings of M [CLS] tokens along the token dimension and obtain vglobal∈R1×d. A linear layer is then applied to vglobal for the purpose of background gastric mucosa classification. The background gastric mucosa represents the overall status of the gastric mucosa, and the diagnosis result forms the first part of the “Examination Conclusion” section in our generated report.

GI disease diagnosis: The next stage is to extract lesion features from the visual embeddings V^ for GI disease diagnosis. However, existing techniques do not leverage relevant prior medical knowledge to enhance their understanding of visual embeddings, making it challenging to link them to specific disease categories. For example, as described in GPT-4, while both S1 and S2 levels of duodenal bulb ulcers exhibit mucosal redness, they differ in edge characteristics: S1 level of ulcers have smoother edges, whereas S2 level of ulcers are characterized by irregular edges. Additionally, S2 level of ulcers often exhibit darker coloration and signs of bleeding. By incorporating such medical knowledge, our MLLM-EDR can be more effectively trained to distinguish between these diseases.

Medical knowledge guidance: We leverage prior medical knowledge embedded in an LLM to enhance the discriminability of the visual embeddings. First, we use the following prompt to generate generic disease appearance descriptions of the nineteen representative GI diseases via GPT-4: “Please describe the general visual appearance details of [disease] in EGD images within thirty words”, where the [disease] token is replaced with each of the nineteen disease names. The obtained descriptions have been confirmed and corrected if necessary by expert endoscopists. Specifically, we invited three expert endoscopists to verify the text descriptions. They examined each description in terms of clinical correctness and completeness. When any endoscopist identified a potential issue (e.g., an incorrect statement or missing details), they discussed whether the description should be revised and how to revise it. If no issues were raised, the description was retained as originally written. It should be noted that this verification process was not labor-intensive, and the experts completed the review and reached consensus quickly. The final descriptions (shown in Supplementary Table 24) encode rich medical knowledge that can enhance the model’s understanding of disease-related visual features, particularly for diseases with visually similar characteristics. These descriptions are fixed and applied across all cases during training.

Next, we employ the text encoder from the pre-trained MedCLIP50 to extract the textual embeddings fT∈RK×d for these descriptions. Three identical interaction blocks are then employed to extract the lesion features V^lesion from V^ under the guidance of fT. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 8, this interaction block is a standard Transformer-style module51,52. It consists of a layer normalization and a self-attention layer, followed by another layer normalization and a cross-attention layer, and finally a feed-forward network. Text description generation: After extracting visual features V^lesion, the next step is to enable a locally deployed multimodal decoder to generate detailed and comprehensive descriptions for the given images. To achieve this, we first construct multimodal prompts that combine V^lesion with a textual instruction to guide the decoder. These multimodal prompts are then fed into the multimodal decoder from pre-trained BLIP-253 to auto-regressively generate the descriptions. Specifically, the textual instruction aims to ensure that the multimodal decoder understands the meaning of the given visual features and determines the actions required based on these features. Following this principle, we construct the textual instruction Tins, i.e., “Please generate an esophagogastroduodenoscopy report based on the provided visual features. Assistant”. This instruction provides an explicit generation objective and guides the decoder to produce a standardized EGD report rather than generic free-form text. We then use the tokenizer of BLIP-2 to transform this instruction into textual embeddings Fins∈RL×d, where L represents the length of the instruction. Subsequently, we concatenate Fins with V^lesion along the token dimension to create the multimodal prompts, which are then input into the multimodal decoder for description generation. This process follows an autoregressive manner, where each token is generated sequentially based on the multimodal prompts and all previously generated tokens. The obtained text descriptions form the “Examination findings” section in our generated report. We finetune the multimodal decoder using LoRA-based finetuning paradigm.

Training configurations

We leverage two types of loss functions to optimize the network. For the tasks of anatomic site identification, background gastric mucosa classification, GI disease diagnosis, and text description generation, we use the widely used cross-entropy loss. Additionally, for the text description generation task, we employ the vision-text contrastive loss between the visual embeddings of the input images and the textual embeddings of the ground truth reports. This loss facilitates alignment between text and vision modality within the embedding space, ensuring consistency between the visual content and the generated descriptions. This alignment can also enhance the semantic accuracy and contextual relevance of the resulting reports. The overall loss is a linear combination of these individual losses. During training, these losses are jointly optimized.

For EGD images in the TJ-EGD dataset, we rescale them to a resolution of 224×224 for training54. We normalize the images using the mean and standard deviation used by the vision encoder of MedCLIP50. Moreover, we adopt a class-balancing data augmentation strategy to reduce training bias toward high-prevalence diseases. Specifically, for each GI disease category, we repeatedly sample cases from that category and apply randomly selected augmentation operations to generate additional training cases until the number of training cases for that category reaches 500 (the largest category contains 455 cases before augmentation). These operations include image rotations (90°, 180°, and 270°), horizontal image flipping, and vertical image flipping. This balanced training distribution enables the model to learn discriminative features for each disease category. Following the guidance55, LoRA is applied to the attention projection layers and feed-forward layers within each Transformer block of the multimodal decoder of BLIP-2 in our work; this way enables parameter-efficient adaptation while keeping the original pretrained weights frozen. We follow common practice31,32 to set the LoRA rank as r=16 and the LoRA scale factor as s=2. We train our MLLM-EDR using the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 5×10−5 and a batch size of 16. The number of training epochs is set to 100. The model is implemented using the public framework PyTorch v1.10 and trained on a workstation equipped with four NVIDIA A40 GPUs.

Deployment requirements

Our model is deployed and tested on an NVIDIA A40 GPU (48GB VRAM), but it can also be deployed on lower-end GPUs as long as the GPU has at least 16 GB of VRAM. For example, we evaluated the model on an NVIDIA 4090 GPU (24 GB VRAM), and the performance remained unchanged. Moreover, the model requires CUDA support for GPU acceleration.

Scalability

Our model can be directly extended to support other endoscopic (e.g., hysteroscopy and laparoscopy) diagnosis and reporting tasks by simply replacing the corresponding medical knowledge and training on their corresponding vision-language pairs. This flexibility enables the model to be easily adapted to different clinical settings and diagnostic requirements.

Statistical analysis

The generated text descriptions are evaluated using BLEU and ROUGE with 95% confidence intervals. The background gastric mucosa classification and GI disease diagnosis results are assessed using accuracy, specificity, sensitivity, and AUC, also with 95% confidence intervals. Statistical significance between models is determined using two-tailed t-tests, with p values less than 0.05 considered significant. Inter-rater agreement is measured using Fleiss’ kappa values, interpreted as follows: 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; 0.81–1.00, excellent28.
