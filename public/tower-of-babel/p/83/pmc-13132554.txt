Infiltrative liver conditions: a pictorial review of malignant and benign entities
Authors: Jordan Zheng Ting Sim, Nicole Kessa Wee, Hsien Min Low
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Infiltrative malignancies of the liver are uncommon and have received comparatively less attention in radiology literature. These ill-defined, nondiscrete disease processes pose a diagnostic challenge to radiologists, as they are often difficult to distinguish from one another. Any disease process that distorts normal hepatic parenchyma and architecture should be considered as a differential. The objective of this article is to offer a concise pictorial review of the possible infiltrative malignancies of the liver and provide reasonable differential diagnoses, which can include benign, non-neoplastic mimickers.

NEOPLASTIC CAUSES

Infiltrative hepatocellular carcinoma

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) can be categorised into the following three major subtypes based on the Eggel growth pattern classification: nodular, massive and diffuse.[1] Infiltrative HCC is characterised by the presence of innumerable tiny tumour nodules in one or more hepatic lobes or the entire liver. Infiltrative HCC has also been described as cirrhotomimetic HCC.[23] It accounts for approximately 7%–20% of HCCs.[23] On imaging, infiltrative HCC has a permeative, ill-defined appearance, often spreading over multiple hepatic segments.[4] On ultrasonography (US), infiltrative HCC is often indistinguishable from cirrhosis,[5] rendering US a suboptimal screening tool for infiltrative HCC. However, it remains useful in the detection of portal venous thrombosis, which is commonly seen in patients with infiltrative HCC, in up to 68%–100% of cases.[67] These tumours generally demonstrate geographic T1 hypointensity, diffuse T2 hyperintensity and restricted diffusion [Figure 1].[8] The typical arterial phase hyperenhancement that radiologists are used to in progressed HCCs is not always seen in infiltrative HCC, which may commonly appear iso- or hypointense in the arterial phase. The washout appearance of the tumour is also reported as irregular and heterogeneous.[4] A reticular appearance in the venous and equilibrium phases has been described, likely related to underlying fibrosis.[7] In the hepatobiliary phase, the tumour appears hypointense because of the lack of hepatocyte-specific contrast agent uptake.[8]

US images show (a) geographic T1 hypointensity; (b) T2 hyperintensity; and (c) restricted diffusion (diffusion weighted imaging; apparent diffusion coefficient not shown). (d) A higher slice of the T2-W sequence demonstrates tumour thrombus within the portal vein (arrow), a common occurrence in infiltrative HCC. (e) Postcontrast subtraction image in the arterial phase (*) demonstrates hypervascular infiltrative disease with washout on the delayed image (f).

Cholangiocarcinoma

Intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma can be grossly classified into three types: mass-forming, periductal-infiltrating and intraductal-polypid. An infiltrative form of mass-forming intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma is not well described in the current literature. Nonetheless, a mass-forming intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma that occupies an entire liver lobe without distortion of the normal liver morphology can potentially have an infiltrative appearance. Computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) may show bile duct dilatation, targetoid diffusion restriction and irregular peripheral enhancement [Figure 2].[4] As a form of differentiation from infiltrative HCCs, cholangiocarcinoma may compress and displace the vessels, but does not typically cause thrombosis.[9]

(a) CT images of a 71-year-old Chinese man show a large irregular mass almost completely replacing the left hepatic lobe, with early peripheral enhancement and delayed central enhancement, characteristic of cholangiocarcinoma, which was confirmed on biopsy. (b) Selected MR images of the same patient show restricted diffusion in the entire hepatic lobe with tumour signal around the proximal aspects of the bilateral hepatic ducts and common hepatic duct, with resultant upstream intrahepatic biliary dilatation (arrows). (c) CT images of another patient show an infiltrative mass in the left hepatic lobe, demonstrating progressive enhancement with multiple hypoattenuating nodules in the right lobe of liver (circles), compatible with intrahepatic metastasis. This was biopsy proven to be cholangiocarcinoma.

Diffuse metastatic disease of the liver: breast cancer

In some cases, metastatic breast cancer to the liver can result in an infiltrative appearance. The infiltrative appearance can occur in both treated and untreated metastatic disease. In the former, the liver may have a nodular appearance resembling cirrhosis and has been termed by some authors as ‘pseudocirrhosis’.[1011] The imaging features are similar to those of cirrhosis, including a lobular hepatic contour, segmental volume loss, hypertrophy of the caudate lobe and ancillary findings such as ascites.[1011] Postcontrast images may demonstrate hypovascularised areas throughout the liver, consistent with areas of focal fibrosis. Liver histology may show desmoplastic infiltration by breast cancer cells. The affected patients may present with imaging and clinical features of cirrhosis in the absence of chronic ethanol consumption or previous hepatitis infection.

Metastatic breast disease to the liver commonly presents as discrete masses demonstrating peripheral enhancement. However, several isolated case reports and case series[121314] have also described untreated metastatic breast cancer to the liver as having an infiltrative appearance. On imaging, these lesions have a geographic, permeative appearance with heterogeneous attenuation and signal intensity on CT and MRI, respectively. Histopathologic analysis of these specimens reveals metastatic carcinoma diffusely infiltrating the sinusoids. It should be noted that intrasinusoidal dissemination of liver metastases is not limited to breast cancers. Indeed, a few case reports have also described transitional cell carcinomas of the urinary bladder and small cell lung carcinomas as exhibiting a similar pattern or metastatic spread in the liver with correspondingly similar radiographic appearances.[1516]

Our patient exhibits an infiltrative, nodular appearance in the subcapsular region of the right hepatic lobe, with restricted diffusion and progressive enhancement [Figure 3].

Diffuse metastatic disease of the liver: melanoma

Diffuse infiltration of the liver secondary to metastatic melanoma is rare and the findings of primary hepatic melanoma are scarce in relevant literature. Imaging features are nonspecific, presenting as a diffusely enlarged liver with no focal lesions and little or no ascites. Consistent with the known characteristics of melanoma, these lesions can demonstrate high signal on T1-weighted images [Figure 4].[1718] There can be mass effect on the hepatic and portal vessels, which will appear compressed, but has a weak association with intraluminal thrombosis. A percutaneous liver biopsy is necessary for definitive diagnosis, although these patients often present with acute liver failure and severe coagulopathy, thus precluding invasive procedures.[17]

A patient with a grossly enlarged liver and multiple lesions. (a) Coronal MR image shows the size and extent of the dominant mass; the largest mass had replaced the entire left hepatic lobe with extension into segment 8, measuring up to 24.5 cm in size. The lesions show (b) T2 hyperintensity with (c) restricted diffusion (apparent diffusion coefficient is not shown). (d–f) MR images show the lesions to be of T1 intermediate signal with arterial hyperenhancement without washout. Of note, scattered T1-hyperintense foci are seen throughout the liver (arrows). Areas of nonenhancement may represent tumour necrosis (* in the postcontrast images e and f). This was biopsy proven to be metastatic melanoma.

NON-NEOPLASTIC CAUSES

Hepatic pyogenic microabscesses

Pyogenic abscesses can be classified into either microabscesses (<2 cm) or macroabscesses (>2 cm). Pyogenic microabscesses can sometimes demonstrate a diffuse miliary pattern, often caused by staphylococcal infections. Pyogenic abscesses can also have a clustered appearance, often associated with coliform bacteria.[19] On sonography, these lesions have a complex cystic appearance, with varying amounts of internal echoes and debris. Presence of gas will result in hyperechoic foci with reverberation artefacts and multiple vertical spikes on both sides of the reference line on Doppler spectral display. Peripheral rim enhancement is seen in postcontrast CT and MR studies. Diffusion weighted imaging/apparent diffusion coefficient sequences also demonstrate restricted diffusion of the contents of the lesion due to hypercellularity of the purulent fluid. In contrast, metastatic adenocarcinoma and cholangiocarcinoma show irregular, thickened areas of diffusion restriction along the periphery of the mass due to the presence of tumour tissue. Reactive perilesional oedema and enhancement can also be observed in abscesses. Clinical features are particularly important in these cases, as patients frequently manifest infective symptoms such as high fever and rigours. Response to antibiotics can also serve as a good diagnostic clue, as in our case [Figure 5].

An 89-year-old Chinese man with pyrexia. (a) MR cholangiopancreatography image shows signal abnormalities in the left hepatic lobe with internal cystic areas, initially thought to be metastatic disease. (b) Follow-up serial CT images obtained over the course of one month following appropriate antimicrobial treatment show a rapid decrease in size and extent of disease (arrows), in keeping with an infective process.

Tuberculosis of the liver

Tuberculosis is known to involve the liver in a variety of patterns, one of which is the generalised miliary form, with diffuse nodularities that can coalesce to mimic an infiltrative lesion. Miliary tuberculosis of the liver has been reported to occur in 50%–80% of all patients with terminal pulmonary tuberculosis.[1920] Imaging features of miliary tuberculosis of the liver are nonspecific, described as hypoattenuating lesions on CT [Figure 6] and T1 hypointense/T2 hypo-isointense on MRI. As these present as microabscesses, they can also show corresponding restricted diffusion with rim enhancement. They can appear both hypo- or hyperechoic on sonography.[19] Given the systemic pattern of infection for tuberculosis, ancillary findings such as involvement of other organs including the spleen and abdominopelvic nodes can also be present. As a result, percutaneous liver biopsy is often required to establish a diagnosis.

(a) CT image on presentation shows heterogeneous parenchymal enhancement with multiple ill-defined, hypodense foci, with similar findings in the spleen. The involvement of the spleen makes a metastatic process less likely. Biopsy demonstrated necrotising granulomatous inflammation and acid-fast bacilli on staining. (b) Follow-up CT image obtained 3 years later shows almost complete resolution of previously noted hypodense foci, with remnant linear areas of scarring.

Radiation-induced hepatitis

Modern external beam radiation therapy techniques are designed to reduce the amount of normal tissue exposed to high-dose radiation, but unintended radiation exposure is still inevitable. The typical imaging findings in any irradiated tissue still hold true: sharply demarcated areas of abnormality can help to suggest this diagnosis [Figure 7] and these can appear band-like if the primary radiation therapy site is extrahepatic (e.g., in the chest).[21] Irradiated liver parenchyma can be hypo- or hyperechoic on US and usually hypodense on CT, while it demonstrates T1 hypointensity and T2 hyperintensity on MRI.[2223] Geographic changes with volume loss in the treated hepatic segments can mimic an infiltrative disease process.

A 55-year-old Chinese man received radiotherapy for nasopharyngeal carcinoma metastases in the lower right ribs. CT images taken at different levels to demonstrate the clear linear demarcation show a geographic area of lower attenuation in the periphery of the right lobe, with a sharply demarcated straight edge (arrows). The vessels and biliary radicles underlying this hypodense area are otherwise unremarkable.

Internal radiation therapy techniques (e.g., radioembolisation for hepatic neoplasms) also carry the risk of possible radiation-induced injury to the surrounding tissues. Post radioembolisation, hepatic malignancies may demonstrate cavitation, enlargement and peripheral enhancement, but treated lesions should facilitate diffusion in the absence of residual blood products, unlike residual viable tumour tissue.[23] Imaging features in irradiated tissues evolve over time; serial imaging and follow-up play a critical role in accurate diagnosis.

Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia: liver involvement

Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT), also known as Osler–Weber–Rendu syndrome, is an autosomal dominant disease that is characterised by multiple vascular malformations (VMs). Hepatic involvement is relatively common, although mostly asymptomatic. Liver involvement in HHT is typically diffuse and heterogeneous, which can lead to diagnostic confusion if underlying HHT is not known [Figure 8]. Ultrasonography is exceptionally useful in the evaluation of liver HHT, allowing for the evaluation of direction and magnitude of blood flow/vascular shunts. A dilated extrahepatic proper hepatic artery (>4 mm) is one of the earliest manifestations of hepatic HHT and can be seen on US, CT and MRI.[24] Both CT and MRI typically demonstrate heterogeneous enhancement of the entire liver, with small VMs (<10 mm) representing diffuse telangiectases.[2425] The presence of diffuse hepatic telangiectases and a dilated common hepatic artery can be considered almost pathognomonic of liver involvement in HHT. Finally, catheter angiography can be useful in characterising arterioportal, arteriosystemic and portosystemic shunts, but endovascular embolisation treatment of liver VMs has fallen out of favour due to high mortality and complication rates.[24]

A 34-year-old Indian woman with known hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia (HHT). Axial CT image of the liver in the arterial phase (windowed for better contrast) shows diffuse and heterogeneous arterial hyperenhancement of the left lobe of liver. This appearance can lead to diagnostic confusion if the history of HHT is not known.

Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome from chemotherapy

Hepatic sinusoidal obstruction syndrome (SOS) is thought to be due to the microvascular deposition of fibrous material resulting in impairment of blood flow in the small intrahepatic veins. This results in vascular liver injury. Sinusoidal obstruction syndrome is a potentially severe complication following haematopoietic stem cell transplants or after systemic chemotherapy, particularly oxaliplatin [Figure 9].[2627] Histologically, SOS shows sinusoidal congestion, sclerosis and collagen deposition in the sinusoids and venules. Chronic SOS reveals a thickened collagen cuff around the central veins. On contrast-enhanced CT and MRI, SOS appears as patchy, heterogeneous liver enhancement. Contrast-enhanced MRI using gadoxetic acid shows a diffuse reticular pattern on the hepatobiliary phase, which can be mistaken as an infiltrative disease to an inexperienced reader [Figure 9].[28]

The patient subsequently received oxaliplatin and capecitabine for sigmoid colon adenocarcinoma. (b) Post-chemotherapy MR image shows a diffuse reticular pattern that is seen in sinusoidal obstruction syndrome, which can be mistaken as an infiltrative disease by an inexperienced reader.

CONCLUSION

Both benign and malignant pathologies of the liver can cause an infiltrative appearance; knowledge and familiarity with this spectrum of conditions can prevent misdiagnosis. Clinical history is also essential, especially if there is a known primary or if infection is one of the differentials. Despite best efforts, a liver biopsy may still be required to establish a definitive diagnosis if imaging is inconclusive.
