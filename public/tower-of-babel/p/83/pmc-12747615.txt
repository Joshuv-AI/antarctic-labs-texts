Exploring Undergraduates' Attitudes Toward Sterilization as Contraception in the Northeastern United States: A Brief Report
Authors: K Olivia Mock, Devika Patel, Anne Moyer
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study analyzed young adults' understanding of and thoughts about permanent contraception amidst increased demand for sterilization in this population following changes in abortion and contraception access in the United States (US).MethodsUndergraduate students at a northeastern US university (N = 53) completed an online questionnaire assessing opinions about sterilization and factors they believed physicians should consider before performing sterilization. Quantitative analysis explored whether attitudes toward vasectomy versus tubal ligation differed by capacity to become pregnant. We used inductive thematic analysis to interpret qualitative data.ResultsThere were no differences in attitudes toward sterilization methods by capacity to become pregnant. However, participants estimated posttubal ligation regret would be significantly more prevalent than postvasectomy regret. Key qualitative themes included patient‐centered care, regret, and sociocultural influences.ConclusionsThis study revealed young adults value bodily autonomy in reproductive choices, regardless of permanence or potential regret, factors some providers weigh heavily. Whether interest in sterilization among this population increases or remains static, it is important for healthcare providers to be aware of these attitudes when handling requests from younger adults.Keywords: contraception, sterilization, United States

Introduction

Reproductive freedom has been increasingly challenged in the United States (US) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Permanent contraception or sterilization (vasectomy or tubal ligation) does not depend on access to medications, devices, or procedures after initial treatment. Although comparably effective, vasectomies are less invasive, less expensive, and largely reversible, but tubal ligation is harder to obtain due to provider concerns about regret. Patient age, relationship status, number of children, and the history of involuntarily or coercively [5] sterilizing vulnerable women 1 [6, 7, 8] are potential sources of this reluctance [9, 10] Permanent contraception rates are approximately three times higher for women than for men in the US and eight times higher worldwide [11, 12, 13], likely because women bear the burden of pregnancy and are historically responsible for contraception [14].

Previously, young adults between the ages of 18 and 26 [15] had the lowest rates of permanent contraceptive use [16, 17, 18, 19]. Media reports indicated an increase in younger adults getting vasectomies [20, 21, 22, 23] and research confirmed an increase in tubal ligation rates among young adults in the 6 months following the Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (Dobbs) decision [24, 25, 26]. Concerns about abortion and contraceptive restrictions [27] and the increase in US adults opting to be childfree [28] are potential factors driving these changes. However, research focused on young adults' attitudes toward permanent contraception is lacking.

To address this gap, we gathered qualitative and quantitative data using a questionnaire developed to assess attitudes and perceptions of barriers to providing permanent contraception. We had two hypotheses: the sample would report significantly more favorable attitudes toward vasectomy than tubal ligation, due to the scrutiny women face when seeking permanent contraception [14], and capacity to become pregnant 2 would have associations with greater receptivity to permanent contraception and reluctance to rely on a sexual partner's use of permanent contraception.

Methods

Participants

We recruited 53 3 undergraduates in the northeastern US, 18 years of age or older, and fluent in English from a Psychology Department Participant Pool in October and November of 2022.

Materials

The questionnaire, hosted on Qualtrics [29], measured demographic characteristics (e.g., capacity to become pregnant) and conceptualization of “sterilization.” Then, participants read descriptions of “vasectomy” and “tubal ligation,” [30, 31] reported perceived procedure effectiveness, and estimated regret prevalence. The subsequent sections assessed perceptions of physicians using patient age, relationship status, number of children, potential regret, or certainty as eligibility criteria for sterilization, assessed willingness to use permanent contraception, and instructed participants to explain their reasoning.

Analytic Plan

Quantitative Analyses

We used SPSS Version 28 to determine whether participants' attitudes were significantly more favorable toward vasectomies vs. tubal ligation, and whether individuals capable of becoming pregnant were significantly more receptive to sterilization through repeated‐measures ANOVA, correlations, and chi‐squared analyses.

Qualitative Analyses

We aimed for a sample size of about 60, to meet thematic saturation [32] the point at which new themes no longer emerge [33]. Using NVivo Version 12 [34], two trained coders used inductive thematic analysis [35] to identify overarching constructs, ancillary factors, and distinctive concepts [36]. We attained good inter‐rater agreement [37] using constant comparative analysis and resolved disagreements through consensus.

Results

Participant Demographics 4

Participants were between 18 and 32 years old (M = 20.00, SD = 2.45) and predominantly single (53%) and childless (94%). A majority identified as female (70%) and heterosexual (74%), with approximately half ever being sexually active, and 75% capable of becoming pregnant. The most commonly used form of contraception was condoms, then short‐acting reversible hormonal contraception, and finally long‐acting reversible contraception (LARC). No participants used permanent contraception; see Table 1. Participants rated vasectomies as 86% effective (SD = 17.34) and tubal ligation as 87% effective (SD = 17.59) (Table 2).

Quantitative Statistics 5

Estimated Regret

Participants' estimated poststerilization regret rates for vasectomies (M = 37%, SD = 26.06) were not as high as the estimated regret rates for tubal ligation (M = 45%, SD = 24.77), F(1, 51) = 6.21, p < 0.05, η 2 = 0.109, with those capable of becoming pregnant providing lower estimates of regret than those not capable of becoming pregnant, see Table 3. Expected regret regarding vasectomies was significantly correlated with the estimated expected regret regarding tubal ligation, r = 0.59, CI (0.41, 1.00), p < 0.01 (see Table 4). A repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed no main effect of capacity to become pregnant on estimated regret, F(1, 51) = 2.17, p > 0.05, η 2 = 0.020, and no interaction with sterilization type, F(1, 51) = 0.42, p > 0.05, η 2 = 0.008.

Qualitative Results

Responses endorsed a patient‐centered care approach, and indicated views on regret and sociocultural factors contribute to attitudes toward sterilization.

Patient Centered Care

Participants emphasized respecting patient autonomy regardless of their age, relationship status, the number of children they have, the potential for regret, or a physician's personal beliefs. A 21‐year‐old woman wrote, “We shouldn't let societal standards of having a ‘nuclear family’ play a role in how people decide what form of contraception they want.” They further acknowledged the certainty of individuals seeking sterilization. A 21‐year‐old woman elaborated, “People who don't want kids tend to be quite certain that they don't want kids. Going under the knife isn't something people do without consideration.”

However, participants also noted the importance of healthcare providers advising patients and ensuring they are adequately informed. A 20‐year‐old woman wrote, “As this is an irreversible procedure, the implications should be heavily discussed with the patient, but it should overall be up to the patient.” A 19‐year‐old woman clarified that “the only reason” a physician should overrule a patient's desires is if there are legitimate medical risks.

Regret Regulation

Responses cited the irreversibility of tubal ligation as a cause of regret. A 19‐year‐old woman indicated “a woman can be dead set” on undergoing sterilization at the time of the procedure but later decides they want to give birth to a child. Participants responded that tubal ligation would have a “higher regret rate” than vasectomy because “regret tends to be felt when a loss is irretrievable,” as stated by an 18‐year‐old man and 24‐year‐old woman respectively. Concerns about permanence were also present. An 18‐year‐old woman was adamant she would never undergo sterilization and asked, “Why would I do something to my body that I can't undo?”

Sociocultural Factors

Participants indicated sociocultural factors including perceptions of masculinity, religious beliefs, societal norms regarding family structure, and historical connotations influence attitudes about sterilization. They suggested men were wary about getting a vasectomy due to its association with decreased virility and a 20‐year‐old woman wrote it could make a person feel like “less of a man.” An 18‐year‐old man, when explaining his estimated postvasectomy regret, wrote, “In my religion, we are taught that having children is something everyone wants eventually, it is in our soul's preferences (fitrah).” A 21‐year‐old woman reported that sterilization made her “think of the forced sterilization/eugenic movement in the US.”

Discussion

General knowledge of permanent contraception was highly variable in our study population. As this study recruited participants from students at a US university, this lack of confidence in the effectiveness of permanent contraception may be attributable to the quality of sex education in the US, which varies from state to state [38]. It is also possible that participants did not carefully read or trust the provided definitions. Despite the endorsement of bodily autonomy, participants did voice greater concern about the permanence of tubal ligation in comparison to vasectomies. Permanence was commonly mentioned as a source of potential regret and student participants thought healthcare providers should view this under the purview of “do no harm” [39].

Participants' estimated regret rate was significantly greater for tubal ligation than for vasectomies and were overestimates of documented regret rates for both procedures [40, 41], possibly due to pronatalist sentiment [42, 43]. Estimates of regret were internally consistent across procedure types, but the factors to consider as eligibility criteria were not. The greater observed rates of tubal ligation versus vasectomy regret likely play a role in this [44]. This finding mirrors anecdotal research on barriers to tubal ligation [9, 10].

The results do not indicate the capacity to become pregnant is predictive of estimated regret regarding sterilization, openness to using permanent contraception, willingness to ask a partner to use permanent contraception, or reluctance to trust a sterilized sexual partner. Because half the sample was not sexually active it is possible the hypothetical nature of the questions influenced their responses.

Limitations

The questionnaire had not undergone pilot testing, possibly allowing for conflicting interpretations of questions and misrepresentations of participants' attitudes. We administered the questionnaire online; thus, there were no data quality assurance or opportunities to ask follow‐up questions. Finally, no participants used permanent contraception, in line with the low rate among American college students [45], precluding learning anything about direct experiences.

Implications and Future Directions

Although this study did not find a significant desire for permanent contraception in young adults described in popular reporting [20, 22], we should not underestimate the emphasis on bodily autonomy. Future work could collect data from larger, more representative samples of young adults in the US using an updated questionnaire. Updates should include an assessment of participants' basic understanding of permanent contraception as the sample's underestimation of effectiveness suggests that there are misconceptions. Due to the concerns about coercion, assessing perceptions of pressures could provide valuable insight as well.

A conceptual replication of this study adapting the questionnaire to assess how physicians feel about taking a patient's biosocial characteristics, potential regret, and certainty into account when counseling someone on permanent contraception. This might reveal biases providers may hold and what criteria they use in the absence of official guidelines. It can also determine if they are aware of and willing to meet young adults' reproductive needs. Ultimately, these results are just scratching the surface of sterilization research, and we need to conduct more research to build a meaningful, robust base of knowledge and understanding.
