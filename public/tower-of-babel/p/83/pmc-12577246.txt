Evaluation of vancomycin utilization in medical and pediatric wards: a retrospective cross-sectional study at Bule Hora University Teaching Hospital, Ethiopia
Authors: Aliyi Gerina, Woyesa Elema, Netsanet Fikadu, Misgana Amanual
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVancomycin is a critical antibiotic used for serious Gram‑positive infections, particularly methicillin‑resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). However, inappropriate use contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR). Introduction

Pharmaceuticals are among the most resource-intensive components of healthcare systems and must be used judiciously to ensure safety, efficacy, and cost-effectiveness. Rational drug use is particularly critical in resource-limited settings where access to essential medicines remains a challenge. Drug Use Evaluation (DUE) serves as a key component of quality assurance, offering a systematic approach to assess whether medications are appropriately prescribed, dispensed, and administered, with the goal of optimizing therapeutic outcomes [1, 2].

Vancomycin, a glycopeptide antibiotic, plays a pivotal role in the treatment of severe infections caused by Gram-positive organisms, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). It acts by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis, thus exhibiting bactericidal activity. However, increasing reports of vancomycin resistance, particularly among enterococci, have prompted international guidelines restricting its use to well-defined clinical scenarios, including: Infections caused by organisms resistant to beta-lactams, Severe allergies to beta-lactam antibiotics, Clostridium difficile-associated colitis, Prophylaxis for endocarditis and certain surgical procedures, and Empirical therapy for suspected MRSA while awaiting culture results [3–5].

Vancomycin remains a cornerstone antibiotic for the treatment of severe Gram-positive infections, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). However, inappropriate use contributes substantially to the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR). To address this challenge, the World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the AWaRe classification, which groups antibiotics into Access, Watch, and Reserve categories based on antibacterial spectrum, resistance potential, and public health importance [6, 7]. While WHO classifies vancomycin under the Watch group, the Ethiopian Essential Medicines List (2020) places it in the Reserve group, underscoring its restricted use in the local context.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a global health threat driven by a complex interplay of overuse, misuse, underuse, and poor-quality antimicrobials. While the biological mechanisms of resistance are natural, human actions particularly irrational prescribing accelerate the problem. Hence, evaluating how vancomycin is used in clinical settings is essential for informing stewardship programs and ensuring responsible prescribing [8, 9].

The consequences of antimicrobial resistance are far-reaching. In 2019, an estimated 1.27 million deaths were directly attributable to antibiotic-resistant bacterial infections, with approximately 5 million additional deaths associated with resistant pathogens globally [10]. Vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) pose a major challenge, with rising resistance trends reported globally. By 2016, vancomycin resistance reached 21.0% in the United States and 9.9% in Europe, with considerable variability among countries. For instance, VRE rates ranged from 0% in Iceland and Luxembourg to 59.1% in Cyprus [11].

Globally, the misuse of vancomycin is a recurring concern. A study from Western Iran reported prolonged antibiotic therapy and hospital stays, with mean durations of 19.08 ± 1.51 days and 22.11 ± 1.76 days, respectively, largely due to irrational antibiotic use [12]. In Eastern India, inappropriate vancomycin use was identified in 42.7% of hospitalized pediatric patients [13]. The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) also emphasizes that hospital-based stewardship programs are critical to ensure rational use of vancomycin and other high-priority antibiotics [14]. Previous studies in Ethiopia and internationally have reported varying levels of inappropriate use, including incorrect indication, dosing, and duration [2, 12, 15]. However, evidence from hospitals in southern Ethiopia remains limited. This study therefore aimed to evaluate the utilization of vancomycin in medical and pediatric wards of Bule Hora University Teaching Hospital (BHUTH).

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective cross‑sectional study was conducted at Bule Hora University Teaching Hospital (BHUTH), located in Bule Hora Town, West Guji Zone, Oromia Regional State, Ethiopia. BHUTH is a government teaching hospital affiliated with Bule Hora University and serves a catchment population of approximately 141,579 people (Health Management information systems of BHUTH, 2024). The hospital provides inpatient and outpatient services, including pediatric and internal medicine care.

Study period

Medical records from May 31, 2023 to June 30, 2024 were reviewed. Data collection was performed between September 10 and November 11, 2024.

Source of population

The source population included all medical records of patients admitted to the medical and pediatric wards of BHUTH between May 31, 2023, and June 30, 2024.

Study population and eligibility criteria

The study population comprised medical records of patients admitted to the medical and pediatric wards during the study period with a clinician‑documented diagnosis of an infectious disease. As microbiological testing was not routinely available, diagnoses were based on clinical judgment and documentation by the treating physician. Records with incomplete information (missing data on dose, frequency, duration, or indication of vancomycin) or non‑infectious admissions were excluded.

Sample size and sampling technique

The required sample size (n = 195) was calculated using a single population proportion formula with P = 15% from a previous study, 95% confidence, and 5% margin of error. After correction for finite population (N = 2760), the final sample was 182. A systematic random sampling method (k = 15) was applied, with proportional allocation between medical and pediatric wards. A flow diagram (Fig. 1) illustrates the selection process.

Study variables

Dependent variable: Appropriateness of vancomycin use.

Independent variables: Age, sex, ward, comorbidities, indication, dose, frequency, duration, concurrent medications, and treatment outcome.

Operational definitions

Standard Treatment Guidelines (STG): Ethiopian national treatment guidelines used to evaluate appropriateness of vancomycin prescribing.

Appropriate use: Prescribing consistent with STG in indication, dose, frequency, and duration.

Inappropriate use: Any deviation from STG recommendations.

Data collection and quality control

Data were extracted using a structured checklist adapted from Ethiopian STG. Medical charts, prescription orders, and admission logs were reviewed. Data collectors were trained prior to collection. Daily supervision and cross‑checking were performed by the investigators. Incomplete or inconsistent entries were verified and corrected.

Data analysis

Data were entered and analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 27. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means ± SD, medians and interquartile ranges) were calculated. Results are presented in tables and figures. A p‑value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant where comparisons were made.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethical Review Committee of Bule Hora University, Institute of Health (Ref. No: BHU/IoH/ERC/0395/67/2024). As anonymized secondary data were used, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

A total of 182 patient records were reviewed from the medical and pediatric wards at HUTH between May 31, 2023 and June 30, 2024. Among them, 87 (47.8%) patients were admitted to the medical ward, while 95 (52.2%) were admitted to the pediatric ward. The majority of the patients were male 110 (58.8%) and from rural areas 111 (61.0%) The age distribution of the patients was as follows: 1–24 months 39 (21.4%), 2–8 years 37 (20.3%) and 26–35 years 25 (13.7%) (Table 1).

Appropretness of vancomycin use

Of the 182 patients, 81 (44.8%) received vancomycin. The most common indications were pyogenic meningitis 64 (78.9%), severe community-acquired pneumonia 11 (13.3%), and sepsis 3 (5.6%). There were 12 cases (14.4%) of inappropriate indication. Vancomycin was accurately dosed in 78 (96.3%) of the patients and 3 (3.7%) had an inappropriate dose. The most common frequency was three times daily 50 (61.7%), followed by twice daily 30 (37%), while 1 (1.3%) had an inappropriate frequency. Regarding treatment duration, 31 (38.3%) patients received it for 7–10 days, while 23 (28.4%) had an inappropriate duration (Table 3).

Discussion

This study assessed vancomycin utilization at BHUTH and identified encouraging practices alongside important challenges. Vancomycin was most frequently prescribed for pyogenic meningitis and severe community-acquired pneumonia, with all prescriptions being empirical (100%). The absence of microbiological confirmation highlights a key diagnostic gap. Comparable studies in Ethiopia [15, 16] and Asia [12, 13] also reported high levels of empirical prescribing, though usually below 100%. This suggests that strengthening microbiology laboratory capacity should be prioritized.

Adherence to indication (84.6%), dose (96.3%), and frequency (98.7%) recommendations was high, consistent with findings from Iran [12] and India [13]. However, treatment duration was frequently inappropriate, a pattern also reported in study done Ethiopia [15]. Our results suggest that prescribers are aware of recommended indication, doses, and freuence but less consistent with duration, making this a key stewardship target.

Vancomycin was frequently co-administered with other antimicrobials, most notably ceftriaxone 34 (42.0%), Ceftriaxone + Artesunate 23 (28.4%), and Ceftriaxone + Ant - tuberculosis 12 (14.8%). While these combinations may be intended to provide broad empirical coverage, they raise concerns regarding potential drug interactions, additive toxicity, and overuse of antibiotics. Without microbiological guidance or antibiogram data, such empirical polypharmacy could result in suboptimal therapy or unnecessary exposure to toxic agents, especially in vulnerable pediatric populations.

Among vancomycin-treated patients, the majority (74.1%) showed clinical improvement, and no adverse drug reactions were recorded. However, there were mortality (11.1%) and treatment failure (8.6%) cases, which could not be definitively linked to vancomycin use due to the study’s retrospective nature and the absence of microbiological confirmation. The absence of reported adverse events should be interpreted with caution, as documentation in routine medical records may be incomplete.

The WHO AWaRe classification [7, 17] and CDC stewardship guidelines [14] emphasize careful monitoring of antibiotics in the Watch and Reserve categories. Our findings, particularly the universal empirical use without culture guidance, highlight stewardship gaps that may accelerate the development of vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE) and other resistant organisms. Addressing these gaps requires investment in laboratory capacity, prescriber training, and enforcement of antimicrobial stewardship policies.

Limitations

This study was retrospective study design, reliance on clinical rather than microbiological diagnosis, and confinement to a single center, which may affect generalizability. Nevertheless, this study adds to the limited evidence base on vancomycin use in Ethiopia and contributes to the broader discussion on stewardship in sub-Saharan Africa.

Conclusion and recommendation

Vancomycin utilization at BHUTH demonstrated generally appropriate indication, dosing and frequency, but all prescriptions were empirical, without microbiological confirmation, and nearly one‑third had inappropriate treatment duration. These practices increase the risk of antimicrobial resistance, especially given vancomycin’s WHO AWaRe classification as a Watch antibiotic.

We recommend strengthening microbiology laboratory capacity, implementing structured antimicrobial stewardship programs, and providing prescriber training to improve compliance with national and international guidelines. Prospective, multi‑center studies are also needed to further evaluate vancomycin and other Watch/Reserve antibiotic use in Ethiopia.
